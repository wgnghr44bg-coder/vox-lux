For decades, Intel was the chip company.
If you owned a computer, there was a good chance it said Intel Inside.
Then, in just a few years, almost everything went wrong.
In 2024, Intel lost almost nineteen billion dollars.
Its stock lost about sixty percent of its value that year.
And in December, its chief executive was pushed out.
@scene open-antwoord
So can Intel come back?
The short answer is: it is trying, and it has powerful friends.
The United States government now owns about ten percent of the company.
Nvidia invested five billion dollars.
And in the third quarter of this year, Intel's revenue grew twenty-one percent.
But the part that matters most, its factories, is still losing billions.
Let's see how a giant fell, and what has to happen for it to rise again.
@scene h1-top
At its peak, Intel was one of the most valuable companies in the world.
Its chips powered almost every personal computer and most of the internet's servers.
It designed its own chips, and it built them in its own factories.
For a long time, nobody could match those factories.
@scene h1-iphone
But Intel made a series of big mistakes.
When Apple was building the first iPhone, it talked to Intel about making the chip.
Intel's chief executive at the time, Paul Otellini, later said he passed, because he did not think it would make enough money.
Phones became the biggest chip market in history, and Intel was not in it.
@scene h1-fabriek
Then its factories fell behind.
For years, Intel struggled to move to smaller, faster chip designs.
Meanwhile, TSMC in Taiwan pulled ahead, and started making the best chips for Apple, AMD and Nvidia.
@scene h1-amd
And then there was AMD.
For most of its history, AMD was the small rival that Intel could ignore.
But AMD stopped building its own factories, and let TSMC make its chips.
With TSMC's better factories, AMD's chips became faster and more efficient than Intel's.
Year after year, AMD took a bigger share of the most profitable market of all, chips for servers.
Intel was now losing on two fronts at once: in design, and in manufacturing.
@scene h1-ai
And then came AI.
The AI boom made Nvidia one of the most valuable companies on Earth.
Intel had almost nothing to sell into it.
@scene h2-gelsinger
In 2021, Intel brought back an engineer named Pat Gelsinger to save it.
His plan was bold: Intel would build chips not just for itself, but for other companies too.
It is called a foundry.
It meant building giant new factories, in Arizona, in Ohio, and in Europe.
And that cost a fortune.
@scene h2-ohio
The biggest symbol of that plan was a huge site near Columbus, Ohio.
Intel called it the future of American chipmaking.
It was supposed to start making chips in 2025.
In early 2025, Intel said the first factory there would not be finished until around 2030.
@scene h2-val
By 2024, the money was running out faster than the plan was working.
Intel cut about fifteen thousand jobs.
It suspended its dividend.
And in December 2024, Gelsinger was forced to retire.
@scene h3-tan
In March 2025, Intel named a new chief executive, Lip-Bu Tan.
He is a veteran chip investor and the former head of the chip design company Cadence.
He cut costs, slowed some of the factory projects, and promised to focus on customers.
@scene h3-trump
Then, in August 2025, something unusual happened.
President Trump publicly called on Tan to resign, over his past investments in China.
Days later, the two met at the White House.
And soon after, the government announced it would take a stake of about ten percent in Intel.
It was paid for with money that had already been promised to Intel under the CHIPS Act.
@scene h3-prijs
The government paid twenty dollars and forty-seven cents per share.
That made Washington one of Intel's biggest shareholders, almost overnight.
Critics called it a strange move for a country that usually leaves companies alone.
Supporters said America cannot afford to depend on factories in Taiwan for its most important chips.
@scene h3-nvidia
In September 2025, Nvidia announced it would invest five billion dollars in Intel, and work with it on new chips.
SoftBank from Japan invested two billion dollars too.
Nvidia paid twenty-three dollars and twenty-eight cents per share.
Think about how strange that is.
Just a few years earlier, Nvidia was the company that left Intel behind.
Now it was helping to keep Intel standing.
Suddenly, the biggest names in tech were betting on Intel again.
@scene h4-nu
So how is it going now?
In the third quarter of this year, Intel's revenue was sixteen and a half billion dollars.
That is twenty-one percent more than a year earlier.
Its data center and AI business grew seventy-eight percent.
@scene h4-foundry
But look closer at the factories.
The foundry business brought in six point seven billion dollars.
And it still lost about one point eight five billion dollars, in just three months.
@scene h4-waarom
Why does the factory business lose so much?
Because a modern chip factory costs tens of billions of dollars to build, long before it earns anything.
And a factory only pays off when it runs close to full.
For now, Intel's factories mostly make Intel's own chips.
To make money, they need other customers, and lots of them.
@scene h4-koers
And investors noticed.
The government had paid about twenty dollars per share.
During a rally that lasted for months, Intel's stock climbed far above that.
According to The Wall Street Journal, at its peak the government's stake was worth about sixty-one billion dollars.
@scene h4-crash
Then came the results on October thirtieth.
Revenue was up, but the factories were still losing money, and the outlook for the next quarter was weaker.
The next day, Intel's stock fell about twenty-seven percent, to around sixty-seven dollars.
According to TechCrunch, that one day wiped about twelve billion dollars off the value of the government's stake.
Even after that fall, taxpayers were still sitting on a paper gain of about nineteen billion dollars.
But it showed how fragile the comeback still is.

The key test is simple: will other companies trust Intel to build their chips?
Lip-Bu Tan says Intel has now signed its first outside customer for its newest process, called eighteen A P.

Neither company has confirmed a deal.
If Apple did come back to Intel, it would be one of the biggest turnarounds in the history of the industry.
@scene h5-wat
So what decides whether Intel comes back?
First, outside customers for the factories, and whether a big name signs up.
Second, whether its newest chips can really compete with TSMC.
And third, whether its AI business keeps growing.
