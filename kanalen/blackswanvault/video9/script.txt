# Titel (voorstel): SpaceX Stock Just Fell Below Its IPO Price. December 8 Could Make It Worse
# Opbouw: antwoord vooraan (schuld voor AI-chips, verliezen van AI, lock-up 8 dec), dan beursgang, het bedrijf, de chips, critici, lock-up, wat nu.
@scene open-val
On Friday, November twentieth, SpaceX shares fell almost eight percent.
They closed at one hundred twenty-seven dollars and seventy-nine cents.
That is below the price of one hundred thirty-five dollars that investors paid when SpaceX went public in June.
On the same day, the Nasdaq one hundred fell almost four percent.
@scene open-top
Just five months ago, this was the biggest stock market debut in history.
Within days, the shares were worth more than two hundred eleven dollars.
Since then, they have lost about forty percent.
@scene open-antwoord
So what went wrong?
The short answer is three things.
First, SpaceX is borrowing billions of dollars to build artificial intelligence, and its lenders are nervous.
Second, the AI part of the company is still losing money.
And third, on December eighth, a big group of early investors will be free to sell their shares.
@scene open-start
To understand why that matters, let's go back to June.
@scene h1-beursgang
On June twelfth, SpaceX started trading on the Nasdaq under the ticker S P C X.
The price was one hundred thirty-five dollars per share.
That valued the whole company at about one point seven seven trillion dollars.
According to CNBC, the offering raised a record eighty-six billion dollars.
The very first trade happened at one hundred fifty dollars.
@scene h1-feest
On its first day, the stock closed at almost one hundred sixty-one dollars.
At that price, the whole company was worth about two point one trillion dollars.
Four days after its debut, it closed above two hundred eleven.
SpaceX joined the Nasdaq one hundred after just fifteen trading days, thanks to a new fast-entry rule.
For a moment, it looked like nothing could stop it.
@scene h1-daling
But the excitement did not last.
On July fifteenth, the shares briefly dropped below the offer price for the first time.
It was the fourth losing day in a row.
By early August, the stock had closed as low as one hundred eight dollars.
It recovered in the fall, but never came close to its June high again.
After the midterm elections on November third, it fell below one hundred twenty-one dollars again.
In Texas, where SpaceX builds its rockets, the incoming governor has called for a pause on new data centers until there are rules for them.
@scene h2-bedrijf
So what are investors actually buying?
SpaceX is no longer just a rocket company.
@scene h2-starlink
Its biggest business is Starlink, the satellite internet service.
In 2025, Starlink brought in about eleven point four billion dollars in revenue.
And it made a profit of about four point four billion.
That is the money machine.
@scene h2-raketten
The rocket business itself, the part most people think of, brought in about four point one billion dollars.
And it lost money, about six hundred fifty-seven million dollars.
@scene h2-xai
Then there is AI.
In February, SpaceX merged with xAI, Elon Musk's artificial intelligence company.
xAI is the company behind the chatbot Grok.
In 2025, that AI business had about three point two billion dollars in revenue.
And it lost about the same amount.
@scene h2-totaal
Put it all together, and SpaceX had about eighteen point seven billion dollars in revenue in 2025.
But overall, it lost almost five billion dollars.
@scene h2-groei
The growth is real.
In the third quarter of this year, revenue jumped one hundred sixty-one percent, to fourteen point six billion dollars.
More than half of that, seven point six billion, came from the AI business.
That is about eight hundred thirty percent more than a year earlier.
But building AI is very expensive.
And that brings us to the chips.
@scene h3-chips
To compete in AI, you need huge numbers of computer chips, mostly from Nvidia.
And SpaceX decided to borrow the money to get them.
@scene h3-lening
According to Bloomberg, SpaceX put together a forty billion dollar financing package.
Thirty-two billion of it came from bonds, and the rest from bank loans.
The money pays for a five-year lease of Nvidia processors.
Goldman Sachs arranged the deal together with Apollo, and the bond giant PIMCO was one of the buyers.
@scene h3-rente
But lenders made SpaceX pay for it.
The biggest part of the bonds pays an interest rate well above the average for large American companies.
That is a sign that lenders see more risk.
@scene h3-ruimte
And then came a report that spooked investors.
According to the Financial Times, the lenders tried to stop Musk from launching those chips into space.
The newspaper cited people familiar with the talks.
@scene h3-waarom
Why would anyone launch chips into space?
Because SpaceX wants to build data centers in orbit, powered by the sun.
On November seventeenth, its finance chief, Bret Johnsen, said the first computing satellites could launch in 2027.
He said SpaceX could become one of the world's largest AI infrastructure providers.
That day, the stock rose about three percent.
@scene h3-onderpand
But for a lender, that plan has a problem.
The chips are the collateral for the loan.
If SpaceX ever fails to pay, the lenders want to take the chips back and sell them.
That is hard to do if the chips are flying around the Earth.
It is not clear whether those limits made it into the final deal.
But on Friday, the stock fell below its offer price.
@scene h4-critici
Some investors warned about this from the start.
Before the listing, the famous investor Michael Burry wrote, quote, "nothing in that S one suggests it is worth one trillion dollars, let alone two trillion."
A columnist at the Financial Times put it more simply.
"The price is nuts."
And analysts at Morningstar said a fair value for the whole company was about seven hundred eighty billion dollars.
They called SpaceX, quote, "significantly overvalued."
That is less than half of what it was valued at in June.
@scene h4-fans
Others are far more positive.
When SpaceX went public, most of the big banks gave it price targets well above the offer price.
They believe Starlink and AI will keep growing fast enough to justify the price.
The most optimistic target, from Raymond James, was eight hundred dollars per share.
Even the lowest, from Stifel, was one hundred ninety.
Today, the stock trades far below all of them.
@scene h5-lockup
Now to the date that many investors have circled.
When a company goes public, early investors and employees usually agree not to sell their shares for a while.
This is called a lock-up.
It stops a flood of shares from hitting the market right after the debut.
At SpaceX, smaller groups of shares were already released in steps, after its quarterly results.
@scene h5-datum
For many of SpaceX's early investors, that lock-up lasts one hundred eighty days.
According to the Motley Fool and companies that track these dates, it ends on December eighth.
Depending on how the rules are counted, hundreds of millions of shares could become free to sell.
For comparison, on a normal day in November, about one hundred million SpaceX shares change hands.
@scene h5-shotwell
Some insiders have already been allowed to sell part of their shares.
In September, SpaceX president Gwynne Shotwell sold about three hundred forty-two thousand shares, worth about fifty-two million dollars.
As of late October, it was the only sale by a top insider on file.
@scene h5-musk
And Elon Musk himself?
He owns about forty-two percent of the shares, and controls about eighty-two percent of the votes.
His shares stay locked until June 2027.
@scene h5-waarom
Why does a lock-up matter?
If many early investors decide to take profits at the same time, there are suddenly far more sellers than buyers.
Many early investors bought long before the listing, at much lower prices.
Even at today's price, many of them would make a big profit by selling.
@scene h6-anthropic
The timing also did not help.
On November nineteenth, the AI company Anthropic went public, raising more than eighty-one billion dollars.
Some analysts say big investors sold other tech stocks to make room for the new shares.
The day after Anthropic's debut, SpaceX fell below its own offer price.
And Anthropic's own shares fell more than nine percent that day too.
@scene h7-volgende
So what should you watch?
First, December eighth, and how much selling comes when the lock-up ends.
Second, the debt, and what the final deal says about launching chips into space.
Third, Starship, because the plan for data centers in orbit depends on it.
And fourth, the AI business, which has to start earning more than it costs.
None of this is financial advice.
@scene slot
SpaceX has changed how the world gets to space.
Now it is betting the company on AI, with borrowed money.
The rockets still fly.
The question is whether the stock can.
