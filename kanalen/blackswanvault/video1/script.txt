@scene h1-taiwan
Jensen Huang was born in Taiwan in 1963.
As a young boy, he was sent to the United States, and as a teenager, he worked at Denny's, washing dishes and waiting tables.
He studied electrical engineering at Oregon State University, and later at Stanford.
Then he designed chips at AMD and at a company called LSI Logic.
He was good at it. But he wanted to build something of his own.
@scene h1-booth
In April 1993, at the age of thirty, he sat down in a Denny's in San Jose with two engineers, Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem.
They had one idea. A special chip, made only for 3D graphics.
@scene h1-concurrentie
At the time, dozens of companies were chasing the same dream.
Most of them would not survive.
The technology was hard, the money ran out fast, and one bad product could end everything.
And Nvidia almost didn't either.
@scene h1-flop
Its first product, the NV1, was a flop.
The company had to let go of many of its people, and it came very close to running out of money.
@scene h1-riva
Then, in 1997, came the RIVA 128.
It sold about a million units in its first four months.
Nvidia was saved.
Huang never forgot how close it had been. Years later, he still told his staff that the company was always only thirty days from going out of business.
@scene h1-geforce
In 1999, Nvidia went public, and it launched a product it called the world's first GPU, the GeForce 256.
For the next ten years, that is what Nvidia was.
A company that made graphics cards for gamers.

@scene h2-idee
But Huang had a bigger idea.
A graphics chip does thousands of small calculations at the same time.
So what if it could do more than draw pictures?
@scene h2-cuda
In 2006, Nvidia launched CUDA, software that let scientists and programmers use graphics chips for almost any kind of math.
Wall Street did not understand it.
It cost a fortune to build, and for years, it made almost no money.
@scene h2-alexnet
Then, in 2012, three researchers in Toronto trained a neural network called AlexNet on two Nvidia gaming cards.
Their names were Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton.
It crushed one of the most famous contests in computer vision.
Almost overnight, the world of artificial intelligence discovered what Nvidia's chips could do.
Hinton would later win a Nobel Prize.
And Sutskever would go on to co-found a company you have definitely heard of.
@scene h2-openai
In 2016, Huang personally delivered the first DGX-1 AI supercomputer to a small research lab in San Francisco.
That lab was called OpenAI.

@scene h3-chatgpt
On the thirtieth of November, 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT.
It reached an estimated one hundred million users in about two months.
@scene h3-iedereen
Suddenly, every tech company on Earth wanted to build its own AI.
Microsoft. Google. Meta. Amazon.
And to do that, they all needed the same thing.
Nvidia chips.
Nvidia's top AI chips became so hard to get that companies waited months for them.
Startups bragged about how many they owned.
For a while, Nvidia chips were almost a currency of their own.
@scene h3-omzet
Look at what happened next.
In fiscal 2023, Nvidia made twenty-seven billion dollars.
One year later, sixty-one billion.
Then one hundred and thirty.
Then two hundred and sixteen.
@scene h3-marge

Nvidia was keeping around seventy cents of gross profit for every dollar of sales.
@scene h3-koers

A share that cost about seventeen dollars when ChatGPT launched, adjusted for splits, was worth more than two hundred and thirty dollars by October 2026.
@scene h3-biljoen
In 2023, Nvidia became a one trillion dollar company.
In July 2025, it became the first company in history worth four trillion dollars.
Three months later, five trillion.
@scene h3-ster
And Jensen Huang became a celebrity.
Sold-out keynotes. Crowds of fans. A CEO treated like a rock star.
Every year, he walked on stage in his black leather jacket and showed the world what was coming next.
First Hopper. Then Blackwell. Then Vera Rubin.
Each one faster, bigger, and more expensive than the last.
@scene h3-maar
But there was one market that Nvidia could never fully control.
China.

@scene h4-regels
For years, China was one of Nvidia's biggest markets.
But from 2022, the United States began to limit which AI chips could be sold there, afraid that they could help China's military.
@scene h4-h20
Nvidia's most powerful chips were banned for China.
So it designed weaker versions, like the H20, just for the Chinese market.
But every time the rules changed, Nvidia had to change its products again.
For Washington, it was about national security.
For Nvidia, it was about billions of dollars in sales.
@scene h4-h200
Then, in December 2025, President Trump said that Nvidia could also sell its more powerful H200 chip to China.
@scene h4-megaspeed
Meanwhile, investigators were watching a company called Megaspeed.
It ran AI computing centers in Asia, and according to The New York Times, it had become one of Nvidia's largest buyers, with about four point eight billion dollars of Nvidia equipment.
@scene h4-onderzoek
Authorities in the United States and in Singapore were investigating whether Megaspeed had helped banned chips reach China.
In October, Huang told Bloomberg that his dealings with Megaspeed were completely normal.
Nvidia said it had no reason to believe its products had been diverted.
@scene h4-vrouw
