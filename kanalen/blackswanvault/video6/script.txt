# BlackswanVault video 6: werkt AI eigenlijk? Eén zin per regel. Feiten: bronnen.md. Getallen voor de stem uitgeschreven.
# Titel (voorstel): Is AI Actually Working? Harvard Just Looked Inside 655 Companies
# Opbouw: antwoord vooraan (AI werkt op de taak, maar het bedrijf eromheen niet), dan geld, onderzoek, flessenhals, banen, geschiedenis, wat nu.
@scene open-geld
This year, the four biggest tech companies in America are spending more than seven hundred billion dollars to build artificial intelligence.
That is more money than most countries earn in a year.
And it all rests on one promise.
That AI will make workers far more productive.
@scene open-onderzoek
On November nineteenth, two researchers at Harvard released the latest version of one of the biggest tests of that promise so far.
They looked inside six hundred fifty-five companies.
More than seven hundred thousand workers.
Four hundred forty-five million pieces of real work.
@scene open-uitkomst
What they found is strange.
With AI agents, engineers wrote forty-four percent more code.
But the amount of finished work barely moved.
@scene open-vraag
So is AI actually working?
The short answer is yes, and no.
AI works on the task.
But the company around it cannot keep up.
And that difference could decide whether this seven hundred billion dollar bet pays off.
Let's start with the money.
@scene h1-uitgaven
At the end of October, the tech giants reported their latest numbers.
Amazon expects to spend about two hundred twenty billion dollars this year.
Alphabet, the owner of Google, between one hundred ninety-five and two hundred five billion.
Microsoft, about one hundred seventy billion.
And Meta, between one hundred thirty-five and one hundred forty-five billion.
Most of it goes to chips, servers and data centers for AI.
Some of it is already paying off.
Microsoft's cloud business, Azure, grew forty-eight percent.
@scene h1-kasstroom
That spending is starting to hurt.
Amazon, Alphabet and Meta all reported negative free cash flow.
That means they spent more cash than their businesses brought in.
Of the four, only Microsoft stayed positive.
Earlier this year, Meta's free cash flow in a single quarter fell to one point two billion dollars, from twenty-six billion a year before.
@scene h1-beleggers
Investors noticed.
After its report, Alphabet's stock fell about ten percent.
And Alphabet said its spending will rise significantly again next year.
Earlier this year, analysts at Bank of America and Evercore estimated that the total could pass one trillion dollars in 2027.
So the question is simple.
What are companies getting back?
@scene h2-onderzoek
That is exactly what the Harvard study tried to measure.
It was written by Fiona Chen and James Stratton, two economics researchers at Harvard.
It is a working paper, so it has not been peer reviewed yet.
But the data behind it is unusual.
@scene h2-data
It comes from Jellyfish, a platform that companies use to track the work of their software teams.
It sees every piece of code an engineer writes, every task they finish, and even their meetings.
And it records when a company starts using AI tools, like GitHub Copilot, Cursor and Claude Code.
The data runs from January 2021 to August 2026.
@scene h2-waarom-software
Software is the best place to test AI.
The big AI labs say AI already writes about eighty percent of their own code.
If AI makes companies more productive anywhere, it should be here.
@scene h2-code
And at first, it looks like it does.
A year after companies started using AI coding assistants, their engineers wrote about thirty percent more lines of code.
Then came AI agents, tools that can write and change code on their own.
They added another forty-four percent.
The number of code changes sent in for approval rose about thirty-six percent.
@scene h2-af
But writing code is not the goal.
The goal is working software.
So the researchers looked at how many tasks were actually finished and shipped to customers.
With AI assistants, there was no increase at all.
With AI agents, finished tasks rose about fourteen percent.
And even that result was borderline.
@scene h2-groter
Maybe the tasks just got bigger?
The researchers checked that too, using AI to read the task descriptions.
The tasks did not get bigger.
So forty-four percent more code became, at best, about fourteen percent more finished work.
@scene h3-flessenhals
So where did all that extra code go?
The answer is a traffic jam.
@scene h3-review
Before new code goes live, another engineer has to check it.
This is called code review.
Someone has to read it, test it, and approve it.
And it is still done mostly by humans.
@scene h3-cijfers
After companies started using AI agents, the time from sending in code to getting it approved rose fifty-six percent.
Code waited longer before anyone looked at it.
And once someone did, the review took longer too.
The number of comments on each change rose almost thirty-nine percent.
@scene h3-wie
There is a reason this is hard to fix.
Engineers usually review code they already know.
In the study, that was the case for about eighty-three percent of all reviews.
So you cannot simply hand the extra work to anyone.
@scene h3-interview
One engineer told the researchers that only one senior engineer was allowed to approve changes on a project.
He was so busy, he could only review the code once a week.
And after some outages caused by AI-written code, new rules required senior engineers to approve AI-generated code.
Which made the traffic jam even worse.
@scene h3-ai-review
What about AI that reviews code?
About seventy-nine percent of the companies already use it.
But they still keep humans in charge, and humans still write the overwhelming majority of reviews.
@scene h3-fabriek
Think of a factory.
You make one machine twice as fast.
But the next station on the line works at the same speed as before.
The factory does not produce twice as much.
It just builds a bigger pile in front of the slow station.
@scene h3-tijd
And writing code is only a small part of the job.
According to the study, engineers spend only thirty to forty percent of their time writing code.
The rest goes to planning, meetings, reviewing and testing.
AI is speeding up the part that was never the whole job.
@scene h3-model
The researchers also estimated what happens inside the companies.
According to their model, AI agents make writing code about seventy-four percent more productive.
But firms moved people from writing code to reviewing it.
Time spent on coding fell about twenty-three percent, while time spent on reviewing rose about forty-three percent.
@scene h4-banen
Then there is the question everyone is asking.
Is AI taking jobs?
Many companies say AI is changing how many engineers they need.
But in this data, the researchers could not find a significant effect on employment.
Firms did not hire many more people.
And they did not cut many either.
Keep in mind that this study only covers software engineers.
It does not tell us what AI is doing to other jobs.
@scene h4-model
That may not last.
The researchers also built a model to look further ahead.
In their simulations, as companies keep adjusting, output grows and total employment declines.
But if the review bottleneck stays, both effects are smaller.
@scene h4-stiglitz
Others are more worried.
On November fourteenth, the Nobel prize winning economist Joseph Stiglitz called the AI boom a bubble.
He was speaking at a business summit in New Delhi.
He said the companies investing massive amounts in AI will not get the returns they expect.
And he said AI is really good at replacing coders and programmers.
He also pointed to a deeper problem.
If AI destroys jobs, who will have the income to buy what it produces?
@scene h5-solow
If this sounds familiar, it should.
In 1987, another Nobel prize winning economist, Robert Solow, wrote one famous sentence.
"You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics."
He meant that companies were buying computers everywhere, but the economy was not getting more productive.
@scene h5-later
Companies had spent years buying computers.
And for a long time, the numbers did not show it.
Then, in the second half of the 1990s, American productivity growth finally sped up.
Economists often link that to companies finally changing how they worked around the new machines.
@scene h5-les
That may be the real lesson of the Harvard study.
A new tool is not enough.
The work around it has to change too.
And that can take years.
The question for investors is whether seven hundred billion dollars a year can wait that long.
@scene h6-volgende
So what should you watch?
First, the next earnings reports at the end of January, and how much the tech giants plan to spend in 2027.
Second, their cash flow, because negative free cash flow cannot last forever.
Third, AI tools that review and test code, because that is where the traffic jam is.
And fourth, whether finished work, not just code, starts to rise.
@scene slot
AI can already help engineers write more code than ever.
The hard part was never just writing it.
It is everything that comes after.
