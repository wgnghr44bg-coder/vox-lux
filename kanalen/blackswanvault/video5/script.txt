# BlackswanVault video 5: storingen in Amerika. Eén zin per regel. Feiten: bronnen.md. Getallen voor de stem uitgeschreven.
# Titel: America Keeps Crashing. Here's What's Actually Going On
# Opbouw: antwoord vooraan (geen aanval; haast om lekken te dichten die AI vond), daarna per storing, hamsteren, AI, wat nu.
@scene open-vlucht
On Friday, November thirteenth, at six minutes to four in the afternoon, the United States stopped every flight heading to its airports.
For more than four hours, the country's air traffic system was effectively shut down.
It was the longest nationwide ground stop in twenty-five years.
@scene open-week
And it was not the only thing that broke that week.
The day before, Microsoft's email went down around the world.
The Monday after, customers of America's biggest bank saw their payments delayed.
A day later, an internal network of the National Security Agency went offline.
And so did parts of Apple.
@scene open-paniek
Four big failures, in four business days.
Online, people started asking the same question.
Is America under attack?
Some did not wait for the answer.
They went to Costco and bought generators.
@scene open-antwoord
So what is actually going on?
The short answer is surprising.
According to the government, this was not a cyberattack.
The most likely explanation is the opposite.
Companies and agencies are rushing to fix more security holes than ever before.
Many of those holes were found by artificial intelligence.
And some of the fixes broke things.
Let's go through it, one failure at a time.
@scene h1-microsoft
It started on Thursday, November twelfth.
Outlook, Teams and other Microsoft 365 services stopped working for businesses, schools, governments and hospitals.
According to CNBC, millions of users lost access to their email.
The problems lasted across two workdays.
@scene h1-oorzaak
A few days later, Microsoft explained what happened.
Back in July, it had prepared a fix for the gateway servers that handle its network traffic.
That fix contained a hidden bug.
For months, nobody noticed.
Then, during the busiest hours in the United States, traffic surged, while fewer servers were available.
The hidden bug was triggered.
Requests piled up until the servers simply stopped responding.
@scene h1-herstel
And fixing it was harder than it sounds.
Every time servers came back online, they were flooded by all the traffic that had been waiting.
And they went down again.
Microsoft said it was deeply sorry.
And it said this was not a hack.
@scene h2-faa
The next day was worse.
On Friday afternoon, air traffic controllers in Chicago started losing communications.
Within about half an hour, the problem spread across the country.
At six minutes to four, Eastern time, the Federal Aviation Administration stopped all flights heading to American airports.
@scene h2-gevolg
Nearly eight thousand flights were canceled that day.
More than ten thousand were delayed.
Flights from Europe to the United States were suspended until the next morning.
And the trouble continued all weekend.
@scene h2-router
What caused it?
The answer, it seems, is one router.
@scene h2-arkansas
In southern Arkansas, a contractor called L3Harris was replacing old copper cables in the air traffic network with fiber optic lines.
As part of that work, a new router was installed.
According to Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy, that router started sending a thousand connection attempts every four to five seconds across the network.
Radar feeds and radio links at seventeen air traffic control centers were disrupted.
@scene h2-open
But that is not the full story yet.
The company that makes the router said its equipment worked exactly as it was configured.
The FAA has stopped installing these routers while it investigates.
And as of this video, the official root cause has still not been announced.
@scene h2-toezicht
There is also an uncomfortable detail.
Two months earlier, a government watchdog had warned that the FAA was not properly supervising the work of its contractors.
Now, Duffy is asking Congress for fifty billion dollars to modernize the system.
@scene h3-maandag
Then came Monday, November sixteenth.
At JPMorgan Chase, the biggest bank in the United States, customer payments were delayed.
The bank blamed a software bug in its own hardware systems.
People sending money to those accounts were delayed too, and it caused jitters across the financial system.
On the same day, a system failure at United Airlines delayed departing flights.
@scene h3-dinsdag
On Tuesday, it was the turn of the National Security Agency.
An internal network it uses to share classified information went offline.
The agency said it happened during scheduled routine maintenance.
That same day, Apple Music, the App Store and Apple's support services were disrupted after what Apple called an internal update.
And Marriott customers could not sign in to their accounts.
@scene h4-paniek
By now, many people had stopped believing in coincidence.
On TikTok and other platforms, videos told people to stock up before the next blackout.
And many listened.
@scene h4-costco
According to the research firm Mizuho, twenty-one of the twenty-six expensive generators on Costco's website were sold out.
Mizuho expects Costco's sales in the United States to grow more than ten percent in November.
It says Sam's Club, Home Depot and Lowe's may benefit too.
Its analyst pointed to anxiety after the Microsoft outage and the ground stop, and to apocalyptic talk on social media.
We will know more on December tenth, when Costco reports its results.
@scene h5-aanval
So, was it an attack?
Everyone involved says no.
The government, Microsoft, JPMorgan, United, Apple and Marriott all say there is no evidence of a cyberattack.
@scene h5-clayton
The Director of National Intelligence, Jay Clayton, was very direct.
"This is not cyberattack," he said on CNBC, "and this is not a coordinated effort by a nation state."
He also said upgrades like these are common, and called for a nationwide upgrade of America's digital infrastructure.
@scene h5-twijfel
Not everyone is satisfied.
House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries said it was too early to rule anything out, and asked the administration to explain itself to Congress.
But one senator offered a different explanation.
@scene h5-warner
Senator Mark Warner, who co-chairs the Senate's cybersecurity caucus, said there is good reason to believe these outages are the unintended consequences of efforts to make our systems safer.
To understand what he means, you need to know what AI has been doing to software this year.
@scene h6-lekken
Every piece of software has weak spots.
Security experts call them vulnerabilities.
Each one that is found and published gets an official number.
@scene h6-teller
In all of 2025, there were fewer than fifty thousand of them.
This year, the count passed one hundred thousand by the middle of November.
More than double.
And according to the researchers who track them, AI-driven bug hunting is driving much of that surge.
@scene h6-maand
Google's security team counted about five thousand new vulnerabilities in January.
By August, it was more than ten thousand a month.
In June, Microsoft fixed a record two hundred and six of them in a single monthly update.
@scene h6-php
Then, in October, came a big one.
A security company used an AI model to find two critical flaws in PHP, the software that powers a huge part of the web.
The flaws allowed attackers to take over a server from anywhere.
The US government told organizations to update right away, or take their systems offline.
And hackers were already using the flaws.
In the hours after the fix came out, one research firm found that about one and a half million of the two point six million servers it watches were still running the old, vulnerable version.
@scene h6-haast
That started a race.
Every company had to fix its systems before attackers found them.
And when you change thousands of systems in a hurry, some of them break.
According to Fox News, Jay Clayton said that some systems did not come back up properly after this patching.
"That is what has happened at the NSA today," he said.
@scene h6-dubbel
Here is the strange part.
AI is helping defenders find holes faster than ever.
But it is also helping attackers.
According to Google, hackers now use AI agents to study new security fixes, and work out which holes they close.
One professor at New York University said AI is making it way easier for attackers to launch cyberattacks.
So the pressure to fix everything, immediately, keeps growing.
@scene h6-tiener
A researcher at the security company SentinelOne put it this way.
"We're in the weird teenage phase of technology."
@scene h7-volgende
So what happens next?
First, the FAA has said it will announce the root cause of the ground stop.
Second, Congress wants answers, and lawmakers are asking for briefings.
Third, on December tenth, Costco will show whether the panic buying was real.
And fourth, the busiest travel week of the year starts with Thanksgiving, on November twenty-sixth.
@scene h7-meer
One cybersecurity expert in London warned that as AI adoption speeds up, we are likely to see more incidents like these.
@scene slot
For years, we were told the biggest danger to our systems was a hacker in another country.
This month showed another danger.
The fixes themselves.
