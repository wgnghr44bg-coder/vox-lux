Tonight we travel to one of the most remote and mysterious places on Earth. It is a small island in the middle of the Pacific Ocean. Its people call it Rapa Nui. Much of the world knows it as Easter Island.

[long-pause]

It is early morning, and the sky is turning pink and gold over the ocean. A soft wind blows across green grassy hills. Wild horses are grazing on the slopes. And along the shore, standing on a long stone platform, there is a row of giant stone figures. There are fifteen of them, side by side. They are taller than a house, with long faces, heavy brows and closed lips. They stand with their backs to the sea, looking inland, over the island. The first rays of the sun light up their stone faces.

[pause]

These giant figures are called moai. There are almost a thousand of them on the island. Some stand on platforms by the sea. Some lie on the ground, as if sleeping. Some are still half buried in the slopes of an old volcano, where they were carved. For hundreds of years, visitors have looked at them and wondered. Who made them? How were they moved? And why?

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:rano-raraku-volcano-slope-moai-heads-emerging-grass-misty-morning}
Tonight we will try to answer some of these questions, gently and slowly. We will learn about the brave sailors who first found this tiny island, in the vastness of the ocean. We will meet a family who lived there long ago, in the time when the great statues were still being made. We will visit the quarry where the moai were carved from the soft volcanic rock. And we will hear how the giant statues may have walked across the island.

[pause]

We will also hear about the trees that disappeared, and the life that changed. We will watch the great race of the birdman, on the cliffs above the sea. We will look at a mysterious writing that nobody can read. And we will meet the Dutch sailors who came to the island on Easter Sunday, almost three hundred years ago, and gave it the name we know today.

[pause]

{img:polynesian-family-stone-house-boat-shaped-grass-roof-hills-ocean-evening}
Some parts of this story are still a mystery. Scientists and the people of Rapa Nui still discuss them today. We will not try to solve every mystery tonight. Some questions may never be fully answered. But we will listen to what is known, and wonder about the rest.

[long-pause]

So let us begin on a quiet hillside on Rapa Nui, about five hundred years ago. The grass is green and the sky is blue. The ocean stretches away on every side, as far as the eye can see. A thin line of smoke rises from a little earth oven near a house. A woman is weaving a basket in the morning sun. A boy is running down to the shore with a fishing line. And far away, on the slope of a volcano, the sound of stone tools tapping on rock drifts softly through the morning air.

[pause]

{img:moai-faces-close-morning-light-long-ears-heavy-brows-stone-texture}
Somewhere up there, men are carving a giant from the mountain. They have been working on it for many months. Soon it will be finished. And then, the whole village will come together, to help it walk to its place by the sea.

[pause]

Down by the shore, the waves are breaking softly on the black volcanic rocks. White foam rushes in and out between the stones. A fisherman is standing on a rock, throwing a line into the sea. Seabirds are calling over the water. And on a little grassy hill above the shore, an old woman sits in the morning sun, watching the ocean, as she has done every morning for many years.

[pause]

Rapa Nui is a place of quiet and space. There are no big cities, no crowds, no noise. There is only the wind in the grass, the sound of the waves, and the call of the seabirds. At night, the sky is so dark and clear that the stars seem very close. People who visit say that the island has a special feeling, peaceful and a little mysterious. As if the ancestors are still there, watching.

[long-pause]

{img:tiny-island-vast-pacific-ocean-from-above-blue-water-all-around}
Rapa Nui is one of the most isolated places where people live, anywhere in the world.

[pause]

It is a small island, shaped like a triangle. It is only about twenty-four kilometres long, and twelve kilometres wide. You could walk across it in a day. At each corner of the triangle, there is an old volcano. The volcanoes are quiet now, and have been for a very long time. Their slopes are covered with grass, and some of their craters are filled with lakes.

[long-pause]

All around the island, there is ocean. The nearest place where people live is a tiny island called Pitcairn, about two thousand kilometres to the west. The nearest big land is the coast of South America, about three and a half thousand kilometres to the east. That is about as far as from Amsterdam to Cairo, but all across open sea. When you stand on Rapa Nui and look out, you see nothing but water, in every direction.

[pause]

{img:rapa-nui-coast-black-volcanic-rocks-waves-crashing-cliffs-green-grass}
The island is made of volcanic rock, black and brown and red. Along the coast, there are cliffs and rocky shores, where the waves crash and spray. There are only a few small sandy beaches. The most beautiful is called Anakena, on the north coast. It has soft white sand, palm trees and calm blue water. It is the place where, the old stories say, the first people came ashore.

[long-pause]

The weather on Rapa Nui is mild, neither very hot nor very cold. It is warm in summer and cool in winter, with rain in all seasons. The wind blows often across the open hills. There are no rivers on the island, because the rain soaks quickly into the porous volcanic rock. The people get their water from the crater lakes, from springs near the shore, and from rain collected in hollows in the rock.

[pause]

{img:crater-lake-rano-kau-reeds-water-volcano-rim-ocean-beyond}
There are very few kinds of plants and animals on the island. Because it is so far from anywhere, few creatures ever reached it. There were no land mammals at all, before people came. There were seabirds, many of them, nesting on the cliffs and little islands. There were lizards and insects. There were fish in the sea around the island. And, long ago, there were forests of tall palm trees.

[long-pause]

The people of Rapa Nui have a beautiful name for their island. They call it Te Pito o te Henua. That means the navel of the world. A navel is the little round mark in the middle of your tummy. So the name means the centre of the world. For the people who lived there, alone in the middle of the great ocean, it truly was the centre of their world.

[pause]

{img:round-smooth-stone-te-pito-kura-navel-of-world-shore-rocks-sunlight}
On the north coast, near the shore, there is a large round stone, very smooth. It is called Te Pito Kura, the golden navel. The old stories say that the first king of the island brought it with him in his canoe, from his homeland far away. People say that the stone feels warm, and that it has special power. Even today, visitors sometimes put their hands on it, and close their eyes, and feel the warmth of the sun in the old smooth rock.

[pause]

Because the island is so small and so far away, the people of Rapa Nui knew it very well. They knew every hill and every rock, every cave and every spring. They gave names to every place, and told stories about them. This rock was where a famous fisherman sat. This cave was where a girl once hid. This hill was where the first sweet potatoes were planted. The whole island was full of stories.

[pause]

From the top of the volcanoes, you can see the whole island at once, and the ocean all around it. The people of Rapa Nui sometimes climbed up there to look out to sea. They looked for the seabirds, coming back to nest. They looked for shoals of fish, making the water shimmer. And perhaps, sometimes, they wondered if there were other lands out there, beyond the edge of the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:polynesian-double-hulled-voyaging-canoe-sails-open-ocean-sunrise-crew}
How did people ever find this tiny island, in the middle of the vast Pacific Ocean? They were some of the greatest sailors who have ever lived. We call them the Polynesians.

[pause]

The Polynesians are the people of the many islands of the eastern Pacific. Over many centuries, they explored and settled thousands of islands, spread across an enormous area of ocean. They found Hawaii in the north, New Zealand in the south-west, and Rapa Nui in the south-east. These three places form a great triangle, called the Polynesian triangle. It is bigger than all of Europe.

[long-pause]

The Polynesians sailed in great canoes, with two hulls side by side, joined by a deck. These double canoes were strong and steady, and could carry many people. They had sails made of woven leaves, and long paddles for steering. On board, there was room for families, food, water, plants and animals. They could sail for weeks across the open sea.

[pause]

{img:navigator-reading-stars-night-sky-canoe-deck-ocean-calm-ancient}
The Polynesians had no compasses or maps. But they were wonderful navigators. They knew the stars, and where each one rises and sets over the sea. They knew the patterns of the waves and the swells, which they could feel through the hull of the canoe. They watched the clouds, which gather over islands. They watched the birds, which fly out to sea in the morning and back to land in the evening. They read the ocean like a book.

[long-pause]

The navigators learned these skills from their teachers, over many years. They learned the names of hundreds of stars. They learned songs and chants that helped them remember the way. Some navigators could lie down in the canoe and feel the swells under their backs, and know which way the land lay. Their knowledge was passed from generation to generation, by memory.

[pause]

{img:canoe-carrying-plants-pigs-chickens-families-ocean-voyage-sunny}
On their voyages, the Polynesians carried everything they needed to start a new life. They carried plants: sweet potatoes, taro, bananas, sugar cane and paper mulberry trees. They carried chickens, pigs and dogs. Some little rats came along too, perhaps as stowaways, perhaps on purpose, as food. When they found a new island, they planted their gardens and made a new home.

[long-pause]

Nobody knows exactly when the first people came to Rapa Nui. Scientists have studied old charcoal and bones, and they think it was about a thousand years ago, perhaps around the year 1200. The people may have come from the islands of Mangareva or the Marquesas, far to the west. It was a very long voyage, perhaps two or three weeks, across empty ocean. Finding Rapa Nui, so small and so far away, must have been a great achievement.

[pause]

{img:sailors-seeing-island-on-horizon-canoe-birds-clouds-joy-dawn}
Imagine what it was like. After many days at sea, with nothing but water all around, the navigator sees a sign. Perhaps a bird, flying toward the horizon in the evening. Perhaps a little cloud, standing still over the sea. Perhaps a change in the waves. The canoe turns toward it. The people watch and wait, full of hope. And then, at dawn, they see it. A little green island, with three volcanoes, rising out of the blue ocean. A new home.

[pause]

The great voyaging canoes were made with great skill, without any metal tools. The builders used stone adzes to shape the hulls from big tree trunks. They tied the pieces together with ropes made of coconut fibre. They sealed the joints with sap from the breadfruit tree. The sails were woven from the leaves of the pandanus plant. Building a great canoe took many months, and many skilled people.

[pause]

On the long voyages, the sailors ate food that would keep for a long time. They ate dried fish and coconuts, fermented breadfruit and dried bananas. They drank rainwater, caught in their sails, and coconut water. They fished as they sailed. They sang songs and chanted to keep their spirits up. And they watched the sky and the sea, day and night, for signs of land.

[pause]

Today, people in Hawaii and other Pacific islands have built new voyaging canoes, just like the old ones. One of them is called Hōkūle'a. Its sailors have crossed the Pacific many times, without compasses or maps, using only the stars, the waves and the birds. In 1999, Hōkūle'a sailed all the way to Rapa Nui. The people of the island came down to the shore to welcome it, with songs and tears of joy.

[long-pause]

{img:anakena-beach-white-sand-palm-trees-calm-turquoise-water-canoes-landing-legend}
The people of Rapa Nui have their own story about how their island was first found. It is a story that has been told for many hundreds of years.

[pause]

Long ago, the story says, there was a king called Hotu Matu'a. He lived on an island far to the west, called Hiva. One night, a wise man in his court had a dream. In the dream, his spirit flew across the ocean, far to the east. There, he saw a beautiful island, with three volcanoes and white sandy beaches. When he woke up, he told the king about it.

[long-pause]

So the king sent seven young men to find the island. They sailed east, following the directions of the dream. After a long voyage, they found it. They explored the island and planted some sweet potatoes. Then the king himself came, with his family and his people, in two great canoes. They landed on the white beach of Anakena, on the north coast. There, the king's wife gave birth to a son. And the new people began their life on Rapa Nui.

[pause]

{img:polynesian-king-family-arriving-beach-canoes-people-carrying-plants-dawn}
Hotu Matu'a divided the island among his sons. Each son and his family received a part of the land, running from the coast up to the middle of the island. Their descendants became the different clans of Rapa Nui. Each clan had its own land, its own villages, its own fishing places and its own sacred platforms by the sea.

[long-pause]

Nobody knows how much of this story is history, and how much is legend. But archaeologists have found that Anakena was indeed one of the first places where people lived on the island. They have found the remains of old houses, and old tools, and the bones of animals that the first people ate. So the old story may remember something true.

[pause]

{img:archaeologists-digging-anakena-beach-old-tools-bones-sieves-today}
When the first people arrived, the island looked very different from today. It was covered with forests of tall palm trees, with trunks as thick as a man. These palms were related to the Chilean wine palm, one of the biggest palms in the world. There were other trees and shrubs too. Millions of seabirds nested on the island. There were dolphins and seals in the sea. It was a rich and green place.

[long-pause]

The first families built their houses near the shore. They fished in the sea and gathered shellfish on the rocks. They hunted seabirds and collected their eggs. They cleared small areas of forest to make gardens. They planted sweet potatoes, taro, bananas and sugar cane. They kept chickens, which became very important to them. The rats that came with them ran wild in the forest. Pigs and dogs did not survive the long voyage, it seems, or did not last long on the island.

[pause]

{img:first-settlers-gardens-forest-clearing-sweet-potato-planting-palm-trees}
Slowly, over many generations, the number of people on the island grew. New villages were built all around the coast. The clans grew bigger and more organised. They had chiefs and priests, craftsmen and fishermen, farmers and builders. And they began to do something extraordinary. They began to carve giant figures of stone, and set them up on platforms around the island, facing the land.

[pause]

The old story says that Hotu Matu'a lived at Anakena for the rest of his life. When he was very old, he climbed up to the top of the volcano Rano Kau, at the other end of the island. From there, he looked out across the ocean, toward his old homeland, far away in the west. He called out to the spirits of his homeland, to say goodbye. Then he came down, and passed away peacefully, among his family.

[pause]

The descendants of Hotu Matu'a remembered his name for many hundreds of years. The chiefs of the island traced their families back to him. At great ceremonies, the wise men chanted the names of all the chiefs, one after another, back to Hotu Matu'a himself. It was a long list, many generations long. And every child of the island grew up knowing the story of the first king.

[long-pause]

{img:rapa-nui-carver-father-strong-tattoos-stone-tool-quarry-morning-1500}
Let us meet a family who lives on Rapa Nui, about five hundred years ago, around the year 1500. It is the time when the great moai are being carved.

[pause]

The father is called Tavake. He is a carver of moai, one of the skilled craftsmen of the island. He is about thirty-five years old, strong and broad-shouldered. His body has tattoos on it, beautiful patterns of lines and curves, which show his clan and his skill. He works at the quarry on the slope of the volcano called Rano Raraku. He is calm and patient, and proud of his work.

[pause]

The mother is called Vai, which means water. She grows the food for the family in their gardens. She weaves mats and baskets from reeds. She makes cloth from the bark of the paper mulberry tree, by beating it flat with a wooden mallet. She also knows how to find shellfish on the rocks and how to cook in an earth oven. She is warm and cheerful, and she sings while she works.

[long-pause]

They have two children. The daughter is called Hina, and she is eleven. She helps her mother in the gardens and with the weaving. She loves to swim in the sea, and she is a very good swimmer. She also loves to listen to the old stories, and she remembers every word. She wants to be a keeper of stories, like her grandmother.

[pause]

{img:girl-eleven-swimming-ocean-waves-rocks-shore-laughing-sunlight-polynesian}
The son is called Mako, and he is eight. Mako is a word for a kind of shark, quick and strong. He is lively and curious, and always running somewhere. He loves to go with his father to the quarry, and watch the carvers at work. He has a little stone tool of his own, and he scratches pictures on rocks with it. He dreams of carving a moai one day, a giant one, the biggest ever.

[long-pause]

The grandmother lives with them too. She is Vai's mother, and her name is Uka. She is old, perhaps sixty, which is old for that time. Her hair is white, and her face is lined. She knows all the old stories and chants of the island. She knows the names of the ancestors, back for many generations. She knows the stars and the winds, the plants and the seasons. Everyone in the village comes to her for advice.

[pause]

{img:old-polynesian-grandmother-telling-stories-children-firelight-night-house}
The family belongs to one of the clans of the island, which lives on the south-east coast. Their village is near a great stone platform by the sea, called an ahu. On the platform, there are several moai, standing in a row, looking inland over the village. They represent the great ancestors of the clan. The family feels that the ancestors are watching over them, protecting them.

[long-pause]

Life is not always easy on the island. The family must work hard to grow enough food. Sometimes there are storms that damage the gardens. Sometimes there is not enough rain. But mostly, life is calm and good. There is fish from the sea, and sweet potatoes from the gardens, and chickens in the yard. There are festivals and games, songs and dances, and stories by the fire at night.

[pause]

{img:family-eating-together-outside-house-sweet-potatoes-fish-evening-ocean-view}
In the evenings, the family sits together outside their house, as the sun goes down over the ocean. They eat sweet potatoes and fish, cooked in the earth oven. They talk about the day. Tavake tells them how the carving is going. Vai tells them about the gardens. Hina and Mako tell them about their adventures. And Grandmother Uka tells them a story, while the stars come out, one by one, over the endless sea.

[pause]

The family also has a few chickens, which wander around the house during the day, pecking at seeds and insects. They are small brown and white chickens, with bright eyes. Mako has a favourite, a little white hen, which follows him around. He has given her a name: Ua, which means rain. She lays an egg almost every day. Mako always finds it, and brings it proudly to his mother.

[long-pause]

{img:hare-paenga-boat-shaped-house-stone-foundation-thatched-roof-entrance-small}
The family lives in a special kind of house, found only on Rapa Nui. It is called a hare paenga, and it is shaped like an upside-down canoe.

[pause]

The house is long and narrow, with rounded ends, like a boat. Its foundation is made of carefully cut stones, laid in a long oval on the ground. Each stone has little holes drilled in its top. Into these holes, long bent sticks are pushed, to make the frame of the roof. The frame is covered with thick layers of grass, reeds and leaves, tied on in neat rows. The roof comes all the way down to the ground, so the house has no walls.

[long-pause]

The entrance is a small low opening in the middle of one side. You have to crawl in on your hands and knees. That keeps the house warm at night, and keeps out the wind and the rain. Inside, it is dim and cosy. The floor is covered with mats of woven reeds. There is not much inside: some sleeping mats, some baskets, and a few tools and bowls. The house is mostly used for sleeping. During the day, the family lives and works outside.

[pause]

{img:inside-boat-house-dim-reed-mats-family-sleeping-small-entrance-light}
In front of the house, there is a little paved area, made of round smooth beach stones. This is where the family sits and works in the daytime. Hina and her mother sit here to weave baskets and mats. Grandmother Uka sits here in the sun. The children play here. And in the evening, the family eats here, looking out over the ocean.

[long-pause]

Near the house, there is the earth oven, called an umu. It is a hole in the ground, lined with stones. To cook, the family lights a fire in the hole and heats the stones until they are very hot. Then they rake out the ashes. They put the food on the hot stones: sweet potatoes, taro, fish, chicken. They cover it with banana leaves, and then with earth. And they leave it to cook slowly, for an hour or two. When they open it, the food is soft and delicious, with a smoky taste.

[pause]

{img:earth-oven-umu-opening-steam-banana-leaves-sweet-potatoes-family-gathered}
There is also a little stone building near the house, for the chickens. It is called a hare moa, a chicken house. It is long and low, made of piled stones, with a small opening. At night, the chickens go inside, where they are safe and warm. Chickens are very important on Rapa Nui. They are the only farm animals on the island. They are given as gifts at feasts and weddings. And their eggs and meat are a special treat.

[long-pause]

Around the house, there are the family's gardens. They are protected by low stone walls, to keep off the wind, which can be strong and salty. Some gardens are planted in little round pits, lined with stones, called manavai. Inside the pits, the plants are sheltered and the soil stays moist. Vai grows bananas there, and sugar cane, and paper mulberry.

[pause]

{img:stone-walled-garden-pit-manavai-banana-plants-sheltered-wind-sunny}
In the evenings, the smoke of many earth ovens rises from the villages along the coast. The smell of cooking drifts on the wind. The chickens go into their stone houses. The children are called in from the shore. And the families crawl into their boat-shaped houses for the night, with the sound of the waves breaking on the rocks below. Above them, the moai on their platform stand quietly, watching over the sleeping village.

[pause]

Inside the house, near the entrance, there are a few little carved figures of wood. They are figures of the household spirits, which protect the family. Some of them are thin, with ribs showing, and big eyes. Others are figures of birds or lizards. Grandmother Uka speaks to them sometimes, softly, asking them to keep the family safe. The children are a little afraid of them, but they also like them.

[pause]

When it rains, the family stays inside the house, under the thick grass roof. The rain drums softly on the roof, but inside it is dry and warm. Grandmother Uka tells stories. Vai weaves. The children play string games. And they listen to the rain, and the wind, and the distant sound of the waves. When the rain stops, they crawl out, and the whole island smells fresh and green.

[long-pause]

{img:rapa-nui-dawn-village-smoke-earth-ovens-moai-platform-ocean-calm-1500}
Let us spend an ordinary day with the family, around the year 1500.

[pause]

The day begins at dawn, when the roosters crow in their stone houses. The family crawls out of their house into the cool morning air. Vai lights a small fire and warms some sweet potatoes from yesterday. The family eats them sitting on the paved stones, looking at the sea. The ocean is calm and silver in the morning light. Seabirds are flying low over the water, looking for fish.

[long-pause]

After breakfast, Tavake sets off to the quarry at Rano Raraku. It is a long walk across the island, perhaps two hours. He carries his stone tools in a basket on his back. Several other carvers from the village go with him. They walk along a path through the grassland, past gardens and other villages. They greet the people they meet. Everyone knows the carvers, and respects them.

[pause]

{img:carvers-walking-path-island-baskets-tools-volcano-ahead-morning-grass}
Mako goes with his father today. He is very excited. He walks fast to keep up with the men, carrying his own little basket with his own little stone tool. At the quarry, he is allowed to watch, and to help a little, as long as he is careful. He has been waiting for this day for weeks.

[long-pause]

Vai and Hina and Grandmother Uka stay at home. In the morning, they work in the gardens. They pull weeds and dig around the sweet potatoes. They pick ripe bananas and cut sugar cane. Hina likes to chew a piece of sugar cane while she works. It is sweet and juicy. Grandmother Uka sits in the shade and sorts seeds for planting.

[pause]

{img:women-girl-working-garden-sweet-potatoes-stone-walls-sunny-morning}
In the afternoon, Hina goes down to the shore with some other girls. They look for shellfish and sea urchins on the rocks, at low tide. They catch little fish in the rock pools with their hands. Then they swim in a sheltered bay, diving under the waves and laughing. Hina is the best swimmer of all the girls. She can hold her breath for a very long time, and dive down deep.

[long-pause]

The fishermen of the village are out at sea, in their small canoes. Wood is precious on the island, so the canoes are made of many small pieces, sewn together. The fishermen use hooks made of bone and stone, and nets made of plant fibres. They catch tuna and other fish, and sometimes a turtle. When they come back in the evening, the whole village comes down to the shore to share the catch.

[pause]

{img:fishermen-small-canoes-returning-shore-catch-villagers-waiting-evening}
In the evening, Tavake and Mako come home from the quarry, tired and dusty. Mako cannot stop talking. He tells his mother and sister and grandmother everything he saw. The giant stone faces in the rock. The carvers, tapping and tapping. The chips of stone flying. And the great moai that his father is working on, which is almost finished. Soon, he says, it will walk. Grandmother Uka smiles at him. Yes, she says. Soon it will walk. And the family eats supper together, as the sun goes down into the sea.

[pause]

Hina has a best friend called Rapa, who lives in the next house. The two girls do everything together. They work in the gardens side by side, and swim in the sea, and weave baskets in the shade. They tell each other secrets and make plans. Rapa wants to be a famous weaver. Hina wants to be a keeper of stories. They promise to help each other, always.

[pause]

In the late afternoon, Grandmother Uka walks slowly along the shore, looking for special stones and shells. She uses them to make necklaces and charms. She finds a little shell, white and pink and shining, and gives it to Hina. Keep it, she says. It is a gift from the sea. Hina puts it in a little woven bag that she wears around her neck. She will keep it all her life.

[pause]

When it gets dark, the family sits outside a little longer, watching the stars come out over the sea. The moon rises from the ocean, big and silver, and lays a shining path across the water. Grandmother Uka hums an old song. Mako leans against his father and yawns. And slowly, one by one, the family crawls into the house to sleep.

[long-pause]

{img:ahu-platform-row-moai-backs-to-sea-facing-village-inland-afternoon}
What are the moai? Why did the people of Rapa Nui carve them?

[pause]

The moai are giant figures of people, carved from stone. Most of them show only the head and the upper body, down to the hips. They have long faces, with deep-set eyes, long straight noses and thin, closed lips. Their ears are very long. Their arms hang down at their sides, with long thin fingers resting on their stomachs. They look calm and serious and powerful.

[long-pause]

The moai are not gods. The people of Rapa Nui believed they were the faces of their great ancestors. When an important chief or leader passed away, a moai might be carved in his memory. The spirit of the ancestor was believed to live in the moai. And the moai watched over the people of the clan, and protected them.

[pause]

{img:moai-giant-heads-close-sunlight-shadows-calm-faces-grass-blue-sky}
The people believed that their ancestors had a special power, called mana. Mana was a kind of spiritual strength. It brought good harvests, plenty of fish and good health. The moai held the mana of the ancestors, and shared it with the living. That is why most of the moai stand with their backs to the sea, looking inland, at the villages and the people. They are watching over them.

[long-pause]

The moai stand on great stone platforms, called ahu. These platforms are built along the coast, all around the island. Each clan had its own ahu, near its village. The ahu were sacred places, where ceremonies were held, and where the dead were laid to rest. Some ahu were small, with just one moai. Some were very big, with many moai standing in a row.

[pause]

{img:ahu-tongariki-fifteen-moai-row-long-platform-ocean-dramatic-sky}
The biggest ahu on the island is called Tongariki. It is about a hundred metres long. And on it stand fifteen moai, side by side, in a long row. It is an amazing sight. The moai are different sizes, some tall and some shorter. They stand with their backs to the sea, looking out over a wide valley toward the volcano where they were carved.

[long-pause]

The stone walls of some ahu are built with great skill. The stones are cut and fitted together so perfectly that you cannot slide a knife between them. This is very difficult to do, with only stone tools. It shows how skilled the builders of Rapa Nui were.

[pause]

{img:ahu-stone-wall-perfectly-fitted-blocks-close-detail-sunlight}
Altogether, the people of Rapa Nui made almost a thousand moai. Most of them were made between about seven hundred and four hundred years ago. The average moai is about four metres tall, and weighs about twelve tons. That is as heavy as two elephants. The biggest one ever stood up on a platform is almost ten metres tall, and weighs more than seventy tons. And there is an even bigger one, still lying in the quarry, unfinished. It is twenty-one metres long. If it had been finished, it would have been as tall as a seven-storey building.

[pause]

Grandmother Uka tells Hina the names of the moai on their village ahu. This one is the great chief who led the clan long ago, she says. This one is his son, who was a great fisherman. This one is his grandson, who built the platform. Hina listens and remembers. One day, she will tell the names to her own children. And so the ancestors will never be forgotten.

[pause]

Each moai is a little different from the others. Some have longer faces, some have wider noses. Some have carvings on their backs, of belts and patterns. Some are tall and thin, and some are short and broad. The carvers made each one with care, to honour a particular ancestor. When you look closely, you can see that every moai has its own character.

[pause]

In the evenings, the people of the village sometimes gather in front of the ahu. They sit on the grass and look up at the moai. They bring small offerings: a little food, some flowers, a cooked chicken. They chant and pray to the ancestors. They ask for good harvests, calm seas and healthy children. Then they go home, feeling that the ancestors are listening.

[long-pause]

{img:rano-raraku-quarry-slope-many-unfinished-moai-carved-into-rock-carvers-working}
Almost all the moai were carved in one place: the slope of an old volcano called Rano Raraku, on the east side of the island.

[pause]

Rano Raraku is made of a special kind of rock, called tuff. Tuff is made of volcanic ash, pressed together over thousands of years. It is soft enough to carve with stone tools, but strong enough to last for centuries. It is yellowish-brown, and full of little bits of darker stone. When it is wet with rain, it turns darker.

[long-pause]

The quarry is on the outside slope of the volcano, and also on the inside, around a little crater lake. Everywhere you look, there are moai, in every stage of being made. Some are just outlines on the rock face. Some are half carved, still attached to the mountain. Some are finished, standing upright in the slope below, buried up to their necks in earth. There are almost four hundred moai at Rano Raraku. It is like a great workshop that was left behind.

[pause]

{img:carvers-basalt-tools-chipping-tuff-rock-moai-outline-quarry-face-dust}
The carvers used tools made of a very hard stone, called basalt. They held them in their hands and struck the softer rock, chipping away little pieces. First they marked out the shape of the moai on the rock face. Usually it was lying on its back, face up. Then they cut a deep channel all around it. They carved the face and the front of the body first, carefully and slowly. They left the back attached to the rock, like a keel, until the very end.

[long-pause]

It took a team of carvers perhaps a year to carve a big moai. Many basalt tools were worn out and broken. Archaeologists have found thousands of them, scattered around the quarry. The carvers were skilled craftsmen, who had learned their art from their fathers and their teachers. They were respected and well looked after. The people of their clan brought them food: fish, chicken, sweet potatoes and bananas.

[pause]

{img:carvers-resting-eating-food-brought-by-villagers-shade-quarry-noon}
When the front of the moai was finished, the carvers cut away the keel underneath, very carefully. Then, with ropes and levers, they slid the moai slowly down the slope of the volcano. At the bottom, they stood it upright in a hole, so they could finish carving the back. Then it was ready to be moved to its platform. Some moai were never moved. They still stand at the foot of the volcano, buried up to their chins, looking out over the land.

[long-pause]

Mako watches his father carving. Tavake is working on the face of a moai, lying on its back in the rock. He is shaping the nose with his basalt tool, tap by tap. The chips fly, and the dust rises. Slowly, the long straight nose appears out of the stone. Mako sits very still, watching. He hardly breathes. It is like magic, he thinks. His father is finding a face hidden inside the mountain.

[pause]

{img:boy-watching-father-carve-moai-face-nose-quarry-dust-sunlight-wonder}
At the end of the day, Tavake lets Mako try. He gives him a small basalt tool and shows him a place on the rock where it does not matter. Mako taps it, carefully, the way his father showed him. A tiny chip of stone flies off. Mako laughs out loud with delight. The other carvers smile. One of them pats him on the back. Another carver, they say. Mako walks home that evening as proud as a king.

[pause]

At the quarry, the carvers work together in teams. There is a master carver, who decides the shape and the size of each moai. There are skilled carvers, who do the fine work on the face and the hands. And there are younger carvers, who do the rough work and learn from the others. Tavake is a skilled carver. One day, he hopes to be a master carver too.

[pause]

In the evenings, after work, some of the carvers stay at the quarry. They sleep in little shelters near the volcano, so they do not have to walk home every day. They cook food over fires and tell stories. They talk about their work, and about the moai they have carved. From the quarry, they can see the whole east coast of the island, and the sea beyond, glowing in the sunset.

[long-pause]

{img:moai-walking-upright-ropes-teams-pulling-sides-rocking-road-island-legend}
Once a moai was finished, it had to be moved from the quarry to its platform by the sea. Some platforms were many kilometres away. How did the people of Rapa Nui move these huge, heavy statues, without wheels, without horses, without machines?

[pause]

This is one of the greatest mysteries of the island. For a long time, nobody knew the answer. Some people thought the moai were rolled on logs, lying on their backs. Some thought they were dragged on wooden sledges. Some thought they were moved by magic, or even by visitors from other worlds. But the people of Rapa Nui had their own answer. They said the moai walked.

[long-pause]

The old stories of the island say that the moai walked from the quarry to their platforms. They say that people with great mana made them walk, with the power of their words. For a long time, visitors thought this was only a legend. But in our own time, scientists have found that it may be closer to the truth than anyone thought.

[pause]

{img:scientists-experiment-replica-moai-ropes-volunteers-rocking-walking-field-today}
A few years ago, a group of scientists made a copy of a moai, the same shape and almost the same weight. They stood it upright. Then they tied three long ropes to its head. Two teams of people pulled on ropes from the left and the right, and one team held a rope from behind. The teams on the sides pulled in turn, left and right, left and right. And the statue began to rock from side to side, and to swivel forward, step by step. It walked.

[long-pause]

With just eighteen people, they moved the statue a hundred metres in less than an hour. It looked just like a giant walking, waddling slowly along. The scientists noticed something else. The moai that were lost along the old roads, lying where they fell, had a special shape. Their bases were wider and rounded at the front, which made them easier to rock. And the old roads across the island were slightly curved, like a gentle trough, which helped to keep the moai steady.

[pause]

So perhaps the old stories were right. Perhaps the moai really did walk, with the help of many hands and strong ropes and careful rhythm. Not everyone agrees. Some scientists still think that some moai were moved lying down, on wooden sledges. Perhaps both ways were used. The mystery is not completely solved. But it is much less of a mystery than it was.

[long-pause]

Let us imagine the day when Tavake's moai walks from the quarry. It is early morning. The whole clan has come to help: men, women, and older children. There are ropes made of plant fibres, thick and strong, twisted together. The moai stands upright at the foot of the volcano, with ropes tied around its head. Everyone takes their place.

[pause]

{img:clan-gathering-ropes-moai-start-walk-morning-singing-chanting-quarry}
The leader starts a chant, a slow rhythm. The people on the left pull, and the moai tips and swings a little forward. The people on the right pull, and it tips and swings again. Left, right, left, right. The great statue rocks and swivels, slowly, step by step, along the road. The people sing as they pull. Children run alongside, cheering. Hina and Mako help to carry the water and the food for the pullers.

[long-pause]

The journey takes many days. Every evening, the moai is held steady with ropes and stones, and the people camp beside the road. They cook sweet potatoes and fish in earth ovens, and sing and tell stories around their fires. The moai stands beside them in the firelight, tall and silent, as if it were listening too. And every morning, the walk begins again.

[pause]

{img:night-camp-beside-standing-moai-road-fires-people-singing-stars}
At last, after many days, they reach the platform by the sea. Now comes the hardest part: raising the moai onto the platform. The people build a ramp of stones and earth. They pull the moai up the ramp, little by little, with levers and ropes. Then they tip it upright, very slowly, onto its place. When it finally stands upright on the platform, looking out over the village, everyone cheers and sings. There is a great feast that night. And the new ancestor takes its place among the others, watching over its people.

[pause]

The ropes were very important. They had to be very strong, to hold the weight of a moai. The people made them from the inner bark of a tree called hau, twisting the fibres together into long, thick ropes. Making the ropes took many days of work by many people. The ropes were carefully looked after, and used again and again for many moai.

[long-pause]

{img:moai-coral-eyes-white-black-pupils-restored-face-sunlight-close}
When a moai stood on its platform at last, there was one more thing to do. It had to be given its eyes.

[pause]

In the quarry, the moai were carved without eyes. They had only deep, empty sockets, under their heavy brows. But on the platform, the people placed eyes in the sockets. The eyes were made of white coral, from the sea, carefully shaped. In the middle of each eye, there was a round pupil, made of dark red stone or black volcanic glass. When the eyes were put in, the moai seemed to come alive.

[long-pause]

For the people of Rapa Nui, the eyes were very important. They believed that the eyes opened the mana of the ancestor. Without eyes, the moai was just a stone. With eyes, it could watch over the people, and share its power. The eyes were placed in a special ceremony, with chants and offerings.

[pause]

{img:ceremony-placing-coral-eyes-moai-priests-villagers-gathered-sunset}
For a long time, nobody knew that the moai had eyes. When visitors came to the island, the eyes had long been lost. Then, in 1978, archaeologists working at Anakena found a piece of white coral, buried in the sand near a fallen moai. It was shaped like an eye. When they put it into the eye socket of the moai, it fitted perfectly. Suddenly, everyone understood. Today, one moai on the island has been given back its eyes. It looks out over the sea, with a calm and watchful gaze.

[long-pause]

Some of the moai also wear hats. These are big cylinders of red stone, placed on top of their heads. The people of Rapa Nui call them pukao. They are made of a red volcanic stone, from a small crater called Puna Pau. The stone is soft and light, and a beautiful reddish colour. The pukao might represent the topknot of hair that the men of the island wore, tied up on top of their heads, sometimes dyed red.

[pause]

{img:puna-pau-red-stone-quarry-pukao-hats-cylinders-lying-grass-crater}
The pukao are very heavy, some of them weighing many tons. Putting them on top of a moai that is already standing was very difficult. Scientists think that the people rolled the pukao up a long ramp, built against the front of the moai. Then they tipped it carefully onto the head. There are still about a hundred pukao on the island, some on the moai, and some lying on the ground, at the quarry or along the roads.

[long-pause]

Grandmother Uka tells Hina and Mako about the ceremony of the eyes. She remembers it from when she was a girl, when a new moai was placed on their village platform. The priests chanted for a long time. The people stood in silence. And then the eyes were placed, and the moai looked out over the village for the first time. Everyone wept with joy, she says. It was as if the ancestor had come home.

[pause]

{img:moai-with-red-pukao-hat-ahu-sunset-golden-sky-calm-ocean-behind}
Hina asks if she can see the eyes of their own moai up close. Grandmother Uka shakes her head. The platform is sacred, she says. Only the priests can go up there. But you can look at them from here, she says. And the ancestors will look back at you. Hina looks up at the moai, standing on their platform in the evening light. Their white coral eyes seem to shine. And she feels that they are smiling at her, just a little.

[pause]

The coral for the eyes came from the reefs around the island. Divers went down to collect it, and the craftsmen shaped it carefully with stone tools. It was hard, slow work. The white coral was polished until it was smooth and shining. The dark pupils were fitted into a hollow in the middle. When the eyes were finished, they were kept in a safe place until the day of the ceremony.

[pause]

The moai with their eyes and their red hats must have been an amazing sight, long ago. Imagine a long row of giants on a platform by the sea, with white coral eyes shining and red stone topknots glowing in the sun. Imagine seeing them from a canoe, coming home from fishing in the evening. They would have looked almost alive, welcoming the fishermen home.

[long-pause]

{img:polynesian-children-playing-shore-rocks-waves-surfing-banana-trunks-sunny}
What is life like for children on Rapa Nui, long ago?

[pause]

Children help their families from a young age. They help in the gardens, pulling weeds and carrying water. They look after the chickens and collect the eggs. They gather shellfish on the rocks, and firewood and dry grass for the ovens. They learn to weave and to fish, to build and to cook, from their parents and grandparents.

[long-pause]

But there is plenty of time to play too. The children of Rapa Nui love the sea. They swim in the bays and dive from the rocks. They play in the waves. And they do something wonderful: they surf. They lie on bundles of reeds, or on the trunks of banana plants, and ride the waves to the shore. Surfing is an old Polynesian game, played on many islands. On Rapa Nui, it is called haka honu, which means to be like a turtle.

[pause]

{img:children-sliding-down-grassy-hill-banana-trunks-laughing-ocean-below}
There is another exciting game, played on the steep grassy slopes of the hills. The children sit on the trunks of banana plants and slide down the hill, very fast. It is called haka pei. It is a little dangerous, and very exciting. The older boys race each other down the steepest slopes. The younger children slide down the gentle ones. Everyone laughs and shrieks and tumbles at the bottom.

[long-pause]

The children also play quieter games. They play with string, making patterns between their fingers. These are called string figures, and the people of Rapa Nui call them kai kai. Each pattern has a name and a little chant or story. One pattern shows a turtle, another a bird, another two people fishing. Grandmother Uka knows dozens of them. She teaches them to Hina, one by one, with the chants that go with them.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-teaching-girl-string-figure-kai-kai-fingers-chanting-shade}
The string figures are not just games. They are a way of remembering. The chants tell old stories, about the ancestors, the gods, the animals and the stars. By learning the string figures, the children learn the history and the knowledge of their people. In this way, the old stories are passed down, from grandmother to grandchild, through the generations.

[long-pause]

The children also learn about the stars, like their ancestors the navigators. On clear nights, Grandmother Uka takes Hina and Mako up a little hill near the village. She shows them the bright stars and tells them their names. She shows them the stars that rise when it is time to plant the sweet potatoes, and the stars that tell when the birds will come back to nest. She tells them about the great canoes that came across the sea, following the stars.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-children-hilltop-night-pointing-stars-milky-way-ocean-dark}
Mako asks if he could find his way across the ocean by the stars, like the old navigators. Grandmother Uka laughs. Perhaps, she says, if you learn them all. It takes many years. Mako says he will learn them all, every single one. Then he lies back on the grass and looks up, trying to count them. He gets to thirty-two, and falls asleep. And Grandmother Uka and Hina sit beside him, looking up at the stars, in the warm night.

[pause]

The children of Rapa Nui also have little toys. They have small tops, made of stone or wood, which they spin on flat rocks. They have little canoes, carved from wood, which they float in the rock pools. They have dolls made of reeds and bark cloth. And they have little bows and arrows, for play, made of thin sticks and plant fibres.

[pause]

When the children are older, they learn the special skills of their families. Hina learns weaving and the old chants from her mother and grandmother. Mako learns about stone from his father, and fishing from his uncle. Each child learns a little of everything, and a lot of one thing. That way, all the knowledge of the island is kept alive, and passed on.

[long-pause]

{img:rapa-nui-ancient-palm-forest-tall-trunks-green-canopy-birds-before}
When the first people came to Rapa Nui, the island was covered with forests of tall palm trees. But when the first European visitors came, hundreds of years later, there were almost no trees at all. What happened to the trees? This is another of the great mysteries of the island.

[long-pause]

The palm trees of Rapa Nui were a giant kind of palm, now gone from the island. Scientists have found their old nuts in caves, and the marks of their roots in the soil. They also studied tiny grains of pollen, kept for thousands of years in the mud at the bottom of the crater lakes. Pollen is the fine dust that flowers make. By counting the pollen of different plants, layer by layer, scientists can see how the plants of the island changed over time.

[pause]

{img:scientist-taking-mud-core-crater-lake-reeds-boat-pollen-study-today}
The pollen shows that the forests slowly disappeared, over several hundred years, after people arrived. By the time the Dutch came, in 1722, the palm forests were almost all gone. There were still some trees and bushes, here and there. But the hills were mostly covered with grass and ferns.

[long-pause]

Why did the trees disappear? For a long time, people believed a simple and sad story. They said that the people of Rapa Nui cut down all the trees, to move their moai and build their canoes. And then, without trees, they could not make canoes, the soil washed away, and life became very hard. This story was told in many books, as a warning about looking after the Earth.

[pause]

{img:polynesian-rats-small-palm-nuts-gnawed-forest-floor-close-gentle}
But in recent years, scientists have found that the story is more complicated. The people did clear forest for their gardens, and they used wood. But there was another cause too: the rats. The little Polynesian rats that came with the first canoes had no enemies on the island. There were millions of them. And they loved to eat the nuts of the palm trees. Almost every old palm nut that scientists have found has little marks of rat teeth on it. So the rats ate the seeds, and new trees could not grow.

[long-pause]

Perhaps the weather changed a little too, with some years of drought. Perhaps it was a mix of all these things together. Nobody knows exactly. And the more scientists learn, the more they admire the people of Rapa Nui. When the trees disappeared, the people did not give up. They found clever new ways to live on their changing island.

[pause]

{img:grassy-hills-rapa-nui-no-trees-wind-stone-walls-gardens-sunny-later}
For Hina and her family, around 1500, the forests are already getting smaller. Grandmother Uka remembers when there were more trees near the village. She remembers walking in the shade of the palms as a girl, and hearing the birds sing in the branches. Now there are fewer trees, and more open grass. But she tells the children not to be sad. The island changes, she says, like everything. And the people change with it. We will find a way, as our ancestors always did.

[pause]

Some scientists also think the trees disappeared partly because of the weather. They think there may have been some long dry periods, with less rain than usual. In dry years, the young trees would have found it harder to grow. Together with the rats and the clearing of land, this may have made it impossible for the forests to recover. It was not one cause, but many, working together over a long time.

[pause]

The people of Rapa Nui also planted other trees, which were useful to them. There was the toromiro, a small tree with hard wood, used for carving. There was the hau, whose bark was used for ropes. There was the paper mulberry, used for cloth. These smaller trees and bushes were looked after carefully, in sheltered places. They were very precious.

[long-pause]

{img:lithic-mulch-garden-stones-scattered-sweet-potato-plants-growing-between-sunny}
As the forests disappeared, the people of Rapa Nui found clever ways to keep growing their food. One of the cleverest was a special kind of garden, made of stones.

[pause]

The island is windy, and without the trees, the wind blew strongly across the fields. It dried out the soil and blew it away. So the farmers began to cover their fields with stones. They broke volcanic rock into small pieces, and spread them over the soil, like a blanket. Then they planted their sweet potatoes and taro between the stones.

[long-pause]

These stone gardens are called rock mulch gardens. They work very well. The stones protect the soil from the wind and the sun. They keep the moisture in the ground, so the plants do not dry out. They warm up in the day and stay warm at night, which helps the plants to grow. And as the stones slowly break down, they add good minerals to the soil. The people of Rapa Nui covered huge areas of the island with these gardens.

[pause]

{img:farmers-spreading-broken-volcanic-stones-field-baskets-wind-grassland}
Vai has several stone gardens near the village. She and Hina carry stones in baskets from a nearby rocky place, and spread them on the soil. It is hard work. But the sweet potatoes grow well between the stones. In autumn, they dig up big, sweet, orange potatoes. Vai is proud of her gardens. She says that the stones are her friends.

[long-pause]

The people also made more of the little round stone pits, the manavai, where plants were sheltered from the wind. Some of them were quite deep, like little wells without water. Inside them, bananas and paper mulberry trees could grow, safe from the wind and the salty spray. From above, the island looked like it was covered with little stone circles.

[pause]

{img:lava-tube-cave-entrance-garden-inside-banana-plants-light-falling-in}
The island also has many caves, long tunnels in the volcanic rock, made by flowing lava long ago. Some of them have openings in the roof, where the light comes in. The people planted gardens in these openings too, sheltered from the wind. Bananas and other plants grew there, in the soft green light. People also used the caves for shelter and storage, and as cool places to rest on hot days.

[long-pause]

The people found new ways to fish, too. Without big trees for big canoes, they could no longer go far out to sea for tuna and dolphins. So they fished closer to the shore, from the rocks and from small canoes. They caught smaller fish, and eels and octopus. They collected more shellfish. And they kept more chickens, in their stone chicken houses.

[pause]

{img:fisherman-rock-shore-line-net-sunset-waves-small-canoe-nearby}
Life on the island changed, slowly, over many generations. But the people of Rapa Nui adapted to their changing island with great cleverness and patience. They kept growing food, and kept their families fed. They kept their stories and their songs. Grandmother Uka tells Hina that the most important thing is to look after the land, and the land will look after you. Hina remembers these words all her life.

[pause]

Today, scientists who study the stone gardens of Rapa Nui are amazed by them. They say the people of the island were very clever farmers. They found ways to grow food on a windy, rocky island, with poor soil and little water. Some modern farmers in other dry and windy places are now learning from these old gardens. The wisdom of Rapa Nui is still useful today.

[pause]

In the evenings, the farmers sometimes sit on the low stone walls of their gardens and look at their crops. The sweet potato leaves move gently in the wind. The banana plants rustle in their sheltered pits. The farmers talk about the rain and the harvest, about their children and their neighbours. And they feel proud of the gardens they have made from stones and wind and patience.

[long-pause]

{img:rano-kau-crater-lake-reeds-steep-rim-ocean-islets-motu-nui-view}
At the south-western corner of the island, there is a great volcano called Rano Kau. Its crater is enormous, more than a kilometre across. Inside, there is a lake, covered with floating islands of reeds. The rim of the crater is very steep, and on one side, it drops straight down into the sea.

[long-pause]

On the edge of the crater rim, high above the ocean, there is a special village. It is called Orongo. It is not a village where people live all the time. It is a sacred place, used for one great ceremony every year. The houses of Orongo are built of flat stones, piled up carefully, with a roof of stone and earth on top. They are low and dark inside, with tiny entrances, like little tunnels.

[pause]

{img:orongo-stone-houses-low-entrance-crater-rim-ocean-cliffs-wind}
From Orongo, you can see three small islands out in the sea, just off the coast. The biggest of them is called Motu Nui. Every spring, great flocks of seabirds come to nest on these little islands. Among them is a bird called the manutara, the sooty tern. It is a graceful black and white bird, with long thin wings. The people of Rapa Nui watch eagerly for its arrival each year.

[long-pause]

Around Orongo, the rocks are covered with carvings, called petroglyphs. Many of them show a strange figure: a man with the head of a bird, with a long curved beak, crouching. This is the birdman, called Tangata Manu. There are hundreds of these carvings, cut into the hard rock with stone tools. Some of them also show Makemake, the creator god, whose face looks like a large round mask.

[pause]

{img:petroglyphs-birdman-carvings-rock-orongo-curved-beak-figures-sunlight}
In later times, the birdman ceremony became the most important ceremony on the island. It was held every year in spring, when the sooty terns came back to nest. People from all the clans of the island came to Orongo. They lived in the stone houses for several weeks, waiting for the birds. And they watched a great contest, the race of the birdman.

[long-pause]

The birdman ceremony probably began a little later than the time of Hina's family, when the making of moai was coming to an end. It became more and more important. But we will imagine that Hina, when she is older, comes to see it. She climbs up the steep path to the crater rim with her family. The wind is strong and cold. Below them, the waves crash against the cliffs. Out at sea, they can see Motu Nui, white with birds.

[pause]

{img:family-climbing-steep-path-crater-rim-orongo-wind-ocean-view-spring}
Grandmother Uka is too old to climb the steep path. But she has told Hina all about it. She has told her about the god Makemake, who brought the birds to the island. She has told her about the race, and the egg, and the great honour of the winner. Hina stands on the edge of the crater, looking out at the little island in the sea. She feels the wind in her hair. And she waits, with everyone else, for the birds to begin to lay their eggs.

[pause]

The stone houses of Orongo are very carefully built. The flat stones are piled up in layers, getting closer and closer together toward the top, until they meet to make a roof. Then the roof is covered with earth and grass. Inside, it is dark and cool, and quiet. Some of the houses have paintings on the stones inside, of birds and boats and the god Makemake. They are some of the oldest paintings on the island.

[pause]

The climb up to Orongo is long and steep. The path winds up the side of the volcano, between grass and rocks. Near the top, the view is breathtaking. On one side, you can look down into the great crater, with its lake of reeds. On the other side, the cliffs drop straight down to the sea, far below. And out in the blue water, the little islands sit quietly, white with birds.

[long-pause]

{img:young-men-climbing-down-cliffs-orongo-reed-floats-sea-below-dawn}
The race of the birdman is the most exciting event of the year on Rapa Nui. It is a test of courage, strength and patience.

[pause]

Each chief who wants to take part chooses a young man to race for him. These young men are strong swimmers and brave climbers. When the time comes, they climb down the steep cliffs below Orongo, almost three hundred metres, to the sea. Then they swim out to the little island of Motu Nui, about one and a half kilometres away. They float on little bundles of reeds, to help them swim, and to carry their food.

[long-pause]

The sea between the coast and the island can be rough and cold. There are strong currents and big waves. Sometimes there are sharks. It is a dangerous swim. But the young men are very good swimmers. They have practised in the sea since they were children. They swim steadily, side by side, holding their reed floats. On the cliffs above, the people watch them, holding their breath.

[pause]

{img:swimmers-reed-bundles-crossing-rough-sea-to-islet-birds-circling}
When they reach Motu Nui, the young men climb up onto the rocks. They find little caves to shelter in. And then they wait. They are waiting for the sooty terns to lay their first eggs of the season. Sometimes they must wait for many days, even weeks. They live on the food they brought, and on rainwater. They watch the birds day and night.

[long-pause]

The first young man to find an egg of the sooty tern is the winner. When he finds it, he climbs to the top of the island and calls out across the sea to his chief, waiting at Orongo. Then he ties the egg in a little band around his forehead. He climbs down to the sea and swims back to the coast, very carefully, so the egg will not break. Then he climbs up the steep cliffs to Orongo, and gives the egg to his chief.

[pause]

{img:young-man-holding-sooty-tern-egg-high-islet-calling-across-sea-joy}
The chief who receives the egg becomes the birdman for that year. He shaves his head and his eyebrows, and paints his face red and black. He lives apart, in a special house, for a whole year. He is treated with great respect. His clan has special honour and special rights for that year. And the young man who won the race is a hero.

[long-pause]

Imagine Hina, standing on the cliff at Orongo, watching. She sees the young men coming back across the sea. One of them is ahead of the others. He is swimming steadily, with the little reed float in front of him. As he comes closer, she can see that he has something tied to his forehead. An egg. The people on the cliff begin to shout and cheer. The young man reaches the rocks below, and begins to climb.

[pause]

{img:crowd-cheering-cliff-top-orongo-swimmer-climbing-with-egg-headband}
He climbs up and up, slowly, carefully, holding the rocks with his hands and feet. The crowd watches in silence now. One wrong move, and he could fall, or break the egg. At last, he reaches the top. He kneels in front of his chief, and holds out the egg, unbroken. And the whole crowd shouts with joy. The chief lifts the egg up high, for everyone to see. The drums begin to beat. And the people sing and dance on the windy cliff top, high above the sea.

[pause]

The young men who race for the chiefs are called hopu. Being chosen as a hopu is a great honour. They train for months before the race. They swim in the sea every day, and climb the cliffs. They eat well, and rest well. Their families are very proud of them. And when the race is over, the winner is remembered and honoured for the rest of his life.

[pause]

That night, there is a great feast at Orongo. The people roast chickens and sweet potatoes in earth ovens on the crater rim. They sing songs of praise for the winner and his chief. The young man sits by the fire, tired and happy, with his family around him. Far below, the waves crash softly against the cliffs. And out at sea, on the little island, the sooty terns settle on their nests for the night.

[long-pause]

{img:rongorongo-wooden-tablet-carved-glyphs-rows-close-museum-light-mystery}
Rapa Nui has one more great mystery. It is a strange kind of writing, called rongorongo. And nobody in the world can read it.

[pause]

Rongorongo is carved on wooden tablets, in small signs called glyphs. The glyphs are little pictures: people and birds, fish and plants, stars and shapes. They are carved in long lines, very small and neat. There are hundreds of different glyphs. They are carved with a shark's tooth or a piece of volcanic glass, into smooth pieces of wood.

[long-pause]

The writing is read in a very unusual way. You start at the bottom left corner of the tablet, and read from left to right. Then, at the end of the line, you turn the tablet upside down, and read the next line, again from left to right. So every second line is upside down. This way of writing is called reverse boustrophedon. It is a long word that means turning like an ox when it ploughs a field.

[pause]

{img:wise-men-reading-tablet-chanting-firelight-house-entrance-night}
The old stories of the island say that only a few wise men, called rongorongo men, could read the tablets. They chanted them at special ceremonies. Every year, there was a great gathering, where they read the tablets aloud. If they made a mistake, they were gently corrected. The tablets were very precious and very sacred. They were kept wrapped in cloth, in special houses.

[long-pause]

Today, only about two dozen rongorongo tablets survive. They are kept in museums all over the world: in Chile, in Europe, in America. The last people who could read them passed away in the hard years of the nineteenth century, which we will hear about later. Since then, nobody has been able to read them. Scholars from many countries have tried, for more than a hundred and fifty years. They have studied every glyph, compared the tablets, looked for patterns. Some have made guesses. But nobody has truly cracked the code.

[pause]

{img:scholar-studying-rongorongo-glyphs-papers-magnifying-glass-desk-lamp}
One part has been understood. On one tablet, there is a series of glyphs that seems to be a calendar, a list of the nights of the moon. That is all. The rest is still a mystery. Perhaps the tablets contain prayers, or the names of ancestors, or old stories, or songs. We may never know.

[long-pause]

Some scholars think that rongorongo may be one of only a few writing systems in the history of the world that were invented completely independently. Most writing systems were learned from someone else. But perhaps the people of Rapa Nui invented their own, on their tiny island, in the middle of the ocean. If that is true, it was a remarkable achievement.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-girl-looking-at-carved-tablet-wrapped-cloth-sacred-house}
Grandmother Uka tells Hina about the tablets. She cannot read them herself. Only the rongorongo men can. But she has seen them, once, at a great gathering, when she was young. She remembers the sound of the chanting, slow and deep, as the wise men read the little signs. It sounded like the sea, she says. Like the voice of the ancestors. Hina wishes she could hear it too. And she wonders what the little signs say.

[pause]

The wooden tablets were very precious, because wood was rare on the island. Some of them were made from pieces of wood that had drifted to the island across the sea. One of them was even made from an old oar, from a European ship. The carvers worked very carefully, so as not to waste any space. Every glyph was small and neat, close to the next.

[pause]

Today, the people of Rapa Nui are proud of their rongorongo. Little copies of the glyphs are carved in wood and sold to visitors. Children learn about the tablets at school. Some artists use the glyphs in their paintings and carvings. And some young people of Rapa Nui are studying the tablets themselves, hoping that one day, perhaps, they will understand what their ancestors wrote.

[long-pause]

{img:dutch-ships-1722-three-sailing-ships-approaching-island-easter-morning}
On Easter Sunday, the fifth of April, 1722, three ships appeared on the horizon, off the coast of Rapa Nui. They were Dutch ships, from the Netherlands, far away on the other side of the world.

[pause]

The ships belonged to the Dutch West India Company. Their commander was a man called Jacob Roggeveen. He was sixty-two years old, from the town of Middelburg, in Zeeland. He had sailed from the Netherlands the summer before, across the Atlantic, around the tip of South America, and into the Pacific. He was searching for a great southern land that many people believed was out there, somewhere in the ocean.

[long-pause]

Because it was Easter Sunday, Roggeveen named the island Paasch Eyland. That means Easter Island in Dutch. And that is the name that most of the world still uses today. The people of the island, of course, already had their own name for it. But the Dutch did not know it.

[pause]

{img:islanders-watching-ships-from-shore-moai-behind-curious-1722-morning}
On the island, the people saw the strange ships coming. They had never seen anything like them. The ships were huge, with tall masts and great white sails. Some of the islanders paddled out in small canoes to look at them. One man even climbed on board one of the ships. He was very curious about everything. He looked at the sails and the ropes, the wooden decks and the strange pale men. The sailors gave him some little presents.

[long-pause]

A few days later, the Dutch went ashore. They saw the people and their houses and gardens. They saw the great stone moai, standing on their platforms. They wrote about them in their logbooks. They were amazed. How could these people have raised such huge statues, without strong ropes or big wood? They saw the people praying in front of the statues, and lighting fires before them.

[pause]

{img:dutch-sailors-1722-standing-before-moai-amazed-islanders-gathered}
Sadly, the visit did not end well. There was a misunderstanding on the beach, and some islanders lost their lives. It is a sad moment, which we remember gently. The Dutch stayed only one day on the island. Then they sailed away, west across the Pacific, and never came back. Roggeveen never found the great southern land he was looking for. But he is remembered as the first European to visit Rapa Nui.

[long-pause]

The Dutch wrote down what they saw. They wrote that the people were friendly and cheerful. They wrote that the people grew bananas, sweet potatoes and sugar cane, and kept chickens. They wrote that some people had long earlobes, stretched with ornaments. They wrote that the statues were standing upright on their platforms, many of them. These notes are very important today. They tell us what the island was like almost three hundred years ago.

[pause]

{img:jacob-roggeveen-ship-cabin-writing-logbook-quill-candle-1722}
For the Netherlands, this is a special part of the story. A Dutch sailor gave the island the name that the whole world knows. In Middelburg, his hometown in Zeeland, people still remember him. And every Easter Sunday, a few people in the Netherlands remember that, on that day in 1722, Dutch sailors first saw the giant stone faces of the island in the middle of the ocean.

[pause]

The Dutch sailors also noticed the long ears of some of the islanders. The people stretched their earlobes by wearing big round ornaments in them, made of wood or bone. Some earlobes were so long that they touched the shoulders. The sailors thought this was very strange. But for the people of Rapa Nui, long ears were a sign of beauty and importance. And many of the moai have long ears too.

[pause]

On the Dutch ships, there was also a young German soldier called Carl Friedrich Behrens. He later wrote a book about the voyage. He described the people of the island as friendly and lively. He wrote about their colourful cloth and their feathered headdresses. He wrote that some people painted their bodies in many colours. His book was read all over Europe. And it made people very curious about the far-away island.

[long-pause]

{img:captain-cook-ships-1774-anchored-off-island-moai-coast-calm-sea}
After the Dutch, more ships came to Rapa Nui. Each visitor wrote down what they saw. And slowly, a picture of the island's changing story appeared.

[pause]

In 1770, two Spanish ships came. They planted crosses on the hills, and claimed the island for the king of Spain. They wrote that the people were friendly. They drew pictures of the moai. Then they sailed away, and never came back.

[long-pause]

In 1774, the famous English explorer James Cook came, on his second great voyage around the world. His men explored the island for a few days. They wrote that many of the moai were no longer standing. Some had fallen on their faces, beside their platforms. The people did not seem to be making new ones anymore. Something had changed on the island.

[pause]

{img:fallen-moai-face-down-beside-ahu-grass-ocean-quiet-afternoon-later}
In 1786, a French explorer called La Pérouse came. He was kind to the people. He gave them seeds and animals: goats and sheep and pigs. He wrote that the people seemed healthy and cheerful. He wrote about their gardens and their houses. He drew a famous picture of the people standing among the moai.

[long-pause]

Over the following years, more and more of the moai fell. By the middle of the nineteenth century, almost none were still standing. Why did they fall? Again, nobody knows for sure. The old stories of the island speak of quarrels between the clans. Perhaps the moai were pulled down during these quarrels. Perhaps earthquakes and storms knocked some of them over. Perhaps, when the old beliefs changed, people no longer looked after them.

[pause]

{img:islanders-gathered-new-beliefs-birdman-ceremony-moai-fallen-background}
The beliefs of the people had changed. The making of moai had come to an end. The ceremony of the birdman, at Orongo, had become more important. Perhaps the people felt that the old ancestors no longer protected them as they once had. Perhaps they turned to the god Makemake and the birdman instead. It is one of the quiet mysteries of Rapa Nui.

[long-pause]

But even with the moai fallen, the people of Rapa Nui went on with their lives. They grew their sweet potatoes in their stone gardens. They fished and kept chickens. They held their birdman ceremonies and their festivals. They told their stories and sang their songs. And they remembered their ancestors, even if the great stone faces now lay in the grass.

[pause]

{img:rapa-nui-family-1780s-garden-stone-house-children-fallen-moai-distance}
If we imagine Hina's great-great-grandchildren, living in the time of Cook and La Pérouse, they would still be living much as she did. They would still crawl into boat-shaped houses at night, and cook in earth ovens. They would still surf the waves on banana trunks, and make string figures with chants. They would still look up at the same stars. And perhaps, sometimes, they would walk past a fallen moai, and put a hand on its stone face, and remember.

[pause]

The visitors also traded with the people of the island. They gave them cloth and metal tools, beads and mirrors. The islanders gave them sweet potatoes and chickens, bananas and fresh water. The islanders were very interested in the strange goods of the visitors. They especially liked hats and cloth, and anything red.

[pause]

Captain Cook's artist, William Hodges, made a famous painting of the moai on the island. It shows several statues standing on a platform by the sea, with a dramatic sky behind them. It is one of the first pictures of the moai that people in Europe ever saw. People were amazed by it. And they began to wonder, for the first time, about the mystery of the giant stone faces.

[pause]

La Pérouse also gave the people of Rapa Nui seeds of new plants: cabbages, carrots, pumpkins and orange trees. He hoped they would grow well on the island. He wrote in his journal that he wished the people a happy future. Sadly, he and his ships were lost at sea a few years later, far away in the Pacific. But his journal had been sent home, and his kind words about Rapa Nui survived.

[long-pause]

{img:rapa-nui-coast-grey-sky-quiet-sad-shore-empty-canoes-1860s-gentle}
In the nineteenth century, very hard times came to Rapa Nui. It is the saddest part of the island's story. We will tell it gently, and briefly.

[pause]

In 1862, ships came from Peru, in South America. They took many people from the island away, to work in Peru, against their will. Among them were the chiefs and many of the wise men, including the rongorongo men who could read the tablets. Very few of them ever came back. Those who did come back brought illness with them, which spread across the island. Many more people passed away.

[long-pause]

By the 1870s, only about a hundred and eleven people were left on Rapa Nui. Once there had been thousands. It was a very dark time. Much of the old knowledge was lost: the reading of the rongorongo tablets, many of the old chants, many of the old stories. We remember all the people who suffered in those years, with sorrow and respect.

[pause]

{img:small-group-rapa-nui-people-1870s-together-shore-hope-sunset}
But the people of Rapa Nui did not disappear. The few who were left held on to what they could. They kept their language and some of their stories. They kept their families together. They looked after each other. Slowly, very slowly, over many years, their numbers began to grow again.

[long-pause]

In 1888, the island became part of Chile, the country in South America. For a long time after that, most of the island was rented to a company that kept sheep. The people of Rapa Nui had to live in one small village, called Hanga Roa, and were not free to move around their own island. Those were difficult years too. But the people kept their hope, and their pride.

[pause]

{img:hanga-roa-village-early-1900s-simple-houses-church-people-gathered}
In 1966, things began to change. The people of Rapa Nui became full citizens of Chile. They could move freely around their island again. They could vote and travel. An airport was built, and visitors began to come. Schools taught the Rapa Nui language again. And the people began to bring back their old traditions, their songs and dances, their carving and their festivals.

[long-pause]

Today, there are several thousand Rapa Nui people living on the island, and many more in Chile and other countries. They are proud of their ancestors and their culture. They speak their language, sing their songs and dance their dances. They carve wood and stone, like their ancestors did. And they look after the moai and the ahu, the sacred places of their island. The old knowledge was not all lost. Much of it survived, in the hearts and memories of the people.

[pause]

{img:rapa-nui-elders-teaching-children-songs-language-today-circle-outdoors}
Many Rapa Nui elders have spent their lives remembering and recording the old stories, chants and string figures. They have taught them to their children and grandchildren. They have worked with scholars to write them down. And so, like Grandmother Uka long ago, they have made sure that the ancestors will not be forgotten.

[pause]

One of the people who helped the island in those hard years was a woman from Rapa Nui called Angata. She was old, wise and brave, and in 1914 she led her people in standing up for their island. She spoke up for their rights and their land. Many people of Rapa Nui still remember her with great respect. She showed that, even in the hardest times, the people of the island had courage and hope.

[long-pause]

{img:explorers-1950s-excavating-moai-raising-ropes-wooden-poles-islanders-help-later}
Over the past hundred years, many scientists and explorers have come to Rapa Nui to study its mysteries.

[pause]

In 1914, an English archaeologist called Katherine Routledge came to the island, with her husband, on their own sailing ship. She stayed for more than a year. She studied the moai and the ahu, and the quarry at Rano Raraku. She talked with the old people of the island and wrote down their memories. Her work was very important, because she recorded many things that might otherwise have been forgotten.

[long-pause]

In 1955, a famous Norwegian explorer called Thor Heyerdahl came. Some years before, he had sailed across the Pacific on a raft of logs, called the Kon-Tiki. He had an idea that the people of Rapa Nui came from South America, not from Polynesia. On Rapa Nui, he did experiments with the islanders. They showed that a moai could be raised up onto its platform, using levers and stones, by a small group of people. His books made the island famous all over the world.

[pause]

{img:kon-tiki-style-balsa-raft-ocean-sail-explorer-1947-later}
Today, we know that Heyerdahl's idea about South America was mostly not right. Scientists have studied the language, the plants and the DNA of the people of Rapa Nui. They all show that the people came from Polynesia, sailing east, just as the old stories say. But scientists have also found that, a long time ago, there may have been some contact between Polynesians and South Americans. The sweet potato, after all, came from South America. So perhaps Polynesian sailors reached South America, and brought it back.

[long-pause]

In the 1990s, a team of Japanese and Chilean experts, working with the people of Rapa Nui, raised the fifteen moai of Ahu Tongariki again. They had been knocked down long ago, and later scattered by a great tsunami, a huge wave from the sea, in 1960. Using a big crane, the team lifted each moai carefully back onto the platform. It took several years. Today, the fifteen moai stand again, in a long row, as they did long ago.

[pause]

{img:crane-lifting-moai-back-onto-ahu-tongariki-1990s-workers-careful-later}
Modern scientists use new tools to study the island. They study the soil and the pollen, the bones and the charcoal. They map the old roads and the gardens with satellites and drones. They make computer models of how the moai were moved. And they work together with the people of Rapa Nui, who share their knowledge and their stories. Little by little, the mysteries become a little less mysterious.

[long-pause]

But some mysteries remain. We still cannot read the rongorongo tablets. We still do not know exactly why the trees disappeared, or why the moai fell. We do not know the names of most of the people who carved the moai, or what they thought and felt. And perhaps that is as it should be. Some things belong to the ancestors, and the island keeps them safe.

[pause]

{img:researcher-and-rapa-nui-elder-walking-together-quarry-moai-talking-today}
Scientists and visitors who come to Rapa Nui often say the same thing. The more you learn about the island, the more you admire its people. They found a tiny island in the biggest ocean in the world. They made a home there, for hundreds of years. They carved giant statues and made them walk. They invented clever gardens and perhaps a writing of their own. And they survived very hard times, and kept their culture alive. That, perhaps, is the greatest wonder of Rapa Nui.

[pause]

Some of the most important researchers today are people of Rapa Nui themselves. They study their own history and culture, as archaeologists and historians, teachers and artists. They know the old stories and the language. They work to protect the moai and the sacred places. And they make sure that the story of Rapa Nui is told by its own people, in their own words.

[pause]

There is also a large museum on the island, in Hanga Roa, about the history of Rapa Nui. Inside, there are old tools and carvings, photographs and maps. There is the white coral eye that was found at Anakena. There are copies of rongorongo tablets. And there is information about the people and their culture, then and now. Visitors and islanders come to learn about the long story of the island.

[long-pause]

{img:rapa-nui-today-hanga-roa-harbour-colourful-boats-church-hills-today}
Today, Rapa Nui is a part of Chile, and a special place, protected by the whole world. Most of the island is a national park, and a World Heritage Site.

[pause]

About eight thousand people live on the island now, most of them in the little town of Hanga Roa, on the west coast. There are little houses with gardens full of flowers. There is a small harbour, with colourful fishing boats. There is a church, with beautiful wooden carvings that mix Christian and Rapa Nui traditions. There are little shops and cafés, schools and a hospital. And there is an airport, where planes come from Chile and Tahiti.

[long-pause]

Visitors come from all over the world to see the moai. They stand in front of the fifteen giants of Ahu Tongariki at sunrise, as the sky turns pink and gold behind them. They walk along the slopes of Rano Raraku, among the great heads buried in the grass. They visit the moai with the coral eyes, at Ahu Ko Te Riku. They climb up to Orongo, and look at the birdman carvings and the little islands in the sea.

[pause]

{img:visitors-sunrise-ahu-tongariki-fifteen-moai-silhouettes-pink-sky-today}
The people of Rapa Nui ask visitors to treat the moai and the sacred places with respect. You must not touch the moai, or climb on the ahu. You must stay on the paths. The moai are not just statues. For the people of Rapa Nui, they are still the faces of their ancestors. And the ancestors are still watching over the island.

[long-pause]

Every year, in February, there is a great festival on Rapa Nui, called Tapati. It lasts for two weeks. There are contests of all the old skills. There are canoe races and swimming races. There are contests of carving and weaving, of singing and dancing. There is a race where young men carry heavy bunches of bananas around a lake. And there is haka pei, the old game of sliding down a steep grassy hill on banana trunks, very fast.

[pause]

{img:tapati-festival-dancers-traditional-dress-feathers-body-paint-today}
During Tapati, the people paint their bodies with patterns, like their ancestors did. They wear costumes of feathers and leaves and bark cloth. They sing the old songs and dance the old dances. Families and clans compete with each other, and cheer each other on. It is a joyful time, full of music and laughter. And it shows that the culture of Rapa Nui is alive and strong.

[long-pause]

The Rapa Nui language is spoken again, by young and old. Children learn it at school. There are songs and books and radio programmes in Rapa Nui. Carvers still make little moai and birdman figures of wood and stone, as their ancestors did. Women still make string figures and teach the chants. And the old stories are told again, to children who listen with wide eyes, just as Hina and Mako did, long ago.

[pause]

{img:anakena-beach-today-moai-on-ahu-palm-trees-white-sand-families-swimming}
On the beach at Anakena, where the first canoes landed long ago, families come to swim and picnic on the weekends. The sand is soft and white, and the water is calm and blue. Palm trees, planted again, give shade on the beach. And on a platform above the sand, a row of moai stands, with red stone pukao on their heads. They look down over the beach, at the children playing in the waves. As if they are watching over them still.

[pause]

The people of Rapa Nui also have a special connection to the sea, still today. Many of them are fishermen, going out in small boats in the early morning. Many are swimmers and surfers, like their ancestors. Every evening, families come down to the little harbour at Hanga Roa to watch the sunset over the ocean. Children jump into the water from the harbour wall, laughing and splashing, as children have always done.

[pause]

Some moai from Rapa Nui were taken away long ago, and are now in museums in other countries. One famous moai, called Hoa Hakananai'a, is in the British Museum in London. The people of Rapa Nui would like their ancestors to come home. They have asked for them to be returned. In recent years, a few objects have been sent back to the island. And the people hope that more will follow.

[long-pause]

{img:rapa-nui-evening-1500-sunset-ocean-village-moai-platform-golden-light}
So we leave Rapa Nui tonight, around the year 1500, as the evening falls.

[pause]

The sun is setting over the ocean, to the west. The sky turns gold, then pink, then a deep soft blue. The waves roll gently onto the rocks below the village. Seabirds fly home to their nests on the cliffs. And on the stone platform by the sea, the moai stand in a row, with the last light of the sun on their backs. Their white coral eyes look out over the village, calm and watchful.

[long-pause]

In the village, the earth ovens have been opened, and the families are eating their supper. The smell of sweet potatoes and fish drifts on the evening breeze. Children are being called in from the shore. The chickens are going into their little stone houses for the night. And smoke rises gently from the fires, into the darkening sky.

[pause]

{img:family-outside-boat-house-evening-fire-eating-grandmother-stories-stars}
Outside their boat-shaped house, Hina's family is sitting together on the paved stones. Tavake is tired from the quarry, with dust still in his hair. He is telling them that the new moai is almost finished. Soon, he says, it will walk. Vai is weaving a basket by the light of the fire. Mako is trying to make a string figure, and getting his fingers tangled. Hina helps him, patiently.

[long-pause]

Grandmother Uka is sitting with her back against a warm stone, looking up at the sky. The first stars are coming out over the ocean. She begins to chant softly, an old chant about the canoes that came across the sea, long ago, following the stars. Her voice is low and slow, like the waves. Hina leans against her and listens. Mako stops tangling his string, and listens too.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-chanting-children-leaning-against-her-night-sky-ocean-moai}
The chant tells of the king Hotu Matu'a and his great canoes. It tells of the dream of the island with three volcanoes. It tells of the white beach of Anakena, and the first sweet potatoes planted in the new land. It tells of the navigators who read the stars and the waves. Hina knows every word.
