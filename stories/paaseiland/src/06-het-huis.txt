The family lives in a special kind of house, found only on Rapa Nui. It is called a hare paenga, and it is shaped like an upside-down canoe.

The house is long and narrow, with rounded ends, like a boat. Its foundation is made of carefully cut stones, laid in a long oval on the ground. Each stone has little holes drilled in its top. Into these holes, long bent sticks are pushed, to make the frame of the roof. The frame is covered with thick layers of grass, reeds and leaves, tied on in neat rows. The roof comes all the way down to the ground, so the house has no walls.

--

The entrance is a small low opening in the middle of one side. You have to crawl in on your hands and knees. That keeps the house warm at night, and keeps out the wind and the rain. Inside, it is dim and cosy. The floor is covered with mats of woven reeds. There is not much inside: some sleeping mats, some baskets, and a few tools and bowls. The house is mostly used for sleeping. During the day, the family lives and works outside.

{img:inside-boat-house-dim-reed-mats-family-sleeping-small-entrance-light}
In front of the house, there is a little paved area, made of round smooth beach stones. This is where the family sits and works in the daytime. Hina and her mother sit here to weave baskets and mats. Grandmother Uka sits here in the sun. The children play here. And in the evening, the family eats here, looking out over the ocean.

--

Near the house, there is the earth oven, called an umu. It is a hole in the ground, lined with stones. To cook, the family lights a fire in the hole and heats the stones until they are very hot. Then they rake out the ashes. They put the food on the hot stones: sweet potatoes, taro, fish, chicken. They cover it with banana leaves, and then with earth. And they leave it to cook slowly, for an hour or two. When they open it, the food is soft and delicious, with a smoky taste.

{img:earth-oven-umu-opening-steam-banana-leaves-sweet-potatoes-family-gathered}
There is also a little stone building near the house, for the chickens. It is called a hare moa, a chicken house. It is long and low, made of piled stones, with a small opening. At night, the chickens go inside, where they are safe and warm. Chickens are very important on Rapa Nui. They are the only farm animals on the island. They are given as gifts at feasts and weddings. And their eggs and meat are a special treat.

--

Around the house, there are the family's gardens. They are protected by low stone walls, to keep off the wind, which can be strong and salty. Some gardens are planted in little round pits, lined with stones, called manavai. Inside the pits, the plants are sheltered and the soil stays moist. Vai grows bananas there, and sugar cane, and paper mulberry.

{img:stone-walled-garden-pit-manavai-banana-plants-sheltered-wind-sunny}
In the evenings, the smoke of many earth ovens rises from the villages along the coast. The smell of cooking drifts on the wind. The chickens go into their stone houses. The children are called in from the shore. And the families crawl into their boat-shaped houses for the night, with the sound of the waves breaking on the rocks below. Above them, the moai on their platform stand quietly, watching over the sleeping village.

Inside the house, near the entrance, there are a few little carved figures of wood. They are figures of the household spirits, which protect the family. Some of them are thin, with ribs showing, and big eyes. Others are figures of birds or lizards. Grandmother Uka speaks to them sometimes, softly, asking them to keep the family safe. The children are a little afraid of them, but they also like them.

When it rains, the family stays inside the house, under the thick grass roof. The rain drums softly on the roof, but inside it is dry and warm. Grandmother Uka tells stories. Vai weaves. The children play string games. And they listen to the rain, and the wind, and the distant sound of the waves. When the rain stops, they crawl out, and the whole island smells fresh and green.
