Over the past hundred years, many scientists and explorers have come to Rapa Nui to study its mysteries.

In 1914, an English archaeologist called Katherine Routledge came to the island, with her husband, on their own sailing ship. She stayed for more than a year. She studied the moai and the ahu, and the quarry at Rano Raraku. She talked with the old people of the island and wrote down their memories. Her work was very important, because she recorded many things that might otherwise have been forgotten.

--

In 1955, a famous Norwegian explorer called Thor Heyerdahl came. Some years before, he had sailed across the Pacific on a raft of logs, called the Kon-Tiki. He had an idea that the people of Rapa Nui came from South America, not from Polynesia. On Rapa Nui, he did experiments with the islanders. They showed that a moai could be raised up onto its platform, using levers and stones, by a small group of people. His books made the island famous all over the world.

{img:kon-tiki-style-balsa-raft-ocean-sail-explorer-1947-later}
Today, we know that Heyerdahl's idea about South America was mostly not right. Scientists have studied the language, the plants and the DNA of the people of Rapa Nui. They all show that the people came from Polynesia, sailing east, just as the old stories say. But scientists have also found that, a long time ago, there may have been some contact between Polynesians and South Americans. The sweet potato, after all, came from South America. So perhaps Polynesian sailors reached South America, and brought it back.

--

In the 1990s, a team of Japanese and Chilean experts, working with the people of Rapa Nui, raised the fifteen moai of Ahu Tongariki again. They had been knocked down long ago, and later scattered by a great tsunami, a huge wave from the sea, in 1960. Using a big crane, the team lifted each moai carefully back onto the platform. It took several years. Today, the fifteen moai stand again, in a long row, as they did long ago.

{img:crane-lifting-moai-back-onto-ahu-tongariki-1990s-workers-careful-later}
Modern scientists use new tools to study the island. They study the soil and the pollen, the bones and the charcoal. They map the old roads and the gardens with satellites and drones. They make computer models of how the moai were moved. And they work together with the people of Rapa Nui, who share their knowledge and their stories. Little by little, the mysteries become a little less mysterious.

--

But some mysteries remain. We still cannot read the rongorongo tablets. We still do not know exactly why the trees disappeared, or why the moai fell. We do not know the names of most of the people who carved the moai, or what they thought and felt. And perhaps that is as it should be. Some things belong to the ancestors, and the island keeps them safe.

{img:researcher-and-rapa-nui-elder-walking-together-quarry-moai-talking-today}
Scientists and visitors who come to Rapa Nui often say the same thing. The more you learn about the island, the more you admire its people. They found a tiny island in the biggest ocean in the world. They made a home there, for hundreds of years. They carved giant statues and made them walk. They invented clever gardens and perhaps a writing of their own. And they survived very hard times, and kept their culture alive. That, perhaps, is the greatest wonder of Rapa Nui.

Some of the most important researchers today are people of Rapa Nui themselves. They study their own history and culture, as archaeologists and historians, teachers and artists. They know the old stories and the language. They work to protect the moai and the sacred places. And they make sure that the story of Rapa Nui is told by its own people, in their own words.

There is also a large museum on the island, in Hanga Roa, about the history of Rapa Nui. Inside, there are old tools and carvings, photographs and maps. There is the white coral eye that was found at Anakena. There are copies of rongorongo tablets. And there is information about the people and their culture, then and now. Visitors and islanders come to learn about the long story of the island.
