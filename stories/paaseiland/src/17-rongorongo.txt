Rapa Nui has one more great mystery. It is a strange kind of writing, called rongorongo. And nobody in the world can read it.

Rongorongo is carved on wooden tablets, in small signs called glyphs. The glyphs are little pictures: people and birds, fish and plants, stars and shapes. They are carved in long lines, very small and neat. There are hundreds of different glyphs. They are carved with a shark's tooth or a piece of volcanic glass, into smooth pieces of wood.

--

The writing is read in a very unusual way. You start at the bottom left corner of the tablet, and read from left to right. Then, at the end of the line, you turn the tablet upside down, and read the next line, again from left to right. So every second line is upside down. This way of writing is called reverse boustrophedon. It is a long word that means turning like an ox when it ploughs a field.

{img:wise-men-reading-tablet-chanting-firelight-house-entrance-night}
The old stories of the island say that only a few wise men, called rongorongo men, could read the tablets. They chanted them at special ceremonies. Every year, there was a great gathering, where they read the tablets aloud. If they made a mistake, they were gently corrected. The tablets were very precious and very sacred. They were kept wrapped in cloth, in special houses.

--

Today, only about two dozen rongorongo tablets survive. They are kept in museums all over the world: in Chile, in Europe, in America. The last people who could read them passed away in the hard years of the nineteenth century, which we will hear about later. Since then, nobody has been able to read them. Scholars from many countries have tried, for more than a hundred and fifty years. They have studied every glyph, compared the tablets, looked for patterns. Some have made guesses. But nobody has truly cracked the code.

{img:scholar-studying-rongorongo-glyphs-papers-magnifying-glass-desk-lamp}
One part has been understood. On one tablet, there is a series of glyphs that seems to be a calendar, a list of the nights of the moon. That is all. The rest is still a mystery. Perhaps the tablets contain prayers, or the names of ancestors, or old stories, or songs. We may never know.

--

Some scholars think that rongorongo may be one of only a few writing systems in the history of the world that were invented completely independently. Most writing systems were learned from someone else. But perhaps the people of Rapa Nui invented their own, on their tiny island, in the middle of the ocean. If that is true, it was a remarkable achievement.

{img:grandmother-girl-looking-at-carved-tablet-wrapped-cloth-sacred-house}
Grandmother Uka tells Hina about the tablets. She cannot read them herself. Only the rongorongo men can. But she has seen them, once, at a great gathering, when she was young. She remembers the sound of the chanting, slow and deep, as the wise men read the little signs. It sounded like the sea, she says. Like the voice of the ancestors. Hina wishes she could hear it too. And she wonders what the little signs say.

The wooden tablets were very precious, because wood was rare on the island. Some of them were made from pieces of wood that had drifted to the island across the sea. One of them was even made from an old oar, from a European ship. The carvers worked very carefully, so as not to waste any space. Every glyph was small and neat, close to the next.

Today, the people of Rapa Nui are proud of their rongorongo. Little copies of the glyphs are carved in wood and sold to visitors. Children learn about the tablets at school. Some artists use the glyphs in their paintings and carvings. And some young people of Rapa Nui are studying the tablets themselves, hoping that one day, perhaps, they will understand what their ancestors wrote.
