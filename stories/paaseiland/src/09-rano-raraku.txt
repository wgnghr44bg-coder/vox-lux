Almost all the moai were carved in one place: the slope of an old volcano called Rano Raraku, on the east side of the island.

Rano Raraku is made of a special kind of rock, called tuff. Tuff is made of volcanic ash, pressed together over thousands of years. It is soft enough to carve with stone tools, but strong enough to last for centuries. It is yellowish-brown, and full of little bits of darker stone. When it is wet with rain, it turns darker.

--

The quarry is on the outside slope of the volcano, and also on the inside, around a little crater lake. Everywhere you look, there are moai, in every stage of being made. Some are just outlines on the rock face. Some are half carved, still attached to the mountain. Some are finished, standing upright in the slope below, buried up to their necks in earth. There are almost four hundred moai at Rano Raraku. It is like a great workshop that was left behind.

{img:carvers-basalt-tools-chipping-tuff-rock-moai-outline-quarry-face-dust}
The carvers used tools made of a very hard stone, called basalt. They held them in their hands and struck the softer rock, chipping away little pieces. First they marked out the shape of the moai on the rock face. Usually it was lying on its back, face up. Then they cut a deep channel all around it. They carved the face and the front of the body first, carefully and slowly. They left the back attached to the rock, like a keel, until the very end.

--

It took a team of carvers perhaps a year to carve a big moai. Many basalt tools were worn out and broken. Archaeologists have found thousands of them, scattered around the quarry. The carvers were skilled craftsmen, who had learned their art from their fathers and their teachers. They were respected and well looked after. The people of their clan brought them food: fish, chicken, sweet potatoes and bananas.

{img:carvers-resting-eating-food-brought-by-villagers-shade-quarry-noon}
When the front of the moai was finished, the carvers cut away the keel underneath, very carefully. Then, with ropes and levers, they slid the moai slowly down the slope of the volcano. At the bottom, they stood it upright in a hole, so they could finish carving the back. Then it was ready to be moved to its platform. Some moai were never moved. They still stand at the foot of the volcano, buried up to their chins, looking out over the land.

--

Mako watches his father carving. Tavake is working on the face of a moai, lying on its back in the rock. He is shaping the nose with his basalt tool, tap by tap. The chips fly, and the dust rises. Slowly, the long straight nose appears out of the stone. Mako sits very still, watching. He hardly breathes. It is like magic, he thinks. His father is finding a face hidden inside the mountain.

{img:boy-watching-father-carve-moai-face-nose-quarry-dust-sunlight-wonder}
At the end of the day, Tavake lets Mako try. He gives him a small basalt tool and shows him a place on the rock where it does not matter. Mako taps it, carefully, the way his father showed him. A tiny chip of stone flies off. Mako laughs out loud with delight. The other carvers smile. One of them pats him on the back. Another carver, they say. Mako walks home that evening as proud as a king.

At the quarry, the carvers work together in teams. There is a master carver, who decides the shape and the size of each moai. There are skilled carvers, who do the fine work on the face and the hands. And there are younger carvers, who do the rough work and learn from the others. Tavake is a skilled carver. One day, he hopes to be a master carver too.

In the evenings, after work, some of the carvers stay at the quarry. They sleep in little shelters near the volcano, so they do not have to walk home every day. They cook food over fires and tell stories. They talk about their work, and about the moai they have carved. From the quarry, they can see the whole east coast of the island, and the sea beyond, glowing in the sunset.
