How did people ever find this tiny island, in the middle of the vast Pacific Ocean? They were some of the greatest sailors who have ever lived. We call them the Polynesians.

The Polynesians are the people of the many islands of the eastern Pacific. Over many centuries, they explored and settled thousands of islands, spread across an enormous area of ocean. They found Hawaii in the north, New Zealand in the south-west, and Rapa Nui in the south-east. These three places form a great triangle, called the Polynesian triangle. It is bigger than all of Europe.

--

The Polynesians sailed in great canoes, with two hulls side by side, joined by a deck. These double canoes were strong and steady, and could carry many people. They had sails made of woven leaves, and long paddles for steering. On board, there was room for families, food, water, plants and animals. They could sail for weeks across the open sea.

{img:navigator-reading-stars-night-sky-canoe-deck-ocean-calm-ancient}
The Polynesians had no compasses or maps. But they were wonderful navigators. They knew the stars, and where each one rises and sets over the sea. They knew the patterns of the waves and the swells, which they could feel through the hull of the canoe. They watched the clouds, which gather over islands. They watched the birds, which fly out to sea in the morning and back to land in the evening. They read the ocean like a book.

--

The navigators learned these skills from their teachers, over many years. They learned the names of hundreds of stars. They learned songs and chants that helped them remember the way. Some navigators could lie down in the canoe and feel the swells under their backs, and know which way the land lay. Their knowledge was passed from generation to generation, by memory.

{img:canoe-carrying-plants-pigs-chickens-families-ocean-voyage-sunny}
On their voyages, the Polynesians carried everything they needed to start a new life. They carried plants: sweet potatoes, taro, bananas, sugar cane and paper mulberry trees. They carried chickens, pigs and dogs. Some little rats came along too, perhaps as stowaways, perhaps on purpose, as food. When they found a new island, they planted their gardens and made a new home.

--

Nobody knows exactly when the first people came to Rapa Nui. Scientists have studied old charcoal and bones, and they think it was about a thousand years ago, perhaps around the year 1200. The people may have come from the islands of Mangareva or the Marquesas, far to the west. It was a very long voyage, perhaps two or three weeks, across empty ocean. Finding Rapa Nui, so small and so far away, must have been a great achievement.

{img:sailors-seeing-island-on-horizon-canoe-birds-clouds-joy-dawn}
Imagine what it was like. After many days at sea, with nothing but water all around, the navigator sees a sign. Perhaps a bird, flying toward the horizon in the evening. Perhaps a little cloud, standing still over the sea. Perhaps a change in the waves. The canoe turns toward it. The people watch and wait, full of hope. And then, at dawn, they see it. A little green island, with three volcanoes, rising out of the blue ocean. A new home.

The great voyaging canoes were made with great skill, without any metal tools. The builders used stone adzes to shape the hulls from big tree trunks. They tied the pieces together with ropes made of coconut fibre. They sealed the joints with sap from the breadfruit tree. The sails were woven from the leaves of the pandanus plant. Building a great canoe took many months, and many skilled people.

On the long voyages, the sailors ate food that would keep for a long time. They ate dried fish and coconuts, fermented breadfruit and dried bananas. They drank rainwater, caught in their sails, and coconut water. They fished as they sailed. They sang songs and chanted to keep their spirits up. And they watched the sky and the sea, day and night, for signs of land.

Today, people in Hawaii and other Pacific islands have built new voyaging canoes, just like the old ones. One of them is called Hōkūle'a. Its sailors have crossed the Pacific many times, without compasses or maps, using only the stars, the waves and the birds. In 1999, Hōkūle'a sailed all the way to Rapa Nui. The people of the island came down to the shore to welcome it, with songs and tears of joy.
