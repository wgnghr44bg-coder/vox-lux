The people of Rapa Nui have their own story about how their island was first found. It is a story that has been told for many hundreds of years.

Long ago, the story says, there was a king called Hotu Matu'a. He lived on an island far to the west, called Hiva. One night, a wise man in his court had a dream. In the dream, his spirit flew across the ocean, far to the east. There, he saw a beautiful island, with three volcanoes and white sandy beaches. When he woke up, he told the king about it.

--

So the king sent seven young men to find the island. They sailed east, following the directions of the dream. After a long voyage, they found it. They explored the island and planted some sweet potatoes. Then the king himself came, with his family and his people, in two great canoes. They landed on the white beach of Anakena, on the north coast. There, the king's wife gave birth to a son. And the new people began their life on Rapa Nui.

{img:polynesian-king-family-arriving-beach-canoes-people-carrying-plants-dawn}
Hotu Matu'a divided the island among his sons. Each son and his family received a part of the land, running from the coast up to the middle of the island. Their descendants became the different clans of Rapa Nui. Each clan had its own land, its own villages, its own fishing places and its own sacred platforms by the sea.

--

Nobody knows how much of this story is history, and how much is legend. But archaeologists have found that Anakena was indeed one of the first places where people lived on the island. They have found the remains of old houses, and old tools, and the bones of animals that the first people ate. So the old story may remember something true.

{img:archaeologists-digging-anakena-beach-old-tools-bones-sieves-today}
When the first people arrived, the island looked very different from today. It was covered with forests of tall palm trees, with trunks as thick as a man. These palms were related to the Chilean wine palm, one of the biggest palms in the world. There were other trees and shrubs too. Millions of seabirds nested on the island. There were dolphins and seals in the sea. It was a rich and green place.

--

The first families built their houses near the shore. They fished in the sea and gathered shellfish on the rocks. They hunted seabirds and collected their eggs. They cleared small areas of forest to make gardens. They planted sweet potatoes, taro, bananas and sugar cane. They kept chickens, which became very important to them. The rats that came with them ran wild in the forest. Pigs and dogs did not survive the long voyage, it seems, or did not last long on the island.

{img:first-settlers-gardens-forest-clearing-sweet-potato-planting-palm-trees}
Slowly, over many generations, the number of people on the island grew. New villages were built all around the coast. The clans grew bigger and more organised. They had chiefs and priests, craftsmen and fishermen, farmers and builders. And they began to do something extraordinary. They began to carve giant figures of stone, and set them up on platforms around the island, facing the land.

The old story says that Hotu Matu'a lived at Anakena for the rest of his life. When he was very old, he climbed up to the top of the volcano Rano Kau, at the other end of the island. From there, he looked out across the ocean, toward his old homeland, far away in the west. He called out to the spirits of his homeland, to say goodbye. Then he came down, and passed away peacefully, among his family.

The descendants of Hotu Matu'a remembered his name for many hundreds of years. The chiefs of the island traced their families back to him. At great ceremonies, the wise men chanted the names of all the chiefs, one after another, back to Hotu Matu'a himself. It was a long list, many generations long. And every child of the island grew up knowing the story of the first king.
