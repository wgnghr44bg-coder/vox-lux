In the nineteenth century, very hard times came to Rapa Nui. It is the saddest part of the island's story. We will tell it gently, and briefly.

In 1862, ships came from Peru, in South America. They took many people from the island away, to work in Peru, against their will. Among them were the chiefs and many of the wise men, including the rongorongo men who could read the tablets. Very few of them ever came back. Those who did come back brought illness with them, which spread across the island. Many more people passed away.

--

By the 1870s, only about a hundred and eleven people were left on Rapa Nui. Once there had been thousands. It was a very dark time. Much of the old knowledge was lost: the reading of the rongorongo tablets, many of the old chants, many of the old stories. We remember all the people who suffered in those years, with sorrow and respect.

{img:small-group-rapa-nui-people-1870s-together-shore-hope-sunset}
But the people of Rapa Nui did not disappear. The few who were left held on to what they could. They kept their language and some of their stories. They kept their families together. They looked after each other. Slowly, very slowly, over many years, their numbers began to grow again.

--

In 1888, the island became part of Chile, the country in South America. For a long time after that, most of the island was rented to a company that kept sheep. The people of Rapa Nui had to live in one small village, called Hanga Roa, and were not free to move around their own island. Those were difficult years too. But the people kept their hope, and their pride.

{img:hanga-roa-village-early-1900s-simple-houses-church-people-gathered}
In 1966, things began to change. The people of Rapa Nui became full citizens of Chile. They could move freely around their island again. They could vote and travel. An airport was built, and visitors began to come. Schools taught the Rapa Nui language again. And the people began to bring back their old traditions, their songs and dances, their carving and their festivals.

--

Today, there are several thousand Rapa Nui people living on the island, and many more in Chile and other countries. They are proud of their ancestors and their culture. They speak their language, sing their songs and dance their dances. They carve wood and stone, like their ancestors did. And they look after the moai and the ahu, the sacred places of their island. The old knowledge was not all lost. Much of it survived, in the hearts and memories of the people.

{img:rapa-nui-elders-teaching-children-songs-language-today-circle-outdoors}
Many Rapa Nui elders have spent their lives remembering and recording the old stories, chants and string figures. They have taught them to their children and grandchildren. They have worked with scholars to write them down. And so, like Grandmother Uka long ago, they have made sure that the ancestors will not be forgotten.

One of the people who helped the island in those hard years was a woman from Rapa Nui called Angata. She was old, wise and brave, and in 1914 she led her people in standing up for their island. She spoke up for their rights and their land. Many people of Rapa Nui still remember her with great respect. She showed that, even in the hardest times, the people of the island had courage and hope.
