After the Dutch, more ships came to Rapa Nui. Each visitor wrote down what they saw. And slowly, a picture of the island's changing story appeared.

In 1770, two Spanish ships came. They planted crosses on the hills, and claimed the island for the king of Spain. They wrote that the people were friendly. They drew pictures of the moai. Then they sailed away, and never came back.

--

In 1774, the famous English explorer James Cook came, on his second great voyage around the world. His men explored the island for a few days. They wrote that many of the moai were no longer standing. Some had fallen on their faces, beside their platforms. The people did not seem to be making new ones anymore. Something had changed on the island.

{img:fallen-moai-face-down-beside-ahu-grass-ocean-quiet-afternoon-later}
In 1786, a French explorer called La Pérouse came. He was kind to the people. He gave them seeds and animals: goats and sheep and pigs. He wrote that the people seemed healthy and cheerful. He wrote about their gardens and their houses. He drew a famous picture of the people standing among the moai.

--

Over the following years, more and more of the moai fell. By the middle of the nineteenth century, almost none were still standing. Why did they fall? Again, nobody knows for sure. The old stories of the island speak of quarrels between the clans. Perhaps the moai were pulled down during these quarrels. Perhaps earthquakes and storms knocked some of them over. Perhaps, when the old beliefs changed, people no longer looked after them.

{img:islanders-gathered-new-beliefs-birdman-ceremony-moai-fallen-background}
The beliefs of the people had changed. The making of moai had come to an end. The ceremony of the birdman, at Orongo, had become more important. Perhaps the people felt that the old ancestors no longer protected them as they once had. Perhaps they turned to the god Makemake and the birdman instead. It is one of the quiet mysteries of Rapa Nui.

--

But even with the moai fallen, the people of Rapa Nui went on with their lives. They grew their sweet potatoes in their stone gardens. They fished and kept chickens. They held their birdman ceremonies and their festivals. They told their stories and sang their songs. And they remembered their ancestors, even if the great stone faces now lay in the grass.

{img:rapa-nui-family-1780s-garden-stone-house-children-fallen-moai-distance}
If we imagine Hina's great-great-grandchildren, living in the time of Cook and La Pérouse, they would still be living much as she did. They would still crawl into boat-shaped houses at night, and cook in earth ovens. They would still surf the waves on banana trunks, and make string figures with chants. They would still look up at the same stars. And perhaps, sometimes, they would walk past a fallen moai, and put a hand on its stone face, and remember.

The visitors also traded with the people of the island. They gave them cloth and metal tools, beads and mirrors. The islanders gave them sweet potatoes and chickens, bananas and fresh water. The islanders were very interested in the strange goods of the visitors. They especially liked hats and cloth, and anything red.

Captain Cook's artist, William Hodges, made a famous painting of the moai on the island. It shows several statues standing on a platform by the sea, with a dramatic sky behind them. It is one of the first pictures of the moai that people in Europe ever saw. People were amazed by it. And they began to wonder, for the first time, about the mystery of the giant stone faces.

La Pérouse also gave the people of Rapa Nui seeds of new plants: cabbages, carrots, pumpkins and orange trees. He hoped they would grow well on the island. He wrote in his journal that he wished the people a happy future. Sadly, he and his ships were lost at sea a few years later, far away in the Pacific. But his journal had been sent home, and his kind words about Rapa Nui survived.
