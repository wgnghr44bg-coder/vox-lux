As the forests disappeared, the people of Rapa Nui found clever ways to keep growing their food. One of the cleverest was a special kind of garden, made of stones.

The island is windy, and without the trees, the wind blew strongly across the fields. It dried out the soil and blew it away. So the farmers began to cover their fields with stones. They broke volcanic rock into small pieces, and spread them over the soil, like a blanket. Then they planted their sweet potatoes and taro between the stones.

--

These stone gardens are called rock mulch gardens. They work very well. The stones protect the soil from the wind and the sun. They keep the moisture in the ground, so the plants do not dry out. They warm up in the day and stay warm at night, which helps the plants to grow. And as the stones slowly break down, they add good minerals to the soil. The people of Rapa Nui covered huge areas of the island with these gardens.

{img:farmers-spreading-broken-volcanic-stones-field-baskets-wind-grassland}
Vai has several stone gardens near the village. She and Hina carry stones in baskets from a nearby rocky place, and spread them on the soil. It is hard work. But the sweet potatoes grow well between the stones. In autumn, they dig up big, sweet, orange potatoes. Vai is proud of her gardens. She says that the stones are her friends.

--

The people also made more of the little round stone pits, the manavai, where plants were sheltered from the wind. Some of them were quite deep, like little wells without water. Inside them, bananas and paper mulberry trees could grow, safe from the wind and the salty spray. From above, the island looked like it was covered with little stone circles.

{img:lava-tube-cave-entrance-garden-inside-banana-plants-light-falling-in}
The island also has many caves, long tunnels in the volcanic rock, made by flowing lava long ago. Some of them have openings in the roof, where the light comes in. The people planted gardens in these openings too, sheltered from the wind. Bananas and other plants grew there, in the soft green light. People also used the caves for shelter and storage, and as cool places to rest on hot days.

--

The people found new ways to fish, too. Without big trees for big canoes, they could no longer go far out to sea for tuna and dolphins. So they fished closer to the shore, from the rocks and from small canoes. They caught smaller fish, and eels and octopus. They collected more shellfish. And they kept more chickens, in their stone chicken houses.

{img:fisherman-rock-shore-line-net-sunset-waves-small-canoe-nearby}
Life on the island changed, slowly, over many generations. But the people of Rapa Nui adapted to their changing island with great cleverness and patience. They kept growing food, and kept their families fed. They kept their stories and their songs. Grandmother Uka tells Hina that the most important thing is to look after the land, and the land will look after you. Hina remembers these words all her life.

Today, scientists who study the stone gardens of Rapa Nui are amazed by them. They say the people of the island were very clever farmers. They found ways to grow food on a windy, rocky island, with poor soil and little water. Some modern farmers in other dry and windy places are now learning from these old gardens. The wisdom of Rapa Nui is still useful today.

In the evenings, the farmers sometimes sit on the low stone walls of their gardens and look at their crops. The sweet potato leaves move gently in the wind. The banana plants rustle in their sheltered pits. The farmers talk about the rain and the harvest, about their children and their neighbours. And they feel proud of the gardens they have made from stones and wind and patience.
