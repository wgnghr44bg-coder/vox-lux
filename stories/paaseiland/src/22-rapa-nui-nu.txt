Today, Rapa Nui is a part of Chile, and a special place, protected by the whole world. Most of the island is a national park, and a World Heritage Site.

About eight thousand people live on the island now, most of them in the little town of Hanga Roa, on the west coast. There are little houses with gardens full of flowers. There is a small harbour, with colourful fishing boats. There is a church, with beautiful wooden carvings that mix Christian and Rapa Nui traditions. There are little shops and cafés, schools and a hospital. And there is an airport, where planes come from Chile and Tahiti.

--

Visitors come from all over the world to see the moai. They stand in front of the fifteen giants of Ahu Tongariki at sunrise, as the sky turns pink and gold behind them. They walk along the slopes of Rano Raraku, among the great heads buried in the grass. They visit the moai with the coral eyes, at Ahu Ko Te Riku. They climb up to Orongo, and look at the birdman carvings and the little islands in the sea.

{img:visitors-sunrise-ahu-tongariki-fifteen-moai-silhouettes-pink-sky-today}
The people of Rapa Nui ask visitors to treat the moai and the sacred places with respect. You must not touch the moai, or climb on the ahu. You must stay on the paths. The moai are not just statues. For the people of Rapa Nui, they are still the faces of their ancestors. And the ancestors are still watching over the island.

--

Every year, in February, there is a great festival on Rapa Nui, called Tapati. It lasts for two weeks. There are contests of all the old skills. There are canoe races and swimming races. There are contests of carving and weaving, of singing and dancing. There is a race where young men carry heavy bunches of bananas around a lake. And there is haka pei, the old game of sliding down a steep grassy hill on banana trunks, very fast.

{img:tapati-festival-dancers-traditional-dress-feathers-body-paint-today}
During Tapati, the people paint their bodies with patterns, like their ancestors did. They wear costumes of feathers and leaves and bark cloth. They sing the old songs and dance the old dances. Families and clans compete with each other, and cheer each other on. It is a joyful time, full of music and laughter. And it shows that the culture of Rapa Nui is alive and strong.

--

The Rapa Nui language is spoken again, by young and old. Children learn it at school. There are songs and books and radio programmes in Rapa Nui. Carvers still make little moai and birdman figures of wood and stone, as their ancestors did. Women still make string figures and teach the chants. And the old stories are told again, to children who listen with wide eyes, just as Hina and Mako did, long ago.

{img:anakena-beach-today-moai-on-ahu-palm-trees-white-sand-families-swimming}
On the beach at Anakena, where the first canoes landed long ago, families come to swim and picnic on the weekends. The sand is soft and white, and the water is calm and blue. Palm trees, planted again, give shade on the beach. And on a platform above the sand, a row of moai stands, with red stone pukao on their heads. They look down over the beach, at the children playing in the waves. As if they are watching over them still.

The people of Rapa Nui also have a special connection to the sea, still today. Many of them are fishermen, going out in small boats in the early morning. Many are swimmers and surfers, like their ancestors. Every evening, families come down to the little harbour at Hanga Roa to watch the sunset over the ocean. Children jump into the water from the harbour wall, laughing and splashing, as children have always done.

Some moai from Rapa Nui were taken away long ago, and are now in museums in other countries. One famous moai, called Hoa Hakananai'a, is in the British Museum in London. The people of Rapa Nui would like their ancestors to come home. They have asked for them to be returned. In recent years, a few objects have been sent back to the island. And the people hope that more will follow.
