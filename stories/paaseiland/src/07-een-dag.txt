Let us spend an ordinary day with the family, around the year 1500.

The day begins at dawn, when the roosters crow in their stone houses. The family crawls out of their house into the cool morning air. Vai lights a small fire and warms some sweet potatoes from yesterday. The family eats them sitting on the paved stones, looking at the sea. The ocean is calm and silver in the morning light. Seabirds are flying low over the water, looking for fish.

--

After breakfast, Tavake sets off to the quarry at Rano Raraku. It is a long walk across the island, perhaps two hours. He carries his stone tools in a basket on his back. Several other carvers from the village go with him. They walk along a path through the grassland, past gardens and other villages. They greet the people they meet. Everyone knows the carvers, and respects them.

{img:carvers-walking-path-island-baskets-tools-volcano-ahead-morning-grass}
Mako goes with his father today. He is very excited. He walks fast to keep up with the men, carrying his own little basket with his own little stone tool. At the quarry, he is allowed to watch, and to help a little, as long as he is careful. He has been waiting for this day for weeks.

--

Vai and Hina and Grandmother Uka stay at home. In the morning, they work in the gardens. They pull weeds and dig around the sweet potatoes. They pick ripe bananas and cut sugar cane. Hina likes to chew a piece of sugar cane while she works. It is sweet and juicy. Grandmother Uka sits in the shade and sorts seeds for planting.

{img:women-girl-working-garden-sweet-potatoes-stone-walls-sunny-morning}
In the afternoon, Hina goes down to the shore with some other girls. They look for shellfish and sea urchins on the rocks, at low tide. They catch little fish in the rock pools with their hands. Then they swim in a sheltered bay, diving under the waves and laughing. Hina is the best swimmer of all the girls. She can hold her breath for a very long time, and dive down deep.

--

The fishermen of the village are out at sea, in their small canoes. Wood is precious on the island, so the canoes are made of many small pieces, sewn together. The fishermen use hooks made of bone and stone, and nets made of plant fibres. They catch tuna and other fish, and sometimes a turtle. When they come back in the evening, the whole village comes down to the shore to share the catch.

{img:fishermen-small-canoes-returning-shore-catch-villagers-waiting-evening}
In the evening, Tavake and Mako come home from the quarry, tired and dusty. Mako cannot stop talking. He tells his mother and sister and grandmother everything he saw. The giant stone faces in the rock. The carvers, tapping and tapping. The chips of stone flying. And the great moai that his father is working on, which is almost finished. Soon, he says, it will walk. Grandmother Uka smiles at him. Yes, she says. Soon it will walk. And the family eats supper together, as the sun goes down into the sea.

Hina has a best friend called Rapa, who lives in the next house. The two girls do everything together. They work in the gardens side by side, and swim in the sea, and weave baskets in the shade. They tell each other secrets and make plans. Rapa wants to be a famous weaver. Hina wants to be a keeper of stories. They promise to help each other, always.

In the late afternoon, Grandmother Uka walks slowly along the shore, looking for special stones and shells. She uses them to make necklaces and charms. She finds a little shell, white and pink and shining, and gives it to Hina. Keep it, she says. It is a gift from the sea. Hina puts it in a little woven bag that she wears around her neck. She will keep it all her life.

When it gets dark, the family sits outside a little longer, watching the stars come out over the sea. The moon rises from the ocean, big and silver, and lays a shining path across the water. Grandmother Uka hums an old song. Mako leans against his father and yawns. And slowly, one by one, the family crawls into the house to sleep.
