Tonight we are going more than eighty years back, to the spring of 1945, to the last days of the Second World War in Europe.

[long-pause]

It is the beginning of May. In the villages of England, the apple trees are in blossom, and the bluebells are out, in the woods, and the swallows have come back, from Africa, and are flying low, over the fields, and the village greens. In the towns, and the cities, the trees are green again, in the parks, and the squares, between the bombed buildings, and the willowherb, with its tall pink flowers, is growing in the ruins.

[pause]

And everyone is waiting.

[pause]

If you like calm stories from the past, like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

For nearly six years, since September 1939, Europe has been at war. Millions of men, and women, have been in uniform, far from home. Cities have been bombed. Families have been separated. Children have been sent away, to the countryside, for safety. Food, and clothes, and almost everything else, have been rationed. The nights have been dark, with the blackout, and the days have been anxious, waiting for news, and letters.

[pause]

{img:family-gathered-around-wireless-radio-1945-living-room-listening}
But now, in the spring of 1945, everyone knows that the end is coming. Every evening, families gather around the wireless, to listen to the news, and every day, the news is better. The armies are moving forward, from the west, and from the east. Town after town, and country after country, is being freed.

[long-pause]

The question is no longer whether the war in Europe will end, but when. Next week? Tomorrow? Today? People buy flags, and bunting, and put them away, ready. They save a little of their rations, for a party. They wait.

[pause]

Tonight, we are going to follow those last days, gently, from a distance, in many places: in the hungry cities of Holland, where food falls from the sky; on the banks of the river Elbe, where two armies meet; in Denmark, where candles are lit in the windows; in a little red schoolhouse, in France, in the middle of the night; and in London, and Paris, and New York, and Moscow, on the days of celebration.

[pause]

{img:london-street-1945-bunting-flags-terraced-houses-spring-sunshine}
We will not dwell on the battles, or the hardships, but on the people: the ordinary people, who lived through it all, and who, in May 1945, after six long years, came out into the streets, and the sunshine, and the lights, and danced, and sang, and wept, and hugged strangers, because the war in Europe was over.

[long-pause]

So let us go back, to the beginning of May, 1945, to a world that is tired, and hopeful, and waiting, with the blossom on the trees.

[long-pause]

{img:allied-soldiers-crossing-rhine-pontoon-bridge-spring-1945-river}
In the spring of 1945, the war in Europe is coming to its end.

[pause]

In the west, the armies of the Allies, the Americans, and the British, and the Canadians, and the French, and the Poles, and many others, have crossed the great river Rhine, in March, and are moving east, across Germany, from town to town, faster than anyone had expected.

[long-pause]

In the east, the great armies of the Soviet Union are moving west, across Poland, and into Germany, toward Berlin. In the south, in Italy, the Allied armies are moving north, through the valleys, toward the Alps.

[pause]

Everywhere, the war is ending, place by place. Towns, and villages, are being reached, one after another, and the people come out, from their cellars, and their houses, and wave white sheets, from the windows, and the soldiers move on. Prisoners of war are being freed, from the camps, thin, and tired, and overjoyed, and begin the long journey home.

[pause]

And the world is learning, too, of terrible things that happened, in the camps, during the war, things that we remember, still, with great sorrow, and which we will leave, gently, in silence, here.

[long-pause]

In April, on the twelfth, in America, the President, Franklin Roosevelt, dies, suddenly, at his little house, in Warm Springs, in Georgia, after leading his country for twelve years, and through nearly the whole war. People all over the world mourn him. The new President is his Vice President, Harry Truman, a modest man, from Missouri.

[pause]

And at the end of April, in Berlin, the war is coming to its end, too. On the first of May, the German radio announces that Hitler is dead. And on the second of May, Berlin surrenders.

[pause]

{img:village-people-waving-white-sheets-windows-soldiers-passing-1945}
Day after day, the German armies surrender, in different places: in Italy, on the second of May, and in north-west Germany, and Holland, and Denmark, on the fourth, and in the south, in Bavaria, and Austria, a few days later.

[long-pause]

All over Europe, people are waiting for the last, great, final surrender, and the end of the war.

[pause]

And in Britain, and America, and all the Allied countries, people are waiting for the announcement: the day that will be called VE Day, Victory in Europe Day. It has been talked about for weeks. The newspapers are full of plans. The shops are full of flags. The churches are ready to ring their bells.

[pause]

{img:english-shop-window-1945-union-jacks-bunting-for-sale-queue}
And in the meantime, life goes on. People go to work, and to the shops, with their ration books, and queue for their food, and go home, and listen to the news, on the wireless, and go to bed, and wonder, will it be tomorrow?

[long-pause]

{img:lancaster-bombers-low-over-dutch-fields-dropping-food-sacks-1945}
In the western part of the Netherlands, in the spring of 1945, the people are hungry.

[pause]

The war has not yet ended there. The great cities of Holland, Amsterdam, and Rotterdam, and The Hague, and Leiden, and Utrecht, and Haarlem, are still cut off, by the front line, along the great rivers. And through the long, cold winter, of 1944 and 1945, there has been very little food, and very little fuel.

[long-pause]

The Dutch call it the Hongerwinter, the Hunger Winter. People have eaten sugar beets, and tulip bulbs, and have walked for days, out into the countryside, pushing prams, and bicycles, without tyres, to try to exchange their silver, and their linen, and their jewellery, with the farmers, for a few potatoes, or a little grain. Many thousands of people did not survive the winter.

[pause]

And now, at the end of April, something wonderful happens.

[pause]

On Sunday, the twenty-ninth of April, 1945, just after midday, the people of Holland hear the roar of aircraft engines, many of them, coming in from the sea. They have heard this sound, so many times, during the war. But this time, it is different.

[long-pause]

Great four-engined bombers, Lancasters, of the Royal Air Force, are flying in, very low, over the fields, and the towns, only a few hundred feet above the ground, so low that the people can see the faces of the crews, waving, in the windows. And from their open bomb bays, there falls, not bombs, but food.

[pause]

Sacks, and bags, and boxes, of flour, and dried egg, and margarine, and sugar, and tea, and chocolate, and tinned meat, and peas, and beans, and biscuits, tumbling down, onto the fields, and the racecourses, and the airfields, marked out with white crosses.

[pause]

{img:food-sacks-falling-on-meadow-dutch-children-running-windmill}
It is Operation Manna, named after the bread that fell from heaven, in the Bible, for the Israelites, in the desert. The Allies have arranged it, with the German commanders, who have agreed not to fire on the planes.

[long-pause]

The people of Holland run out, onto the streets, and the rooftops, and the church towers, and wave, with sheets, and flags, and handkerchiefs, and orange cloths, and cry, and cheer. Some of them have painted messages, on the roofs, and in the fields, in big letters: Thank you, boys. Many thanks.

[pause]

The next day, the American bombers join in, in Operation Chowhound. For ten days, until the eighth of May, hundreds of aircraft fly, every day, from England, over the North Sea, and drop their food, more than eleven thousand tons of it, altogether. Then, the food comes by lorry, and by ship, as well.

[pause]

{img:dutch-family-kitchen-1945-white-bread-loaf-table-children-joy}
The food is collected, and shared out, by the Dutch, and given to the people, through the shops, and the kitchens. And for the first time in months, the children of Amsterdam, and Rotterdam, and The Hague, eat white bread, and taste chocolate.

[long-pause]

Many of the airmen, who flew in Operation Manna, said, for the rest of their lives, that it was the best thing they did, in the whole war. And many of the Dutch children, who stood in the fields, and waved, remembered it, for the rest of their lives, too: the great planes, flying low, over the tulip fields, in the spring sunshine, and the food, falling from the sky, like a gift.

[long-pause]

{img:lancaster-crew-seven-airmen-standing-by-aircraft-airfield-spring-1945}
Let us go to an airfield, in the flat countryside of Lincolnshire, in the east of England, on a spring morning, at the end of April, 1945.

[pause]

On the airfield, the great Lancaster bombers stand, on their concrete pads, around the edge of the field, black, and huge, with their four engines, and their long wings, and their tall tail fins, and their crews, gathering beside them, in their flying jackets, and boots, in the cool morning air.

[long-pause]

Each Lancaster has a crew of seven: the pilot, and the flight engineer, who sits beside him, and watches the engines, and the fuel, and the navigator, at his little table, with his maps, and the wireless operator, with his radio, and the bomb aimer, lying in the glass nose, at the front, and two gunners, in their turrets, one in the middle, on the top, and one in the tail, at the very back.

[pause]

They are very young. The pilot is twenty-two. The rear gunner is nineteen. They come from all over Britain, and from Canada, and Australia, and New Zealand. They have flown many times, at night, over Germany, and come home, tired, in the dawn. But this morning, they are going to fly in daylight, and very low, and they are going to drop food.

[pause]

{img:ground-crew-loading-food-sacks-into-bomb-bay-lancaster-morning}
The ground crew, the mechanics, and the armourers, many of them women, from the Women's Auxiliary Air Force, have spent the night, loading the bomb bays, not with bombs, but with sacks, tied together, in bundles, in special panniers, of flour, and sugar, and tinned meat, and dried egg, and chocolate.

[long-pause]

Some of the airmen have added a little something of their own: bars of chocolate, from their own rations, and sweets, and cigarettes, tied to little parachutes, made from handkerchiefs, with notes, for the children who will find them.

[pause]

The engines start, one by one, with a cough, and a roar, and the great planes roll slowly, along the perimeter track, and turn onto the runway, and one after another, they thunder down it, and lift, heavily, into the air, and turn east, over the sea.

[pause]

{img:lancaster-cockpit-view-low-over-dutch-polders-canals-windmills}
They fly over the North Sea, very low, only a few hundred feet above the waves, and then, the coast of Holland appears, ahead of them: the long, pale line of the dunes, and the beaches, and then the flat, green land, criss-crossed with canals, and dykes, and ditches, shining in the sun, and the windmills, and the church towers, and the red roofs of the villages, and the tulip fields, in great stripes of red, and yellow, and pink, and white.

[long-pause]

And everywhere, the people. On the roofs, and in the streets, and in the fields, and on the dykes, thousands of people, waving: with sheets, and towels, and tablecloths, and flags, and their arms. The crew can see their faces, looking up. Someone has written, in big white letters, on a field: Thanks.

[pause]

The bomb aimer, lying in the nose, calls out, over the intercom, as they come over the dropping zone, marked with white crosses, on a racecourse, and presses his button, and the bomb doors open, and the sacks fall away, behind them, onto the grass.

[pause]

{img:lancaster-banking-over-crowd-waving-field-tulips-sunlit}
And the pilot banks the great plane, gently, around, so that the crew can look down, and see the people, running out, onto the field, and they wave back, from the windows, and the turrets, and some of them have tears in their eyes.

[long-pause]

Then they turn for home, back over the sea, toward England. And on the way home, the crew are very quiet. That evening, in the mess, and in the pubs, in the villages, near the airfield, they talk about it, and the next day, they fly again.

[pause]

Many years later, when they were old men, some of them went back, to Holland, and were welcomed, as heroes, by the children, now grown old too, who had stood in the fields, and waved.

[long-pause]

{img:elbe-river-torgau-broken-bridge-spring-1945-soldiers-meeting}
On the twenty-fifth of April, 1945, in the middle of Germany, something happens that people have been waiting for, for years.

[pause]

The two great armies, coming from the west, and from the east, meet.

[long-pause]

It happens beside the river Elbe, a wide, slow, grey-green river, flowing through the flat, green countryside of Saxony, between meadows, and willows, and little villages, with red roofs, and church towers.

[pause]

Small patrols of American soldiers, from the 69th Infantry Division, have been sent forward, to look for the Soviet army, which is said to be very close. They drive along the country roads, in their jeeps, through the quiet villages, carefully, looking, and listening.

[pause]

And near the little village of Strehla, and later that afternoon at the town of Torgau, with its old castle, beside the river, and its broken bridge, they see soldiers on the other side, in different uniforms, and with red stars on their caps. They wave, and call out, and fire coloured flares, to show who they are, and the soldiers on the other side wave back.

[long-pause]

At Torgau, an American lieutenant, William Robertson, and his men, climb across the twisted girders of the broken bridge, over the river, and on the other side, they meet the Soviet soldiers, of the 58th Guards Rifle Division. They shake hands, and hug, and laugh, and slap each other on the back, and cannot speak each other's languages, but it does not matter.

[pause]

They share what they have: cigarettes, and chocolate, and bread, and sausage, and vodka, and wine. They exchange badges, and buttons, and watches, and addresses. Someone brings out an accordion, and they sing, and dance, on the riverbank, in the spring sunshine.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-singing-accordion-riverbank-celebration-willows-spring}
The photographers come, and take pictures, of the American, and Soviet soldiers, shaking hands, on the broken bridge, and the pictures are printed in newspapers all over the world, the next days. In London, and New York, and Moscow, the news is announced, at the same time, with joy: the armies have met. Germany is cut in two. The end is near.

[long-pause]

The soldiers at the Elbe make a promise to each other, on that day, that they will work for peace, and friendship, between their countries, so that there will never again be a war like this one.

[pause]

It was a promise that was hard to keep, in the years that followed, when the world became divided, again, in a long, cold rivalry. But the soldiers remembered it. Some of them met again, years later, as old men, on the banks of the Elbe, and shook hands again, and laid flowers, on the riverbank.

[pause]

{img:torgau-memorial-river-elbe-flowers-quiet-today-old-castle}
Today, at Torgau, there is a memorial, beside the river, to the meeting on the Elbe. And every year, on the twenty-fifth of April, people still come there, to remember the day when two armies met, in the spring sunshine, and shook hands, and sang, by the river.

[long-pause]

{img:british-housewife-queue-grocer-ration-books-1945-shop-counter}
Let us go to an ordinary street, in an English town, in the spring of 1945.

[pause]

It is a street of little brick houses, in a row, each with its front door, and its window, and its little front garden, and its back yard. Some of the houses have been damaged, by bombs, earlier in the war, and their windows are still boarded up. At the end of the street, there is a gap, where two houses used to be, and the children play there, among the bricks, and the weeds.

[long-pause]

In one of the houses lives a family: a mother, and a grandmother, and three children. The father is far away, in the army, in Italy. The eldest son is in the navy. They have not seen them for a long time, more than two years. But they write letters, every week, and the letters come, sometimes in bundles, many at once, and sometimes not for weeks, and the mother reads them out, at the kitchen table, in the evening, and keeps them, in a box, tied with a ribbon.

[pause]

Every morning, the mother goes to the shops, with her ration books, and stands in the queue, at the grocer's, and the butcher's, and the baker's, to buy the family's food, for the week.

[pause]

Each person may have, every week, only a little: a few ounces of bacon, and butter, and cheese, and sugar, and tea, and margarine, and cooking fat, one egg, if there are any, and a little meat, measured by price, not weight, and sweets, a few ounces, each month. Bread, and potatoes, are not rationed, and the mothers of Britain have become very clever, at making meals out of very little: carrot cake, and potato pie, and mock banana, made from parsnips.

[long-pause]

The children have not seen a banana, or an orange, for years. The youngest has never seen one at all. There is dried egg, from America, in tins, which is made into omelettes, and scrambled eggs, and cakes. And everyone has been digging for victory: growing vegetables, in their gardens, and their allotments, and even in the parks, and keeping hens, and rabbits, in their back yards.

[pause]

Clothes are rationed too, and everyone mends, and patches, and darns, and makes do. The children wear their older brothers' and sisters' clothes, and the mothers make dresses from curtains, and blouses from parachute silk, when they can get it.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-bomb-site-street-1945-terraced-houses-sunny}
And for nearly six years, there has been the blackout. Every evening, at dusk, every window, in every house, must be covered, with thick black curtains, or blinds, so that not a chink of light can be seen, from the street, or the sky. The street lamps have been dark. The cars have driven with hooded headlamps. And people have walked home, through the dark streets, with little torches, pointed at the ground.

[long-pause]

But last September, the blackout was eased, to a dim-out, and now, in the spring of 1945, the lights are slowly coming back. At the end of April, the great clock faces of Big Ben, in London, were lit again, for the first time in more than five years, and crowds gathered, in the evening, to watch, and cheered, when the lights came on.

[pause]

The children, who were born during the war, have never seen a street, lit up at night. They look out of their windows, in wonder.

[pause]

{img:big-ben-clock-face-lit-again-evening-crowd-watching-1945}
In the evening, the family sit around the kitchen table, or by the fire, and listen to the wireless, and the grandmother knits, and the children do their homework, and the mother writes a letter, to the father, in Italy, and tells him that the lilac is out, in the back yard, and that the children are well, and that, perhaps, soon, he will be home.

[long-pause]

{img:bbc-wireless-set-wooden-cabinet-glowing-dial-1945-evening-lamp}
In the spring of 1945, the most important thing in every house is the wireless.

[pause]

It is a big wooden box, on the sideboard, or on a shelf, by the fire, with a round speaker, covered with cloth, and knobs, and a glowing dial, with the names of faraway places on it: London, and Paris, and Hilversum, and Luxembourg, and Moscow. It takes a little while to warm up, when it is switched on, and then, it hums, and crackles, and the voices come.

[long-pause]

For six years, the wireless has been the voice of the war. Every evening, at nine o'clock, the whole country has stopped, to listen to the news, on the BBC, read by calm, steady voices, which, during the war, began to say their names, so that people would know that it was really the BBC, and not an enemy pretending: here is the news, and this is John Snagge reading it.

[pause]

On the wireless, people have heard the King, and Mr Churchill, the Prime Minister, speaking to the nation, at the darkest moments of the war, and they have gathered around, in their homes, in the factories, and the pubs, and the shelters, to listen, together.

[pause]

{img:factory-workers-listening-radio-loudspeaker-canteen-1940s}
But the wireless has also been a comfort, and a friend, and has made people laugh, and sing, and forget, for a while.

[long-pause]

There are comedy shows, like ITMA, It's That Man Again, with its silly characters, and its catchphrases, which everyone repeats, the next day, at work, and at school. There is Workers' Playtime, with music, and comedians, broadcast from factory canteens, at lunchtime. There is Music While You Work, played in the factories, to keep the workers cheerful. There is Children's Hour, every afternoon, with stories, and plays, and Uncle Mac, saying goodnight, children, everywhere.

[pause]

And there are the singers. The favourite of all is Vera Lynn, a young woman, from East Ham, in London, with a clear, warm voice, who is called the Forces' Sweetheart, because the soldiers, far away, love her songs, which remind them of home: We'll Meet Again, and The White Cliffs of Dover.

[pause]

{img:young-woman-singer-microphone-1940s-studio-band-warm-light}
In the occupied countries of Europe, too, people have listened to the BBC, in secret, under the blankets, in the attics, and the cellars, with the volume turned down low, because it was forbidden. In Holland, in Denmark, in France, in Norway, they have listened for the news, and for the coded messages, and for the voices of their own exiled governments, and queens, and kings, speaking to them from London.

[long-pause]

And in the first days of May, 1945, the wireless is on, all day long, in every home, as people wait for the news, the announcement that will tell them that the war in Europe is over, so that they can put out their flags, at last, and ring the bells.

[long-pause]

{img:land-girls-1945-working-field-hay-cart-green-jumpers-breeches}
During the war, the women of Britain have done almost everything.

[pause]

When the men went away, to the army, and the navy, and the air force, somebody had to do their work: in the factories, and on the farms, and on the railways, and the buses, and in the offices, and the hospitals. And so, the women did it.

[long-pause]

In the factories, they made aeroplanes, and ships, and tanks, and guns, and parachutes, and uniforms, working long shifts, day, and night, in overalls, and headscarves, tied in a knot, on the top of their heads, to keep their hair out of the machines. They drove buses, and trams, and lorries, and ambulances. They worked as postwomen, and porters, and engineers, and welders, and crane drivers.

[pause]

On the farms, there was the Women's Land Army: tens of thousands of young women, many of them from the cities, who had never seen a cow, before, who went to work on the farms, ploughing, and milking, and harvesting, and looking after the animals, and the land, to feed the country. They wore green jumpers, and brown breeches, and felt hats, and they were called the land girls.

[pause]

{img:women-factory-workers-headscarves-aircraft-assembly-line-1940s}
In the forces, there were women too: in the Women's Royal Naval Service, the Wrens, and the Women's Auxiliary Air Force, and the Auxiliary Territorial Service, where Princess Elizabeth served. They worked as drivers, and mechanics, and radio operators, and cooks, and clerks, and plotters, moving the little markers, of the aircraft, on the great map tables, in the operations rooms, and on the anti-aircraft guns, and the searchlights.

[long-pause]

And at home, there were the mothers, and grandmothers, who kept the families going, through the bombing, and the rationing, and the blackout, and the worry, queuing for food, and making do, and mending, and caring for the children, and the old, and often working too, part-time, in the factories, or in the Women's Voluntary Service, serving tea, and soup, and comfort, to the people who had been bombed out of their homes.

[pause]

On VE Day, the women of Britain celebrate, with everyone else, in the streets, in their uniforms, and their overalls, and their best dresses.

[pause]

{img:wrens-waaf-women-uniform-celebrating-ve-day-arms-linked-street}
And after the war, things are not quite the same, for them. Many of the women give up their jobs, when the men come home, and go back to their homes, and their families. But they have learned that they can do things that people thought only men could do, and they do not forget it. And their daughters, and granddaughters, do not forget it either.

[long-pause]

Many years later, in 2005, a memorial was unveiled, in Whitehall, in London, to the women of the Second World War: a tall bronze sculpture, with the uniforms, and the overalls, and the work clothes, of the women, hanging on pegs, all around it, as if they had just taken them off, at the end of the war, and gone home.

[long-pause]

{img:canadian-soldiers-jeep-dutch-town-crowds-orange-flags-may-1945}
On the evening of the fourth of May, 1945, the news comes over the wireless, on the BBC, in Dutch, from London: the German forces in the Netherlands, and in north-west Germany, and in Denmark, have surrendered.

[pause]

That afternoon, on a windswept hill, on Lüneburg Heath, in northern Germany, in a tent, with a plain army table, covered with a blanket, the British Field Marshal Montgomery has received the surrender of the German commanders, and they have signed it, with an ordinary pen, which, Montgomery said, could have been bought in any shop for a few pence. It will take effect the next morning, at eight o'clock.

[long-pause]

In Holland, the people can hardly believe it. That evening, they come out into the streets, in the twilight, and stand, and talk, and some of them dare, for the first time in five years, to put a light in the window, and take down the blackout paper.

[pause]

And the next day, the fifth of May, is Liberation Day. In the town of Wageningen, in the middle of the country, beside the river Rhine, the Canadian general, Charles Foulkes, meets the German general, Johannes Blaskowitz, in a hotel, called Hotel de Wereld, the Hotel of the World, to arrange the surrender of the German troops in Holland. And the next day, the sixth of May, it is signed, in the great hall of the agricultural college, nearby.

[pause]

{img:dutch-girls-orange-ribbons-dancing-street-canadian-soldiers-may}
In the following days, the Canadian soldiers drive into the great cities of the west, into Utrecht, and Amsterdam, and Rotterdam, and The Hague, in their jeeps, and lorries, and tanks, and the whole population comes out to meet them.

[long-pause]

The streets are full of people, cheering, and singing, and dancing, and waving the red, white, and blue flag of the Netherlands, which has been hidden away, for five years, and wearing orange, the colour of the royal family, the House of Orange: orange ribbons, and orange sashes, and orange paper hats, and orange flowers, and anything orange they can find. Children climb onto the tanks, and the jeeps. Girls kiss the soldiers. Old men weep. The church bells ring.

[pause]

The Canadian soldiers give away everything they have: cigarettes, and chocolate, and chewing gum, and white bread, and biscuits, to the thin, hungry people, who crowd around them. Many of the soldiers remember it, for the rest of their lives, as the happiest days of the war.

[pause]

{img:dutch-street-party-1945-long-table-orange-decorations-children}
In every street, in every town, and village, there are street parties, with long tables, and chairs, carried out of the houses, and decorations, of orange paper, and flags, hanging between the houses, and music, and dancing, in the squares, and the children's games, and races, and everyone sings the national anthem, the Wilhelmus.

[long-pause]

Not long afterwards, the Queen, Wilhelmina, comes home, from her exile, in England, where she spoke to her people, on the radio, all through the war. And the royal family come home, too, from Canada, where Princess Juliana had lived, with her children, during the war.

[pause]

Princess Juliana was so grateful to Canada, for keeping her family safe, and for the Canadian soldiers, who freed the Netherlands, that, after the war, she sent a gift, of a hundred thousand tulip bulbs, to Ottawa, the capital of Canada. And every year since then, the Netherlands has sent tulips, to Ottawa. Today, every spring, there is a great tulip festival there, with a million tulips, flowering in the parks, beside the river.

[pause]

{img:ottawa-tulip-festival-spring-parliament-hill-tulip-beds-today}
And every year, on the fifth of May, the Dutch celebrate Liberation Day, Bevrijdingsdag, with festivals, and music, all over the country. And the night before, on the fourth of May, at eight o'clock in the evening, the whole country stops, for two minutes of silence, to remember all those who died.

[long-pause]

The trains stop. The traffic stops. The people in the streets, and in the houses, and in the shops, stand still, in silence. And then, the next morning, the fifth of May, the flags come out, and the music begins.

[long-pause]

{img:danish-window-candles-lit-evening-may-1945-copenhagen-street}
In Denmark, too, on the evening of the fourth of May, 1945, the people are listening to the wireless.

[pause]

At twenty-five minutes to nine, in the evening, a voice, on the Danish service of the BBC, from London, interrupts the programme, with a special announcement. The German forces in Denmark have surrendered. Denmark is free.

[long-pause]

And all over the country, in Copenhagen, and Aarhus, and Odense, and every little town, and village, and farmhouse, people jump up, from their chairs, and hug each other, and cry, and laugh, and run out into the streets.

[pause]

And then, they do something beautiful. They tear down the black paper, and the blackout curtains, from their windows, which they have had to keep closed, every evening, for five years. And they put candles, lit candles, in the windows, to show that the darkness is over, and the light has come back.

[pause]

{img:copenhagen-crowds-street-cheering-danish-flags-evening-1945}
All over Denmark, that evening, in every street, every window glows, with the soft light of candles, in the spring twilight. And the people walk, through the streets, in the evening, singing, and looking at the lights, in the windows, all the way along, flickering, in thousands, and thousands, of windows.

[long-pause]

The next day, the fifth of May, the Danish flag, the Dannebrog, red, with a white cross, flies everywhere, from every flagpole, and window, and the people celebrate, in the streets, and the parks, and the squares, and the British soldiers arrive, and are greeted with flowers.

[pause]

And every year since then, on the evening of the fourth of May, the Danes put candles in their windows, again, to remember the evening when the news came, and the darkness ended.

[pause]

{img:quiet-danish-street-today-candles-windows-evening-may-blue-dusk}
If you walk through a Danish town, on the evening of the fourth of May, even today, you will see them: little candles, glowing, in the windows of the houses, and the flats, and the shops, one after another, all along the street, in the blue spring dusk, as a quiet thank you, and a memory, of the day the lights came back.

[long-pause]

In Norway, too, the war ends, a few days later, on the eighth of May, peacefully. And on the seventeenth of May, Norway's national day, the people celebrate, as they have not been able to for five years, with the children's parades, and flags, and music, through the streets. And in June, the King, Haakon, comes home, from exile, in England, by ship, into the harbour at Oslo, on the same day that he had left, five years before, and the whole city comes out to welcome him.

[long-pause]

{img:reims-red-brick-schoolhouse-headquarters-night-1945-lights-windows}
In the city of Reims, in the north of France, in the region of Champagne, there is a school: a long, plain building, of red brick, with rows of windows, near the railway station.

[pause]

During the last months of the war, it has been used as the headquarters of the Supreme Allied Commander, the American General Dwight Eisenhower, and his staff. The classrooms have become offices, full of desks, and maps, and telephones, and typewriters.

[long-pause]

On the night of the sixth of May, 1945, in one of those classrooms, the map room, with its walls covered with maps, showing the positions of all the armies, a plain table has been set up, with chairs, and microphones, and lights, for the photographers.

[pause]

And at about half past two in the morning, on the seventh of May, the German representative, General Alfred Jodl, comes into the room, and sits down, at the table, and signs the surrender of all the German forces, everywhere, to take effect at one minute past midnight, on the night of the eighth to the ninth of May. Then the Allied officers sign too, for the Americans, and the Soviet Union, and, as a witness, for France.

[pause]

It is over in a few minutes. It is two forty-one in the morning.

[long-pause]

General Eisenhower does not come into the room. He is waiting, in his office, next door. When it is over, Jodl is brought to him, and Eisenhower asks him, through an interpreter, whether he understands what he has signed. He says yes. And then, he is taken away.

[pause]

And Eisenhower's staff try to write a message, to tell the governments, in Washington, and London, what has happened. They try many grand, long, important sentences. But in the end, Eisenhower writes it himself, very simply:

[pause]

The mission of this Allied force was fulfilled at 0241, local time, May 7th, 1945.

[pause]

{img:eisenhower-office-desk-pen-message-dawn-quiet-relief}
Then, he goes to bed, for a few hours.

[long-pause]

The next day, on the eighth of May, the Soviet leader, Stalin, asks for a second signing, in Berlin, so that the surrender will be signed in the capital of Germany, before the Soviet commanders, who have fought their way there. And late that evening, in a house in Karlshorst, a quiet suburb of Berlin, in a hall that was once the officers' mess of an army school, the German commanders sign again, before Marshal Zhukov, and the Allied generals.

[pause]

By the time it is finished, it is already past midnight, in Moscow, and the ninth of May. And so, in Russia, and many of the countries of eastern Europe, Victory Day is still celebrated, today, on the ninth of May.

[pause]

{img:reims-surrender-museum-map-room-preserved-maps-table-today}
The little red schoolhouse, in Reims, is still there, today. The map room has been kept, exactly as it was, on that night, with the maps on the walls, and the table, and the chairs, and it is now a museum, the Museum of the Surrender, where visitors can stand, in the quiet room, and imagine that night, at two forty-one in the morning, when the war in Europe came to its end.

[long-pause]

{img:london-crowds-waiting-whitehall-may-7-1945-newspapers-rumours}
In London, on Monday, the seventh of May, 1945, everybody knows that something has happened. But nobody has told them officially.

[pause]

The news of the surrender at Reims has been kept secret, for a day, so that it can be announced at the same time, in London, and Washington, and Moscow. But a news agency has already reported it, in America, and the rumours have spread, all over London, by the morning.

[long-pause]

All day, people wait. Crowds gather, in Whitehall, and outside Buckingham Palace, and in Trafalgar Square, and Piccadilly Circus, waiting for the news. Office workers lean out of their windows. People buy newspapers, every edition, looking for the announcement. Some people start to celebrate, anyway, and throw paper, out of the office windows, like snow. The flags come out, on the buildings. The church bells are ready.

[pause]

But the hours pass, and there is no announcement. Everyone is confused, and a little impatient.

[pause]

In the shops, people are buying flags, and bunting. The government has announced that, for the celebrations, people may buy red, white, and blue cotton bunting, without using their clothing coupons, and the shops sell out, quickly. Women sit at their sewing machines, making flags, and rosettes, and decorations, out of anything they can find, old dresses, and curtains, and scraps of cloth.

[long-pause]

At last, at about twenty to eight, in the evening, the Ministry of Information makes a short announcement, on the wireless: tomorrow, Tuesday, the eighth of May, will be Victory in Europe Day, and will be a public holiday. The Prime Minister will speak to the nation, at three o'clock in the afternoon. And the King will speak, at nine o'clock in the evening. And the day after, Wednesday, will be a holiday, too.

[pause]

So it is true. It is over. And tomorrow is the day.

[pause]

{img:family-kitchen-1945-evening-sewing-flags-children-excited-wireless}
That evening, in houses all over Britain, people get ready. The mothers take out the flags, and the bunting, that they have saved, and the children help to hang them, from the windows, and across the street, from house to house. The neighbours talk, over the garden walls, about the party, they will have, tomorrow. Everyone gives a little of their rations: a little sugar, and margarine, and flour, and dried egg, to make cakes, and sandwiches, and jellies, for the children.

[long-pause]

And in the night, over London, a great thunderstorm comes.

[pause]

The thunder rolls, over the rooftops, and the lightning flashes, over the city, and the rain pours down, heavy, and warm, washing the streets, and the dust, of the bombed buildings, and soaking the flags, and the bunting, hanging, ready, in the streets.

[pause]

{img:thunderstorm-over-london-night-lightning-rooftops-st-pauls-rain}
For six years, the sky over London has been full of danger, at night. But tonight, the flashes, and the rumbles, are only lightning, and thunder, and the people lie in their beds, and listen to the rain, and some of them laugh, and say that it is the last air raid, the sky's own fireworks, for the end of the war.

[long-pause]

And in the morning, the storm has passed. The sky is washed, and clear, and the sun is shining, warm, on the wet streets, and the flags, hanging from every window, are drying, and lifting, in the breeze.

[pause]

It is the eighth of May, 1945. It is VE Day.

[long-pause]

{img:english-church-bell-tower-bell-ringers-ropes-sunlight-1945}
On VE Day, all over Britain, the church bells ring.

[pause]

For most of the war, the church bells of Britain were silent. In June 1940, when it was feared that Britain might be invaded, the government ordered that the bells must not be rung, except as a warning, if the enemy came. So, for years, the bells hung silent, in their towers, in every town, and village, and on Sundays, the churches were quiet.

[long-pause]

In 1942, after a great victory, in the desert, in Egypt, at El Alamein, the bells were rung, once, for a Sunday, to celebrate. And in 1943, when the danger of invasion had passed, the bells were allowed to ring again, on Sundays. But on VE Day, they ring as they have never rung before.

[pause]

In every church tower, in the country, the bell ringers climb up the narrow, winding stairs, to the ringing chamber, a little room, high up, in the tower, where the ropes hang down, from the bells, above, with their striped woollen grips, called sallies, red, and white, and blue.

[pause]

{img:bell-ringers-chamber-circle-ropes-sallies-pulling-tower-window}
They stand in a circle, each one holding a rope, and they pull, and the great bells, above them, swing round, full circle, and ring out, one after another, in a falling peal, and then in changes, the patterns of English bell ringing, which are like a kind of mathematics, played on bells, the bells changing places, in the order, every time they ring.

[long-pause]

Many of the bell ringers have been away, at the war, and some of the ringers are new, and young, and some are very old, and have been ringing since they were boys. And they ring, and ring, all day long, on VE Day, and their arms ache, and they do not care.

[pause]

And the sound of the bells spreads, out over the rooftops, and the streets, and the fields, and the woods, and the rivers, one church answering another, from village to village, across the whole country, so that, wherever you are, on VE Day, in Britain, you can hear the bells.

[pause]

{img:village-church-ve-day-congregation-leaving-sunshine-bells-flags}
At Westminster Abbey, and Saint Paul's Cathedral, in London, there are great services of thanksgiving, and the people queue, in long lines, to go in, and give thanks. Saint Paul's Cathedral stood, through all the bombing of London, with the fires, all around it, and its great dome, rising out of the smoke, became a symbol of hope, for the whole country, in the darkest days. On VE Day, it is full, all day, with services, one after another.

[long-pause]

And in the evening, as the sun sets, the bells ring again, for evensong, and the people of the villages walk to church, in the long, golden light of a May evening, and sing the old hymns, Now Thank We All Our God, and O God, Our Help in Ages Past, and come out, again, into the dusk, and the sound of the bells, and the bonfires, beginning to glow, on the hills.

[long-pause]

{img:ve-day-trafalgar-square-crowds-flags-sunshine-nelsons-column-1945}
On the morning of the eighth of May, 1945, all of London comes into the centre of the city.

[pause]

They come by train, and bus, and Underground, and bicycle, and on foot, from every part of London, and from the towns around, in their best clothes, and their uniforms, with flags, and rosettes, and paper hats, and red, white, and blue ribbons in their hair.

[long-pause]

By midday, the streets of the West End are packed with people: Whitehall, and the Mall, and Trafalgar Square, and Piccadilly Circus, and Regent Street, and Oxford Street, so full that the traffic cannot move, and the buses, and the taxis, are stuck, in the crowds, with people climbing on their roofs.

[pause]

There are soldiers, and sailors, and airmen, from Britain, and America, and Canada, and Australia, and New Zealand, and France, and Poland, and Norway, and Holland, and many other countries. There are women in uniform, and nurses, and land girls, and factory girls, and office workers, and old men, and grandmothers, and children, on their fathers' shoulders.

[pause]

People climb up the lamp posts, and the statues, and onto the lions, at the foot of Nelson's Column, and into the fountains, in Trafalgar Square, and splash about, in the water. They wave flags, and sing, and dance, and form long lines, holding hands, and dancing the conga, and the Lambeth Walk, and the hokey-cokey, through the streets, and the crowds.

[long-pause]

Strangers hug each other, and kiss each other, and shake hands. Sailors dance with girls they have never met. Old ladies dance with soldiers. Everyone is smiling, and laughing, and singing: Roll Out the Barrel, and Knees Up Mother Brown, and There'll Always Be an England, and Land of Hope and Glory, and It's a Long Way to Tipperary.

[pause]

At three o'clock, the crowds fall silent. Loudspeakers have been set up, in the streets, and squares, and in the parks, and in the villages, and in every house, the wireless is turned on. And Mr Churchill speaks, from the Cabinet Room, in Downing Street.

[pause]

{img:crowd-silent-listening-loudspeakers-whitehall-3pm-churchill-broadcast}
He tells them, in his deep, slow voice, that the war in Europe is over. That the German surrender was signed, the day before, at Reims, and that hostilities will end, officially, at one minute after midnight, tonight. And he says: We may allow ourselves a brief period of rejoicing. And at the end, he says: Advance, Britannia! Long live the cause of freedom! God save the King!

[long-pause]

And the crowds cheer, and cheer, and the bells of the churches ring out, all over London, and all over the country, and the ships on the river sound their sirens, and their horns.

[pause]

Later that afternoon, Mr Churchill comes out, onto a balcony, of the Ministry of Health, in Whitehall, with the members of his government, and the crowds below, filling Whitehall, from one end to the other, cheer him, and sing For He's a Jolly Good Fellow. And he holds up his hand, and makes his famous sign, two fingers, in the shape of a V, for victory. And he says to them: This is your victory. And the crowd shouts back: No, it's yours.

[pause]

{img:churchill-balcony-whitehall-v-sign-crowd-below-flags-may-1945}
And all through the afternoon, and the evening, the crowds go on growing, and celebrating, in the sunshine.

[long-pause]

Many of the people of London, who were there that day, remember it for the rest of their lives: the noise, and the crowds, and the joy, and the feeling of being part of something enormous, and the strange, wonderful feeling, that, tonight, nobody would be afraid of the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:buckingham-palace-balcony-royal-family-crowds-mall-may-1945}
In front of Buckingham Palace, on the afternoon of the eighth of May, the crowd is enormous.

[pause]

It fills the whole space, in front of the palace, around the great white Victoria Memorial, and stretches back, all the way along the Mall, under the trees, toward Admiralty Arch, tens of thousands of people, waving flags, and calling: We want the King! We want the King!

[long-pause]

And the doors onto the balcony open, and the royal family come out: King George the Sixth, in his naval uniform, and Queen Elizabeth, in a pale blue dress, and hat, and their two daughters, Princess Elizabeth, who is nineteen, in her uniform of the Auxiliary Territorial Service, the ATS, where she has been training, as a driver, and a mechanic, and Princess Margaret, who is fourteen.

[pause]

The crowd roars. They wave, and the royal family waves back. And they stand there, for a few minutes, looking out over the great sea of people, and the flags, and then they go back in.

[pause]

But the crowd will not go away. They keep calling for them. And so, the royal family come out onto the balcony, again, and again, eight times, during the afternoon, and the evening. Once, Mr Churchill comes out with them, and stands beside the King, and the crowd cheers louder than ever.

[long-pause]

The King, and the Queen, have stayed in London, all through the war, even when Buckingham Palace was bombed, in 1940. The Queen said, at the time, that she was glad they had been bombed, because now she could look the East End in the face. They visited the bombed streets, and the hospitals, and the factories, and the people loved them for it.

[pause]

And now, on this evening, the two princesses ask their parents if they can go out, into the crowds, themselves, just this once. And the King, and the Queen, say yes.

[pause]

{img:princess-elizabeth-margaret-incognito-crowd-mall-evening-officers}
So, at about ten o'clock in the evening, the two princesses slip out of the palace, by a side door, with a small group of young officers, and friends, to look after them, and go out, into the crowds, in the Mall, and Whitehall, and the West End, without anyone knowing who they are.

[long-pause]

Princess Elizabeth pulls her uniform cap down low, over her eyes, so that no one will recognise her. And they walk, through the streets, with everyone else, arm in arm, in the dark, and the lights, and join in the singing, and the conga, and the hokey-cokey, among the crowds of strangers.

[pause]

Many years later, when she was the Queen, she remembered that night, and said: I remember lines of unknown people linking arms and walking down Whitehall, all of us just swept along on a tide of happiness and relief.

[pause]

{img:crowds-linking-arms-whitehall-night-singing-floodlights-1945}
At the end of the evening, the princesses stand in the crowd, outside Buckingham Palace, with all the other people, and call, with them: We want the King! We want the Queen! And then they watch, from the crowd, as their own parents come out onto the balcony, and wave.

[long-pause]

It was, Princess Elizabeth said later, one of the most memorable nights of her life. And then, at last, after midnight, they slip back in, through the side door, into the palace, and go to bed, while outside, the crowds go on singing, under the floodlights, until the early hours of the morning.

[long-pause]

{img:king-george-vi-microphone-desk-broadcast-palace-evening-1945}
At nine o'clock in the evening, on the eighth of May, the King speaks to his people, on the wireless.

[pause]

All over Britain, and all over the Empire, and the Commonwealth, in Canada, and Australia, and India, and Africa, and on the ships, at sea, and in the camps, of the soldiers, far away, people gather around the wireless, and fall silent, to listen.

[long-pause]

King George the Sixth is a shy, modest man, who never expected to be king. He became king, in 1936, when his elder brother, Edward, gave up the throne. And all his life, he has had a stammer: a difficulty in speaking, so that the words get stuck, and will not come. For him, speaking in public, and on the wireless, to millions of people, is very hard.

[pause]

But for years, he has worked, with a speech therapist, an Australian, named Lionel Logue, to learn to breathe, and to speak, slowly, and calmly, through the difficult words. And all through the war, he has spoken to his people, on the wireless, at Christmas, and at the great moments, carefully, and slowly, and they have loved him for it, because they know how hard it is for him.

[pause]

{img:families-listening-king-speech-radio-1945-living-room-quiet}
Tonight, Lionel Logue is with him, at the palace, as he speaks.

[long-pause]

The King speaks slowly, and carefully, and gently. He gives thanks, for the end of the war in Europe. He remembers those who will not come home, and those who have suffered, and lost, so much. He thanks the people, of the whole Commonwealth, for all they have done, and endured. And he reminds them that the war is not yet over, everywhere, that in the Far East, it still goes on.

[pause]

And he says that they should go forward, now, to build a better world, and a lasting peace, so that what they have been through will not have been in vain.

[pause]

{img:village-church-bells-ringing-evening-ve-day-bell-ringers-rope}
In the homes, and the pubs, and the streets, people listen, quietly, and when he finishes, they stand, for the national anthem.

[long-pause]

When the broadcast is over, Lionel Logue writes in his diary that the King spoke beautifully, and that he was very proud of him.

[pause]

And then, outside, the celebrations begin again, and go on, into the night, as the lights come on, all over Britain, for the first time in nearly six years.

[long-pause]

{img:churchill-cigar-bowler-hat-v-sign-car-crowds-may-1945}
On VE Day, the man the crowds want to see, more than anyone, after the King, is Winston Churchill.

[pause]

He is seventy years old, short, and round, with a round, pink face, and a few wisps of hair, and a big cigar, and a bow tie, spotted, and a black hat. He has been Prime Minister since May 1940, exactly five years before, when the war was going very badly, and he has led the country, through all of it.

[long-pause]

In the summer of 1940, when France had fallen, and Britain stood alone, and invasion seemed close, he spoke to the people, on the wireless, and in Parliament, with great, rolling, stirring words, that gave them courage, and hope. We shall fight on the beaches, he said. We shall never surrender. And: This was their finest hour.

[pause]

He worked, all through the war, day and night, in the underground rooms, beneath Whitehall, the Cabinet War Rooms, where he slept, sometimes, and from where he spoke on the wireless, and in his offices, and on his travels, all over the world, to meet the other leaders, President Roosevelt, and Marshal Stalin. He visited the bombed streets of London, and the soldiers, at the front, and the people cheered him, everywhere he went.

[pause]

{img:cabinet-war-rooms-underground-map-room-telephones-lamps}
He worked very hard, and slept in the afternoons, and dictated letters, and speeches, to his secretaries, late into the night, often in his dressing gown, walking up and down, with his cigar. He loved painting, and bricklaying, and his garden, and his cats, and his poodle, and his wife, Clementine, to whom he wrote loving letters, all his life.

[long-pause]

On VE Day, he has lunch with the King, at Buckingham Palace, and then he drives back to Downing Street, through the crowds, which are so thick, that his car can hardly move, and has to be pushed, by the people, the last part of the way. He sits on the top of the back of the open car, and waves, and makes his V sign, and the crowds cheer, and sing.

[pause]

At three o'clock, he speaks to the nation, from the Cabinet Room. Then he goes to the House of Commons, and gives the same news to the Members of Parliament, who cheer, and wave their order papers. And then he leads them all, across the road, to Saint Margaret's Church, beside Westminster Abbey, for a service of thanksgiving.

[pause]

{img:whitehall-ministry-of-health-balcony-evening-floodlit-crowd-singing}
And in the evening, he comes out, again, onto the balcony, in Whitehall, in the floodlights, and the crowd sings Land of Hope and Glory, and he conducts them, waving his arms, and then speaks to them, again, and says that, in all their long history, they have never seen a greater day than this.

[long-pause]

Two months later, in the general election, the people of Britain will vote for a new government, and Churchill will no longer be Prime Minister. He is hurt, and surprised. But many of the people who voted against him still love him, and are grateful to him, and they say that he was the right man for the war, but that they want something new, for the peace.

[pause]

He will become Prime Minister again, in 1951, and live until 1965, when he is ninety. And when he dies, the whole country will mourn him, and the cranes, along the river Thames, in London, will bow down, as his coffin passes, on a boat, along the river.

[long-pause]

{img:st-pauls-cathedral-floodlit-night-1945-searchlights-v-sky-crowds}
On the evening of VE Day, as the sun goes down, something happens in London that many children have never seen in their lives.

[pause]

The lights come on.

[long-pause]

For nearly six years, London has been dark at night. Now, at dusk, the great buildings of London are floodlit, for the first time since 1939: Saint Paul's Cathedral, its great dome glowing, white, and gold, against the dark sky, and Buckingham Palace, and the Houses of Parliament, and Big Ben, and the National Gallery, and Nelson's Column, and the Tower of London.

[pause]

And in the sky, above the city, the searchlights, which for years have swept the sky, at night, searching for enemy aircraft, now make patterns of light, crossing, and waving, and forming great V signs, for victory, high over the rooftops, like a great, silent firework display.

[pause]

The ships on the river turn on their lights, and fire rockets, and flares. In the parks, people light bonfires, and dance around them. In the streets, people take down their blackout curtains, and leave their windows uncovered, and their lights on, so that the light pours out, onto the streets, golden, and warm.

[long-pause]

A little girl, who was born during the war, stands at the window, with her mother, and looks out, at the street, with all the windows lit, and the street lamps glowing, and asks, what is it? She has never seen a street at night with lights.

[pause]

All over the country, in every town, and village, there are bonfires. On the hills, and the greens, and the bombed sites, people pile up wood, and old furniture, and blackout curtains, and burn them, joyfully, and dance around the flames. Some of the bonfires have a figure on the top, of the enemy leader, made of old clothes and straw, which is burned, like Guy Fawkes, on Bonfire Night.

[pause]

{img:village-green-bonfire-night-ve-day-people-dancing-around-fire}
And people let off the fireworks they have been saving, and the rockets go up, and burst, in showers of coloured stars, over the rooftops, and the children cheer.

[long-pause]

In London, the crowds go on, all night. In Piccadilly Circus, people dance around the statue of Eros, which has been boarded up, and taken away, for safety, during the war, so that only the boarded base remains, and the people climb on it, anyway. In Trafalgar Square, the singing goes on, till dawn.

[pause]

And at last, in the early hours of the morning, tired, and happy, and hoarse from singing, the people walk home, through the lit streets, or sleep, in the parks, under the trees, on the grass, wrapped in their coats, and their flags, while the floodlights shine on Saint Paul's, and the searchlights slowly fade, and the sky, in the east, grows pale.

[pause]

{img:dawn-after-ve-day-london-park-people-sleeping-grass-flags-quiet}
And for the first time in six years, nobody, anywhere in Britain, is afraid of the night sky.

[long-pause]

{img:children-1945-waving-flags-front-gate-terraced-house-sunny-may}
For the children of 1945, VE Day is the most exciting day of their lives.

[pause]

Many of them cannot remember a time before the war. They were born in 1938, or 1939, or 1940, and they have grown up with the war all around them: with the blackout, and the sirens, and the air raid shelters, and the gas masks, and the ration books, and their fathers far away.

[long-pause]

They have slept in shelters, in the back garden, little curved huts of corrugated iron, half buried in the earth, called Anderson shelters, with bunk beds inside, damp, and cold, and smelling of earth, where the family went, when the sirens sounded, at night. Or under the kitchen table, in a steel cage, called a Morrison shelter. Or on the platforms of the London Underground, in long rows, with blankets, and pillows, with thousands of other families.

[pause]

They have collected shrapnel, the jagged little pieces of metal, from the shells, and the bombs, and swapped them, at school, like marbles, or cards. They have collected scrap metal, and paper, and saucepans, and old bones, for the war effort. They have carried their gas masks, in cardboard boxes, on a string, everywhere they went, and practised putting them on, at school, and made rude noises with them.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-anderson-shelter-garden-vegetables-1945-spring}
They have never tasted a banana, or seen a lemon, and sweets have been rationed, and ice cream has been almost impossible to find. Their toys have been home-made: wooden aeroplanes, and rag dolls, and knitted animals, made from old jumpers, unpicked, and knitted again.

[long-pause]

And now, the war is over. And everything is suddenly exciting, and new.

[pause]

On VE Day, they are allowed to stay up, late, much later than ever before, and to go out into the streets, at night, and see the bonfires, and the fireworks, and the lights. They eat jelly, and cake, at the street party, until they cannot eat any more. They wave their little paper flags, and wear their paper hats, and run around, among the grown-ups, who are all, for once, laughing, and singing, and dancing, and not telling them to be quiet.

[pause]

{img:children-sitting-on-wall-watching-fireworks-night-1945-faces-glow}
Years later, when they are old, and are asked what they remember about VE Day, they say: the lights. Seeing the street lamps on, and the windows lit up, for the first time. Or: the bonfire, at the end of our street, and my father, home on leave, lifting me up, on his shoulders, to see it. Or: the jelly. Or: my mother crying, and laughing, at the same time, and I did not understand why.

[long-pause]

And one of them, a little girl, remembered, all her life, lying in bed, on the night of VE Day, very late, too excited to sleep, and looking at the ceiling of her bedroom, where, for the first time, the light from the street lamp, outside, shone through the gap in the curtains, and made a soft, golden stripe, across the ceiling. And she lay, and looked at it, and listened to the singing, in the street below, until she fell asleep.

[long-pause]

{img:thames-ships-midnight-may-8-1945-sirens-lights-river-floodlit}
At one minute past midnight, on the night of the eighth to the ninth of May, 1945, the war in Europe officially ends.

[pause]

All day long, the people have been celebrating. But it is only now, at this moment, in the middle of the night, that the guns, everywhere in Europe, must fall silent.

[long-pause]

In London, the crowds are still in the streets, in Whitehall, and the Mall, and Trafalgar Square, and Piccadilly, under the floodlights. And as midnight comes, Big Ben strikes twelve, slowly, deep, and clear, over the crowds, and the river, and the whole of London listens.

[pause]

And then, on the river Thames, and in the docks, and in every harbour, all around the coasts of Britain, the ships sound their sirens, and their horns, and their whistles, all together: the tugs, and the barges, and the steamers, and the warships, and the merchant ships. Many of them sound the letter V, in Morse code, three short blasts, and one long one: dot, dot, dot, dash. V for victory.

[pause]

{img:harbour-night-ships-lights-sirens-searchlights-celebration-1945}
It is the same rhythm as the opening notes of Beethoven's Fifth Symphony, which the BBC has played, all through the war, at the start of its broadcasts to occupied Europe, as a secret sign of hope: da, da, da, dum.

[long-pause]

On the warships, the sailors fire rockets, and flares, and turn on their searchlights, and their lights, and the harbours glow, and flash, in red, and green, and white, and the water reflects it all.

[pause]

And then, slowly, the sound of the sirens dies away, and the night grows quieter.

[long-pause]

In France, and Belgium, and Holland, and Denmark, and Norway, and Italy, and Germany, and Austria, and Czechoslovakia, and Poland, and on all the battlefields of Europe, the soldiers, in their camps, and their trenches, and their billets, hear that it is over. Some of them cheer, and fire into the air. But many of them simply sit, quietly, in the dark, and smoke a cigarette, and look at the stars, and think about home, and about their friends, and listen to the silence.

[pause]

For the first time in nearly six years, there is no war in Europe.

[pause]

{img:soldier-sitting-quiet-night-stars-camp-fire-after-war-1945}
And in the little houses, and the flats, and the farms, all over Europe, the mothers, and the grandmothers, and the children, are asleep, or lying awake, listening, to the singing, and the sirens, far away, and then, to the silence.

[long-pause]

It is a strange, and wonderful, silence: the silence of a night with nothing to fear. And all over Europe, people turn over, in their beds, and pull up their blankets, and sleep, at last, deeply, and peacefully, until the morning.

[long-pause]

{img:ve-day-street-party-long-tables-children-paper-hats-bunting-1945}
In the streets of Britain, VE Day is the day of the street parties.

[pause]

In thousands of streets, all over the country, in the cities, and the towns, and the villages, the neighbours bring out their kitchen tables, and their chairs, and put them in a long row, down the middle of the street, and cover them with white tablecloths, and sheets.

[long-pause]

Over the street, from house to house, they hang bunting, and flags, and paper chains, and garlands, red, and white, and blue, and pictures of the King, and the Queen, and Mr Churchill, in the windows. And on the walls, they hang signs, painted by hand: Welcome Home, and Victory, and Well Done, Boys.

[pause]

And then the food comes out. Everyone has saved a little, from their rations, for weeks, and given it, to make the party. There are sandwiches, of fish paste, and spam, and jam, and egg, made with dried egg, and sausage rolls, and little cakes, and jam tarts, and jellies, in every colour, wobbling on their plates, and blancmange, and a big cake, with icing, made with sugar that the whole street has saved.

[pause]

The children sit at the long tables, in their best clothes, and their paper hats, and eat, and eat, more than they have eaten for years, while the mothers, and grandmothers, pour the tea, from big brown teapots, and pass round the plates.

[long-pause]

Then there are games, and races, for the children, down the street: running races, and sack races, and egg and spoon races, and three-legged races. And prizes: a sixpence, or a little toy, or a bag of sweets.

[pause]

Someone brings out a piano, from one of the houses, and wheels it into the street, and plays, all afternoon, and evening, and everyone sings, all the old songs, and dances, in the street: the old people, and the young, the mothers, and the children, and the soldiers, home on leave.

[pause]

{img:piano-in-street-neighbours-singing-dancing-evening-terraced-houses}
There is a fancy dress parade, for the children, who are dressed up, in costumes made of crepe paper, and old clothes: as Britannia, with a shield, and a helmet, and as soldiers, and sailors, and nurses, and John Bull, and the King, and Mr Churchill, with a cigar, made of rolled-up paper.

[long-pause]

And in the evening, there is a bonfire, at the end of the street, on the bombed site, and the old blackout curtains are thrown onto it, and burned, and everyone cheers.

[pause]

In the photographs of that day, which people kept, all their lives, we see them: the long tables, down the middle of the little streets, and the children, in their paper hats, with their mouths full of cake, and the mothers, in their aprons, and the grandmothers, in their hats, and the flags, and the bunting, over their heads, and everyone smiling.

[pause]

{img:old-photograph-style-street-party-group-smiling-1945-black-white-feel}
And yet, at many of the tables, there are empty chairs. There are fathers, and sons, and brothers, still far away, in the army, or the navy, who will not come home for many months. And there are some who will not come home at all. Many of the people at the parties are thinking of them, quietly, in the middle of the singing, and the laughter.

[long-pause]

So VE Day is a day of joy, and of relief, and of sadness, too, all mixed together. And people remember it, all their lives, as one of the most important days they ever lived through.

[long-pause]

{img:english-village-ve-day-green-flags-cottages-maypole-sunshine-1945}
Let us go to a little village, in the English countryside, on VE Day.

[pause]

It is a village of old stone cottages, with thatched roofs, and gardens, full of flowers, around a green, with a church, and a pub, and a pond, with ducks, and a war memorial, from the first war, with the names of the young men of the village who did not come home, in 1918.

[long-pause]

The village has been quiet, during the war, far from the bombs, mostly. Many of the young men are away, in the army. Some land girls have come, to work on the farms, and some evacuee children, from London, have lived here, with the village families, and gone to the village school, and learned to milk the cows, and collect the eggs, and climb the trees, and some of them have stayed, all through the war.

[pause]

On the morning of VE Day, the church bells ring, and the whole village comes out, onto the green. The flags are flying, from the church tower, and the pub, and the cottages, and the bunting is strung across the lane, and round the green, between the trees.

[pause]

In the afternoon, there is a tea, for the children, in the village hall, with long tables, and sandwiches, and cakes, and jelly, and a big cake, with VICTORY written on it, in icing, made by the baker. And then, there are sports, on the green: races, for the children, and the grown-ups, and a tug of war, between the men, and the women, which the women win, and a cricket match, between the married men, and the single men.

[long-pause]

The old men sit on the bench, outside the pub, in the sunshine, and watch, and remember the end of the other war, in 1918, when they were young, and came home, from France.

[pause]

The vicar holds a service, of thanksgiving, in the church, and the church is fuller than it has been for years, and the people sing, and pray, and read the names, of the young men of the village who have died, in this war, and the names are added, later, to the old memorial, on the green.

[pause]

{img:village-pub-evening-ve-day-singing-piano-lanterns-garden}
In the evening, the pub is crowded, and the doors are open, and the singing spills out, onto the green, and someone plays the piano, and someone plays the accordion, and there is dancing, on the grass, in the long evening light, to the old tunes.

[long-pause]

And when it gets dark, there is a bonfire, on the hill, above the village, and the whole village climbs up, to watch it, and from the top of the hill, they can see other bonfires, burning, on the other hills, all around, far away, one after another, across the dark countryside, like a chain of lights, from village to village, as they did, long ago, for the great events of English history.

[pause]

And the children, and the evacuees, and the land girls, and the old men, and the mothers, stand around the fire, in the dark, with their faces glowing, and sing, and watch the sparks fly up, into the sky, and the stars.

[pause]

{img:hilltop-bonfire-night-villagers-silhouette-distant-fires-hills}
And then, they walk home, slowly, down the hill, along the lanes, in the dark, with the smell of the may blossom, in the hedges, and the owls calling, and the sound of the singing, still coming, faintly, from the pub, and go to bed, tired, and happy, in the quiet village, at the end of the war.

[long-pause]

{img:paris-champs-elysees-ve-day-crowds-arc-de-triomphe-flags-1945}
On the eighth of May, 1945, it is not only London that celebrates.

[pause]

In Paris, the bells of all the churches ring, and the crowds pour into the streets, onto the Champs-Élysées, and the Place de la Concorde, and around the Arc de Triomphe, with the flags of France, and Britain, and America, and the Soviet Union, waving. General de Gaulle speaks, on the radio. The people sing the Marseillaise, and dance, in the streets, and the squares, and climb onto the jeeps, of the American soldiers, all night long.

[long-pause]

Paris was freed, in August of the year before, in 1944, so the people of Paris have already had their great day of liberation. But now, the whole war in Europe is over, and they celebrate again, and the lights of Paris shine, and the great monuments are floodlit, for the first time in years.

[pause]

In New York, it is morning, when the news comes. President Truman speaks to the nation, on the radio, at nine o'clock in the morning. It is his sixty-first birthday. He tells the people of America that the war in Europe is over, but that the war in the Pacific still goes on, and that there is still work to be done.

[pause]

But in New York, the crowds gather, anyway, in Times Square, in their hundreds of thousands, and the office workers, in the tall buildings, throw ticker tape, and torn-up paper, and telephone directories, out of the windows, so that it falls, like snow, on the streets below, and the people cheer, and dance, and kiss, and the ships, in the harbour, sound their whistles.

[long-pause]

In Moscow, the news comes in the early hours of the next morning, the ninth of May. The radio announces it, at about two o'clock in the morning, and the people wake up, and dress, and pour into the streets, and into Red Square, in the dark, and the dawn, singing, and cheering, and crying.

[pause]

All day long, the streets of Moscow are full of people, celebrating. They pick up soldiers, who are walking past, and throw them up into the air, again and again, and catch them, laughing. And in the evening, there is a great salute, of thirty salvos, from a thousand guns, and fireworks, and searchlights, over the Kremlin, and Red Square, and the river.

[pause]

{img:moscow-red-square-victory-fireworks-night-may-9-1945-crowds-kremlin}
The Soviet Union has suffered more in the war than any other country. Millions of its people have died, and its cities, and villages, have been destroyed. So the joy, in Moscow, on the ninth of May, is mixed with great sorrow, too, for all that has been lost.

[long-pause]

And all over the world, in Canada, and Australia, and New Zealand, and South Africa, and India, and in the countries of Europe, freed, one by one, Belgium, and Luxembourg, and Czechoslovakia, and Poland, and Greece, and Yugoslavia, and in every place where people have waited for this day, there are bells, and flags, and singing, and prayers, of thanksgiving.

[pause]

{img:small-town-church-thanksgiving-service-may-1945-congregation-flowers}
In the churches, and the chapels, and the synagogues, all over the world, there are services of thanksgiving, and people come, in their thousands, to give thanks, and to pray, quietly, for those they have lost, and for peace.

[long-pause]

{img:london-morning-after-ve-day-streets-paper-flags-quiet-sweepers}
The next day, the ninth of May, is a holiday, too, in Britain. VE Day plus one.

[pause]

In the morning, London is quiet. The streets are covered with paper, and streamers, and flags, and confetti, and broken bottles, and people are sleeping, in the parks, and on the benches, and on the steps of the buildings, with their flags, wrapped around them.

[long-pause]

The street sweepers come out, early, with their brooms, and their carts, and begin to clear up. The buses, and the trains, start running again. And slowly, the people wake up, and stretch, and go home, for breakfast.

[pause]

But by the afternoon, the crowds are back, in the West End, and outside the palace, and in Trafalgar Square, and the celebrations begin again, a little quieter, a little more tired, but still happy.

[pause]

{img:king-queen-car-driving-east-end-crowds-cheering-1945}
On this day, the King, and the Queen, drive through the East End of London, in an open car, through the streets that were bombed, so badly, during the war, and the people of the East End come out, in their thousands, to cheer them, and wave their flags, from the windows, and the doorways, and the bomb sites.

[long-pause]

In the streets, the parties go on: the children who had a party yesterday, have another one today, with the food that was left over, and the grown-ups sit outside, in the sunshine, on their chairs, on the pavement, and talk, and drink tea, and rest.

[pause]

And in many homes, the people sit quietly, and think about everything that has happened, in the six years of the war, and the people they have lost, and the people who are still far away, and they write letters, and read the newspapers, with their great headlines: VICTORY, and IT'S OVER, and the photographs of the crowds, the day before.

[pause]

{img:family-garden-afternoon-tea-1945-deckchairs-newspaper-headlines}
The newspapers are kept, and folded, and put away, in drawers, and boxes, and attics, as a memory of these days, and many of them are still there, today, yellowed, and fragile, found by grandchildren, and great-grandchildren, clearing out old houses.

[long-pause]

And on the tenth of May, the people go back to work. The factories start again, and the offices, and the shops, and the schools, and the trains. Life goes on. There is still rationing, and there are still queues, and there are still soldiers far away, and there is still much to do.

[pause]

But it is different now. The fear has gone. And in the evenings, as the sun sets, the lights come on, in the windows, and the street lamps, and nobody draws the blackout curtains, any more.

[long-pause]

{img:guernsey-st-peter-port-harbour-may-1945-crowds-british-ships}
There is one small part of Britain that is still waiting, on VE Day: the Channel Islands.

[pause]

The Channel Islands are little islands, in the sea, between England and France, much closer to France: Jersey, and Guernsey, and Alderney, and Sark, and some even smaller ones. They belong to the British Crown, and are the only British land that was occupied, by the German army, during the war, for nearly five years, since the summer of 1940.

[long-pause]

For five years, the islanders have lived under occupation, cut off from Britain, and from the world. In the last winter, after the Allies had freed France, the islands were completely cut off, and food ran very short, and the people were hungry, and cold, with no fuel, and almost nothing to eat. In December 1944, a Red Cross ship, the Vega, came, from Lisbon, with food parcels, and it came again, several times, and saved many people.

[pause]

On the afternoon of VE Day, the islanders listen, in secret, on their hidden wireless sets, to Mr Churchill's speech, at three o'clock. And they hear him say: our dear Channel Islands are also to be freed today.

[pause]

{img:jersey-islanders-waving-flags-harbour-wall-royal-navy-destroyer}
And the next morning, the ninth of May, two British destroyers, HMS Bulldog, and HMS Beagle, arrive, off the islands, and the German commanders come aboard, and sign the surrender. And the British soldiers come ashore, at Saint Peter Port, in Guernsey, and Saint Helier, in Jersey.

[long-pause]

The whole population comes down to the harbours, to meet them. They have been hiding their Union Jacks, for five years, and now they hang them from every window, and wave them, on the harbour walls, and the quays, and cheer, and sing, and the church bells ring, and the people climb onto the lorries, and hug the soldiers, and give them flowers.

[pause]

The soldiers give the children sweets, and chocolate, and oranges, and the children, many of whom have never seen an orange, look at them, and do not know what they are, or how to eat them.

[pause]

{img:island-children-receiving-oranges-soldiers-1945-harbour-sunshine}
In Sark, the smallest of the islands, the Dame of Sark, the lady who rules the island, under the Crown, has remained, all through the war, and has stood up to the German officers, with great dignity, and courage. When the British arrive, she greets them, at the harbour.

[long-pause]

Many of the children of the islands had been sent away to England, in 1940, just before the Germans came, and have grown up there, far from their families, for five years. Now, slowly, they come home, on the ships, grown taller, and older, and speaking with English accents, to parents who have waited for them, every day.

[pause]

And every year, on the ninth of May, the people of Jersey, and Guernsey, still celebrate Liberation Day, with parades, and music, and flags, and services, and the old songs, in the harbours, where, in 1945, the ships came in.

[long-pause]

{img:british-soldiers-burma-jungle-camp-1945-listening-radio-tents}
On VE Day, many of the soldiers of Britain, and the Commonwealth, are not at home, but far away.

[pause]

In Italy, and Germany, and Austria, and Holland, and Norway, they celebrate where they are: in their camps, and the towns they have reached, with whatever they can find. They fire flares into the sky, and drink to the end of the war, and sing, and write letters home, saying: it's over, I'll be home soon.

[long-pause]

But for many of them, it is not over. Far away, in Asia, the war still goes on.

[pause]

In Burma, in the jungle, and the mountains, and the rivers, the British Fourteenth Army, with soldiers from Britain, and India, and Nepal, and East Africa, and West Africa, is still fighting. They call themselves the Forgotten Army, because, they say, nobody at home thinks about them, or knows where they are. Just a few days before VE Day, at the beginning of May, they reached the great city of Rangoon, in time for the rains.

[pause]

On VE Day, they hear the news, on their radios, in the heat, and the rain, in the jungle, and they are glad, of course. But for them, the war is not finished yet. They will have to wait, until the middle of August, before the war in the Far East, too, comes to its end.

[long-pause]

And in the Pacific Ocean, the soldiers, and sailors, and airmen of America, and Australia, and New Zealand, and Britain, are still fighting, on the islands, and the seas, far from home.

[pause]

And so, the soldiers far away do not celebrate in the same way as the people at home. They think of home, and of their families, celebrating in the streets, and of when they will see them again.

[pause]

{img:soldier-writing-letter-home-tent-lamp-night-1945-photo-family}
And they write letters. Letters are everything, for the soldiers, and for their families.

[long-pause]

In the war, letters are written by the millions, every day, and travel thousands of miles, by ship, and by plane. To save space, and weight, some letters are written on special forms, called airgraphs, which are photographed, onto tiny pieces of film, and flown home, and printed out again, at the other end, small, and grey, but readable.

[pause]

The soldiers write about the weather, and the food, and their friends, and the places they have seen, but not about where they are, or what they are doing, because that is secret. And the letters are read, by the censors, before they are sent, and some words are cut out, or blacked out.

[pause]

{img:mother-reading-letter-kitchen-window-1945-smiling-children}
And at home, the families write back, about the children, and the garden, and the neighbours, and the news of the street, and the price of things, and the dog, and the weather, and they send photographs, of the children, who are growing up, so fast, without their fathers.

[long-pause]

On VE Day, and in the days after, the letters go back and forth, full of joy, and hope: Not long now. I'll be home soon. Keep the light on for me.

[long-pause]

{img:soldiers-returning-home-railway-station-families-embracing-1945}
In the weeks, and months, after VE Day, the soldiers begin to come home.

[pause]

It does not happen all at once. There are millions of them, and they must wait for their turn, according to how old they are, and how long they have served. Some come home in the summer of 1945. Others not until 1946, or even 1947. But week by week, month by month, the trains, and the ships, bring them home.

[long-pause]

At the railway stations, the families wait, on the platforms, with flags, and flowers, and the children, in their best clothes, and when the train comes in, and the doors open, and the soldiers climb down, with their kit bags, on their shoulders, there are shouts, and tears, and hugs, and kisses.

[pause]

Some of the children do not know their fathers. They were babies, when their fathers went away, and now they are five, or six, years old, and they stand, shyly, behind their mothers, looking at this tall stranger, in uniform, who picks them up, and holds them, and cries.

[pause]

When the soldiers leave the army, they are given a set of new clothes, called a demob suit: a suit, and a shirt, and a tie, and shoes, and a raincoat, and a hat. And they go home, in their new suits, to start their ordinary lives again.

[long-pause]

It is not always easy. The soldiers have been away for years, and have seen, and done, things that they do not want to talk about. Their wives have run the homes, and worked, in the factories, and on the farms, and have become strong, and independent. The children have grown up. Everyone has changed. And it takes time, and patience, and love, to become a family again.

[pause]

And the prisoners of war come home, too: the men who were captured, and spent years, in prison camps, in Germany, and Italy, and Poland. They come home, thin, and tired, many of them in the great bombers of the Royal Air Force, which fly them home, from Belgium, and Germany, by the thousand, in the spring of 1945, landing at the airfields of England, where they are given tea, and sandwiches, and a warm welcome, and a telegram home.

[pause]

{img:evacuee-children-returning-london-station-labels-suitcases-mothers}
And the children come home too.

[long-pause]

At the beginning of the war, in 1939, hundreds of thousands of children were sent away, from the cities, to the countryside, to keep them safe from the bombs. They were called evacuees. They went by train, with labels, tied to their coats, with their names, and a little suitcase, and their gas masks, in cardboard boxes, and lived with families they had never met, in villages, and farms, and country towns.

[pause]

Many of them had already come home, during the war. But in 1945, the last of them come back, to the cities, to their families. Some of them have been away for five years, and have grown up in the country, with its fields, and animals, and woods, and do not want to leave. Some have been very happy, and stay friends, with their country families, all their lives. Others have been homesick, every day, and are overjoyed to be back.

[pause]

{img:family-reunited-front-door-terraced-house-1945-father-home-children}
And so, one by one, the families of Britain come together again, around the kitchen tables, and the fires, in the little houses, and the flats, and the farms, and begin, slowly, to build their lives again.

[long-pause]

{img:english-seaside-summer-1945-families-beach-deckchairs-pier}
And then, it is the summer of 1945.

[pause]

For six years, the beaches of the south, and east, of England have been closed. They were covered with barbed wire, and concrete blocks, and mines, and gun emplacements, and the piers had gaps cut in them, so that they could not be used by an enemy landing. Nobody could go to the seaside.

[long-pause]

Now, slowly, the soldiers, and the engineers, clear the beaches, and take away the barbed wire, and the concrete, and make them safe again. And in the summer of 1945, the people of Britain go to the seaside, again, for the first time in years.

[pause]

They go by train, from the cities, in their thousands, packed into the carriages, with their buckets, and spades, and their sandwiches, and their flasks of tea, to Brighton, and Margate, and Southend, and Blackpool, and Scarborough, and Weymouth, and all the other seaside towns.

[pause]

{img:children-building-sandcastles-beach-1945-buckets-spades-sunshine}
And on the beaches, the children, many of whom have never seen the sea, run down, to the water's edge, and paddle, and squeal, at the cold water, and build sandcastles, and dig holes, and look for crabs, in the rock pools, and ride on the donkeys, along the sand.

[long-pause]

The grown-ups sit in deckchairs, with their trousers rolled up, and handkerchiefs, knotted, on their heads, against the sun, and sleep, and read the newspapers, and eat ice cream, when they can find it, and fish and chips, wrapped in newspaper, which were never rationed, all through the war.

[pause]

The piers are mended, and the funfairs open again, and the Punch and Judy shows, and the bandstands, with the brass bands, playing, on the promenade, and the seaside landladies, in their boarding houses, put up their signs again: Vacancies. Sea views.

[pause]

{img:seaside-promenade-evening-1945-lights-bandstand-couples-walking}
In the evenings, the families walk along the promenade, in the warm summer air, and watch the sun set, over the sea, and the lights come on, along the seafront, and on the pier, strings of coloured bulbs, reflected in the water, which, only a few months before, had to be dark.

[long-pause]

And the soldiers, coming home, on leave, and for good, go to the seaside, too, with their wives, and their children, and sit on the beach, in the sunshine, and listen to the waves, and the gulls, and the children laughing, and feel, for the first time in years, that they can rest.

[pause]

It is, many people remember later, a golden summer, the summer of 1945: the first summer of peace, with the long, light evenings, and the beaches, and the gardens, full of roses, and the families, together again.

[long-pause]

{img:rebuilding-london-1946-workers-bricks-scaffolding-new-houses}
After the celebrations, the world begins, slowly, to rebuild.

[pause]

In June 1945, in San Francisco, in America, the representatives of fifty countries meet, and sign the Charter of the United Nations, a new organisation, to keep the peace, between the nations of the world, so that there will never again be a war like this one.

[long-pause]

In Britain, in July 1945, there is a general election, the first for ten years. And to the surprise of many people, Mr Churchill, the great war leader, loses, and a new government, led by Clement Attlee, a quiet, modest man, comes to power, promising to build a better Britain, after the war: with new houses, and work, and help for those in need, and free health care, for everyone. In 1948, the National Health Service begins.

[pause]

In the middle of August, 1945, the war in Asia, too, comes to its end. And there are celebrations again, in the streets, on VJ Day, Victory over Japan Day, and at last, the Second World War is over, everywhere.

[pause]

But it is not easy, after the war. In Britain, rationing goes on, for years, and in some ways becomes even stricter. Bread is rationed, for the first time, in 1946. The last rationing, of meat, does not end until 1954, nine years after VE Day.

[long-pause]

There is a great shortage of houses, because so many have been destroyed, by the bombs. So the government builds prefabs: little prefabricated houses, made in factories, in pieces, and put together, in a day or two, on the bombed sites, and in the parks, with a kitchen, and a bathroom, and a little garden. They were meant to last ten years. Some of them are still lived in, today, and their families love them.

[pause]

In Europe, the destruction is much greater. Whole cities lie in ruins. Millions of people have lost their homes, and are far from where they were born, and must find their way home, or find a new home, somewhere else. In 1948, America begins the Marshall Plan, sending money, and food, and machines, to help the countries of Europe rebuild.

[pause]

{img:european-city-rebuilding-1950s-new-buildings-old-church-tower}
And slowly, over many years, the cities are rebuilt. The rubble is cleared away. The bridges are repaired. The churches, and the cathedrals, and the town halls, and the old streets, are rebuilt, sometimes stone by stone, as they were before. And new towns, and new buildings, rise up, in the gaps.

[long-pause]

And slowly, the countries of Europe, which fought each other, in two great wars, in thirty years, begin to work together, to trade with each other, and to make sure that there will never be another war between them. In 1951, six countries, France, and West Germany, and Italy, and the Netherlands, and Belgium, and Luxembourg, join together, to share their coal, and steel. From this small beginning, grows, over many years, the European Union.

[pause]

{img:children-1950s-street-playing-new-housing-peaceful-afternoon}
And the children of the war, who stood at the windows, on VE Day, and saw the lights come on, for the first time, grow up, in a Europe at peace, and go to school, and work, and marry, and have children of their own, and grandchildren, and great-grandchildren, and tell them, sometimes, about the day the war in Europe ended, and the street parties, and the jellies, and the bonfires, and the lights.

[long-pause]

{img:cenotaph-whitehall-poppies-wreaths-quiet-morning-today}
Today, more than eighty years later, VE Day is still remembered.

[pause]

Every year, on the eighth of May, in Britain, and France, and many other countries, there are services, and ceremonies, and moments of silence, and the flags fly. In France, the eighth of May is a public holiday, Victoire 1945. In Russia, and many other countries, the ninth of May is Victory Day. In the Netherlands, there is the fourth of May, the evening of remembrance, and the fifth of May, Liberation Day. In Denmark, the candles are lit, in the windows.

[long-pause]

On the great anniversaries, the celebrations are larger. In 1995, fifty years after VE Day, the Queen, Elizabeth, who had danced in the crowds as a young princess, stood again, on the balcony of Buckingham Palace, with her mother, and her sister, Princess Margaret, and looked out, over the crowds, in the Mall, as they had done in 1945, and Vera Lynn sang, again, for the crowds.

[pause]

In 2020, on the seventy-fifth anniversary, people stayed at home, because of the pandemic, but they had little street parties, anyway, on their doorsteps, and in their front gardens, with bunting, and cups of tea, and sang We'll Meet Again, from their windows. And in 2025, on the eightieth anniversary, there were parades, and flypasts, and street parties, once again, and church bells, and beacons, lit across the country.

[pause]

{img:ve-day-anniversary-street-party-today-bunting-families-tea}
The people who remember VE Day are now very old. Most of them have gone. But their memories have been written down, and recorded, and filmed, and their photographs kept, in albums, and boxes, and archives, so that we can still see them, and hear their voices: the children in their paper hats, the sailors in the fountains, the princesses in the crowds, the families around the wireless.

[long-pause]

And in many places, quiet reminders of those days remain: the memorials, in every town, and village, with their lists of names, and the poppies, and the wreaths, laid in front of them, every year. The little red schoolhouse, in Reims. The riverbank, at Torgau. The tulips, in Ottawa. The candles, in the windows of Denmark.

[pause]

So we leave the spring of 1945, tonight, as the evening falls, on the eighth of May.

[pause]

{img:english-street-dusk-1945-lights-in-windows-bunting-quiet-after-party}
The street parties are ending. The tables are being carried back into the houses, and the chairs, and the children have gone to bed, tired, and full of cake, with their paper hats still on, and are falling asleep, to the sound of the singing, still drifting in, through the open windows. The bunting is stirring, gently, in the evening breeze. And all along the street, for the first time in six years, the windows are glowing, with light, warm, and golden, without any curtains drawn, and the street lamps are shining, softly, on the pavements. And in the sky above, the stars are coming out, one by one, over a quiet, peaceful sky.
