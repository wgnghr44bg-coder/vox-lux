Tonight we go more than eighty years back. We go to the spring of 1945, to the last days of the Second World War in Europe.

[long-pause]

It is the beginning of May. In the villages of England, the apple trees are in blossom. Bluebells cover the woods. The swallows have come back from Africa. They fly low over the fields and the village greens. In the towns and cities the trees are green again. A tall pink flower grows in the ruins where houses used to stand.

[pause]

And everyone is waiting.

[pause]

If you like calm stories from the past, like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

For nearly six years, Europe has been at war. Millions of men and women have worn uniforms, far from home. Cities have been bombed. Families have been split up. Children have been sent away to the countryside to be safe. Food and clothes and almost everything else have been rationed. That means each person may only buy a small amount. The nights have been dark, with no lights allowed. And the days have been full of worry, waiting for news and letters.

[pause]

{img:family-gathered-around-wireless-radio-1945-living-room-listening}
But now, in the spring of 1945, everyone knows the end is coming. Every evening, families sit around the radio to hear the news. And every day the news is better. The armies are moving forward from the west and from the east. Town after town, country after country, is being set free.

[long-pause]

The question is no longer if the war in Europe will end. The question is when. Next week? Tomorrow? Today? People buy flags and put them away, ready. They save a little of their food for a party. And they wait.

[pause]

Tonight we will follow those last days, gently and from far away. We will visit the hungry cities of Holland, where food falls from the sky. We will go to a river in Germany, where two armies meet. We will see candles lit in the windows of Denmark. We will visit a little red school in France in the middle of the night. And we will be in London and Paris and New York and Moscow on the days of joy.

[pause]

{img:london-street-1945-bunting-flags-terraced-houses-spring-sunshine}
We will not stay with the battles or the hard things. We will stay with the people. The ordinary people who lived through it all. In May 1945, after six long years, they came out into the streets and the sunshine. They danced and sang and cried. They hugged strangers. Because the war in Europe was over.

[long-pause]

So let us go back to the beginning of May 1945. The world is tired and hopeful and waiting, with blossom on the trees.

[long-pause]

{img:allied-soldiers-crossing-rhine-pontoon-bridge-spring-1945-river}
In the spring of 1945, the war in Europe is coming to its end.

[pause]

In the west, the Allied armies have crossed the great river Rhine. These are the Americans and the British, the Canadians and the French and many others. They are moving east across Germany, from town to town. They are moving faster than anyone expected.

[long-pause]

In the east, the great armies of the Soviet Union are moving west toward Berlin. In the south, in Italy, other Allied armies are moving north toward the mountains.

[pause]

Everywhere, the war is ending, place by place. In town after town, people come up out of their cellars. They hang white sheets from the windows. And the soldiers move on. Prisoners of war are set free from the camps. They are thin and tired and overjoyed. And they begin the long journey home.

[pause]

The world is also learning of terrible things that happened in some camps during the war. We remember them still, with great sorrow. And we will leave them here, gently, in silence.

[long-pause]

In April, in America, President Roosevelt passes away suddenly. He has led his country for twelve years, through nearly the whole war. People all over the world are sad. The new President is Harry Truman, a modest man from Missouri.

[pause]

At the end of April, the war is ending in Berlin too. On the first of May, German radio says that Hitler is dead. And the next day, Berlin gives up.

[pause]

{img:village-people-waving-white-sheets-windows-soldiers-passing-1945}
Day after day, the German armies give up in different places. In Italy. In the north of Germany and in Holland and Denmark. And a few days later, in the south.

[long-pause]

All over Europe, people are waiting for the very last surrender. The end of the war.

[pause]

In Britain and America, people are waiting for the big announcement. The day will be called VE Day. Victory in Europe Day. It has been talked about for weeks. The newspapers are full of plans. The shops are full of flags. The churches are ready to ring their bells. But nobody knows the date yet.

[pause]

{img:english-shop-window-1945-union-jacks-bunting-for-sale-queue}
And in the meantime, life goes on. People go to work. They go to the shops with their ration books and queue for food. They go home and listen to the news. They go to bed and wonder. Will it be tomorrow?

[long-pause]

{img:lancaster-bombers-low-over-dutch-fields-dropping-food-sacks-1945}
In the west of the Netherlands, in the spring of 1945, the people are hungry.

[pause]

The war has not ended there yet. The great Dutch cities are still cut off. Amsterdam and Rotterdam, The Hague and Utrecht. Through the long cold winter there has been very little food and very little fuel.

[long-pause]

The Dutch call it the Hunger Winter. People have eaten sugar beets and even tulip bulbs. They have walked for days into the countryside. They pushed prams and old bicycles with no tyres. They tried to swap their silver and their sheets with farmers, for a few potatoes. Many thousands of people did not live through that winter. And the food is still running out. How much longer can they hold on?

[pause]

Then, at the end of April, something wonderful happens.

[pause]

On Sunday the twenty-ninth of April, just after midday, the people of Holland hear the roar of many plane engines. They come in from the sea. People have heard that sound so many times in the war. Their hearts sink. But this time it is different.

[long-pause]

Big bombers with four engines are flying in. They fly very low over the fields and towns. They are so low that people can see the faces of the crews, waving at the windows. And from the open doors under the planes there falls not bombs, but food.

[pause]

Sacks and bags and boxes come tumbling down. Flour and dried egg and margarine. Sugar and tea and chocolate. Tins of meat and beans and biscuits. They land on fields and race tracks marked with big white crosses.

[pause]

{img:food-sacks-falling-on-meadow-dutch-children-running-windmill}
It is called Operation Manna. Manna is the bread that fell from heaven in the Bible. The Allies have made a deal with the German commanders. The Germans have promised not to shoot at the planes.

[long-pause]

The people of Holland run out into the streets. They climb onto rooftops and church towers. They wave sheets and flags and handkerchiefs and orange cloths. They cry and cheer. Some have painted words on the roofs and the fields in big letters. Thank you, boys. Many thanks.

[pause]

The next day the American bombers join in. For ten days, hundreds of planes fly every day from England over the sea. Altogether they drop more than eleven thousand tons of food. Then food comes by lorry and by ship as well.

[pause]

{img:dutch-family-kitchen-1945-white-bread-loaf-table-children-joy}
The Dutch collect the food and share it out to the people. And for the first time in months, the children of Amsterdam and Rotterdam eat white bread. And they taste chocolate.

[long-pause]

Many of the airmen said, for the rest of their lives, that this was the best thing they did in the whole war. And many Dutch children never forgot it either. The great planes flying low over the tulip fields in the spring sun. And the food falling from the sky, like a gift.

[long-pause]

{img:lancaster-crew-seven-airmen-standing-by-aircraft-airfield-spring-1945}
Let us go to an airfield in the flat east of England, on a spring morning at the end of April.

[pause]

The great bombers stand around the edge of the field. They are black and huge, with four engines and long wings and tall tails. Their crews gather beside them in flying jackets and boots, in the cool morning air.

[long-pause]

Each plane has a crew of seven. There is the pilot. Beside him sits the engineer, who watches the engines and the fuel. The navigator sits at a little table with his maps. Another man works the radio. One man lies in the glass nose at the front, to aim the drop. And two gunners sit in small glass bubbles, one on top and one at the very back.

[pause]

They are very young. The pilot is twenty-two. The back gunner is nineteen. They come from all over Britain, and from Canada and Australia and New Zealand. They have flown many times at night over Germany. But this morning they will fly in daylight, and very low. And they will drop food.

[pause]

{img:ground-crew-loading-food-sacks-into-bomb-bay-lancaster-morning}
The ground crew have worked all night. Many of them are women. They have filled the planes with sacks of flour and sugar, tinned meat, dried egg and chocolate.

[long-pause]

Some airmen have added a little something of their own. Bars of chocolate from their own rations. Sweets. They tie them to small parachutes made from handkerchiefs. They add notes for the children who will find them.

[pause]

The engines start one by one, with a cough and a roar. The great planes roll slowly to the runway. One after another they thunder down it and lift heavily into the air. Then they turn east, over the sea. There is one worry on every man's mind. Will the Germans keep their promise? Or will someone fire?

[pause]

{img:lancaster-cockpit-view-low-over-dutch-polders-canals-windmills}
They fly very low over the North Sea, just above the waves. Then the coast of Holland appears ahead. A long pale line of dunes and beaches. Then the flat green land, with canals and ditches shining in the sun. Windmills and church towers. Red roofs. And tulip fields in great stripes of red and yellow and pink and white.

[long-pause]

And everywhere there are people. On roofs and in streets and fields. Thousands of people, waving sheets and towels and flags and their arms. The crew can see their faces looking up. Nobody fires. Someone has written on a field in big white letters. Thanks.

[pause]

As they come over the drop zone, the man in the nose calls out. He presses his button. The doors under the plane open. And the sacks fall away behind them onto the grass.

[pause]

{img:lancaster-banking-over-crowd-waving-field-tulips-sunlit}
The pilot tips the great plane gently around, so the crew can look down. They see the people running out onto the field. They wave back from the windows. And some of them have tears in their eyes.

[long-pause]

Then they turn for home, back over the sea. On the way home, the crew are very quiet. That evening they talk about it. And the next day they fly again.

[pause]

Many years later, when they were old men, some of them went back to Holland. They were welcomed as heroes. The children who had waved in the fields were old now too. And they still remembered.

[long-pause]

{img:elbe-river-torgau-broken-bridge-spring-1945-soldiers-meeting}
On the twenty-fifth of April, in the middle of Germany, something happens that people have waited years for.

[pause]

The two great armies, coming from the west and from the east, meet.

[long-pause]

It happens beside the river Elbe. It is a wide slow grey-green river. It flows through flat green country, past meadows and willows and small villages with red roofs.

[pause]

Small groups of American soldiers have been sent ahead. They are looking for the Soviet army. People say it is very close. The Americans drive slowly along the country roads in their jeeps. They look and they listen. Nobody is quite sure what will happen when they meet. Will the other side know who they are?

[pause]

Near the town of Torgau, by an old castle and a broken bridge, they see soldiers on the other side of the river. These soldiers wear different uniforms, with red stars on their caps. The Americans wave and shout. They fire coloured flares to show who they are. And the soldiers on the other side wave back.

[long-pause]

An American lieutenant and his men climb across the twisted steel of the broken bridge. On the other side they meet the Soviet soldiers. They shake hands and hug and laugh. They slap each other on the back. They cannot speak each other's language. But it does not matter.

[pause]

They share what they have. Cigarettes and chocolate, bread and sausage. They swap badges and buttons and addresses. Someone brings out an accordion. And they sing and dance on the river bank in the spring sun.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-singing-accordion-riverbank-celebration-willows-spring}
Photographers take pictures of the soldiers shaking hands on the broken bridge. The pictures are printed in newspapers all over the world. In London and New York and Moscow, the news is announced at the same time. The armies have met. Germany is cut in two. The end is near.

[long-pause]

That day, the soldiers by the river make a promise to each other. They will work for peace and friendship between their countries. So that there will never be a war like this again.

[pause]

It was a hard promise to keep. In the years that followed, the world became divided again. But the soldiers remembered. Some of them met again years later, as old men, on the banks of the same river. They shook hands again and laid flowers by the water.

[pause]

{img:torgau-memorial-river-elbe-flowers-quiet-today-old-castle}
Today at Torgau there is a memorial beside the river. Every year on the twenty-fifth of April, people still come there. They remember the day two armies met in the spring sunshine, and shook hands, and sang by the river.

[long-pause]

{img:british-housewife-queue-grocer-ration-books-1945-shop-counter}
Let us go to an ordinary street in an English town, in the spring of 1945.

[pause]

It is a street of small brick houses in a row. Each has a front door and a window and a tiny front garden. Some of the houses were damaged by bombs earlier in the war. Their windows are still covered with boards. At the end of the street there is a gap where two houses used to be. Children play there among the bricks and the weeds.

[long-pause]

In one house lives a family. A mother, a grandmother and three children. The father is far away, in the army in Italy. The eldest son is in the navy. They have not seen them for more than two years. But they write letters every week. Sometimes the letters come in a bundle, many at once. Sometimes none come for weeks. Then the mother checks the door every morning. In the evening she reads the letters aloud at the kitchen table. She keeps them in a box tied with a ribbon.

[pause]

Every morning the mother goes to the shops with her ration books. She stands in the queue at the grocer and the butcher and the baker.

[pause]

Each person may only have a little every week. A bit of bacon and butter and cheese. A bit of sugar and tea. One egg, if there are any. A little meat. And a few sweets each month. Bread and potatoes are not rationed. The mothers of Britain have become very clever at making meals out of very little. Carrot cake. Potato pie. Even pretend banana, made from a white root vegetable.

[long-pause]

The children have not seen a real banana or an orange for years. The youngest has never seen one at all. There is dried egg in tins from America. It is made into omelettes and scrambled eggs and cakes. And everyone grows vegetables, in their gardens and even in the parks. Many families keep hens in the back yard.

[pause]

Clothes are rationed too. Everyone mends and patches and makes do. The children wear their older brothers' and sisters' clothes. Mothers make dresses from old curtains.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-bomb-site-street-1945-terraced-houses-sunny}
And for nearly six years there has been the blackout. Every evening at dusk, every window must be covered with thick black curtains. Not a chink of light may show, from the street or from the sky. The street lamps have been dark. Cars have driven with covered lights. People have walked home through dark streets with small torches pointed at the ground.

[long-pause]

But now the lights are slowly coming back. At the end of April, the great clock faces of Big Ben in London were lit again. It was the first time in more than five years. Crowds gathered in the evening to watch. And when the lights came on, they cheered.

[pause]

The children who were born during the war have never seen a street lit up at night. They look out of their windows in wonder.

[pause]

{img:big-ben-clock-face-lit-again-evening-crowd-watching-1945}
In the evening the family sits by the fire and listens to the radio. The grandmother knits. The children do their homework. The mother writes a letter to the father in Italy. She tells him the lilac is out in the back yard. She tells him the children are well. And she writes that perhaps, soon, he will be home.

[long-pause]

{img:bbc-wireless-set-wooden-cabinet-glowing-dial-1945-evening-lamp}
In the spring of 1945, the most important thing in every house is the radio. People call it the wireless.

[pause]

It is a big wooden box on a shelf by the fire. It has a round speaker covered with cloth, and knobs, and a glowing dial. On the dial are the names of faraway places. London and Paris and Moscow. When you switch it on, it takes a little while to warm up. Then it hums and crackles. And the voices come.

[long-pause]

For six years, the radio has been the voice of the war. Every evening at nine o'clock, the whole country stops to listen to the news. The news readers have calm steady voices. During the war they started to say their names. That way people would know it really was the BBC, and not the enemy pretending.

[pause]

On the radio, people have heard the King. They have heard Mr Churchill, the Prime Minister, speaking to the country in the darkest moments of the war. They gathered around to listen together, at home and in factories and in shelters.

[pause]

{img:factory-workers-listening-radio-loudspeaker-canteen-1940s}
But the radio has also been a comfort and a friend. It has made people laugh and sing and forget for a while.

[long-pause]

There are funny shows with silly characters. Everyone repeats their jokes the next day at work and at school. There is music at lunchtime, played from factory canteens. There is music while you work, to keep the workers cheerful. And every afternoon there is a children's hour, with stories and plays. At the end, a friendly voice says goodnight, children, everywhere.

[pause]

And there are the singers. The favourite of all is Vera Lynn, a young woman from London. She has a clear warm voice. The soldiers far away love her songs, because they remind them of home. One of them is called We'll Meet Again.

[pause]

{img:young-woman-singer-microphone-1940s-studio-band-warm-light}
In the occupied countries of Europe, people have listened to the BBC in secret. It was forbidden. If they were caught, they could be arrested. So they listened under blankets, in attics and in cellars, with the sound turned very low. In Holland and Denmark and France and Norway, they listened for news. They listened for coded messages. And they listened for the voices of their own queens and kings, speaking to them from London.

[long-pause]

And in the first days of May, the radio is on all day long in every home. People are waiting for one piece of news. The news that the war in Europe is over. Then they can hang out their flags at last, and ring the bells.

[long-pause]

{img:land-girls-1945-working-field-hay-cart-green-jumpers-breeches}
During the war, the women of Britain have done almost everything.

[pause]

When the men went away to fight, someone had to do their work. In the factories and on the farms. On the railways and the buses. In offices and hospitals. And so the women did it.

[long-pause]

In the factories they built aeroplanes and ships and tanks. They made parachutes and uniforms. They worked long shifts, day and night, in overalls. They tied scarves on their heads to keep their hair out of the machines. They drove buses and lorries and ambulances. They worked as engineers and welders and crane drivers.

[pause]

On the farms there was the Women's Land Army. Tens of thousands of young women went to work on farms. Many came from the cities and had never seen a cow before. Now they ploughed and milked and brought in the harvest. They fed the country. They wore green jumpers and brown trousers and felt hats. People called them the land girls.

[pause]

{img:women-factory-workers-headscarves-aircraft-assembly-line-1940s}
There were women in the army and navy and air force too. Princess Elizabeth served as well. Women worked as drivers and mechanics and radio operators. Some stood around big map tables. They moved little markers to show where the planes were. Others worked the big searchlights at night.

[long-pause]

And at home there were the mothers and grandmothers. They kept the families going through the bombing and the rationing and the worry. They queued for food and mended clothes and cared for the children and the old. Many worked part time too. Some served tea and soup and comfort to people who had lost their homes in the bombing.

[pause]

On VE Day, the women of Britain celebrate with everyone else in the streets. Some wear their uniforms. Some wear their overalls. Some wear their best dresses.

[pause]

{img:wrens-waaf-women-uniform-celebrating-ve-day-arms-linked-street}
After the war, things are not quite the same for them. Many give up their jobs when the men come home. But they have learned something. They can do things that people thought only men could do. They do not forget it. And their daughters and granddaughters do not forget it either.

[long-pause]

Many years later, a memorial was set up in London for the women of the war. It is a tall bronze statue. All around it hang uniforms and overalls and work clothes, on pegs. It looks as if the women have just taken them off at the end of the war, and gone home.

[long-pause]

{img:canadian-soldiers-jeep-dutch-town-crowds-orange-flags-may-1945}
On the evening of the fourth of May, the news comes over the radio, in Dutch, from London. The German forces in the Netherlands and Denmark and the north of Germany have given up.

[pause]

That afternoon, on a windy hill in the north of Germany, the British commander Montgomery sat in a tent. There was a plain army table covered with a blanket. The German commanders came in and signed. They used an ordinary pen. Montgomery said it could have been bought in any shop for a few pennies. The surrender will begin the next morning at eight o'clock.

[long-pause]

In Holland, people can hardly believe it. That evening they come out into the streets in the twilight. They stand and talk. Is it really true? Some of them dare to put a light in the window for the first time in five years. They tear down the black paper from the glass.

[pause]

The next day, the fifth of May, is Liberation Day. In a town in the middle of the country, a Canadian general meets a German general. They meet in a hotel called the Hotel of the World. They talk about how the German soldiers in Holland will lay down their weapons. The next day it is signed.

[pause]

{img:dutch-girls-orange-ribbons-dancing-street-canadian-soldiers-may}
In the days that follow, Canadian soldiers drive into the great cities of the west. Utrecht and Amsterdam and Rotterdam and The Hague. They come in jeeps and lorries and tanks. And the whole population comes out to meet them.

[long-pause]

The streets are full of people cheering and singing and dancing. They wave the red, white and blue Dutch flag. It has been hidden away for five years. And they wear orange, the colour of the royal family. Orange ribbons and orange sashes and orange paper hats. Anything orange they can find. Children climb onto the tanks. Girls kiss the soldiers. Old men cry. The church bells ring.

[pause]

The Canadian soldiers give away everything they have. Cigarettes and chocolate and chewing gum and white bread. The thin hungry people crowd around them. Many soldiers remembered these as the happiest days of the war.

[pause]

{img:dutch-street-party-1945-long-table-orange-decorations-children}
In every street, in every town and village, there are street parties. People carry their tables and chairs out of the houses. Orange paper and flags hang between the houses. There is music and dancing in the squares. There are games and races for the children. And everyone sings the national anthem together.

[long-pause]

Not long after, Queen Wilhelmina comes home from England. All through the war, she spoke to her people on the radio from London. The rest of the royal family comes home too, from Canada. Princess Juliana had lived there with her children during the war.

[pause]

She was so grateful to Canada. Canada had kept her family safe. And Canadian soldiers had freed her country. So after the war, she sent a gift of a hundred thousand tulip bulbs to Canada's capital. Every year since then, the Netherlands has sent tulips. Today there is a great tulip festival there every spring, with a million tulips by the river.

[pause]

{img:ottawa-tulip-festival-spring-parliament-hill-tulip-beds-today}
And every year on the fifth of May, the Dutch celebrate Liberation Day with festivals and music all over the country. The night before, on the fourth of May at eight o'clock, the whole country stops for two minutes of silence. They remember all those who died.

[long-pause]

The trains stop. The traffic stops. People in the streets and houses and shops stand still, in silence. And then the next morning the flags come out, and the music begins.

[long-pause]

{img:danish-window-candles-lit-evening-may-1945-copenhagen-street}
In Denmark too, on the evening of the fourth of May, people are listening to the radio.

[pause]

At twenty-five minutes to nine in the evening, a voice from London stops the programme. There is a special message. The German forces in Denmark have given up. Denmark is free.

[long-pause]

All over the country, people jump up from their chairs. They hug each other and cry and laugh. And they run out into the streets.

[pause]

And then they do something beautiful. They tear down the black paper and the dark curtains from their windows. For five years they have had to keep them closed every evening. And they put candles in the windows. Lit candles. To show that the darkness is over and the light has come back.

[pause]

{img:copenhagen-crowds-street-cheering-danish-flags-evening-1945}
That evening, all over Denmark, every window glows with the soft light of candles in the spring twilight. People walk through the streets singing. They look at the lights in the windows, all the way along. Thousands and thousands of little flames, flickering.

[long-pause]

The next day, the red and white Danish flag flies everywhere. From every flagpole and every window. People celebrate in the streets and parks and squares. British soldiers arrive and are greeted with flowers.

[pause]

And every year since then, on the evening of the fourth of May, the Danes put candles in their windows again. They remember the evening the news came and the darkness ended.

[pause]

{img:quiet-danish-street-today-candles-windows-evening-may-blue-dusk}
If you walk through a Danish town on that evening, even today, you will see them. Little candles glowing in the windows of houses and flats and shops. One after another, all along the street, in the blue spring dusk. A quiet thank you, and a memory, of the day the lights came back.

[long-pause]

In Norway too, the war ends a few days later, peacefully. On Norway's national day in May, people celebrate as they have not been able to for five years. Children march in parades with flags and music. And in June, the king comes home from England by ship. He sails into the harbour of Oslo on the same day he left, five years before. And the whole city comes out to welcome him.

[long-pause]

{img:reims-red-brick-schoolhouse-headquarters-night-1945-lights-windows}
In the city of Reims, in the north of France, there is a school. It is a long plain building of red brick with rows of windows, near the railway station.

[pause]

In the last months of the war, it has been used by the top Allied commander, the American General Eisenhower, and his staff. The classrooms have become offices. They are full of desks and maps and telephones and typewriters.

[long-pause]

On the night of the sixth of May, in one of those classrooms, a plain table has been set up. The walls are covered with maps showing where all the armies are. There are chairs and microphones and lights for the photographers. Everyone is waiting. Will the Germans really come? Will they really sign?

[pause]

At about half past two in the morning, a German general walks into the room. He sits down at the table. And he signs the surrender of all the German forces, everywhere. Then the Allied officers sign too.

[pause]

It is over in a few minutes. It is two forty-one in the morning.

[long-pause]

General Eisenhower does not come into the room. He waits in his office next door. When it is over, the German general is brought to him. Eisenhower asks him if he understands what he has signed. He says yes. And then he is taken away.

[pause]

Eisenhower's staff try to write a message to the governments in Washington and London. They try many grand long sentences. But in the end, Eisenhower writes it himself, very simply. The mission of this Allied force was fulfilled at 0241, local time, May 7th, 1945.

[pause]

{img:eisenhower-office-desk-pen-message-dawn-quiet-relief}
Then he goes to bed for a few hours.

[long-pause]

The next day, the Soviet leader Stalin asks for a second signing in Berlin. He wants it signed in the German capital, in front of the Soviet commanders. Late that evening, in a quiet part of Berlin, the German commanders sign again.

[pause]

By the time it is finished, it is already past midnight in Moscow. It is the ninth of May. So in Russia and many countries of eastern Europe, Victory Day is still celebrated on the ninth of May.

[pause]

{img:reims-surrender-museum-map-room-preserved-maps-table-today}
The little red school in Reims is still there today. The map room has been kept exactly as it was that night. The maps are still on the walls. The table and the chairs are still there. It is now a museum. Visitors can stand in the quiet room and imagine that night, at two forty-one in the morning, when the war in Europe came to its end.

[long-pause]

{img:london-crowds-waiting-whitehall-may-7-1945-newspapers-rumours}
In London, on Monday the seventh of May, everybody knows something has happened. But nobody has told them for sure.

[pause]

The news from Reims is being kept secret for a day. It is meant to be announced at the same time in London and Washington and Moscow. But a news agency in America has already told the story. By the morning, rumours are all over London.

[long-pause]

All day, people wait. Crowds gather near the government buildings and outside Buckingham Palace and in the big squares. Office workers lean out of their windows. People buy every new newspaper, looking for the news. Some start to celebrate anyway. They throw paper out of office windows like snow. The flags come out on the buildings. The church bells are ready.

[pause]

But the hours pass, and nothing is announced. People are confused and a little impatient. Is it today? Is it not?

[pause]

In the shops, people buy flags. The government says people may buy red, white and blue cloth without using their clothing tickets. The shops sell out fast. Women sit at their sewing machines. They make flags and decorations out of anything they can find. Old dresses and curtains and scraps of cloth.

[long-pause]

At last, at about twenty to eight in the evening, a short message comes on the radio. Tomorrow will be Victory in Europe Day. It will be a holiday. The Prime Minister will speak at three o'clock in the afternoon. The King will speak at nine in the evening. And the day after will be a holiday too.

[pause]

So it is true. It is over. And tomorrow is the day.

[pause]

{img:family-kitchen-1945-evening-sewing-flags-children-excited-wireless}
That evening, in houses all over Britain, people get ready. Mothers take out the flags they have saved. The children help hang them from the windows and across the street, from house to house. Neighbours talk over the garden walls about tomorrow's party. Everyone gives a little of their food. A little sugar, some flour, some dried egg. They will make cakes and sandwiches and jellies for the children.

[long-pause]

And in the night, a great thunderstorm comes over London.

[pause]

Thunder rolls over the rooftops. Lightning flashes over the city. Heavy warm rain pours down. It washes the streets and the dust of the bombed buildings. And it soaks the flags hanging ready in the streets.

[pause]

{img:thunderstorm-over-london-night-lightning-rooftops-st-pauls-rain}
For six years, the sky over London has been full of danger at night. But tonight the flashes and rumbles are only lightning and thunder. People lie in their beds and listen to the rain. Some of them laugh. They say it is the sky's own fireworks, for the end of the war.

[long-pause]

In the morning the storm has passed. The sky is washed and clear. The sun shines warm on the wet streets. And the flags hanging from every window dry and lift in the breeze.

[pause]

It is the eighth of May, 1945. It is VE Day.

[long-pause]

{img:english-church-bell-tower-bell-ringers-ropes-sunlight-1945}
On VE Day, all over Britain, the church bells ring.

[pause]

For most of the war, the church bells were silent. Early in the war, people feared that an enemy army might land in Britain. So the government said the bells must not ring. They would only ring as a warning, if the enemy came. For years, the bells hung silent in their towers. On Sundays the churches were quiet.

[long-pause]

Later in the war, when the danger had passed, the bells were allowed to ring again on Sundays. But on VE Day they ring as they have never rung before.

[pause]

In every church tower in the country, the bell ringers climb the narrow winding stairs. At the top is a little room. Ropes hang down from the bells above. Each rope has a soft striped woollen grip, red and white and blue.

[pause]

{img:bell-ringers-chamber-circle-ropes-sallies-pulling-tower-window}
The ringers stand in a circle, each holding a rope. They pull. The great bells above them swing right round and ring out, one after another. Then the bells change places in the order, again and again. It is like a kind of maths, played on bells.

[long-pause]

Many of the ringers have been away at war. Some of the ringers are new and young. Some are very old and have rung since they were boys. And they ring and ring all day long. Their arms ache. They do not care.

[pause]

The sound of the bells spreads over the rooftops and the fields and the woods. One church answers another, from village to village, across the whole country. Wherever you are in Britain on VE Day, you can hear the bells.

[pause]

{img:village-church-ve-day-congregation-leaving-sunshine-bells-flags}
In London there are great church services to give thanks. People queue in long lines to go in. Saint Paul's Cathedral is full all day. All through the bombing of London, it stood. Fires burned all around it. But its great dome rose out of the smoke. It became a sign of hope for the whole country in the darkest days.

[long-pause]

In the evening, as the sun sets, the bells ring again. The people of the villages walk to church in the long golden light of a May evening. They sing the old hymns. Then they come out again into the dusk. The bells are ringing. And on the hills, the bonfires begin to glow.

[long-pause]

{img:ve-day-trafalgar-square-crowds-flags-sunshine-nelsons-column-1945}
On the morning of the eighth of May, all of London comes into the centre of the city.

[pause]

They come by train and bus and bicycle and on foot. They come from every part of London and the towns around. They wear their best clothes and their uniforms. They carry flags and wear paper hats and red, white and blue ribbons in their hair.

[long-pause]

By midday the streets of the West End are packed with people. The streets are so full that the traffic cannot move. Buses and taxis are stuck in the crowds. People climb on their roofs.

[pause]

There are soldiers and sailors and airmen from Britain and America and Canada and Australia. From France and Poland and Norway and Holland and many other countries. There are women in uniform and nurses and land girls and factory girls. There are office workers and old men and grandmothers. And children ride on their fathers' shoulders.

[pause]

People climb up the lamp posts and the statues. They climb onto the great stone lions in Trafalgar Square. They jump into the fountains and splash about. They wave flags and sing and dance. They make long lines holding hands and dance through the streets and the crowds.

[long-pause]

Strangers hug each other and kiss each other and shake hands. Sailors dance with girls they have never met. Old ladies dance with soldiers. Everyone is smiling and laughing and singing the old songs.

[pause]

At three o'clock, the crowds go quiet. Loudspeakers have been put up in the streets and squares. In every house, the radio is on. And Mr Churchill speaks from Downing Street.

[pause]

{img:crowd-silent-listening-loudspeakers-whitehall-3pm-churchill-broadcast}
He tells them in his deep slow voice that the war in Europe is over. The fighting will officially end at one minute after midnight tonight. We may allow ourselves a short time of joy, he says. And at the end he says, long live the cause of freedom. God save the King.

[long-pause]

And the crowds cheer and cheer. The church bells ring out all over London. And the ships on the river sound their horns.

[pause]

Later that afternoon, Mr Churchill comes out onto a balcony above the crowd. The whole street below is packed from one end to the other. They cheer him and sing to him. He holds up his hand and makes his famous sign. Two fingers in the shape of a V, for victory. This is your victory, he tells them. And the crowd shouts back, no, it's yours.

[pause]

{img:churchill-balcony-whitehall-v-sign-crowd-below-flags-may-1945}
All through the afternoon and the evening, the crowds keep growing and celebrating in the sunshine.

[long-pause]

Many people who were there that day remembered it for the rest of their lives. The noise and the crowds and the joy. The feeling of being part of something enormous. And the strange wonderful feeling that tonight, nobody would be afraid of the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:buckingham-palace-balcony-royal-family-crowds-mall-may-1945}
In front of Buckingham Palace, on the afternoon of the eighth of May, the crowd is enormous.

[pause]

It fills the whole space in front of the palace. It stretches back all along the wide road under the trees. Tens of thousands of people wave flags and call out. We want the King! We want the King!

[long-pause]

The doors to the balcony open. The royal family come out. King George in his navy uniform. Queen Elizabeth in a pale blue dress and hat. And their two daughters. Princess Elizabeth is nineteen. She wears her army uniform. She has been training as a driver and a mechanic. Princess Margaret is fourteen.

[pause]

The crowd roars. They wave, and the royal family waves back. They stand there for a few minutes, looking out over the great sea of people and flags. Then they go back in.

[pause]

But the crowd will not go away. They keep calling. So the royal family comes out again and again. Eight times during the afternoon and evening. Once, Mr Churchill comes out with them. He stands beside the King. And the crowd cheers louder than ever.

[long-pause]

The King and Queen stayed in London all through the war. Even when Buckingham Palace was bombed. They visited the bombed streets and the hospitals and the factories. And the people loved them for it.

[pause]

And now, this evening, the two princesses ask their parents a question. May they go out into the crowds themselves, just this once? The King and Queen think about it. And they say yes.

[pause]

{img:princess-elizabeth-margaret-incognito-crowd-mall-evening-officers}
At about ten o'clock in the evening, the two princesses slip out of the palace by a side door. A small group of young officers and friends go with them, to look after them. They go out into the crowds. Nobody knows who they are.

[long-pause]

Princess Elizabeth pulls her cap down low over her eyes so no one will recognise her. Will anyone notice? Nobody does. They walk through the streets with everyone else, arm in arm, in the dark and the lights. They join in the singing and the dancing among crowds of strangers.

[pause]

Many years later, when she was Queen, she remembered that night. She said she remembered lines of strangers linking arms and walking down the street. All of us, she said, just swept along on a tide of happiness and relief.

[pause]

{img:crowds-linking-arms-whitehall-night-singing-floodlights-1945}
At the end of the evening, the princesses stand in the crowd outside Buckingham Palace. They call out with all the other people. We want the King! We want the Queen! And then they watch from the crowd as their own parents come out onto the balcony and wave.

[long-pause]

It was one of the most special nights of her life, Princess Elizabeth said later. At last, after midnight, the two sisters slip back in through the side door and go to bed. And outside, the crowds go on singing under the bright lights, until the early hours of the morning.

[long-pause]

{img:king-george-vi-microphone-desk-broadcast-palace-evening-1945}
At nine o'clock in the evening, on the eighth of May, the King speaks to his people on the radio.

[pause]

All over Britain and all over the world, people gather around their radios. In Canada and Australia and India and Africa. On ships at sea, and in soldiers' camps far away. Everyone goes quiet to listen.

[long-pause]

King George is a shy and modest man. He never expected to be king. He became king when his older brother gave up the throne. And all his life he has had a stammer. Words get stuck and will not come out. For him, speaking to millions of people on the radio is very hard.

[pause]

For years he has worked with a speech teacher from Australia, called Lionel Logue. He has learned to breathe and to speak slowly and calmly through the hard words. All through the war he has spoken to his people at the big moments. Slowly and carefully. And the people love him for it, because they know how hard it is for him.

[pause]

{img:families-listening-king-speech-radio-1945-living-room-quiet}
Tonight, Lionel Logue is with him at the palace as he speaks. Every word matters tonight. Will the words come?

[long-pause]

The King speaks slowly and gently. And the words come. He gives thanks for the end of the war in Europe. He remembers those who will not come home. He remembers those who have suffered and lost so much. He thanks the people for all they have done. And he reminds them that the war is not yet over everywhere. Far away in the east, it still goes on.

[pause]

He says they must now go forward and build a better world and a lasting peace. That way, all they have been through will not have been for nothing.

[pause]

{img:village-church-bells-ringing-evening-ve-day-bell-ringers-rope}
In homes and pubs and streets, people listen quietly. When he finishes, they stand up for the national anthem.

[long-pause]

When it is over, Lionel Logue writes in his diary. The King spoke beautifully, he writes. And he was very proud of him.

[pause]

And then, outside, the celebrations begin again. They go on into the night, as the lights come on all over Britain, for the first time in nearly six years.

[long-pause]

{img:churchill-cigar-bowler-hat-v-sign-car-crowds-may-1945}
On VE Day, after the King, the man the crowds most want to see is Winston Churchill.

[pause]

He is seventy years old. He is short and round, with a round pink face and a big cigar. He wears a spotted bow tie and a black hat. He became Prime Minister exactly five years ago. The war was going very badly then. And he has led the country through all of it.

[long-pause]

In the summer of 1940, Britain stood alone. Many feared an enemy army would land any day. Churchill spoke to the people on the radio and in Parliament. His words were big and rolling and stirring. They gave people courage and hope. We shall fight on the beaches, he said. We shall never surrender.

[pause]

All through the war he worked day and night. Sometimes he worked from secret rooms under the ground in London. He even slept there sometimes. He travelled all over the world to meet the other leaders. He visited the bombed streets and the soldiers. And people cheered him wherever he went.

[pause]

{img:cabinet-war-rooms-underground-map-room-telephones-lamps}
He worked very hard. He took naps in the afternoon. Then he worked late into the night, often in his dressing gown, walking up and down with his cigar. He loved painting and building brick walls in his garden. He loved his cats and his poodle. And he loved his wife Clementine. He wrote her loving letters all his life.

[long-pause]

On VE Day he has lunch with the King at the palace. Then he drives back through the crowds. They are so thick that his car can hardly move. The people push it the last part of the way. He sits up high on the back of the open car. He waves and makes his V sign. And the crowds cheer and sing.

[pause]

At three o'clock he speaks to the nation. Then he goes to Parliament and gives the news there too. The members cheer and wave their papers. Then he leads them all across the road to a church to give thanks.

[pause]

{img:whitehall-ministry-of-health-balcony-evening-floodlit-crowd-singing}
In the evening he comes out again onto the balcony, in the bright lights. The crowd sings a great old song, and he waves his arms like a conductor. Then he speaks to them again. In all our long history, he says, we have never seen a greater day than this.

[long-pause]

Two months later there will be an election. The people will vote for a new government. And Churchill will no longer be Prime Minister. He is hurt and surprised. But many who voted against him still love him and are grateful. They say he was the right man for the war. But for the peace they want something new.

[pause]

He will become Prime Minister again a few years later. He will live to be ninety. And when he passes away, the whole country will mourn him. As his coffin passes on a boat along the Thames, the tall cranes along the river will slowly bow down.

[long-pause]

{img:st-pauls-cathedral-floodlit-night-1945-searchlights-v-sky-crowds}
On the evening of VE Day, as the sun goes down, something happens in London that many children have never seen in their lives.

[pause]

The lights come on.

[long-pause]

For nearly six years, London has been dark at night. Now, at dusk, the great buildings are lit up for the first time since 1939. Saint Paul's Cathedral, its great dome glowing white and gold against the dark sky. Buckingham Palace. The Houses of Parliament and Big Ben. Nelson's Column and the Tower of London.

[pause]

And in the sky above the city, the searchlights move. For years they swept the sky at night, looking for enemy planes. Now they make patterns of light. They cross and wave and form great V signs, high over the rooftops, like a great silent firework show.

[pause]

The ships on the river turn on their lights and fire rockets. In the parks, people light bonfires and dance around them. In the streets, people take down their black curtains. They leave their windows bare and their lights on. Golden warm light pours out onto the streets.

[long-pause]

A little girl born during the war stands at the window with her mother. She looks out at the street. All the windows are lit. The street lamps are glowing. What is it, she asks. She has never seen a street lit up at night.

[pause]

All over the country there are bonfires. On the hills and the village greens and the bombed sites. People pile up wood and old furniture and the hated black curtains. They burn them joyfully and dance around the flames.

[pause]

{img:village-green-bonfire-night-ve-day-people-dancing-around-fire}
People let off the fireworks they have been saving. Rockets go up and burst into showers of coloured stars over the rooftops. And the children cheer.

[long-pause]

In London the crowds go on all night. People dance in Piccadilly Circus. The famous statue there was taken away during the war, to keep it safe. Only its boarded-up base is left. People climb on it anyway. In Trafalgar Square the singing goes on until dawn.

[pause]

At last, in the early hours, people walk home through the lit streets. They are tired and happy and hoarse from singing. Some sleep in the parks under the trees, wrapped in their coats and flags. The lights still shine on Saint Paul's. The searchlights slowly fade. And the sky in the east grows pale.

[pause]

{img:dawn-after-ve-day-london-park-people-sleeping-grass-flags-quiet}
And for the first time in six years, nobody anywhere in Britain is afraid of the night sky.

[long-pause]

{img:children-1945-waving-flags-front-gate-terraced-house-sunny-may}
For the children of 1945, VE Day is the most exciting day of their lives.

[pause]

Many of them cannot remember a time before the war. They have grown up with the war all around them. With dark nights and warning sirens and shelters. With gas masks and ration books. And with their fathers far away.

[long-pause]

They have slept in shelters in the back garden. These were little curved huts of metal, half buried in the earth. Inside were bunk beds. They were damp and cold and smelled of earth. When the sirens went at night, the family hurried out to them. Others slept under a steel table in the kitchen. And in London many slept on the platforms of the underground railway, in long rows, with thousands of other families.

[pause]

They have collected little bits of metal from the bombs and swapped them at school, like marbles. They have collected old pots and pans and paper to help the war. They have carried their gas masks in cardboard boxes on a string everywhere they went. At school they practised putting them on. And they made rude noises with them.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-anderson-shelter-garden-vegetables-1945-spring}
They have never tasted a banana or seen a lemon. Sweets have been rationed. Ice cream has been almost impossible to find. Their toys have been home made. Wooden aeroplanes and rag dolls and knitted animals made from old jumpers.

[long-pause]

And now the war is over. And suddenly everything is exciting and new.

[pause]

On VE Day, they may stay up late, much later than ever before. They go out into the streets at night to see the bonfires and fireworks and lights. They eat jelly and cake at the street party until they cannot eat any more. They wave little paper flags and wear paper hats. They run around among the grown-ups. And for once, the grown-ups are all laughing and singing and dancing. Nobody tells them to be quiet.

[pause]

{img:children-sitting-on-wall-watching-fireworks-night-1945-faces-glow}
Years later, when they are old, people ask them what they remember. The lights, they say. Seeing the street lamps on for the first time. Or the bonfire at the end of our street, and my father, home on leave, lifting me onto his shoulders. Or the jelly. Or my mother crying and laughing at the same time. I did not understand why.

[long-pause]

One of them, a little girl, remembered one thing all her life. She lay in bed that night, very late, too excited to sleep. She looked up at the ceiling of her bedroom. For the first time, the light of the street lamp outside shone through the gap in the curtains. It made a soft golden stripe across the ceiling. And she lay and looked at it. She listened to the singing in the street below. Until she fell asleep.

[long-pause]

{img:thames-ships-midnight-may-8-1945-sirens-lights-river-floodlit}
At one minute past midnight, on the night of the eighth to the ninth of May, the war in Europe officially ends.

[pause]

All day long people have been celebrating. But it is only now, in the middle of the night, that the guns everywhere in Europe must go silent.

[long-pause]

In London the crowds are still in the streets, under the bright lights. As midnight comes, Big Ben strikes twelve. Slowly, deep and clear, over the crowds and the river. And the whole of London listens.

[pause]

And then, on the Thames and in every harbour around the coasts of Britain, the ships sound their horns and whistles all together. Tugs and barges and steamers and warships. Many of them play the letter V in Morse code. Three short blasts and one long one. Dot, dot, dot, dash. V for victory.

[pause]

{img:harbour-night-ships-lights-sirens-searchlights-celebration-1945}
It is the same rhythm as the first notes of a famous piece by Beethoven. All through the war, the BBC played those notes at the start of its broadcasts to the occupied countries. It was a secret sign of hope. Da, da, da, dum.

[long-pause]

On the warships, sailors fire rockets and turn on their lights. The harbours glow and flash red and green and white. And the water reflects it all.

[pause]

Then slowly the sound of the horns dies away. And the night grows quieter.

[long-pause]

All over Europe, on all the battlefields, soldiers hear that it is over. Some of them cheer. But many of them simply sit quietly in the dark. They look at the stars. They think about home and about their friends. And they listen to the silence.

[pause]

For the first time in nearly six years, there is no war in Europe.

[pause]

{img:soldier-sitting-quiet-night-stars-camp-fire-after-war-1945}
And in the little houses and flats and farms all over Europe, mothers and grandmothers and children are asleep. Or they lie awake, listening to the singing and the horns far away. And then to the silence.

[long-pause]

It is a strange and wonderful silence. The silence of a night with nothing to fear. All over Europe, people turn over in their beds. They pull up their blankets. And at last they sleep, deeply and peacefully, until the morning.

[long-pause]

{img:ve-day-street-party-long-tables-children-paper-hats-bunting-1945}
In the streets of Britain, VE Day is the day of the street parties.

[pause]

In thousands of streets all over the country, neighbours bring out their kitchen tables and chairs. They put them in a long row down the middle of the street. They cover them with white tablecloths and sheets.

[long-pause]

Across the street, from house to house, they hang flags and paper chains, red and white and blue. They put pictures of the King and Queen and Mr Churchill in the windows. And on the walls they hang hand-painted signs. Welcome Home. Victory. Well Done, Boys.

[pause]

Then the food comes out. For weeks, everyone has saved a little from their rations for the party. There are sandwiches of fish paste and jam and egg. There are sausage rolls and little cakes and jam tarts. There are jellies in every colour, wobbling on their plates. And there is a big cake with icing, made with sugar the whole street has saved.

[pause]

The children sit at the long tables in their best clothes and paper hats. They eat and eat, more than they have eaten for years. The mothers and grandmothers pour the tea from big brown teapots and pass round the plates.

[long-pause]

Then there are games and races for the children down the street. Running races and sack races and egg and spoon races. Who will keep the egg on the spoon all the way to the end? The whole street cheers. There are prizes. A sixpence, or a little toy, or a bag of sweets.

[pause]

Someone wheels a piano out of one of the houses into the street. They play all afternoon and evening. Everyone sings the old songs and dances in the street. Old people and young ones. Mothers and children and soldiers home on leave.

[pause]

{img:piano-in-street-neighbours-singing-dancing-evening-terraced-houses}
There is a dressing up parade for the children. They wear costumes made of crepe paper and old clothes. Some dress as soldiers and sailors and nurses. Some dress as the King. And one boy dresses as Mr Churchill, with a cigar made of rolled-up paper.

[long-pause]

In the evening there is a bonfire at the end of the street, on the bomb site. The old black curtains are thrown on it and burned. And everyone cheers.

[pause]

People kept the photographs of that day all their lives. In them we see the long tables down the middle of the little streets. The children in their paper hats with their mouths full of cake. The mothers in their aprons. The flags over their heads. And everyone smiling.

[pause]

{img:old-photograph-style-street-party-group-smiling-1945-black-white-feel}
And yet, at many of the tables, there are empty chairs. Fathers and sons and brothers are still far away. They will not come home for many months. And some will not come home at all. Many of the people at the parties think of them quietly, in the middle of the singing and the laughter.

[long-pause]

So VE Day is a day of joy and relief. And of sadness too, all mixed together. And people remember it all their lives as one of the most important days they ever lived through.

[long-pause]

{img:english-village-ve-day-green-flags-cottages-maypole-sunshine-1945}
Let us go to a little village in the English countryside on VE Day.

[pause]

It has old stone cottages with straw roofs and gardens full of flowers. They stand around a village green. There is a church and a pub and a pond with ducks. And there is a stone memorial from the first great war. On it are the names of the young men of the village who did not come home.

[long-pause]

The village has been fairly quiet during the war, far from the bombs. Many of the young men are away in the army. Land girls have come to work on the farms. And some children from London have lived here, sent away to be safe. They went to the village school. They learned to milk cows and find eggs and climb trees. Some have stayed all through the war.

[pause]

On the morning of VE Day the church bells ring. The whole village comes out onto the green. Flags fly from the church tower and the pub and the cottages. Bunting hangs across the lane and between the trees.

[pause]

In the afternoon there is a tea for the children in the village hall. There are sandwiches and jelly and a big cake with VICTORY written on it in icing. Then there are games on the green. Races for children and grown-ups. A tug of war between the men and the women. The rope goes this way, then that way. And the women win.

[long-pause]

The old men sit on the bench outside the pub in the sunshine. They watch. And they remember the end of the other war, when they were young and came home from France.

[pause]

The vicar holds a service of thanks in the church. It is fuller than it has been for years. People sing and pray. They read the names of the young men of the village who died in this war. Later, those names will be added to the old memorial on the green.

[pause]

{img:village-pub-evening-ve-day-singing-piano-lanterns-garden}
In the evening the pub is crowded. The doors are open and the singing spills out onto the green. Someone plays the piano and someone plays the accordion. People dance on the grass in the long evening light.

[long-pause]

When it gets dark, there is a bonfire on the hill above the village. The whole village climbs up to watch. From the top they can see other bonfires burning on other hills, far away. One after another, across the dark countryside. Like a chain of lights from village to village.

[pause]

The children and the land girls and the old men and the mothers stand around the fire in the dark. Their faces glow. They sing and watch the sparks fly up into the sky and the stars.

[pause]

{img:hilltop-bonfire-night-villagers-silhouette-distant-fires-hills}
Then they walk home slowly down the hill along the lanes in the dark. The hedges smell of white blossom. The owls are calling. The singing still comes faintly from the pub. And they go to bed tired and happy in the quiet village, at the end of the war.

[long-pause]

{img:paris-champs-elysees-ve-day-crowds-arc-de-triomphe-flags-1945}
On the eighth of May, it is not only London that celebrates.

[pause]

In Paris, the bells of all the churches ring. Crowds pour into the streets, onto the great avenue and around the famous arch. Flags of France and Britain and America and the Soviet Union wave everywhere. General de Gaulle speaks on the radio. People sing the French anthem and dance in the streets and squares all night long. They climb onto the jeeps of the American soldiers.

[long-pause]

Paris was freed the summer before. So the people of Paris have already had their great day. But now the whole war in Europe is over. And they celebrate again. The lights of Paris shine. The great monuments are lit up for the first time in years.

[pause]

In New York, it is morning when the news comes. President Truman speaks on the radio at nine o'clock. It is his birthday. He tells the people of America that the war in Europe is over. But the war in the Pacific still goes on, he says. There is still work to do.

[pause]

But in New York, the crowds gather anyway in Times Square, hundreds of thousands of them. Office workers in the tall buildings throw paper out of the windows. Torn paper and long paper strips fall like snow on the streets below. People cheer and dance and kiss. And the ships in the harbour sound their whistles.

[long-pause]

In Moscow the news comes in the early hours of the next morning. The radio announces it at about two o'clock at night. People wake up and get dressed. They pour into the streets and into Red Square, in the dark and the dawn. They sing and cheer and cry.

[pause]

All day long the streets of Moscow are full of people. They pick up soldiers walking past and toss them up into the air, again and again, and catch them, laughing. In the evening there is a huge salute of guns and fireworks and searchlights over the Kremlin and the river.

[pause]

{img:moscow-red-square-victory-fireworks-night-may-9-1945-crowds-kremlin}
The Soviet Union has suffered more in the war than any other country. Millions of its people have died. Its cities and villages have been destroyed. So the joy in Moscow is mixed with deep sorrow too, for all that has been lost.

[long-pause]

And all over the world there are bells and flags and singing and prayers of thanks. In Canada and Australia and New Zealand. In South Africa and India. In the countries of Europe, freed one by one. In every place where people have waited for this day.

[pause]

{img:small-town-church-thanksgiving-service-may-1945-congregation-flowers}
In churches and chapels and synagogues all over the world, there are services of thanks. People come in their thousands. They give thanks. And they pray quietly for those they have lost, and for peace.

[long-pause]

{img:london-morning-after-ve-day-streets-paper-flags-quiet-sweepers}
The next day, the ninth of May, is a holiday too in Britain.

[pause]

In the morning, London is quiet. The streets are covered with paper and streamers and flags. People are sleeping in the parks and on benches and on the steps of buildings, wrapped in their flags.

[long-pause]

The street sweepers come out early with their brooms and carts. They begin to clear up. The buses and trains start running again. Slowly people wake up and stretch and go home for breakfast.

[pause]

But by the afternoon the crowds are back. They gather outside the palace and in Trafalgar Square. The celebrations begin again. A little quieter and a little more tired. But still happy.

[pause]

{img:king-queen-car-driving-east-end-crowds-cheering-1945}
On this day, the King and Queen drive through the East End of London in an open car. They drive through the streets that were bombed so badly during the war. The people of the East End come out in their thousands to cheer. They wave their flags from windows and doorways.

[long-pause]

In the streets the parties go on. Children who had a party yesterday have another one today, with the food that was left over. The grown-ups sit outside on chairs on the pavement. They talk and drink tea and rest in the sunshine.

[pause]

In many homes, people sit quietly and think about the six years of war. About the people they have lost. About those who are still far away. They write letters. They read the newspapers, with their great headlines. VICTORY. IT'S OVER. And they look at the photos of the crowds from the day before.

[pause]

{img:family-garden-afternoon-tea-1945-deckchairs-newspaper-headlines}
People fold those newspapers and put them away in drawers and boxes and attics. They keep them as a memory of these days. Many are still there today, yellow and fragile. Grandchildren find them when they clear out old houses.

[long-pause]

On the tenth of May, people go back to work. The factories start again, and the offices and the shops and the schools. Life goes on. There is still rationing and there are still queues. There are still soldiers far away. And there is still much to do.

[pause]

But it is different now. The fear has gone. In the evenings, as the sun sets, the lights come on in the windows and the street lamps. And nobody draws the black curtains any more.

[long-pause]

{img:guernsey-st-peter-port-harbour-may-1945-crowds-british-ships}
On VE Day, one small part of Britain is still waiting. The Channel Islands.

[pause]

These are little islands in the sea between England and France. They lie much closer to France. They are called Jersey and Guernsey and Alderney and Sark. They belong to the British Crown. And they are the only British land that the German army took during the war. For nearly five years, the Germans have been in charge there.

[long-pause]

For five years, the islanders have been cut off from Britain and from the world. In the last winter it was even worse. Food ran very short. People were hungry and cold, with no fuel and almost nothing to eat. Then, just before Christmas, a Red Cross ship came with food parcels. It came back several times. And it saved many lives.

[pause]

On the afternoon of VE Day, the islanders listen to Mr Churchill on hidden radios. Radios are still forbidden on the islands. They hold their breath. And they hear him say it. Our dear Channel Islands are also to be freed today.

[pause]

{img:jersey-islanders-waving-flags-harbour-wall-royal-navy-destroyer}
The next morning, two British warships arrive off the islands. The German commanders come aboard and sign the surrender. And British soldiers come ashore at the island harbours.

[long-pause]

The whole population comes down to the harbours to meet them. For five years they have kept their British flags hidden. Now they hang them from every window. They wave them on the harbour walls. They cheer and sing. The church bells ring. People climb onto the lorries and hug the soldiers and give them flowers.

[pause]

The soldiers give the children sweets and chocolate and oranges. Many of the children have never seen an orange. They look at it. They do not know what it is, or how to eat it.

[pause]

{img:island-children-receiving-oranges-soldiers-1945-harbour-sunshine}
On Sark, the smallest island, there is a lady who rules the island for the Crown. She stayed all through the war. She stood up to the German officers with great calm and courage. Now she greets the British soldiers at the harbour.

[long-pause]

In 1940, just before the Germans came, many island children were sent away to England. For five years they have grown up there, far from their families. Now, slowly, they come home on the ships. They are taller and older. They speak with English accents. And their parents, who have waited for them every day, are standing on the quay.

[pause]

Every year on the ninth of May, the people of Jersey and Guernsey still celebrate Liberation Day. There are parades and music and flags, in the harbours where the ships came in.

[long-pause]

{img:british-soldiers-burma-jungle-camp-1945-listening-radio-tents}
On VE Day, many soldiers of Britain and the Commonwealth are not at home. They are far away.

[pause]

In Italy and Germany and Holland and Norway, they celebrate where they are. In their camps and in the towns they have reached. They fire flares into the sky and sing. And they write letters home. It's over, they write. I'll be home soon.

[long-pause]

But for many of them it is not over. Far away in Asia, the war still goes on.

[pause]

In Burma, in the jungle and the mountains, a British army is still fighting. It has soldiers from Britain and India and Nepal and Africa. They call themselves the Forgotten Army. Nobody at home thinks about us, they say. Nobody even knows where we are. Just a few days ago, they reached a great city there, just before the heavy rains began.

[pause]

On VE Day they hear the news on their radios, in the heat and the rain. They are glad, of course. But for them the war is not finished yet. They will have to wait until the middle of August. Only then will the war in the east come to its end too.

[long-pause]

In the Pacific Ocean, too, soldiers and sailors from America and Australia and New Zealand and Britain are still fighting, far from home.

[pause]

So the soldiers far away do not celebrate the way people do at home. They think of home. They picture their families celebrating in the streets. And they wonder when they will see them again.

[pause]

{img:soldier-writing-letter-home-tent-lamp-night-1945-photo-family}
And they write letters. For the soldiers and their families, letters are everything.

[long-pause]

In the war, millions of letters are written every day. They travel thousands of miles by ship and plane. To save space, some letters are photographed onto tiny bits of film. The film is flown home. There the letters are printed out again, small and grey, but you can still read them.

[pause]

The soldiers write about the weather and the food and their friends. But they may not write where they are or what they are doing. That is secret. Before a letter is sent, someone reads it. Sometimes words are cut out or blacked out.

[pause]

{img:mother-reading-letter-kitchen-window-1945-smiling-children}
At home, the families write back. About the children and the garden and the neighbours. About the dog and the weather. They send photos of the children, who are growing up so fast, without their fathers.

[long-pause]

On VE Day and in the days after, the letters go back and forth, full of joy and hope. Not long now. I'll be home soon. Keep the light on for me.

[long-pause]

{img:soldiers-returning-home-railway-station-families-embracing-1945}
In the weeks and months after VE Day, the soldiers begin to come home.

[pause]

It does not happen all at once. There are millions of them. They must wait their turn, depending on their age and how long they have served. Some come home in the summer of 1945. Others not until a year or two later. But week by week and month by month, the trains and ships bring them home.

[long-pause]

At the railway stations, families wait on the platforms with flags and flowers. The children wear their best clothes. Every time a train comes in, they look along the doors. Is he on this one? Then the doors open. Soldiers climb down with kit bags on their shoulders. There are shouts and tears and hugs and kisses.

[pause]

Some of the children do not know their fathers. They were babies when their fathers left. Now they are five or six. They stand shyly behind their mothers. They look at this tall stranger in uniform. He picks them up and holds them. And he cries.

[pause]

When the soldiers leave the army, they are given new clothes. A suit and shirt and tie, shoes and a raincoat and a hat. They go home in their new suits to start their ordinary lives again.

[long-pause]

It is not always easy. The soldiers have been away for years. They have seen things they do not want to talk about. Their wives have run the homes and worked in factories and on farms. They have become strong and independent. The children have grown up. Everyone has changed. It takes time and patience and love to become a family again.

[pause]

The prisoners of war come home too. These are men who were captured and spent years in prison camps. They come home thin and tired. Many fly home in the great bombers, thousands of them, landing at English airfields. They are given tea and sandwiches and a warm welcome. And a telegram goes to their families.

[pause]

{img:evacuee-children-returning-london-station-labels-suitcases-mothers}
And the children come home too.

[long-pause]

At the start of the war, hundreds of thousands of children were sent away from the cities. They went to the countryside to be safe from the bombs. They travelled by train with name labels tied to their coats. They each had a little suitcase and a gas mask in a cardboard box. They lived with families they had never met before.

[pause]

Many came home during the war. But now the last of them come back. Some have been away for five years. They have grown up in the country, with fields and animals and woods. Some do not want to leave. Some were very happy and stay friends with their country families all their lives. Others were homesick every single day. They are overjoyed to be back.

[pause]

{img:family-reunited-front-door-terraced-house-1945-father-home-children}
And so, one by one, the families of Britain come together again. They sit around kitchen tables and fires, in little houses and flats and farms. And slowly they begin to build their lives again.

[long-pause]

{img:english-seaside-summer-1945-families-beach-deckchairs-pier}
And then it is the summer of 1945.

[pause]

For six years, the beaches of the south and east of England have been closed. They were covered with barbed wire and concrete blocks. Gaps were cut in the piers so an enemy could not use them. Nobody could go to the seaside.

[long-pause]

Now, slowly, soldiers and engineers clear the beaches. They take away the wire and the concrete. They check every metre of sand. They make the beaches safe again. And in the summer of 1945, the people of Britain go to the seaside for the first time in years.

[pause]

They go by train from the cities in their thousands. The carriages are packed. They bring buckets and spades and sandwiches and flasks of tea. They go to all the seaside towns along the coast.

[pause]

{img:children-building-sandcastles-beach-1945-buckets-spades-sunshine}
On the beaches, many children see the sea for the first time. They run down to the water's edge. They paddle and squeal at the cold water. They build sandcastles and dig holes. They look for crabs in the rock pools. And they ride donkeys along the sand.

[long-pause]

The grown-ups sit in deckchairs with their trousers rolled up. Some tie knotted handkerchiefs on their heads against the sun. They sleep and read the newspaper. They eat ice cream when they can find it. And they eat fish and chips wrapped in paper. Fish and chips were never rationed, all through the war.

[pause]

The piers are mended. The funfairs open again, and the puppet shows. Brass bands play on the seafront. And the seaside landladies put up their signs again. Rooms free. Sea views.

[pause]

{img:seaside-promenade-evening-1945-lights-bandstand-couples-walking}
In the evenings, families walk along the seafront in the warm summer air. They watch the sun set over the sea. Then the lights come on along the front and on the pier. Strings of coloured bulbs shine in the water. Only a few months ago, all of this had to be dark.

[long-pause]

The soldiers come home on leave, and for good. They go to the seaside too, with their wives and children. They sit on the beach in the sunshine. They listen to the waves and the gulls and the children laughing. And for the first time in years, they feel they can rest.

[pause]

Many people later remembered it as a golden summer. The first summer of peace. With long light evenings, beaches and gardens full of roses, and families together again.

[long-pause]

{img:rebuilding-london-1946-workers-bricks-scaffolding-new-houses}
After the celebrations, the world slowly begins to rebuild.

[pause]

In June 1945, people from fifty countries meet in San Francisco, in America. They sign the plan for a new organisation, the United Nations. Its job is to keep the peace between the nations of the world. So that there will never be a war like this again.

[long-pause]

In Britain there is an election in July, the first for ten years. To the surprise of many, Mr Churchill loses. A new government comes in, led by a quiet modest man called Clement Attlee. It promises to build a better Britain. With new houses and work and help for those in need. And free health care for everyone. A few years later, that health service begins.

[pause]

In the middle of August, the war in Asia comes to its end too. There are celebrations in the streets again. And at last the Second World War is over everywhere.

[pause]

But life after the war is not easy. In Britain, rationing goes on for years. In some ways it gets even stricter. The last rationing, of meat, will not end until nine years after VE Day.

[long-pause]

There are not enough houses, because so many were destroyed by bombs. So the government builds little houses in factories, in pieces. They are put together in a day or two on the bombed sites. Each one has a kitchen and a bathroom and a small garden. They were meant to last ten years. Some are still lived in today. Their families love them.

[pause]

In Europe the damage is much greater. Whole cities lie in ruins. Millions of people have lost their homes. They are far from where they were born. They must find their way home, or find a new home somewhere else. America sends money and food and machines to help Europe rebuild.

[pause]

{img:european-city-rebuilding-1950s-new-buildings-old-church-tower}
And slowly, over many years, the cities are rebuilt. The rubble is cleared away. The bridges are repaired. Churches and town halls and old streets are rebuilt, sometimes stone by stone, just as they were. And new buildings rise up in the gaps.

[long-pause]

And slowly the countries of Europe begin to work together. They fought each other in two great wars in thirty years. Now they start to trade with each other. They want to make sure there will never be another war between them. A few years after the war, six countries agree to share their coal and steel. From that small beginning, over many years, the European Union grows.

[pause]

{img:children-1950s-street-playing-new-housing-peaceful-afternoon}
And the children of the war grow up in a Europe at peace. They are the same children who stood at their windows on VE Day and saw the lights come on. They go to school and work and marry. They have children and grandchildren of their own. And sometimes they tell them about the day the war in Europe ended. About the street parties and the jellies and the bonfires. And the lights.

[long-pause]

{img:cenotaph-whitehall-poppies-wreaths-quiet-morning-today}
Today, more than eighty years later, VE Day is still remembered.

[pause]

Every year on the eighth of May, there are ceremonies and moments of silence in Britain and France and many other countries. In France it is a holiday. In Russia and many other countries, the ninth of May is Victory Day. In the Netherlands there is the evening of remembrance on the fourth of May, and Liberation Day on the fifth. And in Denmark, candles are lit in the windows.

[long-pause]

On the big anniversaries, the celebrations are larger. Fifty years after VE Day, the Queen stood again on the balcony of Buckingham Palace. She was the same Elizabeth who had danced in the crowds as a young princess. Her mother and her sister stood beside her. They looked out over the crowds, just as they had in 1945. And Vera Lynn sang again for the crowds.

[pause]

Seventy-five years after VE Day, people had to stay at home because of an illness going round the world. But they had little parties anyway, on their doorsteps and in their front gardens. There was bunting and cups of tea. And people sang We'll Meet Again from their windows. Eighty years after, there were parades and street parties once again. Church bells rang. And fires were lit on hills across the country.

[pause]

{img:ve-day-anniversary-street-party-today-bunting-families-tea}
The people who remember VE Day are very old now. Most of them have gone. But their memories have been written down and recorded and filmed. Their photos are kept in albums and boxes. So we can still see them and hear their voices. The children in their paper hats. The sailors in the fountains. The princesses in the crowds. The families around the radio.

[long-pause]

In many places, quiet reminders of those days remain. The memorials in every town and village, with their lists of names, and the red poppies laid in front of them every year. The little red school in Reims. The river bank at Torgau. The tulips in Canada. The candles in the windows of Denmark.

[pause]

So we leave the spring of 1945 tonight, as the evening falls on the eighth of May.

[pause]

{img:english-street-dusk-1945-lights-in-windows-bunting-quiet-after-party}
The street parties are ending. The tables and chairs are carried back into the houses. The children have gone to bed, tired and full of cake. Some still have their paper hats on. They fall asleep to the singing that drifts in through the open windows. The bunting stirs gently in the evening breeze. And all along the street, for the first time in six years, the windows are glowing. Warm golden light, with no curtains drawn. The street lamps shine softly on the pavements. And in the sky above, the stars come out one by one, over a quiet and peaceful sky.

[long-pause]
