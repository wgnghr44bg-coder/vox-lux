In front of Buckingham Palace, on the afternoon of the eighth of May, the crowd is enormous.

It fills the whole space, in front of the palace, around the great white Victoria Memorial, and stretches back, all the way along the Mall, under the trees, toward Admiralty Arch, tens of thousands of people, waving flags, and calling: We want the King! We want the King!

--

And the doors onto the balcony open, and the royal family come out: King George the Sixth, in his naval uniform, and Queen Elizabeth, in a pale blue dress, and hat, and their two daughters, Princess Elizabeth, who is nineteen, in her uniform of the Auxiliary Territorial Service, the ATS, where she has been training, as a driver, and a mechanic, and Princess Margaret, who is fourteen.

The crowd roars. They wave, and the royal family waves back. And they stand there, for a few minutes, looking out over the great sea of people, and the flags, and then they go back in.

But the crowd will not go away. They keep calling for them. And so, the royal family come out onto the balcony, again, and again, eight times, during the afternoon, and the evening. Once, Mr Churchill comes out with them, and stands beside the King, and the crowd cheers louder than ever.

--

The King, and the Queen, have stayed in London, all through the war, even when Buckingham Palace was bombed, in 1940. The Queen said, at the time, that she was glad they had been bombed, because now she could look the East End in the face. They visited the bombed streets, and the hospitals, and the factories, and the people loved them for it.

And now, on this evening, the two princesses ask their parents if they can go out, into the crowds, themselves, just this once. And the King, and the Queen, say yes.

{img:princess-elizabeth-margaret-incognito-crowd-mall-evening-officers}
So, at about ten o'clock in the evening, the two princesses slip out of the palace, by a side door, with a small group of young officers, and friends, to look after them, and go out, into the crowds, in the Mall, and Whitehall, and the West End, without anyone knowing who they are.

--

Princess Elizabeth pulls her uniform cap down low, over her eyes, so that no one will recognise her. And they walk, through the streets, with everyone else, arm in arm, in the dark, and the lights, and join in the singing, and the conga, and the hokey-cokey, among the crowds of strangers.

Many years later, when she was the Queen, she remembered that night, and said: I remember lines of unknown people linking arms and walking down Whitehall, all of us just swept along on a tide of happiness and relief.

{img:crowds-linking-arms-whitehall-night-singing-floodlights-1945}
At the end of the evening, the princesses stand in the crowd, outside Buckingham Palace, with all the other people, and call, with them: We want the King! We want the Queen! And then they watch, from the crowd, as their own parents come out onto the balcony, and wave.

--

It was, Princess Elizabeth said later, one of the most memorable nights of her life. And then, at last, after midnight, they slip back in, through the side door, into the palace, and go to bed, while outside, the crowds go on singing, under the floodlights, until the early hours of the morning.
