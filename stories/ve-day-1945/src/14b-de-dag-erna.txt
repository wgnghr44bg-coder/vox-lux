The next day, the ninth of May, is a holiday too in Britain.

In the morning, London is quiet. The streets are covered with paper and streamers and flags. People are sleeping in the parks and on benches and on the steps of buildings, wrapped in their flags.

--

The street sweepers come out early with their brooms and carts. They begin to clear up. The buses and trains start running again. Slowly people wake up and stretch and go home for breakfast.

But by the afternoon the crowds are back. They gather outside the palace and in Trafalgar Square. The celebrations begin again. A little quieter and a little more tired. But still happy.

{img:king-queen-car-driving-east-end-crowds-cheering-1945}
On this day, the King and Queen drive through the East End of London in an open car. They drive through the streets that were bombed so badly during the war. The people of the East End come out in their thousands to cheer. They wave their flags from windows and doorways.

--

In the streets the parties go on. Children who had a party yesterday have another one today, with the food that was left over. The grown-ups sit outside on chairs on the pavement. They talk and drink tea and rest in the sunshine.

In many homes, people sit quietly and think about the six years of war. About the people they have lost. About those who are still far away. They write letters. They read the newspapers, with their great headlines. VICTORY. IT'S OVER. And they look at the photos of the crowds from the day before.

{img:family-garden-afternoon-tea-1945-deckchairs-newspaper-headlines}
People fold those newspapers and put them away in drawers and boxes and attics. They keep them as a memory of these days. Many are still there today, yellow and fragile. Grandchildren find them when they clear out old houses.

--

On the tenth of May, people go back to work. The factories start again, and the offices and the shops and the schools. Life goes on. There is still rationing and there are still queues. There are still soldiers far away. And there is still much to do.

But it is different now. The fear has gone. In the evenings, as the sun sets, the lights come on in the windows and the street lamps. And nobody draws the black curtains any more.
