On VE Day, many of the soldiers of Britain, and the Commonwealth, are not at home, but far away.

In Italy, and Germany, and Austria, and Holland, and Norway, they celebrate where they are: in their camps, and the towns they have reached, with whatever they can find. They fire flares into the sky, and drink to the end of the war, and sing, and write letters home, saying: it's over, I'll be home soon.

--

But for many of them, it is not over. Far away, in Asia, the war still goes on.

In Burma, in the jungle, and the mountains, and the rivers, the British Fourteenth Army, with soldiers from Britain, and India, and Nepal, and East Africa, and West Africa, is still fighting. They call themselves the Forgotten Army, because, they say, nobody at home thinks about them, or knows where they are. Just a few days before VE Day, at the beginning of May, they reached the great city of Rangoon, in time for the rains.

On VE Day, they hear the news, on their radios, in the heat, and the rain, in the jungle, and they are glad, of course. But for them, the war is not finished yet. They will have to wait, until the middle of August, before the war in the Far East, too, comes to its end.

--

And in the Pacific Ocean, the soldiers, and sailors, and airmen of America, and Australia, and New Zealand, and Britain, are still fighting, on the islands, and the seas, far from home.

And so, the soldiers far away do not celebrate in the same way as the people at home. They think of home, and of their families, celebrating in the streets, and of when they will see them again.

{img:soldier-writing-letter-home-tent-lamp-night-1945-photo-family}
And they write letters. Letters are everything, for the soldiers, and for their families.

--

In the war, letters are written by the millions, every day, and travel thousands of miles, by ship, and by plane. To save space, and weight, some letters are written on special forms, called airgraphs, which are photographed, onto tiny pieces of film, and flown home, and printed out again, at the other end, small, and grey, but readable.

The soldiers write about the weather, and the food, and their friends, and the places they have seen, but not about where they are, or what they are doing, because that is secret. And the letters are read, by the censors, before they are sent, and some words are cut out, or blacked out.

{img:mother-reading-letter-kitchen-window-1945-smiling-children}
And at home, the families write back, about the children, and the garden, and the neighbours, and the news of the street, and the price of things, and the dog, and the weather, and they send photographs, of the children, who are growing up, so fast, without their fathers.

--

On VE Day, and in the days after, the letters go back and forth, full of joy, and hope: Not long now. I'll be home soon. Keep the light on for me.
