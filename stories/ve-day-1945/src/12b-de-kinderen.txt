For the children of 1945, VE Day is the most exciting day of their lives.

Many of them cannot remember a time before the war. They have grown up with the war all around them. With dark nights and warning sirens and shelters. With gas masks and ration books. And with their fathers far away.

--

They have slept in shelters in the back garden. These were little curved huts of metal, half buried in the earth. Inside were bunk beds. They were damp and cold and smelled of earth. When the sirens went at night, the family hurried out to them. Others slept under a steel table in the kitchen. And in London many slept on the platforms of the underground railway, in long rows, with thousands of other families.

They have collected little bits of metal from the bombs and swapped them at school, like marbles. They have collected old pots and pans and paper to help the war. They have carried their gas masks in cardboard boxes on a string everywhere they went. At school they practised putting them on. And they made rude noises with them.

{img:children-playing-anderson-shelter-garden-vegetables-1945-spring}
They have never tasted a banana or seen a lemon. Sweets have been rationed. Ice cream has been almost impossible to find. Their toys have been home made. Wooden aeroplanes and rag dolls and knitted animals made from old jumpers.

--

And now the war is over. And suddenly everything is exciting and new.

On VE Day, they may stay up late, much later than ever before. They go out into the streets at night to see the bonfires and fireworks and lights. They eat jelly and cake at the street party until they cannot eat any more. They wave little paper flags and wear paper hats. They run around among the grown-ups. And for once, the grown-ups are all laughing and singing and dancing. Nobody tells them to be quiet.

{img:children-sitting-on-wall-watching-fireworks-night-1945-faces-glow}
Years later, when they are old, people ask them what they remember. The lights, they say. Seeing the street lamps on for the first time. Or the bonfire at the end of our street, and my father, home on leave, lifting me onto his shoulders. Or the jelly. Or my mother crying and laughing at the same time. I did not understand why.

--

One of them, a little girl, remembered one thing all her life. She lay in bed that night, very late, too excited to sleep. She looked up at the ceiling of her bedroom. For the first time, the light of the street lamp outside shone through the gap in the curtains. It made a soft golden stripe across the ceiling. And she lay and looked at it. She listened to the singing in the street below. Until she fell asleep.
