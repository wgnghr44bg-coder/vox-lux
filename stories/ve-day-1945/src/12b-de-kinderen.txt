For the children of 1945, VE Day is the most exciting day of their lives.

Many of them cannot remember a time before the war. They were born in 1938, or 1939, or 1940, and they have grown up with the war all around them: with the blackout, and the sirens, and the air raid shelters, and the gas masks, and the ration books, and their fathers far away.

--

They have slept in shelters, in the back garden, little curved huts of corrugated iron, half buried in the earth, called Anderson shelters, with bunk beds inside, damp, and cold, and smelling of earth, where the family went, when the sirens sounded, at night. Or under the kitchen table, in a steel cage, called a Morrison shelter. Or on the platforms of the London Underground, in long rows, with blankets, and pillows, with thousands of other families.

They have collected shrapnel, the jagged little pieces of metal, from the shells, and the bombs, and swapped them, at school, like marbles, or cards. They have collected scrap metal, and paper, and saucepans, and old bones, for the war effort. They have carried their gas masks, in cardboard boxes, on a string, everywhere they went, and practised putting them on, at school, and made rude noises with them.

{img:children-playing-anderson-shelter-garden-vegetables-1945-spring}
They have never tasted a banana, or seen a lemon, and sweets have been rationed, and ice cream has been almost impossible to find. Their toys have been home-made: wooden aeroplanes, and rag dolls, and knitted animals, made from old jumpers, unpicked, and knitted again.

--

And now, the war is over. And everything is suddenly exciting, and new.

On VE Day, they are allowed to stay up, late, much later than ever before, and to go out into the streets, at night, and see the bonfires, and the fireworks, and the lights. They eat jelly, and cake, at the street party, until they cannot eat any more. They wave their little paper flags, and wear their paper hats, and run around, among the grown-ups, who are all, for once, laughing, and singing, and dancing, and not telling them to be quiet.

{img:children-sitting-on-wall-watching-fireworks-night-1945-faces-glow}
Years later, when they are old, and are asked what they remember about VE Day, they say: the lights. Seeing the street lamps on, and the windows lit up, for the first time. Or: the bonfire, at the end of our street, and my father, home on leave, lifting me up, on his shoulders, to see it. Or: the jelly. Or: my mother crying, and laughing, at the same time, and I did not understand why.

--

And one of them, a little girl, remembered, all her life, lying in bed, on the night of VE Day, very late, too excited to sleep, and looking at the ceiling of her bedroom, where, for the first time, the light from the street lamp, outside, shone through the gap in the curtains, and made a soft, golden stripe, across the ceiling. And she lay, and looked at it, and listened to the singing, in the street below, until she fell asleep.
