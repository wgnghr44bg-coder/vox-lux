On the evening of the fourth of May, the news comes over the radio, in Dutch, from London. The German forces in the Netherlands and Denmark and the north of Germany have given up.

That afternoon, on a windy hill in the north of Germany, the British commander Montgomery sat in a tent. There was a plain army table covered with a blanket. The German commanders came in and signed. They used an ordinary pen. Montgomery said it could have been bought in any shop for a few pennies. The surrender will begin the next morning at eight o'clock.

--

In Holland, people can hardly believe it. That evening they come out into the streets in the twilight. They stand and talk. Is it really true? Some of them dare to put a light in the window for the first time in five years. They tear down the black paper from the glass.

The next day, the fifth of May, is Liberation Day. In a town in the middle of the country, a Canadian general meets a German general. They meet in a hotel called the Hotel of the World. They talk about how the German soldiers in Holland will lay down their weapons. The next day it is signed.

{img:dutch-girls-orange-ribbons-dancing-street-canadian-soldiers-may}
In the days that follow, Canadian soldiers drive into the great cities of the west. Utrecht and Amsterdam and Rotterdam and The Hague. They come in jeeps and lorries and tanks. And the whole population comes out to meet them.

--

The streets are full of people cheering and singing and dancing. They wave the red, white and blue Dutch flag. It has been hidden away for five years. And they wear orange, the colour of the royal family. Orange ribbons and orange sashes and orange paper hats. Anything orange they can find. Children climb onto the tanks. Girls kiss the soldiers. Old men cry. The church bells ring.

The Canadian soldiers give away everything they have. Cigarettes and chocolate and chewing gum and white bread. The thin hungry people crowd around them. Many soldiers remembered these as the happiest days of the war.

{img:dutch-street-party-1945-long-table-orange-decorations-children}
In every street, in every town and village, there are street parties. People carry their tables and chairs out of the houses. Orange paper and flags hang between the houses. There is music and dancing in the squares. There are games and races for the children. And everyone sings the national anthem together.

--

Not long after, Queen Wilhelmina comes home from England. All through the war, she spoke to her people on the radio from London. The rest of the royal family comes home too, from Canada. Princess Juliana had lived there with her children during the war.

She was so grateful to Canada. Canada had kept her family safe. And Canadian soldiers had freed her country. So after the war, she sent a gift of a hundred thousand tulip bulbs to Canada's capital. Every year since then, the Netherlands has sent tulips. Today there is a great tulip festival there every spring, with a million tulips by the river.

{img:ottawa-tulip-festival-spring-parliament-hill-tulip-beds-today}
And every year on the fifth of May, the Dutch celebrate Liberation Day with festivals and music all over the country. The night before, on the fourth of May at eight o'clock, the whole country stops for two minutes of silence. They remember all those who died.

--

The trains stop. The traffic stops. People in the streets and houses and shops stand still, in silence. And then the next morning the flags come out, and the music begins.
