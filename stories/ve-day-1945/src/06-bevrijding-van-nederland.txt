On the evening of the fourth of May, 1945, the news comes over the wireless, on the BBC, in Dutch, from London: the German forces in the Netherlands, and in north-west Germany, and in Denmark, have surrendered.

That afternoon, on a windswept hill, on Lüneburg Heath, in northern Germany, in a tent, with a plain army table, covered with a blanket, the British Field Marshal Montgomery has received the surrender of the German commanders, and they have signed it, with an ordinary pen, which, Montgomery said, could have been bought in any shop for a few pence. It will take effect the next morning, at eight o'clock.

--

In Holland, the people can hardly believe it. That evening, they come out into the streets, in the twilight, and stand, and talk, and some of them dare, for the first time in five years, to put a light in the window, and take down the blackout paper.

And the next day, the fifth of May, is Liberation Day. In the town of Wageningen, in the middle of the country, beside the river Rhine, the Canadian general, Charles Foulkes, meets the German general, Johannes Blaskowitz, in a hotel, called Hotel de Wereld, the Hotel of the World, to arrange the surrender of the German troops in Holland. And the next day, the sixth of May, it is signed, in the great hall of the agricultural college, nearby.

{img:dutch-girls-orange-ribbons-dancing-street-canadian-soldiers-may}
In the following days, the Canadian soldiers drive into the great cities of the west, into Utrecht, and Amsterdam, and Rotterdam, and The Hague, in their jeeps, and lorries, and tanks, and the whole population comes out to meet them.

--

The streets are full of people, cheering, and singing, and dancing, and waving the red, white, and blue flag of the Netherlands, which has been hidden away, for five years, and wearing orange, the colour of the royal family, the House of Orange: orange ribbons, and orange sashes, and orange paper hats, and orange flowers, and anything orange they can find. Children climb onto the tanks, and the jeeps. Girls kiss the soldiers. Old men weep. The church bells ring.

The Canadian soldiers give away everything they have: cigarettes, and chocolate, and chewing gum, and white bread, and biscuits, to the thin, hungry people, who crowd around them. Many of the soldiers remember it, for the rest of their lives, as the happiest days of the war.

{img:dutch-street-party-1945-long-table-orange-decorations-children}
In every street, in every town, and village, there are street parties, with long tables, and chairs, carried out of the houses, and decorations, of orange paper, and flags, hanging between the houses, and music, and dancing, in the squares, and the children's games, and races, and everyone sings the national anthem, the Wilhelmus.

--

Not long afterwards, the Queen, Wilhelmina, comes home, from her exile, in England, where she spoke to her people, on the radio, all through the war. And the royal family come home, too, from Canada, where Princess Juliana had lived, with her children, during the war.

Princess Juliana was so grateful to Canada, for keeping her family safe, and for the Canadian soldiers, who freed the Netherlands, that, after the war, she sent a gift, of a hundred thousand tulip bulbs, to Ottawa, the capital of Canada. And every year since then, the Netherlands has sent tulips, to Ottawa. Today, every spring, there is a great tulip festival there, with a million tulips, flowering in the parks, beside the river.

{img:ottawa-tulip-festival-spring-parliament-hill-tulip-beds-today}
And every year, on the fifth of May, the Dutch celebrate Liberation Day, Bevrijdingsdag, with festivals, and music, all over the country. And the night before, on the fourth of May, at eight o'clock in the evening, the whole country stops, for two minutes of silence, to remember all those who died.

--

The trains stop. The traffic stops. The people in the streets, and in the houses, and in the shops, stand still, in silence. And then, the next morning, the fifth of May, the flags come out, and the music begins.
