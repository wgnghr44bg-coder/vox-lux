Let us go to an airfield in the flat east of England, on a spring morning at the end of April.

The great bombers stand around the edge of the field. They are black and huge, with four engines and long wings and tall tails. Their crews gather beside them in flying jackets and boots, in the cool morning air.

--

Each plane has a crew of seven. There is the pilot. Beside him sits the engineer, who watches the engines and the fuel. The navigator sits at a little table with his maps. Another man works the radio. One man lies in the glass nose at the front, to aim the drop. And two gunners sit in small glass bubbles, one on top and one at the very back.

They are very young. The pilot is twenty-two. The back gunner is nineteen. They come from all over Britain, and from Canada and Australia and New Zealand. They have flown many times at night over Germany. But this morning they will fly in daylight, and very low. And they will drop food.

{img:ground-crew-loading-food-sacks-into-bomb-bay-lancaster-morning}
The ground crew have worked all night. Many of them are women. They have filled the planes with sacks of flour and sugar, tinned meat, dried egg and chocolate.

--

Some airmen have added a little something of their own. Bars of chocolate from their own rations. Sweets. They tie them to small parachutes made from handkerchiefs. They add notes for the children who will find them.

The engines start one by one, with a cough and a roar. The great planes roll slowly to the runway. One after another they thunder down it and lift heavily into the air. Then they turn east, over the sea. There is one worry on every man's mind. Will the Germans keep their promise? Or will someone fire?

{img:lancaster-cockpit-view-low-over-dutch-polders-canals-windmills}
They fly very low over the North Sea, just above the waves. Then the coast of Holland appears ahead. A long pale line of dunes and beaches. Then the flat green land, with canals and ditches shining in the sun. Windmills and church towers. Red roofs. And tulip fields in great stripes of red and yellow and pink and white.

--

And everywhere there are people. On roofs and in streets and fields. Thousands of people, waving sheets and towels and flags and their arms. The crew can see their faces looking up. Nobody fires. Someone has written on a field in big white letters. Thanks.

As they come over the drop zone, the man in the nose calls out. He presses his button. The doors under the plane open. And the sacks fall away behind them onto the grass.

{img:lancaster-banking-over-crowd-waving-field-tulips-sunlit}
The pilot tips the great plane gently around, so the crew can look down. They see the people running out onto the field. They wave back from the windows. And some of them have tears in their eyes.

--

Then they turn for home, back over the sea. On the way home, the crew are very quiet. That evening they talk about it. And the next day they fly again.

Many years later, when they were old men, some of them went back to Holland. They were welcomed as heroes. The children who had waved in the fields were old now too. And they still remembered.
