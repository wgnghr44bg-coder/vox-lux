During the war, the women of Britain have done almost everything.

When the men went away, to the army, and the navy, and the air force, somebody had to do their work: in the factories, and on the farms, and on the railways, and the buses, and in the offices, and the hospitals. And so, the women did it.

--

In the factories, they made aeroplanes, and ships, and tanks, and guns, and parachutes, and uniforms, working long shifts, day, and night, in overalls, and headscarves, tied in a knot, on the top of their heads, to keep their hair out of the machines. They drove buses, and trams, and lorries, and ambulances. They worked as postwomen, and porters, and engineers, and welders, and crane drivers.

On the farms, there was the Women's Land Army: tens of thousands of young women, many of them from the cities, who had never seen a cow, before, who went to work on the farms, ploughing, and milking, and harvesting, and looking after the animals, and the land, to feed the country. They wore green jumpers, and brown breeches, and felt hats, and they were called the land girls.

{img:women-factory-workers-headscarves-aircraft-assembly-line-1940s}
In the forces, there were women too: in the Women's Royal Naval Service, the Wrens, and the Women's Auxiliary Air Force, and the Auxiliary Territorial Service, where Princess Elizabeth served. They worked as drivers, and mechanics, and radio operators, and cooks, and clerks, and plotters, moving the little markers, of the aircraft, on the great map tables, in the operations rooms, and on the anti-aircraft guns, and the searchlights.

--

And at home, there were the mothers, and grandmothers, who kept the families going, through the bombing, and the rationing, and the blackout, and the worry, queuing for food, and making do, and mending, and caring for the children, and the old, and often working too, part-time, in the factories, or in the Women's Voluntary Service, serving tea, and soup, and comfort, to the people who had been bombed out of their homes.

On VE Day, the women of Britain celebrate, with everyone else, in the streets, in their uniforms, and their overalls, and their best dresses.

{img:wrens-waaf-women-uniform-celebrating-ve-day-arms-linked-street}
And after the war, things are not quite the same, for them. Many of the women give up their jobs, when the men come home, and go back to their homes, and their families. But they have learned that they can do things that people thought only men could do, and they do not forget it. And their daughters, and granddaughters, do not forget it either.

--

Many years later, in 2005, a memorial was unveiled, in Whitehall, in London, to the women of the Second World War: a tall bronze sculpture, with the uniforms, and the overalls, and the work clothes, of the women, hanging on pegs, all around it, as if they had just taken them off, at the end of the war, and gone home.
