In the weeks, and months, after VE Day, the soldiers begin to come home.

It does not happen all at once. There are millions of them, and they must wait for their turn, according to how old they are, and how long they have served. Some come home in the summer of 1945. Others not until 1946, or even 1947. But week by week, month by month, the trains, and the ships, bring them home.

--

At the railway stations, the families wait, on the platforms, with flags, and flowers, and the children, in their best clothes, and when the train comes in, and the doors open, and the soldiers climb down, with their kit bags, on their shoulders, there are shouts, and tears, and hugs, and kisses.

Some of the children do not know their fathers. They were babies, when their fathers went away, and now they are five, or six, years old, and they stand, shyly, behind their mothers, looking at this tall stranger, in uniform, who picks them up, and holds them, and cries.

When the soldiers leave the army, they are given a set of new clothes, called a demob suit: a suit, and a shirt, and a tie, and shoes, and a raincoat, and a hat. And they go home, in their new suits, to start their ordinary lives again.

--

It is not always easy. The soldiers have been away for years, and have seen, and done, things that they do not want to talk about. Their wives have run the homes, and worked, in the factories, and on the farms, and have become strong, and independent. The children have grown up. Everyone has changed. And it takes time, and patience, and love, to become a family again.

And the prisoners of war come home, too: the men who were captured, and spent years, in prison camps, in Germany, and Italy, and Poland. They come home, thin, and tired, many of them in the great bombers of the Royal Air Force, which fly them home, from Belgium, and Germany, by the thousand, in the spring of 1945, landing at the airfields of England, where they are given tea, and sandwiches, and a warm welcome, and a telegram home.

{img:evacuee-children-returning-london-station-labels-suitcases-mothers}
And the children come home too.

--

At the beginning of the war, in 1939, hundreds of thousands of children were sent away, from the cities, to the countryside, to keep them safe from the bombs. They were called evacuees. They went by train, with labels, tied to their coats, with their names, and a little suitcase, and their gas masks, in cardboard boxes, and lived with families they had never met, in villages, and farms, and country towns.

Many of them had already come home, during the war. But in 1945, the last of them come back, to the cities, to their families. Some of them have been away for five years, and have grown up in the country, with its fields, and animals, and woods, and do not want to leave. Some have been very happy, and stay friends, with their country families, all their lives. Others have been homesick, every day, and are overjoyed to be back.

{img:family-reunited-front-door-terraced-house-1945-father-home-children}
And so, one by one, the families of Britain come together again, around the kitchen tables, and the fires, in the little houses, and the flats, and the farms, and begin, slowly, to build their lives again.
