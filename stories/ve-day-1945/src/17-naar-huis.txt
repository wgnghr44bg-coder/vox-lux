In the weeks and months after VE Day, the soldiers begin to come home.

It does not happen all at once. There are millions of them. They must wait their turn, depending on their age and how long they have served. Some come home in the summer of 1945. Others not until a year or two later. But week by week and month by month, the trains and ships bring them home.

--

At the railway stations, families wait on the platforms with flags and flowers. The children wear their best clothes. Every time a train comes in, they look along the doors. Is he on this one? Then the doors open. Soldiers climb down with kit bags on their shoulders. There are shouts and tears and hugs and kisses.

Some of the children do not know their fathers. They were babies when their fathers left. Now they are five or six. They stand shyly behind their mothers. They look at this tall stranger in uniform. He picks them up and holds them. And he cries.

When the soldiers leave the army, they are given new clothes. A suit and shirt and tie, shoes and a raincoat and a hat. They go home in their new suits to start their ordinary lives again.

--

It is not always easy. The soldiers have been away for years. They have seen things they do not want to talk about. Their wives have run the homes and worked in factories and on farms. They have become strong and independent. The children have grown up. Everyone has changed. It takes time and patience and love to become a family again.

The prisoners of war come home too. These are men who were captured and spent years in prison camps. They come home thin and tired. Many fly home in the great bombers, thousands of them, landing at English airfields. They are given tea and sandwiches and a warm welcome. And a telegram goes to their families.

{img:evacuee-children-returning-london-station-labels-suitcases-mothers}
And the children come home too.

--

At the start of the war, hundreds of thousands of children were sent away from the cities. They went to the countryside to be safe from the bombs. They travelled by train with name labels tied to their coats. They each had a little suitcase and a gas mask in a cardboard box. They lived with families they had never met before.

Many came home during the war. But now the last of them come back. Some have been away for five years. They have grown up in the country, with fields and animals and woods. Some do not want to leave. Some were very happy and stay friends with their country families all their lives. Others were homesick every single day. They are overjoyed to be back.

{img:family-reunited-front-door-terraced-house-1945-father-home-children}
And so, one by one, the families of Britain come together again. They sit around kitchen tables and fires, in little houses and flats and farms. And slowly they begin to build their lives again.
