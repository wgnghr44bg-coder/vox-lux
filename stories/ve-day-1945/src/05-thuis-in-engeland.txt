Let us go to an ordinary street, in an English town, in the spring of 1945.

It is a street of little brick houses, in a row, each with its front door, and its window, and its little front garden, and its back yard. Some of the houses have been damaged, by bombs, earlier in the war, and their windows are still boarded up. At the end of the street, there is a gap, where two houses used to be, and the children play there, among the bricks, and the weeds.

--

In one of the houses lives a family: a mother, and a grandmother, and three children. The father is far away, in the army, in Italy. The eldest son is in the navy. They have not seen them for a long time, more than two years. But they write letters, every week, and the letters come, sometimes in bundles, many at once, and sometimes not for weeks, and the mother reads them out, at the kitchen table, in the evening, and keeps them, in a box, tied with a ribbon.

Every morning, the mother goes to the shops, with her ration books, and stands in the queue, at the grocer's, and the butcher's, and the baker's, to buy the family's food, for the week.

Each person may have, every week, only a little: a few ounces of bacon, and butter, and cheese, and sugar, and tea, and margarine, and cooking fat, one egg, if there are any, and a little meat, measured by price, not weight, and sweets, a few ounces, each month. Bread, and potatoes, are not rationed, and the mothers of Britain have become very clever, at making meals out of very little: carrot cake, and potato pie, and mock banana, made from parsnips.

--

The children have not seen a banana, or an orange, for years. The youngest has never seen one at all. There is dried egg, from America, in tins, which is made into omelettes, and scrambled eggs, and cakes. And everyone has been digging for victory: growing vegetables, in their gardens, and their allotments, and even in the parks, and keeping hens, and rabbits, in their back yards.

Clothes are rationed too, and everyone mends, and patches, and darns, and makes do. The children wear their older brothers' and sisters' clothes, and the mothers make dresses from curtains, and blouses from parachute silk, when they can get it.

{img:children-playing-bomb-site-street-1945-terraced-houses-sunny}
And for nearly six years, there has been the blackout. Every evening, at dusk, every window, in every house, must be covered, with thick black curtains, or blinds, so that not a chink of light can be seen, from the street, or the sky. The street lamps have been dark. The cars have driven with hooded headlamps. And people have walked home, through the dark streets, with little torches, pointed at the ground.

--

But last September, the blackout was eased, to a dim-out, and now, in the spring of 1945, the lights are slowly coming back. At the end of April, the great clock faces of Big Ben, in London, were lit again, for the first time in more than five years, and crowds gathered, in the evening, to watch, and cheered, when the lights came on.

The children, who were born during the war, have never seen a street, lit up at night. They look out of their windows, in wonder.

{img:big-ben-clock-face-lit-again-evening-crowd-watching-1945}
In the evening, the family sit around the kitchen table, or by the fire, and listen to the wireless, and the grandmother knits, and the children do their homework, and the mother writes a letter, to the father, in Italy, and tells him that the lilac is out, in the back yard, and that the children are well, and that, perhaps, soon, he will be home.
