Let us go to an ordinary street in an English town, in the spring of 1945.

It is a street of small brick houses in a row. Each has a front door and a window and a tiny front garden. Some of the houses were damaged by bombs earlier in the war. Their windows are still covered with boards. At the end of the street there is a gap where two houses used to be. Children play there among the bricks and the weeds.

--

In one house lives a family. A mother, a grandmother and three children. The father is far away, in the army in Italy. The eldest son is in the navy. They have not seen them for more than two years. But they write letters every week. Sometimes the letters come in a bundle, many at once. Sometimes none come for weeks. Then the mother checks the door every morning. In the evening she reads the letters aloud at the kitchen table. She keeps them in a box tied with a ribbon.

Every morning the mother goes to the shops with her ration books. She stands in the queue at the grocer and the butcher and the baker.

Each person may only have a little every week. A bit of bacon and butter and cheese. A bit of sugar and tea. One egg, if there are any. A little meat. And a few sweets each month. Bread and potatoes are not rationed. The mothers of Britain have become very clever at making meals out of very little. Carrot cake. Potato pie. Even pretend banana, made from a white root vegetable.

--

The children have not seen a real banana or an orange for years. The youngest has never seen one at all. There is dried egg in tins from America. It is made into omelettes and scrambled eggs and cakes. And everyone grows vegetables, in their gardens and even in the parks. Many families keep hens in the back yard.

Clothes are rationed too. Everyone mends and patches and makes do. The children wear their older brothers' and sisters' clothes. Mothers make dresses from old curtains.

{img:children-playing-bomb-site-street-1945-terraced-houses-sunny}
And for nearly six years there has been the blackout. Every evening at dusk, every window must be covered with thick black curtains. Not a chink of light may show, from the street or from the sky. The street lamps have been dark. Cars have driven with covered lights. People have walked home through dark streets with small torches pointed at the ground.

--

But now the lights are slowly coming back. At the end of April, the great clock faces of Big Ben in London were lit again. It was the first time in more than five years. Crowds gathered in the evening to watch. And when the lights came on, they cheered.

The children who were born during the war have never seen a street lit up at night. They look out of their windows in wonder.

{img:big-ben-clock-face-lit-again-evening-crowd-watching-1945}
In the evening the family sits by the fire and listens to the radio. The grandmother knits. The children do their homework. The mother writes a letter to the father in Italy. She tells him the lilac is out in the back yard. She tells him the children are well. And she writes that perhaps, soon, he will be home.
