In the spring of 1945, the war in Europe is coming to its end.

In the west, the armies of the Allies, the Americans, and the British, and the Canadians, and the French, and the Poles, and many others, have crossed the great river Rhine, in March, and are moving east, across Germany, from town to town, faster than anyone had expected.

--

In the east, the great armies of the Soviet Union are moving west, across Poland, and into Germany, toward Berlin. In the south, in Italy, the Allied armies are moving north, through the valleys, toward the Alps.

Everywhere, the war is ending, place by place. Towns, and villages, are being reached, one after another, and the people come out, from their cellars, and their houses, and wave white sheets, from the windows, and the soldiers move on. Prisoners of war are being freed, from the camps, thin, and tired, and overjoyed, and begin the long journey home.

And the world is learning, too, of terrible things that happened, in the camps, during the war, things that we remember, still, with great sorrow, and which we will leave, gently, in silence, here.

--

In April, on the twelfth, in America, the President, Franklin Roosevelt, dies, suddenly, at his little house, in Warm Springs, in Georgia, after leading his country for twelve years, and through nearly the whole war. People all over the world mourn him. The new President is his Vice President, Harry Truman, a modest man, from Missouri.

And at the end of April, in Berlin, the war is coming to its end, too. On the first of May, the German radio announces that Hitler is dead. And on the second of May, Berlin surrenders.

{img:village-people-waving-white-sheets-windows-soldiers-passing-1945}
Day after day, the German armies surrender, in different places: in Italy, on the second of May, and in north-west Germany, and Holland, and Denmark, on the fourth, and in the south, in Bavaria, and Austria, a few days later.

--

All over Europe, people are waiting for the last, great, final surrender, and the end of the war.

And in Britain, and America, and all the Allied countries, people are waiting for the announcement: the day that will be called VE Day, Victory in Europe Day. It has been talked about for weeks. The newspapers are full of plans. The shops are full of flags. The churches are ready to ring their bells.

{img:english-shop-window-1945-union-jacks-bunting-for-sale-queue}
And in the meantime, life goes on. People go to work, and to the shops, with their ration books, and queue for their food, and go home, and listen to the news, on the wireless, and go to bed, and wonder, will it be tomorrow?
