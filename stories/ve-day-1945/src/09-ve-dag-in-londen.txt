On the morning of the eighth of May, all of London comes into the centre of the city.

They come by train and bus and bicycle and on foot. They come from every part of London and the towns around. They wear their best clothes and their uniforms. They carry flags and wear paper hats and red, white and blue ribbons in their hair.

--

By midday the streets of the West End are packed with people. The streets are so full that the traffic cannot move. Buses and taxis are stuck in the crowds. People climb on their roofs.

There are soldiers and sailors and airmen from Britain and America and Canada and Australia. From France and Poland and Norway and Holland and many other countries. There are women in uniform and nurses and land girls and factory girls. There are office workers and old men and grandmothers. And children ride on their fathers' shoulders.

People climb up the lamp posts and the statues. They climb onto the great stone lions in Trafalgar Square. They jump into the fountains and splash about. They wave flags and sing and dance. They make long lines holding hands and dance through the streets and the crowds.

--

Strangers hug each other and kiss each other and shake hands. Sailors dance with girls they have never met. Old ladies dance with soldiers. Everyone is smiling and laughing and singing the old songs.

At three o'clock, the crowds go quiet. Loudspeakers have been put up in the streets and squares. In every house, the radio is on. And Mr Churchill speaks from Downing Street.

{img:crowd-silent-listening-loudspeakers-whitehall-3pm-churchill-broadcast}
He tells them in his deep slow voice that the war in Europe is over. The fighting will officially end at one minute after midnight tonight. We may allow ourselves a short time of joy, he says. And at the end he says, long live the cause of freedom. God save the King.

--

And the crowds cheer and cheer. The church bells ring out all over London. And the ships on the river sound their horns.

Later that afternoon, Mr Churchill comes out onto a balcony above the crowd. The whole street below is packed from one end to the other. They cheer him and sing to him. He holds up his hand and makes his famous sign. Two fingers in the shape of a V, for victory. This is your victory, he tells them. And the crowd shouts back, no, it's yours.

{img:churchill-balcony-whitehall-v-sign-crowd-below-flags-may-1945}
All through the afternoon and the evening, the crowds keep growing and celebrating in the sunshine.

--

Many people who were there that day remembered it for the rest of their lives. The noise and the crowds and the joy. The feeling of being part of something enormous. And the strange wonderful feeling that tonight, nobody would be afraid of the sky.
