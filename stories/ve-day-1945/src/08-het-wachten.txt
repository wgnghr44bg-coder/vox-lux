In London, on Monday, the seventh of May, 1945, everybody knows that something has happened. But nobody has told them officially.

The news of the surrender at Reims has been kept secret, for a day, so that it can be announced at the same time, in London, and Washington, and Moscow. But a news agency has already reported it, in America, and the rumours have spread, all over London, by the morning.

--

All day, people wait. Crowds gather, in Whitehall, and outside Buckingham Palace, and in Trafalgar Square, and Piccadilly Circus, waiting for the news. Office workers lean out of their windows. People buy newspapers, every edition, looking for the announcement. Some people start to celebrate, anyway, and throw paper, out of the office windows, like snow. The flags come out, on the buildings. The church bells are ready.

But the hours pass, and there is no announcement. Everyone is confused, and a little impatient.

In the shops, people are buying flags, and bunting. The government has announced that, for the celebrations, people may buy red, white, and blue cotton bunting, without using their clothing coupons, and the shops sell out, quickly. Women sit at their sewing machines, making flags, and rosettes, and decorations, out of anything they can find, old dresses, and curtains, and scraps of cloth.

--

At last, at about twenty to eight, in the evening, the Ministry of Information makes a short announcement, on the wireless: tomorrow, Tuesday, the eighth of May, will be Victory in Europe Day, and will be a public holiday. The Prime Minister will speak to the nation, at three o'clock in the afternoon. And the King will speak, at nine o'clock in the evening. And the day after, Wednesday, will be a holiday, too.

So it is true. It is over. And tomorrow is the day.

{img:family-kitchen-1945-evening-sewing-flags-children-excited-wireless}
That evening, in houses all over Britain, people get ready. The mothers take out the flags, and the bunting, that they have saved, and the children help to hang them, from the windows, and across the street, from house to house. The neighbours talk, over the garden walls, about the party, they will have, tomorrow. Everyone gives a little of their rations: a little sugar, and margarine, and flour, and dried egg, to make cakes, and sandwiches, and jellies, for the children.

--

And in the night, over London, a great thunderstorm comes.

The thunder rolls, over the rooftops, and the lightning flashes, over the city, and the rain pours down, heavy, and warm, washing the streets, and the dust, of the bombed buildings, and soaking the flags, and the bunting, hanging, ready, in the streets.

{img:thunderstorm-over-london-night-lightning-rooftops-st-pauls-rain}
For six years, the sky over London has been full of danger, at night. But tonight, the flashes, and the rumbles, are only lightning, and thunder, and the people lie in their beds, and listen to the rain, and some of them laugh, and say that it is the last air raid, the sky's own fireworks, for the end of the war.

--

And in the morning, the storm has passed. The sky is washed, and clear, and the sun is shining, warm, on the wet streets, and the flags, hanging from every window, are drying, and lifting, in the breeze.

It is the eighth of May, 1945. It is VE Day.
