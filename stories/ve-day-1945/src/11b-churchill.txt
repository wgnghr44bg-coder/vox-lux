On VE Day, the man the crowds want to see, more than anyone, after the King, is Winston Churchill.

He is seventy years old, short, and round, with a round, pink face, and a few wisps of hair, and a big cigar, and a bow tie, spotted, and a black hat. He has been Prime Minister since May 1940, exactly five years before, when the war was going very badly, and he has led the country, through all of it.

--

In the summer of 1940, when France had fallen, and Britain stood alone, and invasion seemed close, he spoke to the people, on the wireless, and in Parliament, with great, rolling, stirring words, that gave them courage, and hope. We shall fight on the beaches, he said. We shall never surrender. And: This was their finest hour.

He worked, all through the war, day and night, in the underground rooms, beneath Whitehall, the Cabinet War Rooms, where he slept, sometimes, and from where he spoke on the wireless, and in his offices, and on his travels, all over the world, to meet the other leaders, President Roosevelt, and Marshal Stalin. He visited the bombed streets of London, and the soldiers, at the front, and the people cheered him, everywhere he went.

{img:cabinet-war-rooms-underground-map-room-telephones-lamps}
He worked very hard, and slept in the afternoons, and dictated letters, and speeches, to his secretaries, late into the night, often in his dressing gown, walking up and down, with his cigar. He loved painting, and bricklaying, and his garden, and his cats, and his poodle, and his wife, Clementine, to whom he wrote loving letters, all his life.

--

On VE Day, he has lunch with the King, at Buckingham Palace, and then he drives back to Downing Street, through the crowds, which are so thick, that his car can hardly move, and has to be pushed, by the people, the last part of the way. He sits on the top of the back of the open car, and waves, and makes his V sign, and the crowds cheer, and sing.

At three o'clock, he speaks to the nation, from the Cabinet Room. Then he goes to the House of Commons, and gives the same news to the Members of Parliament, who cheer, and wave their order papers. And then he leads them all, across the road, to Saint Margaret's Church, beside Westminster Abbey, for a service of thanksgiving.

{img:whitehall-ministry-of-health-balcony-evening-floodlit-crowd-singing}
And in the evening, he comes out, again, onto the balcony, in Whitehall, in the floodlights, and the crowd sings Land of Hope and Glory, and he conducts them, waving his arms, and then speaks to them, again, and says that, in all their long history, they have never seen a greater day than this.

--

Two months later, in the general election, the people of Britain will vote for a new government, and Churchill will no longer be Prime Minister. He is hurt, and surprised. But many of the people who voted against him still love him, and are grateful to him, and they say that he was the right man for the war, but that they want something new, for the peace.

He will become Prime Minister again, in 1951, and live until 1965, when he is ninety. And when he dies, the whole country will mourn him, and the cranes, along the river Thames, in London, will bow down, as his coffin passes, on a boat, along the river.
