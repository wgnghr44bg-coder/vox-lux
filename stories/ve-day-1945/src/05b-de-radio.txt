In the spring of 1945, the most important thing in every house is the radio. People call it the wireless.

It is a big wooden box on a shelf by the fire. It has a round speaker covered with cloth, and knobs, and a glowing dial. On the dial are the names of faraway places. London and Paris and Moscow. When you switch it on, it takes a little while to warm up. Then it hums and crackles. And the voices come.

--

For six years, the radio has been the voice of the war. Every evening at nine o'clock, the whole country stops to listen to the news. The news readers have calm steady voices. During the war they started to say their names. That way people would know it really was the BBC, and not the enemy pretending.

On the radio, people have heard the King. They have heard Mr Churchill, the Prime Minister, speaking to the country in the darkest moments of the war. They gathered around to listen together, at home and in factories and in shelters.

{img:factory-workers-listening-radio-loudspeaker-canteen-1940s}
But the radio has also been a comfort and a friend. It has made people laugh and sing and forget for a while.

--

There are funny shows with silly characters. Everyone repeats their jokes the next day at work and at school. There is music at lunchtime, played from factory canteens. There is music while you work, to keep the workers cheerful. And every afternoon there is a children's hour, with stories and plays. At the end, a friendly voice says goodnight, children, everywhere.

And there are the singers. The favourite of all is Vera Lynn, a young woman from London. She has a clear warm voice. The soldiers far away love her songs, because they remind them of home. One of them is called We'll Meet Again.

{img:young-woman-singer-microphone-1940s-studio-band-warm-light}
In the occupied countries of Europe, people have listened to the BBC in secret. It was forbidden. If they were caught, they could be arrested. So they listened under blankets, in attics and in cellars, with the sound turned very low. In Holland and Denmark and France and Norway, they listened for news. They listened for coded messages. And they listened for the voices of their own queens and kings, speaking to them from London.

--

And in the first days of May, the radio is on all day long in every home. People are waiting for one piece of news. The news that the war in Europe is over. Then they can hang out their flags at last, and ring the bells.
