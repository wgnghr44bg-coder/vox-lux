In the days after the fire, San Francisco became a city of tents.

[long-pause]

In Golden Gate Park, in the Presidio, in squares and open spaces all over the western part of the city, long rows of white army tents appeared.

[pause]

The army brought them in by the thousand, and set them up in neat lines, with little streets running between them.

[pause]

Each tent had a number, and each family had a place.

[long-pause]

The same park that had once been sand dunes, and then had been planted, tree by tree, now became a home for tens of thousands of people.

[pause]

John McLaren, the patient gardener, must have watched with mixed feelings, as his lawns filled with tents.

[pause]

But he helped wherever he could, and when it was all over, he planted the lawns again.

[long-pause]

Life in the camps was simple.

[pause]

Water was carried in buckets.

[pause]

People queued for bread, and for soup, and for coffee.

[pause]

Children played games between the rows of tents, and ran through the trees, and climbed the rocks by the lake.

[pause]

For some of them, it was almost an adventure.

[long-pause]

For a while, the city had one unusual rule.

[pause]

No one was allowed to cook indoors.

[pause]

Too many chimneys had been damaged, and no one wanted another ham and eggs fire.

[pause]

So people cooked in the street.

[long-pause]

All along the pavements, little outdoor kitchens appeared.

[pause]

Old stoves were carried out of damaged houses, and set up on the kerb.

[pause]

People built small shelters around them, out of boards, and old doors, and scraps of canvas.

[pause]

And every evening, the streets filled with the smell of frying onions, and baking bread, and fresh coffee.

[long-pause]

People being people, they soon began to decorate their street kitchens.

[pause]

They gave them names, and painted signs.

[pause]

One was called the Hotel de Fresh Air.

[pause]

Another was called the Camp Contented.

[pause]

And one family put up a sign that said, eat, drink and be merry, for tomorrow we may have to go to Oakland.

[long-pause]

Neighbours ate together, and talked late into the evening, sitting on boxes and doorsteps under the stars.

[pause]

Many people later said that, in spite of everything, they had never felt closer to the people around them.

[long-pause]

Help began to arrive from all over the country, and from all over the world.

[pause]

Trains rolled in, loaded with flour, and blankets, and tents, and medicine.

[pause]

Ships sailed through the Golden Gate with food and supplies.

[pause]

Cities across America collected money, and sent it west.

[pause]

Help came from countries far across the ocean, too.

[long-pause]

The post office did something rather lovely.

[pause]

For a while, people were allowed to send letters without stamps.

[pause]

And they wrote on whatever they could find.

[pause]

Scraps of paper, pieces of cardboard, the backs of old envelopes, and even strips of cloth.

[pause]

Just a few words, to tell their families far away that they were safe.

[pause]

We are all well.

[pause]

Don't worry.

[pause]

Letters to follow.

[pause]

And the post office delivered them, every one it could.

[long-pause]

Near Market Street, there was a tall cast iron fountain, which had been given to the city years before by a famous actress and singer named Lotta Crabtree.

[pause]

It had come through the fire, and it became a meeting place.

[pause]

People left notes there, pinned to boards, and tucked into the ironwork, for friends and relatives they had lost track of.

[pause]

Looking for Mary, from Folsom Street.

[pause]

Papa is at the Presidio.

[pause]

All safe in Berkeley.

[long-pause]

{img:photographer-ruins-evening}
Some people wrote about what they saw, and some people took photographs.

[pause]

The author Jack London was living across the bay, and he came into the city to report on the fire for a magazine.

[pause]

He wrote about the courage and calm of the people, and how, in all those long hours, he never saw anyone lose their temper, or turn against a neighbour.

[long-pause]

A photographer named Arnold Genthe had lost his cameras when his studio burned.

[pause]

So he went to a camera shop, which had been damaged, and the owner simply told him to take whatever he needed.

[pause]

With his borrowed camera, Genthe walked through the city, taking pictures.

[pause]

One of them became very famous.

[pause]

It looks down a steep street, towards the smoke rising from the city below, with a few people standing quietly on the pavement, watching.

[pause]

It is a strangely peaceful image.

[pause]

Like a moment of stillness, in the middle of everything.

[long-pause]

When people looked back on those days, many years later, they did not only remember the fire.

[pause]

They remembered the street kitchens, and the tents in the park.

[pause]

They remembered sharing a stove with the family next door, and hearing someone play a guitar in the evening.

[pause]

They remembered that, for a little while, it did not matter who was rich and who was poor.

[pause]

Everyone stood in the same bread line.

[pause]

Everyone cooked in the same street.

[long-pause]

And slowly, as the spring turned into summer, they began to talk about the future.
