Nine years after the earthquake, San Francisco was ready to show the world what it had done.

[long-pause]

In the middle of the city, a new City Hall had risen, with a great dome of grey and gold, even taller than the dome of the Capitol in Washington.

[pause]

Its marble staircase swept up beneath the dome, wide and graceful, lit by tall windows.

[pause]

The old City Hall had been lost in the earthquake.

[pause]

This new one was meant to say that San Francisco was back, and prouder than ever.

[long-pause]

And there was even more to celebrate.

[pause]

Far away in Central America, a great canal had just been opened, across the narrow strip of land at Panama.

[pause]

Ships could now sail from the Atlantic to the Pacific without making the long, stormy journey around South America.

[pause]

So San Francisco decided to hold a world's fair.

[long-pause]

{img:exposition-1915-palaces}
They called it the Panama-Pacific International Exposition.

[pause]

It was built on the shore of the bay, on land that had once been marshes and tidal flats, near the Presidio.

[pause]

In just a few years, an entire dream city was built there.

[long-pause]

There were great exhibition palaces, painted in soft colours.

[pause]

Warm ivory, and pale pink, and sea green, and the gentle orange of a sunset.

[pause]

There were courtyards with fountains, and gardens full of flowers, and long avenues lined with palm trees.

[long-pause]

People came from all over the world to see it.

[pause]

Countries built their own pavilions, and filled them with their art, and their inventions, and their foods.

[pause]

There was a working model of the Panama Canal, which visitors could watch from a moving platform.

[pause]

And there was a car factory, where visitors could watch new motor cars being put together, one after another.

[long-pause]

{img:tower-of-jewels-night}
At the centre of it all stood the Tower of Jewels.

[pause]

It was a tall tower, rising in tiers like a wedding cake.

[pause]

And it was covered in more than a hundred thousand pieces of coloured cut glass, hanging from little wires, so that they could move in the breeze.

[long-pause]

By day, the glass sparkled in the sunshine.

[pause]

And at night, the tower was lit by powerful searchlights, in many different colours.

[pause]

The glass jewels shimmered and trembled, casting little points of light all around.

[pause]

People said it was like standing beneath a tower of stars.

[long-pause]

On foggy nights, the searchlights shone up into the mist, and painted the sky in great soft beams of colour.

[pause]

Many visitors had never seen anything like it.

[pause]

Electric light was still something new and magical, and here it was, turning a whole city into a dream.

[long-pause]

{img:palace-of-fine-arts-lagoon}
Almost all of the exposition was temporary.

[pause]

The buildings were made of wood and plaster, and when the fair closed, at the end of the year, they were taken down, one by one.

[pause]

But one of them was so loved that the people of the city could not bear to lose it.

[long-pause]

It was called the Palace of Fine Arts.

[pause]

It was designed by an architect named Bernard Maybeck, who wanted it to feel like an old Roman ruin, gently fading beside a quiet lake.

[pause]

There is a great open rotunda, like a dome without walls, held up by tall columns.

[pause]

And around it, a curving row of columns, with sad, graceful figures on top, looking down into flower boxes.

[long-pause]

The rotunda stands beside a still lagoon, where swans and ducks glide slowly across the water, and willow trees trail their branches.

[pause]

Over the years, the plaster began to crumble.

[pause]

So, many years later, the people of San Francisco raised the money to rebuild it, this time in stronger materials, so that it could last.

[long-pause]

It is still there today.

[pause]

People come to sit by the water, and feed the ducks, and take wedding photographs beneath the dome.

[pause]

It is one of the most peaceful places in the whole city.

[pause]

And it is a quiet reminder of the year when San Francisco invited the whole world to come and see how it had risen again.

[long-pause]

When the exposition opened, a little less than nine years after the fire, it would have been hard for a visitor to believe that most of the city had once been ashes.

[pause]

The streets were busy.

[pause]

The cable cars were running again, climbing the hills with their bells ringing.

[pause]

And in the evenings, the fog still came rolling in through the Golden Gate, just as it always had.
