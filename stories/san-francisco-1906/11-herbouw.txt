Even before the fire was out, some people were already thinking about how to rebuild.

[long-pause]

One of them was a young banker named Amadeo Giannini.

[pause]

He was the son of Italian immigrants, and he had grown up in the city, working on the waterfront, selling fruit and vegetables with his stepfather.

[pause]

Only two years before the earthquake, he had opened a small bank in North Beach, called the Bank of Italy.

[pause]

It was a bank for ordinary working people.

[pause]

Fishermen, and bakers, and shopkeepers, and labourers, people the big banks often did not want as customers.

[long-pause]

On the morning of the earthquake, Giannini hurried to his bank.

[pause]

As the fire came closer, he borrowed two wagons from his stepfather's produce business, with their horses.

[pause]

He loaded the bank's gold and papers into the wagons, and then, it is said, he covered them with crates of oranges and vegetables, so that no one would guess what was underneath.

[pause]

And he drove them out of the city, to his home, many miles to the south.

[long-pause]

The other big banks were closed.

[pause]

Their vaults were too hot to open, and would be for weeks.

[pause]

But just a few days after the fire, Giannini was back on the waterfront.

[pause]

He set up a plank of wood across two barrels, and made it his desk.

[pause]

And there, in the open air, he began to lend money to people who wanted to rebuild.

[long-pause]

He did not ask for much paperwork.

[pause]

He looked people in the eye, and listened to their plans, and shook their hands.

[pause]

A fisherman who needed a new boat.

[pause]

A baker who needed a new oven.

[pause]

A family who needed timber for a new house.

[long-pause]

It is said that nearly everyone paid him back.

[pause]

His little bank grew, and grew, and many years later, it would become one of the largest banks in the world.

[pause]

But it began on a plank, beside the water, in a city that had just lost almost everything.

[long-pause]

As the months passed, the people living in tents needed something more solid.

[pause]

Winter was coming, and the tents were cold and damp when the rain came.

[long-pause]

So the city and the relief committees began to build small wooden cottages.

[pause]

Thousands of them.

[pause]

They were very simple, just one, two or three little rooms, with a door, a few windows, and a small stove inside.

[pause]

And they were all painted the same dark green colour, the colour of park benches, so that they would blend in with the trees.

[long-pause]

Rows and rows of these little green cottages appeared in the parks and squares.

[pause]

Families moved in, and planted flowers by the doors, and hung curtains in the windows.

[pause]

They paid a small rent, just a couple of dollars a month.

[pause]

And when the camps finally closed, they were allowed to keep their cottage.

[long-pause]

So people hired horses and wagons, and carried their little houses away, through the streets, to a piece of land somewhere in the city.

[pause]

Some joined two or three cottages together, to make a bigger home.

[pause]

Some added a porch, or a second storey, or a garden fence.

[long-pause]

And some of these earthquake cottages are still standing today, more than a hundred years later.

[pause]

If you know what to look for, you can find them, tucked away on quiet streets, hidden inside larger houses.

[pause]

Small, humble, and still keeping people warm.

[long-pause]

All across the burned part of the city, the rebuilding began.

[pause]

First, the rubble had to be cleared away.

[pause]

Mountains of broken bricks, and twisted iron, and ashes.

[pause]

Horses and wagons carried it off, load after load, day after day.

[pause]

Much of it was dumped along the edge of the bay, and some of the new land made from it can still be walked on today.

[long-pause]

Then came the builders.

[pause]

Carpenters, and bricklayers, and stonemasons arrived from all over the country, looking for work.

[pause]

The sound of hammers and saws rang out across the city from morning till night.

[pause]

New buildings rose on the old foundations, some of them stronger and grander than before, with steel frames inside them.

[long-pause]

The work went astonishingly fast.

[pause]

Within three years, much of the burned area had been rebuilt.

[pause]

Visitors who came back could hardly believe it.

[pause]

Streets that had been nothing but ashes were now lined with new shops, and offices, and hotels.

[long-pause]

The Palace Hotel, which had survived the earthquake but not the fire, was rebuilt on the same spot.

[pause]

Its great open court, where the carriages used to drive in, became a beautiful dining room, under a glass ceiling filled with soft light.

[pause]

It is still there, and people still sit beneath that glass roof, and drink their tea.

[long-pause]

San Francisco had burned before, in the days of the gold rush.

[pause]

And each time, it had built itself up again.

[pause]

The old phoenix on the city's seal, rising from the flames, had never seemed more fitting.
