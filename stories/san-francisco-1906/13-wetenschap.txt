While the city was being rebuilt, a group of scientists set out to understand what had happened beneath it.

[long-pause]

Just a few days after the earthquake, the governor of California asked Andrew Lawson, the geologist from Berkeley, to lead a special commission.

[pause]

Its task was simple to describe, and very hard to do.

[pause]

To find out exactly what had happened on that April morning, and why.

[long-pause]

So Lawson and his colleagues set out into the countryside.

[pause]

They travelled on horseback, and on foot, and by wagon, following the line of the fault for hundreds of miles.

[pause]

They measured broken fences, and shifted roads, and twisted railway lines.

[pause]

They talked to farmers, and lighthouse keepers, and ranchers, asking them what they had felt, and heard, and seen.

[long-pause]

They found that the fault had torn open along almost three hundred miles, from south of San Francisco all the way to the northern coast.

[pause]

In places, they could see a long narrow crack running across the fields, like a seam in the earth.

[pause]

And they found that the land on the western side had moved to the northwest, sometimes by more than fifteen feet.

[long-pause]

{img:scientist-study-maps}
They collected thousands of reports from all over California.

[pause]

Where had the shaking been strongest?

[pause]

Which buildings had stood firm, and which had fallen?

[pause]

On the map, they coloured in the places where the damage had been worst.

[pause]

And they noticed something important.

[long-pause]

The worst damage was often on soft ground.

[pause]

On land made by filling in marshes and shallow bays, like the old gold rush waterfront in San Francisco.

[pause]

Buildings standing on solid rock, on the hills, had often come through much better.

[pause]

The ground beneath your feet, it turned out, mattered just as much as the building itself.

[long-pause]

After two years of work, the commission published its report.

[pause]

It was a huge book, filled with maps, and photographs, and careful measurements.

[pause]

And it is still considered one of the most important studies of an earthquake ever made.

[long-pause]

One of the scientists who worked on it was a quiet professor named Harry Fielding Reid.

[pause]

He looked at all those measurements, and he had an idea.

[pause]

He imagined the rock along the fault slowly bending, year after year, as the land on each side was dragged in different directions.

[pause]

Storing up energy, like a stretched spring, or a bent stick.

[pause]

Until at last it broke, and snapped back.

[long-pause]

He called this the theory of elastic rebound.

[pause]

It is the same idea we met earlier in our story, and it is still how scientists understand earthquakes today.

[pause]

It all began with fences, and roads, and careful notebooks, on the green hills of California.

[long-pause]

In the years that followed, scientists built instruments to listen to the ground.

[pause]

They were called seismographs.

[pause]

The earliest ones had a heavy weight hanging from a frame, and a fine needle that traced a line on a turning drum of paper.

[pause]

When the ground moved, the frame moved with it, but the heavy weight stayed almost still.

[pause]

And the needle drew a wavy line, a picture of the shaking.

[long-pause]

Today, there are many thousands of these instruments all over the world, far more sensitive than the old ones.

[pause]

In California, they listen to the ground day and night.

[pause]

They hear tiny tremors that no person could ever feel.

[pause]

And they help engineers design buildings, and bridges, and pipes that can bend and sway, instead of breaking.

[long-pause]

San Francisco learned its lessons too.

[pause]

Soon after the fire, the city built a special water system just for fighting fires.

[pause]

It had its own reservoirs, high on the hills, and its own strong pipes.

[pause]

It had pumping stations that could take water straight from the bay.

[pause]

And beneath many of the city's crossroads, new underground cisterns were built, great round tanks full of water, waiting quietly, just in case.

[pause]

If you look down at the street in San Francisco, you can sometimes see a circle of bricks in the pavement, marking where one of them lies.

[long-pause]

And every year, on the eighteenth of April, the city remembers.

[long-pause]

Very early in the morning, before sunrise, people gather at Lotta's Fountain, on Market Street.

[pause]

The same tall iron fountain, where people once left notes for their lost friends and families.

[long-pause]

For many years, some of the people who came were survivors of the earthquake themselves.

[pause]

At first, there were many of them.

[pause]

Then, as the years passed, there were fewer and fewer.

[pause]

Grandmothers and grandfathers, some in wheelchairs, who had been small children on that April morning, wrapped in blankets on the pavement.

[pause]

They told their stories, and they were given a place of honour.

[long-pause]

Now, the last of them have gone.

[pause]

But the people still come.

[pause]

Some wear old-fashioned clothes, long skirts and bowler hats, just like in the photographs.

[pause]

There are speeches, and a few quiet words.

[pause]

And at twelve minutes past five, as the sky begins to grow light over the bay, sirens sound across the city.

[pause]

And then there is a moment of silence.

[long-pause]

Afterwards, some people walk to the little hydrant on Church Street, and paint it gold again.

[pause]

And then they go and have breakfast, together.
