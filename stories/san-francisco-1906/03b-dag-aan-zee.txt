Before we come to the evening of our story, let's spend one more ordinary day in the city, the way its people did.

[long-pause]

We'll start early, down at the water, where the fishing boats come in.

[pause]

The fishermen here are mostly Italian, many of them from Genoa, and Sicily, and the little towns along the coast near Naples.

[pause]

Their boats are small and brightly painted, with a single triangular sail, called a lateen sail.

[pause]

People call them feluccas, and on a calm morning you can see dozens of them, gliding back through the Golden Gate with the tide, their sails shining white against the grey water.

[long-pause]

The boats are full of fish.

[pause]

Salmon, and sole, and sardines, and rock cod.

[pause]

And, in the winter months, great piles of Dungeness crab, with their broad reddish shells.

[long-pause]

Along the wharf, big iron pots of water are set over little fires.

[pause]

The crabs are cooked right there, on the dock, and sold to anyone who passes.

[pause]

The steam rises up into the cool morning air, and mixes with the smell of salt, and tar, and wood smoke.

[long-pause]

The fishermen sit on the edge of the wharf, mending their nets with wooden needles.

[pause]

They talk, and argue, and laugh in Italian, and sometimes one of them begins to sing a song from home.

[pause]

Seagulls sit on the posts nearby, watching everything very closely, and waiting for their chance.

[long-pause]

Along the shore, there are other fishing camps too.

[pause]

Chinese fishermen catch tiny shrimp in the shallow waters of the bay, and dry them in the sun on wide wooden platforms.

[pause]

The dried shrimp are packed into sacks, and sent all the way across the ocean, to be sold in China.

[pause]

Everyone in this city seems to have come from somewhere else, and everyone has brought something with them.

[long-pause]

Now, let's follow the smell of warm bread.

[long-pause]

In the days of the gold rush, a baker from France named Isidore Boudin settled in San Francisco.

[pause]

He made bread in the French way, long loaves with a crackling crust.

[pause]

But the bread here turned out a little different from the bread at home.

[long-pause]

To make it rise, the bakers used a sourdough.

[pause]

A soft, living dough, full of wild yeasts, which is kept from one day to the next.

[pause]

Each morning, the baker takes a little of yesterday's dough, feeds it with fresh flour and water, and uses it to start today's bread.

[pause]

And the rest is saved for tomorrow.

[long-pause]

Here, in the cool, foggy air by the bay, the dough grew wonderfully sour and tangy.

[pause]

The gold miners loved it.

[pause]

It was chewy, and filling, and it kept for days in a miner's pack.

[pause]

Some of the old miners were even nicknamed sourdoughs, because they always carried a little pot of starter with them, to bake bread wherever they went.

[long-pause]

By nineteen hundred and six, the Boudin bakery had been making bread for more than fifty years, always from that same living dough, fed and kept alive every single day.

[pause]

In the mornings, the bakers pull the loaves from the big brick ovens with long wooden paddles.

[pause]

The crust crackles as it cools.

[pause]

And the smell drifts out into the street, where the first customers are already waiting.

[long-pause]

We'll come back to this bakery later.

[pause]

Because there is a lovely story about what happened to that dough.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, let's take a streetcar all the way out to the western edge of the city, where the land ends, and the Pacific Ocean begins.

[long-pause]

Here, on a rocky cliff above the waves, stands the Cliff House.

[pause]

It looks like a castle from a fairy tale, painted white, with towers, and turrets, and pointed roofs, and a tall spire on top.

[pause]

It was built by a wealthy man named Adolph Sutro, who owned much of the land out here, and who wanted everyone in the city to be able to enjoy the ocean.

[long-pause]

Visitors come on Sundays, in their best clothes, to eat lunch in the dining rooms, and to look out through the big windows at the sea.

[pause]

Just off the shore, a few small rocky islands rise out of the water.

[pause]

They are called Seal Rocks.

[pause]

And they are covered with sea lions, dozens and dozens of them, lying in the sun, and barking, and sliding lazily into the waves.

[long-pause]

Children lean on the railings and laugh at them.

[pause]

Couples walk arm in arm along the cliff path.

[pause]

The wind is fresh, and full of salt, and the waves roll in, one after another, and break in white foam on the rocks below.

[long-pause]

Just beside the Cliff House, in a little cove, is another of Sutro's wonders.

[pause]

The Sutro Baths.

[long-pause]

It is an enormous glass building, like a great greenhouse, built over the edge of the sea.

[pause]

Inside, there are seven swimming pools, one after another, some fresh water, and some filled with sea water, each one a different temperature, from cool to warm.

[pause]

At high tide, the ocean itself flows in through a channel in the rocks, and fills the pools.

[long-pause]

Thousands of people can swim here at once.

[pause]

There are slides, and diving boards, and swinging rings hanging from the high iron beams.

[pause]

All around the pools rise rows of seats, where people can sit and watch, and listen to the band.

[pause]

And there is a museum too, full of strange and curious things collected from all over the world.

[long-pause]

It is a place of echoes, and splashing, and laughter, with the sound of the real ocean just outside the glass.

[long-pause]

As the sun sinks lower, the visitors begin to make their way home.

[pause]

The streetcars fill up with tired children, and wet bathing suits wrapped in towels, and bags of sweets.

[pause]

Behind them, the sea turns gold, and then pink, and then a deep, soft blue.

[pause]

And far out on the horizon, a low bank of fog is gathering, ready to come in for the night.
