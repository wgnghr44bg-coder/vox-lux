Not every part of the rebuilding was easy.

[long-pause]

Before the fire, some powerful people in the city had long wanted Chinatown moved.

[pause]

It stood on valuable land, close to the centre of the city, and they had their eye on it.

[pause]

After the fire, they saw their chance.

[pause]

There were plans to send the Chinese community away, to the far edge of the city, or even across the bay.

[long-pause]

But the people of Chinatown had other ideas.

[pause]

Many of them owned their land, or had long leases on it.

[pause]

The government of China itself protested, and sent officials to speak for them.

[pause]

And the merchants of Chinatown quietly made it clear that if they were forced to leave, their trade, and the money it brought to San Francisco, might leave with them.

[long-pause]

In the end, the plans were given up.

[pause]

And the people of Chinatown came home, and began to rebuild, on the same streets where they had lived before.

[long-pause]

{img:chinatown-new-rooftops}
A businessman named Look Tin Eli had an idea.

[pause]

What if the new Chinatown looked even more beautiful than the old one?

[pause]

What if it looked like the China that visitors imagined, with curving roofs, and painted balconies, and little towers like pagodas?

[long-pause]

So the new buildings were given upturned eaves, and roofs of green tiles, and railings painted in red and gold.

[pause]

Lanterns were hung along the streets.

[pause]

And visitors came to walk through it, and to eat in its restaurants, and to buy tea and silk in its shops.

[long-pause]

The Chinatown that people visit today, with its colourful rooftops and busy markets, is largely the Chinatown that was built after nineteen hundred and six.

[pause]

It was a way of saying, we are staying.

[pause]

This is our home.

[long-pause]

{img:paper-sons-ship}
The fire also changed many lives in a quieter way.

[pause]

In those years, the laws of the United States made it very difficult for people from China to come and live in America.

[pause]

But there was one important exception.

[pause]

Anyone born in the United States was an American citizen.

[pause]

And the children of American citizens could come to join them, even if they had been born in China.

[long-pause]

The city's Hall of Records, with its birth certificates and papers, had been lost in the fire.

[pause]

So when many Chinese men in San Francisco said that they had been born in the city, there was no way to prove that they had not.

[pause]

They became citizens.

[pause]

And then, in the years that followed, they could send for their children from China.

[long-pause]

Sometimes those were real sons and daughters.

[pause]

And sometimes they were young people from the same village, or the children of friends, who took on a new family name for the journey.

[pause]

People came to call them paper sons, and paper daughters, because their family ties existed only on paper.

[long-pause]

Before they sailed, they studied thick books of questions and answers about their new families.

[pause]

How many windows were in the family home?

[pause]

Where did the family keep the rice?

[pause]

Which way did the front door face?

[pause]

They memorised every detail, on the long voyage across the Pacific.

[long-pause]

{img:angel-island-hills}
When they arrived, many of them waited on an island in the bay, called Angel Island.

[pause]

It is a green, hilly island, with views of the Golden Gate, and the city in the distance.

[pause]

There, they were questioned by officials, sometimes for weeks, or even months.

[long-pause]

While they waited, some of them wrote poems on the wooden walls of the buildings where they stayed.

[pause]

Poems about the long journey, and about homesickness, and about the sea.

[pause]

Many of those poems are still there, carved into the wood, and people still come to read them.

[long-pause]

In the end, many thousands of families began their lives in America in this way.

[pause]

Their children, and grandchildren, and great-grandchildren became doctors, and teachers, and builders, and cooks, and shopkeepers, all over the country.

[pause]

And many of them can trace their story back to a single morning in April, and to a fire that burned a building full of papers.

[pause]

Out of something so sad, something new and hopeful had grown.
