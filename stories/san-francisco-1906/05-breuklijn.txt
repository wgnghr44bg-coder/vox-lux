Before we go on, let's step away from the city for a moment, and think about the ground itself.

[long-pause]

The surface of the Earth is not one solid shell.

[pause]

It is made of enormous pieces, called plates, fitting together a little like the pieces of a cracked eggshell.

[pause]

These plates are always moving.

[pause]

Very, very slowly.

[pause]

About as fast as your fingernails grow.

[long-pause]

Along the coast of California, two of these great plates meet.

[pause]

On one side is the Pacific plate, which carries most of the ocean floor, and a thin slice of California along with it.

[pause]

On the other side is the North American plate, which carries almost the whole of the continent.

[long-pause]

And they are not moving towards each other, or away from each other.

[pause]

They are sliding past each other, side by side.

[pause]

The Pacific plate is gliding slowly towards the northwest, while the rest of America stays behind.

[long-pause]

The long line where they meet is called the San Andreas Fault.

[pause]

It runs for hundreds of miles through California, from the deserts in the south, all the way up to the rugged coast in the north.

[pause]

In some places, you can see it clearly, as a long straight valley, or a line of little ponds, or a row of low ridges running across the land.

[pause]

In other places, it is hidden beneath fields, or forests, or the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:fault-valley-ponds}
Here is the important thing.

[pause]

Along most of its length, the two sides of the fault do not slide smoothly.

[pause]

They are pressed together, and they catch, and they stick.

[long-pause]

So the plates keep moving, deep down, but the edges are locked together.

[pause]

And the rock along the fault slowly bends, like a wooden ruler being gently pushed at one end.

[pause]

Year after year.

[pause]

Decade after decade.

[pause]

The strain builds up, silently, and invisibly.

[long-pause]

Until one day, the rock can bend no further.

[pause]

It slips.

[pause]

And in a few moments, the two sides of the fault jump forward, catching up on all those years of movement at once.

[long-pause]

That sudden slip is what we call an earthquake.

[long-pause]

In nineteen hundred and six, nobody yet understood this.

[pause]

A geologist named Andrew Lawson, from the university in Berkeley, had studied the long valley south of San Francisco, and had given the fault its name, after a small lake that lay along it.

[pause]

But no one knew how far it stretched, or what it could do.

[pause]

That knowledge would come later, and it would come, in large part, from what happened next.

[long-pause]

{img:dawn-over-bay}
It is now just after five o'clock, on the morning of Wednesday, the eighteenth of April.

[pause]

The sky over the bay is pale, and the first light is touching the hills.

[pause]

The city is still mostly asleep.

[long-pause]

A few miles out to sea, off the coast, not far from the Golden Gate, the rocks along the fault finally give way.

[long-pause]

At twelve minutes past five, there is a first gentle tremor.

[pause]

A shiver in the ground, that lasts for a few seconds.

[pause]

Some people half wake, and wonder what it was.

[pause]

In San Francisco, small tremors were not unusual.

[pause]

Many simply turned over, and closed their eyes again.

[long-pause]

About twenty seconds later, the real earthquake arrived.

[long-pause]

The ground began to move, strongly, and it kept on moving.

[pause]

For something like forty five seconds, perhaps a little longer, the whole city swayed and rolled.

[pause]

It is hard to imagine how long that feels, while it is happening.

[pause]

People later said it seemed to last for minutes.

[long-pause]

And all the while, the break in the rock was racing along the fault, in both directions at once.

[pause]

Northwards, up the coast, past the cliffs and forests, towards Cape Mendocino.

[pause]

And southwards, down the long valleys, towards the old mission town of San Juan Bautista.

[pause]

In less than two minutes, the fault had torn open along almost three hundred miles.

[long-pause]

In the countryside north of the city, near a place called Point Reyes, the land on one side of the fault shifted by as much as twenty feet.

[pause]

A fence that had run in a straight line across a field was suddenly broken in two, with one half moved far along from the other.

[pause]

A road was cut and shifted sideways.

[pause]

Rows of trees in an orchard were no longer lined up.

[long-pause]

Then, just as suddenly as it began, the shaking stopped.

[long-pause]

And for a moment, there was an extraordinary silence.

[pause]

Many people who lived through it remembered that silence more than anything else.

[pause]

No birds singing.

[pause]

No horses.

[pause]

No voices.

[pause]

Just a strange, deep quiet, and the soft grey light of the morning.

[long-pause]

And then, slowly, the city began to wake up to what had happened.
