San Francisco was not the only place that felt the earthquake that morning.

[pause]

Let's leave the city for a little while, and travel along the fault, and see what the people in the countryside saw.

[long-pause]

North of the Golden Gate, the land is green and rolling, with dairy farms, and cattle, and long views out over the sea.

[pause]

Here, near Point Reyes, the fault runs right through the fields.

[long-pause]

Early that morning, the farmers were already up, milking their cows in the barns.

[pause]

When the ground began to move, the cows lowed and stamped, and the milk pails rolled across the floor.

[pause]

One farmer later said that he saw the ground in his field ripple, like a sheet being shaken out.

[long-pause]

When it was over, and the farmers walked out into the morning, they found the land had changed.

[pause]

A long crack ran across the pasture.

[pause]

A path that had led straight to the barn now jogged sideways, and then carried on.

[pause]

A small tree that had stood on one side of a fence was now some distance further along it.

[long-pause]

Nobody had ever seen anything quite like it.

[pause]

Later, the scientists would come, with their measuring tapes and notebooks, and those broken fences would help them understand the whole earthquake.

[long-pause]

{img:lighthouse-coast-morning}
Further north, along the rocky coast, stood the lighthouses.

[pause]

Tall white towers, on lonely headlands above the sea, with keepers who lived beside them with their families.

[pause]

Every night, the keepers climbed the winding stairs, and lit the great lamps, and kept them burning until dawn.

[long-pause]

At the lighthouse on Point Arena, the tower was badly cracked by the shaking.

[pause]

The keepers were not hurt.

[pause]

But the great glass lens, which had turned faithfully every night for many years, was damaged, and the tower would have to be rebuilt.

[pause]

For a while, a temporary light was set up, so that the ships at sea would still be guided safely past the rocks.

[pause]

A few years later, a new, stronger tower rose in its place.

[long-pause]

Along the coast, and in the little towns inland, people came out of their houses, just as they had in the city.

[pause]

In the town of Santa Rosa, among the orchards and farms, many buildings were damaged, and it was a hard day for the people there.

[pause]

But neighbours helped each other, just as they did everywhere, and the town was rebuilt.

[long-pause]

{img:stanford-quad-arches}
To the south of San Francisco, the shaking reached a young university called Stanford.

[pause]

Its buildings were made of warm sandstone, with long rows of rounded arches, and red tiled roofs, set among palm trees and fields.

[long-pause]

It was early in the morning, and most of the students were asleep in their rooms.

[pause]

Several of the stone buildings were damaged, and a tall chimney fell.

[pause]

But the students were mostly safe, and they soon came out to see what had happened.

[long-pause]

On the front of one building, high above the ground, there had been a marble statue of a famous scientist, a man named Louis Agassiz, who had studied fish, and glaciers, and fossils.

[pause]

The earthquake had shaken the statue off its ledge.

[pause]

And it had fallen, head first, straight down into the concrete walkway below.

[pause]

There it stood, upside down, with its head buried in the pavement, and its feet in the air.

[long-pause]

Students and professors came to look at it, and some of them could not help smiling.

[pause]

The president of the university is said to have remarked that Agassiz had always been better in the abstract than in the concrete.

[pause]

The statue was soon lifted out, and put back in its place.

[pause]

And it is still there today, standing the right way up.

[long-pause]

{img:mission-san-juan-bautista}
Further south still, where the broken part of the fault came to an end, lay the little town of San Juan Bautista.

[pause]

It is a quiet place, with an old Spanish mission church, a grassy plaza, and a long view over the valley.

[pause]

The mission church was shaken, and some of its walls were damaged.

[pause]

But in time, it was repaired, and it still stands today, beside the plaza, with its bells.

[long-pause]

Curiously, the mission stands right at the edge of the fault.

[pause]

If you walk behind it, and look down the slope, you can see the flat valley below, where the land drops away.

[pause]

That little step in the ground is the San Andreas Fault itself.

[pause]

And there, today, a quiet path runs along it, where visitors can stand with one foot on each side.

[long-pause]

So on that spring morning, along almost three hundred miles of California, people woke up to the same strange shaking.

[pause]

Farmers, and lighthouse keepers, and students, and priests.

[pause]

All of them stepping outside, into the early light, and looking around at a world that had shifted, just a little.

[long-pause]

But the biggest story that day was still unfolding in the city.

[pause]

So let's go back to San Francisco, and to its streets, in those first hours of the morning.
