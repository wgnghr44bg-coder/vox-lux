Over the next three days, many thousands of people worked to save their city.

[pause]

Firemen, soldiers, sailors, policemen, and ordinary citizens, side by side.

[long-pause]

The firemen worked until they could barely stand.

[pause]

Their horses pulled the heavy steam engines from one street to the next, through smoke and falling ash.

[pause]

When one hydrant was dry, they searched for another.

[pause]

They pumped water from old underground tanks, called cisterns, which had been built beneath the streets many years before, in the gold rush days, and then almost forgotten.

[pause]

They pumped water from the sewers.

[pause]

They even pumped water from the bay, stretching their hoses as far as they could reach.

[long-pause]

{img:golden-hydrant}
At one hydrant, high up near the edge of the Mission district, the water was still flowing.

[pause]

It was a small, ordinary looking fire hydrant, at the corner of Twentieth and Church Streets.

[pause]

Firemen used it for hours, and with its water they saved a whole neighbourhood.

[pause]

Today, that little hydrant is painted gold.

[pause]

And every year, on the morning of the eighteenth of April, people come and give it a fresh coat of gold paint, to say thank you.

[long-pause]

{img:us-mint-building}
One of the most remarkable stories of those days took place at the United States Mint.

[pause]

The Mint was a solid, grey stone building, with tall columns at the front, a little south of Market Street.

[pause]

It was where coins were made.

[pause]

And in its vaults, at that moment, lay a great deal of the nation's gold.

[pause]

Millions and millions of dollars' worth.

[long-pause]

The man in charge of the Mint was named Frank Leach.

[pause]

When he saw the fire coming, he gathered his workers, about fifty of them, and a small group of soldiers.

[pause]

And he decided they would not leave.

[long-pause]

The Mint had its own well, in the courtyard, and its own pump.

[pause]

So the workers ran hoses through the building, and up onto the roof.

[pause]

They closed the heavy iron shutters on the windows.

[pause]

And as the fire swept past on every side, they sprayed water on the walls, and the roof, and on anything that began to smoulder.

[long-pause]

It was hot, and hard, and it went on for hours.

[pause]

But by the evening, the fire had passed by.

[pause]

All around, the neighbourhood was gone.

[pause]

But the old Mint was still standing, with its gold safe inside.

[long-pause]

In the weeks that followed, the Mint became a kind of bank for the whole city, because the other banks had lost their buildings.

[pause]

It is still there today, and people call it the Granite Lady.

[long-pause]

{img:telegraph-hill-italians}
And now, remember the wine.

[long-pause]

On Telegraph Hill, above North Beach, lived many Italian families, in small wooden houses clinging to the steep slopes.

[pause]

As the fire crept up the hill towards them, they found that there was no water left in the pipes.

[pause]

So they looked around for what they did have.

[long-pause]

In their cellars, many of them kept barrels of red wine, made from grapes they had pressed themselves.

[pause]

And so, the story goes, they rolled the barrels out into the street.

[pause]

They soaked blankets and sacks in the wine, and hung them over the walls and roofs of their houses.

[pause]

They beat out the sparks with wet cloths.

[pause]

And on the very top of the hill, a number of their little houses were saved.

[long-pause]

It is a wonderful picture to hold in your mind.

[pause]

A whole neighbourhood, working together on a steep hillside, with red wine running down the gutters, and the smell of it mixing with the smoke.

[pause]

Some of those houses still stand today, among the gardens and the winding stairways of Telegraph Hill.

[long-pause]

{img:rescuers-carrying-belongings}
All over the city, there were smaller acts of kindness, that no one wrote down.

[pause]

People helped their elderly neighbours down the stairs.

[pause]

Strangers shared carts and wheelbarrows, to carry what they could.

[pause]

Children led horses through the smoke.

[pause]

Doctors and nurses set up hospitals in parks, and in tents, and cared for anyone who came to them.

[long-pause]

People carried the strangest collection of belongings through the streets.

[pause]

Sewing machines.

[pause]

Birdcages.

[pause]

Family photographs, and bundles of letters.

[pause]

Some people pushed pianos, or dragged heavy trunks along the pavement, until the scraping sound of trunks on stone became one of the sounds of those days.

[long-pause]

Many people walked slowly west, away from the fire, towards the open spaces of the parks and the Presidio.

[pause]

They did not know where they would sleep that night.

[pause]

But they went together, and they looked after one another on the way.

[long-pause]

It is often said that disasters bring out the best in people.

[pause]

In San Francisco, in April of nineteen hundred and six, that was very often true.
