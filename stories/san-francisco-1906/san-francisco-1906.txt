A city of steep hills, and cable cars, and soft grey fog that rolls in from the sea each evening.

[long-pause]

We are not here to be frightened.

[pause]

{img:strait-without-bridge}
Let's begin at the water.

[pause]

On the western edge of North America, there is a narrow opening in the coastline, where the ocean meets a great sheltered bay.

[pause]

Today, a tall red bridge crosses that opening.

[pause]

But in our story, there is no bridge yet.

[pause]

Only the water, moving in and out with the tides, and the steep green headlands on either side.

[long-pause]

Sailors called this opening the Golden Gate.

[pause]

Beyond it, the bay spreads out wide and calm, with islands resting in its middle, and gentle hills rising all around its shores.

[pause]

It is one of the finest natural harbours in the world.

[long-pause]

And almost every summer evening, something quiet happens here.

[pause]

Out at sea, a bank of fog gathers.

[pause]

It creeps towards the land, low and white, and then it slips in through the Golden Gate, as if the bay were breathing it in.

[pause]

It spills over the hills, and flows down into the valleys, and covers the rooftops like a soft grey blanket.

[long-pause]

People who live here say you can hear the fog before you see it.

[pause]

Out on the water, the foghorns begin to call, low and slow, one after another.

[pause]

It is a sound that has been lulling this city to sleep for a very long time.

[long-pause]

{img:ohlone-shore}
Long before there was any city, this land was home to the Ohlone people.

[pause]

For thousands of years, they lived in small villages around the bay.

[pause]

They gathered acorns from the oak trees, and ground them into flour.

[pause]

They fished in the shallow water, and collected mussels and oysters along the shore.

[pause]

And they built light boats from bundles of tule reeds, which floated easily across the calm water.

[long-pause]

Over the centuries, the shells from all those meals piled up into great mounds along the shoreline.

[pause]

Some of them were as tall as a house.

[pause]

They were a kind of quiet record, showing how many generations had lived beside this water, and been fed by it.

[long-pause]

In the year seventeen seventy six, Spanish soldiers and missionaries arrived.

[pause]

They built a small fort near the Golden Gate, which they called the Presidio.

[pause]

And a few miles inland, in a sheltered valley where the fog was thinner and the sun came out more often, they built a mission church.

[long-pause]

[pause]

Its roof was held up by great wooden beams, tied together with strips of rawhide.

[pause]

Inside, the ceiling was painted with patterns in soft reds and ochres, in designs taken from Ohlone baskets.

[long-pause]

Its official name was the Mission of Saint Francis of Assisi.

[pause]

But people came to call it Mission Dolores, after a little creek that once ran nearby.

[pause]

It is still standing today, the oldest building in the whole city.

[pause]

And that is something worth remembering, as our story goes on.

[long-pause]

For many years, very little happened here.

[pause]

There was the fort, and the mission, and a few ranches where cattle grazed on the golden hills.

[pause]

Then, near a small cove on the eastern side, a little trading village grew up.

[pause]

It was called Yerba Buena, after a sweet-smelling wild mint that grew on the slopes nearby.

[long-pause]

Yerba Buena was a sleepy place.

[pause]

A few wooden houses, a store or two, and a muddy beach where ships would sometimes anchor to trade for cattle hides.

[pause]

By the middle of the eighteen forties, only a few hundred people lived there.

[long-pause]

In eighteen forty seven, the little village took a new name.

[pause]

It would be called San Francisco, after the mission, and after the great bay beside it.

[pause]

At the time, it hardly seemed to matter.

[pause]

It was still just a small, quiet settlement at the edge of the world.

[long-pause]

But that was about to change, very quickly indeed.

At first, the news travelled slowly.

[pause]

People heard the rumours, and shrugged.

[pause]

After that, nothing could stop it.

Within weeks, the little town had almost emptied.

[pause]

Shopkeepers closed their stores.

[pause]

Sailors left their ships.

[pause]

[long-pause]

And then, from all over the world, people began to arrive.

They came from Mexico, and Chile, and China, and Australia, and France, and Germany, and Ireland.

[long-pause]

They were called the forty-niners, after the year eighteen forty nine, when most of them arrived.

And almost all of them came through the Golden Gate, into the harbour of San Francisco.

[long-pause]

Soon, the harbour was full of ships.

[pause]

Hundreds of them, their tall masts standing together like a leafless forest.

[pause]

But many of these ships would never sail again.

[pause]

As soon as they dropped anchor, their crews jumped ashore and headed for the gold fields, leaving the ships floating empty in the bay.

So the people of San Francisco found other uses for them.

[pause]

Some ships were pulled up onto the mud, and turned into warehouses.

[pause]

One became a hotel.

[pause]

Another became a store, and another, it is said, even served as a jail.

[long-pause]

As the town grew, the shoreline was filled in with sand and rubble, and new streets were built out over the water.

[pause]

Some of those old ships were simply buried where they lay.

[pause]

Even today, when builders dig down beneath the streets of the city's financial district, they sometimes find the wooden hull of a gold rush ship, resting quietly in the old mud.

[long-pause]

[pause]

There was nowhere near enough room for them all.

[pause]

People lived in tents, and in shacks made of packing crates, and in canvas houses that glowed like lanterns at night, when a candle was lit inside.

[long-pause]

The streets were mud in the winter, and dust in the summer.

[pause]

Prices were astonishing.

[pause]

An egg might cost a dollar.

[pause]

A pair of boots might cost as much as a small house back east.

[long-pause]

And because nearly everything was built of wood and canvas, fire was a constant worry.

[pause]

In the first years of the gold rush, the young city burned several times.

[pause]

And each time, the people simply cleared away the ashes, and built again.

[pause]

It was already becoming a kind of habit here.

[pause]

Building, and rebuilding, and never quite giving up.

[long-pause]

In fact, the seal of the city shows a bird called a phoenix, rising from the flames.

[pause]

It is a reminder of those early days, and of a city that would not stay down for long.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-hills-1880}
The gold rush did not last forever.

[pause]

[pause]

Silver was found in the mountains of Nevada, and much of that wealth flowed back into the city.

[pause]

Banks were built, and theatres, and churches, and fine hotels.

[pause]

A railroad was laid across the whole continent, linking California with the rest of the country.

[long-pause]

The city spread out over its hills.

[pause]

Rows of wooden houses climbed the slopes, painted in soft colours, with bay windows and carved decorations under their roofs.

[pause]

[long-pause]

San Francisco had its share of colourful characters.

[pause]

[long-pause]

[pause]

Restaurants gave him meals, and theatres saved a seat for him.

When he died, thousands of people came to his funeral.

[long-pause]

By the end of the nineteenth century, San Francisco had become the greatest city on the western coast of America.

[pause]

It was a place of ships and trade, of new arrivals and big dreams.

[pause]

A city that had grown up almost overnight, and had never stopped moving since.

[pause]

It is a big city now, with more than four hundred thousand people.

[pause]

The biggest city anywhere on the western side of the country.

Men in bowler hats hurry along the pavements, and women in long skirts and wide hats step carefully around the puddles.

[pause]

Newspaper boys call out the headlines on every corner.

And down the middle of the street, the cable cars come gliding past, their bells ringing clear and bright.

The cable cars are one of the marvels of San Francisco.

The city's hills are so steep that horses could barely climb them, especially on wet days, when the stones were slippery.

Beneath the street, in a narrow slot, a thick steel cable runs in an endless loop, pulled along by great wheels in a powerhouse.

[pause]

If you stand quietly at the side of the road, you can hear the cable humming beneath your feet.

A little way up Market Street stands one of the grandest buildings in the city.

All around the court, balconies with white columns rise up, floor after floor, towards the glass ceiling far above.

Not so long ago, this land was nothing but sand dunes, blowing in the wind off the sea.

People said nothing would ever grow there.

Slowly, year by year, the dunes turned into meadows and forests.

It is a peaceful, green place.

[pause]

Busy, and proud, and full of life.

[pause]

A little noisy, a little rough around the edges, but always looking forward.

Along the shore, there are other fishing camps too.

[pause]

The dried shrimp are packed into sacks, and sent all the way across the ocean, to be sold in China.

Everyone in this city seems to have come from somewhere else, and everyone has brought something with them.

In the days of the gold rush, a baker from France named Isidore Boudin settled in San Francisco.
