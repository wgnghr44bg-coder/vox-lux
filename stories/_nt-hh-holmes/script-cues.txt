{img:001-police-officers-entering-dark-brick-building-1895}

Chicago. July, 1895. A few police officers stand in front of a large brick building on Sixty-Third Street. It is three floors high, with shops on the ground floor and rows of windows above. People in the neighborhood have walked past it every day for years. Today, the officers have a key, a lantern, and a long list of questions.

[long-pause]

{img:002-long-dim-hallway-many-doors-1890s}

Inside, the hallways are narrow. They turn in strange directions. Some doors open onto brick walls. Some rooms have no windows at all. One officer will later say that it felt as if the building had been made to confuse anyone who walked inside. They walk slowly, from room to room, and they write down everything they see.

[long-pause]

{img:003-empty-office-desk-papers-lamp-1890s}

The man who built this place is not here. He is sitting in a prison in Philadelphia, more than seven hundred miles away. He is calm. He is polite. He smiles at the guards. He says he has done nothing wrong. His real name is Herman Webster Mudgett. But the world knows him by another name. H. H. Holmes.

[long-pause]

{img:004-chicago-worlds-fair-white-city-lights-night}

Two summers earlier, this same city had shown the world its brightest face. The great World's Fair of 1893. White palaces, electric lights, and a giant wheel turning slowly in the sky. Millions of visitors came. Many of them were young, and alone, and looking for a room. Just a few miles away, Holmes had a building full of rooms.

[long-pause]

{img:005-detective-reading-letters-by-lamp-1895}

How many people did he really harm? Was his building truly a "murder castle," as the newspapers would call it? Or did the stories grow bigger with every telling? To answer these questions, a patient detective would follow a trail across America and Canada. And what he found would shock America.

[long-pause]

{img:006-oil-lamp-on-table-dark-room-calm}

This is the story of H. H. Holmes. Of the strange building on Sixty-Third Street, of the bright city of the World's Fair, and of the people who trusted the wrong man. We will tell it gently, and only with what the records show. So turn down the lights, settle in, and let's go back to the year 1861.

[long-pause]

{img:007-new-hampshire-farmhouse-snow-1860s}

Gilmanton, New Hampshire. A small farming town of hills, stone walls and white wooden churches. Here, on the sixteenth of May, 1861, a boy named Herman Webster Mudgett was born. His father, Levi, was a farmer. His family was strict and religious. Life was simple. There was snow in winter, mud in spring, and hard work all year round.

[long-pause]

{img:008-one-room-schoolhouse-new-england-1870s}

Herman went to the little schoolhouse in the village. He was clever, much cleverer than most of the boys around him. He liked books. He liked to take things apart and see how they worked. Later in life, he would tell many stories about his childhood. Some of them may be true. Many of them were probably invented. With Holmes, it is always hard to tell.

[long-pause]

{img:009-country-church-wedding-1878-from-behind}

In 1878, when he was only seventeen, Herman married a local girl named Clara Lovering. They had a son together. But Herman did not want to stay on a farm in New Hampshire. He wanted more. More money, more respect, more of the world. And he was ready to leave almost anything behind to get it.

[long-pause]

{img:010-university-of-michigan-old-building-1880s}

He decided to become a doctor. He studied medicine, and in 1884 he finished medical school at the University of Michigan in Ann Arbor. In those days, medical students worked with real bodies, given to the school for study. It was ordinary science. But young Mudgett noticed something that other students did not think about. He noticed how much money a death could bring.

[long-pause]

{img:011-life-insurance-office-1880s-papers}

Life insurance was a new and growing business in America. A family paid a little money each month, and if someone died, the company paid a large sum. In his later confession, Holmes claimed that even as a student, he had played tricks on insurance companies. We cannot be sure those stories are true. But money, and the easy ways to take it, would become the center of his whole life.

[long-pause]

{img:012-train-station-1880s-man-with-suitcase-from-behind}

After medical school, he moved from place to place. He left his wife and son behind. He worked a little here and a little there. He tried business ideas that never quite worked. Wherever he went, he left behind unpaid bills and people who felt cheated. And then, in 1886, he arrived in a city that was growing faster than any other place on Earth.

[long-pause]

{img:013-chicago-1886-busy-street-horse-carts}

Chicago. Fifteen years earlier, a great fire had burned much of the city. Now it was rising again, bigger than ever. Trains came in from every direction. Factories and stockyards filled the air with smoke. New buildings went up every week. Thousands of people arrived every month, looking for work and a new life. It was the perfect place for a man who wanted to start again.

[long-pause]

{img:014-man-signing-hotel-register-pen-1886}

When he arrived, he gave himself a new name. Not Herman Mudgett, but Henry Howard Holmes. Doctor H. H. Holmes. It was short, and it sounded respectable. He was twenty-five years old. He had good clothes, a soft voice and bright blue eyes. People who met him later said the same thing again and again. He was charming. He was easy to like.

[long-pause]

{img:015-englewood-chicago-street-corner-1887}

He went to a part of the city called Englewood, on the south side. It was a new neighborhood then, full of families, small shops and wooden houses. There were railway lines nearby, and it was easy to travel into the city center. At the corner of Sixty-Third Street and Wallace Street, there was a drugstore.

[long-pause]

{img:016-old-drugstore-interior-glass-bottles-1887}

The drugstore belonged to a woman named Elizabeth Holton. She needed help in the shop. Holmes, the young doctor, seemed perfect. He was polite, he knew about medicine, and he worked hard. Soon he was running the store. And not long after, he owned it.

[long-pause]

{img:017-chicago-1890-panorama-river-bridges-smoke}

Let's take a moment to look around the city that Holmes chose. In 1890, Chicago had more than one million people. Only sixty years earlier, it had been a small trading post by a muddy river. Now it was the second largest city in America. It had grown so fast that even the people who lived there could hardly believe it.

[long-pause]

{img:018-horse-streetcar-chicago-1890-passengers}

In the mornings, the streets filled with noise. Horse-drawn wagons carried coal, milk and ice. Streetcars rattled along their rails, pulled by horses or by cables running under the street. Newspaper boys shouted the latest headlines on the corners. And over everything hung the smell of smoke, from thousands of chimneys and factories.

[long-pause]

{img:019-tall-office-buildings-loop-1890-street-level}

Downtown, something new was happening. Builders had learned how to make tall buildings with frames of iron and steel. Ten floors, twelve floors, sixteen floors. People called them skyscrapers. Elevators carried office workers up and down all day long. Visitors from small towns stood on the sidewalks and stared up at them in wonder.

[long-pause]

{img:020-stockyards-1890-wooden-pens-distance}

To the south and west lay the great stockyards, where cattle and pigs arrived by train from all over the country. Tens of thousands of people worked there, and in the packing houses nearby. The work was hard and the pay was low. But it was work, and it drew families from Ireland, Germany, Poland and many other places.

[long-pause]

{img:021-boarding-house-parlor-1890-piano-lamp}

Many newcomers lived in boarding houses. For a few dollars a week, you got a small room, and meals at a long table with other lodgers. Young clerks, shop girls, typists and factory workers sat side by side at breakfast. They talked about their jobs, their families back home, and their plans for the future.

[long-pause]

{img:022-shop-girls-walking-home-evening-1890-from-behind}

For young women, Chicago offered something new. There were jobs in department stores, offices and telephone exchanges. A typist could earn her own money and rent her own room. On Sunday afternoons, they walked by the lake in their best hats, or went to the theater with friends. For many of them, it was the most exciting time of their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:023-englewood-wooden-houses-1890-quiet-street-trees}

Englewood, where Holmes lived, was quieter than the city center. It was a neighborhood of young trees, wooden houses with front porches, and new brick shops. Two important railway lines crossed nearby, so it was easy to reach downtown. Families moved there to have a little more space, and a little more peace.

[long-pause]

{img:024-drugstore-soda-fountain-1890-marble-counter}

Drugstores in those days were busy, friendly places. They sold medicines and powders, soaps and perfumes, cigars and writing paper. Many had a soda fountain, with a long marble counter, where people could buy a cold drink on a hot afternoon. A pharmacist was a respected man in the neighborhood. People trusted him with their health, and with their secrets.

[long-pause]

{img:025-gas-lamp-lighter-evening-street-1890-from-behind}

When evening came, a lamplighter walked down the street with his long pole, lighting the gas lamps one by one. Families sat down to supper. Children did their homework by the light of oil lamps. Somewhere a train whistle sounded, far away. It was an ordinary neighborhood, full of ordinary people. Which is exactly why no one paid much attention to the new doctor on the corner.

[long-pause]

{img:026-empty-drugstore-counter-evening-light}

For many years, people told a dark story about Mrs. Holton. They said she simply disappeared, and that Holmes told everyone she had moved to California. It became part of the legend. But in recent years, historians have looked again at the old records. Many now believe Mrs. Holton was not one of his victims at all. She may simply have sold the store, and moved on with her life. It is a good reminder. With Holmes, the legend and the truth are not always the same.

[long-pause]

{img:027-wedding-1887-minneapolis-church-from-behind}

In 1887, Holmes married again. Her name was Myrta Belknap. There was only one problem. He was still married to Clara, back in New Hampshire. He had never divorced her. Myrta and Holmes had a daughter, and Myrta lived mostly in a town outside Chicago. She knew very little about what her husband did in the city. And that was just how he liked it.

[long-pause]

{img:028-small-town-house-wilmette-1888-garden-fence}

Myrta and their little daughter, Lucy, lived in a small house in Wilmette, north of the city. Holmes visited them on weekends. He brought presents, played with his daughter, and seemed like a loving father. Myrta later said he was always kind to them. She never saw the other side of him. That was one of his greatest talents. He could be a completely different man, depending on who was watching.

[long-pause]

{img:029-empty-lot-construction-site-chicago-1888}

Across the street from the drugstore, there was an empty lot. Holmes bought it. And he began to build. He said it would be a fine building, with shops on the ground floor and apartments above. It would take years to finish. And the way he built it was very, very unusual.

[long-pause]

{img:030-workers-bricks-scaffold-1889-from-behind}

Holmes hired workers, and then, after a few weeks, he fired them. Then he hired new ones. Each group of workers only saw a small part of the building. No one, except Holmes, knew the whole plan. He also rarely paid his bills. He bought furniture, bricks and tools on credit, and when the sellers came to collect their money, he talked his way out of it, again and again.

[long-pause]

{img:031-three-story-brick-building-corner-1892}

By 1892, the building was finished. It was three floors high and took up a whole corner. On the ground floor were shops, including Holmes's own drugstore. The upper floors had dozens of rooms, long hallways, stairs and offices. People in the neighborhood simply called it "the Castle," because it was so big, with its little towers and bay windows on the corner.

[long-pause]

{img:032-narrow-staircase-dim-gaslight-1892}

Inside, there were some unusual things. Some hallways were very narrow. Some rooms had several doors. There was a large walk-in safe, and a big furnace in the cellar. Later, newspapers would describe secret passages, trap doors and terrible machines. Some of those stories were true. Many were made much worse by reporters who wanted to sell more papers. What is certain is that Holmes built the place in his own way, and kept its secrets to himself.

[long-pause]

{img:033-chicago-newspaper-headline-worlds-fair-1890}

And just as the Castle was being finished, Chicago received wonderful news. The city had won the right to hold a great World's Fair. It would celebrate four hundred years since Columbus had crossed the Atlantic. People from all over America, and all over the world, would come. Millions of them. And they would all need somewhere to stay.

[long-pause]

{img:034-chicago-skyline-smoke-1892-dusk}

Holmes saw the opportunity at once. He told people that part of his building would be a hotel for visitors to the fair. It was only a few miles from the fairgrounds, with trains running nearby. It seemed like a smart business idea. But some of the people who came to the Castle in those years were never seen again.

[long-pause]

{img:035-man-counting-money-desk-lamp-1892-hands}

All this time, Holmes was also running small schemes. He sold a machine that he claimed could turn water into gas for cooking. He sold medicines that did nothing. He bought furniture and never paid for it. When the furniture company came to take it back, it had somehow disappeared from the building. He was always busy, always smiling, and always one step ahead of the people he owed.

[long-pause]

{img:036-worlds-fair-construction-workers-jackson-park-1892}

On the other side of Chicago, by the shore of Lake Michigan, thousands of workers were building the fair. They turned swamps and sand into a dream city, with lagoons, bridges and enormous white halls. The most famous architects in America worked day and night. Everyone in Chicago was watching. It was going to be the greatest show the world had ever seen.

[long-pause]

{img:037-young-woman-arriving-chicago-train-station-from-behind}

At the same time, young women were coming to Chicago in large numbers. They came from farms and small towns, to work in offices, shops and factories. For the first time, many of them had their own money and their own rooms. It was a new kind of freedom. But it also meant that if one of them disappeared, there was often no one nearby to notice.

[long-pause]

{img:038-jewelry-counter-1890-glass-case-gaslight}

One of the first people we know about is Julia Conner. She came to Chicago with her husband, Ned, and their little daughter, Pearl. Ned worked at the jewelry counter in Holmes's building. Julia worked as a bookkeeper for Holmes. She was clever, and quick with numbers. Over time, Ned and Julia's marriage fell apart, and Ned moved away.

[long-pause]

{img:039-christmas-1891-window-candle-snow}

Julia and Pearl stayed. Pearl was about eight years old. Around Christmas of 1891, Julia and her daughter vanished. When people asked, Holmes said they had gone away, to live somewhere else. Nobody looked too closely. In a big, busy city, people came and went every day. Years later, Holmes admitted that they had died in his building.

[long-pause]

{img:040-typewriter-desk-office-1892-window}

In the spring of 1892, Holmes hired a new secretary. Her name was Emeline Cigrand. She was in her twenties, from a small town in Indiana. Friends described her as bright, pretty and kind. She had been working at a hospital in the town of Dwight, Illinois, and Holmes offered her a much better job, with a much better salary, in the big city.

[long-pause]

{img:041-letters-tied-with-ribbon-on-table}

Emeline wrote letters home to her family. She told them about her work, about the city and about the exciting fair that was being built. She also told them she was engaged to be married. Then, in December 1892, the letters stopped. Her family never heard from her again. Holmes later claimed she had left to get married. But no one ever saw her alive again.

[long-pause]

{img:042-worlds-fair-opening-day-crowds-may-1893}

On the first of May, 1893, the World's Fair opened its gates. People called it the White City. Huge white buildings stood around calm lagoons. There were fountains, gondolas, statues and gardens. When night came, thousands of electric lights switched on together. For many visitors, it was the first time they had ever seen electric light. It looked like magic.

[long-pause]

{img:043-ferris-wheel-1893-against-evening-sky}

In the middle of the fair stood something completely new. A giant wheel, more than two hundred and fifty feet tall, built by an engineer named George Ferris. Each of its cars was as big as a small house, and could carry dozens of people. They rose slowly into the sky, and from the top, they could see the whole fair, the lake, and the endless roofs of Chicago.

[long-pause]

{img:044-visitors-walking-white-city-lagoon-1893-from-behind}

Over six months, the fair had more than twenty-seven million visits. Families came from farms in Iowa. Business men came from New York. Visitors came from Europe, Asia and South America. They saw new machines, new foods, and new ideas. For one summer, Chicago was the center of the world.

[long-pause]

{img:045-court-of-honor-1893-golden-statue-lagoon}

If you had bought a ticket to the fair in 1893, it would have cost you fifty cents. You might have arrived by the new elevated railway, or by steamboat across the lake. You would walk in, and suddenly you would find yourself in the Court of Honor. A long, calm basin of water, surrounded by enormous white buildings. At one end stood a giant golden statue of a woman, holding her arms up to the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:046-electricity-building-1893-interior-light-bulbs}

In the Electricity Building, you could see the newest wonders of the age. Electric lights in every color. Telephones, and machines that recorded the human voice. The whole fair was lit by a new kind of electric current, made by the Westinghouse company with the ideas of the inventor Nikola Tesla. For many visitors, it was a first glimpse of the future.

[long-pause]

{img:047-wooded-island-japanese-temple-1893-garden}

In the middle of the lagoons was a quiet place called the Wooded Island. It was covered with trees, flowers and winding paths. Japan had built a beautiful wooden temple there, with curved roofs and painted panels. Visitors walked slowly across the little bridges, away from the crowds, and listened to the birds.

[long-pause]

{img:048-midway-plaisance-1893-crowds-pavilions}

From the main fair, a long avenue called the Midway Plaisance stretched for about a mile. It was full of restaurants, shows and villages from far away countries. There were camels and music, tents and towers, and smells of food that most visitors had never tasted. It was loud, colorful and fun. The giant wheel stood right in the middle of it.

[long-pause]

{img:049-womens-building-1893-white-facade-visitors}

There was also a Women's Building, designed by a young woman architect named Sophia Hayden. Inside, there were paintings, books and inventions made by women from all over the world. For many female visitors, it was a proud moment. The world was changing, and women were changing it too.

[long-pause]

{img:050-family-picnic-lawn-1893-fair-afternoon-from-behind}

Families brought picnic baskets and sat on the grass by the water. Children ran between the statues. Old farmers who had never left their counties stood in front of steam engines and shook their heads in amazement. Some visitors came back again and again, saving their money for one more day at the fair.

[long-pause]

{img:051-chicago-day-october-1893-huge-crowd-flags}

The biggest day of all came on the ninth of October, 1893. Chicago Day. It was the anniversary of the Great Fire, and the city wanted to celebrate how far it had come. More than seven hundred thousand people came to the fair on that single day. In the evening, there were fireworks over the lake, and the white buildings glowed in the night.

[long-pause]

{img:052-hotel-room-1893-iron-bed-lamp}

And some of those visitors stayed in rooms on Sixty-Third Street. We do not know exactly how many guests came to Holmes's building that summer. The records are not complete. Later, newspapers claimed that dozens, or even hundreds, of visitors had disappeared there. But historians today say there is no real proof for numbers like that. The truth, as far as we can tell, is smaller. But it is still terrible.

[long-pause]

{img:053-young-woman-reading-letter-window-1893-from-behind}

In 1893, Holmes met a young woman named Minnie Williams. She had grown up in Mississippi and Texas. Her parents had died when she was young, and she and her sister had been raised by relatives. Minnie had inherited money, and land in Fort Worth, Texas. She was gentle and trusting, and she fell in love with the charming doctor.

[long-pause]

{img:054-couple-walking-chicago-street-1893-from-behind}

Holmes did not tell her about his wives. He used a false name with her, and called himself Henry Gordon. Minnie moved to Chicago, and worked for him. She trusted him completely. Little by little, Holmes took control of her money, and of her land in Texas.

[long-pause]

{img:055-train-arriving-chicago-summer-1893-steam}

In the summer of 1893, Minnie's sister, Anna, came to visit. Anna was a teacher. She was excited to see her sister, and excited to see the famous fair. The two sisters had always been close. In early July, 1893, they both disappeared. Like Julia and Emeline before them, they were simply gone.

[long-pause]

{img:056-texas-prairie-town-1893-wooden-buildings}

After the sisters vanished, Holmes went to Fort Worth. Using papers that had belonged to Minnie, he took over her land. He planned to build another big building there, just like the one in Chicago. He also had a new idea for making money. Horses. He bought horses on credit in Texas, sent them north and sold them, and never paid for them. This crime would one day help to catch him.

[long-pause]

{img:057-worlds-fair-closing-night-lights-october-1893}

At the end of October, 1893, the World's Fair closed. Two days before the end, the mayor of Chicago, Carter Harrison, was shot and killed at his home by a disappointed office seeker. The final celebrations were cancelled. The great white palaces stood empty. Within a year, fires and wind would destroy most of them. The dream city was over.

[long-pause]

{img:058-quiet-street-englewood-autumn-1893-night}

The money was running out for Holmes too. He owed thousands of dollars to people all over Chicago. He had tried to get insurance money for a fire in his building, but the insurance company was suspicious, and refused to pay. People with unpaid bills were losing their patience. It was time, once again, for Holmes to move on.

[long-pause]

{img:059-denver-1894-street-mountains-distance}

In January 1894, he married for the third time. In Denver, Colorado, he married a young woman named Georgiana Yoke. She was from Indiana, and she believed his name was Henry Mansfield Howard. She had no idea about his other two wives, Clara and Myrta. And she had no idea about the people who had vanished on Sixty-Third Street.

[long-pause]

{img:060-texas-horses-corral-1894-dust}

In Texas, Holmes's horse trick went wrong. The seller realized he had been cheated, and went to the police. A warrant was written for the arrest of the man who had stolen the horses. Holmes left Texas quickly. He thought he had escaped once again. But that piece of paper would stay in a police office in Fort Worth, waiting for the right moment.

[long-pause]

{img:061-carpenter-workshop-tools-1890s}

To understand what came next, we need to meet another man. His name was Benjamin Pitezel. He was a carpenter from Indiana. He had worked with Holmes for years, on the building in Chicago and on many of his schemes. Pitezel was not a clever man. He drank too much, and he trusted Holmes completely. Holmes always seemed to have a plan.

[long-pause]

{img:062-family-photograph-frame-on-table-five-children}

Pitezel had a wife named Carrie, and five children. The oldest girls were Dessie and Alice. Then came Nellie, and a boy named Howard. And there was a little baby named Wharton. The family did not have much money. They moved often, from town to town, always waiting for the next plan that would make them rich.

[long-pause]

{img:063-insurance-policy-papers-fountain-pen-1894}

The plan was simple, at least on paper. Benjamin Pitezel would buy life insurance for ten thousand dollars, from the Fidelity Mutual Life Association in Philadelphia. Then they would fake his death. They would find a body, make it look like Pitezel, and collect the money. Ten thousand dollars was a huge amount in 1894. More than most people earned in twenty years.

[long-pause]

{img:064-st-louis-jail-cell-1894-iron-bars}

But before the plan could begin, Holmes got into trouble. In the summer of 1894, he was arrested in St. Louis, Missouri, for a different swindle. He spent a few days in jail. And there, in his cell, he met a famous train robber named Marion Hedgepeth.

[long-pause]

{img:065-two-men-talking-jail-cell-shadows}

Holmes needed a good lawyer, and Hedgepeth knew one. In exchange, Holmes promised to pay Hedgepeth five hundred dollars. And he told the train robber about the insurance plan. It was a mistake. Holmes never paid the money. And Hedgepeth did not forget.

[long-pause]

{img:066-philadelphia-row-houses-callowhill-street-1894}

In August 1894, Benjamin Pitezel went to Philadelphia. He rented a small house on Callowhill Street, under the name B. F. Perry. He hung up a sign in the window, and pretended to sell patents to inventors. It was all part of the plan. He was waiting for Holmes to arrive.

[long-pause]

{img:067-closed-door-row-house-early-morning-1894}

On the fourth of September, 1894, a man came to visit Mr. Perry. He found the house silent. Upstairs, he found Mr. Perry, dead. The police thought it was an accident, perhaps an explosion while working with chemicals. The man was buried as an unknown person. No one knew that his real name was Benjamin Pitezel.

[long-pause]

{img:068-insurance-company-office-philadelphia-1894}

A few weeks later, Holmes came to the insurance company with a lawyer, and with Alice, Pitezel's fifteen-year-old daughter. They said the dead man was really Benjamin Pitezel. The body was identified, and the company paid out the money. Most of it went to Holmes. Carrie Pitezel received only a small part. She believed her husband was still alive, and hiding, just as they had planned.

[long-pause]

{img:069-old-letter-envelope-postmark-1894}

But Pitezel was not hiding. He was dead. Holmes would later say many different things about what happened in that house. First he said it was a trick, with a body from a medical school. Then he said Pitezel had taken his own life. In the end, a court would decide that Holmes had killed him. The plan to fake a death had become a real death.

[long-pause]

{img:070-three-children-walking-railway-platform-from-behind-1894}

Then Holmes did something that still troubles everyone who reads this story. He told Carrie Pitezel that her husband was hiding, and that the children could go to see him. He took three of the children with him. Alice, who was fifteen. Nellie, who was eleven. And Howard, who was only eight.

[long-pause]

{img:071-steam-train-crossing-countryside-autumn-1894}

What followed was a long and winding journey. Holmes and the children traveled from city to city. Cincinnati. Indianapolis. Detroit. Toronto. At the same time, he kept Carrie and her other children in other hotels, often just a few streets away. And he kept his new wife, Georgiana, in yet another hotel. None of them knew the others were so close.

[long-pause]

{img:072-hotel-writing-desk-letter-pen-1894}

Alice wrote letters to her mother during the trip. She wrote about the cold, about being homesick, and about how much she missed her family. She wrote that Howard was being naughty, and that she wished she could see her mother again. Holmes never posted these letters. He kept them. Many months later, they would be found among his things.

[long-pause]

{img:073-toronto-1894-quiet-residential-street-autumn}

In October 1894, Holmes arrived in Toronto, in Canada, with the two girls. He rented a small house on an ordinary street. A neighbor later remembered him borrowing a shovel. A few days later, he left Toronto. The girls did not leave with him.

[long-pause]

{img:074-detective-agency-office-1894-map-on-wall}

Meanwhile, in his cell in St. Louis, the train robber Marion Hedgepeth decided to talk. He told the police about Holmes, and about the plan to fake a death for insurance money. The story reached the insurance company, and the company was alarmed. They hired the Pinkerton Detective Agency, the most famous detectives in America, to find Holmes.

[long-pause]

{img:075-boston-street-1894-men-in-hats-from-behind}

It took some time. But on the seventeenth of November, 1894, detectives arrested Holmes in Boston. They did not arrest him for murder. No one yet knew there had been any murder. They arrested him for the horses he had stolen in Texas, and for the insurance fraud. Holmes stayed calm. He thought he could talk his way out of this, just as he always had.

[long-pause]

{img:076-philadelphia-moyamensing-prison-1895-stone-walls}

He was taken to Philadelphia. He admitted to the insurance fraud. But he said Pitezel was alive. He said Pitezel had gone to South America, and that the children were with him, safe and happy. He spun story after story. The police did not know what to believe. And Carrie Pitezel still did not know where her children were.

[long-pause]

{img:077-woman-at-train-window-1895-from-behind-worry}

Carrie Pitezel was also arrested for a short time, because the police thought she had been part of the insurance fraud. She was tired and frightened, and she still did not understand what had happened to her family. Holmes told her not to worry. He told her the children were safe in London, with a kind woman who was looking after them. It was another lie.

[long-pause]

{img:078-detective-frank-geyer-desk-lamp-1895-from-behind}

In the summer of 1895, the case was given to a Philadelphia detective named Frank Geyer. He was a careful, patient man, with many years of experience. He promised Carrie Pitezel that he would find her children.

[long-pause]

{img:079-bundle-of-old-letters-detective-reading-1895}

Geyer started with the letters. The ones Alice had written to her mother, which Holmes had never posted. Each letter mentioned a city, a hotel or a street. They were like a map. Geyer decided to follow that map, city by city, in the footsteps of Holmes and the children.

[long-pause]

{img:080-hotel-front-desk-register-book-1895}

He went to Cincinnati first. Then to Indianapolis. In every city, he went from hotel to hotel, and from house to house, showing photographs and asking questions. Had anyone seen a man with two girls and a little boy? Did they remember the names they used? Slowly, patiently, he found traces of them everywhere.

[long-pause]

{img:081-telegraph-office-1895-messages-wires}

Geyer worked closely with the local police in each city. He sent telegrams back to Philadelphia almost every day. Newspapers across America began to follow his search. People were reading about it in their kitchens and on their trains. Everyone wanted to know the same thing. Where were the children?

[long-pause]

{img:082-detroit-1895-street-hotel-facade}

In Detroit, Geyer found more hotels and more rented rooms. He discovered something remarkable. In the autumn of 1894, Holmes had kept three groups of people in the same city at the same time. The children in one place. Their mother, Carrie, with the baby and her oldest daughter, in another. And his wife Georgiana in a third. Each of them waiting, and none of them aware of the others.

[long-pause]

{img:083-railway-timetable-papers-on-table-1895}

Geyer laid out the dates on paper, side by side. The train tickets. The hotel registers. Alice's letters. Slowly, a picture appeared. Holmes had moved everyone around like pieces on a board, always one step ahead, always making sure they never met. It was careful, cold, and very clever. And it showed Geyer exactly how this man thought.

[long-pause]

{img:084-newspaper-reading-man-train-carriage-1895-from-behind}

At times, the search seemed hopeless. Months had passed. People forgot faces, and some hotels had thrown away their old registers. But Geyer kept going. He wrote later that he could not stop thinking about the mother waiting at home, who still did not know what had happened to her children.

[long-pause]

{img:085-toronto-1895-row-of-houses-summer-day}

In July 1895, Geyer arrived in Toronto. He went from street to street, asking about houses that had been rented for only a short time the autumn before. It was slow work. Then, at last, he found a small house on a street called St. Vincent Street. A neighbor remembered the man, the two girls, and the borrowed shovel.

[long-pause]

{img:086-small-garden-gate-wooden-house-toronto-dusk}

On the fifteenth of July, 1895, Geyer and the Toronto police searched the house. There, they found Alice and Nellie. They had died many months before. Geyer had to tell their mother. Carrie Pitezel had waited and hoped for almost a year. Now that hope was gone.

[long-pause]

{img:087-indiana-small-town-street-1895-summer}

But there was still Howard. The little boy had not been in Toronto. Geyer went back to the United States, and kept searching. In late August, 1895, in Irvington, a small town near Indianapolis, he found a house that Holmes had rented the autumn before. And there, he found what was left of Howard. He was eight years old.

[long-pause]

{img:088-empty-swing-tree-autumn-light-calm}

Let us stop for a moment, and remember these three children, not as part of a crime story, but as who they were. Alice, fifteen, who wrote letters home and dreamed of seeing her mother. Nellie, eleven, who stayed close to her big sister. And Howard, eight, a lively little boy, full of energy, who did not always do as he was told. They deserved to grow up.

[long-pause]

{img:089-chicago-police-1895-lanterns-outside-brick-building}

While Geyer followed the trail, the police in Chicago finally turned their attention to the building on Sixty-Third Street. In July 1895, they began to search the Castle, room by room. They found the large safe, the furnace, and many odd features. In the cellar, they found evidence that convinced them people had died there.

[long-pause]

{img:090-newspaper-boys-selling-papers-chicago-1895}

The newspapers went wild. Reporters from all over the country came to Chicago. They wrote about secret rooms, hidden staircases and terrible machines. Crowds gathered outside the building. Some people even wanted to turn it into a museum, where visitors could pay to walk through the rooms. The story grew bigger every day.

[long-pause]

{img:091-building-fire-night-1895-smoke-firemen-distance}

Then, in August 1895, the Castle caught fire. Part of the building burned. No one ever found out for certain who started the fire, or why. Some people thought it was someone who wanted to hide evidence. Others thought it was angry neighbors. Many of the building's secrets were lost forever.

[long-pause]

{img:092-courthouse-philadelphia-1895-autumn-steps}

On the twenty-eighth of October, 1895, Holmes went on trial in Philadelphia. He was charged with only one murder. The murder of his friend and partner, Benjamin Pitezel. The courtroom was packed. Reporters filled every seat. People waited outside for hours, hoping to catch a glimpse of the famous doctor.

[long-pause]

{img:093-courtroom-1895-wooden-benches-tall-windows}

At first, Holmes asked to defend himself. He was a doctor, and he thought he was smarter than any lawyer. He spoke well and asked clever questions. But the evidence was strong. The insurance papers. The false names. The letters. And the long journey with the children, which Detective Geyer described carefully to the jury.

[long-pause]

{img:094-courtroom-witness-chair-1895-window-light}

One by one, the witnesses spoke. The man who had found the body on Callowhill Street. The insurance company officers. Carrie Pitezel, who told the jury about her husband and her children, and about the promises Holmes had made to her. Even Georgiana, his third wife, came to court. She had finally learned the truth about the man she had married.

[long-pause]

{img:095-jury-room-1895-table-chairs-lamp}

On the second of November, 1895, the jury went out to decide. They did not need long. After only a few hours, they came back. Guilty of murder in the first degree. Holmes showed no emotion. He was sentenced to death.

[long-pause]

{img:096-prison-cell-desk-writing-candle-1896}

While he waited in prison, Holmes wrote. He wrote his own life story, in which he said he was innocent. Then, a few months later, he did the opposite. A newspaper paid him a large sum of money for a full confession. In it, he claimed that he had killed twenty-seven people.

[long-pause]

{img:097-prison-corridor-1896-stone-arches-light}

In Moyamensing Prison, Holmes was a polite and well-behaved prisoner. He read books, wrote letters and received visitors. He asked for good food, and he talked with the guards about religion. Some of them said he seemed like a gentleman. It is hard to imagine. The same smiling face that had fooled so many people was still fooling some of them, right to the very end.

[long-pause]

{img:098-newspaper-printing-press-1896}

It was front-page news across America. But the confession did not add up. Some of the people Holmes said he had killed were actually still alive. Some of the stories could not have happened the way he described. It seems he wanted to be the most famous criminal in America. And he wanted the money. The truth, once again, was hidden in his lies.

[long-pause]

{img:099-moyamensing-prison-gate-morning-may-1896}

On the seventh of May, 1896, H. H. Holmes was executed at Moyamensing Prison in Philadelphia. He was thirty-four years old. Witnesses said he was calm until the very end. His last words were polite, and short. He was buried in a cemetery outside the city, and at his own request, his coffin was filled with cement, so that no one could ever take his body.

[long-pause]

{img:100-cemetery-old-headstones-trees-mist-calm}

For more than a hundred years, there were rumors that Holmes had somehow escaped. Some people said another man had been hanged in his place. In 2017, scientists finally opened the grave, with the permission of his family. They studied the remains carefully, including his teeth. It was him. The rumor was finally over.

[long-pause]

{img:101-st-louis-1890s-saloon-street-night-distance}

And what happened to the train robber, Marion Hedgepeth, who had first told the police about the insurance plan? Because he had helped the case, he was later released from prison. But he did not change his ways. In 1910, he was shot and killed by a police officer while trying to rob a saloon in Chicago. The city of Holmes had one last story to tell.

[long-pause]

{img:102-old-books-library-table-lamp-research}

So how many people did H. H. Holmes really kill? The honest answer is that we do not know. He confessed to twenty-seven, but his confession was full of lies. Newspapers later spoke of hundreds of victims, but there is no evidence for that. Historians today can be fairly sure of about nine.

[long-pause]

{img:103-city-map-1893-chicago-on-desk-magnifying-glass}

There may have been others. People who came to Chicago and simply disappeared, whose names were never recorded. In 1893, it was easy for a person to vanish in a big city, far from home. We may never know their names. And that, perhaps, is the saddest part of this story.

[long-pause]

{img:104-castle-building-mystery-1890s-dark-windows-night}

And what about the Castle? The famous "murder castle," with its trap doors and secret rooms? Historians who have studied the old plans and reports say the truth was less dramatic than the legend. Many of the strange features were exaggerated by reporters. It was a badly built, confusing building, made by a man who liked secrets. That was dark enough.

[long-pause]

{img:105-bookshop-window-1950s-crime-books-night}

The legend of Holmes grew again in the twentieth century. Books and articles retold the story, adding new details each time. In 2003, a writer named Erik Larson published a book called The Devil in the White City. It told the story of the World's Fair and of Holmes side by side. It became a bestseller, and millions of new readers learned his name.

[long-pause]

{img:106-candles-lit-in-dark-church-remembrance}

But in this story, the people who matter most are not Holmes. They are the people he hurt. Julia Conner, and her daughter, Pearl. Emeline Cigrand, who wrote happy letters home. Minnie Williams and her sister, Anna. Benjamin Pitezel. And his children, Alice, Nellie and Howard. They had names, and families, and dreams.

[long-pause]

{img:107-woman-standing-at-window-1896-from-behind-grief}

And we should remember those who were left behind. Carrie Pitezel, who lost her husband and three of her children. Emeline's parents, who waited for letters that never came. The families of Julia and Pearl, and of Minnie and Anna. For them, the story never really ended. They carried it for the rest of their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:108-detective-walking-away-street-1895-from-behind-calm}

And we can remember Frank Geyer, the detective who would not give up. He wrote a book about his search, called The Holmes-Pitezel Case. He followed a trail of old letters across America and Canada, because he had made a promise to a mother. Without him, we might never have known what really happened.

[long-pause]

{img:109-modern-quiet-street-chicago-south-side-evening}

Today, nothing is left of the Castle on Sixty-Third Street. The building was torn down in 1938. In its place, there is a post office. People come and go, buying stamps and sending parcels. Most of them have no idea what once stood there. And maybe that is how it should be.

[long-pause]

{img:110-jackson-park-lagoon-quiet-evening-museum-reflection}

And the White City? Almost all of it is gone too. The great white palaces burned, or were taken down, soon after the fair. But one building survived. Today it is the Museum of Science and Industry. And in Jackson Park, by the lake, the quiet lagoons are still there, reflecting the evening sky, just as they did in 1893.

[long-pause]

{img:111-lake-michigan-shore-calm-dusk-gentle-waves}

The bright summer of 1893 is long gone. The crowds, the music, the giant wheel turning slowly in the sky. And the strange brick building on Sixty-Third Street, with its narrow halls and closed doors. All of it has faded into the past, like voices in another room.

[long-pause]

{img:112-old-photograph-album-closed-on-table-lamp}

What remains are the records. Old letters, court papers and newspapers, slowly turning yellow in archives. And the names of the people who deserve to be remembered. We have told their story tonight, as carefully and honestly as we could.

[long-pause]

{img:113-single-candle-flame-dark-room-calm}

Thank you for walking with me through old Chicago tonight. Let the bright lights of the fair grow dim, and the city fall asleep. Rest now. Let the night be still. Good night.
