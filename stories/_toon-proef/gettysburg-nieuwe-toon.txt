In 1863, Gettysburg had only about two thousand four hundred people, fewer than a big school today.

Picture yourself walking into Pennsylvania on a soft spring morning in 1863. The apple trees on the hillsides are in blossom, pink and white. You pass farmhouses and barns, and long lines of wooden fences. A cow lifts its head as you go by, then goes back to its grass. Somewhere behind you, a farmer's wagon creaks along with sacks of grain for the mill. Now, you might expect a famous town to be big and busy. But Gettysburg is small. About two thousand four hundred people live here. That's fewer than in many big schools today. The houses are neat, of brick and wood, along a few straight streets. A man named James Gettys drew those streets about eighty years ago, and sold the plots one by one. I've always liked that. The whole town still carries the name of the man who sketched it out. Would you have guessed, walking in, that one day the whole world would know this name?

Ten roads met in Gettysburg, like the spokes of a wagon wheel meeting at the hub.

So what makes this quiet little town special? Honestly, it's the roads. No fewer than ten of them come together here, from every direction. If you looked at a map, you'd see them meet like the spokes of a wheel. There's the Chambersburg Pike, coming in from the mountains to the west. There's the road to Baltimore, running south. And there are roads to Harrisburg, York and Hanover. Farmers bring their produce in along these roads. Stagecoaches rattle through every day, raising a little dust. Since 1858, there's even a small railway from Hanover. For a town this size, that's rather grand. You might call Gettysburg a sleepy place. Yet all those roads mean that sooner or later, everyone passes through. Keep them in mind. A little later in our story, they'll matter a great deal.

From the cupola on the seminary roof, you could see for miles, all the way to the blue mountains.

Let's climb up and look. On a ridge to the west stands a fine brick seminary, with a little white cupola on the roof. You go up the narrow wooden stairs. The steps creak under your feet. At the top, you push open a small door, and the evening air touches your face. Below you, the land rolls gently up and down in long, low ridges, covered with fields and woods and orchards. To the south, a long ridge runs away for about two miles and ends at two rocky, wooded hills. The local people call them the Round Tops. Far off, the blue line of the South Mountains rises along the horizon. Stand here a moment. The town below. The ridges and the farms. And the mountains, turning purple as the sun goes down.

Some older people in Gettysburg still spoke German at home, two or three generations after their families arrived.

Now, who are your neighbours here? Mostly farmers and craftsmen, with a few shopkeepers, teachers and ministers. Many of their families came from Germany, Scotland or Ireland. You'll hear names like Weikert, Trostle and Shriver. And in the evening, by the stove, some of the older people still speak German. It's a funny mix of old country words and English ones, and visitors from the city sometimes smile at it. Of course, in a town this small, nothing stays secret for long. If a new bonnet appears in church on Sunday, everyone has talked about it by Monday. I find that rather comforting, don't you? On Sunday mornings, the bells ring out across the rooftops, one after another. It's a peaceful, ordinary place. And tonight, it's just waiting for you to settle in.
