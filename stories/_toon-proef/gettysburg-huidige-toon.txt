In 1863, Gettysburg had only about two thousand four hundred people, fewer than a big school today.

It is the spring of 1863, in the south of Pennsylvania. The apple trees on the hillsides are in blossom, pink and white. The fields along the roads are green with young wheat and corn. Farmhouses stand among their barns and springhouses, with long lines of wooden fences between them. Cows graze in the meadows beside the creeks. A farmer's wagon creaks slowly along the road, loaded with sacks of grain for the mill. And in the middle of all this green, there is a small country town. It is called Gettysburg. About two thousand four hundred people live here. That is fewer than in many big schools today. The houses are neat, of brick and wood, along a few straight streets. A church spire or two rises above the rooftops. The town was laid out about eighty years earlier by a man named James Gettys. He drew the streets and sold the plots, and the town still carries his name.

Ten roads met in Gettysburg, like the spokes of a wagon wheel meeting at the hub.

What makes Gettysburg a little unusual is its roads. No fewer than ten of them come together here, from every direction. On a map, they look like the spokes of a wheel meeting at the hub. There is the Chambersburg Pike, coming in from the mountains to the west. There is the road to Baltimore, running south. There are roads to Harrisburg and York and Hanover, to the north and east. And there are roads to Emmitsburg and Taneytown, just a few miles away in Maryland. Farmers bring their produce into town along these roads. Merchants send their goods out along them. Stagecoaches and wagons pass through every day, raising a little dust in dry weather. Since 1858, there has also been a small railway, coming in from Hanover in the east. For a town of this size, Gettysburg is very well connected. Remember those roads. A little later, they will matter a great deal.

From the cupola on the seminary roof, you could see for miles, all the way to the blue mountains.

Around the town, the land rolls gently up and down in long, low ridges. They are covered with fields and woods and orchards. To the west, there is a ridge with a Lutheran seminary on top. It is a fine brick building with a little white cupola on the roof. From up there, a person can see for miles across the countryside. To the south, just outside the town, there is a hill with a cemetery on it. From there, a long and gentle ridge runs south for about two miles. At its end stand two rocky, wooded hills. The local people call them the Round Tops. Further off, to the west and north, the blue line of the South Mountains rises along the horizon. If you stood in the cupola on a clear evening, you would see it all at once. The town below. The ridges and the farms. And the mountains, turning purple as the sun went down behind them.

Some older people in Gettysburg still spoke German at home, two or three generations after their families arrived.

The people of Gettysburg are mostly farmers and craftsmen. There are shopkeepers and teachers, ministers and lawyers too. Many of their families came here from Germany or Scotland or Ireland, two or three generations ago. Many still have German names, like Weikert, Trostle and Shriver. Some of the older people still speak German at home, by the stove in the evening. The local German is a mix of old country words and English ones. Visitors from the cities sometimes smile at it. These are hardworking, churchgoing people. They know their neighbours, and they know their neighbours' business too. Nothing much stays secret in a town of this size. If a new bonnet appears in church on Sunday, everyone has talked about it by Monday. And on Sunday mornings, the church bells ring out across the rooftops, one after another, calling them to worship. It is a peaceful, ordinary, pleasant place, much like hundreds of other small towns across the country.
