Before they leave China by sea, Marco describes the great port of Zaiton, which we now call Quanzhou. It lies on the southeast coast of China.

He says it is one of the two greatest ports in the world. For every ship of pepper that sails to Egypt to bring pepper to Europe, he says, a hundred ships come to Zaiton.

--

Quanzhou in 1292 is a city of the whole world.

There are merchants from Persia and Arabia, from India and Korea and Japan. There are mosques for the Muslim merchants and Hindu temples for the Indian merchants. Some of their carved stones can still be seen today. There are Christian churches and Buddhist temples too, all side by side.

{img:old-mosque-stone-gate-quanzhou-merchants-arab-chinese}
The harbour is full of ships. There are great Chinese junks with four masts and sails of bamboo matting. Their hulls are divided into separate sealed rooms. So if one part springs a leak, the ship does not sink. Europe will not use this clever idea for hundreds of years.

--

Marco describes the big Chinese ships he will sail on. Each one has sixty cabins for the merchants, each private and comfortable. The hulls are made of double planks, nailed with iron and sealed with lime and oil.

They are so big that they need two or three hundred sailors. And they carry huge cargoes, thousands of baskets of pepper.

{img:junk-deck-cabins-bamboo-sails-sailors-loading-pepper-baskets}
Behind them they tow smaller boats, which are big ships in their own right. When there is no wind, these smaller boats tow the great ship along.

--

In the spring of 1292, the fourteen great ships of the Great Khan set sail from Zaiton. On board are the Polos, the young bride, her escort and six hundred people. The northeast wind fills their sails, and they head south into the open sea.

Behind them, the coast of China slowly sinks below the horizon, with its hills and its pagodas and its great harbour. Marco stands at the rail and watches it go. He will never see it again. And ahead of him lies a long, uncertain voyage across seas he has never sailed.
