Let us spend an evening in a Mongol camp on the grasslands, on the way to the court of the Great Khan.

The camp is a small group of round felt tents, called gers, standing on the open grass near a river. The herds graze around them. In the distance lie low, rolling hills under an enormous sky.

--

A ger is a clever and beautiful home. Its round wall is made of a wooden lattice that folds up like a fan. Long, thin poles, painted red or orange, run from the top of the wall to a wooden ring in the middle of the roof, like the spokes of a wheel. Over this frame go thick layers of felt, made from sheep's wool. In the middle of the roof is an opening for the smoke and the light.

The door always faces south, toward the sun and away from the cold north wind.

Inside, everything has its place. In the middle is the fire, the most sacred spot in the ger. At the back, opposite the door, is the place of honour, where the head of the family and the guests sit. The men's things are on the left as you come in. The women's things, the pots and the food, are on the right.

--

Marco writes that the Mongols are very careful about their customs in the ger. You must not step on the threshold when you come in. You must not throw water on the fire or poke it with a knife, because a spirit lives in the fire. A guest who forgets these rules may give great offence. So travellers watch their hosts, and copy what they do.

He says the biggest gers are carried on carts without being taken down. The largest carts are pulled by more than twenty oxen. A friar who visited the Mongols saw these great carts rolling across the grass, like a whole city on the move.

{img:great-ox-cart-carrying-whole-felt-tent-steppe-moving}
In the evening the herds come home. The women milk the mares. The milk is poured into a big leather bag by the door and beaten with a wooden stick, thousands of times, until it turns sour and fizzy. This is the Mongols' favourite drink.

--

Supper is mutton boiled in a big pot over the fire, with the broth drunk from bowls, and cheese and dried curds. Guests are always welcome and always fed. On the steppe, a traveller turned away might die of cold or hunger. So taking in strangers is a sacred duty.

After supper the family sits around the fire. Someone plays a fiddle with a horse's head carved at the top. They sing the long songs of the steppe, slow and sad and beautiful, about horses and mothers and the sky.

{img:horsehead-fiddle-player-singing-ger-fire-family-listening}
Then they lie down to sleep on felt mats and furs around the fire. Outside, the horses stamp and the dogs bark, and the stars turn slowly across the great sky over the sleeping steppe.
