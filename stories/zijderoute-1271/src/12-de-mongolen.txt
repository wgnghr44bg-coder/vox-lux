Who are the Mongols, whose empire stretches across the whole of Asia, in 1271?

They are a nomadic people, from the great grasslands of Mongolia, in the heart of Asia, north of China. For thousands of years, they and their ancestors have lived on the steppe: wide, rolling plains of grass, under an enormous sky, with mountains on the horizon, hot in summer, and terribly cold in winter.

--

They live in round tents of felt, called gers, or yurts, which can be taken down, and loaded onto carts, or camels, and moved, in a few hours. They move with the seasons, from pasture to pasture, with their herds: sheep, and goats, and cattle, and yaks, and camels, and above all, horses.

The Mongols live on horseback. Children learn to ride before they can walk. They drink mare's milk, fermented into a slightly alcoholic drink, called kumis, which they love. They eat mutton, and dried curds, and cheese, and yoghurt. They wear long coats of felt and fur, and leather boots, and fur hats.

{img:mongol-woman-milking-mare-ger-camp-morning-children-horses}
Marco writes about them with admiration. He says that the Mongol women do all the work of the household, and buy and sell, and look after the herds, and drive the carts, while the men hunt, and fight, and look after the hawks. He says that the Mongols are very hardy, and can ride for days, without food, living on mare's milk, and the blood of their horses, and sleeping on the ground, in their armour.

--

For centuries, the Mongols were divided into many tribes, often fighting each other. Then, at the beginning of the thirteenth century, a great leader united them: Temujin, who took the name Genghis Khan, which means something like universal ruler.

Genghis Khan and his armies conquered northern China, and Central Asia, and Persia. His sons and grandsons went on, and conquered Russia, and Korea, and the Middle East, and southern China. By 1271, the Mongol empire is the largest land empire that has ever existed, stretching from Korea to Hungary, from Siberia to the borders of India.

The conquests were terrible, and many cities were destroyed, and many people lost their lives. We will not dwell on that tonight. But once the conquests were over, the Mongol rulers brought a kind of order, and peace, to Asia, and opened the roads to trade.

--

One of the most remarkable things about the Mongol empire is its postal system, called the yam.

All along the main roads of the empire, every twenty-five or thirty miles, there are post stations, with inns, and stables, and hundreds of horses, ready for the messengers of the Khan. Marco says there are more than ten thousand of these stations, and three hundred thousand horses.

{img:post-station-yam-horses-stables-messenger-arriving-dust}
The Khan's messengers, carrying his letters and orders, ride from one station to the next, at full gallop, and change horses at each one, and ride on. They wear belts hung with bells, so that the people at the next station hear them coming, and have a fresh horse ready. In this way, Marco says, a message can travel two hundred, or even three hundred miles, in a day and a night.

--

There are runners too, on foot, who carry messages, and small packages, from station to station, three miles apart, running, with bells on their belts. And so, Marco says, the Great Khan receives news, and fruits, from places ten days' journey away, in a day and a night.

Travellers with a golden tablet, a paiza, like the Polos, can use the post stations too, and get fresh horses, and food, and lodging, at each one, and travel quickly, and safely, across the empire.

{img:runner-with-bells-belt-road-countryside-china-morning}
And so, as the Polos come into the lands of the Great Khan, his messengers ride ahead of them, to tell him that the Latin merchants, who he sent to the Pope, are coming back at last.
