Kublai Khan, in 1275, is about sixty years old.

He is the grandson of Genghis Khan. Marco describes him: of good stature, neither tall nor short, but of medium height, well built, with a fair complexion, sometimes rosy, like a rose, and black eyes, and a well-shaped nose.

--

He became Great Khan, the ruler of all the Mongols, in 1260, after a struggle with his brother. And in 1271, the year the Polos left Venice, he declared a new dynasty, in China, the Yuan dynasty, and became emperor of China.

He is a great ruler. He is clever, and curious, and ambitious, and tolerant. Unlike his grandfather, he loves China, and its culture, and has learned to speak Chinese, although he still prefers Mongol customs, and loves to hunt, and drink kumis, and live in tents, in summer, on the grasslands.

{img:emperor-studying-scrolls-advisers-chinese-mongol-palace}
He is interested in everything: in religions, and sciences, and medicine, and astronomy, and engineering, and trade. He invites scholars, and priests, and experts, from all over the world, to his court: Chinese, and Persians, and Arabs, and Tibetans, and Turks, and Christians, and Muslims, and Buddhists, and Taoists. He listens to them all, and uses their talents.

--

He is tolerant of all religions. Marco writes that the Khan celebrates the great feasts of the Christians, and the Muslims, and the Jews, and the Buddhists, and that when he was asked why, he said that there are four prophets, worshipped by all the world: Jesus, and Muhammad, and Moses, and the Buddha. And he honours all four, so that whichever is the greatest in heaven will help him.

He has four wives, empresses, and many other wives. His favourite, his chief wife, is Chabi, a wise and gentle woman, who is a great influence on him, and helps him to rule. She is a Buddhist, and is kind to the people of China.

And Kublai has many children. His eldest son by Chabi, Zhenjin, has been named his heir, the crown prince, and is being educated in Chinese learning.

--

Kublai is a great hunter. Marco describes his hunts: the Khan rides out, with thousands of men, and hawks, and falcons, and dogs, and leopards, and lynxes, and even tigers, trained to hunt, and great eagles, which can catch wolves. He rides in a beautiful wooden room, carried on the backs of four elephants, covered inside with cloth of gold, and outside with lion skins, with his falconers beside him.

When cranes fly over, his barons tell him, and he opens the roof of the room, and lets his falcons fly, and watches them, as he lies on his couch. Marco says that no man in the world has ever had such pleasure, or ever will.

{img:elephants-carrying-pavilion-hunting-party-falcons-plain}
He also describes the great feasts of the Khan, on his birthday, and at the New Year, when all the lords, and officials, of the empire, thousands of them, come to the court, dressed in robes of the same colour, given to them by the Khan, of silk and gold, with belts of gold and silver, and pearls, and precious stones. And they bring him gifts: a hundred thousand white horses, and five thousand elephants, covered with cloth, carrying chests of gold and silver.

--

Is all this true? Marco loved numbers, and he loved to describe wonders, and perhaps he exaggerated, sometimes. Some of his readers, in Europe, thought he was lying, and called his book Il Milione, the book of a million lies.

But much of what he describes is confirmed by Chinese and Persian writers of the same time. Kublai Khan really was the greatest ruler in the world, and his court the most splendid.

{img:new-year-court-white-robes-lords-bowing-throne-hall-gold}
And Kublai likes Marco. He sees that the young man is clever, and observant, and learns languages quickly, and tells good stories. And he decides to use him.
