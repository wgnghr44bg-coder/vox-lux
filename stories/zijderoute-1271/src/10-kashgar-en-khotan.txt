Kashgar is a great oasis city, at the western end of the Taklamakan desert, at the foot of the mountains, where the roads of the Silk Road meet: from the west, over the Pamirs, and from the north, and from the south, and from India.

It is a city of merchants, and farmers, and craftsmen, of mud-brick houses, with flat roofs, and gardens, and orchards, and fields, watered by streams from the mountains, and canals.

--

Marco writes that the people of Kashgar live by trade and by crafts, and that they have beautiful gardens and orchards and vineyards, and fine estates, and grow a great deal of cotton. He writes that many merchants set out from here, to trade all over the world.

The bazaar of Kashgar is famous. Every week, people come from all the villages around, and from the mountains, and from across the desert, to buy and sell: horses, and camels, and donkeys, and sheep, and cotton, and silk, and carpets, and knives, and pots, and fruit, and melons, and grapes, and apricots, and nuts, and bread, fresh from the clay ovens.

{img:kashgar-bazaar-melons-apricots-bread-ovens-donkeys-crowd}
The melons of Kashgar, and the grapes, and the apricots, are among the sweetest in the world, grown in the hot sun, with water from the mountain snows. Dried, they are carried along the Silk Road, all the way to China, and Persia.

--

From Kashgar, the Polos follow the southern road, around the edge of the great Taklamakan desert, from oasis to oasis, eastward, toward China.

The Taklamakan is one of the largest sand deserts in the world. Its name is said to mean, in the local language, the place you go into and never come out. It is a great sea of sand dunes, some of them a hundred metres high, stretching for a thousand kilometres, with hardly a drop of water.

Nobody crosses it. The roads go around it, to the north, and the south, along the foot of the mountains, from one oasis to the next, where the rivers, coming down from the mountains, flow out into the desert, and disappear into the sand.

--

Along the southern road, there are oasis towns: Yarkand, and Khotan, and Pem, and Charchan.

Khotan is famous for its jade. Marco writes that in the rivers there, jade is found, and that it is carried to China, and sold for a great price. For more than two thousand years, the Chinese have prized the jade of Khotan above all other stones, white and green, smooth and cool, and have carved it into beautiful things: bowls, and ornaments, and seals, and figures. The jade is found in the beds of the rivers, in the summer, when the water is low, washed down from the mountains, by the melting snow.

{img:people-searching-river-bed-white-jade-stones-khotan-summer}
Khotan is also famous for its silk, and its carpets. There is an old story, that a princess of China, long ago, married the king of Khotan, and smuggled silkworm eggs out of China, hidden in her headdress, so that the people of Khotan could make their own silk, because the Chinese had forbidden anyone to take silkworms out of the country.

--

The oasis towns are green islands, in the desert.

There are fields of wheat, and cotton, and orchards of apricots, and mulberries, and walnuts, and pomegranates, and vineyards, and tall poplar trees, lining the roads and the canals, to give shade, and to break the wind. The houses are of mud brick, with flat roofs, where the people sleep in summer, and dry their fruit. The water is carefully shared out, by canals, and little channels, to every field.

{img:poplar-lined-canal-oasis-fields-mud-houses-evening-light}
And beyond the last field, the desert begins, suddenly, like a wall of sand. And the travellers must fill their water bags, and load their camels, and set out across it, to the next oasis, several days away.
