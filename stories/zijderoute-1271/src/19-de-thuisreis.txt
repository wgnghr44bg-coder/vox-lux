In 1292, at last, the Polos get their chance to go home.

The Mongol ruler of Persia, Arghun Khan, a great-nephew of Kublai, has lost his favourite wife. And as she was dying, she asked him to marry a lady of her own tribe, from Mongolia. So Arghun sent ambassadors to Kublai Khan, to ask for a bride.

--

Kublai chose a young lady of seventeen, called Kököchin, beautiful and gracious, Marco says. But the land route back to Persia was blocked, by wars among the Mongol princes. So the ambassadors decided to go by sea, around the south of Asia, through the Indian Ocean.

And they asked the Great Khan to let the three Latins, the Polos, who were experienced travellers, and knew the sea, go with them, as guides and companions. The Khan did not want them to go. But at last, he agreed.

He gave them two golden tablets, and letters to the kings of Europe, and to the Pope. And he gave them rich gifts. And he fitted out a great fleet for them: fourteen great ships, Chinese junks, with four masts, and many sails, and dozens of cabins, carrying six hundred people, and food for two years.

--

They sailed from the great port of Quanzhou, in the south of China, which Marco calls Zaiton, one of the greatest ports in the world, where hundreds of ships came every year, from India, and the Spice Islands, and Arabia, with pepper, and spices, and precious stones.

The voyage was long and hard. They sailed south, along the coast of Vietnam, and then to the island of Sumatra, where they had to wait five months, for the monsoon winds to change. They built a camp on the shore, with a fence of wood, to protect themselves.

{img:sumatra-coast-tropical-camp-palisade-ships-anchored-waiting}
Marco describes Sumatra: its pepper, and its spices, and its rhinoceroses, which he thought were unicorns, but says are very ugly, and nothing like the unicorns in European stories, which let themselves be caught by maidens. And the sago palms, from which the people make a kind of flour. And the people, who were not yet Muslim, mostly, though some of the ports had converted.

--

Then they sailed on, past Ceylon, which Marco says is the finest island of its size in the world, with rubies, and sapphires, and the mountain where Adam is said to be buried. And along the coast of India, which Marco describes at length: the pearl fishers, diving in the sea; the pepper of Malabar; the great kingdoms, and their customs.

And at last, after two years at sea, they arrived at Hormuz, in the Persian Gulf, the port they had left, twenty years before.

{img:pearl-divers-boats-calm-sea-india-coast-morning}
Many of the people who set out on the voyage did not survive it. Marco says that of the six hundred, only eighteen were left. Perhaps that is an exaggeration. But it was a very hard journey.

And when they arrived in Persia, they found that Arghun Khan had passed away, while they were at sea. So the young lady Kököchin married his son, Ghazan, instead. Marco says that she wept, when the Polos left her, because she had grown so fond of them, on the long voyage.

--

From Persia, the Polos travelled overland, to Tabriz, and then north, to the city of Trebizond, on the Black Sea. And there, they heard that Kublai Khan had passed away, in 1294, at the age of about seventy-nine.

From Trebizond, they sailed to Constantinople, and from there, home, to Venice.

They arrived in Venice in 1295. They had been away for twenty-four years. Marco was now forty-one.

--

There is a famous story, told many years later, about their return.

When they arrived at their house in Venice, their relatives did not recognize them. They looked like Mongols, with their long beards, and their strange, worn, shabby clothes, and they spoke Venetian with a strange accent, mixed with foreign words. Nobody believed that they were the Polos, who everyone thought had died long ago.

{img:venetian-feast-hall-travellers-cutting-seams-jewels-spilling}
So they invited all their relatives to a great feast. And at the end of the feast, they brought out their old, shabby travelling coats, and took knives, and cut open the seams. And out poured a stream of jewels, rubies, and sapphires, and emeralds, and diamonds, and pearls, which they had sewn into the lining of their clothes, for safety, on the long journey home. And then everyone believed them.

It is probably only a story. But it is a good one.
