Tonight we are going about seven hundred and fifty years back, to the city of Venice in the year 1271. And from there, we will follow the old roads of the Silk Road, all the way across Asia.

--

A young man of seventeen is about to leave home with his father and his uncle. He does not know it yet, but the journey will last twenty-four years. He will cross deserts and mountains. He will sleep in caravan inns and in the tents of nomads. And at last he will come to the court of the greatest ruler in the world.

His name is Marco Polo. Tonight we will travel with him, slowly, along the Silk Road.

If you enjoy slow journeys into the past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can easily find the next one.

--

Venice in 1271 is one of the richest cities in the world.

It is built on more than a hundred little islands, in a shallow lagoon at the top of the Adriatic Sea. Instead of streets, it has canals. Instead of carts, it has boats. The houses and churches stand on millions of tree trunks, driven deep into the mud. The water laps at their doorsteps.

--

Venice has no king. It is ruled by a council of rich merchant families, and by a leader they choose, called the Doge. The Doge lives in a great palace beside the church of Saint Mark. Saint Mark is the city's own saint. The Venetians say his bones were smuggled out of Egypt long ago, hidden in a barrel of pork.

The church of Saint Mark is covered with domes. Inside, golden pictures made of tiny stones glitter in the candlelight. In front of it lies a great square, and beyond that the harbour, full of ships.

{img:saint-marks-basilica-domes-golden-mosaics-piazza-crowds}
Above the main door of the church stand four great horses of golden bronze. The Venetians took them from the city of Constantinople, when they captured it sixty-seven years before.

--

Venice lives by trade.

Its ships sail all over the Mediterranean Sea, to Constantinople, to Egypt and to North Africa. They carry wool, cloth and timber to the East. And they bring back the treasures of the East. Spices like pepper and cinnamon. Silk and cotton. Sugar, perfumes and precious stones.

{img:venetian-merchants-warehouse-spice-sacks-silk-bales-quay}
These goods come from very far away. They come from India, and from China, which the Europeans call Cathay. They travel thousands of miles, passed from one merchant to the next. At last they reach the ports of the Mediterranean, where the Venetians buy them, and sell them all over Europe for a big profit.

--

The long road that brings silk from China to the West is what we now call the Silk Road.

It is not really one road. It is a great web of caravan tracks, mountain passes and desert trails, more than six thousand kilometres long. It has been used for more than a thousand years.

But very few people travel the whole way. Goods are passed along from city to city, each merchant taking them a little further. Hardly any Europeans have ever been to China. Most of them barely know where it is.

--

In 1271, though, the road is more open than ever before.

In the last fifty years, almost all of Asia has been conquered by one people, the Mongols. And the Mongols keep the roads safe, so that merchants can travel from one end to the other under their protection. But how long will that peace last? Nobody can say.

{img:venetian-family-house-canal-balcony-evening-lamps}
And in Venice, in 1271, there is one family of merchants who have already made that long journey, and come back. And now they are about to go again. They are the Polo family.
