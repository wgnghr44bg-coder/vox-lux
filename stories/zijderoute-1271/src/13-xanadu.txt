In the spring of 1275, three and a half years after they left Venice, the Polos come to the summer palace of Kublai Khan, at a city called Shangdu, on the grasslands, north of Beijing, on the edge of Mongolia.

Marco calls it Ciandu. In English, it will later be called Xanadu.

--

The Great Khan has sent riders, forty days' journey, to meet them, and bring them to him, with every comfort. And at Shangdu, they are taken to the palace, and brought before the Great Khan, Kublai, the ruler of the greatest empire in the world.

They kneel before him, and bow down, with their faces to the ground, as is the custom. And Kublai tells them to rise, and receives them with great honour, and joy, and asks them about their journey, and their health, and about the Pope.

{img:polos-kneeling-before-kublai-khan-throne-hall-courtiers}
They give him the letters, and the gifts, from Pope Gregory. And the holy oil, from the lamp in Jerusalem, which he receives with great respect. And then he sees young Marco, and asks who he is. And Niccolò says: Sire, this is my son, and your servant. And Kublai says: he is welcome. And Marco, at twenty-one, enters the service of the Great Khan.

--

Shangdu is a wonderful place.

Marco describes the palace: a great palace of marble and other fine stones, with halls and rooms all painted and gilded, with pictures of men and beasts and birds and trees and flowers, so beautiful that they delight the eye. Around it, there is a great wall, enclosing a park, of sixteen miles around, with streams, and fountains, and meadows, and woods, full of every kind of wild animal: deer, and stags, and roebucks, which the Khan keeps to feed his falcons and his hawks.

In the park, there is another palace, made entirely of cane, of bamboo, gilded and varnished inside, so that rain cannot harm it, held up by gilded pillars, each with a dragon coiled around it. It is held together with two hundred silken cords, and can be taken apart and moved, like a tent. The Khan lives in it in summer, in June, July and August, when the weather is cool there, on the high grassland.

--

The Khan rides through the park, with a leopard behind him on his horse, which he lets loose to catch deer. And he has ten thousand white horses, and white mares, whose milk is drunk only by the Khan and his family, and one other tribe, who once helped Genghis Khan in battle.

Every year, on the twenty-eighth day of August, the Khan leaves Shangdu, and before he goes, the milk of the white mares is sprinkled on the ground, and in the air, as an offering to the spirits of the earth, and the sky, so that they will protect the Khan, and his family, and his people, and his herds.

{img:white-horses-herd-grassland-summer-evening-light}
More than five hundred years later, in 1797, an English poet, Samuel Taylor Coleridge, will read Marco's description of Shangdu, in a book of travels, and fall asleep, and dream, and wake up, and write a famous poem about it: In Xanadu did Kubla Khan a stately pleasure-dome decree.

--

Today, the ruins of Shangdu are still there, on the grassland of Inner Mongolia, in northern China: low walls of earth, in a great square, covered with grass, and wildflowers, with the wind blowing across them, and the sheep grazing among them. It is a World Heritage Site.

But in 1275, it is full of life: the court of the Great Khan, with his family, and his wives, and his lords, and his officers, and his soldiers, and his ministers, and his astrologers, and his doctors, and his falconers, and his musicians, and ambassadors and merchants from all over the world.

{img:court-banquet-tent-grassland-musicians-dancers-lamps}
And three young Venetians, and their father, who have come all the way from the other end of the world.
