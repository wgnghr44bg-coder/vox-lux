Let us think for a while about the great road Marco Polo is about to follow.

In Marco's time, nobody called it the Silk Road. That name was invented about six hundred years later by a German geographer. But the roads themselves are very old. In 1271 they have already been used for more than fifteen hundred years.

--

They began around the time of Christ, when China and the Roman Empire were both strong. The Chinese sent silk westward, and the Romans loved it. They paid huge prices for it. Some Roman dresses were so thin that Roman writers complained about them. And the Romans had no idea how silk was made. Some thought it grew on trees.

Silk was the most famous thing on the road. But it was far from the only thing.

{img:bales-of-silk-colourful-bolts-caravanserai-courtyard-merchants}
From the East came silk, porcelain, paper and tea. From the West came horses, glass, silver and wool. From India came cotton, pepper and precious stones. And from Central Asia came melons, grapes and carpets.

--

But the road carried more than goods.

Ideas travelled along it too, and religions and inventions. Buddhism travelled from India to China along the Silk Road. Paper and gunpowder and the compass travelled from China to the West. Grapes and glass-making went the other way.

The road was not one road but many. They crossed each other, split and joined again, like threads in a great cloth, woven across Asia.

--

The main routes started in the old capital of China, in the east. They ran west along a narrow strip between mountains and desert, to the edge of a great desert called the Taklamakan.

There the road split. It went around the desert to the north and the south, from oasis to oasis. Then it climbed over the high Pamir mountains, the roof of the world. From there it went on to the great cities of Central Asia, like Samarkand and Bukhara, and then across Persia, to the shores of the Mediterranean.

{img:oasis-town-poplars-mud-walls-mountains-desert-edge}
It was a road through some of the hardest country on earth. There were burning deserts and freezing mountains. There were high, windy plains where nothing grows. Travellers suffered from heat and cold, from thirst and hunger, from sandstorms, snowstorms and robbers. Not every traveller who set out on this road came home again.

--

But in 1271 the road is safer than it has ever been, because of the Mongols.

Fifty years before, the Mongols swept across Asia, led by the great conqueror Genghis Khan. They were horsemen from the grasslands of Mongolia. They conquered everything from China to Persia and Russia. It was a time of terrible destruction. But afterwards, most of Asia was ruled by one family.

{img:mongol-horsemen-steppe-grassland-felt-tents-herds}
And the Mongol rulers wanted trade. They protected the merchants and kept the roads safe. They built rest houses and stations for fresh horses along the way. Historians call it the Mongol peace. For about a hundred years, a merchant could travel from the Black Sea to China in fairly good safety.

--

So in these years, more Europeans than ever before travel into Asia. Merchants, missionaries and ambassadors.

A Franciscan friar called William had travelled to the Mongol capital nearly twenty years before, and wrote a wonderful account of his trip. But Marco Polo's journey will be the longest of them all. And his book will be the most famous.
