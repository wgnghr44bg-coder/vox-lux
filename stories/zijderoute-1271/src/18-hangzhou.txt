Of all the cities that Marco Polo saw in China, the one he loved best, and described most beautifully, is the great city of Hangzhou, in the south, which he calls Kinsai, the city of heaven.

Until 1276, just after the Polos arrived in China, Hangzhou was the capital of the Song dynasty, the old Chinese emperors of the south, before Kublai Khan's armies conquered them. It is the largest and richest city in the world.

--

Marco writes that it is, without doubt, the finest and most splendid city in the world. It is a hundred miles around, he says, and it has twelve thousand stone bridges, over its canals, so high that ships can pass under them. Perhaps the numbers are too large. But Hangzhou really was enormous, with perhaps a million people, or more.

The city is built between a great river, the Qiantang, and a beautiful lake, the West Lake, surrounded by green hills, with temples, and pagodas, and gardens, and palaces, and pavilions, along its shores.

Marco writes about the lake: that around it, there are beautiful palaces and houses, of the nobles, and temples, and monasteries. And in the middle, two islands, with palaces, where people go for weddings and feasts. And on the lake, there are many boats, pleasure boats, large and small, with tables and chairs, and everything needed for a party, painted, and decorated, where people go out with their friends, and their families, and eat, and drink, and enjoy themselves, and look at the beautiful views, of the city, and the hills, and the temples.

--

He writes about the markets of Hangzhou: ten great squares, with markets, three days a week, where forty or fifty thousand people come, with every kind of food: game, and fowl, and ducks, and geese, and fruit, enormous pears, white inside, and fragrant, and peaches, yellow and white, and grapes, and fish, from the river and the lake and the sea, so much fish that you would think it could never all be sold. But within a few hours, it is all gone.

He writes about the streets, paved with stone and brick, and the canals, with their boats, and the houses, of three and four storeys, and the shops, and the workshops, of twelve great guilds of craftsmen, each with thousands of workshops.

{img:hangzhou-market-fish-fruit-baskets-stalls-crowds-canal}
He writes about the baths, three thousand of them, with hot and cold water, where people bathe every day. And the people themselves: peaceful, and gentle, and honest, and courteous, living together in friendship, so that a whole street seems like one family. And the ladies, beautiful, and elegant, dressed in silk, and jewels.

--

At night, he says, the city is guarded by watchmen, on every bridge, who walk the streets, and if they see a light burning after the curfew, they knock on the door, and ask why. And there are watchtowers, on hills, with wooden drums, to give the alarm if there is a fire, because fires are very dangerous, in a city of wooden houses.

He describes the custom, when a child is born, of writing down the day, and the hour, and the sign of the planets, so that the astrologers can foretell the child's future, and the way that the people have their names written on their doors, so that the officials know how many people live in each house.

{img:night-hangzhou-lanterns-canal-bridge-watchman-quiet}
Hangzhou is still a beautiful city today, and the West Lake is still there, with its willows, and its causeways, and its pagodas, and its pleasure boats, and its tea plantations on the hills around. The Chinese have an old saying: above, there is heaven, and below, there are Suzhou and Hangzhou.
