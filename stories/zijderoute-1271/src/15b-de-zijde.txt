How is silk made?

For thousands of years, the Chinese kept it a secret. Anyone who tried to take silkworms out of China could be put to death. So for centuries, people in the West wore silk and paid huge sums for it, without knowing where it came from.

--

Silk comes from a little caterpillar called the silkworm. It is really the caterpillar of a moth. And it eats only one thing, the leaves of the white mulberry tree.

In the villages of China, families raise silkworms in their houses in spring. The worms live on flat bamboo trays, stacked on shelves. The tiny worms hatch from eggs, and then they eat and eat, day and night. The women and children pick fresh mulberry leaves for them several times a day. The room fills with a soft sound like gentle rain. It is the sound of thousands of silkworms munching.

After about a month, the silkworms are thousands of times bigger. Then they stop eating and begin to spin. Each one spins a cocoon around itself from one single thread of silk. It turns its head round and round for two or three days, until it is wrapped in a little white cocoon about the size of a thumb.

--

That single thread is very fine, very strong and very long. It can be almost a kilometre long.

To get the thread, the cocoons are dropped into hot water. Then the women find the end of the thread with a little brush, and wind it carefully onto a reel. They join the threads of several cocoons together, to make one stronger thread. It is careful work. One clumsy move, and the thread breaks.

{img:woman-reeling-silk-cocoons-hot-water-basin-wooden-reel}
The raw silk is washed, dyed in beautiful colours and woven on looms into cloth. There is plain silk, shiny satin, silk with patterns, and silk so fine you can almost see through it. The hardest patterns are woven on huge looms by two people at once. One sits high up on the loom, lifting the threads for the pattern, while the other weaves.

--

In China, people who can afford it use silk for everything. For clothes and bedding, for paintings and writing, for fans and banners, and even for the strings of musical instruments.

Marco writes that in Khanbaliq, more than a thousand cartloads of silk arrive every day. And in the southern city of Hangzhou, he says, almost everyone wears silk, because there is so much of it.

{img:drawloom-two-weavers-silk-brocade-pattern-workshop-light}
By Marco's time, the secret of silk has long since spread beyond China, even as far as Italy. But Chinese silk is still the finest in the world. And the little silkworms munching their leaves in quiet villages are still the beginning of the great road that carries their name.
