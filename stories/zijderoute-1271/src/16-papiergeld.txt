Of all the wonders Marco sees in China, the one that amazes him most is money made of paper.

In Europe in the thirteenth century, all money is made of metal. Coins of gold, silver and copper. Nobody has ever heard of paper money. But in China, emperors have used it for hundreds of years.

--

Marco describes how it is made in the Khan's workshop in the capital.

The paper is made from the bark of the mulberry tree, the same tree that feeds the silkworms. The soft inner bark is soaked and pounded into a dark paper. It is cut into pieces of different sizes for different values. Each piece is signed and sealed by several officials. Last of all it is stamped with the Khan's own seal in bright red ink. And then it is money, worth as much as gold or silver.

Everyone in the empire must accept it, Marco says, or face death. So the Khan, he says, is richer than anyone in the world, because he can make as much money as he likes out of paper.

When the notes get old and worn, people bring them back and swap them for new ones, for a small fee.

--

Marco writes that with this paper money you can buy anything. Pearls, gold, silver, anything at all. Foreign merchants who arrive with gold and jewels must sell them to the Khan for paper money. Then they can spend it anywhere in the empire.

Europeans who read his book could hardly believe it. Money made of paper sounded like a fairy tale, or a trick. Europe would not use paper money for another three or four hundred years.

{img:merchant-counting-paper-notes-shop-counter-silk-bolts}
Marco describes other things that are new to Europeans too.

--

He describes porcelain, the beautiful hard white pottery of China. It is so fine that light shines through it. In one city, he says, porcelain bowls are so cheap that you can buy eight of them for one small Venetian coin. Our word porcelain comes from the Italian word Marco used. It is the name of a kind of shiny white seashell.

He describes the great rivers of China, with thousands of boats on them. And the Grand Canal, which Kublai is making longer, to carry rice from the south to the capital.

And he describes a strange mineral dug from a mountain. It can be spun into cloth that does not burn. To clean it, you simply throw it in the fire.

--

He also describes how the Khan looks after his people.

When the harvest fails in a region, because of storms or floods or locusts, the Khan takes no taxes from that region, and sends grain from his own stores. In good years, he fills great storehouses with grain. Then in years of hunger, he can sell it cheaply to the people. Every day in the capital, he gives food to thousands of poor people.

{img:granary-officials-distributing-grain-sacks-poor-families}
Marco writes all this with wonder. Perhaps he makes the Khan sound a little better than he really was. But China in the thirteenth century truly was richer and more advanced than Europe in many ways. It had great cities, printing, paper money, porcelain, canals, silk and millions of people. And a young man from Venice was seeing all of it with his own eyes.
