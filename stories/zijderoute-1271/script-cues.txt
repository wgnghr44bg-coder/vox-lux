Tonight we are going about seven hundred and fifty years back, to the city of Venice, in the year 1271, and from there, along the old roads of the Silk Road, all the way across Asia.

[long-pause]

A young man of seventeen is about to leave home, with his father and his uncle, on a journey that will last twenty-four years. He will cross deserts and mountains, sleep in caravan inns and in the tents of nomads, and come at last to the court of the greatest ruler in the world.

[pause]

His name is Marco Polo. And tonight, we will travel with him, slowly, along the Silk Road.

[pause]

If you enjoy slow journeys into the past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, so that you can find the next one easily.

[long-pause]

Venice, in 1271, is one of the richest cities in the world.

[pause]

It is built on more than a hundred little islands, in a shallow lagoon, at the top of the Adriatic Sea, in the north east of Italy. Instead of streets, it has canals. Instead of carts, it has boats. The houses and palaces and churches stand on millions of wooden piles, tree trunks driven deep into the mud, and their foundations are washed by the water.

[long-pause]

The city is a republic, ruled not by a king, but by a council of rich merchant families, and an elected leader, called the Doge, who lives in a great palace beside the church of Saint Mark, the patron saint of Venice, whose bones, the Venetians say, were brought from Alexandria, in Egypt, by two Venetian merchants, hidden in a barrel of pork.

[pause]

The church of Saint Mark is covered with domes, and inside, with golden mosaics, which glitter in the candlelight. In front of it, there is the great square, the Piazza, and the bell tower, and the harbour, full of ships.

[pause]

{img:saint-marks-basilica-domes-golden-mosaics-piazza-crowds}
And on the front of the church, above the main door, there stand four great horses of gilded bronze, which the Venetians brought back from Constantinople, when they captured that city, in 1204, sixty-seven years before.

[long-pause]

Venice lives by trade.

[pause]

Its ships sail all over the Mediterranean Sea: to Constantinople, and the Black Sea, and to Syria, and Palestine, and Egypt, and North Africa. They carry the goods of Europe, wool and cloth and timber and metal and slaves, to the East. And they bring back the goods of the East: spices, pepper and ginger and cinnamon and cloves, and silk, and cotton, and sugar, and dyes, and perfumes, and precious stones, and porcelain, and carpets.

[pause]

{img:venetian-merchants-warehouse-spice-sacks-silk-bales-quay}
These goods come from very far away: from India, and the islands of the Indies, and from China, which the Europeans call Cathay. They travel for thousands of miles, along the sea routes, and the land routes, passed from merchant to merchant, until they reach the ports of the Mediterranean, where the Venetians buy them, and bring them home, and sell them all over Europe, at great profit.

[long-pause]

The long road that brings silk from China to the West is what we now call the Silk Road.

[pause]

It is not really one road. It is a great network of routes, of caravan tracks and mountain passes and river crossings and desert trails, stretching for more than six thousand kilometres, from China, across Central Asia, and Persia, to the shores of the Mediterranean and the Black Sea. It has been used for more than a thousand years, by merchants, and pilgrims, and soldiers, and missionaries, and travellers.

[pause]

Very few merchants travel the whole length of it. Goods are passed along, from one city to the next, from one merchant to another, each taking them a little further. Few Europeans have ever been to China. Most of them hardly know where it is.

[long-pause]

But in 1271, the road is more open than it has ever been.

[pause]

Because, in the last fifty years, almost all of Asia, from China to the borders of Europe, has been conquered by a single people: the Mongols. And the Mongols keep the roads safe, so that merchants can travel along them, from one end to the other, under their protection.

[pause]

{img:venetian-family-house-canal-balcony-evening-lamps}
And in Venice, in 1271, there is a family of merchants who have already made that journey, and come back, and are about to go again. The Polo family.

[long-pause]

{img:two-venetian-merchants-brothers-1260-ship-deck-constantinople}
Marco Polo's father is called Niccolò, and his uncle is called Maffeo. They are merchants of Venice, from a family of merchants, who have a house in the city, and also a house and a business in Constantinople, and another in Soldaia, a port on the Black Sea, in the Crimea.

[pause]

In 1260, eleven years before our story begins, the two brothers set out from Constantinople, on a trading journey, to the lands of the Mongols, north of the Black Sea, with a load of jewels to sell.

[long-pause]

They went to the court of a Mongol prince, Berke Khan, who ruled the western part of the Mongol empire, called the Golden Horde, on the great plains of what is now southern Russia. They gave him their jewels, and he gave them goods worth twice as much in return.

[pause]

But then a war broke out, between Berke and another Mongol prince, and the road back to the west was closed. So the brothers went east, instead, further and further, across the steppes, to the great city of Bukhara, in Central Asia. And there they stayed for three years, waiting for the war to end.

[pause]

In Bukhara, they met an ambassador, sent by another Mongol prince, on his way to the court of the Great Khan, the supreme ruler of all the Mongols, in China. The ambassador had never seen Italians before. He was fascinated by them, and invited them to come with him, to meet the Great Khan, who, he said, had never seen any Latins, as he called Europeans, and would be very glad to meet them.

[long-pause]

So the two brothers travelled with the ambassador, for a whole year, eastward, across Central Asia, to China, and the court of the Great Khan. His name was Kublai.

[pause]

Kublai Khan received them very kindly. He asked them many questions, about Europe, and its kings, and the Pope, and the Christian religion, and how they lived, and how they made war, and how they kept the peace. And they answered him as well as they could, in the Mongol language, or in Turkish, which they had learned on their travels.

[pause]

Kublai was a curious and clever man. He wanted to know more about the West. So he sent the brothers back to Europe, as his ambassadors, with a letter to the Pope, asking him to send a hundred learned Christian men, who could explain their religion, and their learning, to the Mongols. And he asked them to bring him some oil from the lamp that burned over the tomb of Christ, in Jerusalem.

[long-pause]

He gave them a golden tablet, called a paiza, a kind of passport, with his name on it, which said that they were his messengers, and that everyone in his empire must give them food, and horses, and guides, and help them on their way.

[pause]

And so, with the golden tablet, the brothers travelled back, across Asia, for three years, and arrived at last at the port of Acre, on the coast of Palestine, in 1269. There, they heard that the Pope had died, and that no new Pope had yet been elected.

[pause]

{img:golden-paiza-tablet-mongol-script-hands-close-candle}
So they went home to Venice, to wait. They had been away for nine years.

[long-pause]

When Niccolò came home, he found that his wife, Marco's mother, had passed away, while he was away. And he found a son, Marco, who was fifteen years old, whom he had last seen as a little boy.

[pause]

They waited in Venice for two years, for a new Pope to be elected. But the cardinals could not agree. And in 1271, the brothers decided they could wait no longer. Kublai Khan would be wondering where they were.

[pause]

{img:father-and-teenage-son-meeting-venetian-house-courtyard}
So they set out again. And this time, they took young Marco with them.

[long-pause]

{img:galley-leaving-venice-lagoon-1271-sails-oars-bell-towers}
In the summer of 1271, Niccolò, Maffeo and young Marco Polo sail out of the lagoon of Venice, eastward, across the Mediterranean Sea.

[pause]

Marco is seventeen. He has grown up in Venice, among merchants, and sailors, and ships. He has learned to read and write, and to count, and to keep accounts, and to judge goods: the quality of silk, and spices, and jewels. He knows a little about the world, from the stories of sailors and merchants on the quays. But he has never been far from home.

[long-pause]

Their ship is a galley, or a round merchant ship, with sails, and perhaps oars, carrying passengers and goods. The voyage across the Mediterranean takes several weeks, stopping at the ports along the way: in Dalmatia, and Greece, and the island of Crete, and Cyprus.

[pause]

They sleep on deck, or in a little cabin, and eat ship's biscuit, and salted meat, and cheese, and onions, and drink wine mixed with water. They watch the dolphins playing around the ship, and the islands passing, blue and grey, in the haze.

[pause]

And at last, they come to Acre, on the coast of Palestine, the last great stronghold of the Crusaders in the Holy Land, a busy, crowded port, full of merchants from Venice and Genoa and Pisa, and knights, and pilgrims, and Arab and Jewish traders, and soldiers.

[long-pause]

In Acre, they meet an important churchman, Teobaldo Visconti, the papal legate, the representative of the Pope in the Holy Land. They tell him about Kublai Khan's letter, and ask his advice.

[pause]

He gives them letters for the Great Khan, explaining that there is still no Pope. And they travel to Jerusalem, a few days' ride away, to fetch the oil from the lamp over the tomb of Christ, as Kublai had asked.

[pause]

{img:jerusalem-church-holy-sepulchre-lamp-pilgrims-candles}
Then they set out again, northward, along the coast, to the port of Laias, in the little Christian kingdom of Armenia, in what is now the south of Turkey, where the caravans begin their long journey into Asia.

[long-pause]

But as they are about to leave Laias, a messenger comes, with extraordinary news. The cardinals have at last elected a new Pope. And the new Pope is Teobaldo Visconti himself, the man they met in Acre. He has taken the name Gregory the Tenth.

[pause]

So the Polos hurry back to Acre, to see him. The new Pope receives them warmly, and gives them letters, and gifts, for Kublai Khan, and his blessing. He cannot send a hundred learned men. But he sends two friars, two Dominican monks, learned men, to go with the Polos, and teach the Great Khan about Christianity.

[pause]

{img:new-pope-blessing-travellers-acre-hall-friars-letters}
And so, at the end of 1271, the little group sets out from Laias, at last, into Asia: Niccolò, and Maffeo, and Marco, and the two friars, and their servants, and their horses, and their baggage, with the golden tablet of the Great Khan, and the letters of the Pope, and the holy oil from Jerusalem.

[long-pause]

But they have not gone far, when they hear that there is war in Armenia, and an army of the Mamluk Sultan of Egypt is invading the country, burning and destroying.

[pause]

The two friars are frightened. They give their letters and their gifts to the Polos, and turn back, and go home. They do not want to go any further.

[pause]

And so the three Venetians go on alone, with their servants, eastward, into the unknown. They will not see Venice again for twenty-four years.

[long-pause]

What does Marco think, as he rides away from the sea, into the mountains of Armenia, at the age of seventeen?

[pause]

We do not know. He does not tell us. His book, which he will write many years later, is not a diary, but a description of the world, and the wonders he saw. He tells us very little about himself, or his feelings.

[pause]

{img:young-traveller-looking-back-sea-from-mountain-road}
But we can imagine him: young, and strong, and curious, and excited, and perhaps a little afraid, riding beside his father and his uncle, with the sea behind him, and the whole of Asia ahead.

[long-pause]

{img:acre-1271-crusader-port-walls-harbour-ships-towers}
Let us stay a little while in Acre, the port where the Polos waited, in the autumn of 1271.

[pause]

Acre stands on a point of land, jutting out into the blue Mediterranean, on the coast of what is now Israel, with great walls of golden stone, and towers, and a harbour, protected by a sea wall, crowded with ships from every port of the Mediterranean.

[long-pause]

In 1271, it is the capital of what is left of the Crusader kingdom of Jerusalem: a thin strip of coast, with a few towns and castles, held by the Christians of Europe, who came to the Holy Land on the crusades, nearly two hundred years before.

[pause]

It is a crowded, busy, noisy city, full of people from everywhere. There are knights, of the great military orders, the Templars, and the Hospitallers, and the Teutonic Knights, each with their own great fortress-like houses, in their own quarters of the city. There are pilgrims, on their way to Jerusalem, and Nazareth, and Bethlehem. There are priests, and monks, and friars.

[pause]

{img:acre-narrow-street-market-merchants-knights-pilgrims-arches}
And there are merchants: from Venice, and Genoa, and Pisa, and Marseilles, and Barcelona, each with their own quarter, with their own church, and market, and warehouses, and courts. And Arab and Jewish and Syrian and Armenian and Greek merchants, trading with them all.

[long-pause]

The Venetian quarter of Acre is a little piece of Venice, by the sea: houses, and warehouses, and a church of Saint Mark, and a market, and a fondaco, a great trading house, with a courtyard, and rooms for merchants, and storerooms for their goods, where the Polos would have stayed.

[pause]

In the markets, they sell the goods of the East, brought by caravans from Damascus and Baghdad: spices, and silk, and cotton, and sugar, from the sugar plantations of the coast, and glass, from the glassworks of Tyre, and dyes, and incense, and perfumes.

[pause]

{img:fondaco-courtyard-venetian-merchants-bales-arches-afternoon}
In the evenings, the Polos would have walked on the sea wall, and watched the sun go down over the sea, toward Venice, far away in the west, and talked about the long road east, which they knew so well, and young Marco did not know at all.

[long-pause]

Twenty years after the Polos passed through, in 1291, Acre will fall, to the Mamluk Sultan of Egypt, and the last Crusaders will leave the Holy Land forever.

[pause]

But today, the old city of Acre is still there, a World Heritage Site, with its old walls, by the sea, and its harbour, full of little fishing boats, and its markets, and its mosques, and its churches, and, underground, the great vaulted halls of the Knights Hospitaller, which were buried for centuries, and have been dug out, and can be visited, cool and dim, under the busy streets.

[long-pause]

{img:camel-caravan-desert-dunes-silk-road-sunset-long-line}
Let us think for a little while about the great road that Marco Polo is about to follow.

[pause]

The name Silk Road was not used in Marco's time. It was invented about six hundred years later, in 1877, by a German geographer, Ferdinand von Richthofen. But the road itself, or rather the many roads, are very old, more than fifteen hundred years old in 1271.

[long-pause]

They began in the time of the Han dynasty in China, and the Roman Empire in the West, around the time of Christ. The Chinese sent silk westward, and the Romans loved it, and paid enormous prices for it. Roman ladies wore silk dresses so thin that Roman writers complained they might as well wear nothing. The Romans did not know how silk was made. Some thought it grew on trees.

[pause]

Silk was the most famous of the goods that travelled along the road. But it was far from the only one.

[pause]

{img:bales-of-silk-colourful-bolts-caravanserai-courtyard-merchants}
From the East came silk, and porcelain, and paper, and tea, and spices, and rhubarb, which was used as medicine, and jade, and lacquer, and furs. From the West came horses, and glass, and silver, and gold, and wool, and linen, and wine, and amber, and coral. From India came cotton, and pepper, and precious stones. From Central Asia came horses, and melons, and grapes, and carpets.

[long-pause]

But the road carried more than goods.

[pause]

Along it travelled ideas, and religions, and inventions, and languages, and art, and music, and food, and plants, and animals, and, sometimes, diseases. Buddhism travelled from India to China along the Silk Road. So did Christianity, of the Nestorian kind, and Manichaeism, and later Islam. Paper, and printing, and gunpowder, and the compass travelled from China to the West. Grapes and alfalfa, and glass-making, travelled from the West to China.

[pause]

The road was not one road, but many, crossing each other, branching and joining, like the threads of a great piece of cloth, woven across Asia.

[long-pause]

The main routes ran from the old capital of China, Chang'an, in the east, westward along a narrow corridor, between mountains and desert, called the Gansu Corridor, to the edge of the great Taklamakan desert.

[pause]

There, the road divided, and went around the desert, to the north and the south, from oasis to oasis, along the foot of the mountains, to Kashgar, at the western edge of China. Then over the great mountains, the Pamirs, the roof of the world, into Central Asia, to the great cities of Samarkand, and Bukhara, and Balkh. And then on, westward, across Persia, to Baghdad, and Tabriz, and the shores of the Mediterranean, and the Black Sea.

[pause]

{img:oasis-town-poplars-mud-walls-mountains-desert-edge}
It was a road through some of the hardest country on earth: burning deserts, freezing mountains, high plateaus, where the wind never stops. Travellers suffered from heat, and cold, and thirst, and hunger, and sandstorms, and snowstorms, and bandits, and wars.

[long-pause]

But in 1271, it is safer than it has ever been, because of the Mongols.

[pause]

Fifty years before, the Mongols, a nomadic people from the grasslands of Mongolia, led by the great conqueror Genghis Khan, had swept across Asia, and conquered everything, from China to Persia, to Russia, to the edges of Europe. It was a time of terrible destruction. But afterward, the whole of Asia was united, for the first time, under one family.

[pause]

{img:mongol-horsemen-steppe-grassland-felt-tents-herds}
The Mongol rulers wanted trade. They protected the merchants, and kept the roads safe, and built rest houses, and relay stations, along the roads. Historians call it the Pax Mongolica, the Mongol peace. For about a hundred years, a merchant could travel from the Black Sea to China, in relative safety, under the protection of the Mongols.

[long-pause]

And so, more Europeans than ever before are travelling into Asia, in these years: merchants, and missionaries, and ambassadors.

[pause]

A Franciscan friar, William of Rubruck, travelled to the Mongol capital, Karakorum, in 1253, and wrote a wonderful account of his journey. Another, John of Plano Carpini, went in 1245. But Marco Polo's journey will be the longest, and his book the most famous, of them all.

[long-pause]

{img:armenian-highlands-mountains-monastery-caravan-road-1272}
From Laias, the Polos ride north and east, into the high lands of Armenia and Anatolia, in what is now eastern Turkey.

[pause]

It is a land of high, green plateaus, and great mountains, and deep valleys, and old stone churches and monasteries, with conical domes, on the hilltops. The people are Armenians, Christians, with their own ancient church, and their own alphabet, and Turks, and Greeks, and Kurds, living side by side.

[long-pause]

Marco writes about the country, briefly: that in Greater Armenia, there are many cities and towns, and that the best cotton cloth in the world is made there, and that in summer, the Mongol armies come there with their herds, because of the good grass, and go away in winter, because of the cold.

[pause]

He writes that in the middle of Armenia, there is a very high mountain, shaped like a cube, on which Noah's Ark is said to have come to rest, after the Flood. It is so high, and so covered with snow, that nobody can climb it. That is Mount Ararat.

[pause]

And he writes about something strange: that near the border with Georgia, there is a spring, from which oil gushes out of the ground, in such quantities that a hundred ships could be loaded with it at once. The oil is not good to eat, but it is good for burning, and for curing the skin diseases of camels. People come from far away to fetch it. That is near Baku, on the Caspian Sea, which, seven hundred years later, will become one of the great oil fields of the world.

[long-pause]

Then they come down into the lands of Persia, to the great city of Tabriz.

[pause]

Tabriz is the capital of the Mongol rulers of Persia, the Ilkhans, who are cousins of Kublai Khan. It is one of the richest and busiest trading cities in the world, at the crossroads of many routes, where merchants come from India, and Baghdad, and Mosul, and Turkey, and the Black Sea, and Europe, and China.

[pause]

{img:tabriz-bazaar-covered-brick-domes-merchants-carpets-silk}
Marco writes that the people of Tabriz live by trade and by crafts, and that they weave many kinds of beautiful cloth of silk and gold. He writes that there are merchants there from Genoa, and from many other places, and that the city is well placed for trade.

[long-pause]

The bazaar of Tabriz is a wonder: long streets, covered with vaults and domes of brick, cool and shadowy, lit by little openings in the roof, through which the sunlight falls in bright beams. On either side, there are hundreds of little shops, each with its own trade: silk merchants, and carpet sellers, and spice dealers, and goldsmiths, and coppersmiths, hammering their pots, and shoemakers, and sellers of fruit and nuts and sweets.

[pause]

The air is full of the smell of spices, and leather, and roasting meat, and bread, and incense, and the sound of voices, in many languages, bargaining, and calling, and laughing.

[pause]

{img:spice-stall-bazaar-pyramids-of-spices-saffron-cumin-light}
There are great caravanserais, too, around the bazaar: inns for merchants, with courtyards, surrounded by rooms, and storerooms, and stables, where the caravans unload, and the merchants stay, and do their business.

[long-pause]

Then the Polos travel on, southeast, across Persia, through the city of Saveh.

[pause]

At Saveh, Marco writes, he was shown the tombs of the three Magi, the three wise men who came from the East to Bethlehem, to worship the baby Jesus. He says that their bodies were still whole, with their hair and beards. He tells a story, which the local people told him, about the three kings, and the star, and a little stone that the child gave them, and a fire that came down from heaven, which the people of the town still worshipped.

[pause]

Marco is always interested in stories like this. He listens to the people he meets, and remembers what they tell him, and writes it down, years later, in his book.

[long-pause]

Persia is a beautiful country, of high plains, and mountains, and deserts, and green oases, with gardens, and orchards, and fields of wheat and barley and cotton.

[pause]

Marco writes that in Persia, there are wonderful horses, which are sold to India, for a great price. And wonderful donkeys, the finest in the world, which are faster than horses, and are much prized by merchants, because they can carry heavy loads, and eat little. And he writes that the people make beautiful things: embroidery, and cloths of silk and gold, and saddles, and bridles, and weapons.

[pause]

{img:persian-garden-orchard-pool-cypress-trees-pavilion}
In the evenings, in the caravanserais, the travellers sit together, in the courtyards, under the stars, and eat, and drink tea, or wine, and tell stories, in Persian, and Turkish, and Arabic. And young Marco listens, and learns the languages, and the customs, and the ways of Asia.

[long-pause]

{img:caravanserai-courtyard-evening-camels-merchants-fires-arches}
How do travellers cross Asia, in the year 1272?

[pause]

They travel in caravans: long lines of camels, and horses, and mules, and donkeys, loaded with goods, and baggage, and food, and water, with merchants, and drivers, and guards, and pilgrims, and other travellers, all travelling together, for safety.

[long-pause]

A caravan might have a few dozen animals, or several hundred. The camels walk in long lines, one behind the other, tied together by ropes, from the saddle of one to the nose of the next, led by a man on foot, or on a donkey, at the front. Each camel carries a load of perhaps two hundred kilograms, packed in bales and bags, tied on either side of a wooden frame, on its back.

[pause]

The camels walk slowly, steadily, at about the speed of a walking man, three or four kilometres an hour. A caravan travels perhaps twenty or thirty kilometres a day, sometimes more, sometimes less.

[pause]

Around the neck of the leading camel, there is a bell, which rings with every step, so that the drivers at the back can hear where the front of the caravan is, even in the dark, or in a sandstorm. The sound of the camel bells is the sound of the Silk Road.

[long-pause]

In Persia, and Central Asia, the caravans often travel at night, in the summer, when it is cooler, under the stars and the moon, and rest in the heat of the day.

[pause]

And at the end of a day's journey, if they are lucky, they come to a caravanserai.

[pause]

{img:caravanserai-fortress-gate-desert-road-arriving-dusk}
A caravanserai is an inn for caravans. It is a large, square building, like a little fortress, with high, thick walls, of stone or mud brick, and a single great gateway, which can be closed at night, to keep out thieves and wild animals. Inside, there is a large open courtyard, with a well, or a pool, in the middle. Around the courtyard, there are rooms, and arched alcoves, where the travellers sleep, and eat, and store their goods. And stables, for the animals.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the caravanserai is busy and noisy. The camels are unloaded, and kneel down in the courtyard, grumbling and groaning, as camels do. The horses and donkeys are watered, and fed. The merchants carry their bales into their rooms, and lock them up. The cooks light their fires, and cook supper: rice, and mutton, and bread, baked on hot stones, and onions, and dried fruit, and nuts.

[pause]

Merchants from many lands meet in the caravanserai, and talk, and exchange news, about the roads ahead, and the prices of goods, and the weather, and wars, and bandits. They trade with each other, too, and do business, by the light of the fires.

[pause]

Many caravanserais are built by rulers, or rich men, as acts of charity, and travellers can stay in them free, or for a small payment. Some are very beautiful, with carved stone gates, and tiled decorations, and domes.

[long-pause]

At night, the gate is closed, and barred. The travellers lie down to sleep, on carpets, or felt mats, in their alcoves, wrapped in their cloaks, with their goods beside them. The animals shift and grumble in the courtyard. The stars shine above, in the square of sky. And the night watchman walks around the walls.

[pause]

And before dawn, the gate is opened again, and the camels are loaded, and the caravan sets off again, in the cold, grey light, with the bells ringing, along the road.

[pause]

{img:caravan-leaving-at-dawn-gate-opening-cold-light-stars-fading}
Day after day, week after week, month after month, the Polos travel like this, across Persia, and on into the great deserts and mountains of Central Asia.

[long-pause]

{img:caravan-cook-fire-iron-pot-flatbread-camels-resting-evening}
What do the travellers eat, on the long road across Asia?

[pause]

They carry food with them, on the camels and horses: flour, and rice, and dried meat, and dried fruit, raisins and apricots and figs and dates, and nuts, almonds and walnuts and pistachios, and hard cheese, and dried yoghurt, rolled into little balls, which can be dissolved in water, to make a sour, refreshing drink.

[long-pause]

And at every town and oasis, and caravanserai, they buy fresh food: bread, and meat, and vegetables, and fruit, and milk.

[pause]

The bread of Central Asia is round and flat, baked on the inside walls of clay ovens, called tandoors, which are like big clay jars, heated with a fire inside. The baker slaps the dough onto the hot wall of the oven, where it sticks, and bakes, in a few minutes, and then peels it off, golden, and crisp, and soft, and sprinkled with seeds.

[pause]

{img:baker-slapping-dough-tandoor-clay-oven-flatbreads-stacked}
There is rice, cooked with mutton, and carrots, and onions, and raisins, in a great iron pot, called plov, or pilaf, the great feast dish of Central Asia, still eaten today. There are dumplings, filled with meat, and onion, steamed, or boiled. And noodles, pulled by hand, long and thin, cooked in soup.

[long-pause]

There is mutton, roasted on skewers, over charcoal, with salt, and cumin, and onions: kebabs. And there are melons, enormous and sweet, and grapes, and apricots, and pomegranates, and peaches, in the oases, in summer.

[pause]

And to drink, there is water, from the wells, and the springs, and the rivers, when it is good. And tea, in the east, which the Chinese and the people of Central Asia drink, but which Marco, strangely, never mentions. And wine, made from grapes, in Persia, and the oases. And kumis, the fermented mare's milk of the Mongols.

[pause]

{img:skewers-mutton-charcoal-grill-bazaar-smoke-evening}
In the deserts, the travellers must be careful with water. They fill great skin bags, and gourds, at every well, and share it out carefully, for themselves, and their animals. A camel can go for a week or more without drinking, but a horse cannot. So in the deserts, camels are best.

[long-pause]

And in the evenings, around the fire, in the caravanserais, or in the open desert, under the stars, the travellers share their food, and talk, and tell stories, in many languages, and sing songs, and play little instruments, like lutes, and flutes, and fall asleep, at last, wrapped in their cloaks, by the glowing embers, with the camels resting around them, chewing quietly in the dark.

[long-pause]

{img:hormuz-port-persian-gulf-hot-white-houses-dhows-palms}
From Persia, the Polos first travel south, through the cities of Yazd, and Kerman, all the way to the Persian Gulf, to the great port of Hormuz.

[pause]

Their plan, it seems, is to take a ship from Hormuz, across the Indian Ocean, to India, and then on to China, by sea. Many merchants go that way.

[long-pause]

Marco describes the journey south, from Kerman, down to the sea. The road goes down from the high, cool plateau of Persia, through mountain passes, and then across a hot plain, full of date palms, and fruit trees, and birds: francolins, a kind of partridge, with black and white feathers, and red beaks. And oxen, white as snow, with humps on their backs, and sheep as big as donkeys, with fat tails.

[pause]

The plain is often raided by bandits, who come out of the hills, and Marco tells a strange story about them: that they can make the day go dark, by magic, so that nobody can see them come. Perhaps he is describing a dust storm.

[pause]

And then, at last, they come to the sea, and to Hormuz.

[long-pause]

Hormuz is a busy, hot, rich port, on the shore of the Persian Gulf. Merchants come there from India, with spices, and precious stones, and pearls, and silk, and ivory, and cloth of gold. And they sell them to merchants from Persia, who carry them inland, to Tabriz, and Baghdad, and the West.

[pause]

Marco writes that the heat in summer is so terrible that the people leave the town, and go to live in gardens by the river, outside the town. And that every day, at midday, a hot wind blows from the desert, so hot that it would kill anyone it caught. So when the people feel it coming, they go into the water, and stay there, up to their necks, until it has passed.

[pause]

{img:shallow-sea-people-standing-in-water-heat-midday-palms}
He writes that the people of Hormuz eat dates, and salted fish, and onions, and that they do not eat bread and meat, because it would make them ill in that heat. And that they grow wheat, and harvest it in March, and that after that, nothing green can be seen, except the date palms, until the next winter.

[long-pause]

But the Polos do not like the ships of Hormuz.

[pause]

Marco writes that they are very bad ships, and dangerous, because they are not built with iron nails, but sewn together with thread made from the husks of coconuts, and that they have only one mast, and one sail, and one rudder, and no deck, and that the cargo is covered only with hides. Many of them are lost at sea.

[pause]

{img:sewn-wooden-dhow-ship-beach-coconut-rope-stitches-close}
So, perhaps because of the ships, or for some other reason, the Polos decide not to go by sea. They turn back, northward, the way they came, and decide to go to China overland, along the Silk Road.

[long-pause]

On the way north, through the deserts of eastern Persia, Marco tells one of the most famous stories in his book: the story of the Old Man of the Mountain.

[pause]

The Old Man, he says, was the leader of a secret sect, who lived in a castle, in a valley in the mountains of northern Persia, called Alamut. In the valley, he had made the most beautiful garden in the world, with every kind of fruit, and the most beautiful palaces, painted with gold, and fountains flowing with wine, and milk, and honey, and lovely maidens, singing and dancing and playing music.

[pause]

He would give the young men who served him a sleeping drink, and carry them into the garden, and when they woke, they believed they were in Paradise. Then he would give them the drink again, and carry them out. And they would do anything he asked, to go back.

[pause]

Marco says that this sect was destroyed by the Mongols, fifteen years before, in 1256, and the castle taken. The story is probably more legend than truth. But it was one of the most famous stories of the Middle Ages.

[long-pause]

Then the Polos cross the great desert of Persia, the Dasht-e Lut, which Marco describes: a journey of seven days, with no water, except a little, bitter and green, which makes travellers ill. No houses, no people, no animals, only sand and stones and salt, shimmering in the heat.

[pause]

{img:salt-desert-persia-white-crust-shimmering-heat-camels-far}
And beyond it, at last, they come to green lands again, and to the city of Balkh, in what is now Afghanistan.

[long-pause]

{img:balkh-ruins-mud-walls-mother-of-cities-plain-mountains}
Balkh is one of the oldest cities in the world. The Persians call it the mother of cities.

[pause]

Alexander the Great came here, sixteen hundred years before, and married a local princess, Roxana. For centuries, it was a great centre of trade, and learning, and religion: Zoroastrian, and Buddhist, and then Muslim.

[long-pause]

But in 1220, fifty years before the Polos arrive, Genghis Khan and his Mongol armies came, and destroyed it.

[pause]

Marco writes that it was once a great and noble city, with many fine palaces and houses of marble, but that the Tartars, as he calls the Mongols, ruined it, and that the palaces are now broken and in ruins. Some people still live there, among the ruins, and slowly, the city is coming back to life. But it will never again be as great as it was.

[pause]

Marco also writes that it was here, in Balkh, that Alexander the Great married the daughter of Darius, the Persian king. He is not quite right. But he is passing on what the local people told him, and the memory of Alexander was still alive all over Central Asia.

[long-pause]

From Balkh, the Polos go east, into the mountains, to a land called Badakhshan, in the far north east of what is now Afghanistan.

[pause]

Badakhshan is a land of high mountains, and deep valleys, and swift rivers, and green pastures. Marco loves it. He writes that it has the best horses, descended, the people say, from Bucephalus, the horse of Alexander the Great. And the finest falcons. And mountains so high that it takes from morning to evening to climb them, with wide plains on top, with good grass, and trees, and springs of the purest water.

[pause]

{img:badakhshan-high-valley-river-green-pasture-snow-peaks}
And he writes about the precious stones of Badakhshan: rubies, which the local people call balas rubies, dug from a mountain, which only the king may mine. And lapis lazuli, the beautiful deep blue stone, flecked with gold, which is ground to make the finest blue paint in the world, ultramarine, which is used by painters, all the way to Italy, for the robes of the Virgin Mary in their pictures.

[long-pause]

In Badakhshan, Marco falls ill.

[pause]

He does not say what the illness was. Perhaps a fever, picked up in the deserts or the hot lowlands. He was ill for about a year. And the people told him to go up into the mountains, where the air is pure and cool, and he went, and stayed there, and soon he was well again.

[pause]

So the Polos stay in Badakhshan for perhaps a year, while Marco recovers. It must have been a strange time for him, a young man of nineteen or twenty, far from home, in the high mountains of Afghanistan, among the shepherds and their flocks, getting better, slowly, in the clean, cold air.

[long-pause]

Marco writes about the people of Badakhshan: that they are Muslims, and brave, and good archers, and hunters, and that they dress in skins, because cloth is so expensive there. And that the women wear enormous trousers, made of a hundred yards of cotton cloth, gathered and pleated, to make their hips look large, because the men think that large hips are beautiful.

[pause]

{img:mountain-village-flat-roofs-terraces-apricot-trees-blossom}
He also writes about the sheep: wild sheep, with enormous curling horns, as long as six palms, from which the shepherds make bowls, to eat from, and fences, to keep in their flocks. Today, these sheep are called Marco Polo sheep, after him, Ovis ammon polii. They still live in the high mountains of the Pamirs.

[long-pause]

{img:pamir-high-plateau-snow-peaks-frozen-lake-yaks-wind}
From Badakhshan, the Polos climb up into the highest mountains they have ever seen: the Pamirs.

[pause]

The local people call it the Bam-i-Dunya, the roof of the world. It is a great high plateau, more than four thousand metres above the sea, surrounded by even higher mountains, covered with snow and ice, all year round. Here, the great mountain ranges of Asia meet: the Hindu Kush, and the Karakoram, and the Tian Shan, and the Kunlun.

[long-pause]

Marco describes it well. He writes that you ride for twelve days across a high plain, called Pamier, with a great lake, from which a fine river flows. It has the best pasture in the world, where a thin horse becomes fat in ten days. And there are great numbers of wild sheep, with huge horns.

[pause]

But it is a lonely and desolate place. For twelve days, you see no houses, and no people, and no birds, because it is so high and so cold. And you must carry all your food with you.

[pause]

And Marco notices something strange: that because of the great cold, fire does not burn as brightly, or give out as much heat, as in other places, and food does not cook as well. He is right, although he does not know why. At such a height, the air is thin, with less oxygen, and water boils at a lower temperature.

[long-pause]

Then, he writes, you ride for forty more days, over mountains and valleys, across rivers and deserts, through a land called Bolor, without houses, or anything green, where the people live high in the mountains, and are wild, and hunt animals, and dress in their skins.

[pause]

The journey across the Pamirs is very hard. The air is thin, and the travellers are short of breath, and have headaches, and feel sick, and tired. The wind blows constantly, cold and fierce. There is snow, even in summer. The paths are narrow, and steep, along the edges of precipices, and across glaciers, and rivers of melting ice.

[pause]

The animals suffer too. Horses and camels slip, and fall. The yaks of the mountains are better: great shaggy black cattle, with long hair, which can carry loads in the snow, and the thin air, and are used by the mountain people.

[long-pause]

There are a few people in the Pamirs: Kyrgyz shepherds, who live in round felt tents, yurts, on the high pastures, with their herds of sheep, and goats, and yaks, and horses, and their fierce dogs. In the summer, they move up into the high valleys, and in winter, they move down to more sheltered places.

[pause]

They are hospitable to travellers. They invite them into their yurts, and give them bowls of hot, salty tea, with butter, and yak's milk, and yoghurt, and bread, and dried cheese, and let them sleep by the fire, on the felt carpets, out of the wind.

[pause]

{img:inside-felt-yurt-fire-family-tea-bowls-travellers-warm}
Inside the yurt, it is warm, and cosy, with the fire burning in the middle, and the smoke going up through a round opening in the roof, and the walls hung with bright felt carpets, and embroidered bags. The family sleeps around the fire, on felt mats, and furs.

[long-pause]

At last, after weeks in the mountains, the Polos come down, out of the Pamirs, into the valleys on the other side, and the land begins to change.

[pause]

The rivers now flow eastward, not westward. The air grows warmer. The mountains open out. There are trees again, poplars, and willows, and fields, and villages. And ahead of them, on the edge of a great desert, there is a green oasis, and a city: Kashgar.

[pause]

{img:descending-mountains-green-valley-poplars-oasis-far-below}
They have crossed the roof of the world. And they are now on the edge of China.

[long-pause]

{img:kashgar-oasis-city-mud-walls-poplars-bazaar-1273}
Kashgar is a great oasis city, at the western end of the Taklamakan desert, at the foot of the mountains, where the roads of the Silk Road meet: from the west, over the Pamirs, and from the north, and from the south, and from India.

[pause]

It is a city of merchants, and farmers, and craftsmen, of mud-brick houses, with flat roofs, and gardens, and orchards, and fields, watered by streams from the mountains, and canals.

[long-pause]

Marco writes that the people of Kashgar live by trade and by crafts, and that they have beautiful gardens and orchards and vineyards, and fine estates, and grow a great deal of cotton. He writes that many merchants set out from here, to trade all over the world.

[pause]

The bazaar of Kashgar is famous. Every week, people come from all the villages around, and from the mountains, and from across the desert, to buy and sell: horses, and camels, and donkeys, and sheep, and cotton, and silk, and carpets, and knives, and pots, and fruit, and melons, and grapes, and apricots, and nuts, and bread, fresh from the clay ovens.

[pause]

{img:kashgar-bazaar-melons-apricots-bread-ovens-donkeys-crowd}
The melons of Kashgar, and the grapes, and the apricots, are among the sweetest in the world, grown in the hot sun, with water from the mountain snows. Dried, they are carried along the Silk Road, all the way to China, and Persia.

[long-pause]

From Kashgar, the Polos follow the southern road, around the edge of the great Taklamakan desert, from oasis to oasis, eastward, toward China.

[pause]

The Taklamakan is one of the largest sand deserts in the world. Its name is said to mean, in the local language, the place you go into and never come out. It is a great sea of sand dunes, some of them a hundred metres high, stretching for a thousand kilometres, with hardly a drop of water.

[pause]

Nobody crosses it. The roads go around it, to the north, and the south, along the foot of the mountains, from one oasis to the next, where the rivers, coming down from the mountains, flow out into the desert, and disappear into the sand.

[long-pause]

Along the southern road, there are oasis towns: Yarkand, and Khotan, and Pem, and Charchan.

[pause]

Khotan is famous for its jade. Marco writes that in the rivers there, jade is found, and that it is carried to China, and sold for a great price. For more than two thousand years, the Chinese have prized the jade of Khotan above all other stones, white and green, smooth and cool, and have carved it into beautiful things: bowls, and ornaments, and seals, and figures. The jade is found in the beds of the rivers, in the summer, when the water is low, washed down from the mountains, by the melting snow.

[pause]

{img:people-searching-river-bed-white-jade-stones-khotan-summer}
Khotan is also famous for its silk, and its carpets. There is an old story, that a princess of China, long ago, married the king of Khotan, and smuggled silkworm eggs out of China, hidden in her headdress, so that the people of Khotan could make their own silk, because the Chinese had forbidden anyone to take silkworms out of the country.

[long-pause]

The oasis towns are green islands, in the desert.

[pause]

There are fields of wheat, and cotton, and orchards of apricots, and mulberries, and walnuts, and pomegranates, and vineyards, and tall poplar trees, lining the roads and the canals, to give shade, and to break the wind. The houses are of mud brick, with flat roofs, where the people sleep in summer, and dry their fruit. The water is carefully shared out, by canals, and little channels, to every field.

[pause]

{img:poplar-lined-canal-oasis-fields-mud-houses-evening-light}
And beyond the last field, the desert begins, suddenly, like a wall of sand. And the travellers must fill their water bags, and load their camels, and set out across it, to the next oasis, several days away.

[long-pause]

{img:lop-desert-flat-gravel-salt-camels-endless-grey-horizon}
At the eastern end of the southern road, the Polos come to a town called Lop, on the edge of a great desert, which Marco calls the desert of Lop.

[pause]

Travellers who want to cross the desert stop at Lop, for a week, to rest themselves and their animals, and to buy food and water for the journey. Because the crossing takes a month, at its narrowest part. And on the way, there is nothing to eat.

[long-pause]

Marco describes the desert: all mountains and valleys of sand, with nothing to eat. But every day's march, or so, there is water, he says, not enough for a large company, but enough for fifty or a hundred people, and their animals. In some places, the water is bitter and salty. In others, it is good.

[pause]

There are no animals, and no birds, because there is nothing for them to eat.

[pause]

{img:desert-well-small-pool-camels-drinking-dawn-sand}
And then Marco tells something that has fascinated readers ever since.

[long-pause]

He writes that, when travellers cross this desert at night, and one of them falls behind, or goes to sleep, and is separated from his companions, he hears spirits talking, which sound like his companions, and call him by his name. And often, these voices lead him away from the road, so that he is lost, and never found again.

[pause]

And sometimes, even in the daytime, the travellers hear the voices of spirits, and the sound of many instruments, especially drums, and the clash of weapons. So, at night, the travellers sleep close together, and before they go to sleep, they set up a sign, pointing the way they must go in the morning. And they hang little bells on all their animals, so that they cannot lose each other.

[pause]

Today, scientists think that Marco may have been describing the singing sands: in some deserts, when the wind blows over the dunes, or the sand slides down their slopes, the sand makes a strange, deep, humming, booming sound, like drums, or a distant organ, which can be heard for miles. There are singing sand dunes near Dunhuang, at the eastern end of the Lop desert, which still sing today.

[long-pause]

At the end of the desert, the Polos come to the city of Shazhou, which we now call Dunhuang, at the western end of the Gansu Corridor, the gateway to China.

[pause]

Dunhuang is a famous oasis, with a crescent-shaped lake, among great golden sand dunes. And near the town, in a cliff, there are hundreds of caves, carved by Buddhist monks, more than a thousand years before, and painted inside, with thousands of beautiful pictures, of the Buddha, and heavenly beings, and flying spirits, and musicians, and dancers, and scenes of daily life, in bright colours.

[pause]

{img:dunhuang-cave-cliff-many-openings-poplars-desert-light}
Marco does not mention the caves. But he writes that the people of this city are mostly idolaters, as he calls Buddhists, and that they have many monasteries and abbeys, full of idols of many kinds, to which they make great sacrifices, and pay great honour.

[long-pause]

He also describes a custom of the people, at funerals: that the family keeps the body of the dead person in the house, in a coffin, sealed with pitch, until the astrologers say that it is the right day for the funeral. And that, meanwhile, every day, they set a table before the coffin, with food and drink, as if the dead person were still alive, and would eat.

[pause]

And on the day of the funeral, they make figures, of paper, of men, and horses, and camels, and money, as big as coins, and burn them with the body, so that the dead person will have all these things in the next world. The Chinese still do this today, burning paper money, and paper houses, and paper cars, and paper phones, at funerals, and at the festival of the dead.

[pause]

{img:paper-figures-offerings-temple-incense-gentle-glow}
And now, at last, the Polos are in China. Ahead of them, there is the long road east, through the Gansu Corridor, between the snowy mountains and the Gobi desert, to the court of the Great Khan.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-steppe-endless-grass-blue-sky-gers-horses-grazing}
Who are the Mongols, whose empire stretches across the whole of Asia, in 1271?

[pause]

They are a nomadic people, from the great grasslands of Mongolia, in the heart of Asia, north of China. For thousands of years, they and their ancestors have lived on the steppe: wide, rolling plains of grass, under an enormous sky, with mountains on the horizon, hot in summer, and terribly cold in winter.

[long-pause]

They live in round tents of felt, called gers, or yurts, which can be taken down, and loaded onto carts, or camels, and moved, in a few hours. They move with the seasons, from pasture to pasture, with their herds: sheep, and goats, and cattle, and yaks, and camels, and above all, horses.

[pause]

The Mongols live on horseback. Children learn to ride before they can walk. They drink mare's milk, fermented into a slightly alcoholic drink, called kumis, which they love. They eat mutton, and dried curds, and cheese, and yoghurt. They wear long coats of felt and fur, and leather boots, and fur hats.

[pause]

{img:mongol-woman-milking-mare-ger-camp-morning-children-horses}
Marco writes about them with admiration. He says that the Mongol women do all the work of the household, and buy and sell, and look after the herds, and drive the carts, while the men hunt, and fight, and look after the hawks. He says that the Mongols are very hardy, and can ride for days, without food, living on mare's milk, and the blood of their horses, and sleeping on the ground, in their armour.

[long-pause]

For centuries, the Mongols were divided into many tribes, often fighting each other. Then, at the beginning of the thirteenth century, a great leader united them: Temujin, who took the name Genghis Khan, which means something like universal ruler.

[pause]

Genghis Khan and his armies conquered northern China, and Central Asia, and Persia. His sons and grandsons went on, and conquered Russia, and Korea, and the Middle East, and southern China. By 1271, the Mongol empire is the largest land empire that has ever existed, stretching from Korea to Hungary, from Siberia to the borders of India.

[pause]

The conquests were terrible, and many cities were destroyed, and many people lost their lives. We will not dwell on that tonight. But once the conquests were over, the Mongol rulers brought a kind of order, and peace, to Asia, and opened the roads to trade.

[long-pause]

One of the most remarkable things about the Mongol empire is its postal system, called the yam.

[pause]

All along the main roads of the empire, every twenty-five or thirty miles, there are post stations, with inns, and stables, and hundreds of horses, ready for the messengers of the Khan. Marco says there are more than ten thousand of these stations, and three hundred thousand horses.

[pause]

{img:post-station-yam-horses-stables-messenger-arriving-dust}
The Khan's messengers, carrying his letters and orders, ride from one station to the next, at full gallop, and change horses at each one, and ride on. They wear belts hung with bells, so that the people at the next station hear them coming, and have a fresh horse ready. In this way, Marco says, a message can travel two hundred, or even three hundred miles, in a day and a night.

[long-pause]

There are runners too, on foot, who carry messages, and small packages, from station to station, three miles apart, running, with bells on their belts. And so, Marco says, the Great Khan receives news, and fruits, from places ten days' journey away, in a day and a night.

[pause]

Travellers with a golden tablet, a paiza, like the Polos, can use the post stations too, and get fresh horses, and food, and lodging, at each one, and travel quickly, and safely, across the empire.

[pause]

{img:runner-with-bells-belt-road-countryside-china-morning}
And so, as the Polos come into the lands of the Great Khan, his messengers ride ahead of them, to tell him that the Latin merchants, who he sent to the Pope, are coming back at last.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-ger-camp-river-valley-evening-smoke-herds-returning}
Let us spend an evening in a Mongol camp, on the grasslands, on the way to the court of the Great Khan.

[pause]

The camp is a little group of round felt tents, gers, standing on the open grass, near a river, with the herds grazing around: horses, and sheep, and goats, and cattle, and a few camels. In the distance, there are low, rolling hills, and the enormous sky.

[long-pause]

A ger is a clever and beautiful home. It has a round wall, made of a wooden lattice, which folds up like a fan, and long, thin poles, painted red or orange, which go from the top of the wall to a wooden ring in the middle of the roof, like the spokes of a wheel. Over this frame, there are thick layers of felt, made from sheep's wool, pressed and rolled, and tied with ropes of horsehair. In the middle of the roof, there is an opening, for the smoke, and the light, which can be covered at night.

[pause]

The door always faces south, toward the sun, and away from the cold north wind.

[pause]

Inside, everything has its place. In the middle, there is the fire, or the hearth, the most sacred place in the ger. Opposite the door, at the back, is the place of honour, where the head of the family sits, and the guests, and the little altar, with images of the family's spirits, and their ancestors. The men's side is on the west, on the left as you come in, where the saddles and the weapons are kept. The women's side is on the east, on the right, where the pots and the food and the churns are.

[long-pause]

Marco writes that the Mongols are very careful about their customs, in the ger. You must not step on the threshold, when you come in. You must not lean against the posts. You must not put your feet toward the fire, or throw water on it, or poke it with a knife, because the spirit of the fire lives there.

[pause]

He says that their gers are so light, and so well made, that they can be carried on carts, without being taken down, the great ones pulled by twenty-two oxen. William of Rubruck, a Franciscan friar, who visited the Mongols twenty years before, saw these great carts, with their tents on them, moving across the steppe, like a city on the move.

[pause]

{img:great-ox-cart-carrying-whole-felt-tent-steppe-moving}
In the evening, the herds come home, and the women milk the mares, and the cows, and the goats, and the sheep. The mare's milk is poured into a great leather bag, hanging by the door, and churned, by beating it with a wooden stick, thousands of times, until it ferments, and becomes kumis, the favourite drink of the Mongols.

[long-pause]

Supper is boiled mutton, in a great pot, over the fire, with the broth drunk from bowls, and dried curds, and cheese, and kumis. Guests are always welcome, and always fed. Hospitality is a sacred duty, on the steppe, where travellers may die of cold, or hunger, if no one takes them in.

[pause]

And after supper, the family sits around the fire, and someone plays a fiddle, with a horse's head carved on top of it, and sings the long songs of the steppe, slow and sad and beautiful, about horses, and mothers, and the grasslands, and the sky.

[pause]

{img:horsehead-fiddle-player-singing-ger-fire-family-listening}
And then they lie down to sleep, on felt mats, and furs, around the fire. And outside, the horses stamp, and the dogs bark, and the stars wheel across the great sky, over the sleeping steppe.

[long-pause]

{img:shangdu-xanadu-summer-palace-grassland-white-walls-1275}
In the spring of 1275, three and a half years after they left Venice, the Polos come to the summer palace of Kublai Khan, at a city called Shangdu, on the grasslands, north of Beijing, on the edge of Mongolia.

[pause]

Marco calls it Ciandu. In English, it will later be called Xanadu.

[long-pause]

The Great Khan has sent riders, forty days' journey, to meet them, and bring them to him, with every comfort. And at Shangdu, they are taken to the palace, and brought before the Great Khan, Kublai, the ruler of the greatest empire in the world.

[pause]

They kneel before him, and bow down, with their faces to the ground, as is the custom. And Kublai tells them to rise, and receives them with great honour, and joy, and asks them about their journey, and their health, and about the Pope.

[pause]

{img:polos-kneeling-before-kublai-khan-throne-hall-courtiers}
They give him the letters, and the gifts, from Pope Gregory. And the holy oil, from the lamp in Jerusalem, which he receives with great respect. And then he sees young Marco, and asks who he is. And Niccolò says: Sire, this is my son, and your servant. And Kublai says: he is welcome. And Marco, at twenty-one, enters the service of the Great Khan.

[long-pause]

Shangdu is a wonderful place.

[pause]

Marco describes the palace: a great palace of marble and other fine stones, with halls and rooms all painted and gilded, with pictures of men and beasts and birds and trees and flowers, so beautiful that they delight the eye. Around it, there is a great wall, enclosing a park, of sixteen miles around, with streams, and fountains, and meadows, and woods, full of every kind of wild animal: deer, and stags, and roebucks, which the Khan keeps to feed his falcons and his hawks.

[pause]

In the park, there is another palace, made entirely of cane, of bamboo, gilded and varnished inside, so that rain cannot harm it, held up by gilded pillars, each with a dragon coiled around it. It is held together with two hundred silken cords, and can be taken apart and moved, like a tent. The Khan lives in it in summer, in June, July and August, when the weather is cool there, on the high grassland.

[long-pause]

The Khan rides through the park, with a leopard behind him on his horse, which he lets loose to catch deer. And he has ten thousand white horses, and white mares, whose milk is drunk only by the Khan and his family, and one other tribe, who once helped Genghis Khan in battle.

[pause]

Every year, on the twenty-eighth day of August, the Khan leaves Shangdu, and before he goes, the milk of the white mares is sprinkled on the ground, and in the air, as an offering to the spirits of the earth, and the sky, so that they will protect the Khan, and his family, and his people, and his herds.

[pause]

{img:white-horses-herd-grassland-summer-evening-light}
More than five hundred years later, in 1797, an English poet, Samuel Taylor Coleridge, will read Marco's description of Shangdu, in a book of travels, and fall asleep, and dream, and wake up, and write a famous poem about it: In Xanadu did Kubla Khan a stately pleasure-dome decree.

[long-pause]

Today, the ruins of Shangdu are still there, on the grassland of Inner Mongolia, in northern China: low walls of earth, in a great square, covered with grass, and wildflowers, with the wind blowing across them, and the sheep grazing among them. It is a World Heritage Site.

[pause]

But in 1275, it is full of life: the court of the Great Khan, with his family, and his wives, and his lords, and his officers, and his soldiers, and his ministers, and his astrologers, and his doctors, and his falconers, and his musicians, and ambassadors and merchants from all over the world.

[pause]

{img:court-banquet-tent-grassland-musicians-dancers-lamps}
And three young Venetians, and their father, who have come all the way from the other end of the world.

[long-pause]

{img:kublai-khan-portrait-mongol-emperor-calm-robes-fur}
Kublai Khan, in 1275, is about sixty years old.

[pause]

He is the grandson of Genghis Khan. Marco describes him: of good stature, neither tall nor short, but of medium height, well built, with a fair complexion, sometimes rosy, like a rose, and black eyes, and a well-shaped nose.

[long-pause]

He became Great Khan, the ruler of all the Mongols, in 1260, after a struggle with his brother. And in 1271, the year the Polos left Venice, he declared a new dynasty, in China, the Yuan dynasty, and became emperor of China.

[pause]

He is a great ruler. He is clever, and curious, and ambitious, and tolerant. Unlike his grandfather, he loves China, and its culture, and has learned to speak Chinese, although he still prefers Mongol customs, and loves to hunt, and drink kumis, and live in tents, in summer, on the grasslands.

[pause]

{img:emperor-studying-scrolls-advisers-chinese-mongol-palace}
He is interested in everything: in religions, and sciences, and medicine, and astronomy, and engineering, and trade. He invites scholars, and priests, and experts, from all over the world, to his court: Chinese, and Persians, and Arabs, and Tibetans, and Turks, and Christians, and Muslims, and Buddhists, and Taoists. He listens to them all, and uses their talents.

[long-pause]

He is tolerant of all religions. Marco writes that the Khan celebrates the great feasts of the Christians, and the Muslims, and the Jews, and the Buddhists, and that when he was asked why, he said that there are four prophets, worshipped by all the world: Jesus, and Muhammad, and Moses, and the Buddha. And he honours all four, so that whichever is the greatest in heaven will help him.

[pause]

He has four wives, empresses, and many other wives. His favourite, his chief wife, is Chabi, a wise and gentle woman, who is a great influence on him, and helps him to rule. She is a Buddhist, and is kind to the people of China.

[pause]

And Kublai has many children. His eldest son by Chabi, Zhenjin, has been named his heir, the crown prince, and is being educated in Chinese learning.

[long-pause]

Kublai is a great hunter. Marco describes his hunts: the Khan rides out, with thousands of men, and hawks, and falcons, and dogs, and leopards, and lynxes, and even tigers, trained to hunt, and great eagles, which can catch wolves. He rides in a beautiful wooden room, carried on the backs of four elephants, covered inside with cloth of gold, and outside with lion skins, with his falconers beside him.

[pause]

When cranes fly over, his barons tell him, and he opens the roof of the room, and lets his falcons fly, and watches them, as he lies on his couch. Marco says that no man in the world has ever had such pleasure, or ever will.

[pause]

{img:elephants-carrying-pavilion-hunting-party-falcons-plain}
He also describes the great feasts of the Khan, on his birthday, and at the New Year, when all the lords, and officials, of the empire, thousands of them, come to the court, dressed in robes of the same colour, given to them by the Khan, of silk and gold, with belts of gold and silver, and pearls, and precious stones. And they bring him gifts: a hundred thousand white horses, and five thousand elephants, covered with cloth, carrying chests of gold and silver.

[long-pause]

Is all this true? Marco loved numbers, and he loved to describe wonders, and perhaps he exaggerated, sometimes. Some of his readers, in Europe, thought he was lying, and called his book Il Milione, the book of a million lies.

[pause]

But much of what he describes is confirmed by Chinese and Persian writers of the same time. Kublai Khan really was the greatest ruler in the world, and his court the most splendid.

[pause]

{img:new-year-court-white-robes-lords-bowing-throne-hall-gold}
And Kublai likes Marco. He sees that the young man is clever, and observant, and learns languages quickly, and tells good stories. And he decides to use him.

[long-pause]

{img:khanbaliq-dadu-city-walls-gates-grid-streets-winter-1276}
In the winter, the Great Khan lives in his capital, a great new city, which the Mongols call Khanbaliq, the city of the Khan, and the Chinese call Dadu, the great capital. It stands where Beijing stands today.

[pause]

Kublai has built it, on the site of an older city, beginning in 1267. It is a planned city, laid out in a perfect square, with straight, wide streets, running from wall to wall, so straight, Marco says, that from one gate you can see across the whole city, to the gate on the other side.

[long-pause]

The city wall, Marco says, is twenty-four miles around, made of earth, thick and high, and whitewashed, with battlements. It has twelve gates, three on each side, and over each gate, a great palace, a gatehouse, where soldiers live, a thousand at each gate.

[pause]

Inside, the city is divided into squares, like a chessboard, with houses, and gardens, and courtyards, for the families of the city. Marco says that the whole city is laid out so beautifully, and so regularly, that it cannot be described.

[pause]

{img:straight-wide-street-dadu-courtyard-houses-trees-carts}
In the middle of the city, there is a great bell tower, with a great bell, which is rung every night, to tell the people that they must stay indoors, and no one may go out in the streets, except for a good reason, such as a woman giving birth, or someone ill, and then they must carry a lantern.

[long-pause]

And in the southern part of the city, there is the palace of the Great Khan.

[pause]

Marco describes it in great detail. It is surrounded by a great wall, a mile on each side, and inside it, another wall, and inside that, the palace itself. It is the greatest palace that ever was, he says. It has no upper floor, but stands on a great platform, ten palms high, with marble steps, and marble walls. The walls inside are covered with gold and silver, and painted with dragons, and beasts, and birds, and horsemen, and battle scenes.

[pause]

The great hall is so large that six thousand people can dine in it. The roof is painted red, and green, and blue, and yellow, and all the colours, and varnished, so that it shines like crystal, and can be seen from far away. Behind the palace, there are the private rooms of the Khan, and his wives, and his treasure.

[long-pause]

Between the walls, there are parks, with meadows, and trees, and every kind of animal, deer and gazelles, and a lake, full of fish, and swans and water birds.

[pause]

And beside the palace, there is a hill, which Kublai has made, with earth dug from the lake, a hundred paces high, and covered with the most beautiful evergreen trees, brought from all over the empire, by elephants, with their roots and earth. The hill is covered with green stones, and on top, there is a green palace. So that everything is green, the hill, and the trees, and the palace. They call it the Green Mount. It is still there today, in Beijing, in Beihai Park.

[pause]

{img:green-hill-evergreen-trees-small-palace-top-lake-beijing}
Marco loves the city. He describes its suburbs, outside the walls, as large as the city itself, full of inns, for the merchants, who come from all over the world. And he says that more precious and costly things come into Khanbaliq than into any other city in the world: every day, more than a thousand carts of silk.

[long-pause]

The winters in Khanbaliq are very cold, with a bitter wind from the north, from Mongolia, and snow, and ice on the lakes.

[pause]

To keep warm, Marco says, the people burn a kind of black stone, which they dig out of the mountains, which burns like wood, but better, and holds the fire better, and longer, so that if you light it in the evening, it burns all night, until the morning. There is so much of it, and it is so cheap, that everyone uses it.

[pause]

{img:courtyard-house-winter-snow-coal-brazier-family-warm}
It is coal. In Europe, in Marco's time, coal is hardly used at all. So it seemed to Marco one of the wonders of China.

[pause]

And the Chinese, he says, also take hot baths, three times a week, and in winter, every day, if they can, heated by this black stone. Because there is not enough wood, for so many baths.

[long-pause]

{img:silkworms-eating-mulberry-leaves-flat-baskets-chinese-house}
How is silk made?

[pause]

For thousands of years, the Chinese kept it a secret. Anyone who tried to take silkworms out of China could be punished by death. And so, for centuries, the people of the West wore silk, and loved it, and paid enormous sums for it, without knowing where it came from.

[long-pause]

Silk comes from a little caterpillar, the silkworm, which is really the caterpillar of a moth. It eats only one thing: the leaves of the white mulberry tree.

[pause]

In the villages of China, in the spring, the families raise silkworms, in their houses, on flat trays of woven bamboo, stacked on shelves. The tiny worms hatch from eggs, and eat, and eat, and eat, day and night, fresh mulberry leaves, picked by the women and children, several times a day. The room is filled with a soft, gentle sound, like rain falling, which is the sound of thousands of silkworms munching leaves.

[pause]

In about a month, the silkworms have grown thousands of times bigger. Then they stop eating, and begin to spin. Each one spins a cocoon around itself, from a single thread of silk, which comes out of its mouth, turning its head round and round, in a figure of eight, for two or three days, until it is completely wrapped in a little white or yellow oval cocoon, about the size of a thumb.

[long-pause]

The single thread of a cocoon is very fine, and very strong, and very long: up to a kilometre long.

[pause]

To get the thread, the cocoons are dropped into hot water, which softens the gum that holds the thread together. Then the women find the end of the thread, with a little brush, and unwind it, carefully, onto a reel, joining the threads of several cocoons together, to make one stronger thread. This is called reeling.

[pause]

{img:woman-reeling-silk-cocoons-hot-water-basin-wooden-reel}
The raw silk is then washed, and dyed, in beautiful colours, and spun, and woven on looms, into cloth: plain silk, and satin, and damask, with patterns woven into it, and brocades, with gold and silver threads, and gauze, so fine it is almost transparent. The most complicated patterns are woven on great drawlooms, with two people working together, one sitting high up on the loom, lifting the threads for the pattern, while the other weaves.

[long-pause]

Silk is used for everything in China, by those who can afford it: clothes, and bedding, and curtains, and paintings, and writing, and banners, and fans, and fishing lines, and musical instruments' strings, and even, Marco says, as a kind of money, in some places.

[pause]

Marco writes that in the city of Khanbaliq, more than a thousand cartloads of silk arrive every day. And that in Hangzhou, everyone, almost, dresses in silk, because there is so much of it.

[pause]

{img:drawloom-two-weavers-silk-brocade-pattern-workshop-light}
By Marco's time, the secret of silk has long since spread beyond China, to Central Asia, and Persia, and Byzantium, and even Italy. But the silk of China is still the finest in the world. And the little silkworms, munching their mulberry leaves, in the quiet villages, are still the beginning of the great road that bears their name.

[long-pause]

{img:paper-money-yuan-dynasty-notes-printed-red-seals-table}
Of all the wonders that Marco Polo sees in China, the one that astonishes him most is money made of paper.

[pause]

In Europe, in the thirteenth century, all money is made of metal: coins of gold, and silver, and copper. Paper money is unknown. But in China, the emperors have been using paper money for hundreds of years.

[long-pause]

Marco describes how it is made, at the Khan's mint, in Khanbaliq.

[pause]

The paper is made from the bark of the mulberry tree, the same tree whose leaves feed the silkworms. The thin inner bark is soaked, and pounded, and made into a kind of black paper. It is cut into pieces of different sizes, for different values. Each piece is signed and sealed by many officials, and stamped with the red seal of the Khan, in vermilion ink. And then it is money, worth as much as gold or silver.

[pause]

Everyone in the empire must accept it, Marco says, on pain of death. And so the Khan, he says, has more treasure than anyone in the world, because he can make as much money as he likes, out of paper.

[pause]

And when the paper notes become old and worn, people can bring them to the mint, and exchange them for new ones, for a small fee.

[long-pause]

Marco writes that with this paper money, people can buy anything: pearls, and precious stones, and gold, and silver, and anything they want. And that merchants who come from abroad, with gold and silver and precious stones, must sell them to the Khan, for paper money, which they can then spend anywhere in the empire.

[pause]

The Europeans who read his book could hardly believe it. Money made of paper seemed like a fairy tale, or a trick. Paper money would not be used in Europe for another three or four hundred years.

[pause]

{img:merchant-counting-paper-notes-shop-counter-silk-bolts}
Marco describes other wonders too, which were new to Europeans.

[long-pause]

He describes porcelain, the beautiful, hard, white, shining pottery of China, so fine that light shines through it, which the Chinese make from a special white clay, and fire in great kilns. He says that in one city, in the south, the people make porcelain bowls, so many and so cheap, that you can buy eight of them for a Venetian groat. The word porcelain comes from the Italian word that Marco used for it, porcellana, the name of a kind of shiny white seashell.

[pause]

He describes the great rivers of China, and the boats on them, thousands and thousands, carrying goods. And the Grand Canal, which Kublai is extending, to carry rice from the south to the capital.

[pause]

He describes asbestos, which he calls salamander, a mineral, dug from a mountain, which can be spun into cloth that does not burn, and is cleaned by being thrown into the fire.

[long-pause]

And he describes the way the Khan takes care of his people.

[pause]

When the harvest fails, in any province, because of storms, or locusts, or floods, the Khan does not take taxes from that province, and sends them grain from his own stores. He keeps great granaries, full of grain, in good years, so that when there is a famine, he can sell it cheaply to the people. And every day, in the capital, he gives food to thousands of poor people.

[pause]

{img:granary-officials-distributing-grain-sacks-poor-families}
Marco writes all this with wonder and admiration. Perhaps he makes the Khan sound a little better than he really was. But China, in the thirteenth century, really was in many ways richer, and more advanced, than Europe: with its great cities, its printing, its paper money, its porcelain, its canals, its silk, and its millions of people.

[long-pause]

{img:marco-polo-young-envoy-riding-chinese-road-escort-paiza}
For seventeen years, Marco Polo lives in China, in the service of Kublai Khan.

[pause]

We do not know exactly what he did. His book is not clear, and some historians doubt some of what he says. But it seems that the Khan used him as a messenger, and an envoy, and perhaps an official, sending him on missions, to different parts of the empire, to carry messages, and to inspect, and to report.

[long-pause]

Marco writes that he learned four languages, and four scripts, quickly. Probably Mongol, and Persian, which was the common language of trade and government, among the foreigners in the Mongol empire, and Turkish, and perhaps a little Chinese, although he never mentions Chinese writing, or tea, or the Great Wall, which has made some people wonder.

[pause]

He writes that the Khan noticed that the envoys he sent out to different parts of the empire came back, and told him only about the business they had been sent to do, and nothing else. And this made the Khan angry, because he was curious, and wanted to hear about the strange customs, and wonders, of the places they had visited.

[pause]

{img:envoy-reporting-to-khan-palace-room-maps-scrolls-listening}
So Marco, being clever, made sure, on his missions, to notice everything: the people, and their customs, and their food, and their clothes, and their religions, and their animals, and their plants, and their cities, and their trade. And when he came back, he told the Khan all about it. And the Khan loved his stories, and sent him on more missions.

[long-pause]

So Marco travelled all over China, for years: south, to the province of Yunnan, in the mountains, near Burma and Tibet. To Tibet itself. And east, to the great cities of the south, along the Grand Canal, and the Yangtze River.

[pause]

He writes about all these places in his book: about the wild mountains of Tibet, where the people burn green bamboo at night, which explodes with a great bang, to frighten away the wild animals. About Yunnan, where the people eat raw meat, and gamble, and use shells as money, and where there are great crocodiles, which he calls snakes with legs. About the hill tribes, who tattoo themselves.

[pause]

About Burma, with its golden and silver pagodas, which he says the king built as his tomb. And about the great river Yangtze, the greatest river in the world, he says, so wide that it is like a sea, with more ships on it than on all the rivers and seas of Christendom put together.

[long-pause]

Marco also claims that he governed the great city of Yangzhou, on the Grand Canal, for three years, by order of the Khan. Some historians doubt this, because his name does not appear in the Chinese records. Perhaps he was a minor official there. Perhaps he exaggerated a little.

[pause]

And he says that his father and his uncle helped the Khan to capture a city in southern China, which had been besieged for three years, by building great stone-throwing machines, siege engines, of a European kind. But this story is almost certainly not true, because the siege ended before the Polos arrived in China.

[pause]

{img:yangzhou-canal-city-bridges-boats-merchants-gardens}
Still, much of what Marco describes is accurate, and could only have been known by someone who had really been there, or listened carefully to people who had.

[long-pause]

Meanwhile, Niccolò and Maffeo, it seems, are trading, and growing rich. They have gold, and silver, and precious stones, and silk.

[pause]

And the years pass. Marco grows from a young man of twenty-one, into a man of thirty-eight. He has spent almost half of his life in China. He speaks the languages, and knows the customs, and has friends, and perhaps a household of his own.

[pause]

{img:mature-marco-polo-courtyard-house-china-evening-thinking}
But the Polos are growing older, and so is the Great Khan. And they begin to think of home, of Venice, which they have not seen for so many years. And they begin to worry: what will happen to them, foreigners, when the old Khan dies, and someone else rules China?

[long-pause]

{img:hangzhou-west-lake-pagodas-willows-pleasure-boats-mist}
Of all the cities that Marco Polo saw in China, the one he loved best, and described most beautifully, is the great city of Hangzhou, in the south, which he calls Kinsai, the city of heaven.

[pause]

Until 1276, just after the Polos arrived in China, Hangzhou was the capital of the Song dynasty, the old Chinese emperors of the south, before Kublai Khan's armies conquered them. It is the largest and richest city in the world.

[long-pause]

Marco writes that it is, without doubt, the finest and most splendid city in the world. It is a hundred miles around, he says, and it has twelve thousand stone bridges, over its canals, so high that ships can pass under them. Perhaps the numbers are too large. But Hangzhou really was enormous, with perhaps a million people, or more.

[pause]

The city is built between a great river, the Qiantang, and a beautiful lake, the West Lake, surrounded by green hills, with temples, and pagodas, and gardens, and palaces, and pavilions, along its shores.

[pause]

Marco writes about the lake: that around it, there are beautiful palaces and houses, of the nobles, and temples, and monasteries. And in the middle, two islands, with palaces, where people go for weddings and feasts. And on the lake, there are many boats, pleasure boats, large and small, with tables and chairs, and everything needed for a party, painted, and decorated, where people go out with their friends, and their families, and eat, and drink, and enjoy themselves, and look at the beautiful views, of the city, and the hills, and the temples.

[long-pause]

He writes about the markets of Hangzhou: ten great squares, with markets, three days a week, where forty or fifty thousand people come, with every kind of food: game, and fowl, and ducks, and geese, and fruit, enormous pears, white inside, and fragrant, and peaches, yellow and white, and grapes, and fish, from the river and the lake and the sea, so much fish that you would think it could never all be sold. But within a few hours, it is all gone.

[pause]

He writes about the streets, paved with stone and brick, and the canals, with their boats, and the houses, of three and four storeys, and the shops, and the workshops, of twelve great guilds of craftsmen, each with thousands of workshops.

[pause]

{img:hangzhou-market-fish-fruit-baskets-stalls-crowds-canal}
He writes about the baths, three thousand of them, with hot and cold water, where people bathe every day. And the people themselves: peaceful, and gentle, and honest, and courteous, living together in friendship, so that a whole street seems like one family. And the ladies, beautiful, and elegant, dressed in silk, and jewels.

[long-pause]

At night, he says, the city is guarded by watchmen, on every bridge, who walk the streets, and if they see a light burning after the curfew, they knock on the door, and ask why. And there are watchtowers, on hills, with wooden drums, to give the alarm if there is a fire, because fires are very dangerous, in a city of wooden houses.

[pause]

He describes the custom, when a child is born, of writing down the day, and the hour, and the sign of the planets, so that the astrologers can foretell the child's future, and the way that the people have their names written on their doors, so that the officials know how many people live in each house.

[pause]

{img:night-hangzhou-lanterns-canal-bridge-watchman-quiet}
Hangzhou is still a beautiful city today, and the West Lake is still there, with its willows, and its causeways, and its pagodas, and its pleasure boats, and its tea plantations on the hills around. The Chinese have an old saying: above, there is heaven, and below, there are Suzhou and Hangzhou.

[long-pause]

{img:chinese-junk-fleet-four-masts-harbour-quanzhou-1292-departure}
In 1292, at last, the Polos get their chance to go home.

[pause]

The Mongol ruler of Persia, Arghun Khan, a great-nephew of Kublai, has lost his favourite wife. And as she was dying, she asked him to marry a lady of her own tribe, from Mongolia. So Arghun sent ambassadors to Kublai Khan, to ask for a bride.

[long-pause]

Kublai chose a young lady of seventeen, called Kököchin, beautiful and gracious, Marco says. But the land route back to Persia was blocked, by wars among the Mongol princes. So the ambassadors decided to go by sea, around the south of Asia, through the Indian Ocean.

[pause]

And they asked the Great Khan to let the three Latins, the Polos, who were experienced travellers, and knew the sea, go with them, as guides and companions. The Khan did not want them to go. But at last, he agreed.

[pause]

He gave them two golden tablets, and letters to the kings of Europe, and to the Pope. And he gave them rich gifts. And he fitted out a great fleet for them: fourteen great ships, Chinese junks, with four masts, and many sails, and dozens of cabins, carrying six hundred people, and food for two years.

[long-pause]

They sailed from the great port of Quanzhou, in the south of China, which Marco calls Zaiton, one of the greatest ports in the world, where hundreds of ships came every year, from India, and the Spice Islands, and Arabia, with pepper, and spices, and precious stones.

[pause]

The voyage was long and hard. They sailed south, along the coast of Vietnam, and then to the island of Sumatra, where they had to wait five months, for the monsoon winds to change. They built a camp on the shore, with a fence of wood, to protect themselves.

[pause]

{img:sumatra-coast-tropical-camp-palisade-ships-anchored-waiting}
Marco describes Sumatra: its pepper, and its spices, and its rhinoceroses, which he thought were unicorns, but says are very ugly, and nothing like the unicorns in European stories, which let themselves be caught by maidens. And the sago palms, from which the people make a kind of flour. And the people, who were not yet Muslim, mostly, though some of the ports had converted.

[long-pause]

Then they sailed on, past Ceylon, which Marco says is the finest island of its size in the world, with rubies, and sapphires, and the mountain where Adam is said to be buried. And along the coast of India, which Marco describes at length: the pearl fishers, diving in the sea; the pepper of Malabar; the great kingdoms, and their customs.

[pause]

And at last, after two years at sea, they arrived at Hormuz, in the Persian Gulf, the port they had left, twenty years before.

[pause]

{img:pearl-divers-boats-calm-sea-india-coast-morning}
Many of the people who set out on the voyage did not survive it. Marco says that of the six hundred, only eighteen were left. Perhaps that is an exaggeration. But it was a very hard journey.

[pause]

And when they arrived in Persia, they found that Arghun Khan had passed away, while they were at sea. So the young lady Kököchin married his son, Ghazan, instead. Marco says that she wept, when the Polos left her, because she had grown so fond of them, on the long voyage.

[long-pause]

From Persia, the Polos travelled overland, to Tabriz, and then north, to the city of Trebizond, on the Black Sea. And there, they heard that Kublai Khan had passed away, in 1294, at the age of about seventy-nine.

[pause]

From Trebizond, they sailed to Constantinople, and from there, home, to Venice.

[pause]

They arrived in Venice in 1295. They had been away for twenty-four years. Marco was now forty-one.

[long-pause]

There is a famous story, told many years later, about their return.

[pause]

When they arrived at their house in Venice, their relatives did not recognize them. They looked like Mongols, with their long beards, and their strange, worn, shabby clothes, and they spoke Venetian with a strange accent, mixed with foreign words. Nobody believed that they were the Polos, who everyone thought had died long ago.

[pause]

{img:venetian-feast-hall-travellers-cutting-seams-jewels-spilling}
So they invited all their relatives to a great feast. And at the end of the feast, they brought out their old, shabby travelling coats, and took knives, and cut open the seams. And out poured a stream of jewels, rubies, and sapphires, and emeralds, and diamonds, and pearls, which they had sewn into the lining of their clothes, for safety, on the long journey home. And then everyone believed them.

[pause]

It is probably only a story. But it is a good one.

[long-pause]

{img:quanzhou-zaiton-harbour-many-junks-warehouses-temples-busy}
Before they leave China, by sea, Marco describes the great port of Zaiton, which we now call Quanzhou, on the south-east coast of China, opposite the island of Taiwan.

[pause]

He says that it is one of the two greatest ports in the world. For every ship loaded with pepper that goes to Alexandria, in Egypt, to bring pepper to Europe, a hundred come to Zaiton. And the Khan takes a great tax on all the goods.

[long-pause]

Quanzhou, in 1292, is a city of the whole world.

[pause]

There are merchants there from Persia, and Arabia, and India, and the islands of the Indies, and Southeast Asia, and Korea, and Japan. There are mosques, for the Muslim merchants, and Hindu temples, for the Indian merchants, with carvings of Hindu gods, which can still be seen today. There are Christian churches, and Buddhist temples, and Taoist temples, and Manichaean shrines, all side by side.

[pause]

{img:old-mosque-stone-gate-quanzhou-merchants-arab-chinese}
The harbour is full of ships: great Chinese junks, with four masts, and many sails of bamboo matting, and watertight compartments in their hulls, so that if one part is holed, the ship does not sink, an invention that will not be used in Europe for hundreds of years. And Arab dhows, and Indian ships, and little sampans, darting about between them.

[long-pause]

Marco describes the great Chinese ships, which he will sail on: they are built of fir wood, he says, with one deck, and on the deck, sixty cabins, for the merchants, each one comfortable, and private. They have one rudder, and four masts, and sometimes two more, which can be put up or taken down. They are sheathed with double planks, nailed with iron, and caulked with lime, and hemp, and oil.

[pause]

They are so large that they need two hundred or three hundred sailors to sail them. And they carry great cargoes: five or six thousand baskets of pepper.

[pause]

{img:junk-deck-cabins-bamboo-sails-sailors-loading-pepper-baskets}
Behind them, they tow little boats, and the boats are so large, that they too need forty or fifty sailors, and they are used to tow the great ship, when there is no wind, and to carry the anchor, and for fishing.

[long-pause]

And in the spring of 1292, the fourteen great ships of the Great Khan, with the Polos, and the young lady Kököchin, and her escort, and the ambassadors, and six hundred people, set sail from the harbour of Zaiton, with the north-east monsoon wind behind them, southward, into the South China Sea.

[pause]

And the coast of China, with its hills, and its pagodas, and its great harbour, slowly sinks below the horizon. Marco will never see it again.

[long-pause]

{img:genoa-prison-1298-two-men-writing-cell-window-light}
Three years after he came home, Marco Polo was taken prisoner.

[pause]

In 1298, Venice and Genoa, the two great rival trading cities of Italy, were at war. Marco, it seems, was the commander of a Venetian galley, and was captured by the Genoese, in a sea battle, and taken to prison, in Genoa.

[long-pause]

In prison, he met another prisoner, a writer from Pisa, called Rustichello, who wrote romances, stories of King Arthur and his knights, in French.

[pause]

And Marco told Rustichello the story of his travels. And Rustichello wrote it down, in French, the language of romances, which was read all over Europe. Perhaps Marco had notes, which he sent for, from Venice. Perhaps he told it all from memory. Perhaps Rustichello added a few things, to make it more exciting.

[pause]

The book was called The Description of the World. But it soon became known by another name: Il Milione, The Million. Perhaps because Marco was always talking about millions of things, in China. Or perhaps because the Polo family was nicknamed Milione. Nobody knows for certain.

[long-pause]

The book was an immediate success. It was copied by hand, again and again, and translated into Latin, and Italian, and German, and Spanish, and many other languages. More than a hundred and fifty manuscripts of it still exist today.

[pause]

Many people did not believe it. They thought that Marco was exaggerating, or inventing, or lying. The stories of paper money, and black stones that burn, and a city with twelve thousand bridges, and millions of people, seemed impossible.

[pause]

{img:readers-medieval-library-book-of-marvels-candle-wonder}
There is a story that, when Marco was dying, in 1324, his friends and his priest asked him to take back the things he had said that were not true. And Marco answered: I have not told half of what I saw.

[long-pause]

After the war, in 1299, Marco was released, and went home to Venice. He married a Venetian lady, Donata Badoer, and they had three daughters: Fantina, and Bellela, and Moreta. He lived quietly, as a merchant, in the family house, near the church of San Giovanni Crisostomo, for the rest of his life.

[pause]

He passed away in January 1324, at the age of about seventy. His will still exists, in the archives of Venice. In it, he leaves money to his wife, and his daughters, and to churches, and frees his servant, a Tartar, a Mongol, called Peter, who had come with him from Asia.

[pause]

He was buried in the church of San Lorenzo, in Venice. His tomb has been lost.

[long-pause]

But his book lived on.

[pause]

For two hundred years, it was the main source of knowledge about Asia, in Europe. Mapmakers used it, to draw the first maps of China, and Central Asia, and the islands of the East. And explorers read it, and dreamed of reaching the rich lands that Marco described.

[pause]

{img:columbus-reading-polo-book-margin-notes-candle-ship-model}
One of them was a sailor from Genoa, called Christopher Columbus. He owned a copy of Marco Polo's book, in Latin, and read it carefully, and wrote notes in the margins, more than seventy of them. And in 1492, he sailed west, across the Atlantic, hoping to reach the lands of the Great Khan, that Marco had described. Instead, he found America.

[pause]

His copy of the book, with his handwritten notes, still exists today, in a library in Seville, in Spain.

[long-pause]

{img:venice-today-grand-canal-gondolas-palaces-evening}
Today, more than seven hundred years later, you can still follow the footsteps of Marco Polo, from Venice, across Asia, to China.

[pause]

In Venice, near the church of San Giovanni Crisostomo, there is a little courtyard, called the Corte del Milione, the courtyard of the Million, where the Polo family house once stood. The house is gone. A theatre stands there now, the Teatro Malibran. But there is a plaque, on the wall, remembering Marco Polo, and his travels.

[long-pause]

Venice is still built on its islands, in its lagoon, with its canals and its bridges and its palaces. The four bronze horses still stand on the front of the church of Saint Mark, or rather copies of them, while the originals are kept safe inside. And the boats still come and go, across the lagoon, in the evening light.

[pause]

The airport of Venice is named after him: Marco Polo Airport.

[pause]

In Tabriz, in Iran, the great covered bazaar, where Marco walked, is still there, rebuilt over the centuries, one of the largest covered bazaars in the world, and a World Heritage Site, with its brick vaults and domes, and its carpet sellers, and spice merchants, and its little shops, as busy as ever.

[long-pause]

In the Pamirs, in Tajikistan, and Afghanistan, and Pakistan, and China, the high plateau, the roof of the world, is still wild and lonely, with its snowy peaks, and its blue lakes, and its Kyrgyz and Tajik shepherds, in their yurts, with their yaks and their sheep, and the great Marco Polo sheep, with their curling horns, still grazing on the high slopes.

[pause]

Today, a great road, the Karakoram Highway, winds over the mountains, from Pakistan into China, through the Khunjerab Pass, nearly five thousand metres high, one of the highest paved roads in the world.

[pause]

{img:karakoram-highway-mountains-road-trucks-snow-peaks}
In Kashgar, the Sunday market still brings people from all the villages around, with their donkeys, and sheep, and horses, and melons, and bread, from the clay ovens, though much of the old city has been rebuilt.

[long-pause]

At Dunhuang, the great golden sand dunes still sing, when the wind blows over them, as they did when Marco crossed the desert of Lop. And the crescent-shaped lake is still there, among the dunes, with a little pavilion on its shore. Visitors ride camels over the dunes, at sunset.

[pause]

And the caves of Dunhuang, the Mogao Caves, are still there, in their cliff, with their wonderful paintings, and statues, which have survived for more than fifteen hundred years. In 1900, a monk discovered a hidden cave, sealed for nine hundred years, full of thousands of ancient manuscripts and paintings, including the oldest printed book in the world that has a date, a copy of a Buddhist text, the Diamond Sutra, printed in the year 868.

[pause]

{img:dunhuang-crescent-lake-dunes-pavilion-camels-sunset}
On the grassland of Inner Mongolia, the ruins of Shangdu, Xanadu, lie quietly in the grass, with the wind blowing over them, and the larks singing. And in Beijing, in Beihai Park, the Green Mount of Kublai Khan, now called Jade Flower Island, still rises above the lake, with a white pagoda on its top.

[long-pause]

In Hangzhou, the West Lake is still one of the most beautiful places in China. People still go out on the lake in boats, and walk along the causeways, under the willows, and visit the temples, and drink the famous green tea of the hills, Dragon Well tea, and watch the sun set over the water, and the pagodas, as Marco did.

[pause]

And all across Asia, from China to Turkey, there are new roads, and railways, and pipelines, along the old routes of the Silk Road, and trains, and trucks, carrying goods, once again, from East to West, and West to East.

[pause]

The world is very different now. But the mountains, and the deserts, and the oases, and the rivers, are still there. And the story of the young man from Venice, who went all the way to the court of the Great Khan, and came back to tell the tale, is still told, all over the world.

[long-pause]

So we come to the end of the long road tonight, with the camel bells growing quiet behind us, and the evening light falling on the lagoon of Venice, where the journey began.

[pause]

The caravanserais under the stars, the roof of the world, the singing sands, the palace of the Great Khan, and a merchant's son who saw more than he could ever tell.

[pause]
