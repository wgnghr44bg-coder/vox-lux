{img:venice-1271-grand-canal-galleys-palaces-morning-light}
Tonight we are going about seven hundred and fifty years back, to the city of Venice in the year 1271. And from there, we will follow the old roads of the Silk Road, all the way across Asia.

[long-pause]

A young man of seventeen is about to leave home with his father and his uncle. He does not know it yet, but the journey will last twenty-four years. He will cross deserts and mountains. He will sleep in caravan inns and in the tents of nomads. And at last he will come to the court of the greatest ruler in the world.

[pause]

His name is Marco Polo. Tonight we will travel with him, slowly, along the Silk Road.

[pause]

If you enjoy slow journeys into the past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can easily find the next one.

[long-pause]

Venice in 1271 is one of the richest cities in the world.

[pause]

It is built on more than a hundred little islands, in a shallow lagoon at the top of the Adriatic Sea. Instead of streets, it has canals. Instead of carts, it has boats. The houses and churches stand on millions of tree trunks, driven deep into the mud. The water laps at their doorsteps.

[long-pause]

Venice has no king. It is ruled by a council of rich merchant families, and by a leader they choose, called the Doge. The Doge lives in a great palace beside the church of Saint Mark. Saint Mark is the city's own saint. The Venetians say his bones were smuggled out of Egypt long ago, hidden in a barrel of pork.

[pause]

The church of Saint Mark is covered with domes. Inside, golden pictures made of tiny stones glitter in the candlelight. In front of it lies a great square, and beyond that the harbour, full of ships.

[pause]

{img:saint-marks-basilica-domes-golden-mosaics-piazza-crowds}
Above the main door of the church stand four great horses of golden bronze. The Venetians took them from the city of Constantinople, when they captured it sixty-seven years before.

[long-pause]

Venice lives by trade.

[pause]

Its ships sail all over the Mediterranean Sea, to Constantinople, to Egypt and to North Africa. They carry wool, cloth and timber to the East. And they bring back the treasures of the East. Spices like pepper and cinnamon. Silk and cotton. Sugar, perfumes and precious stones.

[pause]

{img:venetian-merchants-warehouse-spice-sacks-silk-bales-quay}
These goods come from very far away. They come from India, and from China, which the Europeans call Cathay. They travel thousands of miles, passed from one merchant to the next. At last they reach the ports of the Mediterranean, where the Venetians buy them, and sell them all over Europe for a big profit.

[long-pause]

The long road that brings silk from China to the West is what we now call the Silk Road.

[pause]

It is not really one road. It is a great web of caravan tracks, mountain passes and desert trails, more than six thousand kilometres long. It has been used for more than a thousand years.

[pause]

But very few people travel the whole way. Goods are passed along from city to city, each merchant taking them a little further. Hardly any Europeans have ever been to China. Most of them barely know where it is.

[long-pause]

In 1271, though, the road is more open than ever before.

[pause]

In the last fifty years, almost all of Asia has been conquered by one people, the Mongols. And the Mongols keep the roads safe, so that merchants can travel from one end to the other under their protection. But how long will that peace last? Nobody can say.

[pause]

{img:venetian-family-house-canal-balcony-evening-lamps}
And in Venice, in 1271, there is one family of merchants who have already made that long journey, and come back. And now they are about to go again. They are the Polo family.

[long-pause]

{img:two-venetian-merchants-brothers-1260-ship-deck-constantinople}
Marco Polo's father is called Niccolò, and his uncle is called Maffeo. They are merchants of Venice. Besides their house in Venice, they have a house and a business in Constantinople, and another on the Black Sea.

[pause]

In 1260, eleven years before our story, the two brothers set out from Constantinople on a trading trip. They headed for the lands of the Mongols, north of the Black Sea, with a load of jewels to sell.

[long-pause]

They went to the court of a Mongol prince who ruled the great plains of what is now southern Russia. They gave him their jewels, and he gave them goods worth twice as much in return.

[pause]

But then a war broke out between two Mongol princes, and the road back west was closed. So the brothers went east instead, further and further, across the grasslands. They came to the great city of Bukhara, in Central Asia. And there they were stuck for three whole years, waiting for the war to end.

[pause]

In Bukhara they met an ambassador on his way to the Great Khan, the highest ruler of all the Mongols, far away in China. The ambassador had never seen Italians before. He was fascinated by them. He said: come with me. The Great Khan has never met men from your lands. He will be very glad to see you.

[long-pause]

So the brothers travelled with him for a whole year, eastward across Asia, to China and the court of the Great Khan. His name was Kublai.

[pause]

Kublai Khan welcomed them kindly. He asked them many questions. About Europe and its kings, about the Pope, and about the Christian faith. They answered as well as they could, in the languages they had learned on the road.

[pause]

Kublai was clever and curious. He wanted to know more about the West. So he sent the brothers back to Europe as his messengers. They carried a letter to the Pope, asking for a hundred learned men to come and teach the Mongols. And he asked them to bring back some oil from the lamp that burns over the tomb of Christ in Jerusalem.

[long-pause]

He gave them a golden tablet with his name on it. It was a kind of passport. It told everyone in his empire to give them food, horses and guides, and to help them on their way.

[pause]

With this golden tablet, the brothers travelled back across Asia for three years. In 1269 they reached the port of Acre, on the coast of the Holy Land. There, bad news was waiting. The Pope had died, and no new Pope had been chosen yet.

[pause]

{img:golden-paiza-tablet-mongol-script-hands-close-candle}
So they went home to Venice to wait. They had been away for nine years.

[long-pause]

When Niccolò came home, he learned that his wife, Marco's mother, had died while he was gone. And he found his son Marco, now fifteen, whom he had last seen as a little boy.

[pause]

They waited in Venice for two years for a new Pope. But the cardinals could not agree. In 1271, the brothers decided they could wait no longer. Kublai Khan would be wondering what had happened to them. And a great ruler does not like to be kept waiting.

[pause]

{img:father-and-teenage-son-meeting-venetian-house-courtyard}
So they set out again. And this time, they took young Marco with them.

[long-pause]

{img:galley-leaving-venice-lagoon-1271-sails-oars-bell-towers}
In the summer of 1271, Niccolò, Maffeo and young Marco Polo sail out of the lagoon of Venice, heading east across the Mediterranean.

[pause]

Marco is seventeen. He grew up in Venice, among merchants, sailors and ships. He can read and write and keep accounts. He knows how to judge silk and spices and jewels. He has heard many stories from sailors on the quays. But he has never been far from home.

[long-pause]

The voyage across the Mediterranean takes several weeks. The ship stops at ports along the way, in Greece, on Crete and on Cyprus.

[pause]

They sleep on deck or in a tiny cabin. They eat hard ship's biscuit, salted meat, cheese and onions, and drink wine mixed with water. They watch the dolphins play around the ship, and the islands drift by in the haze.

[pause]

At last they come to Acre, on the coast of the Holy Land. It is the last great stronghold of the Crusaders, the Christian knights from Europe. It is a busy, crowded port, full of merchants, knights and pilgrims.

[long-pause]

In Acre they meet an important churchman, Teobaldo Visconti. He represents the Pope in the Holy Land. They tell him about Kublai Khan's letter and ask his advice.

[pause]

He gives them letters for the Great Khan, explaining that there is still no Pope. Then the Polos ride to Jerusalem, a few days away, to fetch the holy oil that Kublai asked for.

[pause]

{img:jerusalem-church-holy-sepulchre-lamp-pilgrims-candles}
Then they travel north along the coast, to the port of Laias. That is where the caravans begin their long journey into Asia.

[long-pause]

But just as they are about to leave, a messenger arrives with amazing news. At last a new Pope has been chosen. And the new Pope is Teobaldo Visconti himself, the very man they met in Acre!

[pause]

So the Polos hurry back to see him. The new Pope receives them warmly. He gives them letters and gifts for Kublai Khan, and his blessing. He cannot send a hundred learned men. But he sends two friars, two learned monks, to travel with them.

[pause]

{img:new-pope-blessing-travellers-acre-hall-friars-letters}
And so, at the end of 1271, the little group sets out into Asia at last. There are the three Polos, the two friars and their servants. They carry the golden tablet, the Pope's letters and the holy oil from Jerusalem.

[long-pause]

But they have not gone far when they hear frightening news. There is war ahead. An army from Egypt is marching into the country, burning as it goes.

[pause]

The two friars are afraid. They hand their letters and gifts to the Polos, and turn back for home.

[pause]

So the three Venetians go on alone with their servants, eastward into the unknown. They will not see Venice again for twenty-four years.

[long-pause]

What does Marco think, as he rides away from the sea into the mountains, at seventeen?

[pause]

We do not know. He never tells us. His book, written many years later, is not a diary. It is a description of the world and its wonders. He says very little about himself or his feelings.

[pause]

{img:young-traveller-looking-back-sea-from-mountain-road}
But we can imagine him. Young and strong and curious, excited and perhaps a little afraid. He rides beside his father and his uncle, with the sea behind him, and the whole of Asia ahead.

[long-pause]

{img:acre-1271-crusader-port-walls-harbour-ships-towers}
Let us stay a little while in Acre, the port where the Polos waited in the autumn of 1271.

[pause]

Acre stands on a point of land that sticks out into the blue sea. It has great walls of golden stone and tall towers. Its harbour is crowded with ships from every port of the Mediterranean.

[long-pause]

In 1271, Acre is the capital of what is left of the Crusader kingdom. It is just a thin strip of coast with a few towns and castles. The Christians of Europe came here on crusade almost two hundred years before. Now they hold on to only this little piece of land, and everyone knows it is in danger.

[pause]

It is a crowded, noisy city, full of people from everywhere. There are knights of the great religious orders, the Templars and the Hospitallers, each with their own fortress-like houses. There are pilgrims on their way to Jerusalem and Bethlehem. There are priests, monks and friars.

[pause]

{img:acre-narrow-street-market-merchants-knights-pilgrims-arches}
And there are merchants from Venice, Genoa and Pisa, each with their own part of town. Arab, Jewish and Greek traders do business with all of them.

[long-pause]

The Venetian quarter of Acre is a little piece of Venice by the sea. It has houses, warehouses and its own church of Saint Mark. And it has a great trading house with a courtyard, where travelling merchants can sleep and store their goods. This is probably where the Polos stayed.

[pause]

In the markets they sell the goods of the East, brought by caravan from Damascus and Baghdad. There are spices, silk and sugar, glass and incense and perfumes.

[pause]

{img:fondaco-courtyard-venetian-merchants-bales-arches-afternoon}
In the evenings, the Polos would have walked on the sea wall. They watched the sun go down over the sea, toward Venice, far away in the west. And they talked about the long road east. The two brothers knew it well. Young Marco did not know it at all.

[long-pause]

Twenty years after the Polos passed through, Acre will fall to the Sultan of Egypt. The last Crusaders will leave the Holy Land forever.

[pause]

But the old city of Acre is still there today. Its walls still stand by the sea, and its harbour is full of little fishing boats. And underground lie the great stone halls of the knights. They were buried for centuries, and now they have been dug out. You can walk through them, cool and dim, under the busy streets.

[long-pause]

{img:camel-caravan-desert-dunes-silk-road-sunset-long-line}
Let us think for a while about the great road Marco Polo is about to follow.

[pause]

In Marco's time, nobody called it the Silk Road. That name was invented about six hundred years later by a German geographer. But the roads themselves are very old. In 1271 they have already been used for more than fifteen hundred years.

[long-pause]

They began around the time of Christ, when China and the Roman Empire were both strong. The Chinese sent silk westward, and the Romans loved it. They paid huge prices for it. Some Roman dresses were so thin that Roman writers complained about them. And the Romans had no idea how silk was made. Some thought it grew on trees.

[pause]

Silk was the most famous thing on the road. But it was far from the only thing.

[pause]

{img:bales-of-silk-colourful-bolts-caravanserai-courtyard-merchants}
From the East came silk, porcelain, paper and tea. From the West came horses, glass, silver and wool. From India came cotton, pepper and precious stones. And from Central Asia came melons, grapes and carpets.

[long-pause]

But the road carried more than goods.

[pause]

Ideas travelled along it too, and religions and inventions. Buddhism travelled from India to China along the Silk Road. Paper and gunpowder and the compass travelled from China to the West. Grapes and glass-making went the other way.

[pause]

The road was not one road but many. They crossed each other, split and joined again, like threads in a great cloth, woven across Asia.

[long-pause]

The main routes started in the old capital of China, in the east. They ran west along a narrow strip between mountains and desert, to the edge of a great desert called the Taklamakan.

[pause]

There the road split. It went around the desert to the north and the south, from oasis to oasis. Then it climbed over the high Pamir mountains, the roof of the world. From there it went on to the great cities of Central Asia, like Samarkand and Bukhara, and then across Persia, to the shores of the Mediterranean.

[pause]

{img:oasis-town-poplars-mud-walls-mountains-desert-edge}
It was a road through some of the hardest country on earth. There were burning deserts and freezing mountains. There were high, windy plains where nothing grows. Travellers suffered from heat and cold, from thirst and hunger, from sandstorms, snowstorms and robbers. Not every traveller who set out on this road came home again.

[long-pause]

But in 1271 the road is safer than it has ever been, because of the Mongols.

[pause]

Fifty years before, the Mongols swept across Asia, led by the great conqueror Genghis Khan. They were horsemen from the grasslands of Mongolia. They conquered everything from China to Persia and Russia. It was a time of terrible destruction. But afterwards, most of Asia was ruled by one family.

[pause]

{img:mongol-horsemen-steppe-grassland-felt-tents-herds}
And the Mongol rulers wanted trade. They protected the merchants and kept the roads safe. They built rest houses and stations for fresh horses along the way. Historians call it the Mongol peace. For about a hundred years, a merchant could travel from the Black Sea to China in fairly good safety.

[long-pause]

So in these years, more Europeans than ever before travel into Asia. Merchants, missionaries and ambassadors.

[pause]

A Franciscan friar called William had travelled to the Mongol capital nearly twenty years before, and wrote a wonderful account of his trip. But Marco Polo's journey will be the longest of them all. And his book will be the most famous.

[long-pause]

{img:armenian-highlands-mountains-monastery-caravan-road-1272}
From Laias, the Polos ride north and east, into the high lands of Armenia, in what is now eastern Turkey.

[pause]

It is a land of high green plains and great mountains, of deep valleys and old stone churches on the hilltops. Armenians live here, Christians with their own old church and their own alphabet. Turks, Greeks and Kurds live here too, side by side.

[long-pause]

Marco writes a little about this country. He says the best cotton cloth in the world is made here. In summer, he says, the Mongol armies come with their herds, because the grass is so good. In winter they leave, because of the cold.

[pause]

He writes that in the middle of Armenia there is a very high mountain where Noah's Ark is said to have come to rest after the Flood. It is so high and so covered in snow, he says, that nobody can climb it. That is Mount Ararat.

[pause]

And he writes about something strange. Near the border with Georgia, he says, there is a spring where oil gushes out of the ground. There is so much of it that a hundred ships could be filled at once. You cannot eat it, but it burns well, and it heals the skin of camels. That place is near Baku. Seven hundred years later, it will become one of the great oil fields of the world.

[long-pause]

Then they come down into Persia, to the great city of Tabriz.

[pause]

Tabriz is the capital of the Mongol rulers of Persia, who are cousins of Kublai Khan. It is one of the richest trading cities in the world. Merchants come here from India, Baghdad, Europe and China.

[pause]

{img:tabriz-bazaar-covered-brick-domes-merchants-carpets-silk}
Marco writes that the people of Tabriz live by trade and crafts. They weave beautiful cloth of silk and gold. He even meets merchants there from Genoa, Venice's great rival.

[long-pause]

The bazaar of Tabriz is a wonder. Its long streets are covered with brick roofs and domes, so it stays cool and shady inside. Bright beams of sunlight fall through little openings in the roof. On both sides there are hundreds of little shops. Silk sellers and carpet sellers, spice dealers and goldsmiths. Coppersmiths hammer their pots, and fruit sellers call out their prices.

[pause]

The air smells of spices, leather and roasting meat. You hear voices in many languages, bargaining and laughing.

[pause]

{img:spice-stall-bazaar-pyramids-of-spices-saffron-cumin-light}
Around the bazaar there are big inns for merchants, called caravanserais. They have courtyards with rooms all around, and stables for the animals. Here the caravans unload, and the merchants do their business.

[long-pause]

Then the Polos travel on, southeast across Persia, through the town of Saveh.

[pause]

In Saveh, Marco writes, he was shown the tombs of the three wise men who came from the East to Bethlehem, to see the baby Jesus. The people of the town tell him an old story about the three kings, a star and a mysterious fire from heaven.

[pause]

Marco loves stories like this. He listens to the people he meets, remembers what they tell him, and writes it all down, years later, in his book.

[long-pause]

Persia is a beautiful country of high plains and mountains, deserts and green oases with gardens and orchards.

[pause]

Marco writes that Persia has wonderful horses, which are sold to India for a high price. And it has the finest donkeys in the world. They are faster than horses and eat very little, so merchants love them. The people make beautiful things, like embroidery, saddles and fine weapons.

[pause]

{img:persian-garden-orchard-pool-cypress-trees-pavilion}
In the evenings, in the inns, the travellers sit together in the courtyards under the stars. They eat and drink and tell stories in Persian, Turkish and Arabic. And young Marco listens. He learns the languages and the customs of Asia. One day, he will need every word he learns here.

[long-pause]

{img:caravanserai-courtyard-evening-camels-merchants-fires-arches}
How do people cross Asia in the year 1272?

[pause]

They travel in caravans. A caravan is a long line of camels, horses, mules and donkeys, loaded with goods and food and water. Merchants, drivers, guards and pilgrims all travel together, because together it is safer.

[long-pause]

A caravan might have a few dozen animals, or several hundred. The camels walk in a long line, one behind the other, tied together with ropes. A man leads the first one on foot. Each camel carries a heavy load in bags tied on both sides of its back.

[pause]

The camels walk slowly and steadily, at about the speed of a walking man. A caravan travels perhaps twenty or thirty kilometres a day.

[pause]

Around the neck of the leading camel hangs a bell, which rings with every step. That way, the drivers at the back can hear where the front is, even in the dark, or in a sandstorm. The sound of camel bells is the sound of the Silk Road.

[long-pause]

In the summer, the caravans often travel at night, when it is cooler, under the stars and the moon. They rest in the heat of the day.

[pause]

And at the end of a long day, if they are lucky, they reach a caravanserai.

[pause]

{img:caravanserai-fortress-gate-desert-road-arriving-dusk}
A caravanserai is an inn for caravans. It looks like a little fortress, square, with high, thick walls and only one great gate. At night the gate is closed, to keep out thieves and wild animals. Inside there is a big open courtyard with a well. Around it are rooms where travellers sleep and store their goods, and stables for the animals.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the caravanserai is busy and noisy. The camels are unloaded and kneel down in the courtyard, grumbling and groaning, as camels do. The horses are watered and fed. The merchants carry their bags into their rooms and lock them up. The cooks light their fires and make supper. There is rice and mutton, flat bread baked on hot stones, and dried fruit.

[pause]

Merchants from many lands meet here and swap news. What is the road ahead like? Are there robbers on the next stretch? Is there war somewhere? On the Silk Road, the right news at the right time can save your life.

[pause]

Many caravanserais were built by rulers or rich men as a good deed. Travellers can stay there for free, or for very little.

[long-pause]

At night, the gate is closed and barred. The travellers lie down on carpets or felt mats, wrapped in their cloaks, with their goods beside them. The animals shift and grumble in the courtyard. Above them, the stars shine in a square of sky. And a watchman walks around the walls.

[pause]

Before dawn, the gate opens again. The camels are loaded, and the caravan sets off once more, in the cold grey light, with the bells ringing.

[pause]

{img:caravan-leaving-at-dawn-gate-opening-cold-light-stars-fading}
Day after day, week after week, the Polos travel like this, across Persia and on toward the great deserts and mountains of Central Asia.

[long-pause]

{img:caravan-cook-fire-iron-pot-flatbread-camels-resting-evening}
What do the travellers eat on the long road across Asia?

[pause]

They carry food with them on the camels. There is flour and rice, dried meat and dried fruit. There are raisins, apricots and nuts. And there are little balls of dried yoghurt, which can be stirred into water to make a sour, cool drink.

[long-pause]

At every town and oasis, they buy fresh food. Bread, meat, vegetables, fruit and milk.

[pause]

The bread of Central Asia is round and flat. It is baked inside big clay ovens shaped like jars, with a fire at the bottom. The baker slaps the dough onto the hot inside wall, where it sticks. A few minutes later he peels it off, golden and crisp, sprinkled with seeds.

[pause]

{img:baker-slapping-dough-tandoor-clay-oven-flatbreads-stacked}
There is rice cooked with mutton, carrots, onions and raisins in a big iron pot. It is called plov, and it is still the great feast dish of Central Asia today. There are dumplings filled with meat, and long noodles pulled by hand and cooked in soup.

[long-pause]

There is mutton roasted on sticks over hot coals, with salt and cumin. And in the oases in summer, there are huge sweet melons, grapes and peaches.

[pause]

To drink there is water from wells and springs. There is wine made from grapes in Persia. And there is a sour drink of fermented horse milk, which the Mongols love.

[pause]

{img:skewers-mutton-charcoal-grill-bazaar-smoke-evening}
In the deserts, water is the biggest worry. The travellers fill big leather bags at every well, and share the water out carefully. A camel can go a week or more without drinking, but a horse cannot. If a well has gone dry, the whole caravan is in trouble. So in the desert, everyone watches the water bags, and nobody wastes a single drop.

[long-pause]

And in the evenings, around the fire, in the inns or out in the open desert, the travellers share their food. They talk and tell stories in many languages. Someone plays a little lute or a flute. And at last they fall asleep, wrapped in their cloaks by the glowing coals, with the camels resting around them, chewing quietly in the dark.

[long-pause]

{img:hormuz-port-persian-gulf-hot-white-houses-dhows-palms}
From Persia, the Polos first travel south, all the way to the Persian Gulf, to the great port of Hormuz.

[pause]

Their plan seems to be to take a ship from Hormuz across the Indian Ocean to India, and then sail on to China. Many merchants go that way.

[long-pause]

Marco describes the road south. It goes down from the high, cool plains of Persia, through mountain passes, onto a hot plain full of date palms and birds. There are white oxen with humps on their backs, and sheep as big as donkeys, with fat tails.

[pause]

The plain is often raided by bandits from the hills. Marco tells a strange story about them. He says they can make the day go dark by magic, so nobody sees them coming. Perhaps he is really describing a dust storm. Either way, the travellers keep close together, and watch the hills.

[pause]

And then, at last, they come to the sea, and to Hormuz.

[long-pause]

Hormuz is a hot, busy and rich port. Merchants come here from India with spices, pearls, silk and ivory. They sell them to Persian merchants, who carry them inland and on to the West.

[pause]

Marco writes that the summer heat is so terrible that people leave the town to live in gardens by the river. And every day at noon, he says, a burning wind blows from the desert. When people feel it coming, they go into the water, and stay there up to their necks until it has passed.

[pause]

{img:shallow-sea-people-standing-in-water-heat-midday-palms}
He writes that the people eat dates, salted fish and onions. They harvest their wheat in March. After that, nothing green can be seen except the date palms, until next winter.

[long-pause]

But the Polos do not like the ships of Hormuz.

[pause]

Marco writes that they are bad and dangerous ships. They are not built with iron nails, but sewn together with thread made from coconut husks. They have only one mast and one sail, and no deck. Many of them are lost at sea.

[pause]

{img:sewn-wooden-dhow-ship-beach-coconut-rope-stitches-close}
So the Polos make a big decision. They will not go by sea. They turn back north, the way they came. They will go to China over land, along the Silk Road. It is a much longer way, through deserts and over the highest mountains in the world. Will it be the right choice?

[long-pause]

On the way north, Marco tells one of the most famous stories in his book, the story of the Old Man of the Mountain.

[pause]

The Old Man, he says, was the leader of a secret group who lived in a castle in the mountains of northern Persia. In a hidden valley he made the most beautiful garden in the world. There were golden palaces, fountains of milk and honey, and music everywhere.

[pause]

He would give young men a sleeping drink, and carry them into the garden. When they woke up, they believed they were in Paradise. Then he gave them the drink again, and carried them out. After that they would do anything he asked, to be allowed back.

[pause]

Marco says the Mongols had destroyed this group fifteen years before. The story is probably more legend than truth. But it became one of the most famous tales of the Middle Ages.

[long-pause]

Then the Polos cross the great salt desert of Persia. Marco describes it as seven days with almost no water, and the little there is tastes bitter and makes people ill. There are no houses, no people and no animals. Only sand, stones and salt, shimmering in the heat.

[pause]

{img:salt-desert-persia-white-crust-shimmering-heat-camels-far}
And beyond it, at last, they come to green land again, and to the city of Balkh, in what is now Afghanistan.

[long-pause]

{img:balkh-ruins-mud-walls-mother-of-cities-plain-mountains}
Balkh is one of the oldest cities in the world. The Persians call it the mother of cities.

[pause]

Alexander the Great came here long ago and married a local princess. For centuries Balkh was a great centre of trade and learning.

[long-pause]

But fifty years before the Polos arrive, Genghis Khan and his Mongol armies came, and destroyed it.

[pause]

Marco writes that it was once a great and noble city, with many fine palaces of marble. Now, he says, they are broken and in ruins. Some people still live among the ruins, and slowly the city is coming back to life. But it will never be as great again.

[pause]

The memory of Alexander the Great is still alive here. The local people tell Marco stories about him. Marco gets some of the details wrong, but he writes down what he hears.

[long-pause]

From Balkh, the Polos go east into the mountains, to a land called Badakhshan, in the far northeast of Afghanistan.

[pause]

It is a land of high mountains, deep valleys, fast rivers and green meadows. Marco loves it. He writes that it has the best horses. The people say they come from the famous horse of Alexander the Great. And the mountains are so high, he says, that it takes from morning to evening to climb them.

[pause]

{img:badakhshan-high-valley-river-green-pasture-snow-peaks}
He also writes about the precious stones of Badakhshan. There are red stones like rubies, which only the king may dig. And there is lapis lazuli, a deep blue stone with flecks of gold. It is ground up to make the finest blue paint in the world. Painters as far away as Italy use it for the robe of the Virgin Mary in their pictures.

[long-pause]

In Badakhshan, Marco falls ill.

[pause]

He does not say what the illness was. Perhaps it was a fever from the hot lowlands. It lasts about a year. For a while, it must have been a real worry for his father and uncle. The local people tell him to go up into the mountains, where the air is pure and cool. He does, and slowly he gets well again.

[pause]

So the Polos stay in Badakhshan for about a year. It must have been a strange time for a young man of nineteen, so far from home, living among shepherds and their flocks in the high mountains, getting better slowly in the clean, cold air.

[long-pause]

Marco writes that the people here are brave and good archers. They dress in skins, because cloth is so expensive. And he writes, with a smile, that the women wear enormous trousers made of a hundred yards of cotton, because the men think wide hips are beautiful.

[pause]

{img:mountain-village-flat-roofs-terraces-apricot-trees-blossom}
He also writes about wild sheep with huge curling horns, as long as a man's arm. The shepherds make bowls from them. Today these sheep are named after him. They are called Marco Polo sheep, and they still live in the high mountains.

[long-pause]

{img:pamir-high-plateau-snow-peaks-frozen-lake-yaks-wind}
From Badakhshan, the Polos climb into the highest mountains they have ever seen, the Pamirs.

[pause]

The local people call this place the roof of the world. It is a high, flat land, more than four thousand metres above the sea. All around it rise even higher mountains, covered with snow and ice all year. Here, the great mountain ranges of Asia meet.

[long-pause]

Marco describes it well. He writes that you ride for twelve days across a high plain with a great lake. The grass there is so good that a thin horse grows fat in ten days. And there are many wild sheep with huge horns.

[pause]

But it is a lonely, empty place. For twelve days you see no houses, no people and not even birds, because it is so high and so cold. You must carry all your food with you. If you run out, there is nowhere to buy more.

[pause]

And Marco notices something strange. Up here, he says, fire does not burn as brightly or as hot as in other places. Food does not cook properly. He is right, though he does not know why. High up, the air is thin, and water boils before it gets really hot.

[long-pause]

After that, he writes, you ride for forty more days over mountains and valleys, across rivers and through empty land. The people who live here are wild hunters who dress in animal skins.

[pause]

The journey is very hard. The air is thin, so the travellers are short of breath. They have headaches and feel sick and tired. The wind never stops. There is snow even in summer. The paths are narrow and steep, running along the edges of cliffs. One wrong step could be the last.

[pause]

The animals suffer too. Horses and camels slip and fall. The yaks of the mountains do better. They are big, shaggy black cattle with long hair. They can carry loads through snow and thin air without trouble.

[long-pause]

A few people live in the Pamirs. They are shepherds who live in round felt tents on the high meadows, with their sheep, goats, yaks and fierce dogs. In summer they move up into the high valleys. In winter they move down to more sheltered places.

[pause]

They are kind to travellers. They invite them into their tents and give them bowls of hot, salty tea with butter, and yoghurt and bread. They let them sleep by the fire, out of the wind.

[pause]

{img:inside-felt-yurt-fire-family-tea-bowls-travellers-warm}
Inside the tent it is warm and cosy. The fire burns in the middle, and the smoke drifts out through a round hole in the roof. Bright felt carpets hang on the walls. The family sleeps around the fire on soft mats and furs.

[long-pause]

At last, after weeks in the mountains, the Polos come down on the other side, and the land begins to change.

[pause]

Now the rivers flow east, not west. The air grows warmer. There are trees again, poplars and willows, and fields and villages. And ahead of them, at the edge of a great desert, lies a green oasis city. Its name is Kashgar.

[pause]

{img:descending-mountains-green-valley-poplars-oasis-far-below}
They have crossed the roof of the world. Now they stand at the edge of China. But between them and the Great Khan, there is still a desert so big that people say no one who goes into it ever comes out.

[long-pause]

{img:kashgar-oasis-city-mud-walls-poplars-bazaar-1273}
Kashgar is a great oasis city at the western end of the Taklamakan desert, at the foot of the mountains. Here, many roads of the Silk Road meet.

[pause]

It is a city of merchants, farmers and craftsmen. The houses are made of mud brick, with flat roofs. There are gardens and orchards, watered by streams that come down from the mountains.

[long-pause]

Marco writes that the people of Kashgar live by trade and crafts. They have beautiful gardens and vineyards, and they grow a lot of cotton. Many merchants set out from here to trade all over the world.

[pause]

The market of Kashgar is famous. Every week, people come from all the villages around, and from the mountains and the desert. They buy and sell horses and camels, sheep and donkeys. There is cotton and silk, knives and pots, and fresh bread from the clay ovens.

[pause]

{img:kashgar-bazaar-melons-apricots-bread-ovens-donkeys-crowd}
The melons, grapes and apricots of Kashgar are among the sweetest in the world. They grow in the hot sun, with water from the melting mountain snow. Dried, they are carried all the way to China and Persia.

[long-pause]

From Kashgar, the Polos follow the southern road around the edge of the Taklamakan desert, from oasis to oasis, eastward toward China.

[pause]

The Taklamakan is one of the biggest sand deserts in the world. People say its name means: the place you go into and never come out. It is a sea of sand dunes, some a hundred metres high, with hardly a drop of water.

[pause]

Nobody tries to cross it. The roads go around it, along the foot of the mountains. They go from one oasis to the next, where rivers come down from the mountains and disappear into the sand.

[long-pause]

Along the southern road lie the oasis towns, one after another.

[pause]

The town of Khotan is famous for its jade. Jade is a hard, smooth stone, white or green, cool to the touch. Marco writes that it is found in the rivers here, and carried to China, where it sells for a great price. For more than two thousand years, the Chinese have loved the jade of Khotan above all other stones. In summer, when the water is low, people walk through the riverbeds, looking for jade washed down from the mountains.

[pause]

{img:people-searching-river-bed-white-jade-stones-khotan-summer}
Khotan is also famous for its silk. There is an old story about it. Long ago, China forbade anyone to take silkworms out of the country. But a Chinese princess married the king of Khotan. And she hid silkworm eggs in her headdress, and smuggled them out. If she had been caught, it would have meant terrible trouble. But she was not. And so Khotan learned to make its own silk.

[long-pause]

The oasis towns are green islands in the desert.

[pause]

There are fields of wheat and cotton, orchards of apricots and walnuts, and vineyards. Tall poplar trees line the roads and canals, to give shade and break the wind. The water is shared out very carefully, through little channels, to every field.

[pause]

{img:poplar-lined-canal-oasis-fields-mud-houses-evening-light}
And beyond the last field, the desert begins suddenly, like a wall of sand. The travellers fill their water bags and load their camels. Then they set out across the sand, toward the next oasis, several days away. They always hope the well there will not be dry.

[long-pause]

{img:lop-desert-flat-gravel-salt-camels-endless-grey-horizon}
At the eastern end of the southern road, the Polos come to a town called Lop, at the edge of another great desert.

[pause]

Travellers stop at Lop for a week, to rest themselves and their animals, and to buy food and water. Because crossing this desert takes a whole month, even at its narrowest part. And on the way, there is nothing to eat.

[long-pause]

Marco describes the desert. It is all hills and valleys of sand, with nothing to eat. But about every day's march, he says, there is water. Not enough for a big group, but enough for fifty or a hundred people and their animals. Some of the water is bitter and salty. Some of it is good.

[pause]

There are no animals and no birds, because there is nothing for them to eat.

[pause]

{img:desert-well-small-pool-camels-drinking-dawn-sand}
And then Marco tells something that has fascinated readers ever since.

[long-pause]

When travellers cross this desert at night, he writes, sometimes one of them falls behind, or falls asleep, and loses his companions. Then he hears voices that sound like his friends, calling him by his name. And often the voices lead him away from the road, so that he is lost, and never found again.

[pause]

Sometimes, even in the daytime, travellers hear strange sounds, like music and drums. So at night, the travellers sleep close together. Before they sleep, they put up a sign pointing the way they must go in the morning. And they hang little bells on all their animals, so that no one gets lost.

[pause]

Today, scientists think Marco may have been describing singing sand. In some deserts, when the wind blows over the dunes, or the sand slides down a slope, it makes a deep humming, booming sound, like drums far away. There are singing dunes at the eastern edge of this desert, and they still sing today.

[long-pause]

At the end of the desert, the Polos reach the town of Dunhuang. It is the gateway to China.

[pause]

Dunhuang is a famous oasis, with a little lake shaped like a crescent moon, among golden sand dunes. Near the town, hundreds of caves are carved into a cliff. Buddhist monks made them, more than a thousand years before. Inside, the walls are painted with thousands of bright pictures of the Buddha, of flying spirits, musicians and dancers.

[pause]

{img:dunhuang-cave-cliff-many-openings-poplars-desert-light}
Marco does not mention the caves. But he writes that most people here are Buddhists, and that they have many monasteries full of statues, which they honour greatly.

[long-pause]

He also describes how the people here say goodbye to their dead. They keep the coffin in the house until the star-readers say it is the right day for the funeral. Every day, they set food and drink in front of it, as if the dead person could still eat.

[pause]

On the day of the funeral, they make figures out of paper, of people, horses and money, and burn them, so the dead will have these things in the next world. Chinese families still do something like this today, burning paper money and paper houses at funerals.

[pause]

{img:paper-figures-offerings-temple-incense-gentle-glow}
And now, at last, the Polos are in China. Ahead of them lies the long road east, between snowy mountains and the Gobi desert, to the court of the Great Khan. How will he greet them, after all these years?

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-steppe-endless-grass-blue-sky-gers-horses-grazing}
Who are the Mongols, whose empire stretches across almost all of Asia in 1271?

[pause]

They are a people of herders and horsemen from the great grasslands of Mongolia, north of China. For thousands of years, they have lived on the steppe. That is a land of wide, rolling grass plains under an enormous sky. It is hot in summer, and terribly cold in winter.

[long-pause]

They live in round tents of felt, which can be taken down and loaded onto carts in a few hours. They move with the seasons from one grassland to the next, with their sheep, goats, cattle and camels, and above all their horses.

[pause]

The Mongols live on horseback. Their children learn to ride almost before they can walk. They drink mare's milk, left to turn sour and fizzy, which they love. They eat mutton and cheese and yoghurt. They wear long coats of felt and fur, and leather boots.

[pause]

{img:mongol-woman-milking-mare-ger-camp-morning-children-horses}
Marco writes about them with admiration. He says the Mongol women run the household. They buy and sell, look after the herds and drive the carts. The men hunt and fight. He says the Mongols are incredibly tough. They can ride for days with almost no food, and sleep on the bare ground.

[long-pause]

For centuries, the Mongols were split into many tribes that often fought each other. Then, early in the thirteenth century, one great leader united them. His name was Temujin, and he took the name Genghis Khan.

[pause]

Genghis Khan and his armies conquered northern China, Central Asia and Persia. His sons and grandsons went on to conquer Russia, Korea and southern China. By 1271, the Mongol empire is the largest land empire the world has ever seen.

[pause]

The conquests were terrible, and many cities were destroyed. We will not dwell on that tonight. But once the conquests were over, the Mongol rulers brought a kind of order to Asia, and they opened the roads to trade.

[long-pause]

One of the most amazing things about the Mongol empire is its postal system.

[pause]

Along the main roads, about every day's ride, there are post stations. Each one has an inn, stables and many horses, always ready for the Khan's messengers. Marco says there are more than ten thousand of these stations.

[pause]

{img:post-station-yam-horses-stables-messenger-arriving-dust}
The Khan's messengers ride from one station to the next at full gallop. At each station they jump onto a fresh horse and ride on. They wear belts hung with bells, so the next station hears them coming, and has a horse ready. In this way, Marco says, a message can travel three hundred miles in a day and a night.

[long-pause]

There are also runners on foot, with bells on their belts. They carry small messages from one station to the next, three miles apart. So the Great Khan gets news from far away very quickly.

[pause]

Travellers with a golden tablet, like the Polos, can use the post stations too. They get fresh horses, food and a bed at each one, and can cross the empire fast and safely.

[pause]

{img:runner-with-bells-belt-road-countryside-china-morning}
And so, as the Polos ride into the lands of the Great Khan, his messengers gallop ahead to tell him. The Latin merchants he sent to the Pope are coming back at last. It has been nine years. Will he still remember them? Will he be pleased, or angry that they took so long?

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-ger-camp-river-valley-evening-smoke-herds-returning}
Let us spend an evening in a Mongol camp on the grasslands, on the way to the court of the Great Khan.

[pause]

The camp is a small group of round felt tents, called gers, standing on the open grass near a river. The herds graze around them. In the distance lie low, rolling hills under an enormous sky.

[long-pause]

A ger is a clever and beautiful home. Its round wall is made of a wooden lattice that folds up like a fan. Long, thin poles, painted red or orange, run from the top of the wall to a wooden ring in the middle of the roof, like the spokes of a wheel. Over this frame go thick layers of felt, made from sheep's wool. In the middle of the roof is an opening for the smoke and the light.

[pause]

The door always faces south, toward the sun and away from the cold north wind.

[pause]

Inside, everything has its place. In the middle is the fire, the most sacred spot in the ger. At the back, opposite the door, is the place of honour, where the head of the family and the guests sit. The men's things are on the left as you come in. The women's things, the pots and the food, are on the right.

[long-pause]

Marco writes that the Mongols are very careful about their customs in the ger. You must not step on the threshold when you come in. You must not throw water on the fire or poke it with a knife, because a spirit lives in the fire. A guest who forgets these rules may give great offence. So travellers watch their hosts, and copy what they do.

[pause]

He says the biggest gers are carried on carts without being taken down. The largest carts are pulled by more than twenty oxen. A friar who visited the Mongols saw these great carts rolling across the grass, like a whole city on the move.

[pause]

{img:great-ox-cart-carrying-whole-felt-tent-steppe-moving}
In the evening the herds come home. The women milk the mares. The milk is poured into a big leather bag by the door and beaten with a wooden stick, thousands of times, until it turns sour and fizzy. This is the Mongols' favourite drink.

[long-pause]

Supper is mutton boiled in a big pot over the fire, with the broth drunk from bowls, and cheese and dried curds. Guests are always welcome and always fed. On the steppe, a traveller turned away might die of cold or hunger. So taking in strangers is a sacred duty.

[pause]

After supper the family sits around the fire. Someone plays a fiddle with a horse's head carved at the top. They sing the long songs of the steppe, slow and sad and beautiful, about horses and mothers and the sky.

[pause]

{img:horsehead-fiddle-player-singing-ger-fire-family-listening}
Then they lie down to sleep on felt mats and furs around the fire. Outside, the horses stamp and the dogs bark, and the stars turn slowly across the great sky over the sleeping steppe.

[long-pause]

{img:shangdu-xanadu-summer-palace-grassland-white-walls-1275}
In the spring of 1275, three and a half years after leaving Venice, the Polos reach the summer palace of Kublai Khan. It lies in a city called Shangdu, on the grasslands north of Beijing.

[pause]

Marco calls it Ciandu. In English, it will later be called Xanadu.

[long-pause]

The Great Khan has sent riders a forty-day journey out to meet them, and to bring them to him in comfort. At Shangdu they are led into the palace, before Kublai, the ruler of the greatest empire in the world.

[pause]

They kneel and bow with their faces to the ground, as is the custom. This is the moment they have travelled so long for. And Kublai tells them to rise. He welcomes them with great honour and joy. He asks about their journey, their health, and the Pope.

[pause]

{img:polos-kneeling-before-kublai-khan-throne-hall-courtiers}
They give him the letters and gifts from the Pope, and the holy oil from Jerusalem, which he receives with great respect. Then he notices young Marco, and asks who he is. Niccolò says: Sire, this is my son, and your servant. And Kublai says: he is welcome. So Marco, at twenty-one, enters the service of the Great Khan.

[long-pause]

Shangdu is a wonderful place.

[pause]

Marco describes a great palace of marble, with halls and rooms all painted and covered in gold, with pictures of people, animals, birds and flowers. Around it lies a walled park, sixteen miles around. Inside there are streams and fountains, meadows and woods, full of deer.

[pause]

In the park there is another palace, made entirely of bamboo. It is covered in gold and varnish, so the rain cannot harm it. Its pillars have golden dragons curled around them. It is held together by two hundred silk cords, and it can be taken apart and moved like a tent. The Khan lives in it in the summer months, when the weather is cool up here on the grassland.

[long-pause]

The Khan rides through his park with a tame leopard sitting behind him on his horse. He lets it loose to catch deer. He also has ten thousand white horses. The milk of the white mares may only be drunk by the Khan and his family.

[pause]

Every year at the end of August, before the Khan leaves Shangdu, the milk of the white mares is sprinkled on the ground and into the air. It is a gift to the spirits of the earth and the sky, so they will protect the Khan and his people.

[pause]

{img:white-horses-herd-grassland-summer-evening-light}
More than five hundred years later, an English poet called Coleridge read Marco's description of Shangdu. He fell asleep, and dreamed. When he woke, he wrote a famous poem about it. It begins: In Xanadu did Kubla Khan a stately pleasure-dome decree.

[long-pause]

Today, the ruins of Shangdu still lie on the grassland of Inner Mongolia. There are low earth walls in a great square, covered in grass and wildflowers. The wind blows across them, and sheep graze among them.

[pause]

But in 1275, it is full of life. Here is the court of the Great Khan, with his family and his lords, his soldiers and his doctors, his star-readers and his musicians. There are ambassadors and merchants from all over the world.

[pause]

{img:court-banquet-tent-grassland-musicians-dancers-lamps}
And now there are three Venetians among them, who have come all the way from the other end of the world. They are welcome guests today. But at a court like this, a guest must always be careful. Favour can be won. And favour can also be lost.

[long-pause]

{img:kublai-khan-portrait-mongol-emperor-calm-robes-fur}
In 1275, Kublai Khan is about sixty years old.

[pause]

He is the grandson of Genghis Khan. Marco describes him as a man of medium height, well built, with a fair face that is sometimes rosy, black eyes and a well-shaped nose.

[long-pause]

He became Great Khan in 1260, after a long struggle with his own brother. And in 1271, the year the Polos left Venice, he started a new family of emperors in China. From then on, he was emperor of China too.

[pause]

He is a great ruler. He is clever, curious and ambitious. Unlike his grandfather, he loves China and its culture. He has learned some Chinese. But he still loves Mongol ways. He loves to hunt, to drink mare's milk, and to live in tents on the grasslands in summer.

[pause]

{img:emperor-studying-scrolls-advisers-chinese-mongol-palace}
He is interested in everything. In religion and science, in medicine, the stars and trade. He invites wise men from all over the world to his court. Chinese and Persians, Arabs and Tibetans, Christians, Muslims and Buddhists. He listens to them all, and uses their talents.

[long-pause]

He respects all religions. Marco writes that the Khan celebrates the great feasts of Christians, Muslims, Jews and Buddhists. When someone asked him why, he said there are four great prophets honoured around the world: Jesus, Muhammad, Moses and the Buddha. He honours all four, he said, so that whichever is greatest in heaven will help him.

[pause]

His favourite wife is Chabi. She is wise and gentle, and she helps him rule. She is a Buddhist, and she is kind to the people of China.

[pause]

Their eldest son has been chosen to be the next emperor. He is being taught in the Chinese way.

[long-pause]

Kublai loves to hunt. Marco describes his great hunts. The Khan rides out with thousands of men, with hawks and falcons and dogs, and even trained leopards. He rides in a little wooden room carried on the backs of four elephants. Inside it is lined with cloth of gold.

[pause]

When cranes fly over, he opens the roof and lets his falcons fly, and watches them from his couch. Marco says no man in the world has ever had such pleasure, or ever will.

[pause]

{img:elephants-carrying-pavilion-hunting-party-falcons-plain}
He also describes the great feasts on the Khan's birthday and at New Year. Thousands of lords come to the court, all dressed in robes of the same colour, given by the Khan. They bring him gifts. A hundred thousand white horses, Marco says, and five thousand elephants covered in cloth.

[long-pause]

Is all this true? Marco loves numbers and wonders, and sometimes he may exaggerate. Later, some readers in Europe will think he is lying. They will call his book The Million, as in a million lies.

[pause]

But much of what he describes is confirmed by Chinese and Persian writers of the same time. Kublai really was the greatest ruler in the world, and his court really was the most splendid.

[pause]

{img:new-year-court-white-robes-lords-bowing-throne-hall-gold}
And Kublai likes Marco. He sees that the young man is clever and watchful. He learns languages quickly and tells good stories. So the Khan decides to give him work. It will take Marco far and wide across China. But it will also make it very hard for the Polos ever to go home.

[long-pause]

{img:khanbaliq-dadu-city-walls-gates-grid-streets-winter-1276}
In winter, the Great Khan lives in his capital. It is a big new city that the Mongols call Khanbaliq, the city of the Khan. It stands where Beijing stands today.

[pause]

Kublai began building it in 1267. It is a planned city, laid out in a perfect square. Its streets are wide and straight. So straight, Marco says, that from one gate you can see right across the city to the gate on the other side.

[long-pause]

The city wall, Marco says, is twenty-four miles around. It is made of earth, thick and high, and painted white. It has twelve gates, three on each side. Above each gate is a gatehouse, where a thousand soldiers live.

[pause]

Inside, the city is divided into squares, like a chessboard. There are houses with gardens and courtyards. Marco says it is all laid out so beautifully that words cannot describe it.

[pause]

{img:straight-wide-street-dadu-courtyard-houses-trees-carts}
In the middle of the city there is a tall bell tower. Every night the great bell rings, and after that nobody may go out into the streets without a good reason, such as a sick person or a baby on the way. And then they must carry a lantern. The night belongs to the Khan's guards.

[long-pause]

In the southern part of the city stands the palace of the Great Khan.

[pause]

Marco describes it in great detail. It is surrounded by a great wall, a mile on each side, and inside that, another wall. The palace stands on a high platform with marble steps. Inside, the walls are covered with gold and silver and painted with dragons, birds and horsemen.

[pause]

The great hall is so big that six thousand people can eat there at once. The roof is painted in red, green, blue and yellow, and varnished so that it shines like crystal and can be seen from far away.

[long-pause]

Between the walls there are parks with meadows and trees, full of deer, and a lake full of fish and swans.

[pause]

Beside the palace, Kublai has made a hill from the earth dug out for the lake. It is covered with beautiful evergreen trees, brought from all over the empire by elephants, roots and all. On top stands a green palace. So everything is green, the hill, the trees and the palace. That hill is still there today, in a park in Beijing.

[pause]

{img:green-hill-evergreen-trees-small-palace-top-lake-beijing}
Marco loves the city. He says the area outside the walls is as big as the city itself, full of inns for merchants from all over the world. And he says that more precious things come into this city than into any other. Every day, more than a thousand carts of silk.

[long-pause]

The winters here are very cold. A bitter wind blows from the north, and the lakes freeze.

[pause]

To keep warm, Marco says, people burn a kind of black stone that they dig from the mountains. It burns like wood, but better. If you light it in the evening, it burns all night. There is so much of it, and it is so cheap, that everyone uses it.

[pause]

{img:courtyard-house-winter-snow-coal-brazier-family-warm}
It is coal. In Europe in Marco's time, hardly anyone uses coal. So to Marco it seems one of the wonders of China.

[pause]

The Chinese, he adds, take hot baths three times a week, and in winter every day if they can. The water is heated with this black stone, because there is not enough wood for so many baths.

[long-pause]

{img:silkworms-eating-mulberry-leaves-flat-baskets-chinese-house}
How is silk made?

[pause]

For thousands of years, the Chinese kept it a secret. Anyone who tried to take silkworms out of China could be put to death. So for centuries, people in the West wore silk and paid huge sums for it, without knowing where it came from.

[long-pause]

Silk comes from a little caterpillar called the silkworm. It is really the caterpillar of a moth. And it eats only one thing, the leaves of the white mulberry tree.

[pause]

In the villages of China, families raise silkworms in their houses in spring. The worms live on flat bamboo trays, stacked on shelves. The tiny worms hatch from eggs, and then they eat and eat, day and night. The women and children pick fresh mulberry leaves for them several times a day. The room fills with a soft sound like gentle rain. It is the sound of thousands of silkworms munching.

[pause]

After about a month, the silkworms are thousands of times bigger. Then they stop eating and begin to spin. Each one spins a cocoon around itself from one single thread of silk. It turns its head round and round for two or three days, until it is wrapped in a little white cocoon about the size of a thumb.

[long-pause]

That single thread is very fine, very strong and very long. It can be almost a kilometre long.

[pause]

To get the thread, the cocoons are dropped into hot water. Then the women find the end of the thread with a little brush, and wind it carefully onto a reel. They join the threads of several cocoons together, to make one stronger thread. It is careful work. One clumsy move, and the thread breaks.

[pause]

{img:woman-reeling-silk-cocoons-hot-water-basin-wooden-reel}
The raw silk is washed, dyed in beautiful colours and woven on looms into cloth. There is plain silk, shiny satin, silk with patterns, and silk so fine you can almost see through it. The hardest patterns are woven on huge looms by two people at once. One sits high up on the loom, lifting the threads for the pattern, while the other weaves.

[long-pause]

In China, people who can afford it use silk for everything. For clothes and bedding, for paintings and writing, for fans and banners, and even for the strings of musical instruments.

[pause]

Marco writes that in Khanbaliq, more than a thousand cartloads of silk arrive every day. And in the southern city of Hangzhou, he says, almost everyone wears silk, because there is so much of it.

[pause]

{img:drawloom-two-weavers-silk-brocade-pattern-workshop-light}
By Marco's time, the secret of silk has long since spread beyond China, even as far as Italy. But Chinese silk is still the finest in the world. And the little silkworms munching their leaves in quiet villages are still the beginning of the great road that carries their name.

[long-pause]

{img:paper-money-yuan-dynasty-notes-printed-red-seals-table}
Of all the wonders Marco sees in China, the one that amazes him most is money made of paper.

[pause]

In Europe in the thirteenth century, all money is made of metal. Coins of gold, silver and copper. Nobody has ever heard of paper money. But in China, emperors have used it for hundreds of years.

[long-pause]

Marco describes how it is made in the Khan's workshop in the capital.

[pause]

The paper is made from the bark of the mulberry tree, the same tree that feeds the silkworms. The soft inner bark is soaked and pounded into a dark paper. It is cut into pieces of different sizes for different values. Each piece is signed and sealed by several officials. Last of all it is stamped with the Khan's own seal in bright red ink. And then it is money, worth as much as gold or silver.

[pause]

Everyone in the empire must accept it, Marco says, or face death. So the Khan, he says, is richer than anyone in the world, because he can make as much money as he likes out of paper.

[pause]

When the notes get old and worn, people bring them back and swap them for new ones, for a small fee.

[long-pause]

Marco writes that with this paper money you can buy anything. Pearls, gold, silver, anything at all. Foreign merchants who arrive with gold and jewels must sell them to the Khan for paper money. Then they can spend it anywhere in the empire.

[pause]

Europeans who read his book could hardly believe it. Money made of paper sounded like a fairy tale, or a trick. Europe would not use paper money for another three or four hundred years.

[pause]

{img:merchant-counting-paper-notes-shop-counter-silk-bolts}
Marco describes other things that are new to Europeans too.

[long-pause]

He describes porcelain, the beautiful hard white pottery of China. It is so fine that light shines through it. In one city, he says, porcelain bowls are so cheap that you can buy eight of them for one small Venetian coin. Our word porcelain comes from the Italian word Marco used. It is the name of a kind of shiny white seashell.

[pause]

He describes the great rivers of China, with thousands of boats on them. And the Grand Canal, which Kublai is making longer, to carry rice from the south to the capital.

[pause]

And he describes a strange mineral dug from a mountain. It can be spun into cloth that does not burn. To clean it, you simply throw it in the fire.

[long-pause]

He also describes how the Khan looks after his people.

[pause]

When the harvest fails in a region, because of storms or floods or locusts, the Khan takes no taxes from that region, and sends grain from his own stores. In good years, he fills great storehouses with grain. Then in years of hunger, he can sell it cheaply to the people. Every day in the capital, he gives food to thousands of poor people.

[pause]

{img:granary-officials-distributing-grain-sacks-poor-families}
Marco writes all this with wonder. Perhaps he makes the Khan sound a little better than he really was. But China in the thirteenth century truly was richer and more advanced than Europe in many ways. It had great cities, printing, paper money, porcelain, canals, silk and millions of people. And a young man from Venice was seeing all of it with his own eyes.

[long-pause]

{img:marco-polo-young-envoy-riding-chinese-road-escort-paiza}
For seventeen years, Marco Polo lives in China, in the service of Kublai Khan.

[pause]

We do not know exactly what he did. His book is not always clear, and historians doubt some of it. But it seems the Khan used him as a messenger and a kind of envoy. He sent Marco on missions to different parts of the empire, to carry messages, to look at things, and to report back.

[long-pause]

Marco writes that he learned four languages quickly. Probably Mongol and Persian, which many foreigners used for trade and government. Perhaps a little Turkish. And perhaps a little Chinese, though he never mentions Chinese writing, or tea, or the Great Wall. That has made some people wonder.

[pause]

He tells us something about the Khan. When the Khan sent people on missions, they came back and told him only about their business, and nothing else. That made the Khan cross. He was curious. He wanted to hear about the strange customs and wonders of the places they had seen.

[pause]

{img:envoy-reporting-to-khan-palace-room-maps-scrolls-listening}
So Marco was clever. On his missions, he noticed everything. The people and their food, their clothes and their beliefs, their animals, their cities and their trade. When he came back, he told the Khan all about it. The Khan loved his stories, and sent him out again.

[long-pause]

So Marco travelled all over China for years. South to the mountains near Burma and Tibet. East to the great cities along the rivers and the Grand Canal.

[pause]

He writes about all these places. About the wild mountains of Tibet, where people burn green bamboo at night. It bursts with a loud bang that frightens away wild animals. About a southern region where people use seashells as money, and where there are huge crocodiles, which he calls snakes with legs.

[pause]

And about the great river Yangtze, the greatest river in the world, he says. It is so wide it is like a sea, with more ships on it than on all the rivers and seas of Europe put together.

[long-pause]

Marco also claims that he governed the great city of Yangzhou for three years. Some historians doubt it, because his name is not in the Chinese records. Perhaps he had a smaller job there. Perhaps he made it sound a little grander than it was.

[pause]

And he says that his father and uncle helped the Khan capture a city by building great stone-throwing machines. But this story is almost certainly not true, because that siege was over before the Polos even arrived.

[pause]

{img:yangzhou-canal-city-bridges-boats-merchants-gardens}
Still, much of what Marco describes is correct. It could only have been known by someone who was really there, or who listened very carefully to people who were.

[long-pause]

Meanwhile, Niccolò and Maffeo are trading and growing rich. They gather gold, silver, jewels and silk.

[pause]

The years pass. Marco grows from a young man of twenty-one into a man of thirty-eight. He has spent almost half his life in China. He knows the languages and the customs. He has friends here.

[pause]

{img:mature-marco-polo-courtyard-house-china-evening-thinking}
But the Polos are getting older, and so is the Great Khan. They think more and more of Venice, which they have not seen for so many years. And a quiet worry grows. The old Khan protects them. But what will happen to three foreigners when he dies, and someone else rules China? They ask the Khan to let them go home. And each time, he says no.

[long-pause]

{img:hangzhou-west-lake-pagodas-willows-pleasure-boats-mist}
Of all the cities Marco saw in China, the one he loved best was Hangzhou, in the south. He calls it the city of heaven.

[pause]

Until just after the Polos arrived, Hangzhou was the capital of the old Chinese emperors of the south. Then Kublai's armies conquered them. It is the largest and richest city in the world.

[long-pause]

Marco writes that it is the finest and most splendid city in the world. He says it has twelve thousand stone bridges over its canals, so high that ships can pass beneath them. His numbers may be too big. But Hangzhou really was enormous, with perhaps a million people.

[pause]

The city lies between a great river and a beautiful lake, the West Lake. Green hills surround the lake, with temples and pagodas and gardens along its shores.

[pause]

Marco writes that on the lake there are many pleasure boats, large and small, painted and decorated, with tables and chairs. People go out on them with friends and family. They eat and drink and enjoy the views of the city, the hills and the temples.

[long-pause]

He writes about the markets of Hangzhou. There are ten great market squares, where tens of thousands of people come three days a week. There is every kind of food. Ducks and geese, huge white pears, peaches and grapes. And so much fish, from the river, the lake and the sea, that you would think it could never all be sold. But within a few hours, it is all gone.

[pause]

He writes about the stone-paved streets and the canals, the houses of three or four floors, and the workshops of the craftsmen, thousands of them.

[pause]

{img:hangzhou-market-fish-fruit-baskets-stalls-crowds-canal}
He writes about the bath houses, with hot and cold water, where people bathe every day. And about the people themselves. He says they are peaceful and gentle and honest, and live together in such friendship that a whole street seems like one family.

[long-pause]

At night, Marco says, watchmen stand on every bridge. If they see a light burning after the curfew, they knock on the door and ask why. On the hills there are watchtowers with great wooden drums. Fire is the great danger in a city of wooden houses. If a fire breaks out, the drums sound the alarm, and everyone comes running.

[pause]

He also describes how, when a child is born, the parents write down the exact day and hour, so the star-readers can tell the child's future. And he says each family writes the names of everyone in the house on their door, so the officials know how many people live there.

[pause]

{img:night-hangzhou-lanterns-canal-bridge-watchman-quiet}
Hangzhou is still a beautiful city today. The West Lake is still there, with its willows and pagodas and pleasure boats, and tea gardens on the hills around it. The Chinese have an old saying: above there is heaven, and below there are Suzhou and Hangzhou.

[long-pause]

{img:chinese-junk-fleet-four-masts-harbour-quanzhou-1292-departure}
In 1292, at long last, the Polos get their chance to go home.

[pause]

The Mongol ruler of Persia has lost his favourite wife. Before she died, she asked him to marry a lady from her own tribe in Mongolia. So he sends messengers to Kublai Khan, to ask for a bride.

[long-pause]

Kublai chooses a young lady of seventeen, called Kököchin. But the road over land is blocked by wars between Mongol princes. So the messengers decide to go by sea, all the way around the south of Asia.

[pause]

They ask the Great Khan to let the Polos come with them, because they are experienced travellers. The Khan does not want them to go. But at last, he agrees.

[pause]

He gives them two golden tablets, letters for the kings of Europe and the Pope, and rich gifts. And he prepares a great fleet. There are fourteen huge Chinese ships with four masts each, carrying six hundred people and food for two years.

[long-pause]

They sail from the great port of Zaiton, in the south of China.

[pause]

The voyage is long and hard. They sail south along the coast, and then to the island of Sumatra. There they must wait five long months for the winds to change. They build a camp on the shore with a wooden fence around it, to keep themselves safe.

[pause]

{img:sumatra-coast-tropical-camp-palisade-ships-anchored-waiting}
Marco describes Sumatra, with its pepper and spices. He sees rhinoceroses, and thinks they must be unicorns. But he says they are very ugly, and nothing like the gentle unicorns of European stories.

[long-pause]

Then they sail on, past the island of Ceylon, which Marco says is the finest island of its size in the world, with rubies and sapphires. And along the coast of India, where he watches pearl divers diving deep into the sea.

[pause]

At last, after two years at sea, they reach Hormuz in the Persian Gulf, the port they had left twenty years before.

[pause]

{img:pearl-divers-boats-calm-sea-india-coast-morning}
Many of the people who set out on the voyage did not survive it. Marco says only eighteen of the six hundred were left. Perhaps that is too low. But it was a very hard journey.

[pause]

And when they reach Persia, they learn that the ruler who asked for the bride has died while they were at sea. So the young lady marries his son instead. Marco says she wept when the Polos left her, because she had grown so fond of them on the long voyage.

[long-pause]

From Persia, the Polos travel over land to the Black Sea. There they hear that Kublai Khan has died, at almost eighty. The great protector of their years in China is gone. And they are on their way home just in time.

[pause]

From the Black Sea they sail to Constantinople, and from there, home to Venice.

[pause]

They arrive in 1295. They have been away for twenty-four years. Marco is now forty-one.

[long-pause]

There is a famous story about their return, told many years later.

[pause]

When they knock on the door of their house, their relatives do not recognise them. They look like Mongols, with long beards and worn, shabby clothes. They speak with a strange accent. Nobody believes these are the Polos. Everyone thought they were dead long ago.

[pause]

{img:venetian-feast-hall-travellers-cutting-seams-jewels-spilling}
So the Polos invite all their relatives to a great feast. At the end of it, they bring out their old travelling coats, and cut open the seams with knives. And out pours a stream of jewels. Rubies and sapphires, emeralds and pearls, sewn into the linings for safety on the long road. And then everyone believes them.

[pause]

It is probably just a story. But it is a good one.

[long-pause]

{img:quanzhou-zaiton-harbour-many-junks-warehouses-temples-busy}
Before they leave China by sea, Marco describes the great port of Zaiton, which we now call Quanzhou. It lies on the southeast coast of China.

[pause]

He says it is one of the two greatest ports in the world. For every ship of pepper that sails to Egypt to bring pepper to Europe, he says, a hundred ships come to Zaiton.

[long-pause]

Quanzhou in 1292 is a city of the whole world.

[pause]

There are merchants from Persia and Arabia, from India and Korea and Japan. There are mosques for the Muslim merchants and Hindu temples for the Indian merchants. Some of their carved stones can still be seen today. There are Christian churches and Buddhist temples too, all side by side.

[pause]

{img:old-mosque-stone-gate-quanzhou-merchants-arab-chinese}
The harbour is full of ships. There are great Chinese junks with four masts and sails of bamboo matting. Their hulls are divided into separate sealed rooms. So if one part springs a leak, the ship does not sink. Europe will not use this clever idea for hundreds of years.

[long-pause]

Marco describes the big Chinese ships he will sail on. Each one has sixty cabins for the merchants, each private and comfortable. The hulls are made of double planks, nailed with iron and sealed with lime and oil.

[pause]

They are so big that they need two or three hundred sailors. And they carry huge cargoes, thousands of baskets of pepper.

[pause]

{img:junk-deck-cabins-bamboo-sails-sailors-loading-pepper-baskets}
Behind them they tow smaller boats, which are big ships in their own right. When there is no wind, these smaller boats tow the great ship along.

[long-pause]

In the spring of 1292, the fourteen great ships of the Great Khan set sail from Zaiton. On board are the Polos, the young bride, her escort and six hundred people. The northeast wind fills their sails, and they head south into the open sea.

[pause]

Behind them, the coast of China slowly sinks below the horizon, with its hills and its pagodas and its great harbour. Marco stands at the rail and watches it go. He will never see it again. And ahead of him lies a long, uncertain voyage across seas he has never sailed.

[long-pause]

{img:genoa-prison-1298-two-men-writing-cell-window-light}
Three years after he came home, Marco Polo became a prisoner.

[pause]

In 1298, Venice and Genoa, the two great rival trading cities of Italy, were at war. Marco seems to have commanded a Venetian ship. He was captured in a sea battle and taken to prison in Genoa. After surviving deserts, mountains and oceans, he ended up in a cell close to home.

[long-pause]

In prison he met another prisoner, a writer from Pisa called Rustichello. Rustichello wrote stories about King Arthur and his knights, in French.

[pause]

Marco told him the story of his travels. And Rustichello wrote it down in French, the language of storybooks all over Europe. Perhaps Marco sent home for his notes. Perhaps he told it all from memory. Perhaps Rustichello added a few things to make it more exciting.

[pause]

The book was called The Description of the World. But it soon got another name: The Million. Perhaps because Marco always talked about millions of things in China. Perhaps because it was a nickname of the Polo family. Nobody knows for sure.

[long-pause]

The book was an instant success. It was copied by hand again and again, and translated into many languages. More than a hundred and fifty handwritten copies still exist today.

[pause]

Many people did not believe it. They thought Marco was exaggerating, or making things up. Paper money? Black stones that burn? A city with twelve thousand bridges? It all sounded impossible.

[pause]

{img:readers-medieval-library-book-of-marvels-candle-wonder}
There is a story that when Marco was dying, his friends and his priest asked him to take back the things that were not true. And Marco answered: I have not told half of what I saw.

[long-pause]

After the war, Marco was set free and went home to Venice. He married a Venetian lady called Donata, and they had three daughters. He lived quietly as a merchant in the family house for the rest of his life.

[pause]

He died in January 1324, at about seventy. His will still exists in the archives of Venice. In it he leaves money to his wife and daughters and to churches. And he frees his servant Peter, a Mongol, who had come with him from Asia.

[pause]

He was buried in a church in Venice. His tomb has been lost.

[long-pause]

But his book lived on.

[pause]

For two hundred years it was Europe's main source of knowledge about Asia. Mapmakers used it to draw the first maps of China and the islands of the East. And explorers read it, and dreamed of reaching those rich lands.

[pause]

{img:columbus-reading-polo-book-margin-notes-candle-ship-model}
One of them was a sailor from Genoa called Christopher Columbus. He owned a copy of Marco Polo's book, and wrote more than seventy notes in its margins. In 1492 he sailed west across the Atlantic, hoping to reach the lands of the Great Khan. Instead, he found America.

[pause]

His copy of the book, with his own handwritten notes, still exists today, in a library in Spain.

[long-pause]

{img:venice-today-grand-canal-gondolas-palaces-evening}
Today, more than seven hundred years later, you can still follow Marco Polo's footsteps from Venice across Asia to China.

[pause]

In Venice there is a little courtyard called the Courtyard of the Million, where the Polo family house once stood. The house is gone, and a theatre stands there now. But a plaque on the wall remembers Marco Polo and his travels.

[long-pause]

Venice still stands on its islands, with its canals and bridges and palaces. The four bronze horses still stand on the front of Saint Mark's church, though these are copies, and the real ones are kept safe inside. The airport of Venice is named after him: Marco Polo Airport.

[pause]

In Tabriz, in Iran, the great covered bazaar where Marco walked is still there. It is one of the largest covered markets in the world, with its brick domes, its carpet sellers and its spice merchants, as busy as ever.

[long-pause]

In the Pamirs, the roof of the world is still wild and lonely. Shepherds still live in felt tents with their yaks and sheep. And the great Marco Polo sheep, with their curling horns, still graze on the high slopes.

[pause]

Today a great road winds over these mountains from Pakistan into China. It crosses a pass almost five thousand metres high, one of the highest paved roads in the world.

[pause]

{img:karakoram-highway-mountains-road-trucks-snow-peaks}
In Kashgar, the weekly market still brings people from all the villages around, with donkeys and sheep and melons, and bread from the clay ovens.

[long-pause]

At Dunhuang, the golden sand dunes still sing when the wind blows over them, just as they did for Marco. The little crescent-shaped lake is still there among the dunes. Visitors ride camels over the sand at sunset.

[pause]

And the painted caves of Dunhuang are still there in their cliff, after more than fifteen hundred years. In 1900, a monk found a hidden cave, sealed for nine hundred years, full of old books and paintings. Among them was the oldest dated printed book in the world, printed in the year 868.

[pause]

{img:dunhuang-crescent-lake-dunes-pavilion-camels-sunset}
On the grasslands of Inner Mongolia, the ruins of Xanadu lie quietly in the grass, with the larks singing above them. And in Beijing, Kublai Khan's green hill still rises above its lake, with a white pagoda on top.

[long-pause]

In Hangzhou, the West Lake is still one of the most beautiful places in China. People still go out on boats, walk under the willows, drink the green tea of the hills, and watch the sun set over the water, as Marco did.

[pause]

And across Asia, new roads and railways follow the old routes of the Silk Road. Trains and trucks carry goods once again from East to West, and West to East.

[pause]

The world is very different now. But the mountains and deserts, the oases and rivers are still there. And the story of the young man from Venice who went all the way to the court of the Great Khan, and came back to tell the tale, is still told all over the world.

[long-pause]

So we come to the end of the long road tonight, with the camel bells growing quiet behind us, and the evening light falling on the lagoon of Venice, where the journey began.

[pause]

The inns under the stars, the roof of the world, the singing sands, the palace of the Great Khan, and a merchant's son who saw more than he could ever tell.

[pause]

{img:camel-silhouettes-dunes-dusk-stars-appearing}
If you enjoyed travelling the Silk Road tonight, you are very welcome to subscribe to the channel, and to come along on another journey soon.

[pause]

There should be another quiet story waiting on the screen now, if you are still awake.

[pause]

Sleep well, and good night.
