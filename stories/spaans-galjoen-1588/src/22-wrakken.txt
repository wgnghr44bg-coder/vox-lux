For hundreds of years, the ships of the Spanish fleet that were lost lay quietly at the bottom of the sea. Then, in our own time, divers and archaeologists began to find them.

One of the most famous was found off the north coast of Ireland, in 1967. It was a galleass called the Girona. A galleass is a big ship that had both sails and oars. Divers from Belgium found her remains among the rocks of a little bay. They brought up many beautiful things from the sea floor.

--

They found gold coins and silver coins, gold chains and rings. They found a little gold salamander, a lizard-shaped jewel set with red stones. They found cameos, small carved pictures of Roman emperors, set in gold. And they found ordinary things too. Cooking pots and plates, buttons and buckles, and the brass instruments of the pilots.

{img:museum-case-gold-salamander-jewel-coins-armada-treasure-later}
Today, these treasures are kept in a museum in Belfast, in Northern Ireland. Visitors come from all over the world to see them. One of the most touching things is a small gold ring. It has words carved inside it, which mean: I have nothing more to give you. Someone gave this ring to someone they loved, long ago. Nobody knows who they were. But the ring has survived for more than four hundred years.

--

Another ship, the Trinidad Valencera, was found in a bay in Donegal, in the north of Ireland. Divers found her big bronze guns lying on the sea floor, with the king's coat of arms on them. They also found many everyday things. There were wooden bowls and plates, leather shoes, and even part of a tent. There was a wooden comb, a bag of olives and some little pottery jars. These ordinary things tell us a great deal about daily life on board.

{img:archaeologists-cleaning-wooden-bowl-shoe-from-wreck-lab-table-later}
Some of the finds are very small. A wooden spoon, with teeth marks on the handle. A little whistle made of bone. A thimble, used for sewing sails or clothes. A rosary, with beads made of wood. Each one belonged to a real person, a sailor or a soldier or a ship's boy. Holding them, archaeologists say, you feel very close to the people of 1588.

--

In Spain, too, people remember the voyage. There are museums of the sea in many port towns. You can see models of galleons, old charts and instruments. You can see astrolabes and compasses and sand-glasses, just like the ones on the Santa Catalina. You can see the clothes that sailors wore, and the tools of the carpenters.

{img:spanish-maritime-museum-galleon-model-astrolabe-display-visitors-today}
In the town of Santander, where many of the ships came home, there is a lovely park by the sea. People walk there in the evenings, looking out over the bay. The fishing boats come and go, just as they did long ago. Children play on the beach. And sometimes, an old man tells his grandchildren about the great ships that came limping home in the autumn of 1588. The story has been told for more than four hundred years. And it is still being told today.

Divers who explore the old wrecks say it is a quiet and peaceful place under the sea. The light comes down through the water in soft green rays. Seaweed sways gently in the current. Fish swim in and out of the old timbers. Crabs live inside the cannons. And the sea floor is covered in fine sand, hiding the old things that lie beneath. The divers move slowly and carefully. They touch nothing until it has been drawn and photographed. And they treat the wrecks with great respect.
