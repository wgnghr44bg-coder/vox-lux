Where do two hundred people sleep on one wooden ship? The answer is: wherever they can find a space.

On a Spanish galleon in 1588, there are no hammocks for the ordinary sailors and soldiers. Hammocks were known in the Americas, but they are not yet common on Spanish ships. Instead, the men sleep on the deck, or on the floor between the guns. They roll themselves in a blanket or a cloak and lie down on the wooden planks. When the weather is warm, many of them prefer to sleep up on the open deck, under the stars.

--

Each man has a small space that he thinks of as his own. Maybe it is a corner beside a gun, or a spot near the mast. He keeps his few things there in a small wooden chest or a cloth bag. There is a spare shirt, a woollen cap, a knife, and perhaps a wooden spoon and a little picture of a saint. That is all. There is no room for anything more.

{img:sailor-small-wooden-chest-belongings-knife-spoon-saint-picture-lantern}
Diego sleeps on the deck under the forecastle, near Tomás. On calm nights, it is pleasant there. The ship rocks gently, and the waves make a soft swishing sound against the sides. The ropes creak, and the wind hums in the rigging. Diego lies on his back and looks up at the stars between the sails. He thinks about home, and he falls asleep.

--

But on rough nights, sleeping is harder. The ship rolls and pitches, and the men roll around on the deck. Water splashes over the side and soaks their blankets. It is cold and wet and uncomfortable. On those nights, the men squeeze together below deck, between the guns, to stay dry. It is crowded and stuffy down there, and it smells of tar and damp wool. But at least it is out of the wind.

{img:crowded-gun-deck-night-men-sleeping-between-cannons-dim-lantern}
The officers have their own small cabins in the sterncastle. They sleep in narrow wooden beds, built against the walls. Some of them have proper mattresses and pillows. The captain's cabin is the best on the ship. It has a big window looking out over the sea behind. But even the captain's cabin is small, and it moves with every wave.

--

On a long voyage, there is not much privacy. Two hundred men live, eat and sleep together in a very small space. They must learn to get along. Most of the time, they do. They share their food and their stories. They help each other with their work. They look after each other when they are tired or sad.

{img:old-sailor-telling-story-ships-boys-lantern-below-deck-night}
Sometimes, before sleeping, Tomás tells Diego and Andrés a story. He tells them about the islands of the Caribbean, with their white beaches and green palm trees. He tells them about whales as big as ships, and fish that fly over the water. He tells them about the great storm off the tip of South America, when he thought the ship would never come home. But it did, he says. And here I am, still sailing, still telling stories. The boys listen in the lantern light until their eyes grow heavy. And then they sleep, side by side, as the ship sails on through the night.

Keeping clean on the ship is not easy. There is no fresh water to spare for washing. So the men wash themselves and their clothes in seawater, in wooden buckets. The salt makes their skin dry and itchy. Their clothes become stiff and rough. When it rains, everyone rushes out onto the deck. They hold out buckets and pots to catch the fresh rainwater. Some of them stand in the rain with their faces turned up to the sky, laughing. Fresh water is a great treasure on a ship.

Sometimes the men talk quietly in the dark before they sleep. They talk about their homes and their families. One misses his wife's cooking. Another misses his little daughter. Another misses the olive trees on his father's farm. They talk about what they will do when they get home. One will buy a boat, and another will open a shop. Another will simply sleep in a real bed for a whole week. And slowly, one by one, the voices fall silent.
