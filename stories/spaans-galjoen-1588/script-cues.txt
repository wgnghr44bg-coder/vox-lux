Tonight we travel more than four hundred years back in time. We go to the great harbour of Lisbon, at the mouth of a wide river. The year is 1588. And in the harbour, more than a hundred big ships are lying at anchor, waiting to sail.

[long-pause]

It is early morning in May, and the water is calm and grey. A light mist hangs over the river. The tall masts of the ships rise up through the mist like a forest of bare trees. Their sails are rolled up tight on the long wooden yards. Flags hang still in the windless air. Gulls cry and circle over the water, looking for scraps.

[pause]

Small rowing boats move between the ships, carrying people and barrels and sacks. A bell rings on one of the ships, and then on another. Somewhere a man is singing a slow song as he works. On the shore, the city of Lisbon is waking up, with its white houses climbing the hills. Church bells ring out across the water. It is quiet and peaceful, and full of quiet waiting.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:galleon-santa-catalina-high-stern-castle-painted-carvings-morning-light}
One of these ships is called the Santa Catalina. She is a galleon, a big Spanish sailing ship with three tall masts. Her back end rises high out of the water, like a little wooden castle. It is painted red and gold and carved with saints and lions. Her sides are dark brown wood, coated with tar to keep out the water. She is about forty metres long. And more than two hundred people will live on board her for months.

[pause]

Tonight we will sail with the Santa Catalina. We will meet a ship's boy of twelve, who is going to sea for the very first time. We will meet the old sailors and the soldiers, the cook and the carpenter and the priest. We will learn how they ate, and slept, and told the time, and found their way across the sea. We will hear their songs in the evening and their prayers at dusk.

[pause]

{img:ships-boy-twelve-leaning-rail-galleon-looking-at-lisbon-morning}
This galleon is part of a very large fleet, which the Spanish call the Most Fortunate Fleet. In English, it will later be called the Spanish Armada. It is going to sail north, to England. And later, it will face long storms and cold seas, far from home. There will be moments when much is at stake. But tonight, we will mostly stay with the ordinary people on board, and with their everyday life at sea.

[long-pause]

So let us go aboard the Santa Catalina, on a spring morning in Lisbon. The deck is wet with dew, and the ropes creak softly as the ship moves on the tide. Sailors are already up, coiling ropes and scrubbing the deck with buckets of seawater. The smell of tar and salt and wood smoke is everywhere.

[pause]

{img:galleon-deck-morning-sailors-scrubbing-coiling-ropes-mist-lisbon}
At the front of the ship, a thin line of smoke rises from the cooking fire. The cook is boiling a big pot of something for breakfast. A cat walks along the rail, looking for mice. And a boy with dark curly hair stands near the mainmast, looking up at the great masts and ropes with wide eyes. His name is Diego. And this is his first morning at sea.

[pause]

Diego has never been on such a big ship before. His father's fishing boat at home is small and open, with one little sail. This ship is like a whole village made of wood. There are people everywhere, climbing and pulling and carrying. There are ladders going down into the darkness, and ropes going up into the sky. Diego stands still and tries to take it all in. A sailor hurrying past bumps into him and laughs. Out of the way, little one, he says kindly. And Diego steps aside, smiling shyly, and keeps on looking.

[long-pause]

{img:king-philip-ii-dark-clothes-desk-papers-escorial-palace-lamp}
In the year 1588, Spain is the most powerful country in Europe. Its king is called Philip the Second. He is about sixty years old, a quiet and serious man who always dresses in black. He works very hard, sitting at his desk for hours every day. He reads thousands of letters and writes notes in the margins. And he rules over a very large empire.

[pause]

Philip rules Spain and Portugal, and parts of Italy and the Netherlands. He also rules great lands across the ocean, in the Americas and in Asia. Every year, ships bring silver from the mines of the Americas back to Spain. People say that the sun never sets on Philip's empire. When it is night in Spain, it is day somewhere else that he rules.

[long-pause]

But Philip has a problem with England. England is ruled by Queen Elizabeth the First. For many years, the two countries have not been friends. They follow different kinds of Christian faith. English ships have been attacking Spanish treasure ships at sea. And England has been helping people in the Netherlands who want to be free of Spanish rule.

[pause]

{img:escorial-palace-spain-grey-stone-mountains-evening-quiet}
So King Philip decides to send a great fleet to England. He plans it for several years. He gathers ships from all over his empire, from Spain and Portugal, from Italy and beyond. He gathers soldiers and sailors, food and water, guns and gunpowder. It is the biggest fleet that anyone in Europe has ever seen.

[long-pause]

The plan is to sail north, up the English Channel, the narrow sea between England and France. There, the fleet will meet a Spanish army waiting in the Netherlands. Then they will cross to England together. It is a complicated plan. It needs good weather and good luck. And many people in Spain are hoping and praying that it will work.

[pause]

{img:spanish-town-church-procession-candles-praying-for-fleet-1588}
All over Spain, people pray for the fleet. In the towns and villages, there are processions with candles through the streets. Priests say special prayers in the churches. Families gather to pray for their sons and brothers and fathers on the ships. Everyone has heard about the great fleet, and everyone has an opinion.

[long-pause]

The commander of the fleet is a nobleman called the Duke of Medina Sidonia. He is a kind and careful man, not a sailor. He did not even want the job at first. He wrote to the king that he got seasick easily, and that he knew little about the sea. But the king insisted. So the duke agreed, and he did his best. He worked very hard to make sure the ships were ready and the men were fed.

[pause]

{img:duke-medina-sidonia-ship-cabin-maps-candle-worried-kind-face}
Life in Spain in 1588 is very different for rich and poor. The nobles live in fine houses with gardens and fountains. They wear silk and velvet, and they ride fine horses. But most people are farmers and fishermen, craftsmen and labourers. They live in small houses of stone and work long hours. Many young men go to sea, or join the army, because there is little work at home.

[pause]

Diego is one of them. His family lives in a small fishing village on the north coast of Spain. His father is a fisherman, and so was his grandfather. There are six children, and not always enough food. So when a ship's officer came to the village, looking for boys, Diego's mother let him go. The pay was small, but it would help the family. And Diego was excited to see the world.

[pause]

In the towns of Spain, the fleet is the talk of every market and every inn. People argue about whether the plan will work. Old soldiers tell stories about other wars and other voyages. Mothers worry about their sons, and wives worry about their husbands. Children play at ships in the streets, with sticks for masts and old cloth for sails. And in the evenings, families gather by their fires and say a prayer for the men at sea. Everyone in Spain, it seems, has someone on the ships.

[long-pause]

{img:galleon-cross-section-decks-hold-barrels-cannons-cabins-illustration-style}
Let us take a closer look at the Santa Catalina, from top to bottom.

[pause]

A galleon is a big, strong ship, built for long voyages across the ocean. She has three masts. The tallest one is in the middle, and it is called the mainmast. The front one is called the foremast. And the one at the back is called the mizzenmast. There is also a long pole sticking out from the very front of the ship, called the bowsprit. On these masts hang the great square sails, and a few triangular ones too.

[long-pause]

At the back of the ship, the stern rises high above the water. This tall part is called the sterncastle. Inside are the cabins of the captain and the officers. They have small windows of glass, looking out over the sea behind. There are wooden walls with carvings, and lanterns hanging from the ceilings. These are the best rooms on the ship. But even they are small and low, and they move with every wave.

[pause]

{img:galleon-captains-cabin-stern-windows-lantern-charts-table-evening}
At the front of the ship, there is a smaller raised part, called the forecastle. That word is still used today. Sailors say it as fo'c'sle. Under the forecastle there is space for stores and ropes. And in the middle of the ship, between the two castles, is the main deck. This is where most of the work is done.

[long-pause]

Below the main deck there are more decks, one under the other. On the gun deck, the heavy guns stand in rows, pointing out of square openings in the sides. When the ship is not in battle, the soldiers and sailors sleep here, between the guns. It is low and dark, and you have to bend your head to walk.

[pause]

At the very bottom of the ship is the hold. It is a deep, dark space, full of barrels and sacks and crates. There are barrels of water and wine, sacks of biscuits, beans and rice. There are casks of salted fish and meat, oil and vinegar. There are spare sails and ropes, gunpowder and cannon balls. Down in the hold, the air smells of tar and damp wood and old bilge water.

[pause]

{img:galleon-hold-dim-lantern-barrels-sacks-ship-rats-shadows}
The ship is made almost entirely of wood. Her frame is made of strong oak from the forests of northern Spain. Her planks are oak and pine. Her masts are tall straight pines from the mountains. It took hundreds of trees to build her. All the gaps between the planks are filled with old rope and covered with hot tar. That keeps the water out, most of the time. But every ship leaks a little, and every day sailors must pump water out of the bottom.

[long-pause]

On the main deck there is a big wheel of wood with long bars, like the spokes of a wheel. This is the capstan. When the anchor must be lifted, many sailors push on the bars and walk round and round. Slowly, the thick anchor rope winds up around the capstan. And the heavy iron anchor comes up from the sea floor. The sailors sing while they push, to keep in step.

[pause]

{img:sailors-pushing-capstan-bars-raising-anchor-singing-deck-sunlight}
Diego explores the ship on his first day, with wide eyes. He climbs down the steep ladders into the dark decks below. He looks at the guns, the barrels and the hanging lanterns. He gets lost twice and has to ask the way. He bumps his head on a beam and rubs it, and an old sailor laughs. You will learn, the old sailor says kindly. Everyone bumps his head on the first day. By the evening, Diego can find his way from the hold to the deck without help. And he feels very proud of himself.

[pause]

There are animals on board too. There is the ship's cat, a thin grey cat who hunts the mice and rats in the hold. There are chickens in wooden cages for the officers' eggs. There are a few goats, tied up near the front of the ship, for fresh milk. And there are horses and mules, standing in narrow stalls below deck. They are meant for the army in the Netherlands. Diego likes to visit them in the evenings. He strokes their noses and talks to them softly, and they seem to like it.

[pause]

At the very back of the ship, high up on the sterncastle, there is a great lantern. It is made of iron and glass, and it is as big as a barrel. At night, a candle burns inside it. The other ships in the fleet can follow its light in the dark. The Santa Catalina's lantern is one of many. On a calm night, the whole sea is dotted with little lights, moving slowly together across the water, like a town of floating stars.

[long-pause]

{img:spanish-fishing-village-north-coast-1588-stone-houses-boats-beach}
Let us go back a few weeks, to Diego's home, in a small fishing village on the north coast of Spain.

[pause]

The village lies in a little bay, between green hills and the grey Atlantic sea. There are about thirty houses of grey stone, with roofs of red tiles. Fishing boats are pulled up on the beach, with nets spread out to dry. There is a small stone church with a bell tower, and a little square with a well. The air always smells of salt and seaweed and wood smoke.

[long-pause]

Diego's family lives in a small house near the beach. There is one main room with a fireplace, and a little room at the back for sleeping. His father goes out fishing every morning before dawn, with his two eldest sons. His mother mends the nets and looks after the younger children. She also keeps a few chickens and a small garden with cabbages and beans.

[pause]

{img:fisherman-family-small-stone-house-fireplace-mother-mending-net-children}
Diego is the third son. He has helped his father on the boat since he was eight. He can row and steer and pull in the nets. He knows the names of the winds and the moods of the sea. He can tell from the clouds when a storm is coming. But the family's boat is small, and there is not always enough fish. So when he was twelve, his parents decided he should go to sea on a big ship.

[long-pause]

One spring day, a ship's officer came to the village. He was looking for boys to work on the galleons of the king. Boys of twelve or thirteen, strong and healthy, who knew the sea. Ship's boys, they were called. In Spanish, a grumete. They would be paid a small wage, and they would learn to be sailors. Diego's mother talked with his father for a long time that night. In the morning, she told Diego he could go.

[pause]

{img:mother-saying-goodbye-to-son-village-path-dawn-small-bundle}
On the morning he left, the whole family came to the end of the village to say goodbye. His mother gave him a small bundle with a clean shirt, a woollen cap and a piece of bread. She also gave him a little wooden cross on a string, to wear around his neck. His father put a hand on his shoulder and told him to listen to the old sailors. His little sister cried and held onto his leg. Diego tried hard not to cry too. Then he turned and followed the officer down the road.

[long-pause]

A ship's boy has many jobs. He scrubs the decks with buckets of seawater and sand. He coils the ropes and helps the sailors pull them. He fetches water and carries food from the cook. He climbs up the rigging to help with the sails, when he is older and braver. And he has a very important job with the sand-glass, which we will learn about later.

[pause]

The youngest boys of all are called pages. They are only eight or ten years old. They serve the officers, carry messages, and sing the prayers at certain times of the day. There are a few pages on the Santa Catalina, younger than Diego. He feels quite grown-up beside them.

[pause]

{img:ships-boys-scrubbing-deck-buckets-sand-laughing-galleon-sunlight}
On his first days aboard, Diego makes friends with another ship's boy called Andrés. Andrés is thirteen, and he has already sailed once to the Americas. He shows Diego how to tie the knots and where to sleep. He tells him which officers are kind and which ones are strict. He shows him the best place on deck to catch the breeze on a hot afternoon. Diego is very glad to have a friend.

[pause]

In the evening, the two boys sit together near the bow of the ship. They look out at the lights of Lisbon twinkling on the hills. Andrés tells stories about the Americas, about parrots and palm trees and hot sunshine. Diego listens with his mouth open. He holds the little wooden cross from his mother in his hand. And he wonders what the long voyage ahead will bring.

[pause]

Andrés also teaches Diego the names of everything on the ship. In Spanish, every rope and every sail has its own special word. The sailors use them all the time, and Diego must learn them quickly. At first, his head is spinning with new words. But Andrés makes it into a game. He points at something and Diego must say its name. If Diego gets it right, Andrés gives him a little piece of dried fig. By the end of the week, Diego knows fifty new words. And he has eaten a great many figs.

[long-pause]

{img:galleon-crew-gathered-deck-sailors-soldiers-officers-1588-morning}
More than two hundred people live on the Santa Catalina. They are like a small floating village, all squeezed together on one wooden ship.

[pause]

There are two main groups of men on board. The first group are the sailors. They sail the ship, work the sails and the ropes, and keep everything in order. There are about seventy of them. The second group are the soldiers. They are there to fight, if there is any fighting. There are about a hundred and twenty of them. The sailors and the soldiers do not always get along. The sailors think the soldiers are clumsy on a ship. The soldiers think the sailors are rough and rude.

[long-pause]

The captain of the ship is a nobleman called Don Alonso. He is about forty, with a neat dark beard and a calm voice. He is in charge of the soldiers and of the whole ship. But he is not really a sailor. So he relies on the master and the pilot to sail the ship for him. The master is in charge of the sailors and the cargo. The pilot is the expert in finding the way across the sea.

[pause]

{img:ship-master-pilot-officers-quarterdeck-talking-compass-sunlight}
Then there are the people with special skills. There is the boatswain, who looks after the sails and the ropes and the anchors. He carries a silver whistle, and he blows it to give orders. There is the carpenter, who repairs anything made of wood, which is almost everything. There is the caulker, who fills the cracks between the planks with rope and tar. And there are the gunners, who look after the big guns.

[long-pause]

There is a barber-surgeon too. He cuts the men's hair and shaves their beards. He also looks after anyone who is ill or hurt. He has a wooden chest full of medicines, bandages and tools. He makes ointments from herbs and oils. He is a cheerful man with a round face, and the men like him.

[pause]

{img:ship-barber-surgeon-chest-medicines-shaving-sailor-on-deck}
There is a priest on board, a friar called Fray Juan. He wears a brown robe with a rope belt. He says mass on Sundays and holy days. He hears the men's worries and comforts them. He blesses the ship and prays for good winds. Fray Juan is old and gentle, with white hair around a bald head. He likes to sit with the ship's boys in the evenings and teach them their letters.

[long-pause]

And there is the cook, a big man called Pedro. He works at the cooking fire at the front of the ship. The fire burns in a big box lined with bricks and sand, so it cannot set the ship alight. Pedro is always grumbling, always busy, and always covered in smoke. But he has a kind heart. He gives the ship's boys the burnt bits from the bottom of the pot, which they love.

[pause]

{img:ships-cook-big-man-firebox-bricks-iron-pot-steam-forecastle}
One of the oldest sailors on board is a man called Tomás. He comes from the Basque country, in the north of Spain, where many of the best sailors come from. He is nearly sixty, with a grey beard and a face brown and lined like old leather. He has sailed all over the world. He has been to the Americas, to Africa, and around the stormy tip of South America. He has more stories than anyone on the ship.

[pause]

On his second day aboard, Diego is trying to tie a knot and getting it wrong. Tomás comes over and watches him for a moment. Then he takes the rope and shows him, slowly. Over, under, and through, he says. Diego tries again, and this time it works. Tomás nods and pats him on the back. Good, he says. You will make a sailor yet. And from that day on, Diego follows old Tomás everywhere.

[pause]

There are a few passengers on board too, people who are neither sailors nor soldiers. There is a young nobleman, who has joined the fleet for honour and adventure. He has brought his own servant and a chest full of fine clothes. There is an old doctor, who looks after the officers. And there is a merchant, who is supposed to buy supplies for the army. They all live in the small cabins under the sterncastle. They eat at the captain's table and talk about books and politics. The sailors think they are rather funny.

[pause]

In the evenings, the different people on the ship often mix together. The soldiers learn sailors' songs, and the sailors learn soldiers' jokes. Men from Andalusia, in the south, sit with men from Galicia, in the north. They speak with different accents and tease each other about it. There are also men from Portugal and Italy, and a few from far-off lands. By the end of the voyage, many of them have become good friends.

[long-pause]

{img:lisbon-quay-1588-loading-barrels-sacks-rowing-boats-galleons-busy}
Before the fleet can sail, the ships must be filled with everything that two hundred people will need for months at sea. In the harbour of Lisbon, this work goes on for weeks.

[pause]

Every day, small boats row out to the ships, loaded with supplies. There are barrels of water and wine, sacks of biscuits and beans, casks of olive oil and vinegar. There are crates of salted fish and barrels of salted meat. There are cheeses wrapped in cloth, strings of onions and garlic, and bags of rice. Everything must be lifted up the side of the ship with ropes and carried down into the hold.

[long-pause]

The most important food on the ship is a kind of hard bread, called ship's biscuit. It is made from flour and water only, with no salt or yeast. It is baked twice, until it is as hard as wood. That way it can last for many months without going bad. In the bakeries of Lisbon and other towns, bakers make millions of these biscuits for the fleet.

[pause]

{img:bakers-ovens-making-ships-biscuit-flat-hard-bread-stacks-bakery}
Water is very important too. Each man needs about a litre and a half of water a day, for drinking and cooking. That is a lot of water for two hundred men for months. So the hold is full of big wooden barrels of water. The barrels are made by coopers, craftsmen who bend thin strips of wood and hold them together with metal hoops. It is skilful work, because the barrels must not leak.

[long-pause]

The guns must be loaded too. Heavy iron and bronze guns are lifted onto the ship with big cranes and ropes. They are rolled into place on their wooden carriages and tied down. Cannon balls of iron and stone are stacked in piles. Barrels of gunpowder are carried very carefully down into a special room, far from any fire. Nobody may carry a light into that room.

[pause]

{img:gunners-lifting-bronze-cannon-crane-ropes-galleon-side-harbour}
While the loading goes on, Diego and Andrés work very hard. They carry sacks and roll barrels and pull on ropes all day. At the end of each day, their arms ache and their hands are sore. But in the evenings, they have a little free time. Sometimes they are allowed to go ashore for a few hours.

[long-pause]

Lisbon is a big and busy city, much bigger than anything Diego has ever seen. Its streets climb steeply up the hills from the river. There are churches with tall bell towers, and fine houses with gardens. There are markets full of things from all over the world. There are spices from India and silk from China, sugar from Brazil and parrots from Africa. People speak many different languages in the streets.

[pause]

{img:lisbon-market-1588-spices-silk-parrots-crowd-hilly-street-evening}
Andrés takes Diego to a little market near the harbour. They look at the strange fruits and the colourful birds. A woman selling oranges gives them one each. Diego has never eaten an orange before. He peels it slowly and eats it piece by piece. It is sweet and juicy and wonderful. He saves the peel and puts it in his pocket, because it smells so good.

[pause]

As the sun goes down, the boys walk back to the harbour. The bells of the churches are ringing for evening prayers. The galleons are dark shapes on the shining water, with little lanterns lit on their decks. The boys climb into a rowing boat and are rowed back to the Santa Catalina. Diego sleeps that night with the orange peel beside him, and dreams of faraway lands.

[pause]

On the last evening before the fleet sails, there is a great service in the cathedral of Lisbon. The duke and all the officers go there to pray. The fleet's great banner is blessed by the archbishop. It is a big flag of red and gold, with a picture of Christ and of Mary. Outside, thousands of people fill the streets and the squares. They hold candles and sing hymns. On the ships in the harbour, the men can hear the singing drifting across the water. And many of them sing along.

[long-pause]

{img:armada-fleet-leaving-lisbon-river-tagus-sails-filling-may-1588}
At last, at the end of May, the fleet is ready to sail. On the morning of departure, all the ships raise their anchors and unfurl their sails. One by one, they move slowly down the river toward the open sea.

[pause]

It is an amazing sight. More than a hundred and thirty ships are moving together, with their sails filling in the wind. There are great galleons and big merchant ships, smaller supply ships and fast little boats. Flags and banners fly from every mast. Drums beat and trumpets sound. People crowd on the shore and on the hills to watch them go.

[long-pause]

On the Santa Catalina, everyone is busy. The sailors haul on the ropes to raise the sails. The boatswain blows his whistle again and again. The great sails drop down from the yards and fill with wind with a loud flapping sound. The ship leans a little to one side and begins to move. Diego feels the deck tilt under his feet. He grabs a rope to steady himself. Tomás laughs and tells him to bend his knees.

[pause]

{img:sailors-hauling-ropes-sails-unfurling-boatswain-whistle-galleon-deck}
Fray Juan stands at the back of the ship and blesses it with holy water. All the men take off their caps and make the sign of the cross. They pray for good winds and a safe voyage. Then they get back to work. The ship sails past the old stone tower at the mouth of the river. And then they are out on the open ocean, with the land slowly getting smaller behind them.

[long-pause]

The fleet sails slowly, because some of the ships are slow and heavy. The faster ships must wait for them. So the fleet can only move as fast as its slowest ship. On some days, they travel only a short distance. The wind blows from the wrong direction, from the north, right in their faces. The ships must zigzag back and forth to make any progress at all.

[pause]

{img:fleet-at-sea-many-ships-spread-out-grey-ocean-slow-progress}
There are other problems too. After a few weeks, some of the food goes bad. The biscuits were not baked well enough, and they turn mouldy. Some of the water barrels were made of green wood, and the water tastes bad. The Duke of Medina Sidonia is worried. He writes letters to the king about it. And then a great storm comes.

[long-pause]

The storm comes in the middle of June, off the north coast of Spain. The wind howls through the ropes, and great grey waves roll in from the ocean. The ships are scattered in all directions. On the Santa Catalina, the sailors work hard to take in the sails. The ship rolls and pitches in the waves. Water splashes over the deck. Diego holds on tight to a rope, wet and cold, his heart beating fast. Tomás is beside him, calm as always.

[pause]

{img:galleon-in-storm-grey-waves-sails-reefed-sailors-holding-ropes-rain}
The storm lasts two days. Then, slowly, the wind drops and the sea calms down. The ships are scattered far and wide, and some have been damaged. The duke decides that the whole fleet must go into the harbour of A Coruña, in the north-west of Spain, to repair the ships. The Santa Catalina is one of the first to arrive. She sails slowly into the calm harbour, with torn sails and a cracked yard.

[pause]

That evening, the men gather on deck and give thanks to God for bringing them safely through the storm. Fray Juan leads the prayers. Then Pedro the cook makes a hot stew of beans and salted pork. It is the best meal Diego has ever eaten, or so it seems to him. He eats it sitting next to Tomás, wrapped in a dry blanket. And then he falls asleep right there on the deck, with his empty bowl in his lap.

[pause]

In the first days at sea, many of the young men are seasick. They lean over the rail, green and miserable, and wish they had never left home. Diego is lucky. Because he grew up on his father's fishing boat, he is used to the movement of the sea. He feels fine. So he helps the sick men instead, bringing them water and a little dry biscuit. Tomás watches him and nods. You are a real sailor's son, he says. The sea is in your blood. Diego feels very proud.

[long-pause]

{img:a-coruna-harbour-1588-galleons-repairing-hilltop-town-lighthouse}
The fleet stays in the harbour of A Coruña for about a month. It is a time of hard work, but also of rest.

[pause]

A Coruña is a busy port town on a rocky point of land. There is an old Roman lighthouse on the hill above it, called the Tower of Hercules. It has stood there for more than a thousand years. Its light has guided ships safely into the harbour for centuries. Diego looks up at it every morning when he wakes. It makes him feel safe.

[long-pause]

The carpenters and the caulkers work from dawn to dusk. They repair the broken masts and yards. They fix the planks that were damaged in the storm. They fill the cracks with new rope and hot tar. The sail-makers sit on the deck with big needles, sewing new patches onto the torn sails. The sound of hammers and saws fills the harbour all day long.

[pause]

{img:ship-carpenters-repairing-mast-yard-tools-sawdust-deck-sunshine}
New food is brought on board, fresh and good this time. There are new biscuits, new barrels of water, and fresh meat and vegetables. The bad food is thrown away. The men are happy to eat fresh bread and fruit again. Some of the sick men are taken ashore to rest and get better. And some new men come on board, to take the places of those who are too ill to sail.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, when the work is done, the men have some free time. Some of them go ashore to the town. Others stay on board and rest. They play cards or dice on the deck. They mend their clothes and wash them in buckets. They write letters home, or ask Fray Juan to write them, if they cannot write themselves.

[pause]

{img:friar-writing-letter-for-sailor-deck-evening-quill-paper-lantern}
Diego cannot write. So one evening, he sits down beside Fray Juan and asks him for help. Fray Juan smiles and takes out his paper and quill. Diego tells him what to write, slowly. Dear mother and father, he says. I am well. The ship is big and the food is good, mostly. I have a friend called Andrés. An old sailor called Tomás is teaching me knots. There was a storm, but I was not afraid, not very much. Please give my love to everyone. Your son, Diego.

[pause]

Fray Juan writes it all down carefully. Then he shows Diego how to write his own name at the bottom. Diego holds the quill and makes the letters slowly, one by one. D, I, E, G, O. It is a bit wobbly, but it is his name. He looks at it for a long time, very pleased. Fray Juan says he will teach him more letters on the voyage. And Diego promises to practise every evening.

[long-pause]

On Sundays, everyone goes to mass. On the Santa Catalina, Fray Juan sets up a little altar on the deck. The men kneel on the wooden planks, with their caps in their hands. The gentle sound of the waves mixes with the prayers. Afterwards, the men have a better meal than usual, with a little extra wine.

[pause]

{img:sunday-mass-galleon-deck-friar-altar-sailors-kneeling-harbour}
Tomás takes Diego up to the lighthouse one evening, when they are allowed ashore. They climb the hill together slowly, because Tomás's knees are old. At the top, they sit on a rock and look out over the sea. The sun is going down, and the water is red and gold. The ships of the fleet lie quietly in the harbour below. Tomás tells Diego about his first voyage, long ago, when he was twelve too. I was frightened, he says. Everyone is, at the start. But the sea becomes your friend, in time. Diego listens and nods. And they walk back down to the harbour in the dusk, as the lighthouse begins to glow above them.

[pause]

While they are in A Coruña, the men are paid some of their wages. Diego gets a few small silver coins. He has never had so much money in his life. He goes to the market in the town with Andrés. He buys a little knife with a wooden handle, because every sailor needs a knife. And he buys a small bag of dried figs to share with his friends. The rest of the money he keeps safely in a little cloth bag around his neck. He wants to bring it home to his mother.

[long-pause]

{img:galleon-at-sea-calm-morning-sun-rising-crew-at-work-wide-ocean}
In the middle of July, the fleet leaves A Coruña and sails north again. Now the weather is better, and the voyage goes more smoothly. Let us spend an ordinary day on the Santa Catalina, at sea.

[pause]

On a ship, the day is divided into watches. A watch is a period of four hours. The crew is split into two groups, and they take turns. One group works while the other group rests. So there are always men awake and working on the ship, by day and by night. Diego belongs to the starboard watch, with Tomás and Andrés.

[long-pause]

How do they know when four hours have passed? They use a sand-glass. It is a glass with two round parts and a narrow neck in the middle. It is filled with sand. When you turn it over, the sand runs from the top into the bottom. It takes exactly half an hour for all the sand to run through. When it is empty, a ship's boy turns it over again.

[pause]

{img:ships-boy-turning-sand-glass-binnacle-compass-lantern-night}
Turning the sand-glass is one of Diego's jobs. Every time he turns it, he must sing a little verse. Blessed be the hour our Lord was born, he sings. Holy Mary who bore him, and Saint John who baptised him. Then he calls out the number of glasses. One glass is gone, and now the second runs. More shall run, if God wills. The whole crew can hear him, and they know the time. After eight glasses, the watch is over.

[long-pause]

The day starts at dawn. A ship's boy sings a morning prayer to wake everyone up. Blessed be the light of day, he sings, and the holy cross. Then the sailors on watch scrub the deck with buckets of seawater. They pump the water out of the bottom of the ship. They check the ropes and the sails. The soldiers come up from below and stretch in the morning air.

[pause]

{img:sailors-pumping-bilge-water-wooden-pump-deck-morning-teamwork}
Breakfast is ship's biscuit, perhaps with a little cheese, and a ration of wine mixed with water. The main meal of the day is at noon. In the afternoon, the men work, or rest, or practise with their weapons. The soldiers practise firing their guns at targets thrown into the sea. The gunners clean the big guns. The sailors mend sails and splice ropes.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, Diego and Andrés have their free time. They sit in the shade of a sail and play a game with little stones. Or they climb a little way up the rigging, to look out over the sea. From up there, they can see all the ships of the fleet, spread out across the water. It is like a great floating city. Andrés counts the ships one day. He gets to a hundred and twenty, and then he loses count.

[pause]

{img:two-ships-boys-sitting-rigging-looking-fleet-afternoon-sun-sea}
In the evening, the whole crew gathers on deck for the evening prayer. The pages sing the Salve Regina, an old hymn to Mary. Their high voices rise up into the evening air. All the men take off their caps and join in. Then the night watch begins. The lanterns are lit, and the men who are off duty find a place to sleep. And the Santa Catalina sails on through the dark, under the stars.

[pause]

Some days at sea are very quiet. There is little wind, and the ship hardly moves. The sails hang loose from the yards, and the sea is as flat as a mirror. On these days, the men have little to do. They sit in the shade and talk, or doze, or fish over the side. The heat is heavy, and everyone moves slowly. The sailors call these days the calms. Some of the men grumble that they will never get anywhere. But Diego rather likes them. The sea is so blue and so still, and the sky is so wide.

[pause]

At night, the ship is quiet and dark. Only a few lanterns are lit, at the stern and near the compass. The men on watch stand by the rail, looking out into the darkness. They listen to the wind and the waves. Every half hour, the boy turns the sand-glass and sings the verse. A lookout at the bow calls out that all is well. Then the next one answers him, from the back of the ship. And the night goes slowly on.

[long-pause]

{img:galleon-noon-meal-sailors-sitting-deck-wooden-bowls-biscuit-sun}
Food is one of the most important things on a long voyage. It is also one of the things the sailors talk about most. Let us see what they eat on the Santa Catalina.

[pause]

Every man gets a ration of food each day. The ration is written down by the king's officials, so everyone gets the same. The most important part is the ship's biscuit, about seven hundred grams a day. It is so hard that the men must soak it in water or wine before they can eat it. Some of the old sailors have very few teeth left. They break their biscuit into little pieces and suck them slowly.

[long-pause]

Each man also gets a ration of wine, about a litre and a half a day. The wine is mixed with water. On a ship, wine is safer to drink than old water, which can go bad in the barrels. There is also a little olive oil and vinegar. Olive oil is poured over the biscuit and the beans, to make them tastier. Vinegar is used to clean things and to keep food fresh.

[pause]

{img:ships-cook-ladling-stew-into-bowls-queue-sailors-forecastle-smoke}
Different days have different food. On some days, there is salted meat, usually pork or beef. On other days, especially Fridays, there is salted fish, often cod or tuna. Catholics do not eat meat on Fridays. On many days there are beans or chickpeas, cooked with rice. There is cheese, too, which is very popular.

[long-pause]

The cook, Pedro, cooks in a big iron pot over the fire at the front of the ship. He soaks the salted meat in seawater first, to take away some of the salt. Then he boils it for a long time, with beans and onions and garlic. The smell drifts all over the ship and makes everyone hungry. When the food is ready, Pedro rings a little bell. The men come with their wooden bowls and line up.

[pause]

{img:sailors-eating-together-small-groups-sharing-pot-deck-laughing}
The men eat in small groups called messes. There are about eight men in each mess. They sit together on the deck, or between the guns below. They share one pot of food between them. Diego eats in the same mess as Tomás and Andrés and a few other sailors. They talk and laugh and argue about everything while they eat. It is the friendliest part of the day.

[long-pause]

The officers eat much better food. They bring their own supplies on board, and their own servants to cook for them. They have hams and sausages, dried fruit and sweet things. They have live chickens in cages and even a few goats, for fresh milk and eggs. Sometimes the captain sends a little of his food to the sailors, as a kindness. Then everyone is very pleased.

[pause]

The sailors catch fish too, when they can. When the ship is moving slowly, they throw lines over the side. Sometimes they catch a big fish, and then there is a feast. Fresh fish, grilled over Pedro's fire, is a wonderful change from salted fish. Diego is good at fishing, because his father taught him well. One afternoon, he catches a large tuna. The whole mess cheers, and Tomás claps him on the back.

[pause]

{img:ships-boy-catching-large-fish-line-over-rail-sailors-cheering}
As the weeks go by, the food gets worse. The biscuits get harder and sometimes a little mouldy. Little beetles get into the flour and the beans. The water in the barrels starts to taste stale. The men grumble about it. But Tomás just shrugs. This is the sea, he says. On land, you eat what you like. At sea, you eat what there is. And he dips his biscuit in his wine and chews it slowly, and smiles.

[pause]

On special days, there is something better to eat. On the feast day of a saint, the captain might give an extra ration of wine to everyone. On Sundays, there might be a little more meat in the stew. And sometimes, Pedro makes a special pudding of rice cooked with a little sugar and cinnamon. The men love it. The smell of cinnamon drifts all over the ship. Even the grumpiest old sailors smile when they smell it. Diego licks his bowl clean, and then he licks his spoon too.

[long-pause]

{img:sailors-sleeping-on-deck-under-stars-cloaks-coiled-ropes-night}
Where do two hundred people sleep on one wooden ship? The answer is: wherever they can find a space.

[pause]

On a Spanish galleon in 1588, there are no hammocks for the ordinary sailors and soldiers. Hammocks were known in the Americas, but they are not yet common on Spanish ships. Instead, the men sleep on the deck, or on the floor between the guns. They roll themselves in a blanket or a cloak and lie down on the wooden planks. When the weather is warm, many of them prefer to sleep up on the open deck, under the stars.

[long-pause]

Each man has a small space that he thinks of as his own. Maybe it is a corner beside a gun, or a spot near the mast. He keeps his few things there in a small wooden chest or a cloth bag. There is a spare shirt, a woollen cap, a knife, and perhaps a wooden spoon and a little picture of a saint. That is all. There is no room for anything more.

[pause]

{img:sailor-small-wooden-chest-belongings-knife-spoon-saint-picture-lantern}
Diego sleeps on the deck under the forecastle, near Tomás. On calm nights, it is pleasant there. The ship rocks gently, and the waves make a soft swishing sound against the sides. The ropes creak, and the wind hums in the rigging. Diego lies on his back and looks up at the stars between the sails. He thinks about home, and he falls asleep.

[long-pause]

But on rough nights, sleeping is harder. The ship rolls and pitches, and the men roll around on the deck. Water splashes over the side and soaks their blankets. It is cold and wet and uncomfortable. On those nights, the men squeeze together below deck, between the guns, to stay dry. It is crowded and stuffy down there, and it smells of tar and damp wool. But at least it is out of the wind.

[pause]

{img:crowded-gun-deck-night-men-sleeping-between-cannons-dim-lantern}
The officers have their own small cabins in the sterncastle. They sleep in narrow wooden beds, built against the walls. Some of them have proper mattresses and pillows. The captain's cabin is the best on the ship. It has a big window looking out over the sea behind. But even the captain's cabin is small, and it moves with every wave.

[long-pause]

On a long voyage, there is not much privacy. Two hundred men live, eat and sleep together in a very small space. They must learn to get along. Most of the time, they do. They share their food and their stories. They help each other with their work. They look after each other when they are tired or sad.

[pause]

{img:old-sailor-telling-story-ships-boys-lantern-below-deck-night}
Sometimes, before sleeping, Tomás tells Diego and Andrés a story. He tells them about the islands of the Caribbean, with their white beaches and green palm trees. He tells them about whales as big as ships, and fish that fly over the water. He tells them about the great storm off the tip of South America, when he thought the ship would never come home. But it did, he says. And here I am, still sailing, still telling stories. The boys listen in the lantern light until their eyes grow heavy. And then they sleep, side by side, as the ship sails on through the night.

[pause]

Keeping clean on the ship is not easy. There is no fresh water to spare for washing. So the men wash themselves and their clothes in seawater, in wooden buckets. The salt makes their skin dry and itchy. Their clothes become stiff and rough. When it rains, everyone rushes out onto the deck. They hold out buckets and pots to catch the fresh rainwater. Some of them stand in the rain with their faces turned up to the sky, laughing. Fresh water is a great treasure on a ship.

[pause]

Sometimes the men talk quietly in the dark before they sleep. They talk about their homes and their families. One misses his wife's cooking. Another misses his little daughter. Another misses the olive trees on his father's farm. They talk about what they will do when they get home. One will buy a boat, and another will open a shop. Another will simply sleep in a real bed for a whole week. And slowly, one by one, the voices fall silent.

[long-pause]

{img:galleon-full-sail-wind-white-canvas-blue-sky-waves-sunny}
A galleon moves only by the power of the wind. So the sails and the ropes are the most important parts of the ship. And working them is the most important job of the sailors.

[pause]

The Santa Catalina has big square sails on her front and middle masts. They hang from long wooden poles called yards, which cross the masts. When the sails are let down, they fill with wind and push the ship forward. On the back mast, there is a big triangular sail, called a lateen sail. It helps the ship to turn and to sail with the wind from the side.

[long-pause]

The sails are made of strong canvas, woven from linen or hemp. They are very big and very heavy. The biggest sail on the mainmast is as big as a house. When it is wet, it is even heavier. Raising it and lowering it takes many men pulling on the ropes together.

[pause]

{img:sailors-climbing-rigging-ratlines-up-mast-to-yard-wind-sunlight}
To climb up the masts, the sailors use a kind of rope ladder, called the ratlines. The ratlines stretch from the sides of the ship up to the top of each mast. Sailors climb them quickly, even when the ship is rolling. At the top of the mainmast, there is a little platform called the top. A sailor stands there as a lookout, watching for land or other ships.

[long-pause]

When the sails must be furled, which means rolled up, the sailors climb up and walk out along the yards. They stand on a rope hanging under the yard, and lean over it. Then they pull the heavy canvas up and tie it with short ropes. It is high and windy up there, and the ship sways from side to side. The sailors must hold on carefully with one hand. They say: one hand for the ship, and one hand for yourself.

[pause]

{img:sailors-on-yard-furling-sail-high-above-deck-sea-below-careful}
There are hundreds of ropes on the ship, and every one has a name and a purpose. Some ropes hold up the masts. Some ropes raise and lower the yards. Some ropes pull the corners of the sails. A good sailor knows every rope by name and by feel, even in the dark. Diego is learning them, one by one. Tomás quizzes him every day. What is this one? And this one? Diego gets more right every week.

[long-pause]

At the end of the summer, Diego climbs to the top of the mainmast for the first time. Tomás climbs beside him, slowly, one step at a time. At first, Diego does not dare to look down. His hands are sweating on the ropes. But Tomás talks to him calmly all the way up. And at last, they reach the top and climb onto the little platform.

[pause]

{img:boy-and-old-sailor-mast-top-platform-looking-out-over-fleet-ocean}
Diego looks out, and he gasps. The whole sea is spread out below him, blue and sparkling, all the way to the horizon. The ships of the fleet are scattered across the water like toys. Their white sails shine in the sun. Seabirds fly below him, between the masts. The wind blows in his hair. And for a moment, he forgets to be afraid. This, says Tomás quietly, is why we go to sea. Diego nods. He will remember this moment for the rest of his life.

[pause]

The ropes on the ship need constant care. The sun and the salt and the wind wear them out. So the sailors spend many hours mending them. They splice broken ropes together, weaving the ends into each other. They rub tar into the ropes to protect them. They replace old ropes with new ones from the store in the hold. Tomás is a master at splicing. His old fingers move quickly, twisting and tucking the strands. Diego watches and tries to copy him. His first splice falls apart. His second one holds.

[long-pause]

{img:ship-pilot-astrolabe-measuring-sun-noon-quarterdeck-1588}
How do you find your way across the open sea, with no land in sight and no maps on a screen? On the Santa Catalina, that is the job of the pilot.

[pause]

The pilot is called Martín. He is a quiet, careful man of about fifty. He has sailed these waters many times. He keeps a small cabin full of charts, instruments and notebooks. He is one of the most important people on the ship. Even the captain listens when the pilot speaks.

[long-pause]

The pilot's most important tool is the compass. It is a magnetic needle that always points north. It sits in a wooden box near the big steering pole at the back of the ship. The box is called the binnacle. At night, a little lantern is lit inside it, so the helmsman can see the compass. The helmsman keeps the ship pointing in the right direction, hour after hour.

[pause]

{img:helmsman-whipstaff-steering-binnacle-compass-lantern-night-galleon}
On a galleon, there is no steering wheel yet. Instead, the helmsman holds a long upright pole called a whipstaff. It is connected to the rudder at the back of the ship. When he pushes the pole one way, the ship turns. When he pushes it the other way, the ship turns back. The helmsman cannot see the sails from where he stands. So an officer above him calls out directions.

[long-pause]

To know how far north or south they are, the pilot measures the height of the sun. He does this every day at noon, when the sun is highest in the sky. He uses an instrument called an astrolabe. It is a heavy brass ring, with a pointer that can turn. He holds it up and looks at the sun through two little holes. Then he reads the angle on the ring. With this number, and a book of tables, he can work out where they are.

[pause]

{img:pilot-writing-logbook-cabin-charts-dividers-candle-evening}
At night, the pilot can also measure the height of the Pole Star. The Pole Star always sits in the north, above the North Pole. The higher it is in the sky, the further north you are. Diego likes to stand beside the pilot on clear nights and watch him work. Martín shows him how to find the Pole Star, by following two bright stars of the Great Bear. Diego practises finding it every night after that.

[long-pause]

How fast is the ship going? To find out, the sailors throw a piece of wood over the side, tied to a long rope. The rope has knots tied in it at equal distances. As the ship moves forward, the rope runs out. A sailor counts how many knots run out while a small sand-glass empties. That tells him the speed of the ship. That is why, even today, the speed of ships is measured in knots.

[pause]

{img:sailors-throwing-log-line-knots-counting-small-sand-glass-stern}
Every evening, the pilot writes everything in his logbook. He writes the direction they sailed, the speed, and the weather. He writes down any land or ships they saw. From all this, he works out on his chart where the ship is. He marks it with a little dot. Day by day, the dots make a line across the chart. It is slow and careful work. But it gets them where they need to go.

[pause]

Diego is amazed by all of it. He did not know that the stars and the sun could tell you where you were. One evening, Martín lets him hold the astrolabe. It is heavy and cold in his hands. One day, Martín says, perhaps you will be a pilot. Diego blushes. He does not think he is clever enough. But he keeps the idea in his heart, like a little seed.

[pause]

The pilot also uses his eyes and ears and nose. He watches the colour of the sea, which changes near land. He watches the birds, because some birds only fly near the coast. He watches the clouds, which often gather over islands. He even smells the wind, which can carry the smell of land, of grass or wood smoke. Old Tomás does the same. He can often tell that land is near long before anyone sees it. The sea talks to you, he tells Diego, if you know how to listen.

[long-pause]

{img:galleon-deck-evening-sunset-sailors-resting-guitar-cards-calm-sea}
Life on a galleon is hard work, but it is not all work. In the evenings, when the sea is calm, the men find ways to enjoy themselves.

[pause]

After the evening prayer, the men who are not on watch gather on the deck. The sun is setting over the sea, and the sky is orange and pink. The air is cooler now. The men sit in groups, talking and laughing. Some of them smoke pipes. Some of them simply sit and watch the sea, enjoying the quiet.

[long-pause]

Music is very popular. Many sailors can play an instrument. Someone has a guitar, a small Spanish guitar with five pairs of strings. Someone else has a little drum, or a flute made of wood. They play the old songs of Spain, from their home towns and villages. Some songs are happy and quick, and the men clap and stamp their feet. Some songs are slow and sad, about love and home and the sea.

[pause]

{img:sailor-playing-spanish-guitar-men-singing-clapping-deck-dusk}
Card games are very popular too, although the officers do not always approve. The men play for small coins, or for a share of their wine. They argue loudly about the rules and laugh when someone loses. Dice games are popular as well. And some men play a game like draughts, on a board scratched into a plank of the deck.

[long-pause]

Storytelling is the best entertainment of all. The old sailors tell stories of their voyages. They talk about strange lands and strange creatures. They talk about mermaids and sea serpents, which some of them swear they have seen. They talk about storms and shipwrecks and lucky escapes. The young men listen with wide eyes. Some of the stories are true, and some are not. But they are all good stories.

[pause]

{img:friar-teaching-ships-boys-letters-slate-lantern-evening-deck}
Fray Juan keeps his promise to teach Diego his letters. Every evening, they sit together near a lantern. Fray Juan has a small slate and a piece of chalk. He writes a letter, and Diego copies it. Then he writes a word, and Diego reads it, slowly. Mar, says Diego. Sea. Barco. Ship. Madre. Mother. By the end of the summer, Diego can read short words. He is very proud.

[long-pause]

Some evenings, the men fish over the side. Some evenings, they wash their clothes in buckets of seawater, and hang them in the rigging to dry. Some evenings, they mend their shoes or carve little things out of wood. Tomás is carving a little wooden ship for Diego. It has three masts and tiny sails made of cloth. He works on it a little every evening, with his sharp knife.

[pause]

{img:old-sailor-carving-small-wooden-ship-knife-lantern-boy-watching}
Later, when it is dark, the stars come out over the sea. There are thousands of them, more than you can ever see on land. The Milky Way stretches across the sky like a river of light. The ship's wake glows faintly behind them, with little green sparks in the water. Tomás says those sparks are tiny sea creatures that shine in the dark.

[pause]

Diego lies on his back on the deck and looks up at the stars. He thinks that the same stars are shining over his village right now. Perhaps his mother is looking at them too. The thought makes him feel a little less far away. The guitar plays softly somewhere nearby. And slowly, his eyes close.

[pause]

On some evenings, there are little plays and games on the deck. Some of the soldiers dress up in funny costumes, made of old sails and ropes. They pretend to be kings and queens, or famous people from stories. Everyone laughs until their sides hurt. On saints' days, there are processions around the deck, with candles and singing. The men carry a little statue of the saint from the bow to the stern and back. It is a small piece of home, out on the wide sea.

[pause]

Diego also learns to play the guitar a little. A soldier from Seville shows him three simple chords. Diego practises them every evening, until his fingertips are sore. Soon he can play a simple song that everyone knows. The men sing along, and they clap when he finishes. Diego blushes, but he is very pleased. He thinks that one day he will play this song for his little sister at home.

[long-pause]

{img:armada-crescent-formation-english-channel-cornwall-coast-distant-july}
At the end of July, the fleet reaches the coast of England. The lookouts see the green hills of Cornwall rising out of the sea. It is the first time most of the men have seen England. They crowd to the rails to look.

[pause]

On the shore, the English have been waiting for the Spanish fleet for months. Beacon fires have been built on the hilltops all along the coast. When the Spanish ships are seen, the beacons are lit. One after another, the fires blaze up along the coast. In this way, the news spreads across England in just a few hours.

[long-pause]

The Spanish fleet sails up the Channel in a special formation. The ships are arranged in a great curve, like a crescent moon. The strongest galleons are at the two ends of the curve. The slower supply ships are in the middle, where they are safest. It is a strong and careful formation. The English watching from the shore are amazed. They have never seen so many ships in one place.

[pause]

{img:beacon-fires-english-hilltops-night-coast-chain-of-lights}
The English fleet comes out of the harbour of Plymouth to meet them. The English ships are smaller and faster than the Spanish galleons. They have more long-range guns. They do not come close. Instead, they follow behind the Spanish fleet and fire from a distance. For a week, the two fleets move slowly up the Channel together.

[long-pause]

On the Santa Catalina, the men watch the English ships with great interest. They can see them clearly, about a kilometre behind. Sometimes there is the sound of guns, like distant thunder. Puffs of white smoke drift over the water. But the Santa Catalina is in the middle of the fleet, and the English ships do not come near her. For Diego, it is all far away and strange, like something in a dream.

[pause]

{img:distant-ships-white-smoke-puffs-calm-sea-channel-summer-haze}
The Duke of Medina Sidonia is worried. The plan is to meet the Spanish army in the Netherlands. But messages to the army are slow, and nobody is sure if the army is ready. The fleet cannot wait forever in the open sea. The duke writes letters every day, and sends small fast boats with messages. But the answers do not come.

[long-pause]

Life on board goes on, even now. The cook still cooks his stews. The boys still turn the sand-glass and sing the hours. The pages still sing the Salve Regina at dusk. Fray Juan says mass on Sunday, on the deck, as the ship sails slowly east. The men pray a little harder than usual.

[pause]

{img:galleon-sailing-past-white-cliffs-dover-evening-light-calm}
On one clear evening, the fleet sails past high white cliffs. They shine in the setting sun, pink and gold. Tomás tells Diego that these are the cliffs of Dover. On the other side of the water, the coast of France is just visible, low and grey. The sea between them is narrow here. Diego looks from one coast to the other. He thinks it is beautiful. And he wonders what will happen next.

[pause]

Along the English coast, people come out to watch the great fleet pass. They stand on the cliffs and the hilltops in crowds. Farmers leave their fields, and fishermen leave their boats. Children sit on their fathers' shoulders to see better. They have heard about the Spanish fleet for months. Now they see it with their own eyes, filling the sea from one side to the other. Some of them are frightened. Some of them are simply amazed. Nobody in England has ever seen anything like it.

[pause]

On the Santa Catalina, Fray Juan asks the boys to help him with something. He has a small box of medals, little round medals of silver with a picture of Mary. He gives one to every man on the ship. The men thank him and hang the medals around their necks, on pieces of string. Diego wears his next to the little wooden cross from his mother. He touches them both, now and then, during the long days that follow.

[long-pause]

{img:armada-anchored-off-calais-evening-many-ships-french-coast-calm}
On the sixth of August, the Spanish fleet drops anchor near the French town of Calais. The duke wants to wait here for news from the army in the Netherlands. The ships lie close together, in a long line along the coast.

[pause]

It is a calm summer evening. The men on the Santa Catalina are glad to rest after the long days of sailing. They can see the town of Calais on the shore, with its walls and church towers. Small French boats come out to sell them fresh food. There are vegetables and fruit, bread and chickens. The men buy what they can and eat well that night.

[long-pause]

The next day passes quietly. The duke waits for news. The men rest, and mend things, and talk. The English fleet is anchored nearby, a little way to the west. Everyone can see the English ships. Everyone wonders what they will do. The evening comes, and the wind picks up a little, blowing toward the Spanish ships.

[pause]

{img:night-sea-calais-dark-ships-lanterns-wind-rising-uneasy-calm}
Around midnight, the lookouts on the Spanish ships see something strange. Lights are moving on the dark water, coming toward them. At first, nobody understands what they are. Then, as they come closer, the men see. They are ships, and they are on fire.

[long-pause]

The English have taken eight old ships and filled them with things that burn easily. They have set them alight and sent them drifting toward the Spanish fleet, pushed by the wind and the tide. The burning ships glow brightly in the dark, sending sparks high into the sky. They come closer and closer. The Spanish ships are anchored close together. If a burning ship touches them, they could catch fire too.

[pause]

{img:distant-glowing-fireships-drifting-dark-water-night-orange-reflection}
On the Santa Catalina, the captain gives quick orders. There is no time to raise the anchor. The sailors cut through the thick anchor rope with axes. Then they raise the sails in the dark, as fast as they can. Diego runs to help pull the ropes, his heart beating fast. All around them, other ships are doing the same. The great fleet scatters in the night, ships moving in every direction.

[long-pause]

The burning ships drift harmlessly past and burn out on the shore. Not a single Spanish ship catches fire. But the careful crescent formation is broken. The ships are spread out along the coast, in the dark, without their anchors. And the wind is still blowing.

[pause]

{img:dawn-after-night-scattered-ships-grey-sea-smoke-drifting-quiet}
When dawn comes, the Spanish ships are scattered far and wide. The English fleet comes to meet them, near a place called Gravelines. That day, there is a long battle at sea. It is the biggest battle of the whole voyage. We will not stay there. It was a hard day for both sides. By evening, the Spanish fleet is still together, but it is tired and damaged.

[pause]

The Santa Catalina was on the edge of the battle, and she is not badly hurt. In the evening, the men gather on the deck, tired and quiet. Fray Juan leads a prayer. Pedro cooks a simple meal. Nobody talks very much. Diego sits close to Tomás, and Tomás puts an arm around his shoulders. We are still here, the old man says quietly. And tomorrow is another day.

[pause]

That night, Diego cannot sleep. He lies on the deck and looks at the stars. He thinks about the burning ships, glowing on the dark water. He thinks about home, and his mother, and the little church in his village. Tomás is lying beside him. He can tell that the boy is awake. Do you want to hear a story, he asks quietly. Diego nods. So Tomás tells him a long, slow story about a whale he once saw in the Caribbean. And by the end of it, Diego is fast asleep.

[long-pause]

{img:fleet-sailing-north-north-sea-grey-sky-strong-wind-1588}
After the battle, a strong wind begins to blow from the south-west. It pushes the Spanish fleet away from the coast, north into the North Sea. The ships cannot sail back against it. They cannot reach the Spanish army. And they cannot go back the way they came, through the narrow Channel.

[pause]

The duke calls his officers together on his ship. They talk for a long time. In the end, they make a difficult decision. They will sail north, all the way around Scotland, and then south past Ireland. That is the only way to get home to Spain. It is a very long way, through cold and stormy waters that few of them know.

[long-pause]

On the Santa Catalina, the captain tells the crew the plan. The men are quiet. Many of them have never sailed in those northern seas. They have heard stories of fog and storms and rocky coasts. But there is no other choice. And at least, they say, they are going home.

[pause]

{img:captain-speaking-to-crew-gathered-deck-serious-faces-grey-light}
The English fleet follows them north for a few days. Then it turns back, because the English ships are short of food and gunpowder too. Soon the Spanish ships are alone on the grey sea. The wind blows cold now, even in August. The men take out their warmest clothes and wrap themselves in blankets on the deck.

[long-pause]

There is a new worry now: food and water. The voyage will be much longer than planned. The duke orders that the food must be shared out very carefully. Each man now gets only half a pound of biscuit a day, and a little water and wine. The horses and mules that were brought for the army must go, because there is not enough water for them. The men are very sad to see them go.

[pause]

{img:sailors-sharing-small-rations-biscuit-water-cups-cold-wind-deck}
On the Santa Catalina, the men share what they have. The officers give some of their own food to the sailors. Pedro the cook makes the beans last as long as he can, adding more water to the stew every day. Diego is hungry most of the time now. But he does not complain. Tomás tells him that hunger is a good teacher. It teaches you to be grateful for every crumb.

[long-pause]

The days pass slowly. The sea is grey, and the sky is grey. Sometimes it rains for days without stopping. The men are tired, cold and hungry. But they keep working. The sailors work the sails and pump out the water. The boys turn the sand-glass and sing the hours. And in the evening, the pages still sing the Salve Regina, although their voices are thinner now.

[pause]

{img:ships-boy-singing-evening-hymn-grey-dusk-sea-men-listening-quietly}
One evening, Diego is the one who sings. The page who usually sings is ill, so Fray Juan asks Diego to do it. Diego is nervous. He stands on the deck in the cold wind and begins to sing, softly at first. Hail, holy queen, mother of mercy. His voice grows stronger as he goes on. The men take off their caps and listen. When he finishes, there is a moment of silence. Then old Tomás nods at him, and wipes his eye with his sleeve.

[pause]

In the North Sea, the days grow shorter and the nights grow colder. The men wear everything they have to keep warm. They wrap themselves in blankets and old sails. They huddle together around Pedro's cooking fire, holding out their hands. Pedro makes hot soup whenever he can, even if it is mostly water with a few beans. Something hot is better than nothing, he says. And the men agree, holding their bowls in their cold hands and breathing in the steam.

[long-pause]

{img:galleon-sailing-past-scottish-islands-fog-cliffs-seabirds-cold}
In the middle of August, the Spanish fleet sails around the far north of Scotland. This is a wild and beautiful place, at the very edge of the known world.

[pause]

There are many small islands here, with steep cliffs and green grass on top. Thousands of seabirds nest on the cliffs. There are puffins with their bright orange beaks, and gannets diving into the sea. Seals lie on the rocks and watch the ships go past with big dark eyes. On some islands, there are little stone houses with smoke rising from their roofs. But there are no trees at all, only grass and heather and rock.

[long-pause]

The weather up here changes quickly. One moment, the sun is shining and the sea is blue. The next moment, a thick fog rolls in, and the men can barely see the front of their own ship. In the fog, the ships of the fleet lose sight of each other. They fire guns and ring bells so they do not run into each other. It is eerie and quiet in the fog, with only the sound of the waves.

[pause]

{img:thick-fog-at-sea-ship-bell-ringing-sailor-lantern-ghostly-calm}
On the Santa Catalina, the pilot Martín is very careful. He does not know these waters well. There are no good charts of this coast. He keeps the ship far out to sea, away from the rocks. Every day, he measures the sun if he can see it. He marks their position on his chart, with careful little dots. Slowly, the line of dots turns west, and then south.

[long-pause]

The men are very tired now. Many of them are ill. The barber-surgeon goes from man to man, doing what he can. He gives them his medicines and his kind words. Fray Juan sits with them and prays. Diego and Andrés help where they can. They carry water and blankets to the sick men. They sit with them and talk to them, so they will not feel alone.

[pause]

{img:ships-boys-carrying-water-blankets-to-tired-sailors-below-deck-lantern}
Diego himself stays healthy, thanks partly to Tomás. The old sailor has a few secret tricks. He has a little bag of dried lemons from Spain, which he has saved for a long time. Every few days, he gives Diego a small piece to suck. Lemons keep you healthy at sea, he says. Nobody else seems to know this. But Tomás learned it on his long voyages, from an old sailor long ago.

[long-pause]

On one calm evening, the fog lifts, and the sky clears. The sun sets slowly over the sea, very late, because it is summer in the north. The sky glows red and orange for hours. The men come up on deck to look at it. They have not seen the sun for days. After the sunset, the sky stays light, a strange pale blue, almost all night.

[pause]

{img:northern-summer-night-sky-pale-blue-glow-galleon-silhouette-calm-sea}
Then something wonderful happens. Ribbons of green light appear in the northern sky. They move and ripple slowly, like curtains in the wind. The men stare in wonder. Some of them cross themselves. They have never seen anything like it. It is the northern lights, Tomás tells Diego. I saw them once before, many years ago. They are a gift from God, to remind us that there is beauty everywhere. Diego watches the lights until they fade, and then he goes to sleep with a smile on his face.

[pause]

One afternoon, a small boat comes out from one of the islands. Two fishermen are in it, with long beards and woollen caps. They speak a language nobody on the Santa Catalina understands. But they hold up some fish and point to the ship. The captain lets them come alongside. The fishermen trade their fish for a few small coins and some cloth. Everyone smiles and nods, and somehow they understand each other. Then the little boat rows away, back to the island. That night, the whole crew eats fresh fish.

[long-pause]

{img:irish-coast-atlantic-cliffs-stormy-sky-distant-ships-far-out}
At the beginning of September, the fleet sails south, down the west coast of Ireland. The duke has warned all the ships to stay far out to sea. The coast of Ireland is rocky and dangerous, he says. Keep well away from it.

[pause]

But the weather turns against them again. Great storms come in from the Atlantic, one after another. The wind blows from the west, pushing the ships toward the land. Some ships are damaged and tired, and they cannot hold their course. They are pushed toward the Irish coast. Some of them find shelter in quiet bays. Others are not so lucky.

[long-pause]

We will not dwell on what happened to those ships. It was a very sad time for many families in Spain. More than twenty ships were lost on the coasts of Ireland and Scotland that autumn. Some of the men who reached the shore were helped by kind Irish families. They were given food and shelter, and some of them later found their way home. We remember all of them gently tonight.

[pause]

{img:irish-cottage-turf-fire-family-sharing-food-with-stranger-sailor}
On the Santa Catalina, the pilot Martín keeps the ship far out at sea, just as the duke ordered. When the storms come, the sailors take in most of the sails. They leave just a little sail up, so the ship can still be steered. The ship rolls and climbs over the huge grey waves. Water pours over the deck. The men tie ropes around themselves, so they will not be washed away.

[long-pause]

For three days and three nights, the storm goes on. Nobody sleeps much. The sailors take turns at the pumps, pumping out the water that comes in. The carpenter and his helpers go down into the hold with lanterns, checking for leaks. Diego works beside Tomás, pulling ropes and pumping water until his arms are numb. He is frightened, but he does not show it. Tomás stays calm, and that helps Diego to stay calm too.

[pause]

{img:sailors-at-pumps-storm-night-lantern-water-on-deck-working-together}
On the fourth morning, the wind begins to drop. The waves get smaller. The clouds break up, and a little blue sky appears. The men come up on deck, wet and exhausted, and look around. The Santa Catalina is battered, but she is still afloat. Her masts are still standing. Her crew is tired, but they are alive.

[long-pause]

Fray Juan gathers everyone on the deck. They kneel down together on the wet planks and give thanks to God. Some of the men are crying with relief. Some are laughing. Some are simply too tired to do anything but sit and look at the sky.

[pause]

{img:crew-kneeling-giving-thanks-deck-after-storm-sun-breaking-through}
Then the pilot comes down from the stern, with his astrolabe in his hand. He has measured the sun for the first time in days. He looks at his chart for a long time. Then he smiles, which he rarely does. We are south of Ireland, he says. The wind is from the north now. If it holds, we will see Spain in two weeks. A great cheer goes up from the men. Diego and Andrés hug each other and dance on the deck. And Pedro the cook goes down to the hold to find something special for supper.

[pause]

In the Irish villages along the coast, there are stories about the Spanish ships that are still told today. Some families say that a Spanish sailor married into the family long ago. Some say that dark-haired children in their villages are descended from the men of the fleet. Nobody knows if these stories are true. But they show that the Irish people remembered the Spanish sailors kindly. And in some places along the coast, there are small monuments to the ships, with flowers left beside them.

[long-pause]

{img:galleon-arriving-santander-harbour-green-hills-september-1588-people-shore}
Two weeks later, in the middle of September, the lookout on the Santa Catalina sees land. He shouts down from the top of the mast. Land! Land! Everyone rushes to the rail. There, on the horizon, is a line of green hills and grey mountains. It is the north coast of Spain. They are home.

[pause]

The Santa Catalina sails slowly into the harbour of Santander. Other ships of the fleet are already there, and more come in every day. On the shore, crowds of people are waiting. They have come from all the towns and villages around. They are looking for their sons and husbands and brothers. Some of them have been waiting for weeks.

[long-pause]

When the ship drops anchor, the men are rowed ashore in small boats. They are thin and tired, with ragged clothes and long beards. Some of them can hardly walk. The people on the shore help them out of the boats. They give them bread and warm soup and fresh water. Monks from the nearby monastery come to care for the sick. The town does everything it can to help.

[pause]

{img:townspeople-helping-tired-sailors-ashore-bread-soup-monks-harbour}
Diego steps onto the stone quay and nearly falls over. After months at sea, the land feels strange and still under his feet. Tomás laughs and holds his arm. Your legs think they are still on the ship, he says. It will pass in a day or two. Diego looks around at the people and the houses and the green hills. Everything looks wonderful to him.

[long-pause]

And then he hears someone calling his name. Diego! Diego! He turns around, and there, pushing through the crowd, is his father. His father has walked for two days to get here, from the fishing village. He heard that the fleet was coming home, and he came to look for his son. Diego runs to him, and his father picks him up and holds him tight.

[pause]

{img:father-embracing-ships-boy-son-harbour-quay-crowd-tears-joy}
That night, Diego says goodbye to his friends on the ship. Andrés is going home to his own family, in another town. The two boys promise to meet again one day. Fray Juan blesses Diego and gives him a small book of prayers. Keep practising your letters, he says. And Pedro the cook gives him a bag of biscuits for the journey. They are the last good ones, Pedro says with a wink.

[long-pause]

Last of all, Diego goes to say goodbye to Tomás. The old sailor is sitting on a barrel on the quay, looking out at the sea. He reaches into his bag and takes out the little wooden ship he has been carving all summer. It is finished now. It has three masts and tiny cloth sails, and the name Santa Catalina painted on the back. He gives it to Diego. Diego holds it in both hands. He cannot say anything. He just hugs the old man for a long time.

[pause]

{img:fishing-village-homecoming-mother-running-to-boy-father-evening-light}
Two days later, Diego and his father walk down the hill into their own village. The sun is setting over the bay, and the fishing boats are coming in. His mother is standing at the door of the house. When she sees them, she drops the net she is mending and runs up the path. His little sister runs behind her, shouting his name. That evening, the whole family eats together around the fire. Diego tells them about the sea, and the storms, and the stars, and the northern lights. And the little wooden ship stands on the shelf above the fireplace, where it will stay for many years.

[pause]

In the village, everyone comes to see Diego in the following days. The neighbours bring little gifts of fish and bread. The old men ask him about the ships and the guns and the storms. The children want to hear about the northern lights. The priest asks him to read a few words from his prayer book, and Diego reads them slowly and proudly. His mother listens with tears in her eyes. Her son went away a boy, she thinks. And he has come back almost a young man.

[pause]

Diego gives his mother the little cloth bag of silver coins. She opens it and looks at the coins for a long time. Then she closes it and holds it against her heart. It is not a great fortune. But it will buy flour and oil for the winter, and new shoes for the little ones. That night, Diego sleeps in his own bed for the first time in months. He sleeps very deeply, and he dreams of the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:king-philip-receiving-news-quiet-palace-room-autumn-1588-window}
Of the hundred and thirty ships that set out from Lisbon, about eighty came home to Spain. Many thousands of men did not return. It was a great sadness for Spain. In towns and villages all over the country, there were families who waited for someone who never came home.

[pause]

When King Philip heard the news, people say he was very calm. I sent my ships to fight against men, he said, not against the winds and the waves. He thanked God that so many had come home. And he made sure that the men who returned were looked after.

[long-pause]

The Duke of Medina Sidonia came home too, tired and ill. He went back to his estates in the south of Spain, near the sea. Many people blamed him for what had happened. But others said he had done his best, in a very hard situation. He had kept the fleet together through great storms and difficult seas.

[pause]

{img:spanish-shipyard-building-new-galleons-1590s-workers-timber-sunlight}
Spain did not give up on its ships. In the years after 1588, Spanish shipyards built many new galleons. They were even better and stronger than before. For many more years, Spanish galleons sailed the oceans of the world. They carried silver from the Americas back to Spain. They sailed across the Pacific Ocean too, from Mexico to the Philippines and back.

[long-pause]

These Pacific ships were called the Manila galleons. Once a year, a great galleon sailed from Acapulco in Mexico all the way to Manila in the Philippines. It carried silver from the Americas. In Manila, the silver was traded for silk and porcelain from China, spices and other treasures. Then the galleon sailed back across the Pacific to Mexico. The voyage took about six months each way. The Manila galleons sailed this route for two hundred and fifty years.

[pause]

{img:manila-galleon-pacific-ocean-sunset-long-voyage-calm-sea}
In England, people celebrated the end of the danger. Queen Elizabeth went to a great service in Saint Paul's Cathedral in London, to give thanks. Medals were made, with words saying that God had blown, and the ships were scattered. For the English, it was a story they would tell proudly for centuries. For the Spanish, it was a sad story, but also a story of courage and endurance.

[long-pause]

And what about the ordinary people of the Santa Catalina? We do not know, because they are imagined. But we can imagine. Perhaps Tomás retired to his little house in the Basque country, and told his stories to his grandchildren. Perhaps Fray Juan went back to his monastery and taught other boys their letters. Perhaps Pedro opened a little inn by the harbour, famous for its stew.

[pause]

{img:old-man-diego-pilot-telling-grandchildren-story-wooden-ship-shelf}
And perhaps Diego became a pilot one day, just as Martín said. Perhaps he sailed to the Americas, and saw the parrots and palm trees Andrés had told him about. And perhaps, when he was an old man, he sat by his fire in the fishing village. On the shelf above him stood a little wooden ship with three masts. And he told his grandchildren about the great voyage of 1588, when he was twelve years old.

[pause]

For the families of the men who did not return, life was very hard. Many of them had lost the person who earned their bread. In some towns, the churches collected money to help them. The king gave some help too, though never quite enough. Mothers and wives went on with their lives as best they could. They kept the memory of their loved ones in their hearts. And every year, on certain days, they lit candles in the churches and said their names.

[pause]

Many of the men who came home went back to sea later. The sea was their life, and they knew no other work. Some sailed with the treasure fleets to the Americas. Some sailed on fishing boats to the cold waters near Newfoundland. Some became pilots and captains themselves. And they carried with them the lessons of 1588: to be patient, to work together, and never to give up.

[long-pause]

{img:divers-exploring-armada-wreck-sea-floor-cannon-coral-light-rays-later}
For hundreds of years, the ships of the Spanish fleet that were lost lay quietly at the bottom of the sea. Then, in our own time, divers and archaeologists began to find them.

[pause]

One of the most famous was found off the north coast of Ireland, in 1967. It was a galleass called the Girona. A galleass is a big ship that had both sails and oars. Divers from Belgium found her remains among the rocks of a little bay. They brought up many beautiful things from the sea floor.

[long-pause]

They found gold coins and silver coins, gold chains and rings. They found a little gold salamander, a lizard-shaped jewel set with red stones. They found cameos, small carved pictures of Roman emperors, set in gold. And they found ordinary things too. Cooking pots and plates, buttons and buckles, and the brass instruments of the pilots.

[pause]

{img:museum-case-gold-salamander-jewel-coins-armada-treasure-later}
Today, these treasures are kept in a museum in Belfast, in Northern Ireland. Visitors come from all over the world to see them. One of the most touching things is a small gold ring. It has words carved inside it, which mean: I have nothing more to give you. Someone gave this ring to someone they loved, long ago. Nobody knows who they were. But the ring has survived for more than four hundred years.

[long-pause]

Another ship, the Trinidad Valencera, was found in a bay in Donegal, in the north of Ireland. Divers found her big bronze guns lying on the sea floor, with the king's coat of arms on them. They also found many everyday things. There were wooden bowls and plates, leather shoes, and even part of a tent. There was a wooden comb, a bag of olives and some little pottery jars. These ordinary things tell us a great deal about daily life on board.

[pause]

{img:archaeologists-cleaning-wooden-bowl-shoe-from-wreck-lab-table-later}
Some of the finds are very small. A wooden spoon, with teeth marks on the handle. A little whistle made of bone. A thimble, used for sewing sails or clothes. A rosary, with beads made of wood. Each one belonged to a real person, a sailor or a soldier or a ship's boy. Holding them, archaeologists say, you feel very close to the people of 1588.

[long-pause]

In Spain, too, people remember the voyage. There are museums of the sea in many port towns. You can see models of galleons, old charts and instruments. You can see astrolabes and compasses and sand-glasses, just like the ones on the Santa Catalina. You can see the clothes that sailors wore, and the tools of the carpenters.

[pause]

{img:spanish-maritime-museum-galleon-model-astrolabe-display-visitors-today}
In the town of Santander, where many of the ships came home, there is a lovely park by the sea. People walk there in the evenings, looking out over the bay. The fishing boats come and go, just as they did long ago. Children play on the beach. And sometimes, an old man tells his grandchildren about the great ships that came limping home in the autumn of 1588. The story has been told for more than four hundred years. And it is still being told today.

[pause]

Divers who explore the old wrecks say it is a quiet and peaceful place under the sea. The light comes down through the water in soft green rays. Seaweed sways gently in the current. Fish swim in and out of the old timbers. Crabs live inside the cannons. And the sea floor is covered in fine sand, hiding the old things that lie beneath. The divers move slowly and carefully. They touch nothing until it has been drawn and photographed. And they treat the wrecks with great respect.

[long-pause]

{img:galleon-calm-sea-sunset-golden-sails-summer-1588-peaceful-evening}
So we leave the Santa Catalina tonight, on a calm summer evening at sea, in the year 1588.

[pause]

The sun is setting over the ocean, and the sky turns orange, then pink, then a deep soft blue. The sea is calm, with long gentle waves. The great sails hang quietly from the yards, filled with a light warm breeze. The ship rocks slowly from side to side. Her wooden timbers creak softly. And the ships of the fleet are spread out across the golden water, as far as the eye can see.

[long-pause]

On the deck, the men who are not on watch are resting. Someone is playing the guitar, softly, an old song from Andalusia. A few men are playing cards in the light of a lantern. Pedro the cook is cleaning his big iron pot, grumbling to himself. The ship's cat is curled up asleep on a coil of rope. And the smell of tar and salt and wood smoke drifts over everything.

[pause]

{img:galleon-deck-dusk-lanterns-guitar-cards-cat-asleep-coiled-rope}
At the stern, the pilot Martín is standing with his astrolabe, looking up at the first stars. Captain Don Alonso is writing in his book by the light of a candle. Fray Juan is sitting on a barrel, reading his prayer book quietly. Down below, the barber-surgeon is putting away his medicines for the night.

[long-pause]

Near the bow, old Tomás sits on a coil of rope, carving his little wooden ship by lantern light. Diego sits beside him, watching. Andrés is lying on his back nearby, looking up at the stars. The sand-glass runs out, and Diego gets up to turn it over. He sings the little verse, softly, in his clear young voice. Blessed be the hour. And then he sits down again beside Tomás.

[pause]

{img:old-sailor-and-boy-bow-lantern-carving-stars-calm-sea-night}
The pages begin to sing the Salve Regina, the evening hymn. All the men take off their caps and join in, quietly. The song rises up into the darkening sky, over the calm sea. When it is finished, there is a long, peaceful silence. Only the sound of the waves, and the creak of the ropes, and the wind in the sails.

[long-pause]

The stars come out, one by one, and then by the thousands. The Milky Way stretches across the sky like a river of light. In the dark water behind the ship, little green sparks glow and fade. Diego lies down on the deck, wrapped in his blanket. He looks up at the stars and thinks of his mother and father, far away in the little fishing village. Perhaps they are looking at the same stars. He holds the little wooden cross around his neck. And slowly, his eyes close.

[pause]

If you enjoyed this voyage on a Spanish galleon in 1588, you are warmly invited to subscribe, so that another calm story from the past is always close by, whenever you need one.

[pause]

{img:galleon-silhouette-night-stars-milky-way-calm-ocean-lantern-glow}
There should be another quiet story waiting on the screen now, if you are still awake.
