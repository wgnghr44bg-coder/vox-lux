Tonight we travel back more than two hundred years, to the year 1811. We go to the south of England, to the county of Hampshire. It is a gentle land of green hills, small woods, winding lanes and quiet villages. And in one of these villages, in a small red-brick house by the road, a woman is sitting at a little table, writing a novel. Her name is Jane Austen.

[long-pause]

It is early morning in spring. A soft mist lies over the fields. The hedgerows are white with hawthorn blossom. Sheep are grazing in the meadows. A cuckoo is calling somewhere in the woods. In the village, the smoke is rising from the chimneys. A farm cart is rattling along the lane. And from the church tower, the bell is ringing seven o'clock.

[pause]

The village is small and peaceful. There is an old stone church, with a square tower. There is an inn, where the coaches stop. There are cottages with thatched roofs and little gardens full of flowers. There is a parsonage, where the vicar lives with his family. And at the edge of the village, behind tall trees, there is a great old house, belonging to the squire, the most important family in the parish.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:parsonage-garden-1811-young-woman-muslin-dress-bonnet-reading-bench}
This is the world of Jane Austen's novels. A world of country villages and parsonages, of great houses and gardens, of morning calls and evening parties, of letters and walks and balls. A world where young women hoped to marry for love, but also had to think about money and family. Where good manners mattered very much. And where life was, for the most part, calm and gentle.

[pause]

Tonight, we will spend a year in this world. We will follow a vicar's family in a Hampshire village, not far from the home of Jane Austen. Especially the eldest daughter, a young woman of seventeen called Anne. We will see how they live, what they wear, what they eat and how they pass their days. We will go with them to a ball, and to the elegant city of Bath, and to London.

[pause]

{img:jane-austen-writing-small-round-table-quill-window-cottage-light}
We will also visit Jane Austen herself, in her cottage at Chawton, and see how she wrote her famous novels. In this very year, 1811, her first novel, Sense and Sensibility, will be published. And we will learn about the times she lived in. The Regency, when a prince ruled England in place of his father. The age of elegant houses, fine carriages and beautiful gardens.

[long-pause]

There were difficult things in those years too. England was at war with Napoleon, far away across the sea. Many people in England were poor, and worked very hard. We will remember them too. But we will mostly stay in the gentle world of the village and the parsonage, as Jane Austen did in her novels.

[pause]

{img:regency-country-lane-carriage-trees-spring-blossom-morning-light}
So let us begin, on a spring morning in 1811, in a quiet village in Hampshire. The mist is lifting from the fields. The birds are singing. And in the parsonage, a young woman is opening her window, and looking out at the morning, wondering what the day will bring.

[pause]

In the lane outside the parsonage, a boy is driving a few cows to the meadow, whistling. A girl is carrying a basket of eggs to the big house. The baker's cart is making its morning round. And an old man is sitting on the bench outside the inn, smoking his pipe, watching the world go by, as he has done every morning for forty years.

[long-pause]

{img:king-george-iii-elderly-windsor-castle-window-white-hair-quiet}
In 1811, the king of England is George the Third. He is seventy-two years old, and has been king for more than fifty years. He is a kind, simple man, who loves farming, music and his large family. People call him Farmer George. But he has been ill, on and off, for many years. And in the winter of 1810, after the death of his favourite daughter, Amelia, he became very ill indeed, and could no longer rule.

[long-pause]

So, in February 1811, Parliament made his eldest son, the Prince of Wales, the ruler of the country in his place. The prince was given the title of Prince Regent. And so the time from 1811 to 1820 is called the Regency. Today, the word Regency is often used for the whole period from about 1795 to 1830, with its special style of clothes, buildings and furniture.

[pause]

The Prince Regent is very different from his father. He is forty-eight years old. He loves luxury, fashion, art, food, drink and parties. He spends enormous amounts of money on palaces, paintings and clothes. He is clever and charming, and has excellent taste. But he is also lazy, extravagant and often in debt. Many people make fun of him. Jane Austen, for one, did not like him at all.

[pause]

{img:prince-regent-1811-elegant-coat-carlton-house-chandeliers-portrait}
But the Prince Regent also left wonderful things behind him. He built the Royal Pavilion in Brighton, by the sea, a fantastic palace with domes and minarets, like something from the Arabian Nights. He supported artists, architects and writers. He encouraged the elegant new streets of London, designed by his architect, John Nash. Regent Street and Regent's Park are named after him.

[pause]

The Regency is a time of great elegance. The fashionable men of the time are called dandies. The most famous of them is a man called Beau Brummell, a friend of the Prince Regent. He dresses very simply, but perfectly. A dark blue coat, a white waistcoat, buff-coloured trousers, and a snow-white cravat, tied in a very complicated knot around his neck. It sometimes takes him all morning to tie his cravat. He changes the way English gentlemen dress, forever.

[long-pause]

{img:royal-navy-frigate-1811-sails-calm-sea-sunset-distant-coast}
But the Regency is also a time of war. Since 1793, almost twenty years, England has been at war with France. First with the French Revolution, and then with Napoleon, who has conquered most of Europe. English soldiers are fighting in Spain and Portugal. And the Royal Navy is at sea, all over the world, blockading the French ports. Many families have a son, a brother or a husband in the army or the navy.

[pause]

Two of Jane Austen's brothers are officers in the navy. And in her novels, there are many naval officers. Brave, kind and good-humoured men, who have made their fortune at sea. In the village of our story, the vicar's family also has a son in the navy. He is a young lieutenant, on a ship far away. And every week, they wait for a letter from him.

[pause]

{img:villagers-reading-newspaper-inn-war-news-1811-candles-pipes}
For most people in the villages of England, the war is far away. They read about it in the newspapers, which arrive a few days late from London. They talk about it at the inn, and after church. They pray for the soldiers and sailors. They pay high taxes, and high prices for bread. But life in the village goes on, much as it always has, with its seasons, its harvests, its church services and its gossip.

[long-pause]

And in a small house in the village of Chawton, in Hampshire, a woman of thirty-five is writing about exactly that life. Not about kings and battles, but about families and villages, about love and marriage, about silly people and sensible ones. She writes about the small world she knows, with great wit and great kindness. And her books will become some of the most loved books in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:steventon-rectory-hampshire-1780s-family-house-garden-children-playing}
Jane Austen was born in December 1775, in a village called Steventon, in Hampshire. Her father, George Austen, was the vicar of the village. He was a kind, clever, scholarly man, who also taught boys in his house, to earn a little more money. Her mother, Cassandra, was lively and witty, and wrote funny little poems. Jane was the seventh of eight children. Six boys and two girls.

[long-pause]

The Austen family was not rich. But it was a happy, busy, clever family. The house at Steventon was full of children, books and laughter. They read novels aloud to each other in the evenings, and laughed at the silly ones. They put on plays in the barn, for the family and the neighbours. And Jane, from the age of about twelve, began to write. Funny little stories, full of absurd characters and wild adventures, to amuse her family.

[pause]

Jane's closest friend, all her life, was her sister Cassandra, who was nearly three years older. They shared a bedroom. They told each other everything. When they were apart, they wrote long letters to each other, full of news and jokes. Many of Jane's letters to Cassandra still survive. They are lively, funny and sometimes sharp. They tell us a great deal about Jane's life, and her world.

[pause]

{img:two-sisters-regency-sharing-letter-window-seat-laughing-bedroom}
As a young woman, Jane went to balls and parties, in the houses of the neighbourhood, and at the assembly rooms in the nearby town of Basingstoke. She loved dancing. She had a little romance with a young Irishman, called Tom Lefroy, when she was twenty. But he went away, and they never met again. Later, she received a proposal of marriage from a rich young man, and accepted it. But the next morning, she changed her mind. She did not love him. And she never married.

[pause]

In her early twenties, at Steventon, Jane wrote the first versions of three novels. One was called Elinor and Marianne. One was called First Impressions. And one was called Susan. Her father liked First Impressions so much that he offered it to a publisher. But the publisher refused, without even reading it. Jane put the manuscripts away. And for a long time, she wrote very little.

[long-pause]

{img:georgian-bath-street-terraced-houses-golden-stone-carriage-rainy-day}
In 1801, her father retired, and the family moved to Bath. Jane did not like Bath very much. She missed the countryside. Then, in 1805, her father passed away. It was a great sorrow, and also a great difficulty. Without him, Jane, her mother and Cassandra had very little money. They moved from one rented house to another, staying with relatives, for several years.

[pause]

At last, in 1809, one of Jane's brothers, Edward, helped them. Edward had been adopted, as a boy, by rich relatives, and had inherited their great estates, in Kent and in Hampshire. One of these estates was at the village of Chawton. And in the village, there was a small house, which Edward offered to his mother and his sisters, to live in, for as long as they liked.

[pause]

{img:chawton-cottage-red-brick-house-roadside-garden-flowers-1811}
So in July 1809, Jane, Cassandra, their mother and their friend Martha Lloyd moved into the house at Chawton. And there, at last, Jane found peace. She had a home of her own, in the countryside she loved, near her family. And she began to write again. She took out her old manuscripts, and began to rewrite them. Elinor and Marianne became Sense and Sensibility. First Impressions became Pride and Prejudice.

[long-pause]

In 1811, Jane is thirty-five. She is tall and slender, with bright hazel eyes, curly brown hair, and rosy cheeks. She wears a little cap on her head, as women of her age did. People who knew her said she was cheerful, quick and kind, with a great sense of humour, and very good at telling stories to children. And in this year, a London publisher has agreed to publish Sense and Sensibility. Jane is nervous, and excited. Her dream is about to come true.

[long-pause]

{img:chawton-cottage-interior-1811-sitting-room-small-table-window-garden-light}
The house at Chawton is a comfortable red-brick house, standing right by the road, at a fork in the village. It was once an inn. It has six bedrooms, a sitting room, a dining room, a kitchen, and a garden at the back, with fruit trees, flowers and vegetables. Coaches and carts pass right by the windows. Jane writes to her brother that the house is very comfortable, and that she likes it very much.

[long-pause]

Every morning, Jane gets up early. Before breakfast, she practises the pianoforte, for about an hour. She plays songs and little pieces, copied by hand into music books. She is not a great musician, but she loves to play. Then she makes breakfast for the family. That is her special job in the household. She makes the tea, and keeps the keys to the tea, sugar and wine, which are precious and locked away.

[pause]

After breakfast, the household work is shared. Cassandra and Martha do most of it. Jane's mother, who is now in her seventies, works in the garden, digging potatoes and weeding the vegetables, in an old green smock, like a labourer. And Jane writes. She sits at a small round table, by the window, in the sitting room. The table is tiny, with twelve sides, just big enough for a few sheets of paper and an inkpot.

[pause]

{img:small-twelve-sided-walnut-table-quill-inkpot-paper-sheets-close}
Jane writes on small sheets of paper, which she can easily hide. Because she does not want the servants, or visitors, to know that she is writing a novel. There is a door between the sitting room and the hall, which creaks when it is opened. Jane asks the family not to fix it. Because whenever she hears it creak, she knows that someone is coming, and she quickly slips her papers under a blotter, or into a drawer, before they come in.

[pause]

She writes with a quill pen, made from a goose feather, which she sharpens with a little knife. She dips it in ink, and writes in small, neat handwriting, crossing out words and adding new ones between the lines. She reads her work aloud to her family in the evenings. They laugh at the funny parts, and give her advice. Her mother especially loves her stories. And Cassandra is always her first and best reader.

[long-pause]

{img:jane-austen-walking-country-lane-bonnet-shawl-basket-hedgerows}
In the afternoons, Jane often goes for a walk. She loves walking. Along the lanes, through the fields and the woods, to the next village, or to the little town of Alton, about two kilometres away, where she does some shopping and visits friends. Sometimes she walks up to the great house, Chawton House, which belongs to her brother Edward, to borrow books from the library, or to visit when Edward is staying there.

[pause]

The village is small, and everyone knows everyone. Jane visits the poor families of the parish, and brings them food and clothes, as ladies of her class were expected to do. She makes clothes for the poor, with the other ladies. She knows all the village children by name. And she watches everyone, with her bright, amused eyes. The vicar, the squire, the gossips, the shy young ladies and the proud old ones. They all find their way, in disguise, into her novels.

[pause]

{img:family-evening-chawton-cottage-reading-aloud-candles-needlework}
In the evenings, the family sits together in the sitting room. They read aloud, sew, play cards or play the pianoforte. Sometimes there are visitors, neighbours from the village, or relatives. Jane's many nephews and nieces love to visit. Their Aunt Jane is their favourite aunt. She tells them wonderful made-up stories, plays games with them, and is always ready for fun.

[long-pause]

One of the nieces later remembered seeing Aunt Jane sitting quietly by the fire, sewing, and then suddenly laughing to herself, jumping up, running across the room to her writing table, writing something down, and then coming back to the fire and her sewing. It was a new idea for a story, which had just come to her. And she had to write it down, before she forgot it.

[long-pause]

{img:regency-parsonage-family-portrait-vicar-wife-two-daughters-drawing-room}
A few kilometres from Chawton, in a village by a little river, there is a parsonage. It is the home of the vicar of the parish, the Reverend Edward Hartley, and his family. They are friends of the Austens, and of many other families in the neighbourhood. And they are the family we will follow tonight, through the year 1811.

[long-pause]

Mr Hartley is about fifty years old. He is a tall, thin, gentle man, with grey hair and spectacles. He loves books, his garden and his family. He preaches every Sunday in the old church, visits the sick and the poor of the parish, christens the babies, marries the young couples and buries the old. He is not a rich man. The parish brings him a modest income. But he is respected and loved by his parishioners.

[pause]

Mrs Hartley is about forty-five. She is a small, energetic, practical woman, who runs the house and the family with great efficiency. She worries a great deal, especially about her daughters, and their future. She wants them to marry well, to men who are kind and respectable, and who have enough money to give them a comfortable life. Because a vicar's daughters, with no fortune of their own, need a good marriage.

[pause]

{img:young-woman-seventeen-regency-white-muslin-dress-curls-window-thoughtful}
The eldest daughter is Anne. She is seventeen years old. She has dark curls, grey eyes and a quiet, thoughtful face. She loves reading, walking and drawing. She plays the pianoforte a little, and sings rather well. She is sensible and kind, but also has a quick sense of humour, which she usually keeps to herself. This year, she has come out into society. That means that she is now old enough to go to balls and parties, and to be courted by young men.

[pause]

The younger daughter is Kitty. She is twelve. She is lively, noisy and curious, with freckles and untidy hair, which never stays under her bonnet. She loves animals, climbing trees and running in the fields, which her mother says is not ladylike at all. She is still in the schoolroom, learning French, geography, music and needlework from her mother and from a governess who comes three times a week.

[long-pause]

{img:young-naval-lieutenant-blue-coat-portrait-miniature-family-treasure}
And there is a son, William. He is twenty, and he is a lieutenant in the Royal Navy, on a ship called the Amelia, somewhere in the Mediterranean. He joined the navy when he was twelve, as a midshipman. The family has not seen him for two years. His letters arrive every few months, carried by other ships. Mrs Hartley keeps them all in a box, tied with a ribbon. And there is a small portrait of him, painted on ivory, in a little frame, on the mantelpiece.

[pause]

The Hartleys know the Austens quite well. Mr Hartley knew Jane's father, years ago. And Mrs Hartley and Mrs Austen exchange recipes and gossip. Sometimes, the Hartleys go to tea at the cottage in Chawton. And Anne admires Miss Jane Austen very much. Miss Austen is always so lively and amusing, and notices everything. Anne does not know that Miss Austen writes novels. Very few people do.

[pause]

{img:two-women-walking-regency-garden-path-older-younger-bonnets-talking}
One afternoon in the spring, when the Hartleys visit Chawton, Miss Austen takes Anne for a walk in the garden. She asks her about her reading, and her drawing, and whether she is looking forward to the spring ball at the assembly rooms. Anne admits that she is a little nervous. Miss Austen laughs, kindly, and says that every girl is nervous at her first ball. And that the most important thing is to dance with someone who makes you laugh.

[long-pause]

Anne remembers this advice. And many years later, when she is an old woman, and the whole world has read the novels of Jane Austen, she will tell her grandchildren about that walk in the garden at Chawton. And about the lively lady in the little cap, who said that the most important thing at a ball was to dance with someone who makes you laugh.

[long-pause]

{img:georgian-parsonage-house-symmetrical-windows-garden-church-tower-behind}
The parsonage of the Hartley family is a pleasant old house, next to the church. It was built about a hundred years before, in the Georgian style. It is square and symmetrical, made of red brick, with tall sash windows, painted white, and a front door in the middle, with a little porch. In front, there is a gravel sweep for carriages, and behind, there is a large garden, with a lawn, flower beds, an orchard and a vegetable garden.

[long-pause]

Inside, the house has two floors and an attic. On the ground floor, there is the drawing room, where the family sits in the afternoons and evenings, and receives visitors. It has a pianoforte, a sofa, chairs, a round table for cards, and a fireplace with a marble mantelpiece. There is the dining room, with a long mahogany table. And there is Mr Hartley's study, full of books, where he writes his sermons.

[pause]

Upstairs, there are the bedrooms. Mr and Mrs Hartley's room, with a big four-poster bed. Anne's room, small and pretty, with a window looking out over the orchard. Kitty's room, full of birds' nests and pressed flowers and other treasures. And William's room, which is kept ready for him, with his things exactly as he left them, for whenever he comes home. In the attic, there are the rooms of the servants.

[pause]

{img:regency-drawing-room-pianoforte-sofa-fireplace-afternoon-light-family}
The Hartleys have three servants. That is quite a small number, for a gentleman's family. There is a cook, a cheerful woman called Mrs Platt, who rules the kitchen. There is a housemaid, a young village girl called Sally, who cleans the house, lights the fires, carries the water and helps with everything. And there is a manservant, an old man called Thomas, who looks after the horse, the garden, the boots and the silver, and drives the little carriage.

[pause]

The kitchen is in the back of the house, down a few steps. It has a big open fireplace, with a spit for roasting meat, and an iron range for boiling and baking. There is a scullery, for washing dishes, and a larder, for storing food. There is a dairy, where Mrs Platt makes butter and cheese from the milk of the family's cow. And there is a little brewhouse, where they brew their own beer.

[long-pause]

{img:regency-kitchen-cook-roasting-spit-fire-copper-pans-maid-helping}
There is no running water in the house. Sally fetches water from a pump in the yard, and carries it up and down the stairs in heavy cans. Every room is heated by its own fire, of wood or coal. Sally must clean the fireplaces and light the fires every morning, before the family gets up. And at night, the house is lit by candles. Wax candles in the drawing room, for company. Cheaper tallow candles everywhere else.

[pause]

The garden is Mr Hartley's great joy. He has planted fruit trees in the orchard. Apples, pears, plums, cherries and a fig tree against the south wall. He grows vegetables, with the help of old Thomas. Peas, beans, cabbages, carrots, onions, potatoes, lettuces and asparagus. And he grows flowers, roses and lilies and sweet peas, which Anne and her mother arrange in vases for the house.

[pause]

{img:vicar-in-garden-pruning-roses-orchard-blossom-straw-hat-spring}
In the spring, the orchard is a mass of blossom, pink and white. Kitty and Anne take their books and their sewing out into the orchard, and sit on a bench under the apple trees, in the shade. The bees hum in the blossom. The blackbirds sing. And from the church tower, next door, the clock strikes the hours, slowly and softly, across the sleepy garden.

[long-pause]

It is not a grand house. It is not like the great houses of the rich, with their halls and galleries and dozens of servants. But it is comfortable, and warm, and full of books and music and flowers. And Anne loves it. When she imagines her future, she sometimes thinks that she would be quite happy to live in a house just like this one, all her life.

[pause]

On wet afternoons, when they cannot walk, Anne and Kitty play battledore and shuttlecock in the long upstairs corridor, or build card houses on the drawing room table. Their mother says that they are too old for such games. But she smiles when she says it. And sometimes, when she thinks nobody is looking, she picks up a battledore herself.

[long-pause]

{img:regency-breakfast-table-tea-urn-toast-rolls-morning-sunlight-family}
A day at the parsonage follows a gentle rhythm. The family gets up at about eight o'clock. Sally has already lit the fires and brought up hot water for washing. The family washes in their bedrooms, in a china bowl, and dresses. Then they go down to breakfast, at about nine or ten o'clock. Breakfast in the Regency is a late meal, after a few hours of morning activities.

[long-pause]

Breakfast is simple. Tea or coffee, or hot chocolate. Toast, rolls and bread, with butter and honey or jam. Sometimes eggs, or cold meat. The family sits around the table in the dining room, and Mr Hartley reads the newspaper, which comes from London twice a week, and reads out the interesting parts. The war in Spain. The Prince Regent. The price of corn. A shipwreck. A new play in London.

[pause]

After breakfast, everyone goes about their business. Mr Hartley goes to his study, to write his sermon, or out into the parish, to visit the sick and the poor. Mrs Hartley meets with Mrs Platt, the cook, to plan the meals for the day, and checks the household accounts. Anne practises the pianoforte, or draws, or reads. And Kitty has her lessons, which she does not enjoy very much.

[pause]

{img:morning-call-regency-drawing-room-visitors-bonnets-tea-polite-conversation}
In the late morning and early afternoon, there are morning calls. This means visiting the neighbours, or receiving them. Even though it is called a morning call, it happens after noon. A visit lasts about a quarter of an hour, never longer, unless you are a close friend. The visitors sit in the drawing room, and talk politely about the weather, the neighbours, the latest news and the health of the family. It is an important part of social life.

[pause]

When the weather is fine, the ladies go for a walk. Walking is one of the great pleasures of country life. Along the lanes, across the fields, through the woods, to the next village. Anne loves to walk, for hours, alone or with Kitty. She takes her sketchbook, and draws the views. Her mother worries that she will get her petticoats muddy, which, in Jane Austen's novels, is a sign of a lively and independent young lady.

[long-pause]

{img:regency-dinner-table-candles-roast-dishes-family-guests-evening}
Dinner is the main meal of the day. It is eaten in the late afternoon, at about four or five o'clock. In London, the fashionable people eat even later, at six or seven. The family dresses for dinner, in their better clothes. There are several dishes, all put on the table at once, and everyone helps themselves and their neighbours. Soup, fish, roast meat, vegetables, pies and puddings. And then the dessert, of fruit, nuts and sweet things.

[pause]

After dinner, the ladies leave the gentlemen at the table, and go to the drawing room. The gentlemen stay for a while, drinking port wine and talking about politics, farming and hunting. Then they join the ladies, for tea and coffee. This is the most pleasant time of the day. The candles are lit. The fire is warm. And the family and their guests spend the evening together.

[pause]

{img:evening-drawing-room-cards-pianoforte-singing-candles-regency-family}
In the evening, they play cards. Whist, which is a game for four, played seriously and quietly. Or noisier games, like speculation, which Jane Austen loved. They play the pianoforte and sing. They read aloud, from a novel or a book of poems. They do needlework. They write letters. They talk. And at about ten or eleven o'clock, they have a light supper, of cold meat, bread and cheese, and a glass of wine. Then everyone takes a candle, and goes up to bed.

[long-pause]

It is a quiet life. Some people might find it a little dull. But Anne does not. She loves the long walks, the music, the books and the evenings by the fire. And there is always something happening. A letter from William. A visit from a neighbour. News of a ball. A new book from the circulating library. A wedding in the village. In a small world, small things are big events. And Jane Austen knew how to make them into wonderful stories.

[long-pause]

{img:regency-fashion-white-muslin-gowns-high-waist-spencer-jacket-bonnet-ribbons}
The clothes of the Regency are some of the most elegant in history. After the stiff, wide, heavy dresses of the eighteenth century, the fashion has completely changed. The ladies of 1811 wear light, simple, flowing dresses, inspired by the statues of ancient Greece and Rome. And the gentlemen wear dark coats, white shirts and tall hats.

[long-pause]

A lady's dress in 1811 has a very high waist, just under the bust, and a long, straight skirt, that falls to the ankles. The sleeves are often short and puffed, for evening, and long and narrow, for day. The neckline is low and square for evening, and filled in with a little lace collar for day. The most fashionable fabric is muslin, a thin, soft cotton, from India. White muslin is the height of elegance. It looks like the white marble of the ancient statues.

[pause]

But white muslin is thin, and England is cold. So the ladies wear many layers. Under the dress, there is a chemise, a long linen undergarment, and a short corset, called stays, which is much softer than the corsets of earlier times. Over the dress, they wear a short little jacket, called a spencer, or a long coat, called a pelisse. And they wrap themselves in a large shawl, often from India, made of beautiful soft wool, with patterns.

[pause]

{img:regency-bonnets-shop-window-straw-silk-ribbons-feathers-display}
Outside, every lady wears a bonnet. A hat with a brim around the face, tied with ribbons under the chin. Bonnets are made of straw, or silk, or felt, and trimmed with ribbons, flowers and feathers. Ladies love to change the trimmings of their bonnets, to make them look new. Jane Austen often wrote to Cassandra about trimming bonnets. Indoors, married women and older ladies wear little white caps, of muslin or lace.

[pause]

On their hands, ladies wear gloves, always, when they go out. Short gloves for the day, and long ones, up to the elbow, for evening. They carry a little bag, called a reticule, because the new dresses are too thin for pockets. They carry a parasol, against the sun, because a pale complexion is fashionable. And on their feet, they wear flat, thin slippers of silk or leather, which are pretty but not very practical in the mud.

[long-pause]

{img:regency-gentleman-tailcoat-cravat-top-hat-boots-walking-cane-street}
The gentlemen wear a coat, cut short in front and with long tails behind, in dark blue, green, brown or black wool. Under it, a waistcoat, often of a lighter colour. A white linen shirt, with a high collar. And around the neck, a long white cloth, called a cravat, folded and tied in a fashionable knot. They wear tight breeches to the knee, with stockings, or the new long trousers, called pantaloons. And on their feet, tall leather riding boots, polished until they shine.

[pause]

On their heads, gentlemen wear a tall hat, with a narrow brim, made of beaver fur or silk. They carry a walking cane, and gloves. Their hair is short and natural, often brushed forward in a fashionable style, copied from the statues of Roman emperors. The powdered wigs of their grandfathers are completely out of fashion now. Only old gentlemen, and servants in grand houses, still wear them.

[pause]

{img:young-women-sewing-dresses-together-patterns-fabric-parsonage-afternoon}
Most ladies of the gentry make many of their own clothes, or at least alter and trim them. They buy fabric in the shops, and sew with a dressmaker, or by themselves. Anne and her mother spend many hours sewing. They look at the fashion plates in the ladies' magazines, which show the latest styles from London, and try to copy them, with cheaper fabrics. Anne is very good at it.

[long-pause]

For her first ball, in the spring of 1811, Anne is making a new dress. It is of white muslin, with a little pattern of tiny green leaves, embroidered by herself. It has short puffed sleeves, and a green silk sash at the high waist. She will wear long white gloves, white silk slippers, and a green ribbon in her dark curls. She works on it every evening, for weeks. And when it is finished, she tries it on in front of the mirror, and turns around, and cannot quite believe that the young lady in the glass is herself.

[long-pause]

{img:regency-woman-writing-letter-writing-desk-quill-sealing-wax-window}
In 1811, there are no telephones, no telegrams and no emails. If you want to talk to someone who is far away, you write them a letter. And people in the Regency write a great many letters. To family, to friends, to business people. Letter-writing is an art, and an important part of daily life. Jane Austen wrote thousands of letters in her life. And in her novels, letters are often the most important moments of the story.

[long-pause]

Writing a letter takes time and care. You sit at a writing desk, with a sheet of good paper, a quill pen and a pot of ink. You write slowly, in your best handwriting. There are no envelopes. When you have finished, you fold the sheet of paper into a small packet, with the writing inside, and write the address on the outside. Then you seal it with a blob of red sealing wax, pressed with a little seal that has your initial on it.

[pause]

Paper is expensive, and so is postage. The person who receives the letter pays the postage, not the sender. And the price depends on the distance, and on the number of sheets. So people try to write as much as possible on one sheet. Sometimes, when they have filled the page, they turn it sideways and write across the lines they have already written. This is called crossing. It makes the letter very hard to read.

[pause]

{img:crossed-letter-close-handwriting-two-directions-old-paper-sealing-wax}
The letters are carried by the Royal Mail. Every day, a mail coach leaves London for every part of the country. It is a fast coach, pulled by four horses, painted red and black, with a guard in a red coat, who carries a horn and a gun to protect the mail. The mail coaches travel day and night, changing horses every ten or fifteen kilometres. A letter from London can reach Hampshire in a day.

[pause]

In the village, the letters arrive at the inn, or at the post office, which is often just a room in a shop. Thomas, the Hartleys' manservant, goes to fetch them every day. And every day, the family waits for him, a little impatiently, to see if there is anything for them. A letter is a great event. It is opened at once, and read aloud to everyone, unless it is private.

[long-pause]

{img:mail-coach-red-black-four-horses-galloping-country-road-guard-horn}
Mrs Hartley writes to her sister in Bath every week, and to her old school friend in London. Anne writes to her cousin Charlotte, who lives in Kent, and who is her closest friend. They tell each other everything. About books and fashions and parties, about the young men they have met, and about their hopes and dreams. Their letters are long, and full of crossed lines, and little drawings in the margins.

[pause]

But the letters the family waits for most are the letters from William, at sea. They arrive only every few months, because they must travel by ship, from the Mediterranean, to Portsmouth, and then by mail coach to the village. When one arrives, Mrs Hartley sits down at once, with her hands trembling, and reads it aloud. William is well. His ship has been to Malta and Gibraltar. He has seen dolphins and flying fish. He sends his love.

[pause]

{img:mother-reading-son-letter-aloud-family-gathered-drawing-room-emotion}
Sometimes, there is no letter for many months. Then the family worries. They read the newspapers carefully, for any news of the Mediterranean fleet. Mrs Hartley cannot sleep. Mr Hartley prays. And Anne tries to cheer everyone up, and says that the post from the Mediterranean is always slow. And then, at last, a letter comes. And everyone breathes again.

[long-pause]

In Jane Austen's novel Persuasion, there is a famous letter. A naval captain writes a letter to the woman he loves, while she is in the same room, talking to someone else. He writes that she pierces his soul, and that he is half agony and half hope. It is one of the most romantic letters in English literature. Anne, of course, does not know it yet. Persuasion will be written years later. But she would have loved it.

[long-pause]

{img:circulating-library-1811-shelves-books-ladies-browsing-reading-room}
In the Regency, reading is one of the great pleasures of life. People read everything. Poetry, history, travel books, sermons, newspapers, magazines. And above all, novels. Novels are very popular, especially with young ladies. Some people disapprove of them, and say that they fill young heads with silly romantic ideas. But everyone reads them anyway.

[long-pause]

Books are expensive in 1811. A new novel, in three small volumes, can cost as much as a week's wages for a working man. So most people do not buy books. Instead, they borrow them from a circulating library. A circulating library is a shop where you pay a yearly fee, and can then borrow books, a few at a time. There are circulating libraries in every town, and in the fashionable places like Bath, they are popular meeting places, where people go to be seen.

[pause]

The Hartleys belong to the circulating library in the nearby town of Alton. Every week, Anne walks there, and changes her books. She chooses novels for herself, books of poetry for her father, and travel books for her mother, who loves to read about faraway places, especially places where William's ship might be. And Kitty always asks for books about animals and adventures.

[pause]

{img:young-woman-reading-novel-garden-bench-afternoon-parasol-book-three-volumes}
The most popular novels of the time are often full of drama. Gothic novels, with ruined castles, secret passages, ghosts and villains. Sentimental novels, with heroines who faint and cry a great deal. Jane Austen loved to read these novels. But she also loved to make fun of them. In one of her novels, Northanger Abbey, the heroine reads so many Gothic novels that she starts to imagine mysteries everywhere, in an ordinary country house.

[pause]

In the autumn of 1811, a new novel appears at the circulating library in Alton. It is called Sense and Sensibility. It is in three volumes. And on the title page, instead of the author's name, it says simply, By a Lady. Nobody knows who the lady is. Anne borrows it, and takes it home. And from the first page, she cannot put it down.

[long-pause]

{img:sense-and-sensibility-first-edition-three-volumes-by-a-lady-title-page}
Sense and Sensibility is the story of two sisters, Elinor and Marianne Dashwood. After their father dies, they must leave their beautiful home, and move with their mother to a small cottage in the country, with very little money. Elinor is calm and sensible. She keeps her feelings to herself. Marianne is passionate and romantic. She shows her feelings to everyone. Both of them fall in love, and both of them have their hearts tested. And in the end, they both find happiness, in different ways.

[pause]

Anne reads the whole book in three days. She laughs at the funny parts, and cries at the sad ones. She recognises so many of the characters. The silly neighbours, the selfish relatives, the kind old colonel. It is as if the author had been watching her own village. She thinks that she is a little like Elinor, sensible and quiet. And Kitty, she thinks, will be a Marianne, one day.

[pause]

{img:jane-austen-holding-printed-copy-own-novel-quiet-smile-cottage-window}
In the cottage at Chawton, Jane Austen holds the three little volumes of her first book in her hands. She has waited for this moment for almost fifteen years. She writes to Cassandra that she can no more forget it than a mother can forget her baby. The book sells well. All the copies are sold within two years, and Jane earns a hundred and forty pounds, the first money she has ever earned by her writing. She is very proud.

[long-pause]

Anne never guesses that the lady who wrote Sense and Sensibility is the same lady in the little cap, who walked with her in the garden at Chawton. Very few people know. Jane wants to keep it a secret. But her brother Henry is so proud of her that he cannot help telling people. And slowly, over the years, the secret comes out. And when Anne finally learns it, many years later, she laughs, and says that she should have guessed.

[long-pause]

{img:regency-assembly-room-ball-chandeliers-couples-country-dance-musicians-gallery}
In April, there is a ball at the assembly rooms in the nearby town. It is Anne's first ball. She has been thinking about it for weeks. Her new dress is ready. Her gloves are washed. Her slippers are new. And on the evening of the ball, she sits in front of the mirror, while her mother arranges her curls and ties the green ribbon in her hair. Kitty sits on the bed, watching, and wishing she were old enough to go.

[long-pause]

The assembly rooms are a big hall, above the inn, in the market town. Once a month, during the winter and spring, there is a public ball there. Anyone who is respectable, and who pays for a ticket, can come. The families of the gentry come from all the villages around, in their carriages. The farmers' families, the doctors, the lawyers and the officers of the regiment that is stationed in the town. It is the great social event of the month.

[pause]

The Hartleys arrive at about eight o'clock, in their little carriage, driven by old Thomas. The hall is already full of light, music and people. Chandeliers hang from the ceiling, full of candles. Along the walls, there are benches, where the mothers and the older ladies sit and watch. At one end, in a little gallery, there are the musicians. A violin, a cello and a pianoforte. And in the middle of the room, the young people are dancing.

[pause]

{img:young-woman-first-ball-nervous-mother-beside-her-bench-candlelit-hall}
In 1811, the most popular dances are the country dances. The dancers stand in two long lines, the gentlemen on one side, the ladies on the other, facing their partners. Then the first couple dances down the line, weaving between the others, and each couple in turn follows them. It is a lively, cheerful dance, with lots of skipping, turning and changing places. A single dance can last half an hour, if the line is long.

[pause]

Dancing is a chance to talk. While you are dancing, you can have a little conversation with your partner, in the moments when you are standing still, waiting for your turn. It is one of the few times when a young man and a young woman can talk privately, without their parents listening. So dancing is very important for falling in love. In Jane Austen's novels, the most important conversations often happen on the dance floor.

[long-pause]

{img:young-gentleman-bowing-asking-young-woman-to-dance-ballroom-polite}
At first, Anne sits with her mother on the bench, and watches. She is nervous. What if nobody asks her to dance? Then the master of ceremonies brings over a young man, and introduces him. In the Regency, a gentleman cannot ask a lady to dance unless they have been properly introduced. The young man bows, and asks if he may have the honour of the next dance. Anne blushes, and says yes.

[pause]

His name is Mr Henry Ellis. He is twenty-four years old. He is a young doctor, who has just come to the town, to work with the old doctor, who is getting tired. He is not very tall, nor very handsome. He has brown hair that sticks up at the back, and a slightly crooked smile. But he is very easy to talk to. And while they dance, he says something so funny about the cello player's wig that Anne laughs out loud, in the middle of the dance.

[pause]

{img:couple-dancing-laughing-country-dance-line-candlelight-ball}
After that, Anne dances every dance. With the vicar's son from the next parish. With a young officer of the regiment, in a red coat, who talks only about himself. With an old friend of her father's, who dances very slowly. And with Mr Ellis, twice. Which is the most that a gentleman may dance with the same lady, at one ball, without people starting to talk. There is a supper, at midnight, of cold chicken, ham, jellies and cakes. And then more dancing, until two in the morning.

[long-pause]

In the carriage, on the way home, in the dark, Anne is very quiet. Her mother asks her if she enjoyed the ball. Anne says yes, very much. Her mother asks which of her partners she liked best. Anne thinks for a moment. And then she remembers the advice of Miss Austen, in the garden at Chawton. And she smiles to herself, in the darkness of the carriage, and says that she liked the one who made her laugh.

[long-pause]

{img:english-country-house-palladian-park-lake-deer-landscape-repton-summer}
In the summer, the Hartleys are invited to spend a few days at a great country house, the home of a rich family in the county. Mrs Hartley's cousin is married to the owner. It is a great honour, and a great excitement. Anne has never stayed in such a grand house before. She packs her best dresses, and her new gloves, and her book. And Kitty is coming too.

[long-pause]

The house stands in a great park, which they reach by a long drive, winding through trees. And then, suddenly, at a bend in the road, it appears. A huge house, of pale stone, with a portico of tall columns in front, like a Greek temple. Wide steps lead up to the door. And in front of the house, there is a lake, with swans, and a wide green lawn, sloping down to the water. Deer are grazing under the old trees.

[pause]

In Jane Austen's novel Pride and Prejudice, the heroine, Elizabeth, sees the great house of Mr Darcy, called Pemberley, in exactly the same way, at a bend in the road. She says that she has never seen a place for which nature has done more. And Anne, at this moment, feels exactly the same. She has never seen anything so beautiful.

[pause]

{img:grand-entrance-hall-country-house-marble-staircase-portraits-footman}
Inside, the house is magnificent. There is an entrance hall, with a marble floor and a great staircase. There are drawing rooms, with silk walls and gilded furniture. There is a library, with thousands of books, from floor to ceiling. There is a long gallery, hung with portraits of the family's ancestors, in old-fashioned clothes. And there are dozens of bedrooms, each with a different coloured silk on the walls.

[pause]

The house is run by an army of servants. A butler, a housekeeper, a cook, footmen, maids, grooms, gardeners and many more. More than thirty of them. The family hardly ever sees most of them. The servants live and work in a separate part of the house, below stairs, and use separate staircases and corridors, so that they are not seen. Everything simply happens, as if by magic. The fires are lit. The meals appear. The beds are made.

[long-pause]

{img:landscape-garden-serpentine-path-temple-folly-trees-lake-strolling-couple}
The park around the house was designed by a famous landscape gardener, Humphry Repton. In the Regency, the fashion is for landscape gardens that look natural, like a beautiful painting of the countryside. Instead of straight paths and clipped hedges, there are winding paths, gentle hills, clumps of trees, a lake that looks like a natural lake, and little temples and ruins, built to be looked at from a distance.

[pause]

Anne and Kitty explore the park. They walk around the lake, and find a little Greek temple on a hill, and a grotto by the water, decorated with shells. They see the deer, very close. They walk through the kitchen gardens, behind high brick walls, where gardeners grow peaches, grapes and even pineapples, in glass houses heated by stoves. Kitty is amazed by the pineapples. She has never seen one.

[pause]

{img:glasshouse-pineapples-peaches-gardener-heated-pinery-walled-garden}
In the evenings, there are dinners for twenty people, with dozens of dishes, served on silver plates, by footmen in livery. There is music, and cards, and a little dancing. The ladies wear their finest clothes. Anne feels a little shy, among so many grand people. But the lady of the house is kind, and asks her to play the pianoforte, and praises her singing. And one evening, Anne has a long conversation in the library with an old gentleman, about books, which she enjoys very much.

[long-pause]

When they go home, at the end of the visit, Anne looks back at the great house, from the bend in the drive, for a long time. It is beautiful. But she thinks that she would not really like to live in it. Too big, too many servants, too many rules. She thinks of the parsonage, with its orchard and its creaking stairs. And she finds that she is looking forward to being home.

[long-pause]

{img:stagecoach-1811-four-horses-passengers-roof-luggage-coaching-inn-yard}
In the autumn, Mrs Hartley's sister invites Anne to come and stay with her in Bath, for a few weeks. It is a wonderful invitation. Bath is the most fashionable town in England, after London. But first, Anne must travel there. And in 1811, travelling is an adventure in itself.

[long-pause]

The Hartleys have only a small carriage, with one horse, which is not suitable for long journeys. So Anne will travel by stagecoach. A stagecoach is a big, heavy coach, pulled by four horses, which carries passengers and parcels along the main roads, from town to town, on a regular timetable. It has room for four or six passengers inside, and more on the roof, which is cheaper, but cold and windy.

[pause]

Anne will travel inside, of course. Young ladies do not travel on the roof. And she will not travel alone. Her father takes her to the coaching inn in the town, early in the morning, and asks a respectable older lady, who is going to Bath too, to look after her on the way. The lady is a widow, kind and talkative, with a little dog in a basket. Anne's trunk is tied onto the roof. And the coach sets off, with a crack of the whip, and the sound of the guard's horn.

[pause]

{img:inside-stagecoach-passengers-bumpy-ride-window-countryside-lady-dog-basket}
The journey from Hampshire to Bath is about a hundred and twenty kilometres. It takes the whole day. The roads are much better than they were fifty years before. Many of them are now turnpike roads, with a hard surface of crushed stone, kept in good repair. Travellers pay a small toll at gates along the way. But even so, the coach bumps and sways, and rattles and creaks. Inside, the passengers are squeezed together, knee to knee.

[pause]

Every fifteen kilometres or so, the coach stops at a coaching inn, to change horses. The tired horses are taken away, and fresh ones are put in, very quickly, in a few minutes. Sometimes, the passengers have time to get out, stretch their legs, and have a cup of tea or a bowl of soup at the inn. At midday, there is a longer stop, for dinner. The inns are busy, noisy places, full of travellers, coachmen, grooms and servants.

[long-pause]

{img:coaching-inn-yard-busy-changing-horses-ostlers-steam-breath-autumn}
Anne looks out of the window, at the countryside rolling by. Green fields and hedges, little villages with church towers, woods turning gold and red with autumn. Market towns, with busy streets. Flocks of sheep, herds of cows, ploughmen in the fields. Then the chalk downs of Wiltshire, wide and open, with great flocks of sheep, and the wind blowing across the grass. The widow talks all the way, about her late husband, her daughters and her dog.

[pause]

Travel in the Regency could also be uncomfortable, and sometimes a little dangerous. Coaches sometimes overturned on bad roads. In winter, they could get stuck in the snow. And there were stories of highwaymen, robbers on horseback, who stopped coaches on lonely roads. But by 1811, highwaymen are rare. And Anne's journey is perfectly peaceful. The only adventure is when the widow's dog escapes at an inn, and runs around the yard, chased by three stable boys.

[pause]

{img:first-view-of-bath-golden-stone-city-hills-river-evening-light-from-road}
In the late afternoon, the coach comes over the top of a hill. And there, below them, in a green valley, is Bath. A whole city of golden stone, glowing in the evening sun. Elegant terraces of houses, curving along the hillsides. Church towers and the great old abbey. The river Avon, winding through the valley. Anne leans out of the window, and stares.

[long-pause]

At the coaching inn in the middle of Bath, her aunt is waiting for her. She is a cheerful, plump lady, in a purple pelisse and a bonnet with feathers. She hugs Anne, and thanks the widow, and pats the dog, and has Anne's trunk put on a porter's cart. And they walk together through the lamplit streets, to her aunt's house, where a warm fire and a good supper are waiting.

[long-pause]

{img:royal-crescent-bath-curved-terrace-golden-stone-lawn-carriages-sunny}
Bath is a city made for pleasure. For almost two thousand years, people have come here for the hot springs, which bubble up out of the ground, warm and full of minerals. The Romans built great baths here, and a temple. And in the eighteenth century, Bath became the most fashionable place in England, where rich people came to drink the waters, bathe, dance, gamble, shop and meet each other.

[long-pause]

The city is built of golden stone, from the hills nearby. The streets are elegant and orderly, with long terraces of houses, all the same, with columns and tall windows. The most famous is the Royal Crescent, a great curving terrace of thirty houses, looking out over a wide lawn. There is also the Circus, a circle of houses around a round open space. Walking through Bath is like walking through a beautiful painting.

[pause]

Every morning, Anne goes with her aunt to the Pump Room. It is a large, elegant hall, next to the old Roman baths. Here, the visitors come to drink the waters of the hot spring, which are said to be good for the health. A man pumps the warm water from a fountain, and serves it in glasses. It is warm, and tastes rather unpleasant, of iron. But everyone drinks it, and says that they feel much better.

[pause]

{img:pump-room-bath-elegant-hall-people-drinking-water-glasses-musicians}
But the real reason people come to the Pump Room is to see and be seen. While they drink their glass of water, they walk up and down the room, greeting their friends, looking at each other's clothes, and gossiping. There is music, from a little orchestra. And there is a big book, in which new arrivals write their names, so that everyone knows who has come to town. Anne's aunt reads the book every morning, very carefully.

[pause]

In the afternoons, there is shopping. Bath has wonderful shops, in its elegant streets. Milliners, selling bonnets and ribbons. Drapers, selling silk, muslin and lace. Jewellers, booksellers, confectioners and perfumers. Anne and her aunt walk up and down Milsom Street, looking in the windows. Her aunt buys her a new bonnet, of straw, trimmed with green ribbon, to match her dress. Anne is delighted.

[long-pause]

{img:bath-assembly-rooms-ballroom-evening-chandeliers-dancing-crowd}
In the evenings, there are concerts, plays at the theatre, and balls at the Upper Assembly Rooms. The Assembly Rooms are grand and beautiful, with an enormous ballroom, lit by great crystal chandeliers, a tea room, and a card room. On ball nights, hundreds of people come. It is very crowded and hot. In Jane Austen's novel Northanger Abbey, the young heroine goes to her first ball here, and the crowd is so thick that she cannot see anything but the feathers of the ladies' hats.

[pause]

Jane Austen herself lived in Bath for five years, from 1801 to 1806. She did not much like it. She found it hot and glaring in summer, and full of silly people. But she used it very well in her novels. Two of her novels, Northanger Abbey and Persuasion, have many scenes in Bath. The Pump Room, the Assembly Rooms, the shops of Milsom Street, the walks along the river. All of them are there, just as she saw them.

[pause]

{img:young-woman-walking-river-avon-bath-pulteney-bridge-autumn-afternoon}
Anne enjoys Bath very much. But not for the balls or the shops. She enjoys the walks, along the river and up into the hills, from where she can see the whole golden city below. She enjoys the library, where she reads the newspapers. And she enjoys the old Roman baths, which she visits with her aunt, and where she looks at the ancient stones, and thinks about the Romans who bathed there, so long ago.

[long-pause]

After three weeks, it is time to go home. Her aunt gives her a little present, a book of poems by Walter Scott, called The Lady of the Lake, which everyone is reading this year. And on the last morning, a letter arrives from her mother. It says that Mr Ellis, the young doctor, has called at the parsonage twice, asking very politely when Miss Hartley will be returning home. Anne reads it three times, and smiles all the way back to Hampshire.

[long-pause]

{img:regency-london-1811-street-carriages-shops-gas-lamps-crowds-elegant}
In the winter, Mr Hartley has to go to London for a few days, on business for the church. And this time, he takes Anne with him. It is her first visit to London. They travel by stagecoach, and stay with an old friend of Mr Hartley's, who lives in a quiet street near Russell Square. For Anne, it is like entering another world.

[long-pause]

London in 1811 is the biggest city in the world. Almost a million people live there. It is enormous, noisy, crowded, dirty and exciting. The streets are full of carriages, carts, horses, sedan chairs and people on foot. Street sellers shout their wares. Church bells ring. Smoke from thousands of coal fires hangs over the city, making the air grey and foggy, and turning the buildings black.

[pause]

But there are also beautiful parts of London. The West End, where the rich and fashionable live, is full of elegant squares, with gardens in the middle, surrounded by tall, handsome houses. There are wide streets, with the finest shops in the world. And there is something very new, which amazes Anne. In some streets, the lamps are lit with gas. Bright, clear lights, much brighter than the old oil lamps, glowing in the winter dusk.

[pause]

{img:gas-lamps-pall-mall-1811-evening-glow-carriages-new-invention}
The first public street with gas lighting in London is Pall Mall, lit up only a few years before, in 1807. People came from all over the city to see it. It seemed like magic. A light that came through pipes, under the street, without oil or candles. Some people were frightened of it. They thought the pipes would explode, or that the gas was poisonous. But soon, more and more streets were lit by gas. And London became a city of light, at night.

[pause]

Anne and her father walk along the fashionable streets. Bond Street, with its jewellers and its tailors. Oxford Street, which is more than two kilometres long, and full of shops of every kind, with big glass windows, lit up at night, displaying beautiful things. Silks and muslins, china and glass, books and prints, hats and gloves. Anne has never seen so many beautiful things in her life.

[long-pause]

{img:hyde-park-1811-fashionable-carriages-riders-ladies-strolling-winter-sun}
In the afternoon, they go to Hyde Park, where the fashionable people of London go to ride and drive, between five and six o'clock. It is a great parade. Ladies in elegant carriages, with footmen behind. Gentlemen on fine horses. Dandies in their perfect coats and cravats, walking slowly, looking at everyone. It is like a great outdoor drawing room. Everyone is looking at everyone else.

[pause]

They visit a famous shop called Gunter's, in Berkeley Square. It is a confectioner's shop, famous for its ices. Even in winter, people come to eat ices at Gunter's. Ladies sit in their carriages outside, and the waiters run across the street to bring them their ices. Anne eats a lemon ice, and a little cake. Her father has a cup of chocolate. It is a wonderful treat.

[pause]

{img:hatchards-bookshop-piccadilly-1811-shelves-gentlemen-reading-fire}
They also visit a bookshop, in Piccadilly, called Hatchard's, which is one of the most famous bookshops in London. There are newspapers on a table, and a fire, and benches, where gentlemen sit and read. Mr Hartley buys a book of sermons, and Anne buys a book of poems. They go to see the great new British Museum, with its collection of ancient statues and curiosities. And on Sunday, they go to a service in Westminster Abbey, where the singing is so beautiful that Anne has tears in her eyes.

[long-pause]

London is exciting. But after a week, Anne is tired. Tired of the noise, the smoke, the crowds and the rush. She longs for the quiet lanes of Hampshire, the orchard, and the sound of the church clock. When the stagecoach finally leaves London, and the green fields appear again on both sides of the road, Anne leans back in her seat and sighs with happiness. London, she thinks, is a wonderful place to visit. But a quiet village is the place to live.

[long-pause]

{img:great-comet-1811-night-sky-long-glowing-tail-village-church-silhouette}
In the autumn of 1811, something extraordinary appears in the sky. A comet. A great, bright comet, with a long, glowing tail, stretching across the sky. It can be seen every clear night, for months, in the northern sky, after sunset. It is one of the brightest comets ever seen. People all over the world look up at it in wonder. And it is called the Great Comet of 1811.

[long-pause]

The comet was first seen in the spring, by an astronomer in France. At first, it was only a faint smudge of light, visible through a telescope. But slowly, through the summer, it came closer and became brighter. By September and October, it could be seen with the naked eye, by everyone. Its head was a bright, fuzzy star. Its tail was a long, curving veil of pale light, stretching across a large part of the sky.

[pause]

In the village, everyone talks about the comet. Every clear evening, after supper, people come out of their houses, and stand in their gardens, or on the village green, and look up at it. Old people say that comets are signs. Signs of war, or famine, or the death of a king. Some people are frightened. But Mr Hartley tells his family that a comet is just a natural thing. A ball of ice and dust, travelling around the sun, on its long journey through space.

[pause]

{img:family-standing-garden-night-looking-up-at-comet-father-pointing-telescope}
Mr Hartley has a small telescope, which he bought many years ago, because he loves the stars. On clear nights, he sets it up in the garden, and the whole family takes turns to look through it. Through the telescope, the comet looks even more beautiful. The bright head, with a kind of glowing cloud around it. And the long tail, streaming away, like hair blowing in the wind. Kitty says it looks like a ghost. Anne says it looks like a bride's veil.

[pause]

Mr Hartley explains that the comet's tail always points away from the sun. The sun's light pushes the dust and gas away from the comet's head, like a wind. He tells them that some comets come back every few years, or every few hundred years. But this one, the astronomers say, will not come back for thousands of years. So nobody alive today will ever see it again. It is a once-in-a-lifetime sight.

[long-pause]

{img:couple-walking-country-lane-night-comet-sky-lantern-autumn}
One evening in October, Mr Ellis, the young doctor, comes to the parsonage, to see Mr Hartley's telescope. He has heard about it, he says. He is very interested in astronomy. Mrs Hartley exchanges a look with her husband. And everyone goes out into the garden, in the cool autumn night. They look at the comet through the telescope, one by one. And then Mr Ellis and Anne stand a little apart from the others, by the orchard wall, looking up at the comet together, and talking quietly about the stars.

[pause]

The comet of 1811 was also connected, in people's minds, with something else. That year, there was a wonderful harvest of grapes in France, and the wine made that year was said to be the best in living memory. People called it the comet wine. Wine merchants proudly put a picture of the comet on the labels and the corks of their bottles, for years afterwards.

[pause]

{img:comet-fading-winter-sky-stars-quiet-village-snow-january-night}
Slowly, through the winter, the comet fades. Every night, it is a little smaller and a little fainter, as it travels away from the sun, into the darkness of space. By January, it can hardly be seen. And then, one night, it is gone. The sky is just the ordinary sky again, with its ordinary stars. But everyone who saw the comet remembers it for the rest of their lives.

[long-pause]

Anne remembers it too. Many years later, she will tell her grandchildren about the great comet of 1811, the year she went to her first ball, and read Sense and Sensibility, and visited Bath and London. And about the autumn night when she stood by the orchard wall, looking up at the comet, with the young doctor who made her laugh. And her grandchildren will look up at the ordinary stars, and try to imagine it.

[pause]

On the last night that the comet can be seen, Mr Hartley sets up his telescope one more time. The whole family takes a last look. A faint little smudge of light, very far away, going home into the dark. Kitty waves goodbye to it. And everyone laughs, and goes inside to the fire, a little sad that it is gone.

[long-pause]

{img:portsmouth-harbour-1811-warships-masts-sailors-quay-grey-sky}
In November, news arrives at the parsonage. William's ship, the Amelia, is coming home to Portsmouth, for repairs, after more than two years at sea. William will have a few weeks of leave. He writes that he will come home for Christmas. Mrs Hartley reads the letter, and bursts into tears of joy. Then she rushes around the house, ordering the cleaning of his room, the airing of his bed, and the baking of his favourite cake.

[long-pause]

Portsmouth is the great naval port of England, on the south coast of Hampshire, about fifty kilometres from the village. It is the home of the Royal Navy. Its harbour is full of warships. Great ships of the line, with three decks of guns, and fast frigates, and little sloops and cutters. There are dockyards, where ships are built and repaired, with thousands of workers. And the town is full of sailors, officers, shopkeepers and taverns.

[pause]

Mr Hartley and Anne go to Portsmouth, to meet William. They travel in the little carriage, through the countryside, and over the hills, until they see the sea, grey and shining, and the harbour full of masts, like a forest of bare trees. The air smells of salt, tar and seaweed. Gulls are crying. And everywhere there are sailors, in their short blue jackets and wide trousers, with their hair in pigtails, rolling along the streets.

[pause]

{img:young-naval-officer-blue-coat-embracing-father-sister-quay-portsmouth}
They wait on the quay, as the boats come in from the ships anchored out in the harbour. And then Anne sees him. A tall young man in a blue naval coat, with gold buttons and a white collar, his face brown from the Mediterranean sun. He jumps out of the boat, and runs up the steps, and embraces his father, and then lifts Anne off her feet and swings her round, as he used to do when she was a little girl. Everybody on the quay smiles.

[pause]

William has changed. He was a boy of eighteen when he left. Now he is a man of twenty, broad-shouldered and confident, with a deeper voice. But his smile is the same. On the way home, in the carriage, he talks without stopping. About Malta, with its golden stone and its great harbour. About Gibraltar, with its rock and its monkeys. About Sicily, and Naples, and the islands of Greece. About storms and calms, dolphins and whales.

[long-pause]

{img:naval-officer-telling-stories-family-fireside-parsonage-evening-candles}
At home, Mrs Hartley weeps again, and hugs him, and then makes him eat three pieces of his favourite cake. Kitty hangs on to his arm and will not let go. The servants come out to greet him. Old Thomas shakes his hand. Mrs Platt the cook cries into her apron. And in the evening, by the fire, the whole family sits around him, and he tells them about his life at sea.

[pause]

He tells them about life on board a warship. The small, dark cabin he shares with another lieutenant. The food, salted meat and hard biscuits, and the occasional treat of fresh fish or fruit from a port. The watches, four hours on and four hours off, day and night. The beauty of the sea at sunset. And the stars in the middle of the ocean, so many and so bright that the sky seems white with them. He does not talk much about the battles. Only that the ship came through safely, and that he is glad to be home.

[pause]

{img:navy-ship-at-sea-mediterranean-blue-water-sails-dolphins-sunny}
In Jane Austen's novels, naval officers are often the heroes. Brave, honest, good-humoured and kind. In Persuasion, she describes their warmth, their friendliness and their love of home. She knew them well, from her brothers Francis and Charles, who were both naval officers, and both, much later, became admirals. She was very proud of them. And she worried about them, as every family with a son at sea worried.

[long-pause]

On his first night home, William sleeps in his own old room, in his own old bed, with the window open, so that he can hear the sounds of the countryside. The wind in the trees. An owl in the churchyard. The church clock striking the hours. No waves, no creaking timbers, no ship's bell. He tells Anne the next morning that it was the quietest night of his life. And that he slept like a log for twelve hours, for the first time in two years.

[long-pause]

{img:regency-christmas-parsonage-holly-ivy-decorations-fireplace-candles}
Christmas comes to the parsonage. In the Regency, Christmas is a time for family, church, food and fun. It lasts for twelve days, from Christmas Day until Twelfth Night, on the fifth of January. The house is decorated with greenery. Holly, ivy, laurel and mistletoe, brought in from the garden and the woods. There are no Christmas trees yet in most English homes. They will become popular later, in the time of Queen Victoria.

[long-pause]

On Christmas morning, the family goes to church. It is Mr Hartley's busiest day of the year. The old church is decorated with holly and ivy, and the bells ring out over the frosty fields. The whole village comes, in their best clothes. The rich families from the great house sit in their private pews, at the front. The farmers and the villagers sit behind. The choir sings carols, accompanied by a little band of village musicians, with a fiddle, a flute and a cello.

[pause]

After church, there is Christmas dinner. It is a great feast. Roast beef, or a goose, or a turkey. Mince pies, which in the Regency still contain real meat, mixed with fruit, sugar and spices. And plum pudding, a heavy, rich pudding of flour, suet, dried fruit, sugar and spices, boiled in a cloth for hours. It is brought to the table with a sprig of holly on top. Everyone eats far too much. Even William, who has had nothing but ship's biscuits for two years.

[pause]

{img:christmas-dinner-regency-family-plum-pudding-holly-goose-candles-laughter}
Christmas is also a time of kindness to the poor. The rich families of the parish give food, coal and warm clothes to the poor families. Mrs Hartley and the girls have been sewing flannel petticoats and woollen stockings for weeks, for the poor children of the village. On Saint Thomas's Day, just before Christmas, the poor women of the village go from house to house, and the better-off families give them money, food or flour, for their Christmas baking.

[pause]

During the twelve days of Christmas, there are parties and visits. Neighbours visit each other, and there are dinners, card parties, and little dances in the drawing room, with the furniture pushed back against the walls. There are games, like blind man's buff, hunt the slipper, and snapdragon, where you must pick raisins out of a dish of burning brandy, without burning your fingers. Kitty loves snapdragon. William is very good at it.

[long-pause]

{img:twelfth-night-party-cake-crown-characters-cards-regency-drawing-room}
The best party of all is on Twelfth Night, the last night of Christmas. There is a special cake, the Twelfth Cake, a big, rich fruit cake, covered with white icing, and decorated with sugar figures. Hidden inside the cake, there is a bean and a pea. Whoever finds the bean in their slice becomes the king for the night. Whoever finds the pea becomes the queen. And everyone else draws a card with a funny character on it, and must play that character all evening.

[pause]

The Hartleys give a Twelfth Night party for their friends and neighbours. Mr Ellis, the young doctor, is invited. The cake is cut. And to everyone's delight, Mr Ellis finds the bean, and Anne finds the pea. So they are the king and queen of Twelfth Night. Everyone cheers. Kitty says that it is fate. Anne blushes. And Mr Ellis, as king, orders that Anne must sing a song. So she sings, at the pianoforte, a little Scottish song, while everyone listens.

[pause]

{img:young-woman-singing-at-pianoforte-candlelight-guests-listening-young-man-watching}
Jane Austen loved Christmas too. In her letters, she wrote about Christmas parties, dances and visits, and about the games and the food. In her novel Emma, there is a famous Christmas Eve dinner party, during a snowstorm. And in Mansfield Park, the young people play a card game called speculation, one winter evening, very noisily, and enjoy themselves very much.

[long-pause]

When the last guests have gone home, on Twelfth Night, the family sits together by the fire, tired and happy. William is going back to his ship in a few days. Mrs Hartley does not want to think about it. But tonight, everyone is together. The fire is warm. The candles are burning low. Outside, the frost is sparkling on the lawn. And the church clock strikes midnight, softly, over the sleeping village.

[long-pause]

{img:early-spring-orchard-parsonage-blossom-young-couple-walking-path}
The winter passes. William goes back to his ship, with a big parcel of his mother's cake and a new pair of woollen stockings from Kitty. The snowdrops come out, and then the primroses. The days get longer. And in March 1812, almost a year after Anne's first ball, something happens in the orchard of the parsonage.

[long-pause]

All winter, Mr Ellis has been visiting the parsonage. At first, once a week. Then twice. He comes to talk to Mr Hartley about books, or to borrow the telescope, or to bring Mrs Hartley some medicine for her headaches, or to ask Kitty about her birds' nests. Everyone knows why he really comes. Even old Thomas, who grumbles that the young doctor's horse eats more of the parsonage's hay than their own horse does.

[pause]

In the Regency, a gentleman who wants to marry a young lady must first be sure that she likes him. And then, usually, he asks her himself. If she says yes, he goes to her father, to ask for his consent. The father wants to know if the young man is respectable, and whether he has enough money to support a wife. A young doctor does not earn very much at first. But he is respectable, and he works hard, and the old doctor is going to retire soon, and leave him his practice.

[pause]

{img:young-man-proposing-orchard-bench-blossom-young-woman-surprised-happy}
On a sunny afternoon in March, Anne is sitting on the bench in the orchard, reading. The apple trees are just beginning to show their first blossoms. The blackbirds are singing. And Mr Ellis comes walking through the gate, with his hat in his hand, looking more nervous than Anne has ever seen him. He sits down beside her on the bench. He looks at his hat for a long time. And then he begins to speak.

[pause]

He does not make a long, fine speech, like the heroes in the novels. He says, simply, that he has loved her since the evening of the ball, when she laughed out loud at his joke about the cello player's wig. That he has very little money, and a small house, and a great deal of work. But that he would try to make her happy, every day of her life, if she would let him. And then he stops, and waits, and looks at her with his crooked smile.

[long-pause]

{img:father-shaking-hands-young-doctor-study-books-approval-warm-light}
Anne says yes. Of course she says yes. She has been waiting for this moment all winter. Mr Ellis jumps up, and drops his hat, and picks it up, and drops it again. Then he goes into the house, to speak to Mr Hartley in his study. Anne sits on the bench, in the sunshine, with her heart beating fast. Kitty, who has been watching the whole thing from the top of an apple tree, climbs down and runs to hug her.

[pause]

Mr Hartley gives his consent at once. He likes the young doctor very much. Mrs Hartley cries, and laughs, and starts planning the wedding immediately. They will be married in the summer, in the old church, by Mr Hartley himself. And they will live in the little house in the town, next to the doctor's surgery, with a small garden at the back, where Anne can plant an apple tree.

[pause]

{img:letter-writing-good-news-family-desk-joy-sealing-wax-spring}
That evening, Anne writes a long letter to her cousin Charlotte, in Kent, crossed in both directions, telling her everything. And Mrs Hartley writes to William, at sea, and to her sister in Bath. And the next day, when the Hartleys go to tea at Chawton, Miss Jane Austen looks at Anne's face, and smiles, and says that she believes there is news. Anne blushes and nods. And Miss Austen squeezes her hand, and asks, with a twinkle in her eye, whether he makes her laugh.

[long-pause]

Anne says that he does. Every day. Miss Austen says that she is very glad. And that, in her experience, that is the best foundation for a happy marriage that she knows. Then she turns to pour the tea, and talks about other things. But Anne sees that she is smiling to herself. And she wonders, not for the first time, what Miss Austen writes at her little table by the window, when nobody is looking.

[long-pause]

{img:pride-and-prejudice-first-edition-1813-books-library-table-later}
In January 1813, Jane Austen's second novel was published. It was called Pride and Prejudice. It is the story of Elizabeth Bennet, a lively, clever young woman, and Mr Darcy, a proud, rich gentleman. At first, they dislike each other very much. But slowly, they learn to understand each other, and to overcome their pride and their prejudice. It became the most loved of all her novels. Jane called it her own darling child.

[long-pause]

After Pride and Prejudice came Mansfield Park, in 1814, and Emma, in 1815. The Prince Regent himself was a great admirer of her novels, and kept a set of them in each of his houses. He even invited her to visit his library, and suggested that she dedicate her next novel to him. Jane did not like the prince very much. But one does not say no to the Prince Regent. So Emma was dedicated to him.

[pause]

Her books were published anonymously, by A Lady, or by the Author of Sense and Sensibility. But more and more people knew who she was. She earned some money from her books, though not very much. About six hundred pounds in her whole life. And she went on living quietly in the cottage at Chawton, writing at her little table, walking to Alton, and playing with her nephews and nieces.

[pause]

{img:winchester-college-street-house-1817-quiet-window-cathedral-close}
In 1816, Jane began to feel ill. She grew weaker and weaker. Nobody quite knows what her illness was. In May 1817, Cassandra took her to Winchester, to be near a good doctor. They stayed in a house in College Street, near the cathedral. And there, on the eighteenth of July 1817, Jane Austen passed away, peacefully, with her head on a pillow on Cassandra's lap. She was only forty-one years old.

[pause]

She was buried in Winchester Cathedral. On her gravestone, there is a long inscription about her kindness, her faith and her goodness. But it does not mention that she was a writer at all. Two of her novels, Northanger Abbey and Persuasion, were published after her death, at the end of 1817. And in them, for the first time, her brother Henry wrote a short biography, telling the world who the lady was.

[long-pause]

{img:jane-austen-house-museum-chawton-today-garden-visitors-later}
The Regency came to an end in 1820, when old King George the Third passed away, and the Prince Regent became King George the Fourth. He reigned for ten years. Then came his brother, William the Fourth, and then, in 1837, his young niece Victoria. And the Regency world, of muslin dresses and country dances, slowly faded into the Victorian age, with its steam trains, factories and crinolines.

[pause]

But Jane Austen's novels did not fade. Slowly, over the nineteenth century, more and more people read them, and loved them. In the twentieth century, they became among the most loved books in the world. They have been translated into dozens of languages. They have been made into films and television series, again and again. Millions of people, all over the world, read them every year. And many people dream of living in the gentle world of Regency England, with its balls and its country houses, its letters and its walks.

[pause]

{img:anne-older-woman-telling-stories-grandchildren-orchard-summer-later}
As for Anne and Mr Ellis, they were married in the summer of 1812, in the old church, by Anne's father. They lived happily in their little house, with its apple tree. They had five children, who were all very good at making people laugh. William became a captain in the navy. Kitty married a farmer, and kept a great many animals. And Anne lived to be a very old woman, and told her grandchildren about the great comet, and the first ball, and the lady in the little cap who wrote novels in secret.

[long-pause]

{img:jane-austens-house-chawton-today-red-brick-garden-flowers-visitors}
Today, the little red-brick house in Chawton, where Jane Austen wrote her novels, is a museum. It is called Jane Austen's House. Visitors come from all over the world to see it. They walk through the rooms where she lived, with her mother, her sister and her friend Martha. And they stand in front of the little table, by the window, in the dining room, where she wrote.

[long-pause]

The table is still there. The same tiny, twelve-sided table, made of walnut wood, with room for just a few sheets of paper and an inkpot. Visitors stand around it, very quietly. It is hard to believe that on this little table, so small and so simple, some of the most famous novels in the world were written. Pride and Prejudice. Emma. Persuasion. On a table no bigger than a tray.

[pause]

In the house, there are other things that belonged to the family. A lock of Jane's hair. A little bracelet. A patchwork quilt, made by Jane, her mother and Cassandra together, with hundreds of tiny diamond-shaped pieces of fabric. Letters, in her small, neat handwriting. First editions of her novels. And the music books, with the songs and pieces she copied out by hand, and played every morning on the pianoforte, before breakfast.

[pause]

{img:patchwork-quilt-diamond-pieces-display-museum-soft-light-later}
In the garden, there are flowers and herbs, like the ones the family grew. The old bakehouse is still there, where the bread was baked. And the visitors can walk out of the gate, and along the lane, past the pond, to Chawton House, the great house that belonged to Jane's brother Edward. It is now a library and a centre for the study of early women writers. Its gardens and its library are open to visitors.

[pause]

Every year, in September, there is a great Jane Austen Festival in Bath. Hundreds of people come, from all over the world, dressed in Regency costume. Ladies in muslin gowns and bonnets, gentlemen in tailcoats, cravats and top hats. They walk in a great procession through the streets of Bath, past the Royal Crescent and the Circus, to the Pump Room. There are balls, with country dances, concerts, talks and walks. For a few days, Bath looks almost exactly as it did in 1811.

[long-pause]

{img:jane-austen-festival-bath-procession-regency-costumes-crowds-sunny-today}
In 2017, two hundred years after her death, Jane Austen's portrait was put on the English ten-pound note. Under her picture, there is a quotation from Pride and Prejudice. I declare after all there is no enjoyment like reading! It is a slightly funny choice, because in the novel, the character who says it is not really interested in reading at all. She is only pretending, to impress Mr Darcy. Jane Austen would have laughed.

[pause]

Her novels are read by millions of people, in schools, universities and at home. They have been made into films and television series many times. Each generation has its own Elizabeth Bennet and its own Mr Darcy. There are also countless books inspired by her novels, sequels, retellings and modern versions. People write about her, talk about her, and dress up as her characters. She has become one of the most loved writers in the world.

[pause]

{img:reader-curled-up-armchair-reading-austen-novel-tea-rainy-window-today}
Why do people still love Jane Austen, more than two hundred years later? Perhaps because her novels are funny, wise and kind. Because her characters feel so real, as if they were people we know. Because they are about love and family and friendship, which never change. And perhaps because her world, the quiet world of villages and parsonages, of walks and letters and country dances, is such a peaceful and pleasant place to visit, especially at the end of a long day.

[long-pause]

And in Winchester Cathedral, there is still her gravestone, in the floor of the north aisle, with its long inscription that does not mention her books. Next to it, on the wall, there is a brass plaque, added much later, which says that she is known to many by her writings. And a stained glass window, in her memory. People come to stand there, and leave flowers. And sometimes, a little note, saying thank you.

[long-pause]

{img:royal-pavilion-brighton-domes-minarets-gardens-sunny-today-visitors}
Regency England is still there to visit too, in many places. In Brighton, on the south coast, there is the Royal Pavilion, the fantastic seaside palace of the Prince Regent. With its domes and minarets on the outside, like a palace in India. And on the inside, rooms decorated in a Chinese style, with dragons, lotus flowers and bamboo. The great banqueting room has a chandelier hanging from the claws of a huge silver dragon. Visitors stare up at it, with open mouths.

[long-pause]

In Bath, the Pump Room is still open. You can still drink a glass of the warm spa water, which still tastes of iron. And you can have tea and cakes, while a little trio plays music, as they did in 1811. The Assembly Rooms are still there, with their great ballroom and their crystal chandeliers, the very same ones that hung there in Jane Austen's time. And the Roman baths, below, steam gently in the open air.

[pause]

The Royal Crescent still curves across its hillside, golden in the evening sun. One of its houses, Number One, is a museum, furnished exactly as a house of the time would have been, with its drawing room, its dining room, its bedrooms and its kitchen. You can see how a family of the Regency lived, from the elegant rooms upstairs to the busy kitchen below.

[pause]

{img:no-1-royal-crescent-museum-drawing-room-regency-furniture-today}
Many of the great country houses of the time are open to visitors. Their grand rooms, their libraries, their portrait galleries. And their parks, designed by Capability Brown and Humphry Repton, with their lakes, temples and woods. Some of them have been used as Pemberley, or other houses, in the films of Jane Austen's novels. Visitors walk around the lakes, and look at the houses from the bend in the drive, and imagine Elizabeth Bennet seeing them for the first time.

[pause]

In Lyme Regis, on the coast of Dorset, there is a long stone harbour wall, curving out into the sea, called the Cobb. In Jane Austen's Persuasion, a famous scene happens there. Jane visited Lyme Regis herself, in 1803 and 1804, and loved it. Visitors still walk along the Cobb, in the wind, and look at the sea, and the cliffs, where people have found fossils of ancient sea creatures for two hundred years.

[long-pause]

{img:the-cobb-lyme-regis-stone-harbour-wall-sea-waves-cliffs-today}
And the countryside of Hampshire is still there. The green hills, the woods, the winding lanes with their high hedges, full of flowers in spring. The little villages with their old churches and their cottages. At Steventon, where Jane was born, the rectory is gone, but the old church still stands, small and grey, in a quiet field, with an ancient yew tree beside it. Visitors walk the lanes that she walked, and look at the fields that she knew.

[pause]

Many of the parsonages of Regency England are still standing, in villages all over the country. Some are still homes for vicars. Others have become private houses. They are often the most beautiful houses in the village, with their Georgian windows, their old gardens and their orchards. When you walk past one, on a quiet afternoon, you can almost imagine a young woman sitting on a bench under the apple trees, reading a novel, and waiting for someone to come through the gate.

[pause]

{img:hampshire-country-lane-today-hedgerows-spring-flowers-old-church}
And everywhere in England, in tea rooms and in drawing rooms, people still drink tea in the afternoon, with cakes, and talk about the weather and the neighbours, just as they did in 1811. Some things change very slowly. And perhaps that is part of the charm of Jane Austen's world. It is not so very far away from our own.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, all over the world, people sit down with one of her novels, and open it at the first page. It is a truth universally acknowledged, begins Pride and Prejudice, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife. And from that first sentence, they are back in the world of muslin dresses and country dances, of morning calls and long walks, of letters and parsonages and balls. And they are happy to be there.

[long-pause]

{img:parsonage-evening-1811-sunset-orchard-church-tower-warm-window-light}
So we leave Regency England tonight, in the year 1811, as the evening falls. The sun is setting over the fields of Hampshire. The sky turns pink and gold, and then soft blue. The rooks are flying home to their trees, calling to each other. And the church clock strikes six, slowly and softly, over the quiet village.

[long-pause]

In the parsonage, the candles are being lit in the drawing room. The fire is crackling. Mrs Hartley is sewing, by the light of a candle. Mr Hartley is reading, with his spectacles on the end of his nose. Kitty is curled up on the sofa, with a book about birds. And Anne is at the pianoforte, playing a gentle little tune, very softly, so as not to disturb anyone.

[pause]

{img:young-woman-playing-pianoforte-candlelight-drawing-room-family-quiet}
In the kitchen, Mrs Platt is preparing a light supper. Sally is warming the beds with a copper warming pan, full of hot coals. Old Thomas is checking that the horse is comfortable for the night. And outside, in the dark garden, an owl is calling from the orchard, where the apple trees are standing quietly, waiting for spring.
