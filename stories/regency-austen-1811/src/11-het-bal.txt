In April, there is a ball at the assembly rooms in the nearby town. It is Anne's first ball. She has been thinking about it for weeks. Her new dress is ready. Her gloves are washed. Her slippers are new. And on the evening of the ball, she sits in front of the mirror, while her mother arranges her curls and ties the green ribbon in her hair. Kitty sits on the bed, watching, and wishing she were old enough to go.

--

The assembly rooms are a big hall, above the inn, in the market town. Once a month, during the winter and spring, there is a public ball there. Anyone who is respectable, and who pays for a ticket, can come. The families of the gentry come from all the villages around, in their carriages. The farmers' families, the doctors, the lawyers and the officers of the regiment that is stationed in the town. It is the great social event of the month.

The Hartleys arrive at about eight o'clock, in their little carriage, driven by old Thomas. The hall is already full of light, music and people. Chandeliers hang from the ceiling, full of candles. Along the walls, there are benches, where the mothers and the older ladies sit and watch. At one end, in a little gallery, there are the musicians. A violin, a cello and a pianoforte. And in the middle of the room, the young people are dancing.

{img:young-woman-first-ball-nervous-mother-beside-her-bench-candlelit-hall}
In 1811, the most popular dances are the country dances. The dancers stand in two long lines, the gentlemen on one side, the ladies on the other, facing their partners. Then the first couple dances down the line, weaving between the others, and each couple in turn follows them. It is a lively, cheerful dance, with lots of skipping, turning and changing places. A single dance can last half an hour, if the line is long.

Dancing is a chance to talk. While you are dancing, you can have a little conversation with your partner, in the moments when you are standing still, waiting for your turn. It is one of the few times when a young man and a young woman can talk privately, without their parents listening. So dancing is very important for falling in love. In Jane Austen's novels, the most important conversations often happen on the dance floor.

--

{img:young-gentleman-bowing-asking-young-woman-to-dance-ballroom-polite}
At first, Anne sits with her mother on the bench, and watches. She is nervous. What if nobody asks her to dance? Then the master of ceremonies brings over a young man, and introduces him. In the Regency, a gentleman cannot ask a lady to dance unless they have been properly introduced. The young man bows, and asks if he may have the honour of the next dance. Anne blushes, and says yes.

His name is Mr Henry Ellis. He is twenty-four years old. He is a young doctor, who has just come to the town, to work with the old doctor, who is getting tired. He is not very tall, nor very handsome. He has brown hair that sticks up at the back, and a slightly crooked smile. But he is very easy to talk to. And while they dance, he says something so funny about the cello player's wig that Anne laughs out loud, in the middle of the dance.

{img:couple-dancing-laughing-country-dance-line-candlelight-ball}
After that, Anne dances every dance. With the vicar's son from the next parish. With a young officer of the regiment, in a red coat, who talks only about himself. With an old friend of her father's, who dances very slowly. And with Mr Ellis, twice. Which is the most that a gentleman may dance with the same lady, at one ball, without people starting to talk. There is a supper, at midnight, of cold chicken, ham, jellies and cakes. And then more dancing, until two in the morning.

--

In the carriage, on the way home, in the dark, Anne is very quiet. Her mother asks her if she enjoyed the ball. Anne says yes, very much. Her mother asks which of her partners she liked best. Anne thinks for a moment. And then she remembers the advice of Miss Austen, in the garden at Chawton. And she smiles to herself, in the darkness of the carriage, and says that she liked the one who made her laugh.
