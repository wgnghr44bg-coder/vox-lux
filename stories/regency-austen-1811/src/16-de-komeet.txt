In the autumn of 1811, something extraordinary appears in the sky. A comet. A great, bright comet, with a long, glowing tail, stretching across the sky. It can be seen every clear night, for months, in the northern sky, after sunset. It is one of the brightest comets ever seen. People all over the world look up at it in wonder. And it is called the Great Comet of 1811.

--

The comet was first seen in the spring, by an astronomer in France. At first, it was only a faint smudge of light, visible through a telescope. But slowly, through the summer, it came closer and became brighter. By September and October, it could be seen with the naked eye, by everyone. Its head was a bright, fuzzy star. Its tail was a long, curving veil of pale light, stretching across a large part of the sky.

In the village, everyone talks about the comet. Every clear evening, after supper, people come out of their houses, and stand in their gardens, or on the village green, and look up at it. Old people say that comets are signs. Signs of war, or famine, or the death of a king. Some people are frightened. But Mr Hartley tells his family that a comet is just a natural thing. A ball of ice and dust, travelling around the sun, on its long journey through space.

{img:family-standing-garden-night-looking-up-at-comet-father-pointing-telescope}
Mr Hartley has a small telescope, which he bought many years ago, because he loves the stars. On clear nights, he sets it up in the garden, and the whole family takes turns to look through it. Through the telescope, the comet looks even more beautiful. The bright head, with a kind of glowing cloud around it. And the long tail, streaming away, like hair blowing in the wind. Kitty says it looks like a ghost. Anne says it looks like a bride's veil.

Mr Hartley explains that the comet's tail always points away from the sun. The sun's light pushes the dust and gas away from the comet's head, like a wind. He tells them that some comets come back every few years, or every few hundred years. But this one, the astronomers say, will not come back for thousands of years. So nobody alive today will ever see it again. It is a once-in-a-lifetime sight.

--

{img:couple-walking-country-lane-night-comet-sky-lantern-autumn}
One evening in October, Mr Ellis, the young doctor, comes to the parsonage, to see Mr Hartley's telescope. He has heard about it, he says. He is very interested in astronomy. Mrs Hartley exchanges a look with her husband. And everyone goes out into the garden, in the cool autumn night. They look at the comet through the telescope, one by one. And then Mr Ellis and Anne stand a little apart from the others, by the orchard wall, looking up at the comet together, and talking quietly about the stars.

The comet of 1811 was also connected, in people's minds, with something else. That year, there was a wonderful harvest of grapes in France, and the wine made that year was said to be the best in living memory. People called it the comet wine. Wine merchants proudly put a picture of the comet on the labels and the corks of their bottles, for years afterwards.

{img:comet-fading-winter-sky-stars-quiet-village-snow-january-night}
Slowly, through the winter, the comet fades. Every night, it is a little smaller and a little fainter, as it travels away from the sun, into the darkness of space. By January, it can hardly be seen. And then, one night, it is gone. The sky is just the ordinary sky again, with its ordinary stars. But everyone who saw the comet remembers it for the rest of their lives.

--

Anne remembers it too. Many years later, she will tell her grandchildren about the great comet of 1811, the year she went to her first ball, and read Sense and Sensibility, and visited Bath and London. And about the autumn night when she stood by the orchard wall, looking up at the comet, with the young doctor who made her laugh. And her grandchildren will look up at the ordinary stars, and try to imagine it.

On the last night that the comet can be seen, Mr Hartley sets up his telescope one more time. The whole family takes a last look. A faint little smudge of light, very far away, going home into the dark. Kitty waves goodbye to it. And everyone laughs, and goes inside to the fire, a little sad that it is gone.
