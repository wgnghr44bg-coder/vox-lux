Regency England is still there to visit too, in many places. In Brighton, on the south coast, there is the Royal Pavilion, the fantastic seaside palace of the Prince Regent. With its domes and minarets on the outside, like a palace in India. And on the inside, rooms decorated in a Chinese style, with dragons, lotus flowers and bamboo. The great banqueting room has a chandelier hanging from the claws of a huge silver dragon. Visitors stare up at it, with open mouths.

--

In Bath, the Pump Room is still open. You can still drink a glass of the warm spa water, which still tastes of iron. And you can have tea and cakes, while a little trio plays music, as they did in 1811. The Assembly Rooms are still there, with their great ballroom and their crystal chandeliers, the very same ones that hung there in Jane Austen's time. And the Roman baths, below, steam gently in the open air.

The Royal Crescent still curves across its hillside, golden in the evening sun. One of its houses, Number One, is a museum, furnished exactly as a house of the time would have been, with its drawing room, its dining room, its bedrooms and its kitchen. You can see how a family of the Regency lived, from the elegant rooms upstairs to the busy kitchen below.

{img:no-1-royal-crescent-museum-drawing-room-regency-furniture-today}
Many of the great country houses of the time are open to visitors. Their grand rooms, their libraries, their portrait galleries. And their parks, designed by Capability Brown and Humphry Repton, with their lakes, temples and woods. Some of them have been used as Pemberley, or other houses, in the films of Jane Austen's novels. Visitors walk around the lakes, and look at the houses from the bend in the drive, and imagine Elizabeth Bennet seeing them for the first time.

In Lyme Regis, on the coast of Dorset, there is a long stone harbour wall, curving out into the sea, called the Cobb. In Jane Austen's Persuasion, a famous scene happens there. Jane visited Lyme Regis herself, in 1803 and 1804, and loved it. Visitors still walk along the Cobb, in the wind, and look at the sea, and the cliffs, where people have found fossils of ancient sea creatures for two hundred years.

--

{img:the-cobb-lyme-regis-stone-harbour-wall-sea-waves-cliffs-today}
And the countryside of Hampshire is still there. The green hills, the woods, the winding lanes with their high hedges, full of flowers in spring. The little villages with their old churches and their cottages. At Steventon, where Jane was born, the rectory is gone, but the old church still stands, small and grey, in a quiet field, with an ancient yew tree beside it. Visitors walk the lanes that she walked, and look at the fields that she knew.

Many of the parsonages of Regency England are still standing, in villages all over the country. Some are still homes for vicars. Others have become private houses. They are often the most beautiful houses in the village, with their Georgian windows, their old gardens and their orchards. When you walk past one, on a quiet afternoon, you can almost imagine a young woman sitting on a bench under the apple trees, reading a novel, and waiting for someone to come through the gate.

{img:hampshire-country-lane-today-hedgerows-spring-flowers-old-church}
And everywhere in England, in tea rooms and in drawing rooms, people still drink tea in the afternoon, with cakes, and talk about the weather and the neighbours, just as they did in 1811. Some things change very slowly. And perhaps that is part of the charm of Jane Austen's world. It is not so very far away from our own.

--

In the evenings, all over the world, people sit down with one of her novels, and open it at the first page. It is a truth universally acknowledged, begins Pride and Prejudice, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife. And from that first sentence, they are back in the world of muslin dresses and country dances, of morning calls and long walks, of letters and parsonages and balls. And they are happy to be there.
