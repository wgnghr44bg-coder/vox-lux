Bath is a city made for pleasure. For almost two thousand years, people have come here for the hot springs, which bubble up out of the ground, warm and full of minerals. The Romans built great baths here, and a temple. And in the eighteenth century, Bath became the most fashionable place in England, where rich people came to drink the waters, bathe, dance, gamble, shop and meet each other.

--

The city is built of golden stone, from the hills nearby. The streets are elegant and orderly, with long terraces of houses, all the same, with columns and tall windows. The most famous is the Royal Crescent, a great curving terrace of thirty houses, looking out over a wide lawn. There is also the Circus, a circle of houses around a round open space. Walking through Bath is like walking through a beautiful painting.

Every morning, Anne goes with her aunt to the Pump Room. It is a large, elegant hall, next to the old Roman baths. Here, the visitors come to drink the waters of the hot spring, which are said to be good for the health. A man pumps the warm water from a fountain, and serves it in glasses. It is warm, and tastes rather unpleasant, of iron. But everyone drinks it, and says that they feel much better.

{img:pump-room-bath-elegant-hall-people-drinking-water-glasses-musicians}
But the real reason people come to the Pump Room is to see and be seen. While they drink their glass of water, they walk up and down the room, greeting their friends, looking at each other's clothes, and gossiping. There is music, from a little orchestra. And there is a big book, in which new arrivals write their names, so that everyone knows who has come to town. Anne's aunt reads the book every morning, very carefully.

In the afternoons, there is shopping. Bath has wonderful shops, in its elegant streets. Milliners, selling bonnets and ribbons. Drapers, selling silk, muslin and lace. Jewellers, booksellers, confectioners and perfumers. Anne and her aunt walk up and down Milsom Street, looking in the windows. Her aunt buys her a new bonnet, of straw, trimmed with green ribbon, to match her dress. Anne is delighted.

--

{img:bath-assembly-rooms-ballroom-evening-chandeliers-dancing-crowd}
In the evenings, there are concerts, plays at the theatre, and balls at the Upper Assembly Rooms. The Assembly Rooms are grand and beautiful, with an enormous ballroom, lit by great crystal chandeliers, a tea room, and a card room. On ball nights, hundreds of people come. It is very crowded and hot. In Jane Austen's novel Northanger Abbey, the young heroine goes to her first ball here, and the crowd is so thick that she cannot see anything but the feathers of the ladies' hats.

Jane Austen herself lived in Bath for five years, from 1801 to 1806. She did not much like it. She found it hot and glaring in summer, and full of silly people. But she used it very well in her novels. Two of her novels, Northanger Abbey and Persuasion, have many scenes in Bath. The Pump Room, the Assembly Rooms, the shops of Milsom Street, the walks along the river. All of them are there, just as she saw them.

{img:young-woman-walking-river-avon-bath-pulteney-bridge-autumn-afternoon}
Anne enjoys Bath very much. But not for the balls or the shops. She enjoys the walks, along the river and up into the hills, from where she can see the whole golden city below. She enjoys the library, where she reads the newspapers. And she enjoys the old Roman baths, which she visits with her aunt, and where she looks at the ancient stones, and thinks about the Romans who bathed there, so long ago.

--

After three weeks, it is time to go home. Her aunt gives her a little present, a book of poems by Walter Scott, called The Lady of the Lake, which everyone is reading this year. And on the last morning, a letter arrives from her mother. It says that Mr Ellis, the young doctor, has called at the parsonage twice, asking very politely when Miss Hartley will be returning home. Anne reads it three times, and smiles all the way back to Hampshire.
