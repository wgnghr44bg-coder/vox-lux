Jane Austen was born in December 1775, in a village called Steventon, in Hampshire. Her father, George Austen, was the vicar of the village. He was a kind, clever, scholarly man, who also taught boys in his house, to earn a little more money. Her mother, Cassandra, was lively and witty, and wrote funny little poems. Jane was the seventh of eight children. Six boys and two girls.

--

The Austen family was not rich. But it was a happy, busy, clever family. The house at Steventon was full of children, books and laughter. They read novels aloud to each other in the evenings, and laughed at the silly ones. They put on plays in the barn, for the family and the neighbours. And Jane, from the age of about twelve, began to write. Funny little stories, full of absurd characters and wild adventures, to amuse her family.

Jane's closest friend, all her life, was her sister Cassandra, who was nearly three years older. They shared a bedroom. They told each other everything. When they were apart, they wrote long letters to each other, full of news and jokes. Many of Jane's letters to Cassandra still survive. They are lively, funny and sometimes sharp. They tell us a great deal about Jane's life, and her world.

{img:two-sisters-regency-sharing-letter-window-seat-laughing-bedroom}
As a young woman, Jane went to balls and parties, in the houses of the neighbourhood, and at the assembly rooms in the nearby town of Basingstoke. She loved dancing. She had a little romance with a young Irishman, called Tom Lefroy, when she was twenty. But he went away, and they never met again. Later, she received a proposal of marriage from a rich young man, and accepted it. But the next morning, she changed her mind. She did not love him. And she never married.

In her early twenties, at Steventon, Jane wrote the first versions of three novels. One was called Elinor and Marianne. One was called First Impressions. And one was called Susan. Her father liked First Impressions so much that he offered it to a publisher. But the publisher refused, without even reading it. Jane put the manuscripts away. And for a long time, she wrote very little.

--

{img:georgian-bath-street-terraced-houses-golden-stone-carriage-rainy-day}
In 1801, her father retired, and the family moved to Bath. Jane did not like Bath very much. She missed the countryside. Then, in 1805, her father passed away. It was a great sorrow, and also a great difficulty. Without him, Jane, her mother and Cassandra had very little money. They moved from one rented house to another, staying with relatives, for several years.

At last, in 1809, one of Jane's brothers, Edward, helped them. Edward had been adopted, as a boy, by rich relatives, and had inherited their great estates, in Kent and in Hampshire. One of these estates was at the village of Chawton. And in the village, there was a small house, which Edward offered to his mother and his sisters, to live in, for as long as they liked.

{img:chawton-cottage-red-brick-house-roadside-garden-flowers-1811}
So in July 1809, Jane, Cassandra, their mother and their friend Martha Lloyd moved into the house at Chawton. And there, at last, Jane found peace. She had a home of her own, in the countryside she loved, near her family. And she began to write again. She took out her old manuscripts, and began to rewrite them. Elinor and Marianne became Sense and Sensibility. First Impressions became Pride and Prejudice.

--

In 1811, Jane is thirty-five. She is tall and slender, with bright hazel eyes, curly brown hair, and rosy cheeks. She wears a little cap on her head, as women of her age did. People who knew her said she was cheerful, quick and kind, with a great sense of humour, and very good at telling stories to children. And in this year, a London publisher has agreed to publish Sense and Sensibility. Jane is nervous, and excited. Her dream is about to come true.
