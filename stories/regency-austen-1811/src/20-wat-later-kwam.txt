In January 1813, Jane Austen's second novel was published. It was called Pride and Prejudice. It is the story of Elizabeth Bennet, a lively, clever young woman, and Mr Darcy, a proud, rich gentleman. At first, they dislike each other very much. But slowly, they learn to understand each other, and to overcome their pride and their prejudice. It became the most loved of all her novels. Jane called it her own darling child.

--

After Pride and Prejudice came Mansfield Park, in 1814, and Emma, in 1815. The Prince Regent himself was a great admirer of her novels, and kept a set of them in each of his houses. He even invited her to visit his library, and suggested that she dedicate her next novel to him. Jane did not like the prince very much. But one does not say no to the Prince Regent. So Emma was dedicated to him.

Her books were published anonymously, by A Lady, or by the Author of Sense and Sensibility. But more and more people knew who she was. She earned some money from her books, though not very much. About six hundred pounds in her whole life. And she went on living quietly in the cottage at Chawton, writing at her little table, walking to Alton, and playing with her nephews and nieces.

{img:winchester-college-street-house-1817-quiet-window-cathedral-close}
In 1816, Jane began to feel ill. She grew weaker and weaker. Nobody quite knows what her illness was. In May 1817, Cassandra took her to Winchester, to be near a good doctor. They stayed in a house in College Street, near the cathedral. And there, on the eighteenth of July 1817, Jane Austen passed away, peacefully, with her head on a pillow on Cassandra's lap. She was only forty-one years old.

She was buried in Winchester Cathedral. On her gravestone, there is a long inscription about her kindness, her faith and her goodness. But it does not mention that she was a writer at all. Two of her novels, Northanger Abbey and Persuasion, were published after her death, at the end of 1817. And in them, for the first time, her brother Henry wrote a short biography, telling the world who the lady was.

--

{img:jane-austen-house-museum-chawton-today-garden-visitors-later}
The Regency came to an end in 1820, when old King George the Third passed away, and the Prince Regent became King George the Fourth. He reigned for ten years. Then came his brother, William the Fourth, and then, in 1837, his young niece Victoria. And the Regency world, of muslin dresses and country dances, slowly faded into the Victorian age, with its steam trains, factories and crinolines.

But Jane Austen's novels did not fade. Slowly, over the nineteenth century, more and more people read them, and loved them. In the twentieth century, they became among the most loved books in the world. They have been translated into dozens of languages. They have been made into films and television series, again and again. Millions of people, all over the world, read them every year. And many people dream of living in the gentle world of Regency England, with its balls and its country houses, its letters and its walks.

{img:anne-older-woman-telling-stories-grandchildren-orchard-summer-later}
As for Anne and Mr Ellis, they were married in the summer of 1812, in the old church, by Anne's father. They lived happily in their little house, with its apple tree. They had five children, who were all very good at making people laugh. William became a captain in the navy. Kitty married a farmer, and kept a great many animals. And Anne lived to be a very old woman, and told her grandchildren about the great comet, and the first ball, and the lady in the little cap who wrote novels in secret.
