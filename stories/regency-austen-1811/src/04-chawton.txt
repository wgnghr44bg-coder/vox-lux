The house at Chawton is a comfortable red-brick house, standing right by the road, at a fork in the village. It was once an inn. It has six bedrooms, a sitting room, a dining room, a kitchen, and a garden at the back, with fruit trees, flowers and vegetables. Coaches and carts pass right by the windows. Jane writes to her brother that the house is very comfortable, and that she likes it very much.

--

Every morning, Jane gets up early. Before breakfast, she practises the pianoforte, for about an hour. She plays songs and little pieces, copied by hand into music books. She is not a great musician, but she loves to play. Then she makes breakfast for the family. That is her special job in the household. She makes the tea, and keeps the keys to the tea, sugar and wine, which are precious and locked away.

After breakfast, the household work is shared. Cassandra and Martha do most of it. Jane's mother, who is now in her seventies, works in the garden, digging potatoes and weeding the vegetables, in an old green smock, like a labourer. And Jane writes. She sits at a small round table, by the window, in the sitting room. The table is tiny, with twelve sides, just big enough for a few sheets of paper and an inkpot.

{img:small-twelve-sided-walnut-table-quill-inkpot-paper-sheets-close}
Jane writes on small sheets of paper, which she can easily hide. Because she does not want the servants, or visitors, to know that she is writing a novel. There is a door between the sitting room and the hall, which creaks when it is opened. Jane asks the family not to fix it. Because whenever she hears it creak, she knows that someone is coming, and she quickly slips her papers under a blotter, or into a drawer, before they come in.

She writes with a quill pen, made from a goose feather, which she sharpens with a little knife. She dips it in ink, and writes in small, neat handwriting, crossing out words and adding new ones between the lines. She reads her work aloud to her family in the evenings. They laugh at the funny parts, and give her advice. Her mother especially loves her stories. And Cassandra is always her first and best reader.

--

{img:jane-austen-walking-country-lane-bonnet-shawl-basket-hedgerows}
In the afternoons, Jane often goes for a walk. She loves walking. Along the lanes, through the fields and the woods, to the next village, or to the little town of Alton, about two kilometres away, where she does some shopping and visits friends. Sometimes she walks up to the great house, Chawton House, which belongs to her brother Edward, to borrow books from the library, or to visit when Edward is staying there.

The village is small, and everyone knows everyone. Jane visits the poor families of the parish, and brings them food and clothes, as ladies of her class were expected to do. She makes clothes for the poor, with the other ladies. She knows all the village children by name. And she watches everyone, with her bright, amused eyes. The vicar, the squire, the gossips, the shy young ladies and the proud old ones. They all find their way, in disguise, into her novels.

{img:family-evening-chawton-cottage-reading-aloud-candles-needlework}
In the evenings, the family sits together in the sitting room. They read aloud, sew, play cards or play the pianoforte. Sometimes there are visitors, neighbours from the village, or relatives. Jane's many nephews and nieces love to visit. Their Aunt Jane is their favourite aunt. She tells them wonderful made-up stories, plays games with them, and is always ready for fun.

--

One of the nieces later remembered seeing Aunt Jane sitting quietly by the fire, sewing, and then suddenly laughing to herself, jumping up, running across the room to her writing table, writing something down, and then coming back to the fire and her sewing. It was a new idea for a story, which had just come to her. And she had to write it down, before she forgot it.
