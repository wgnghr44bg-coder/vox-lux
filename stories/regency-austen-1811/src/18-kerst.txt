Christmas comes to the parsonage. In the Regency, Christmas is a time for family, church, food and fun. It lasts for twelve days, from Christmas Day until Twelfth Night, on the fifth of January. The house is decorated with greenery. Holly, ivy, laurel and mistletoe, brought in from the garden and the woods. There are no Christmas trees yet in most English homes. They will become popular later, in the time of Queen Victoria.

--

On Christmas morning, the family goes to church. It is Mr Hartley's busiest day of the year. The old church is decorated with holly and ivy, and the bells ring out over the frosty fields. The whole village comes, in their best clothes. The rich families from the great house sit in their private pews, at the front. The farmers and the villagers sit behind. The choir sings carols, accompanied by a little band of village musicians, with a fiddle, a flute and a cello.

After church, there is Christmas dinner. It is a great feast. Roast beef, or a goose, or a turkey. Mince pies, which in the Regency still contain real meat, mixed with fruit, sugar and spices. And plum pudding, a heavy, rich pudding of flour, suet, dried fruit, sugar and spices, boiled in a cloth for hours. It is brought to the table with a sprig of holly on top. Everyone eats far too much. Even William, who has had nothing but ship's biscuits for two years.

{img:christmas-dinner-regency-family-plum-pudding-holly-goose-candles-laughter}
Christmas is also a time of kindness to the poor. The rich families of the parish give food, coal and warm clothes to the poor families. Mrs Hartley and the girls have been sewing flannel petticoats and woollen stockings for weeks, for the poor children of the village. On Saint Thomas's Day, just before Christmas, the poor women of the village go from house to house, and the better-off families give them money, food or flour, for their Christmas baking.

During the twelve days of Christmas, there are parties and visits. Neighbours visit each other, and there are dinners, card parties, and little dances in the drawing room, with the furniture pushed back against the walls. There are games, like blind man's buff, hunt the slipper, and snapdragon, where you must pick raisins out of a dish of burning brandy, without burning your fingers. Kitty loves snapdragon. William is very good at it.

--

{img:twelfth-night-party-cake-crown-characters-cards-regency-drawing-room}
The best party of all is on Twelfth Night, the last night of Christmas. There is a special cake, the Twelfth Cake, a big, rich fruit cake, covered with white icing, and decorated with sugar figures. Hidden inside the cake, there is a bean and a pea. Whoever finds the bean in their slice becomes the king for the night. Whoever finds the pea becomes the queen. And everyone else draws a card with a funny character on it, and must play that character all evening.

The Hartleys give a Twelfth Night party for their friends and neighbours. Mr Ellis, the young doctor, is invited. The cake is cut. And to everyone's delight, Mr Ellis finds the bean, and Anne finds the pea. So they are the king and queen of Twelfth Night. Everyone cheers. Kitty says that it is fate. Anne blushes. And Mr Ellis, as king, orders that Anne must sing a song. So she sings, at the pianoforte, a little Scottish song, while everyone listens.

{img:young-woman-singing-at-pianoforte-candlelight-guests-listening-young-man-watching}
Jane Austen loved Christmas too. In her letters, she wrote about Christmas parties, dances and visits, and about the games and the food. In her novel Emma, there is a famous Christmas Eve dinner party, during a snowstorm. And in Mansfield Park, the young people play a card game called speculation, one winter evening, very noisily, and enjoy themselves very much.

--

When the last guests have gone home, on Twelfth Night, the family sits together by the fire, tired and happy. William is going back to his ship in a few days. Mrs Hartley does not want to think about it. But tonight, everyone is together. The fire is warm. The candles are burning low. Outside, the frost is sparkling on the lawn. And the church clock strikes midnight, softly, over the sleeping village.
