Tonight we are going more than three hundred and fifty years back, to the old city of London, in the long, warm summer of the year 1666.

[long-pause]

It is early morning, and the sun is rising over the river Thames.

[pause]

The light falls on a city of timber and plaster and stone, crowded along the north bank of the river: a great jumble of steep roofs and tall gables, chimneys and church towers, more than a hundred of them, rising above the rooftops. And above them all, on its hill, stands the enormous old cathedral of Saint Paul's, long and grey and ancient, with a great square tower in the middle.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into history, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel. Now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

The river is already busy.

[pause]

Little boats, called wherries, are rowing across the water, carrying passengers from one bank to the other. Barges loaded with hay and coal and timber drift down with the tide. Tall ships stand at anchor below the bridge, their masts and rigging dark against the morning sky.

[pause]

And across the river, from the city to the borough of Southwark on the south bank, there stretches London Bridge, the only bridge across the Thames in London: a long, narrow bridge of stone arches, with houses and shops built all along it, on both sides, three and four and five storeys high, hanging out over the water, so that crossing it is like walking down a narrow street.

[long-pause]

London, in 1666, is one of the largest cities in Europe.

[pause]

Perhaps four or five hundred thousand people live here, in the old walled City, and in the suburbs that have spread out beyond the walls in every direction: west to Westminster, where the King has his palace, and east toward the docks, and north into the fields.

[pause]

{img:city-wall-gate-ludgate-carts-travellers-1666}
The old City of London is still surrounded by its ancient wall, built by the Romans more than fourteen hundred years before, and rebuilt many times since, with great gates where the roads come in: Ludgate, Newgate, Aldersgate, Cripplegate, Moorgate, Bishopsgate, Aldgate. At night, the gates are shut.

[long-pause]

Inside the wall, the streets are narrow and crowded and winding, much as they were in the Middle Ages.

[pause]

The houses are mostly built of timber, with walls of wattle and plaster between the wooden beams, and many of them are very old. Each storey sticks out a little further over the street than the one below it, so that at the top, the houses on opposite sides of a narrow lane almost touch, and people can shake hands across the street from their top windows. Down below, the lanes are dim and shady, even at midday.

[pause]

{img:jettied-timber-houses-narrow-lane-overhanging-1666}
In the lanes, there are shops, and taverns, and workshops, and little churches, one in almost every street. There are more than a hundred parish churches in the City, each with its own bells.

[long-pause]

It is a city of trade.

[pause]

Ships come up the Thames from all over the world: from Holland and France and Spain, from the Baltic and the Mediterranean, from the new colonies in America and the West Indies, and from the far-off East Indies. They bring wine and silk and spices, sugar and tobacco, timber and tar, and carry away English wool and cloth.

[pause]

{img:wharves-billingsgate-ships-unloading-casks-1666}
In the City, the merchants buy and sell, in the great courtyard of the Royal Exchange, and in their counting houses and warehouses near the river. The craftsmen make everything that the city needs. The shopkeepers sell it.

[long-pause]

It has been a hard time for London, lately.

[pause]

Last year, in 1665, a terrible plague came to the city, and many people were lost. And this year, England is at war with the Dutch, at sea, and the people of London have heard the sound of the guns of the great sea battles, booming far away across the water.

[pause]

But now, in the summer of 1666, the plague has faded away, and the people who fled to the country have come home, and the city is busy and noisy and full of life again.

[long-pause]

And it is a dry summer. A very dry summer.

[pause]

There has been very little rain since the spring. The fields around London are brown and dusty. The wooden houses of the city are as dry as tinder, after months of sunshine. The timber beams, the plaster, the thatched roofs of the poorer houses, the warehouses full of oil and tar and timber and coal and hemp along the river, all of them dry, dry, dry.

[pause]

{img:dry-summer-london-lane-dust-sunlight-1666}
Nobody worries about it very much. Fires happen in London often, and are put out. The city has survived fires before.

[pause]

And in a house near the Tower of London, a busy, cheerful, curious man of thirty-three is writing, as he does every night, in his secret diary.

[long-pause]

{img:cheapside-street-shop-signs-crowds-1666}
The streets of London in 1666 are noisy, crowded, colourful, and rather smelly.

[pause]

There are no street names on the walls, and no house numbers. Instead, every shop and tavern and many houses have a painted sign hanging over the door, on an iron bracket: a golden key, a blue boar, a red lion, a bell, a swan, a bunch of grapes, three crowns, a Bible, a pair of scissors. People give directions by the signs. Go down Fish Street Hill, and turn left at the Star, and it is the third house past the sign of the Golden Ball.

[long-pause]

The streets are paved with rough stones, with a gutter running down the middle, into which everything is thrown: rubbish and slops and the water from the kitchens.

[pause]

When it rains, the gutters overflow, and the water runs down the lanes toward the river. In the dry summer, the dust blows everywhere. People walk close to the walls of the houses, where the pavement is a little cleaner and drier, and those who walk closest to the wall have the best place. Gentlemen argue about who should take the wall.

[pause]

There are carts and wagons, and coaches, which are new and fashionable, and horses, and sedan chairs, little boxes carried on poles by two strong men, in which rich ladies and gentlemen are carried through the streets. And there are crowds of people on foot, of every kind.

[long-pause]

The noise of London is famous.

[pause]

The street sellers cry their wares, in sing-song voices, all day long: Hot pudding pies! Fresh mackerel! Cherries ripe! New oysters! Any old iron? Who'll buy my sweet lavender? Knives to grind! Chairs to mend! Water, fresh water!

[pause]

{img:street-criers-17th-century-baskets-cherries-oysters}
The bells of the hundred churches ring, for services and weddings and funerals, and to tell the hours. Apprentices stand at the doors of their masters' shops, calling to the passers-by: What d'ye lack? What d'ye lack? Carts rumble over the stones. Dogs bark. Horses neigh. Blacksmiths hammer.

[long-pause]

Water is carried through the streets by water carriers, men and women with great wooden tankards on their backs, who fill them at the public conduits, the fountains where water comes in through lead pipes from springs outside the city, and sell it at the doors.

[pause]

There is also a new waterworks, at the north end of London Bridge, where great water wheels, turned by the tide flowing through the arches of the bridge, pump water from the Thames into wooden pipes, which carry it under the streets to the houses of people who can pay for it.

[pause]

{img:water-carrier-wooden-tankard-conduit-fountain}
There is also the New River, a long channel dug about fifty years ago, bringing fresh water from springs in the countryside twenty miles away, to a reservoir at Islington, and then through wooden pipes into the city.

[long-pause]

Every street has its own character.

[pause]

Cheapside, the widest and grandest street in the City, is lined with the shops of the goldsmiths, glittering with silver and gold, and the mercers, selling silks and velvets. Thames Street, along the river, is lined with warehouses, full of oil and tar and wine and hemp and coal. Fish Street Hill leads down to London Bridge, past the fishmongers. Pudding Lane, a narrow little lane nearby, runs down toward the river, and is home to bakers and butchers.

[pause]

In the lanes around Saint Paul's, there are the booksellers and the printers. In Eastcheap, the butchers. In Cornhill, the clothiers. In Lombard Street, the bankers.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, the streets grow darker, because there are no street lamps, except a few lanterns that householders are supposed to hang outside their doors on dark nights.

[pause]

People who go out at night carry their own lanterns, or hire a link boy, a boy with a burning torch, to light their way home through the dark streets. The watchmen walk the streets, with their lanterns and their staffs, calling out the hours, and the weather: Past ten o'clock, and a fine summer's night!

[pause]

{img:link-boy-torch-dark-street-17th-century-night}
And then, one by one, the candles go out in the windows, and the city goes to sleep, under the summer stars, with only the watchmen awake, and the bells ringing the hours, and the river flowing quietly under the bridge.

[long-pause]

{img:samuel-pepys-periwig-portrait-study-candle}
In a house in Seething Lane, near the Tower of London, lives a man named Samuel Pepys.

[pause]

He is thirty-three years old in 1666: a short, plump, lively man, with a round face, bright, curious brown eyes, a big nose, and a great curling periwig of dark hair, which is the fashion of the time. He is an important official in the Navy Office, which looks after the ships of the King's navy, and he works very hard, and is very good at his job.

[long-pause]

And every night, before he goes to bed, he writes his diary.

[pause]

He has been writing it since the first of January, 1660, more than six years. He writes it in a secret shorthand, a system of little signs and squiggles, so that nobody else can read it, in neat, bound books, which he keeps locked away in his study.

[pause]

In his diary, Pepys writes about everything. About his work, and the people he meets, and the things he sees and hears. About what he ate for dinner, and what he wore, and what he bought, and how much it cost. About his wife, and his servants, and his quarrels and his worries. About music and plays and books. About his hopes and his fears and his mistakes. He is completely honest, because he thinks nobody will ever read it.

[long-pause]

Because of his diary, we know Samuel Pepys better than almost anyone who lived in the seventeenth century.

[pause]

He was born in London, in 1633, the son of a tailor, in a little house near Fleet Street. He was a clever boy, and went to Saint Paul's School, and then to Cambridge University. Then, through the help of a rich and powerful cousin, Edward Montagu, he got a job in the government, and he has risen higher and higher ever since.

[pause]

{img:navy-office-clerks-desks-papers-seething-lane}
He has seen great events. When he was a boy of fifteen, he stood in the crowd and watched King Charles the First executed outside the Banqueting House at Whitehall. When he was twenty-seven, he sailed with the fleet to Holland, to bring the new king, Charles the Second, home to England, and he was there when the king stepped ashore at Dover.

[long-pause]

Pepys lives with his wife, Elizabeth.

[pause]

She is twenty-five, pretty and lively and French, the daughter of a poor French gentleman, and she married Samuel when she was only fourteen. They love each other, and quarrel often, and make up, and go to the theatre together, and play music together, and go for walks in the fields, and on the river.

[pause]

{img:young-wife-elizabeth-pepys-lute-parlour-window}
They have no children. They live in a comfortable house, attached to the Navy Office, with a little garden, and several servants: a cook, a maid, a boy, and Elizabeth's companion. Pepys's clerk, a young man named Will Hewer, lives with them too, and is almost like a son to them.

[long-pause]

Pepys loves life.

[pause]

He loves good food and good wine, and fine clothes, and new books, and pretty women, rather too much. He loves music above all things. He plays the lute and the viol and the flageolet, a little flute, and he sings, and he writes songs. He loves the theatre, and goes whenever he can, though he often makes a vow not to go so often, and then breaks it.

[pause]

He is curious about everything. He goes to see new inventions, and new buildings, and strange animals, and the dissection of bodies at the surgeons' hall. He becomes a member of the new Royal Society, where scientists meet to discuss experiments and discoveries. He buys a telescope, and a microscope, and looks at the moon, and at fleas.

[long-pause]

In his diary, he writes about the city around him, and about the people he meets: the King and the Duke of York, the admirals and the sea captains, the merchants and the shopkeepers, the actors and the musicians, his servants and his family, the boatmen on the river.

[pause]

He writes about the weather, and the gossip, and the news from the war. He writes about the plague, and the comet that appeared in the sky. He writes about his money, and counts it, and is pleased when he has more.

[pause]

{img:pepys-counting-money-strongbox-study-evening}
He is often vain, and sometimes foolish, and sometimes unkind. But he is also generous and kind and brave, and very human. And reading his diary, more than three hundred and fifty years later, it is as if he were sitting beside us, telling us about his day.

[long-pause]

Every night, when the household has gone to bed, Pepys sits at the table in his study, with a candle, and his diary, and writes.

[pause]

Then he finishes, as he so often does, with the same words: And so to bed.

[long-pause]

{img:pepys-waking-morning-bedchamber-curtains-1666}
Let us spend an ordinary summer day with Samuel Pepys, in the year 1666.

[pause]

He wakes early, at about five or six o'clock, in the big curtained bed that he shares with Elizabeth. Sometimes, before getting up, they lie and talk for a while, about the household, and their plans, and the news. Then he gets up, and his boy helps him to dress: his linen shirt, his breeches and stockings, his waistcoat and coat, his shoes with silver buckles, and his periwig.

[long-pause]

He goes down to his office, which is in the same building, the Navy Office, a big old house in Seething Lane.

[pause]

There, he works all morning, with the other officers of the Navy Board, at a long table covered with papers, and with his clerks, writing letters, checking accounts, signing contracts, and talking to the people who come to see him: sea captains wanting money for their ships, merchants selling timber and rope and tar and canvas and food for the navy, sailors wanting their wages, which are often very late.

[pause]

It is hard work. The navy is fighting a great war against the Dutch, and needs ships, and guns, and sailors, and food, and money, and there is never enough of anything, especially money. Pepys works very hard, and is very good at it, and is becoming one of the most important men in the navy.

[long-pause]

At midday, he goes home for dinner, the main meal of the day.

[pause]

Dinner might be a joint of roast beef or mutton, a pie, a fowl, some fish, with bread and cheese, and a dish of peas or beans or salad from the market, and beer or wine. On special days, when there are guests, there are many more dishes, and Pepys writes proudly in his diary about how fine the dinner was, and how much it cost.

[pause]

{img:17th-century-dinner-table-pie-fowl-wine-guests}
After dinner, he might go back to the office, or he might go out, on business, or for pleasure.

[long-pause]

Pepys goes everywhere by water, if he can.

[pause]

From the stairs at the end of the lanes near his house, he calls a boatman, and is rowed up the river to Whitehall, or Westminster, or across to Southwark, or down to the dockyards at Deptford and Woolwich, where the King's ships are built and repaired. The river is the quickest way to travel in London, much quicker than the crowded, narrow streets.

[pause]

On the way, he sits in the stern of the little boat, in the sunshine, watching the river and the ships and the city going by, and sometimes he sings, or reads a book, or plays his flageolet.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, he might go to Whitehall, to the King's palace, a huge, rambling collection of old buildings along the river at Westminster, with courtyards and galleries and gardens.

[pause]

There, he meets the Duke of York, the King's brother, who is the Lord High Admiral, the head of the navy, and talks to him about the business of the fleet. Sometimes he sees the King himself, walking in the gardens with his little spaniels, or playing tennis, or feeding the ducks in Saint James's Park.

[pause]

{img:whitehall-palace-galleries-courtiers-gardens-1666}
He listens to the gossip of the court, about who is in favour and who is not, and writes it all down in his diary.

[long-pause]

In the late afternoon, if he can, he goes to the theatre.

[pause]

The theatres were closed for many years, during the time of the Puritans, who thought that plays were sinful. But when King Charles came back, six years ago, they were opened again, and there are now two theatres in London: the King's House, in Drury Lane, and the Duke's House, in Lincoln's Inn Fields.

[pause]

For the first time in England, women are allowed to act on the stage. Pepys loves the plays, and the actresses, especially a pretty, witty young actress named Nell Gwyn, who sells oranges in the theatre, and acts in comedies. He goes to see Shakespeare, and new comedies, and plays with music and dancing, and writes his opinion of each one in his diary: a most excellent play, or the most insipid ridiculous play that ever I saw in my life.

[long-pause]

In the evening, he goes home, through the streets or on the river, as the sun goes down over the city.

[pause]

{img:pepys-singing-friends-music-parlour-candlelight-evening}
He has supper with Elizabeth, perhaps with friends, and afterwards they play music together, in the parlour: Pepys on his viol, Elizabeth singing, a friend on the lute, by the light of the candles. Or they walk in the garden, in the warm summer evening, and look at the stars.

[pause]

And then, at last, when the house is quiet, he goes to his study, and lights a candle, and writes his diary. And so to bed.

[long-pause]

{img:17th-century-kitchen-hearth-spit-cook-maid}
The house in Seething Lane is run by Elizabeth Pepys, with the help of her servants.

[pause]

There is a cook, who rules the kitchen; a chambermaid, who cleans the rooms and makes the beds; a younger maid, who helps with everything; a boy, who runs errands and waits at table and helps Pepys to dress; and, for some years, a young gentlewoman, who is Elizabeth's companion, and keeps her company, and walks out with her, and sings with her, and helps her to dress.

[long-pause]

The kitchen is on the ground floor, with a great open fireplace, where all the cooking is done.

[pause]

There is a spit for roasting meat, turned slowly in front of the fire, by a boy, or by a little dog running in a wooden wheel, or by a clockwork jack, which is new and fashionable. There are iron pots hanging from hooks over the fire, for boiling and stewing. There is a brick oven, in the wall beside the fireplace, for baking bread and pies.

[pause]

On the walls, there are shelves of pewter plates and dishes, polished until they shine, and copper pans, and wooden bowls, and earthenware jugs. Bunches of herbs hang from the beams. There is a big wooden table in the middle, scrubbed white, where the cook prepares the food.

[long-pause]

Elizabeth goes to the market herself, sometimes, with her maid, or sends the cook, to buy meat and fish and vegetables and fruit, and the other things the household needs.

[pause]

She plans the meals with the cook, especially when there are guests coming to dinner, which Pepys loves. On those days, the whole household works for hours, to prepare a great dinner: perhaps a dish of marrow bones, a leg of mutton, a loin of veal, a dish of fowl, a great venison pasty, a tart, and cheese, with plenty of wine.

[pause]

{img:dinner-guests-table-pewter-venison-pasty-candlelight}
Pepys writes proudly in his diary about these dinners, and about how well everything was done, and how his guests enjoyed themselves. Or, when things go wrong, about how cross he was with the cook.

[long-pause]

The clothes of the household are washed every few weeks, in a great wash day, which begins before dawn, and goes on all day.

[pause]

The water is heated in great coppers, and the clothes are soaked and boiled and beaten and scrubbed with soap made from fat and wood ash, and rinsed, and wrung out, and hung to dry on lines in the garden, or in the attic if it is raining. Then they are ironed, with heavy irons heated in front of the fire. It is hard work, and the whole household helps.

[pause]

{img:wash-day-copper-steam-maids-linen-garden-lines}
Pepys's fine clothes, his silk coats and lace collars and cuffs, are cleaned and mended carefully, because they are very expensive. He loves fine clothes, and writes in his diary about every new suit, and its colour, and how much it cost, and how well it looked.

[long-pause]

Elizabeth spends her days running the household, and also enjoying herself, as much as she can.

[pause]

She takes lessons in dancing and singing and painting, from teachers who come to the house. She goes shopping with her companion, in the Royal Exchange and in Cheapside. She visits her friends, and goes to church, and to the theatre with her husband. She reads French romances, long, exciting stories of love and adventure, which Pepys thinks are silly.

[pause]

She is often lonely, because Samuel is so busy, and is out so much, and they have no children. They quarrel, sometimes, about money, and about his jealousy, and about his flirting with other women. But they love each other.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, when there are no guests, the household gathers together.

[pause]

Pepys teaches the boy to sing, and plays music with Elizabeth and her companion. The maids sew and mend by the fire. Sometimes Pepys reads aloud, or tells them stories about the court and the navy. On summer evenings, they sit in the little garden behind the house, and the leads, the flat roof, where Pepys sometimes takes his flageolet, and plays, in the moonlight.

[pause]

{img:household-music-evening-parlour-servants-sewing-fire}
And at about ten o'clock, the household says prayers together, as many families did, and then the candles are carried up the stairs, one by one, and the doors are locked, and the house goes quiet.

[long-pause]

{img:thames-watermen-wherries-stairs-summer-morning-1666}
In 1666, the river Thames is the great highway of London.

[pause]

There are very few bridges, only London Bridge, in the City, and the streets are narrow and crowded and slow. So almost everyone who wants to travel more than a short distance takes a boat. The river is crowded with boats, from early morning until late at night.

[long-pause]

The boatmen of the Thames are called watermen.

[pause]

There are thousands of them. They row little boats called wherries: long, narrow, light boats, with a pointed bow, and a cushioned seat in the stern for the passengers, who sit facing forward, under a little awning in the sun or the rain. The waterman sits in the middle, facing backward, and rows with two oars, or sometimes there are two watermen, with four oars, for a faster journey.

[pause]

All along both banks of the river, there are stairs: stone or wooden steps leading down to the water, at the end of the lanes, where the watermen wait with their boats for passengers. When someone comes down the stairs, the watermen all shout at once: Oars! Oars! Sculls! Sculls! each trying to win the customer.

[long-pause]

The watermen are famous for their rough language, and their jokes, and their songs.

[pause]

They have their own company, with a hall, and rules, and apprentices, who learn the river for seven years before they can carry passengers on their own. They know every current and every eddy of the river, and the tides, which rise and fall twice a day, by more than twenty feet, and change the river completely from hour to hour.

[pause]

{img:thames-at-low-tide-mud-boats-old-london-bridge}
The most dangerous part of the river is London Bridge.

[long-pause]

London Bridge is a wonder of the world.

[pause]

It was built about four hundred and fifty years ago, of stone, on nineteen narrow arches, standing on great boat-shaped platforms of timber and stone, called starlings, in the river. The starlings are so big that they block more than half the river, so that the water rushes through the narrow arches like a waterfall, especially when the tide is turning, with a roar that can be heard far away.

[pause]

Shooting the bridge, passing through the arches in a boat, is very exciting and quite dangerous. Many passengers prefer to get out of the boat before the bridge, and walk around, and get in again on the other side. There is a saying that London Bridge was made for wise men to go over, and fools to go under.

[long-pause]

On top of the bridge, there are houses and shops, all along both sides, so close together that the roadway between them is only about twelve feet wide, and often jammed with carts and coaches and people.

[pause]

The shops sell everything: gloves and hats, needles and pins, silks and ribbons. The houses are tall, with overhanging upper storeys, and some of them are built right across the roadway, like a tunnel. In the middle of the bridge, there is an old chapel, and at the southern end, there is a great stone gate.

[pause]

{img:shops-on-old-london-bridge-narrow-roadway-crowds}
The people who live on the bridge say that they can fish from their windows, and that they are lulled to sleep at night by the roar of the water below.

[long-pause]

Below the bridge, the river is full of ships.

[pause]

This part of the river is called the Pool of London, and here the great sailing ships from overseas tie up, or lie at anchor in the middle of the stream, and are unloaded by lighters, small barges, which carry the goods to the wharves along the river bank. Their masts stand like a forest, and their flags of many nations flutter in the wind.

[pause]

{img:pool-of-london-tall-ships-lighters-17th-century}
Further down the river, at Deptford and Woolwich, there are the royal dockyards, where the King's warships are built and repaired: great wooden ships with three masts and rows of guns, which are sent out to fight the Dutch.

[long-pause]

Upstream, above the bridge, the river is quieter, and lined with the great houses and gardens of the nobility, along the Strand, between the City and Westminster.

[pause]

Their gardens run down to the river, with their own stairs and their own boats, and the lords and ladies go up and down the river in their own barges, with liveried oarsmen. The King has a magnificent royal barge, painted and gilded, with a canopy, which is rowed by many oarsmen in red coats.

[pause]

And on summer evenings, people go out on the river for pleasure, in boats, to listen to music, or to row upriver to the villages and gardens beyond Westminster, among the fields and the meadows.

[long-pause]

Pepys loves the river.

[pause]

He writes about it often in his diary: about rowing up to Whitehall on a fine morning, or down to Greenwich on a summer evening, singing with his friends in the boat, and the moon shining on the water. He writes about the watermen, and their stories, and the price of a boat.

[pause]

{img:summer-evening-boat-music-moonlight-river-friends}
And soon, in a few weeks' time, he will spend many hours on the river, in a little boat, watching his city, and writing about what he sees.

[long-pause]

{img:royal-exchange-courtyard-merchants-colonnade-1666}
In the heart of the City of London stands the Royal Exchange.

[pause]

It is a grand building, built nearly a hundred years before by a rich merchant named Sir Thomas Gresham, around a great open courtyard, surrounded by covered walks with columns, and a tall bell tower, with a golden grasshopper on the top, which was Gresham's sign.

[long-pause]

In the courtyard, every day at midday, the merchants of London meet, to buy and sell, and hear the news.

[pause]

They gather in groups, according to the countries they trade with: the merchants who trade with Spain in one place, the merchants who trade with Holland in another, the merchants who trade with the East Indies in another. They talk about the prices of goods, and the arrival of ships, and the news from abroad, and make their bargains, and shake hands on them.

[pause]

Upstairs, around the courtyard, there are galleries full of little shops, more than a hundred of them, selling fine goods: lace and gloves, silks and ribbons, perfumes and fans, jewellery and books. They are mostly run by women, and fashionable ladies and gentlemen come to walk in the galleries, and look at the goods, and be seen.

[long-pause]

The greatest shopping street of all is Cheapside.

[pause]

Cheapside is wide and long, the widest street in the City, running from Saint Paul's Cathedral toward the Royal Exchange. In the Middle Ages, it was a great open market, and the name Cheap means market, in old English.

[pause]

{img:cheapside-wide-street-shops-goldsmiths-conduit}
Now, it is lined with tall, fine houses, with shops on the ground floor: mercers, selling silk and velvet; drapers, selling woollen cloth; haberdashers, selling ribbons and buttons and thread; and above all, the goldsmiths, whose shops glitter with silver plates and gold cups and jewels, and who also act as bankers, keeping money safe for their customers, and lending it out.

[long-pause]

The shops have no glass in their windows.

[pause]

Instead, the front of the shop has wooden shutters, which open in the morning, the bottom one folding down to make a counter, and the top one folding up to make an awning. The goods are displayed on the counter, and the shopkeeper and his apprentices stand behind it, calling out to the people passing by.

[pause]

The apprentices are boys and young men, who have been sent by their families to learn a trade, and live with their master and his family for seven years. They are famous for their high spirits, and their fights, and their games of football in the streets on holidays.

[long-pause]

There are markets all over the City.

[pause]

At Leadenhall, there is a great market for meat and poultry and leather and cloth. At Newgate, there is a market for meat. At Billingsgate, by the river, there is the fish market. At Queenhithe, there is a market for corn and flour. At Smithfield, outside the walls, there is a great market for cattle and horses, and every summer, a famous fair, Bartholomew Fair, with puppet shows and tightrope walkers and roast pork and gingerbread.

[pause]

{img:leadenhall-market-17th-century-stalls-poultry-meat}
In the streets, too, there are stalls and barrows and baskets everywhere, selling fruit and vegetables and flowers and fish, brought in from the countryside and the river every morning.

[long-pause]

The craftsmen of London make everything that the people of the city need.

[pause]

In the workshops, behind the shops and in the lanes and alleys, there are tailors and shoemakers, hatters and glovers, carpenters and joiners, coopers making barrels, smiths and pewterers and cutlers, clockmakers and instrument makers, printers and bookbinders, brewers and bakers.

[pause]

Each craft has its own guild, called a livery company, with its own hall, and its own coat of arms, and its own rules, and its own feasts and processions. There are more than fifty of them: the Mercers and the Grocers, the Drapers and the Fishmongers, the Goldsmiths and the Skinners, the Merchant Taylors and the Haberdashers, the Salters and the Ironmongers, the Vintners and the Clothworkers, the Bakers and the Brewers.

[long-pause]

Among the bakers of the City, there is one named Thomas Farriner.

[pause]

He has a bakery in Pudding Lane, a narrow lane running down from Eastcheap toward the river and London Bridge. He is a respected man, in his fifties, a member of the Bakers' Company, and he bakes bread and ship's biscuit, hard, dry biscuit that does not go bad, for the sailors of the King's navy.

[pause]

{img:pudding-lane-bakery-oven-ship-biscuit-baker-17th-century}
Every morning, very early, he lights the fire in his big brick oven, and bakes. And every night, he lets the fire die down, and goes to bed, with his family, in the rooms above the shop.

[long-pause]

{img:17th-century-children-playing-street-hoops-tops}
The streets and lanes of London in 1666 are full of children.

[pause]

They play in the streets, and in the churchyards, and in the fields outside the walls, and on the banks of the river. They play with hoops and sticks, and spinning tops, and marbles, and balls. They play games of hide and seek, and leapfrog, and blind man's buff. They fly kites in the fields, and fish from the river stairs, and swim in the river in summer, which their mothers forbid.

[long-pause]

Children of poor families begin to work very young.

[pause]

They help their parents at home and in the shop, from the age of five or six. They run errands, and carry water, and mind the babies, and sell things in the streets. At twelve or fourteen, many boys are sent to be apprentices, to learn a trade, and many girls go into service, as maids in the houses of richer families.

[pause]

But many children, too, go to school, for at least a few years.

[long-pause]

There are many schools in London.

[pause]

There are little schools, run by a woman in her own house, where small children learn their letters from a hornbook: a little wooden paddle with a sheet of paper on it, protected by a thin, clear sheet of horn, with the alphabet and the Lord's Prayer printed on it. The children hold it by the handle, and recite the letters, over and over.

[pause]

{img:little-children-hornbook-dame-school-room}
Then there are the grammar schools, for boys, where they learn Latin and Greek, from morning until evening, six days a week, with a stern master, who keeps a birch rod on his desk. The most famous is Saint Paul's School, beside the cathedral, where Pepys himself went as a boy, and where he learned so much Latin that he could read and write it all his life.

[long-pause]

Girls of richer families are taught at home, or at little boarding schools, in the villages around London, at Hackney and Chelsea and Putney.

[pause]

They learn to read and write, and to do sums, and French, and music and singing and dancing, and needlework, and how to run a household. Some of them learn much more: languages, and history, and philosophy, from their fathers or their brothers or their tutors.

[pause]

{img:girls-needlework-sampler-window-light-school}
One of the things that every girl makes is a sampler: a piece of linen, embroidered with the alphabet, and numbers, and little pictures of flowers and birds and houses, and her name, and the date, to show her skill with the needle. Some of these samplers still survive today, more than three hundred years later, with the little names of the girls who made them stitched in the corner.

[long-pause]

Children's lives in the seventeenth century are often short, and many families lose children to illness.

[pause]

But they are loved, and played with, and spoiled, as much as children are today. Their parents make them little toys: wooden dolls with painted faces, and little cradles for them, and tiny pewter dishes and pots for playing house; wooden horses on wheels, and hobby horses; little wooden soldiers and drums and trumpets; whistles and rattles.

[pause]

On holidays, they are taken to the fairs, at Smithfield and Southwark, where there are puppet shows, and rope dancers, and jugglers, and fire eaters, and gingerbread, and toys, and strange animals to see.

[long-pause]

In the winter, when it is very cold, the Thames sometimes freezes over, above the bridge, because the narrow arches of London Bridge slow the water down.

[pause]

Then the children, and the grownups too, go out onto the ice, and slide and skate and play, and there is a frost fair, with booths and stalls on the ice, selling food and drink and toys, and people roast an ox, and play football, and print their names on little cards on a printing press set up on the ice, as souvenirs.

[pause]

{img:frost-fair-frozen-thames-booths-skaters-children}
In the summer, the children swim in the river, and play in the fields, and pick flowers and blackberries in the hedges, and catch frogs in the ponds, and come home tired and dirty and happy, as the sun goes down over the city.

[long-pause]

{img:17th-century-coffee-house-long-table-men-pipes-news}
In 1666, there is a new and fashionable kind of place in London: the coffee house.

[pause]

The first one opened only about fourteen years ago, in a little alley off Cornhill, run by a Greek man named Pasqua Rosée, who had learned to make coffee in the Turkish lands, where it was already popular. Londoners had never tasted anything like it. Soon, there were coffee houses all over the city, dozens of them, more every year.

[long-pause]

A coffee house is a large, plain room, usually on the ground floor, with long wooden tables and benches, and a big fire at one end, where the coffee is made, in great pots, and kept hot.

[pause]

For a penny, anyone can come in, and sit down, and drink a dish of coffee, as they call it, because it is served in little bowls without handles, and stay as long as they like. Coffee is hot and black and bitter, and some people think it tastes horrible. But it keeps you awake, and alert, and it does not make you drunk, like beer and wine.

[pause]

There are newspapers to read, and pamphlets, and news sheets. And above all, there is talk.

[long-pause]

The coffee houses are the place where London talks.

[pause]

Men of every kind come in: merchants and lawyers, doctors and scientists, writers and poets, shopkeepers and craftsmen, sea captains and clergymen. They sit down together at the long tables, and talk, about politics, and business, and the war, and the news, and the latest books and plays, and the gossip of the court. Anyone can talk to anyone, whatever their rank. People call the coffee houses penny universities, because for a penny, you can learn more there than at Oxford or Cambridge.

[pause]

{img:coffee-house-debate-scholars-merchants-clay-pipes}
Many of the coffee houses have their own special customers. In one, the merchants who trade with the East Indies meet. In another, the scientists of the Royal Society. In another, the poets and the writers. Later, some of these coffee houses will grow into great institutions: the Stock Exchange, and the great insurance company, Lloyd's of London, which began in a coffee house.

[long-pause]

There are also taverns and inns, hundreds of them, much older than the coffee houses.

[pause]

The taverns are where people go to drink wine, and eat, and meet their friends. They have names like the Mitre, the Globe, the Dolphin, the Sun, the Mermaid, the King's Head, and painted signs hanging over their doors. Inside, there are small rooms, with wooden panelling and fireplaces, where groups of friends can have dinner in private.

[pause]

{img:tavern-private-room-panelling-fire-friends-dinner}
Pepys loves the taverns. He goes to them often, with his friends and colleagues, for dinner, and to talk, and drink a glass of wine, and sing. He writes about the food, and the wine, and the company, and how much it all cost, and sometimes, about how much he drank, and how ashamed he was the next morning.

[long-pause]

The great inns are on the main roads in and out of the city, and are the starting points for the coaches and the carriers' wagons that travel to every part of England.

[pause]

They are built around large courtyards, with galleries around the upper floors, where the bedrooms are, and stables for the horses below. In the courtyards, the coaches arrive and depart, with a great clatter, and the passengers climb in and out, and the ostlers lead the horses to the stables, and the porters carry the luggage.

[pause]

{img:coaching-inn-courtyard-galleries-coach-departing}
In the old days, before the theatres were built, the actors used to perform their plays in the courtyards of the inns, with the audience standing in the yard, and sitting in the galleries above.

[long-pause]

And there are the alehouses, small, humble places, often just a room in someone's house, with a few benches and a barrel of ale, where working people go in the evening to drink a pot of ale and talk with their neighbours.

[pause]

Ale and beer are what almost everyone drinks, all day long, men and women and children, because the water is often not safe to drink. There are brewhouses all over London, and the smell of the brewing drifts through the streets.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, the coffee houses and the taverns are full of talk and laughter and smoke.

[pause]

Everyone smokes tobacco, in long white clay pipes, which are sold, already filled, in the taverns and the coffee houses, for a penny. The smoke hangs in clouds under the low ceilings, and the candles flicker, and the voices rise and fall.

[pause]

{img:evening-coffee-house-candlelight-smoke-conversation}
And in the summer of 1666, the talk is all about the war with the Dutch, and the great sea battles, and the dry weather, and the price of bread, and whether the plague will come back, and the new comet that some people say they have seen in the sky, and what it might mean.

[long-pause]

{img:old-st-pauls-cathedral-gothic-nave-tower-1666}
On the highest hill in the City of London stands the great old cathedral of Saint Paul.

[pause]

It is enormous: one of the largest churches in Europe, more than five hundred and eighty feet long, built of grey stone, in the Gothic style, with tall pointed windows and flying buttresses. It was built more than four hundred years before, in the Middle Ages, and it is very old, and in places, rather shabby.

[long-pause]

Once, it had a great spire, one of the tallest in the world, rising high above the city. But about a hundred years ago, the spire was struck by lightning, and burned, and was never rebuilt. Now, the cathedral has only a great, square, stubby tower in the middle.

[pause]

During the years of the civil war, when the Puritans ruled England, the cathedral was badly treated. Soldiers stabled their horses in the nave. Shops and stalls were built against its walls. Its statues were broken.

[pause]

Now, the King and the Church are trying to repair it. There is scaffolding against the walls, and stonemasons at work. A young architect and scientist named Christopher Wren has been asked to make plans for its repair. Just a few days before the end of August 1666, he and some other gentlemen walked around the cathedral, inspecting it, and Wren suggested adding a great dome.

[long-pause]

The cathedral is not only a church. It is also a kind of public meeting place.

[pause]

The long middle aisle of the nave is called Paul's Walk, and for many years, it has been one of the busiest places in London. People walk up and down it, all day long, to meet their friends, and do business, and exchange news and gossip, and show off their fine clothes. Lawyers meet their clients there. Servants looking for work stand by certain pillars. Merchants make deals.

[pause]

{img:pauls-walk-nave-crowds-gossip-fashionable-people}
People say that if you want to know the latest news, you should go to Paul's Walk. Some people think it is very disrespectful, in a church, and complain about it. But it goes on anyway.

[long-pause]

Around the cathedral, there is a great open space, called Saint Paul's Churchyard.

[pause]

Here, there are the shops and stalls of the booksellers and the printers. Paul's Churchyard is the centre of the book trade in England. Almost every book published in London is sold here. The booksellers have their shops all around the churchyard, with their signs hanging over the doors: the Bible, the Rose, the Green Dragon, the Angel.

[pause]

Pepys loves to browse in the bookshops of Paul's Churchyard, and spends many hours there, and much money, buying books: histories and poetry, plays and sermons, books about music and mathematics and the navy.

[long-pause]

Under the cathedral, there is a great crypt, which is the church of Saint Faith, and is used as a church by the people of the parish.

[pause]

The booksellers of the churchyard use the crypt to store their books, in great piles, because it is safe and dry, with thick stone walls and a stone vault above. At the end of August 1666, the crypt is full of books, thousands and thousands of them, worth a great deal of money.

[pause]

{img:stone-crypt-piled-books-paper-candle-vaults}
They believe that there is no safer place in London.

[long-pause]

From the top of the great tower of Saint Paul's, there is a wonderful view over the whole of London.

[pause]

To the east, the City, with its hundred church towers, and its crowded roofs, down to the Tower of London, and beyond, the river winding away toward the sea, crowded with ships. To the south, the river, and London Bridge, and Southwark on the far side, with its inns and its theatres. To the west, the Strand and Westminster, with its abbey and its palace. To the north, the fields and the villages, and the green hills of Hampstead and Highgate.

[pause]

On a clear day, people say, you can see for twenty miles.

[long-pause]

On Sundays, the cathedral is full of people, for the services.

[pause]

The great organ plays, and the choir of men and boys sings, and the bells ring out across the city. In the old days, before the civil war, there were also sermons in the churchyard, at an open-air pulpit called Paul's Cross, where great crowds stood in the open air to listen. It was pulled down by the Puritans, but the old people of London still remember it.

[pause]

{img:cathedral-choir-organ-candles-sunday-evensong}
In the evening, the bells of Saint Paul's ring for evensong, and their deep voices drift out over the rooftops of the city, and the swallows wheel around the old grey tower, in the golden light of the setting sun.

[long-pause]

{img:charles-ii-tall-dark-periwig-spaniels-st-james-park}
The King of England in 1666 is Charles the Second.

[pause]

He is thirty-six years old: very tall, more than six feet, with a long, dark, swarthy face, heavy-lidded dark eyes, a thin black moustache, and a great black periwig of curling hair falling to his shoulders. He is witty and charming and lazy and clever, and very fond of pleasure, and of ladies, and of his little spaniels, which follow him everywhere.

[long-pause]

His life has been extraordinary.

[pause]

When he was a boy, there was a terrible civil war in England, between his father, King Charles the First, and Parliament. His father lost the war, and was put on trial, and executed, in 1649, outside the Banqueting House at Whitehall. Young Charles, who was eighteen, was in exile in France and Holland.

[pause]

He tried to win back his throne, and was defeated, and had to flee for his life, in disguise, through the English countryside, for six weeks, with soldiers searching for him everywhere. Once, he hid all day in the branches of a great oak tree, at a place called Boscobel, while the soldiers searched the wood below him. At last, he escaped to France, and lived in exile, poor and unhappy, for nine years.

[long-pause]

Then, in 1660, the English people grew tired of the government of the Puritans, and invited him to come home and be their king.

[pause]

He landed at Dover in May 1660, on the deck of a ship on which Samuel Pepys was also sailing, and rode in triumph to London, through cheering crowds, on his thirtieth birthday, the twenty-ninth of May. The church bells rang all over the country. There were bonfires in the streets, and people danced and drank toasts to the King. People called it the Restoration.

[pause]

{img:restoration-procession-cheering-crowds-1660-london}
And with the King came back many things that the Puritans had forbidden: the theatres, and music, and dancing, and Christmas, and maypoles, and horse racing, and fine clothes, and fun.

[long-pause]

The King lives in the Palace of Whitehall, at Westminster, a huge, rambling palace of more than a thousand rooms, along the river.

[pause]

It is not a grand, handsome building, like the palaces of France. It is a jumble of old buildings, of different ages and styles, around courtyards and gardens, connected by long galleries and passages, with lodgings for the courtiers and the servants, and kitchens and stables, and a tennis court, and a bowling green, and a cockpit.

[pause]

Anyone who is respectably dressed can walk in the galleries of Whitehall, and watch the King and the Queen go to chapel, or eat their dinner in public, which they often do. Pepys goes there often, on business, and loves to watch the court.

[long-pause]

The King loves science and inventions.

[pause]

He has a laboratory at Whitehall, where he does experiments in chemistry, and he has given his name to the new Royal Society, a group of scientists and gentlemen who meet every week to discuss the latest discoveries, and do experiments, and look through telescopes and microscopes. He loves ships and the sea, too, and knows a great deal about ship design, and often goes down to the dockyards to see the new ships being built.

[pause]

{img:king-watching-shipbuilding-dockyard-deptford}
He loves to walk, very fast, in Saint James's Park, beside the palace, where he has made a long, straight canal, and keeps ducks and geese and pelicans, which were given to him by the Russian ambassador. The people of London come to watch him walk, followed by his courtiers, puffing and panting to keep up with him, and his little dogs.

[long-pause]

His brother, James, the Duke of York, is the Lord High Admiral, the head of the navy, and Pepys's master.

[pause]

James is a serious, honest, rather stiff man, very different from his witty brother. He is brave, and has fought in the great sea battles against the Dutch, on the deck of his flagship. He works hard, and he trusts Pepys, and relies on him.

[pause]

In a few weeks' time, the King and the Duke of York will both show the people of London a side of themselves that nobody expected.

[long-pause]

On summer evenings, the court of King Charles is full of music and dancing and gaiety.

[pause]

There are balls, and plays performed in the palace, and concerts by the King's own band of twenty-four violins, in imitation of the court of the King of France. The courtiers dress in silks and satins and lace, with ribbons and feathers and jewels. The ladies wear low-cut dresses, and their hair in ringlets, and little black patches on their faces, as the fashion is.

[pause]

{img:court-ball-violins-candles-whitehall-dancing-silks}
And far away, in the crowded streets of the City, the shopkeepers and the craftsmen and the apprentices and their families go about their business, and look up at the sky, and wonder when it will rain.

[long-pause]

{img:theatre-royal-drury-lane-1666-exterior-coaches-crowd}
In the afternoons, at about half past three, the people of London who love plays make their way to the theatres.

[pause]

There are two playhouses in 1666. The King's Company acts at the Theatre Royal, in Bridges Street, near Drury Lane, which was built just three years earlier. The Duke's Company acts at a theatre in Lincoln's Inn Fields, in an old tennis court that has been converted into a playhouse.

[long-pause]

The theatres are new and exciting.

[pause]

For eighteen years, while the Puritans ruled England, the theatres were closed, because the Puritans believed that plays were wicked. Actors were arrested, and the old theatres, where Shakespeare's plays had been performed, were pulled down. But when King Charles came back, he gave permission for two companies of actors to perform again, and he loves the theatre, and often comes to see the plays himself.

[pause]

The new theatres are indoors, with a roof, unlike the old open-air theatres of Shakespeare's time. They are lit by candles, in great chandeliers hanging over the stage and the audience, and along the front of the stage. And they have painted scenery, which can be changed, sliding in from the sides of the stage, showing palaces and gardens and streets and forests and seas, which amazes the audiences.

[long-pause]

And, for the first time in England, women act on the stage.

[pause]

In Shakespeare's time, all the women's parts were played by boys. But King Charles, who has seen the theatres of France, where women act, has ordered that women's parts should be played by women. Now, the actresses are among the most famous and admired people in London.

[pause]

{img:young-actress-backstage-mirror-candles-costume}
The most famous of all is Nell Gwyn. She is about sixteen years old in 1666, small and pretty, with red-brown hair and a quick, cheeky wit. She grew up very poor, in the streets around Covent Garden, and began by selling oranges to the audience in the theatre. Then she became an actress, and is wonderful in comedies, and the audiences love her. Later, she will become a favourite of the King himself.

[long-pause]

The audience is a mixture of every kind of person.

[pause]

In the boxes, along the sides of the theatre, sit the lords and ladies of the court, in their silks and lace and jewels. In the pit, the floor in front of the stage, where there are benches, sit the gentlemen and the lawyers and the merchants, and the young men about town, and people like Pepys. In the galleries above, sit the servants and the apprentices and the ordinary people, who have paid less for their seats.

[pause]

{img:theatre-audience-pit-boxes-galleries-orange-sellers}
The orange sellers walk up and down, selling oranges, and the audience eats, and talks, and laughs, and flirts, and shouts at the actors, and sometimes quarrels and fights. Going to the theatre is as much about seeing the audience as seeing the play.

[long-pause]

The plays are of every kind.

[pause]

There are the old plays of Shakespeare, and Ben Jonson, and Beaumont and Fletcher, often changed and rewritten to suit the new taste, with music and dancing added. And there are new plays, written by the playwrights of the day: witty comedies about the love affairs of fashionable young people in London, and grand tragedies in rhyming verse about kings and queens and heroes in faraway lands.

[pause]

Pepys goes to see everything, and writes about it all. He loves Macbeth, and The Tempest, and Hamlet. He thinks Romeo and Juliet is the worst play he has ever heard, and A Midsummer Night's Dream is insipid and ridiculous, though he likes the dancing and the pretty women.

[long-pause]

The theatres are closed for long periods, during the plague, and during the period of mourning after the fire.

[pause]

But they open again, and the crowds come back, and the candles are lit, and the music plays, and the curtain rises, and the actors and actresses come out, in their fine costumes, onto the candlelit stage.

[pause]

{img:theatre-evening-crowd-leaving-coaches-link-boys}
And at about six or seven o'clock, the play ends, and the audience pours out into the street, talking and laughing, and calling for their coaches and their sedan chairs, or hiring link boys to light them home, through the dark streets, under the summer stars.

[long-pause]

{img:empty-london-street-1665-summer-quiet-closed-shutters}
The summer before, in 1665, had been a very sad one for London.

[pause]

In the spring of that year, a plague had come to the city, as it had come many times before, every few years, for three hundred years, since the time of the Black Death. But this time, it was worse than anyone could remember.

[long-pause]

We will not tell this part of the story in detail tonight. It is enough to say that, all through the summer and the autumn of 1665, the sickness spread through the crowded streets and lanes of London, especially in the poor parishes outside the walls.

[pause]

Many people left the city. The King and the court went to Salisbury, and then to Oxford. The rich merchants and the lawyers went to their houses in the country. The doctors, many of them, left too. Those who could not leave, the poor, stayed, and did the best they could.

[pause]

By the end of the year, perhaps a hundred thousand people had been lost, about a quarter of the people of London. The streets were quiet. The shops were shut. Grass grew in the roads.

[long-pause]

Samuel Pepys stayed in London, for most of that time, because his work at the Navy Office was too important to leave. He sent Elizabeth away to Woolwich, a few miles down the river, where it was safer, and visited her when he could.

[pause]

He wrote about it all in his diary, honestly, and sometimes with great sadness. He wrote about the quiet streets, and the closed houses, and the bells. He wrote about being afraid. But he also wrote about his work, and his music, and his friends, and about doing his duty.

[pause]

{img:pepys-working-late-candle-office-alone-1665}
And at the end of that year, on the last day of December, 1665, he wrote that he had never lived so merrily, and never got so much money, as he had in that plague year. Pepys was always honest in his diary, even when he was not very admirable.

[long-pause]

In the winter, the plague slowly faded.

[pause]

The cold weather came, and the sickness grew less, and less, and by the spring of 1666, it had almost gone. The King and the court came back to Whitehall. The merchants came back to their shops and their counting houses. The families came back to their homes.

[pause]

London began to fill up again, and the shops opened, and the streets grew crowded and noisy, and the bells rang for weddings and christenings, and life went on.

[long-pause]

In the spring of 1666, there were many new things to talk about.

[pause]

There was the war against the Dutch, which had been going on for two years, at sea. In June, there was a tremendous sea battle, the greatest anyone had ever seen, which lasted for four days, off the coast of Kent and Essex. The people of London could hear the guns, booming far away across the water, day after day.

[pause]

{img:distant-sea-battle-ships-smoke-horizon-calm-coast}
Pepys went into the park at Greenwich to listen, and heard them, faintly, like distant thunder. The English fleet was badly beaten. But a few weeks later, in another battle, they won, and the people of London rang the church bells, and lit bonfires in the streets, to celebrate.

[long-pause]

There was also, that year, a strange feeling in the air.

[pause]

Some people had noticed that the year was 1666, and that the number six hundred and sixty-six was mentioned in the Bible, in the Book of Revelation, as the number of the beast. Some people believed that something terrible would happen in that year. Pamphlets and almanacs were printed, predicting disasters.

[pause]

Most sensible people laughed at such ideas. Pepys did not believe in them. But they were talked about in the coffee houses and the taverns, all the same.

[long-pause]

And then there was the weather.

[pause]

That summer, it was very hot and very dry. There was hardly any rain from the spring onwards. The wells and the streams ran low. The fields were brown. The roads were dusty. And the wooden houses of London, which had been soaked by the rains of many winters, dried out completely, and became as dry as kindling.

[pause]

{img:parched-fields-outside-london-dusty-road-summer-1666}
At the end of August, a wind began to blow, from the east. A strong, dry, warm wind, off the sea and across the flat marshes of Essex, and on into the city, rattling the shutters and the signs, and whirling the dust along the streets.

[pause]

It blew all through the last days of August, and into September.

[long-pause]

{img:spring-garden-vauxhall-arbours-walks-summer-evening-1666}
On fine summer afternoons, the people of London love to get out of the crowded, noisy, smoky city, into the fresh air of the countryside.

[pause]

The countryside is very close. A short walk north, out through the city gates, and you are in the fields: meadows full of buttercups and cows, with hedges and trees, and little villages, Islington, Hoxton, Hackney, with their inns and their gardens.

[long-pause]

On the other side of the river, at Vauxhall, there is a famous pleasure garden, called the New Spring Garden.

[pause]

To get there, you take a boat across the river from the stairs at Westminster or the Temple. It is a large, beautiful garden, with long, shady walks between hedges and trees, and arbours and bowers, where people can sit and eat and drink, and listen to the nightingales singing in the trees.

[pause]

There is no fee to get in. People bring their own food, or buy cakes and cheesecakes and syllabubs and wine, and walk, and talk, and flirt, and listen to the musicians, who play in the bowers. It is very fashionable, and very pleasant, on a summer evening.

[long-pause]

Pepys loves the Spring Garden. He goes there often, with Elizabeth, and with his friends.

[pause]

He writes that it is very pleasant and cheap going there, for a man may spend what he will, or nothing, all as one. But to hear the nightingale and other birds, and here fiddles and there a harp, and here a Jew's trump, and here laughing, and there fine people walking, is mighty divertising.

[pause]

{img:musicians-harp-fiddle-garden-arbour-people-listening}
He walks there, and listens to the birds, and eats a cheesecake, and drinks a glass of wine, and goes home by boat in the moonlight, singing.

[long-pause]

Closer to the city, there are other places to walk.

[pause]

There are Moorfields, just outside the city wall to the north, a great open space of grass and trees and gravel walks, where the people of the City walk in the evenings, and the women spread their washing on the grass to dry, and the young men play games and practise archery.

[pause]

There is Hyde Park, to the west, where the fashionable people drive round and round in their coaches, in a great circle called the Ring, to see and be seen, and the ladies show off their fine dresses and their coaches. Pepys sometimes hires a coach, so that he and Elizabeth can drive round the Ring with the fashionable people.

[long-pause]

And there is Saint James's Park, beside the palace of Whitehall, where the King has made a long, straight canal, lined with trees, and keeps ducks and geese and birds of every kind, and the pelicans from Russia.

[pause]

The people of London walk there, and watch the birds, and watch the King, who walks very fast, with his spaniels and his courtiers, and plays a game called pall mall, a kind of croquet, along a long, straight alley, which later gives its name to a famous street.

[pause]

{img:st-james-park-canal-ducks-pelicans-walkers-summer}
In the winter, when the canal freezes, the courtiers skate on it, in a new Dutch way, gliding gracefully along on iron skates, which the King has brought back from Holland. Pepys and Evelyn both write about it, amazed at how fast and how gracefully the skaters move.

[long-pause]

There are other pleasures too.

[pause]

There are bowling greens, behind the taverns, where the gentlemen play bowls on summer evenings. There are archery grounds. There are boat trips on the river, up to Barn Elms or Richmond, among the green meadows and the willows, with a picnic. There are horse races, and cockfights, and bear gardens on the south bank, which Pepys goes to see, and does not like very much.

[pause]

And there are the simple pleasures of walking in the fields, picking flowers, sitting under a tree, and looking back at the city, with its church towers and its great old cathedral, on its hill, golden in the evening sun.

[long-pause]

In the summer of 1666, Pepys and Elizabeth go out to the fields and the gardens many times.

[pause]

They walk in the Spring Garden, and drive in Hyde Park, and take boats up the river. They sing in the boat, on the way home, as the sun goes down, and the river turns gold, and the city appears ahead of them, with its hundred church towers, and the lights coming on in the windows.

[pause]

{img:couple-boat-singing-sunset-river-city-towers-golden}
It is a lovely summer, warm and dry and golden. And nobody, as they sit in their boats on the river in the summer evenings, listening to the nightingales, imagines what will happen at the beginning of September.

[long-pause]

{img:pudding-lane-night-narrow-lane-timber-houses-moon}
On the night of Saturday, the first of September, 1666, Thomas Farriner, the baker of Pudding Lane, goes to bed as usual.

[pause]

He has finished his baking for the day. He has, he will say afterwards, raked out the fire in his oven, and checked it, at about ten o'clock, and seen that it was safe. He has left some wood beside the oven, ready for the morning. And he goes up the stairs, with his son and his daughter and his maid, to the rooms above the shop, and goes to sleep.

[long-pause]

Outside, the east wind is blowing, strong and dry and warm, along Pudding Lane, and down toward the river.

[pause]

The lane is narrow and dark, and the tall timber houses lean over it on both sides. All around, in the lanes going down to the river, there are warehouses and yards, full of things that burn very easily: timber and coal, hay and straw, oil and tallow, pitch and tar and hemp and brandy, stored for the ships and the shops.

[pause]

The watchmen walk through the dark streets, calling the hour. The bells ring midnight. The city sleeps.

[long-pause]

At about one o'clock in the morning, on Sunday, the second of September, Thomas Farriner's household wakes up, and smells smoke.

[pause]

Somehow, a spark from the oven, or from somewhere else in the bakery, has caught. Nobody will ever know exactly how. The ground floor of the bakery is already full of smoke and fire.

[pause]

{img:dark-house-window-glow-night-sleepers-waking}
The family cannot get down the stairs. So they climb out of an upstairs window, onto the roof of the house next door, and escape, across the rooftops, into the next house, and down into the street. Their maid, frightened, does not follow them, and is lost. She is one of the very few people who will lose their lives in the great fire, as far as anyone knows.

[long-pause]

The neighbours come out of their houses, and try to put out the fire, with buckets of water, passed from hand to hand, from the nearest pump.

[pause]

But the bakery is full of wood and flour, and the wind is blowing hard, and the fire spreads to the houses next door, and the houses across the lane, which almost touch it, and then to the houses beyond. Sparks and burning pieces of wood fly through the air on the wind, and land on the roofs further down the lane, toward the river.

[pause]

{img:neighbours-buckets-chain-narrow-lane-night-firelight}
At about three o'clock in the morning, the Lord Mayor of London, Sir Thomas Bloodworth, is woken up, and comes to look. He sees a fire in a small lane, like many fires he has seen before, and is annoyed at being woken. He does not think it is serious. He goes back to bed.

[long-pause]

In Seething Lane, about half a mile away, Samuel Pepys's maid, Jane, has been up late, getting ready for a dinner the next day.

[pause]

At about three o'clock in the morning, she sees a glow in the sky, toward the river, and goes and wakes her master, to tell him that there is a great fire in the City.

[pause]

{img:maid-waking-master-candle-bedchamber-night-glow-window}
Pepys gets up, in his nightgown, and goes to her window, and looks. He sees the fire, some distance away, and thinks it is far enough off, and not very serious. He goes back to bed, and falls asleep again.

[long-pause]

When he wakes again, at about seven o'clock, it is a bright, sunny Sunday morning.

[pause]

He goes to the window, and looks. The fire is still burning. Jane tells him that she has heard that more than three hundred houses have been burned in the night, and that the fire is now burning all down Fish Street Hill, by London Bridge.

[pause]

So Pepys gets dressed, and goes out, and walks to the Tower of London, which is close by, and climbs up onto one of the high walls, with the son of the Lieutenant of the Tower, to get a better view.

[long-pause]

From the walls of the Tower, he sees the fire.

[pause]

He sees the houses at the end of London Bridge burning, and the fire spreading along the river front, through the warehouses of Thames Street, driven by the east wind. It is a great fire now, much bigger than any he has ever seen.

[pause]

{img:view-from-tower-walls-river-bridge-distant-haze-morning}
He goes down to the river, and gets into a boat, and is rowed up the river, past the bridge, and watches.

[pause]

He writes later, in his diary, about the people he saw, hurrying to save their goods, carrying them down to the river, and throwing them into the boats and barges, or into the water, to save them. And about the poor people, who stayed in their houses until the very fire touched them, and then ran into the boats, or clambered from one pair of stairs by the waterside to another.

[pause]

And he writes about the pigeons, who would not leave their houses, but hovered about the windows and balconies, until they were singed, and fell down.

[long-pause]

{img:boats-on-thames-people-carrying-goods-stairs-haze}
All through that Sunday morning, the fire spreads along the river, westward, driven by the east wind.

[pause]

The people living in the lanes near the river begin to flee. They carry their goods down to the river stairs, in their arms and on their backs: beds and chests and chairs, pots and pans, clothes and books, and pile them into boats, or onto barges, and are rowed away across the river, or up the river, to safety. The river is soon crowded with boats, piled high with furniture.

[long-pause]

Pepys watches for about an hour, from his boat.

[pause]

He sees that nobody is trying to stop the fire. Everyone is busy saving their own things, and running away. And he sees that the wind is driving the fire into the City, toward the houses and the churches and the warehouses, and that if nothing is done, it will burn the whole of London.

[pause]

So he tells the boatman to row him up the river to Whitehall, to the King's palace, as fast as he can.

[long-pause]

At Whitehall, Pepys goes into the chapel, where many of the courtiers are, and tells them what he has seen. They take him to the King.

[pause]

Pepys tells the King and the Duke of York that unless the King orders houses to be pulled down, to stop the fire, nothing will stop it. The King listens, and agrees, and tells Pepys to go to the Lord Mayor, and tell him, from the King, to spare no houses, but to pull them down before the fire, in every direction. And the Duke of York says that if the Lord Mayor needs soldiers, he shall have them.

[pause]

{img:king-and-duke-listening-pepys-palace-gallery}
Pepys hurries back to the City, by coach and on foot, to find the Lord Mayor.

[long-pause]

He finds him at last, in Canning Street, looking exhausted, with a handkerchief around his neck. When Pepys gives him the King's message, the Lord Mayor cries out, like a fainting woman, Pepys writes: Lord, what can I do? I am spent. People will not obey me. I have been pulling down houses, but the fire overtakes us faster than we can do it.

[pause]

In those days, there was no fire brigade in London. Fighting fires was the job of the people of each parish, with leather buckets, and ladders, and great iron hooks on long poles, to pull down burning houses, and a few simple pumps, called fire engines, which squirted a little water. But against a fire like this, they were useless.

[long-pause]

All through the afternoon, the fire spreads.

[pause]

The streets are crowded with people, carrying their goods, and carts, and horses, all trying to get away, and getting in each other's way. The air is full of smoke, and the noise of the crackling flames, and the crash of falling houses, and the shouting of the people.

[pause]

{img:crowded-street-people-carrying-chairs-bundles-afternoon}
In the late afternoon, Pepys meets Elizabeth, and some friends, and they go out onto the river again, in a boat, and watch the fire from the water. The wind is so strong, and the smoke so thick, that they are showered with sparks, which Pepys calls fire-drops, and must keep away from the shore.

[long-pause]

As evening comes, they go to a little alehouse on the south bank of the river, in Southwark, and stay there until it is dark, watching the fire.

[pause]

And Pepys writes about what they saw, in some of the most famous words in his diary. He writes that as it grew darker, the fire appeared more and more, in corners and upon steeples, and between churches and houses, as far as they could see up the hill of the City. And that it was not like the fine flame of an ordinary fire.

[pause]

They saw the fire as one entire arch, from one end of London Bridge, up the hill, for an arch of above a mile long. It made him weep, he writes, to see it: the churches and the houses, all alight at once, far away across the dark water.

[long-pause]

Then they go home to Seething Lane, sadly, late at night.

[pause]

Pepys's house is not yet in danger. But the fire is coming closer. So that night, with the help of his clerk, Will Hewer, and his friends, he carries his money, and his plate, and his best things, and his precious diary, down into his cellar, or packs them up, ready to be carried away.

[pause]

{img:man-packing-chest-candlelight-books-papers-night}
It is a bright moonlit night, he writes, and very hot, and the light of the fire makes it as bright as day. Nobody in London sleeps much that night.

[long-pause]

{img:carts-leaving-city-gates-moorfields-crowds-smoke-distant}
On Monday, the third of September, the fire spreads further.

[pause]

The wind is still blowing from the east, strong and steady, and the fire moves westward and northward through the City, from street to street, faster than anyone can stop it. It reaches Cornhill, and Lombard Street, and the Royal Exchange, the great building where the merchants meet, which burns in the afternoon.

[long-pause]

The people of the City are leaving.

[pause]

All day long, the streets are crowded with people, carrying everything they can, and carts and wagons, piled high, trying to get out through the gates of the city, to the fields outside the walls. The price of a cart goes up and up, until people are paying ten times the usual price. The river is crowded with boats.

[pause]

In the fields to the north of the city, at Moorfields, and in the fields of Islington and Highgate, thousands of families camp in the open, with their belongings around them, under sheets and blankets, and watch the glow of the fire in the sky over the city.

[long-pause]

Pepys spends the day packing.

[pause]

In the early morning, at four o'clock, he sends his money and his plate and his best things away, in a cart, to the house of a friend at Bethnal Green, a village outside the city, riding beside the cart in his nightgown. Then he comes back, and goes on packing, all day, with Elizabeth and the servants, and carries more of their things down to the river, to be taken away by boat.

[pause]

{img:cart-at-dawn-village-road-bethnal-green-belongings}
He is exhausted, and dirty, and worried. But he is still writing his diary, every night.

[long-pause]

On Monday, the King gives his brother, the Duke of York, the job of fighting the fire.

[pause]

The Duke sets up posts, all around the edge of the fire, each one commanded by a nobleman or a courtier, with soldiers, and with men to fight the fire, and food and drink and money to pay them. He rides from post to post, all day and all night, encouraging the men, and working with his own hands to pass buckets of water, and pull down houses.

[pause]

The people of London are amazed. They have never seen a prince working like this, among them, covered in dirt and sweat. They begin to cheer him.

[long-pause]

On Tuesday, the fourth of September, the fire reaches Saint Paul's Cathedral.

[pause]

Everyone had thought that the great old cathedral, with its thick stone walls, would be safe. That was why the booksellers had stored their books in the crypt. But there was wooden scaffolding against the walls, for the repairs, and the roof was covered with lead, laid on great wooden beams.

[pause]

{img:old-cathedral-distant-evening-glow-sky-from-fields}
On Tuesday evening, the scaffolding caught fire, and then the roof. And the great old cathedral, which had stood on its hill for nearly six hundred years, was lost, in a few hours, while the people watched from the fields outside the city, and from the river, and from the hills around London.

[long-pause]

That same day, John Evelyn, a gentleman and writer, who also kept a diary, walked through the city, and wrote about what he saw.

[pause]

He wrote that the stones of Saint Paul's flew like grenades, and the lead of the roof melted and ran down the streets in a stream. He wrote that the sky was like the top of a burning oven, and that the light of the fire could be seen forty miles away, and that at night it was as light as day for ten miles around.

[pause]

{img:evelyn-writing-diary-country-house-window-glow-distant}
And he wrote about the people, thousands of them, camping in the fields, with what little they had saved, in tents and huts, poor and sad, but patient.

[long-pause]

On Tuesday, too, the fire comes close to Seething Lane, where Pepys lives, and to the Navy Office, and the Tower of London.

[pause]

Pepys and Sir William Penn, another officer of the Navy Board, who lives next door, dig a pit in the garden, and bury some of their most precious things, to save them: their wine, and Pepys's parmesan cheese, a large, round, hard Italian cheese, which he values very much. It is one of the most famous moments in his diary, because it is so ordinary, and so Pepys.

[pause]

He sends Elizabeth and Will Hewer away to Woolwich, with his gold, by boat, for safety. And he stays behind, to help to save the Navy Office.

[long-pause]

To save the Navy Office, and the Tower, Pepys asks for workmen from the royal dockyards at Deptford and Woolwich, who know how to pull down buildings quickly.

[pause]

They come up the river, and work through the night, pulling down the houses in the path of the fire, and blowing up some of them with gunpowder from the Tower, to make great gaps that the fire cannot cross. That night, Pepys sleeps, for a little while, on the floor of his office, in his clothes.

[pause]

{img:workmen-pulling-down-timber-house-hooks-ropes-gap}
And outside, in the darkness, the wind blows, and the fire burns, and the city waits.

[long-pause]

{img:quiet-dawn-wind-dropped-smoke-rising-straight-old-city}
On the morning of Wednesday, the fifth of September, Samuel Pepys wakes up, at about two o'clock in the morning, to the news that the fire has reached Barking Church, at the bottom of Seething Lane, very close to his house.

[pause]

He gets up, and goes out, and looks. And then, as the sun comes up, he sees something wonderful.

[long-pause]

The wind has dropped.

[pause]

For four days, the east wind has blown steadily, driving the fire before it. But now, in the early morning, it has died away, and the air is still. And without the wind, the fire is slowing down.

[pause]

The great gaps that the dockyard men and the soldiers have made, by pulling down and blowing up the houses in the path of the fire, are holding. The fire comes to the edge of the gaps, and stops. At Barking Church, the fire burns the porch and the dial, but the church is saved. And the Navy Office, and Pepys's house, and the Tower of London, are saved too.

[long-pause]

All through Wednesday, the soldiers and the workmen and the people of London fight the fire, all around its edges, at every street, pulling down houses, and pouring water, and beating out the flames.

[pause]

The King himself is there, on horseback, with the Duke of York, riding from place to place, encouraging the men, and handing out gold coins to the workers. At one point, the King gets down from his horse, and stands in a line of men passing buckets of water, from hand to hand, up to his ankles in water and mud, with his fine clothes covered in soot.

[pause]

{img:king-in-bucket-chain-with-workmen-sooty-clothes}
The people of London never forget it. For the rest of his life, they will remember the king who helped to fight the great fire with his own hands.

[long-pause]

By the evening of Wednesday, the fire is under control, almost everywhere.

[pause]

Here and there, it still burns, in the cellars and the warehouses, and the heaps of ruins, for days and weeks afterwards, smoking and smouldering. But it is no longer spreading. The great fire of London is over.

[pause]

That evening, Pepys climbs to the top of the steeple of Barking Church, and looks out over the city. He writes that he saw the saddest sight of desolation that ever he saw.

[long-pause]

The next day, Thursday, Pepys walks through the ruins.

[pause]

He walks through the streets where he grew up, and went to school, and courted his wife, and bought his books, and drank in the taverns, and heard the bells. They are all gone. Where the crowded lanes and the tall houses stood, there is now a great open space, covered with ashes and rubble and fallen stones, with here and there the tower of a church, or a stone wall, still standing, black against the sky.

[pause]

{img:man-walking-through-ashes-ruins-quiet-morning-mist}
The ground is so hot that it burns his shoes. He finds a little cat, which has been hiding in a hole in a chimney, with its fur singed, but still alive. He picks up a piece of glass from the Mercers' Chapel, melted and bent by the heat, and puts it in his pocket, to keep.

[long-pause]

The fire had burned for four days.

[pause]

It had destroyed most of the old City of London, inside the walls: about thirteen thousand houses, eighty-seven parish churches, Saint Paul's Cathedral, the Royal Exchange, the Guildhall, the Custom House, forty-four halls of the livery companies, and many prisons and markets and gates.

[pause]

{img:open-landscape-ruins-old-wall-gate-autumn-light}
About seventy or eighty thousand people, perhaps more, had lost their homes. But, as far as anyone knows, very few people lost their lives. Most people had time to escape, and did.

[long-pause]

In the fields around London, the homeless families camped for days and weeks, in tents and huts made of sheets and boards.

[pause]

The King ordered bread to be brought from the villages around London and given out to them. He ordered the neighbouring towns to take them in, and give them shelter, and let them work at their trades. He rode out to Moorfields, and spoke to the people there, and told them that the fire was an act of God, and not caused by any enemy, and that he would look after them.

[pause]

Slowly, the families found places to live: with relatives in the suburbs, in the towns and villages around London, in huts and shacks built on the ruins of their old homes.

[long-pause]

On Friday night, the seventh of September, Pepys sleeps in his own bed again, for the first time in a week, in his own house, in Seething Lane, which has been saved.

[pause]

He writes in his diary that he slept well, though he still had dreams of the fire, and of houses falling down.

[pause]

{img:pepys-house-bedchamber-candle-tired-sleep-night}
And somewhere in the garden, in its hole in the ground, his parmesan cheese is still waiting for him, safe and sound, we hope.

[long-pause]

{img:wren-drawing-plans-new-city-desk-candle-1666}
Within days of the fire, people began to make plans for a new London.

[pause]

Christopher Wren, the young architect and scientist who had been asked to repair Saint Paul's, drew a plan for a completely new city, with wide, straight streets, and great squares and avenues, like the cities of Italy and France, with a grand new cathedral in the middle. He showed it to the King, just a few days after the fire. Other people drew other plans.

[long-pause]

But the plans were never carried out.

[pause]

The people of London wanted their homes back, and their shops, and their businesses, as quickly as possible. And every piece of land in the City belonged to someone, who wanted to build on it again, in the same place. So, in the end, the new London was built on the old plan, with the same streets, more or less, as before, though some of them were made wider and straighter.

[pause]

Surveyors walked through the ruins, measuring and marking out the old plots of land, and the old streets, so that everyone could find their own land again, among the ashes.

[long-pause]

But the new London was different, in one very important way.

[pause]

The King and Parliament made a law, which said that all new houses in the City must be built of brick or stone, not wood. And there were rules about how high they could be, and how thick their walls must be, and that the upper storeys must not hang out over the street. Never again would London be a city of wooden houses, leaning over narrow lanes.

[pause]

{img:bricklayers-building-new-brick-houses-scaffolding-1667}
Over the next few years, the City was rebuilt, house by house, street by street. Thousands of builders came to London, from all over the country, to work. Brickworks were set up in the fields around the city, making millions of bricks. The sound of hammers and saws filled the air, all day long, for years.

[long-pause]

By about 1671, five years after the fire, most of the houses had been rebuilt.

[pause]

They were tall, plain, handsome houses of red brick, with flat fronts, and sash windows, and tiled roofs, in neat rows along the streets. The new Royal Exchange was built, grander than before. The halls of the livery companies were rebuilt, one by one. The Custom House, and the Guildhall, and the markets.

[pause]

London was rising again, from its ashes, bigger and stronger and safer than before.

[long-pause]

The churches took longer.

[pause]

Of the eighty-seven churches that had burned, fifty-one were rebuilt, and Christopher Wren designed every one of them. Each one was different, and each one had a different tower or steeple, of stone or lead, some tall and slender, some short and square, some like wedding cakes, some like lanterns.

[pause]

{img:wren-church-steeple-white-stone-blue-sky}
For years, Wren was busy with them all, drawing plans, and visiting the building sites, and choosing the carpenters and the carvers and the plasterers. Inside, the churches were light and white and beautiful, with clear glass windows, and fine wooden carvings, and plasterwork ceilings.

[long-pause]

And then there was Saint Paul's.

[pause]

At first, people tried to repair the old cathedral. But the walls were too badly damaged, and after a few years, they gave up, and pulled down what was left, with gunpowder and battering rams. And Christopher Wren was asked to design a new cathedral.

[pause]

He made many designs, over several years. And in 1675, nine years after the fire, the first stone of the new cathedral was laid.

[pause]

It took thirty-five years to build. Wren was forty-three years old when it was begun, and seventy-eight when it was finished, in 1710. He was one of the very few architects in history who lived to see his greatest building completed from beginning to end.

[long-pause]

The new Saint Paul's is very different from the old one.

[pause]

It is built of white Portland stone, from the south coast of England, and in the middle, it has a great dome, one of the largest in the world, rising high above the city, with a golden ball and cross on the top. Inside, it is light and spacious and beautiful, with huge arches, and the dome soaring high above.

[pause]

{img:st-pauls-dome-white-stone-completed-1710-sunset}
Wren is buried in the crypt, under the dome. On a plain stone over his grave, there is a Latin inscription, which says: Reader, if you seek his monument, look around you.

[long-pause]

Near the place where the fire began, at the bottom of Fish Street Hill, close to Pudding Lane, Wren and his friend, the scientist Robert Hooke, built a great monument, to remember the fire.

[pause]

It is a tall, round column of white stone, two hundred and two feet high, with a golden urn of flames on the top. It is exactly as tall as the distance from its base to the place in Pudding Lane where the fire began. Inside, there is a narrow spiral staircase, of three hundred and eleven steps, which visitors can climb, to a little balcony near the top, and look out over the city.

[pause]

People simply call it the Monument.

[long-pause]

And Thomas Farriner, the baker of Pudding Lane, rebuilt his bakery, and went on baking, for the rest of his life. He always said that he had put out his oven properly, that night, and that it was not his fault.

[pause]

{img:rebuilt-bakery-brick-pudding-lane-baker-morning}
Nobody knows for sure. But in 1986, three hundred and twenty years after the fire, the Worshipful Company of Bakers, the bakers' guild of London, made a formal apology, for the fire that had started in one of their members' bakeries, all those years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:pepys-older-study-bookcases-candle-diary-closing}
Samuel Pepys went on writing his diary for nearly three more years after the fire.

[pause]

For months afterwards, he had bad dreams about fires, and could not sleep well. But life went on. He worked hard at the Navy Office, and the war with the Dutch came to an end. He went to the theatre, and played his music, and bought books, and quarrelled and made up with Elizabeth, and wrote it all down, every night.

[long-pause]

But his eyes were getting weaker.

[pause]

Writing by candlelight, night after night, for years, in his tiny shorthand, had strained them, and he was afraid that he was going blind. So, on the thirty-first of May, 1669, he wrote the last entry in his diary.

[pause]

He wrote that he must stop, because of his eyes, and that he could not do it any longer. And he ended with words that are still moving to read: And so I betake myself to that course, which is almost as much as to see myself go into my grave. For which, and all the discomforts that will accompany my being blind, the good God prepare me.

[pause]

He did not go blind. His eyes recovered. But he never wrote his diary again.

[long-pause]

That autumn, he took Elizabeth on a holiday to France and Holland, which she had always wanted.

[pause]

But on the way home, she fell ill with a fever. And in November 1669, she passed away, in London, at the age of twenty-nine. Pepys was heartbroken. He put up a beautiful marble monument to her in his parish church, Saint Olave's, in Hart Street, near Seething Lane, which had survived the fire, high on the wall, where he could see it from his seat in the church. He never married again.

[pause]

{img:st-olaves-church-marble-bust-lady-wall-quiet}
He went on to become one of the greatest officials in the history of the navy. He reformed the way the navy was run, built new ships, and made the navy into one of the greatest in the world. He became a member of Parliament, and the president of the Royal Society, where he helped to publish the great book by Isaac Newton about gravity and the movement of the planets.

[long-pause]

In his old age, he lived in a house in Clapham, then a village outside London, with his faithful old clerk, Will Hewer, who had become a rich man himself.

[pause]

He spent his time with his books, which he loved more than anything. He had about three thousand of them, which he kept in twelve beautiful glass-fronted bookcases, made specially for him by the shipwrights of the navy, arranged carefully in order of size, so that they looked neat. He catalogued them, and had them bound, and added to them, until the end of his life.

[pause]

{img:glass-fronted-bookcases-library-old-books-quiet-room}
He died in 1703, at the age of seventy. He left his library, with all its books and its bookcases, to his old college at Cambridge, Magdalene College, where it is still kept today, exactly as he arranged it.

[long-pause]

Among the books, there were six volumes, bound in brown leather, written in a strange shorthand that nobody could read.

[pause]

They were his diary.

[pause]

For more than a hundred years, the volumes sat on the shelves of the library, unread. Then, in the early nineteenth century, a young student at Magdalene, named John Smith, was given the job of deciphering them. It took him three years, working twelve or fourteen hours a day.

[pause]

In 1825, a part of the diary was published, at last. People were amazed. Here was a man from the seventeenth century, speaking to them, as if he were in the room: about the Great Fire, and the plague, and the King, and the theatres, and his wife, and his cheese, and his music, and his mistakes. Since then, it has been read by millions of people, all over the world.

[long-pause]

John Evelyn lived until 1706, and wrote his diary too, for more than sixty years, and planted trees, and wrote books about gardens and forests, and about the smoke of London, which he hated.

[pause]

Christopher Wren lived until 1723, to the age of ninety. In his old age, it is said, he liked to be driven into London, once a year, and to sit for a while under the great dome of his cathedral, in silence.

[pause]

{img:old-architect-sitting-under-great-dome-light-quiet}
King Charles the Second reigned until 1685, and is remembered as the Merry Monarch, the king who brought back the theatres and the music and the fun, and who helped to fight the great fire with his own hands.

[long-pause]

{img:city-of-london-today-st-pauls-dome-evening-river}
The City of London is still there today, on the north bank of the Thames, in the same place, on the same hills, within the line of the same old walls.

[pause]

It is a very different place now. Tall towers of glass and steel rise above the old streets, and the City is one of the greatest centres of banking and business in the world. Hundreds of thousands of people come to work there every day, and in the evening, most of them go home, and the streets grow quiet.

[long-pause]

But the old City is still there, underneath, if you know how to look.

[pause]

Many of the streets follow the same lines as they did before the fire, and have the same names: Cheapside, Cornhill, Lombard Street, Eastcheap, Fish Street Hill, Thames Street, Pudding Lane. In some places, the narrow old alleys and courts still wind between the buildings, as they did in Pepys's day.

[pause]

{img:narrow-old-city-alley-courtyard-lamp-evening-today}
And the churches of Christopher Wren are still there, many of them, scattered among the office buildings, with their white towers and steeples rising up among the glass towers, like old friends among strangers. Their bells still ring. Some of them are open during the day, and people come in from their offices, at lunchtime, to sit quietly for a while, or to listen to a concert.

[long-pause]

Saint Paul's Cathedral still stands on its hill, with its great white dome, which can be seen from all over London.

[pause]

For centuries, it was the tallest building in the city. During the Second World War, when London was bombed, and many buildings around it were destroyed, the cathedral survived, and a famous photograph of its dome, rising above the smoke, became a symbol of the courage of the people of London.

[pause]

{img:st-pauls-interior-dome-mosaics-light-quiet-today}
Visitors can climb up inside the dome, to the Whispering Gallery, where a whisper on one side of the dome can be heard on the other side, more than a hundred feet away. And they can climb higher still, to the Golden Gallery, at the very top, and look out over the whole of London, as Pepys once looked out from the tower of the old cathedral.

[long-pause]

The Monument still stands at the bottom of Fish Street Hill, near Pudding Lane, tall and white, with its golden flames at the top.

[pause]

Visitors still climb its three hundred and eleven steps, round and round, up the narrow spiral staircase, to the little balcony at the top, and look out over the river and the City, and get a certificate at the bottom, to say that they have done it.

[pause]

At the foot of the Monument, on its base, there are carved inscriptions in Latin, describing the fire, and a carved picture, showing King Charles, in Roman armour, coming to help the ruined city.

[long-pause]

Pudding Lane is still there, a narrow little street running down the hill toward the river.

[pause]

There is no bakery there now. But there is a small plaque on the wall, which says that near this spot stood the house of Thomas Farriner, the King's baker, in which the Great Fire of London began, on the second of September, 1666.

[pause]

{img:pudding-lane-today-narrow-street-plaque-quiet}
And Seething Lane is still there, near the Tower of London, where Pepys lived. The Navy Office is gone. But there is a little garden there now, called the Seething Lane Garden, with a bust of Samuel Pepys, and little plaques in the paving, telling the story of his diary. And Saint Olave's church is still there, in Hart Street, with the marble bust of Elizabeth Pepys still looking down from the wall, and Samuel himself buried beneath the floor, beside her.

[long-pause]

The river Thames still flows past the City, wide and brown and tidal, rising and falling twice a day.

[pause]

There are many bridges now. London Bridge has been rebuilt several times, and has no houses on it any more. But the little boats of the watermen are gone, and the river is quieter than it was in Pepys's day, except for the tourist boats and the barges.

[pause]

{img:thames-evening-tower-bridge-lights-calm-water}
On an evening in late summer, when the sun goes down behind Westminster, and the river turns to gold, and the lights come on along the banks, and the bells of the old Wren churches ring out across the City, it is not hard to imagine the old city that Pepys knew, and loved, and wrote about, and watched burning from a little boat on the river, on a warm September night, long ago.

[long-pause]

So we leave London for tonight, the old city by the river, with its great white dome glowing in the twilight, and its church bells ringing across the quiet evening streets, and the river flowing softly under the bridges, toward the sea.
