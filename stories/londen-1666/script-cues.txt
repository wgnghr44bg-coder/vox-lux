{img:01-pudding-lane-bakery-night-1666-oven-glow}

In September 1666, old London Bridge still carried its crowded houses high above the Thames, and the long grey cathedral of St Paul's stood on its hill. Then, in the dark hours of a Sunday morning, a small fire began in a bakery in Pudding Lane.

[long-pause]

The Great Fire of London began in a bakery in Pudding Lane. The fire started in a small shop near the river. It belonged to a baker named Thomas Farriner. His bakery stood in Pudding Lane, a narrow street close to London Bridge. Farriner was not an ordinary baker. He supplied ship's biscuits to the Royal Navy. On the evening of Saturday the first of September, he finished his work and went to bed. He later swore that he had put out his oven. Yet some time after midnight, the house filled with smoke. The family woke and found the ground floor on fire. Farriner, his daughter and a servant escaped through an upper window and across the roofs. A maid in the house did not get out, and she died. Historians still do not know exactly how the fire began. A spark from the oven is the most likely cause. Whatever started it, that small blaze in Pudding Lane became one of the most famous fires in history.

[long-pause]

{img:02-lord-mayor-woken-night-candle-1666}

The Lord Mayor of London thought the fire was nothing to worry about. In the early hours of the morning, the Lord Mayor was woken and told about the fire. His name was Sir Thomas Bloodworth. He came to look at the burning houses. At that point, only a few buildings were alight. Fires were common in London, and most were put out quickly. Bloodworth was not impressed. According to a famous account, he said that a woman could put it out on her own. Then he went back to bed. Officials could have pulled down the houses around the fire to stop it spreading. That was the usual method. But the owners would have to be paid, and Bloodworth hesitated. Even so, it is easy to blame him with hindsight. No one could know that this fire would be different. Yet his slow reaction cost the city precious hours. By the time the sun rose, the fire had already reached the warehouses along the river.

[long-pause]

Should these unhurried hours by the river help you rest, a subscription keeps more of London's old stories within reach.

[long-pause]

{img:03-thames-street-warehouses-river-wharf-1666}

The fire quickly reached warehouses full of oil, tallow, coal and timber. Pudding Lane ran down towards Thames Street, close to the river. Along Thames Street stood warehouses and cellars packed with goods. There was oil, tar, pitch and tallow for candles. There were stacks of timber, coal and hemp for ropes. There was brandy and wine. These were almost perfect fuel for a fire. Once the flames reached them, the fire grew far too strong to stop with buckets. The wharves along the river also burned. This was a serious problem. The river was the main source of water for fighting fires. With the waterfront in flames, it became very hard to reach it. The fire also destroyed the waterwheels under London Bridge. Those wheels had pumped river water into pipes for part of the city. Even so, many people still did not panic. On Sunday morning, most Londoners thought the fire would soon be under control.

[long-pause]

{img:04-east-wind-sparks-flying-over-rooftops-dusk}

A strong east wind and a long dry summer helped the fire spread. The summer of 1666 had been unusually hot and dry. For weeks there had been very little rain. The timber houses of London were dry through and through. Then, in early September, a strong wind began to blow from the east. It carried sparks and burning pieces of wood over the rooftops. New fires started far from the main blaze. Houses in London were built very close together. Many upper floors leaned out over the street, so that the tops of houses on opposite sides almost touched. Fire could jump easily from one side to the other. The wind kept blowing for three days. Even so, the city had been warned many times. Laws already said that new houses should be built of brick or stone. Yet these rules were often ignored, because timber was cheaper. In September 1666, the price of that cheapness became clear.

[long-pause]

{img:05-samuel-pepys-window-watching-fire-night}

Samuel Pepys was woken at three in the morning to look at the fire. Much of what we know about the fire comes from a diary. It was written by Samuel Pepys, an official at the Navy Office. In the early hours of Sunday, his maid Jane woke him. She had seen the fire from a window and was worried. Pepys got up, put on his nightgown and looked out. He thought the fire was far enough away. So he went back to bed. A few hours later, he rose again. Jane told him that some three hundred houses had burned during the night. Pepys walked to the Tower of London and climbed up to get a better view. From there he saw a great fire around the end of London Bridge. He took a boat on the river to look more closely. Even so, at first he wrote calmly. Like many Londoners, he did not yet understand what was happening. His diary records how that calm slowly turned to fear.

[long-pause]

{img:06-people-carrying-goods-to-river-boats-1666}

Many Londoners loaded their belongings into boats on the Thames. On Sunday, Pepys watched people trying to save their things. Some threw their goods into lighters and small boats on the river. Others carried them from house to house, moving them again and again as the fire came closer. The poor stayed in their homes until the flames almost touched them. Then they ran to the boats or climbed down the stairs to the water. Pepys noticed something touching. The pigeons did not want to leave their houses. They flew around the windows and balconies until some of them burned their wings and fell. The river became crowded with boats full of furniture, bedding and boxes. Watermen charged high prices to carry goods away. Even so, the river was a place of safety. Fire could not cross water easily. For many families, a small boat on the Thames held everything they still owned.

[long-pause]

{img:07-pepys-boat-river-whitehall-palace-1666}

Pepys went by boat to Whitehall to tell the king about the fire. Later that Sunday morning, Pepys made an important decision. He took a boat up the river to Whitehall Palace, where the king lived. There he told King Charles the Second and his brother James, the Duke of York, what he had seen. He told them that unless houses were pulled down, nothing could stop the fire. The king listened. He ordered Pepys to go to the Lord Mayor with a message. The Lord Mayor should pull down houses in every direction. Pepys found Bloodworth in the street, looking exhausted, with a handkerchief round his neck. The Mayor said that people would not obey him. He said he had been pulling down houses, but the fire was faster. Even so, the royal order gave the work new authority. Yet it was already very late. By Sunday evening, the fire stretched in a great arc along the river.

[long-pause]

{img:08-fire-arc-across-city-seen-from-bankside-night}

Pepys described the fire at night as a great arch of flame, a mile long. On Sunday night, Pepys and his wife watched the fire from a small alehouse on the south bank of the river. As darkness fell, the fire looked bigger and more frightening. Pepys described it as an arch of fire, stretching from one side of the city to the other. He guessed it was about a mile long. He could hear the cracking of houses as they fell. He wrote that it made him weep to see it. From the south bank, the whole city seemed to glow. Smoke rose high into the sky and was carried away by the wind. Even so, few people died in the fire itself. Most had time to escape. The fire moved quickly, but people could see it coming. Yet the loss of homes was enormous. Families watched from the other side of the river as their streets disappeared.

[long-pause]

{img:09-house-being-pulled-down-firehook-crowd-1666}

To stop the fire, men tried to pull down houses with long iron hooks. London had no fire brigade in 1666. Each parish kept some simple equipment. There were leather buckets, ladders and long poles with large iron hooks at the end. These firehooks were used to pull down houses. The idea was to create a gap that the fire could not cross. Men climbed up, fixed the hooks and pulled with ropes until walls and roofs fell. There were also a few hand-pumped fire engines. They were small and hard to move through the narrow streets. Some of them fell into the river or were lost in the flames. Even so, pulling down houses was the right idea. The problem was speed. The wind blew the fire across the gaps before they were wide enough. Yet the firefighters kept working day and night. Many of them were ordinary citizens, soldiers and sailors who simply joined in.

[long-pause]

{img:10-pepys-burying-cheese-wine-garden-night-1666}

Pepys buried his wine and his Parmesan cheese in his garden. By Tuesday the fourth of September, the fire was getting close to Pepys's own house. He lived in Seething Lane, near the Tower of London. He sent most of his valuable things away by cart. Then he did something that many people still remember. With a colleague, Sir William Penn, he dug a pit in the garden. In it they put their wine. Pepys also buried his Parmesan cheese and some other things. Parmesan was an expensive Italian cheese, and he clearly valued it. He also buried official papers from the Navy Office. It sounds amusing today. Yet it was a sensible thing to do. A hole in the earth was one of the safest places during a fire. Gold, silver and papers were often buried in the same way. In the end, Pepys's house survived. Historians still smile at the picture of an important official, digging by lantern light to save his cheese.

[long-pause]

{img:11-old-st-pauls-cathedral-nave-books-stored-1666}

The booksellers of London stored their books in St Paul's Cathedral for safety. The area around St Paul's Cathedral was the centre of the book trade. Booksellers and printers had their shops in the churchyard. When the fire came closer, they thought of a safe place for their stock. They carried thousands of books down into the crypt of the cathedral. The crypt was underground, with thick stone walls. Surely the fire could not reach it there. Other people also stored their goods inside the great church. The cathedral seemed solid and safe. It was built of stone and had stood for centuries. Yet there was a problem. The cathedral had wooden scaffolding around it, because repairs were under way. Its huge roof was covered with lead, laid on timber. On Tuesday evening, the fire reached St Paul's. The scaffolding caught first. Then the roof began to burn. The booksellers lost almost everything. Their loss was one of the great cultural disasters of the fire.

[long-pause]

{img:12-st-pauls-burning-roof-glow-night-distance}

Melted lead from the roof of St Paul's ran through the streets like a river. The burning of St Paul's was the most dramatic moment of the fire. The roof timbers caught alight and burned fiercely. The lead that covered the roof began to melt. It poured down from the building. Witnesses said it flowed through the streets around the cathedral. Stones cracked and burst in the heat. Parts of the vaulted roof fell into the nave. The writer John Evelyn walked through the ruins a few days later. He described the stones as calcined, burned almost to powder, and the ground still too hot to walk on. He saw that the great church was a ruin. The books in the crypt had burned for days. Even so, some parts of the walls were still standing. At first, people hoped the old cathedral could be repaired. Yet the damage was too great. A completely new cathedral would one day rise in its place.

[long-pause]

{img:13-king-charles-passing-buckets-fire-line-1666}

King Charles the Second and his brother helped fight the fire themselves. The king did not stay safely in his palace. Charles the Second and his brother James, the Duke of York, came into the city to help. James was put in charge of the firefighting. He rode from place to place, giving orders and encouraging the men. Soldiers were brought in to help and to keep order. Both brothers were seen working with their own hands. According to several accounts, the king passed buckets of water along a line and stood up to his ankles in mud and water. He also handed out money to the workers to keep them going. People were impressed. Kings did not usually do such things. Even so, the royal brothers also had a practical reason. They feared that the fire might cause disorder or even rebellion. Yet their presence made a real difference to the morale of the city. Many Londoners remembered their courage for years.

[long-pause]

{img:14-gunpowder-firebreak-houses-blown-up-dust}

On the last days of the fire, houses were blown up with gunpowder to make firebreaks. Pulling down houses by hand was too slow. So on Tuesday, a new method was tried. Gunpowder was brought from the Tower of London and the navy stores. Houses in the path of the fire were blown up. The explosions brought them down much faster than hooks and ropes. This created wide gaps that the fire could not easily jump. Seamen from the naval dockyards were skilled at this kind of work. Pepys himself suggested bringing men from the dockyards to help. Near the Tower, houses were blown up to protect the fortress, which held a great store of gunpowder. If that store had exploded, the damage would have been terrible. Even so, the method was not without risk. Some people were angry that their houses were destroyed before the fire even reached them. Yet the firebreaks worked. Together with a change in the weather, they finally began to hold the fire back.

[long-pause]

{img:15-wind-dropping-smoke-rising-quiet-dawn-1666}

On Tuesday night, the east wind finally dropped. For three days, the wind had driven the fire westward. Then, on the night of Tuesday the fourth of September, the wind began to die down. This was the turning point. Without the wind, the fire no longer jumped across the gaps. The firebreaks made with gunpowder now held. By Wednesday, the firefighters were able to put out many smaller fires. Some flames broke out again in a few places, but they were brought under control. By Thursday the sixth of September, the Great Fire was largely over. The ground, however, stayed hot for a long time. Cellars full of coal and goods smouldered for weeks. In some places, smoke still rose months later. Even so, the city could finally breathe again. Pepys climbed the steeple of a church near his house and looked out. He called it the saddest sight of desolation he had ever seen.

[long-pause]

{img:16-ruins-old-london-walls-chimneys-smoke-morning}

The fire destroyed about thirteen thousand houses and eighty-seven churches. When the fire was over, the people of London counted the damage. It was enormous. Around thirteen thousand two hundred houses had burned. Eighty-seven parish churches were destroyed, along with St Paul's Cathedral. The Guildhall, the Royal Exchange and many company halls were gone. Most of the old walled city, the part known as the City of London, was in ruins. Around seventy thousand people had lost their homes. That was most of the people who lived inside the walls. Yet the official number of deaths was very small. Only a handful of deaths were recorded. Some historians think the real number was higher, because the deaths of poor people were not always written down. Even so, there is no proof of thousands dying. The fire was above all a disaster of homes and property. A whole old city had vanished in four days.

[long-pause]

{img:17-london-wall-gate-ludgate-timber-houses-1666}

In 1666, the old City of London was still surrounded by a Roman and medieval wall. Before the fire, London was a city of two parts. At its heart was the old City of London. It was still surrounded by a wall, first built by the Romans and later repaired many times. Gates in the wall had names that are still known today, like Ludgate, Newgate, Aldgate, Bishopsgate and Moorgate. Inside the wall, about eighty thousand people lived in crowded streets. Outside the wall, new suburbs had grown in every direction. To the west lay Westminster, where the king, Parliament and the great lords lived. Across the river lay Southwark. Altogether, greater London had perhaps four or five hundred thousand people. It was one of the largest cities in Europe. Even so, the walls no longer had much military purpose. They were mostly a boundary and a place to collect tolls. Yet they still shaped the city, and the fire stopped not far from them in several places.

[long-pause]

{img:18-jettied-timber-houses-narrow-street-overhanging}

The upper floors of many London houses leaned out over the street. Many houses in old London were built with timber frames. The spaces between the beams were filled with lath and plaster. To gain more space, builders made each upper floor stick out further than the one below. This was called jettying. In narrow streets, the top floors on opposite sides came very close together. Sometimes neighbours could almost shake hands across the street from their upper windows. The streets below were dark, even at midday. The houses were often four or five floors high. Shops were on the ground floor, and families lived above. Even so, these buildings had a certain charm. Their carved beams and painted plaster were admired by some visitors. Yet they were also dangerous. Wood and plaster burned easily, and the narrow gaps let fire leap across streets. After 1666, new laws banned this way of building in the City.

[long-pause]

{img:19-hanging-shop-signs-cheapside-street-1666}

Houses in London had no street numbers, so people used painted signs. In 1666, there were no numbers on London's houses. Instead, shops and many houses had signs. They hung out over the street on iron brackets. Each sign showed a picture, because many people could not read well. There were golden keys, black swans, red lions, bells, anchors and Bibles. An address might be given as "at the sign of the Golden Ball, in Cheapside". People found their way by these pictures. Some signs became very large and heavy. They creaked and swung in the wind. Occasionally one fell and hurt someone below. Even so, the signs made the streets colourful and lively. They also became part of the identity of each business. Many London inns and pubs still carry names that come from these old signs. Yet the system was confusing for strangers. In the 1760s, a law ordered most signs to be taken down, and numbers slowly replaced them.

[long-pause]

{img:20-water-carrier-wooden-tankard-conduit-street}

Many Londoners bought their water from water carriers who filled tall wooden containers at public conduits. Getting water was part of daily life in old London. Some came from wells and pumps. Some came from public conduits, stone fountains fed by pipes from springs outside the city. Water carriers filled tall wooden containers at the conduits. They carried them on their shoulders to houses and sold the water by the load. There were also newer systems. From 1582, waterwheels under London Bridge pumped river water into pipes. In 1613, a long channel called the New River was opened. It brought clean water from springs in Hertfordshire, about forty miles away. Wooden pipes carried it into the houses of people who could pay. Even so, many poor people still relied on the river and on shallow wells. Water from these sources was often dirty. Yet the New River was a remarkable achievement for its time. Parts of it still supply London with water today.

[long-pause]

{img:21-sea-coal-smoke-chimneys-over-roofs-1666}

London was already famous for its smoky air in the 1660s. Londoners burned a great deal of coal. It came by ship from Newcastle in the north and was called sea coal. Coal was cheaper than wood, and it burned hot. It was used in homes, bakeries, breweries and workshops. The result was smoke. On still days, a grey haze hung over the city. It blackened walls, stained clothes and made people cough. In 1661, the writer John Evelyn wrote a small book about it. He called it Fumifugium. In it he complained to the king about the smoke of London. He suggested moving smoky industries out of the city and planting sweet-smelling gardens around it. His ideas were not carried out. Even so, his little book is remembered as one of the first writings about air pollution. London's smoke would become even worse over the next two centuries. Yet Evelyn's complaint shows that people already understood the problem long ago.

[long-pause]

{img:22-night-lantern-hanging-outside-door-street}

Householders were required to hang lanterns outside their doors on dark nights. There were no street lamps in London in 1666. At night, the streets were very dark. To help a little, the city had rules. Householders on main streets were supposed to hang a lantern with a candle outside their doors on dark evenings. This was usually required during the winter months and on nights without a moon. The lanterns gave only a small pool of light. People going out at night often hired a link boy. He was a boy who carried a burning torch and walked ahead to light the way. Watchmen also patrolled with lanterns and called out the hours. Even so, the dark streets could be unsafe. Many people stayed indoors after nightfall. Pepys mentions walking home with a link boy several times in his diary. Yet the little lights at doorways gave the city a quiet, warm look on winter nights.

[long-pause]

{img:23-river-thames-watermen-wherries-steps-1666}

The fastest way across London was often by boat on the Thames. The streets of London were narrow and crowded. So the river was the city's main road. Thousands of watermen worked on the Thames. They rowed small boats called wherries. People waited at stone steps along the riverbanks and called for a boat. A waterman would row them up or down the river, or across to the other side. The fares were set by law. Pepys used boats almost every day. He travelled to Whitehall, to the dockyards and to the theatres across the river. Watermen were known for their strong arms and their rough language. They shouted to attract passengers and argued with each other over customers. Even so, travel by river was usually pleasant. It was faster than walking and cleaner than the muddy streets. On a calm evening, the Thames was full of small boats gliding between the lights of the city.

[long-pause]

{img:24-pepys-writing-diary-shorthand-candle-study}

Samuel Pepys wrote his famous diary in a secret shorthand. Samuel Pepys kept his diary from January 1660 to May 1669. He wrote almost every day, often late in the evening. He used a system of shorthand, a fast way of writing with special symbols. It was not a true secret code. Many educated people of the time could learn it. Yet it kept his words private from servants and family. Pepys wrote about everything. He described great events, like the return of the king and the Great Fire. He also wrote about his meals, his clothes, his quarrels and his worries. He was very honest, even about his own faults. In the end, the diary filled six volumes and more than a million words. Pepys left it to his old college in Cambridge. It sat unread for more than a century. Then, in the 1820s, a student decoded it. Since then it has become one of the most loved diaries in the English language.

[long-pause]

{img:25-navy-office-clerks-desks-papers-seething-lane}

Pepys worked at the Navy Office, where he helped supply the king's ships. Pepys was not born rich. His father was a tailor. Yet Pepys was clever and hard-working, and he had a powerful relative. Through this cousin, he got a job at the Navy Office in 1660. His title was Clerk of the Acts. The office managed the supplies for the Royal Navy. It bought timber, rope, sails, food and guns. It paid the sailors and the shipbuilders. Pepys threw himself into the work. He studied arithmetic so that he could check the accounts. He learned about the different kinds of timber and rope. He visited the dockyards often. He also accepted gifts from merchants who wanted contracts, which was common at the time. Even so, he became one of the most capable officials in the country. Later in his life, he rose to the highest office in the navy administration. Many historians see him as one of the founders of a professional navy.

[long-pause]

{img:26-pepys-wife-elizabeth-portrait-room-window}

Pepys married Elizabeth St Michel when she was only fifteen years old. Pepys's wife was named Elizabeth. Her father was a French Protestant who had come to England. She and Samuel married in 1655, when she was fifteen and he was twenty-two. They were poor at first and lived in a single room. Their marriage was loving, but often difficult. The diary records many quarrels and many reconciliations. Pepys could be jealous and controlling. He was also unfaithful to her, and he wrote about it in his diary. Elizabeth was lively and fond of music, dancing and fashion. She kept dogs and caged birds. She sometimes felt lonely, because the couple had no children. Even so, the diary also shows tenderness. Pepys worried when she was ill and enjoyed walks and outings with her. Elizabeth died of a fever in 1669, at only twenty-nine. Pepys never married again. He placed a fine monument to her in their parish church.

[long-pause]

{img:27-seething-lane-house-garden-navy-office-1666}

Pepys lived in a house attached to the Navy Office in Seething Lane. The Navy Office stood in Seething Lane, close to the Tower of London. The officials lived in houses that were part of the office buildings. Pepys and his family had rooms there, with a small garden behind. He could walk from his bedroom to his desk in a minute. His neighbours were his colleagues, including Sir William Penn. Penn's son, also called William, later founded the colony of Pennsylvania. The two families visited each other often, played cards and argued. Pepys worshipped at the nearby church of St Olave, Hart Street. He and Elizabeth had their own pew in a gallery. The house was improved over the years as Pepys grew richer. He added new rooms, new furniture and new decorations. Even so, it was a working house, full of papers and visitors. During the Great Fire, it survived, though the flames came very close. The church of St Olave also survived and still stands today.

[long-pause]

{img:28-pepys-playing-flageolet-music-evening-room}

Pepys loved music and played several instruments. Music was one of the great pleasures of Pepys's life. He played the lute, the viol and the flageolet, a small kind of wooden flute. He also sang, and he wrote a few songs of his own. One of them was a setting of words by Shakespeare. Pepys often made music with friends in the evening. He sometimes played on the leads, the flat roof of his house, on warm summer nights. He took his flageolet with him on journeys. In his diary he wrote that music was the thing of the world that he loved most. He paid for singing lessons for himself and his wife. He even took servants partly because they could sing. Even so, he was not a professional. He was an enthusiastic amateur, like many educated people of his time. Yet his love of music fills the pages of the diary. It reminds readers that daily life in 1666 was not only work and worry.

[long-pause]

{img:29-pepys-books-library-glass-cases-shelves}

Pepys built a library of about three thousand books, which still survives. Pepys was a passionate collector of books. He bought them throughout his life. He arranged them carefully, ordering them by size so that the shelves looked neat. When his shelves were full, he had special bookcases made. They had glass doors and were built of oak. They are among the earliest bookcases of their kind in England. By the end of his life, he owned around three thousand books. He also collected prints, maps, music and old ballads. When he died in 1703, he left the library to Magdalene College in Cambridge. It is still there, in its original bookcases, in the same order he gave it. His diary is part of the collection. Even so, the library also holds many ordinary things. There are popular songs, cheap pamphlets and little books for children. Pepys saved things that others threw away. Thanks to him, much of the everyday print of the 1600s survives.

[long-pause]

{img:30-kitchen-maid-hearth-pots-london-1666}

Even a middle-class family in London usually had servants. In 1666, servants were a normal part of household life. Even fairly modest families employed at least one maid. The Pepys household had several servants over the years. There was usually a cook maid, a chamber maid and a boy. Servants lived in the house and ate with the family or in the kitchen. Their wages were low, but they received food, a bed and sometimes clothing. A maid's work began before dawn. She lit the fires, fetched water, cooked, cleaned and washed. Most servants were young. Many came from the countryside and planned to marry after a few years in service. Pepys wrote a great deal about his servants. Some he liked very much. Others he quarrelled with or dismissed. Even so, the relationship was close in many ways. Servants knew the family's secrets. In the diary, they appear as real people with their own lives and moods.

[long-pause]

{img:31-dinner-table-london-1666-meat-pies-pewter}

The main meal of the day was eaten around midday and was called dinner. Londoners in the 1660s ate their main meal at noon or in the early afternoon. It was called dinner. Breakfast was small, often just bread and ale. Supper in the evening was usually light too. Dinner, though, could be large. For those who could afford it, meat was central. There was beef, mutton, pork, chicken, rabbit and fish. Pies were very popular, filled with meat, fish or fruit. Vegetables were eaten too, though less than today. Pepys described many dinners in his diary. Once, for a special occasion, he served a great feast to friends. It included rabbits, a leg of mutton, fowls, a tart and much else. Even so, poorer families ate much more simply. Bread, cheese, pottage and a little meat on Sundays were common. Ale and beer were drunk by everyone, even children, because water was not always safe. Meals were also a time for family and friends to gather and talk.

[long-pause]

{img:32-first-cup-of-tea-1660-table-china-cup}

Pepys recorded drinking a cup of tea for the first time in 1660. Tea was a very new drink in England in the 1660s. It came from China, carried by Dutch and English ships. It was expensive and rare. On the twenty-fifth of September 1660, Pepys wrote that he sent for a cup of tee, a China drink, which he had never drunk before. This is one of the earliest mentions of tea drinking in England. At first, tea was sold in coffee houses as a kind of medicine. People claimed it helped with headaches and tiredness. Later, Pepys came home to find his wife making tea. An apothecary had told her it was good for her cold. Even so, tea was not yet the national drink. Ale and beer were far more common. Yet tea's popularity grew quickly, especially after Charles the Second married a Portuguese princess who loved it. Within a hundred years, tea was drunk all over the country, by rich and poor alike.

[long-pause]

{img:33-clothes-fashion-silk-coat-wig-1666}

In the 1660s, fashionable men began wearing large curled wigs. Fashion changed quickly after the king returned in 1660. Charles the Second had lived in France for years, and French styles came with him. Fashionable men wore long curled wigs that fell over their shoulders. Pepys bought his first wig in 1663. He worried about it at first, but he soon got used to it. Wigs were made from real human hair and were expensive. They also needed regular care, because they could get lice. In 1666, the king introduced a new style of dress. It was a long coat with a matching waistcoat underneath. This is often seen as the origin of the modern suit. Pepys followed fashion closely. He spent large sums on clothes, and he recorded each new purchase with pleasure. Even so, most people dressed far more simply, in wool and linen. Yet the fashions of the wealthy were visible everywhere on the streets.

[long-pause]

{img:34-washing-day-linen-tub-yard-maid-1666}

Washing day came only every few weeks, and it was a great effort. Washing clothes in 1666 was hard, slow work. In many households, the big wash happened once a month or even less often. On washing day, maids rose very early. They heated large amounts of water in coppers or pots. The linen was soaked, scrubbed, beaten and rinsed. Lye, made from wood ash, was used to clean it. Soap was also used, but it was expensive. Then everything was wrung out by hand and hung to dry. In bad weather, wet linen hung all over the house for days. Pepys mentions washing days in his diary, often with some annoyance. The house was full of steam and wet cloth. Even so, clean linen was very important. People did not wash their bodies often, but they changed their shirts and shifts. White linen next to the skin was thought to keep the body clean. Clean collars and cuffs were a sign of respect.

[long-pause]

{img:35-apothecary-shop-jars-shelves-london-1666}

When people were ill, they often went first to the apothecary. Medicine in 1666 was very different from today. Doctors were expensive, and many of their treatments did little good. Most people went first to an apothecary. Apothecaries sold medicines from shops lined with painted jars. They made pills, syrups and ointments from plants, minerals and sometimes animal parts. Many remedies were based on old ideas about four humours in the body. Bleeding, or letting blood, was a common treatment. Pepys himself suffered from a painful bladder stone. In 1658, before the diary began, he had a dangerous operation to remove it. He survived, which was quite lucky. Every year afterwards, on the anniversary, he held a small celebration to give thanks. He kept the stone in a little case. Even so, not all medicine was useless. Some herbal remedies did help. Yet illness was a constant fear. Families relied on prayer, luck and the care of those around them.

[long-pause]

{img:36-children-playing-street-old-london-1666}

Many London children started work by the age of seven or eight. Childhood in London in the 1660s was short for most children. In poor families, children worked from an early age. Boys and girls ran errands, helped in shops and looked after younger brothers and sisters. Some sold small things in the streets. Others swept chimneys, a hard and dangerous job. Around the age of fourteen, many boys became apprentices. They served a master for seven years to learn a trade. Girls often went into service as maids. Children from wealthier families had more time for school. Boys learned Latin and arithmetic. Girls learned to read, write, sew and play music. Yet all children found time for play. They played with tops, balls, hoops and dolls. They played games like blind man's buff and leapfrog. Even so, many children did not survive to adulthood. Disease took many young lives. Families loved their children deeply, and they grieved for those they lost.

[long-pause]

{img:37-old-london-bridge-houses-arches-1666-river}

Old London Bridge was lined with houses and shops along its whole length. In 1666, there was only one bridge across the Thames in London. It had been built of stone in the late 1100s and early 1200s. It stood on nineteen narrow arches. Along the top ran a street lined with houses and shops. Some of the buildings were five or six floors high. They hung out over the river on both sides. The roadway between them was narrow and crowded. Carts, horses and walkers all squeezed through. At the southern end stood a great gatehouse. Near the middle stood a famous building called Nonsuch House. It had been made in the Netherlands, then shipped to London in pieces and put together with wooden pegs. Even so, the bridge was always busy and often in need of repair. Yet it was one of the wonders of London. Visitors from abroad came to see it and to walk across a street that stood above a river.

[long-pause]

{img:38-shooting-the-bridge-boat-rapids-arches}

Going under London Bridge by boat was so dangerous that many passengers got out and walked. The arches of old London Bridge were narrow. The stone piers that held them up were wide and bulky. Together, they blocked much of the river. When the tide flowed in or out, the water rushed through the narrow gaps. On one side, the water could be much higher than on the other. The river roared through the arches like a small waterfall. Boatmen called passing through them shooting the bridge. It was exciting and dangerous. Boats sometimes overturned, and people drowned. Many cautious passengers preferred to get out before the bridge. They walked around it and joined their boat again on the other side. There was a saying that London Bridge was made for wise men to go over and fools to go under. Even so, skilled watermen shot the bridge every day. Pepys did it often, though sometimes he chose to walk around instead.

[long-pause]

{img:39-london-bridge-gap-burned-1633-houses-north-end}

An earlier fire in 1633 left a gap on London Bridge that helped save Southwark in 1666. London Bridge had suffered from fires before. In 1633, a fire destroyed many houses at the northern end of the bridge. Afterwards, some of those buildings were not rebuilt. An open space remained on the bridge. In 1666, the Great Fire reached the bridge from the city side. It burned the houses at the northern end. But when it reached the gap left by the earlier fire, it could not easily jump across. The open space acted as a firebreak. As a result, the fire did not spread along the whole bridge. Southwark, on the south bank, was largely saved. Even so, the bridge was damaged, and its waterwheels were destroyed. Yet it is a curious twist of history. A disaster in 1633 helped prevent a greater one thirty-three years later. London Bridge was repaired, and houses stood on it until the 1750s.

[long-pause]

{img:40-billingsgate-fish-market-river-boats-1666}

Billingsgate fish market was famous for its loud and colourful language. Near London Bridge stood Billingsgate, the city's great landing place for fish. Boats brought in fish from the North Sea, the river and the coasts. Fishwives bought it there and carried it through the streets in baskets on their heads. They sold herring, mackerel, oysters, cod and eels. The market was loud and crowded. The fishwives of Billingsgate were known for their strong voices and their rough words. Their swearing was so famous that the word billingsgate came to mean coarse language. Yet the fishwives were also hard-working women who supported their families. Many were widows. Billingsgate had been a market for centuries, and it continued long after the fire. The fish market stayed near the river until the 1980s, when it moved to a new site. Even so, the old name lives on. People in London still speak of billingsgate when they mean a stream of colourful insults.

[long-pause]

{img:41-ships-moored-pool-of-london-masts-tower}

Below London Bridge, the river was crowded with ships from all over the world. Large ships could not pass under London Bridge. So they stopped below it, in a stretch of river called the Pool of London. Here hundreds of ships lay at anchor or tied up at wharves. Their masts rose like a forest above the water. They came from the Baltic, the Netherlands, France, Spain, Italy and the Americas. They brought timber, wine, spices, sugar, tobacco and cloth. They carried away English wool, cloth and other goods. Small boats called lighters carried cargo between the ships and the shore. Customs officers inspected the goods and collected taxes. The trade made London rich. Merchants built fine houses and great halls. Even so, 1666 was a difficult year. England was at war with the Dutch, and trade suffered. Yet the Pool of London remained the busy heart of the nation's trade. It would stay that way for nearly three hundred more years.

[long-pause]

{img:42-swans-on-thames-calm-evening-1666}

The swans on the Thames belonged to the king and to two London companies. The Thames was full of swans in the 1600s. Visitors often mentioned them. They glided along the river among the boats. In England, swans were special birds. By old law, all unmarked mute swans on open waters belonged to the Crown. Two of the London livery companies, the Vintners and the Dyers, also had the right to own swans. Every year, a ceremony called swan upping took place. Men in boats rowed up the river and caught the young swans. They marked them to show who owned them. Swans were also eaten at grand feasts, though they were a rare and expensive dish. Even so, most Londoners simply enjoyed watching them. The swans were part of the calm beauty of the river. Swan upping still takes place on the Thames every summer. Today, though, it is done to count and protect the birds, not to mark them for the table.

[long-pause]

{img:43-cheapside-market-stalls-crowds-1666}

Cheapside was the main shopping street of old London. Cheapside was the broadest and busiest street in the old city. Its name came from an old English word for market. For centuries, it had been the place to buy and sell. Stalls filled the middle of the street. Along both sides stood shops. The goldsmiths had their shops in a famous row called Goldsmiths' Row. Their windows glittered with silver plate and jewels. Goldsmiths also acted as early bankers. Merchants left money with them for safekeeping. Cheapside was also a place for celebrations. Processions passed along it, and crowds gathered for royal events. Even so, it was not always a pleasant street. It was noisy, crowded and dirty. Yet it was the heart of the city's trade. The fire destroyed Cheapside completely. It was rebuilt in brick, and the street still exists today. Many of its side streets keep the names of the old trades, like Bread Street, Milk Street and Wood Street.

[long-pause]

{img:44-street-seller-crying-wares-basket-1666}

Street sellers walked the city shouting the names of their goods. Shops and markets were not the only places to buy things. The streets were full of travelling sellers. Each had a special cry. Sellers called out to sell oranges, apples, cherries and oysters. Others sold matches, brooms, ballads, ink and milk. Some offered services instead. There were knife grinders, chair menders and rat catchers. Their cries rose above the noise of the street. Many were half sung, with a tune that everyone recognised. People could tell what was for sale before they saw it. Artists later made famous sets of pictures called the Cries of London. They showed these sellers with their baskets and goods. Even so, life for the street sellers was hard. Many were poor women and children. They walked many miles a day for a small profit. Yet their voices were part of the music of old London, from early morning to dusk.

[long-pause]

{img:45-royal-exchange-courtyard-merchants-1666}

Merchants from all over Europe met at the Royal Exchange to do business. The Royal Exchange was a great building in the heart of the city. It was founded by a rich merchant named Sir Thomas Gresham. It opened in 1571. Its design was based on the exchange in Antwerp. It had a large open courtyard surrounded by covered walkways. Merchants met there every day to buy and sell goods and make deals. Upstairs there were small shops selling fine goods, like lace, gloves and perfume. Different groups of merchants stood in their own areas. There was a place for those who traded with Spain, another for the Baltic and so on. Pepys visited often, to hear news and meet people. Even so, the Exchange was not only for business. It was also a fashionable place to walk and be seen. The Great Fire destroyed the building. It was rebuilt and later burned again in 1838. The present Royal Exchange is the third on the same site.

[long-pause]

{img:46-leadenhall-market-poultry-meat-stalls-1666}

Leadenhall Market was one of the largest meat and poultry markets in Europe. Leadenhall was one of the great markets of London. It took its name from an old mansion with a lead roof that had once stood on the site. By the 1600s, it was a busy market for meat, poultry, game, leather and wool. Visitors from abroad were amazed by its size. One foreign traveller wrote that he had never seen so much meat in one place. Beef, mutton, chickens, ducks and geese were sold in great quantities. The market survived the Great Fire with some damage. Afterwards, it was rebuilt with three covered courtyards. Even so, Leadenhall's history goes back much further. The site is near the centre of Roman London. Archaeologists have found remains of the great Roman hall beneath it. Today, Leadenhall Market has a beautiful Victorian roof. Yet it is still a market, and people still come to eat and shop there, as they have for centuries.

[long-pause]

{img:47-book-stalls-st-pauls-churchyard-1666}

St Paul's Churchyard was the centre of the English book trade. Around the old cathedral of St Paul's stood many small shops. Most of them belonged to booksellers and printers. For more than a hundred years, this had been the centre of the book trade in England. Each shop had its own sign. Books were sold unbound, as loose printed sheets. The buyer chose a binding later. People came to browse, buy and hear the latest news. New plays, sermons, poems and pamphlets appeared there first. Pepys was a regular visitor. He often stopped to look at books on his way through the city. Sometimes he read a book in the shop without buying it. The booksellers belonged to a guild called the Stationers' Company. It controlled the printing of books in England. Even so, the trade suffered terribly in 1666. When the fire came, the booksellers lost not only their shops but also the stock they had stored in the cathedral. Many were ruined.

[long-pause]

{img:48-goldsmith-banker-counting-coins-scales-1666}

London's goldsmiths also acted as some of the first bankers. In the 1600s, there were no banks in London as we know them today. Instead, people turned to goldsmiths. Goldsmiths had strong rooms and safes to protect their gold and silver. Merchants began to leave their money with them for safekeeping. The goldsmith gave them a written receipt. Soon people began passing these receipts to others as payment. In this way, the receipts became an early kind of paper money. Goldsmiths also lent money and paid interest on deposits. Some lent large sums to the king himself. This was risky, because the king did not always repay. Pepys kept some of his money with a goldsmith. In 1667, when Dutch warships sailed into the Medway, he feared for it and sent his gold to be buried in the country. Even so, the goldsmiths played an important role in London's economy. Their work helped lay the foundations for modern banking. Some old London banks can trace their roots back to goldsmiths of this period.

[long-pause]

{img:49-new-spring-garden-vauxhall-paths-trees-1666}

On summer evenings, Pepys liked to take a boat across the river to the New Spring Garden at Vauxhall. Across the Thames, in the fields of Lambeth, lay a pleasure garden. It was called the New Spring Garden. Later it became famous as Vauxhall Gardens. In the 1660s, it was a simple and pleasant place. There were shady walks, hedges and trees. Visitors could buy food and drink and listen to music. Entry was free, and people paid only for what they ate. Pepys went there many times. He usually travelled by boat with his wife or friends. He enjoyed hearing the nightingales and other birds singing in the trees. He also liked to watch the people. Fine ladies, young apprentices and families all came to walk. Even so, the garden could become rowdy late in the evening. Pepys sometimes complained about the behaviour of young gallants. Yet for most visitors, it was a calm escape from the noise of the city. For a few pennies, Londoners could spend a summer evening among trees.

[long-pause]

{img:50-st-james-park-canal-pelicans-ducks-1666}

In 1664, the Russian ambassador gave King Charles a pair of pelicans for St James's Park. St James's Park lay beside the royal palaces. After 1660, Charles the Second had it redesigned. A long straight canal was dug down the middle. Rows of trees were planted along it. The king loved the park. He walked there almost every day, often very fast, so that his courtiers struggled to keep up. He liked to feed the ducks. He also kept many birds and animals there. In 1664, the Russian ambassador gave the king a pair of pelicans. They lived on the water among the ducks. Visitors came to stare at these strange birds with their great beaks. Even so, the park was open to many ordinary people as well. Londoners strolled there and hoped to see the king. Today, pelicans still live in St James's Park. They are descendants of later gifts, but they continue a tradition that began more than three hundred and fifty years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:51-pall-mall-game-long-alley-mallet-ball}

The king played a game called pall mall, which gave its name to a famous London street. One of the king's favourite games was called pall mall. It came from France and Italy. Players used a long wooden mallet to hit a wooden ball along a straight alley. The aim was to send the ball through a hoop at the end in as few strokes as possible. It was a little like golf and croquet combined. The alley needed to be long and smooth. A special one was laid out near St James's Park. Its surface was made of crushed shells, which helped the ball roll well. Pepys watched the king and his brother play. He also noted with interest how the alley was kept smooth. Even so, the game was not new in 1660. It had been played in England for some decades. Yet its name lived on. The street that ran beside the old alley is still called Pall Mall. The nearby street called The Mall also takes its name from the game.

[long-pause]

{img:52-skating-frozen-canal-st-james-park-winter}

Pepys saw people skating on the frozen canal in St James's Park, a sport new to England. Winters in the 1600s were often colder than today. Historians call this period the Little Ice Age. Sometimes the Thames itself froze over. In December 1662, Pepys walked in St James's Park on a cold day. He saw people sliding on the ice of the new canal with skates. He wrote that it was a very pretty art. Skating had been popular in the Netherlands for a long time. Many courtiers had learned it during their exile there. They brought the sport back with them. John Evelyn also saw the skaters that same week. He described them sliding along with great speed. Even so, skating was still a novelty for most English people. Yet it soon caught on. On cold winters, Londoners gathered on frozen ponds and canals to skate, slide and play. A few years later, in 1683 and 1684, the Thames froze so hard that a whole fair was held on the ice.

[long-pause]

{img:53-bartholomew-fair-smithfield-booths-puppets}

Every August, Bartholomew Fair filled Smithfield with booths, puppet shows and crowds. Bartholomew Fair was one of the great events of the London year. It was held every August in Smithfield, just outside the city wall. It had begun in the Middle Ages as a cloth fair. By the 1600s, it was mostly about entertainment. There were rows of booths selling toys, gingerbread, ribbons and roast pork. There were puppet shows, rope dancers and performing animals. There were people showing strange curiosities, like giants and dwarfs and unusual animals. Pepys went to the fair several times. He enjoyed the puppet plays very much. Once he saw a horse that could count money. People of every rank mixed in the crowds. Even so, the fair had a bad reputation too. Pickpockets and tricksters were common. The authorities tried many times to control or shorten it. Yet it continued for centuries. It finally ended in the 1850s, after more than seven hundred years.

[long-pause]

{img:54-parish-church-sunday-sermon-pews-1666}

Going to church on Sunday was expected of everyone, and sermons could last an hour or more. Sunday in 1666 was a day for church. By law, people were expected to attend their parish church. Most did. They dressed in their best clothes and sat in wooden pews. Wealthy families often had their own boxed pews. The service included prayers, psalms and readings. The sermon was the centre of it. A good preacher could hold the attention of the congregation for an hour or more. Pepys went to church most Sundays. He commented on every sermon in his diary. Some he admired. Others he found dull. More than once, he admitted that he fell asleep. He also confessed that he spent some services looking at the pretty women in the congregation. Even so, religion mattered deeply to people of the time. Sunday was also a day of rest from work. In the afternoon, many families walked in the fields or visited friends. It was the quietest day of the week in the busy city.

[long-pause]

{img:55-islington-fields-country-path-walkers-1666}

On fine days, Londoners walked out to the villages of Islington and Hackney for cakes and ale. Just beyond the edge of London lay open countryside. A short walk north brought people to fields, farms and little villages. Islington was one of the most popular. It was known for its dairies, its inns and its fresh air. Pepys and his wife often walked or rode there on summer afternoons. They visited an old inn where they ate cheesecakes and drank ale. Other Londoners went to Hackney, Hampstead or across the river to the fields of Southwark. Families picnicked, children played, and young couples courted. Archers practised in the fields north of the city. The walk back in the evening was part of the pleasure. Even so, these villages were changing. London was growing quickly, and houses spread steadily into the fields. Today, Islington and Hackney are busy parts of the city. Yet for Londoners of 1666, they were a peaceful escape. A few miles on foot took them from the smoky streets into the green.

[long-pause]

{img:56-hyde-park-carriages-tour-circle-evening}

Fashionable Londoners drove their carriages in slow circles around Hyde Park to be seen. Hyde Park lay to the west of the city. It had been a royal hunting ground. In the 1600s, it was opened to the public. After the king returned, it became a fashionable place. Wealthy people drove their carriages around a circular track called the Ring, or the Tour. They went round and round slowly, greeting friends and looking at each other's clothes and horses. It was the place to see and be seen. Pepys longed to have his own carriage. For years, he could only watch, or hire a coach. Finally, in 1668, he bought one. He was very proud of it. He drove to the park with his wife and enjoyed being among the fashionable people. Even so, he worried about what others would think. Some might say he was showing off. Yet the habit of the evening drive continued for centuries. Hyde Park remained the favourite promenade of fashionable London for a very long time.

[long-pause]

{img:57-coffeehouse-interior-long-table-men-pamphlets-1666}

London's first coffeehouse opened in 1652, and within a few years there were dozens. Coffee was a new arrival in London. The first coffeehouse opened in 1652, in a small alley near Cornhill. It was run by a man named Pasqua Rosee, who came from the Ottoman lands. He had learned to make coffee there. At first, many people thought the bitter black drink was strange. Some said it tasted like soot. Yet coffeehouses quickly became popular. By the 1660s, there were many of them in the city. They were warm, well lit and full of talk. For a penny, a customer could come in, sit down and join the conversation. Men read newspapers and pamphlets there and argued about politics, trade and science. Because people learned so much in them, coffeehouses were sometimes called penny universities. Even so, they were mostly for men. Women rarely went inside. Yet the coffeehouse changed London life. Business, news and ideas now had a new home.

[long-pause]

{img:58-coffee-pot-fire-small-cups-counter}

Coffee in the 1660s was strong, bitter and served without milk. The coffee of 1666 was quite different from today. The beans were roasted over a fire and ground by hand. They were boiled in water in large pots. The drink was thick, strong and bitter. It was served in small dishes without handles. Some people added sugar. Milk was rarely used. Coffeehouses also sold chocolate, which was even more expensive, and tea, which was very new. Some sold sherbet, a sweet drink made with fruit. Alcohol was usually not served. That made coffeehouses different from taverns. People said that coffee kept the mind clear, while ale made it cloudy. Pepys drank coffee often. He visited coffeehouses to hear news and meet colleagues. Even so, not everyone liked them. Some critics claimed they were places of gossip and idleness. Yet their popularity kept growing. Some later coffeehouses became famous institutions, and one of them grew into a great insurance market.

[long-pause]

{img:59-tavern-interior-wooden-booths-ale-1666}

Taverns, inns and alehouses were found on almost every street in London. London had hundreds of places to drink. There were taverns, which mainly sold wine. There were alehouses, which sold ale and beer to ordinary people. There were inns, which offered rooms for travellers and stabling for horses. Each had its own sign hanging outside. Taverns were places for business as well as pleasure. Merchants signed contracts there. Friends met for dinner. Clubs and societies held their meetings in private rooms. Pepys visited taverns very often. He drank, ate and talked there with colleagues and friends. He also made promises to drink less, and he often broke them. Even so, these places were the social centre of the city. People of all ranks gathered in them. Some old London pubs still stand on the same sites. A few were rebuilt soon after the Great Fire. The Ye Olde Cheshire Cheese in Fleet Street was rebuilt in 1667 and still serves drinks today.

[long-pause]

{img:60-london-gazette-newspaper-printing-1666}

The London Gazette, first printed in 1665, is still published today. News in 1666 travelled more slowly than today. Yet Londoners were hungry for it. In November 1665, a new official newspaper appeared. It was printed in Oxford at first, because the court had fled there from the plague. It was called the Oxford Gazette. A few months later, when the court returned, it moved to the capital and became the London Gazette. It came out twice a week. It printed royal announcements, news from abroad and reports of naval battles. In September 1666, it published an account of the Great Fire. The printing office itself had to move during the fire. Even so, the Gazette was not the only source of news. People also read handwritten newsletters and cheap pamphlets. They heard news in coffeehouses and taverns. Yet the London Gazette was remarkable. It has been published without a break ever since. It is one of the oldest surviving newspapers in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:61-bills-of-mortality-printed-sheet-table}

Every week, London printed a list of the deaths in each parish, called the Bills of Mortality. Long before modern statistics, London kept count of its dead. Each week, parish clerks reported how many people had died and been baptised in their parish. The figures were collected and printed on a single sheet. These were called the Bills of Mortality. People bought them to see how healthy the city was. They also listed causes of death. Some of these causes sound strange today. They include grief, fright, teeth and being found dead in the street. The causes were often decided by searchers, usually elderly women, who looked at the bodies. They had no medical training. In 1662, a man named John Graunt studied the Bills carefully. He looked for patterns in births and deaths. His small book is now seen as one of the first works of statistics. Even so, the figures were not always accurate. Yet the Bills give a rare window into the lives and deaths of ordinary Londoners.

[long-pause]

{img:62-news-crowd-reading-notice-church-door}

Most people heard the news by word of mouth rather than reading it. Many Londoners in 1666 could read, at least a little. In the city, perhaps more than half of men could sign their names. Yet printed news was still not part of everyone's daily life. Most people heard about events by talking. They shared news in markets, churches, taverns and shops. Proclamations were read aloud in public places and nailed to church doors. Town criers rang their bells and called out announcements. Ballad sellers sang songs about recent events, and people bought the printed words. During the Great Fire, rumours spread very quickly. Some people believed that the French or the Dutch had started it. England was at war with both. Foreigners were attacked in the streets. Even so, the rumours were false. Yet they show how fear could travel faster than the truth. In a city with no radio or telephones, news moved from person to person, one conversation at a time.

[long-pause]

{img:63-old-st-pauls-exterior-square-tower-no-spire-1666}

Old St Paul's was one of the longest churches in Europe. The cathedral that burned in 1666 was not the one people see today. Old St Paul's was a huge Gothic building. It had been built over many years after a fire in 1087. It was finished in the 1300s. The cathedral was about five hundred and eighty feet long. That made it one of the longest churches in Europe. It once had a very tall wooden spire, covered in lead. Some say it was the tallest in England. Yet in 1561, lightning struck the spire, and it burned. It was never rebuilt. By 1666, the cathedral had only a square central tower. The building was old and in poor condition. In the 1630s, the architect Inigo Jones had added a classical portico with tall columns at the west end. Even so, the old church was much loved. For centuries, it had been the great landmark of the city, visible from far across the countryside.

[long-pause]

{img:64-pauls-walk-nave-people-strolling-talking}

The nave of Old St Paul's was used as a public walkway and meeting place. Inside Old St Paul's, the long central aisle had an unusual use. People treated it almost like a street. It was known as Paul's Walk. Londoners walked up and down it to meet friends, hear news and do business. Lawyers met clients there. Servants looked for new masters. Some people even used the nave as a shortcut, carrying goods through the church. Merchants made deals beside the pillars. Gossips exchanged the latest stories. Fashionable young men strolled there to show off their clothes. The noise sometimes disturbed the services in the choir. Church leaders complained about it many times. Even so, Paul's Walk remained popular for many years. In the plays of the early 1600s, characters often meet in Paul's. It shows how closely the church and city life were bound together. A cathedral was not only a place of prayer. It was also part of the daily life of the town.

[long-pause]

{img:65-wren-evelyn-surveying-old-st-pauls-1666}

Just a week before the fire, Christopher Wren had proposed giving Old St Paul's a dome. By the 1660s, the old cathedral was in poor shape. Its walls leaned, and its tower was weak. A group of experts was asked to look at it. One of them was a young scientist and architect named Christopher Wren. Another was John Evelyn, the writer and diarist. On the twenty-seventh of August 1666, they examined the building together. Wren suggested repairing the old church and replacing the tower with a great dome. Others wanted to keep the old style. The group did not agree. Then, only days later, the Great Fire broke out. The cathedral was destroyed. Even so, the plans were not entirely wasted. The idea of a dome stayed in Wren's mind. Years later, he would design a completely new cathedral. Yet at the time, no one could have guessed how quickly the old debate would be decided. The fire settled the question in a single night.

[long-pause]

{img:66-st-faiths-crypt-stone-pillars-dim-light}

The parish church of St Faith was hidden inside the crypt of St Paul's. Beneath Old St Paul's lay a large crypt. Part of it served as a separate parish church, dedicated to Saint Faith. Local people worshipped there underground. The Stationers' Company, which controlled the book trade, also used the crypt. It was here that the booksellers stored their books when the fire approached. They believed the thick walls and stone vaults would protect them. When the roof of the cathedral fell in, burning timber crashed into the church above. The heat broke through to the crypt. The books caught fire and burned for about a week. Some accounts say the loss was worth a vast sum of money. Pepys heard about it and wrote of the great loss to the booksellers. Even so, a few things in the cathedral survived. Some monuments were damaged but not destroyed. One statue of the poet John Donne, wrapped in his burial shroud, came through the fire and can still be seen today.

[long-pause]

{img:67-john-donne-shroud-statue-marble-niche}

The statue of the poet John Donne was one of the few monuments to survive the fire. John Donne was one of the great poets of the early 1600s. He was also Dean of St Paul's, the head of the cathedral clergy. He died in 1631. Before his death, he did something unusual. He had a portrait painted of himself wrapped in a burial shroud, standing upright. A sculptor named Nicholas Stone used this painting to carve a marble statue. It was placed in Old St Paul's. During the Great Fire, the cathedral roof fell, and most monuments were destroyed. Yet Donne's statue survived. It fell into the crypt and was found afterwards with only slight damage. Scorch marks can still be seen on its base. Today, it stands in the new St Paul's Cathedral. Even so, it is a strange sight. Donne appears calm and still, as if waiting quietly for the end. For many visitors, it is one of the most moving links between the old cathedral and the new one.

[long-pause]

{img:68-charles-ii-return-1660-procession-london}

King Charles the Second returned to London in 1660, after years of exile. The 1660s began with a great celebration. For eleven years, England had had no king. Charles the First had been executed in 1649. The country was ruled for a time by Oliver Cromwell. After Cromwell died, the government fell into confusion. In 1660, Parliament invited the old king's son to return. Charles the Second arrived in London on his thirtieth birthday, the twenty-ninth of May. Crowds filled the streets. Flowers were strewn on the road, and fountains ran with wine. Pepys was there. He had sailed with the fleet that brought the king home from the Netherlands. This period is called the Restoration. Even so, the joy did not last forever. The 1660s brought plague, war and fire. Yet the return of the king changed the mood of London. Music, theatre and fashion came back to life after the quieter years under Cromwell.

[long-pause]

{img:69-whitehall-palace-banqueting-house-river-1666}

The king lived at Whitehall, the largest palace in Europe at the time. Whitehall Palace stood beside the Thames, between the city and Westminster. It was enormous. Some say it was the largest palace in Europe. It had more than a thousand rooms. Yet it was not one grand building. It was a maze of old and new houses, courtyards, gardens and galleries. It had grown over more than a century. Its finest building was the Banqueting House, designed by Inigo Jones in the 1620s. Its ceiling had been painted by Rubens. Courtiers, servants, officials and visitors crowded the palace every day. Pepys came often on Navy business. He walked through the galleries hoping to see the king or the great lords. Even so, Whitehall did not survive. Most of the palace burned in another fire in 1698. Only the Banqueting House remains today. It is one of the few royal buildings in London that still stands from that time.

[long-pause]

{img:70-restoration-theatre-stage-candles-audience-1666}

After 1660, women appeared on the English stage for the first time. Under Cromwell, the theatres of London had been closed. When the king returned, they opened again. Two companies were given royal permission to perform. They built new indoor theatres with painted scenery and candlelit stages. Something else changed too. Before, female roles were played by boys. Now, women became actresses. This was a great novelty. Audiences crowded in to see them. One of the most famous was Nell Gwyn. She began by selling oranges in the theatre and became a popular comic actress. She later became a companion of the king. Pepys loved the theatre. He went very often, sometimes several times a week. He wrote about the plays, the actors and the audience. He sometimes made vows to go less, and then broke them. Even so, the theatres were closed again for a long time in 1665 and 1666, because of the plague. Their reopening was a sign that life was returning.

[long-pause]

{img:71-royal-society-meeting-telescope-instruments}

The Royal Society, founded in 1660, brought scientists together to share experiments. The 1660s were an exciting time for science. In November 1660, a group of learned men met in London. They decided to form a society to study nature through experiments. The king gave them his support. In 1662, they received a royal charter and became the Royal Society. Its members included Robert Boyle, who studied gases, and Robert Hooke, who studied tiny things with a microscope. Christopher Wren was a founding member too. The society met regularly to watch experiments and discuss new ideas. Pepys joined in 1665. He did not fully understand all the science, but he found it fascinating. Later in his life, he even became its president. Even so, not everyone took the society seriously. Some people laughed at its experiments. Yet its motto expressed a powerful idea. It meant, roughly, take nobody's word for it. The Royal Society still exists and remains one of the world's leading scientific academies.

[long-pause]

{img:72-hooke-micrographia-flea-drawing-book-open}

In 1665, Robert Hooke published a book with giant drawings of a flea and a louse. Robert Hooke was one of the most inventive scientists of his time. He worked for the Royal Society, preparing experiments for its meetings. In 1665, he published a remarkable book called Micrographia. It showed what he had seen through a microscope. It included large, detailed drawings of tiny things. There was a flea, a louse, the eye of a fly and the point of a needle. Some of the drawings folded out to a great size. Readers had never seen anything like them. Hooke also looked at a thin slice of cork. He saw tiny boxes and called them cells. That word is still used in biology today. Pepys bought the book and stayed up until two in the morning reading it. He called it the most ingenious book he had ever read. Even so, Hooke was not only a scientist. After the Great Fire, he helped survey and rebuild the city. He also worked with Wren on many buildings.

[long-pause]

{img:73-dutch-war-ships-battle-distance-calm-sea}

In 1666, England was at war with the Netherlands, the greatest trading nation of the time. The year of the fire was also a year of war. England and the Dutch Republic were fighting over trade and the sea. Both nations had large navies and rich merchant fleets. In June 1666, there was a huge sea battle that lasted four days. Pepys heard the guns from London, far away across the land. The English fleet suffered heavy losses. In August, the English struck back by burning Dutch merchant ships. As a Navy official, Pepys was deeply involved. He worked hard to find money, men and supplies for the fleet. The war was expensive, and the navy was often short of cash. Sailors went unpaid for months. Even so, the war had little direct effect on daily life in London until the fire. Then rumours spread that Dutch or French agents had started it. Yet the following year brought real disaster. In 1667, Dutch ships sailed into the Medway and attacked the English fleet in its own harbour.

[long-pause]

{img:74-plague-quiet-street-closed-doors-1665-london}

A year before the fire, London suffered its last great outbreak of plague. The year 1665 is remembered as the year of the Great Plague. The disease had visited London many times before. But this outbreak was especially severe. It began in the spring in the poor suburbs outside the walls. By the summer, it was spreading through the whole city. The Bills of Mortality recorded nearly seventy thousand deaths from plague that year. Many historians think the real number was closer to one hundred thousand. That was perhaps a quarter of the people of London. Those who could afford it left the city. The king and his court moved to Salisbury and then to Oxford. Many wealthy merchants and doctors also fled. Even so, many people stayed. The poor could not leave. Officials, clergy and some doctors remained to help. Pepys stayed in London for much of the year, working at the Navy Office. His diary gives a quiet, sad record of a city falling silent.

[long-pause]

{img:75-red-cross-painted-door-plague-house-1665}

Houses with plague were shut up, and a red cross was painted on the door. The authorities had rules for dealing with plague. When someone in a house fell ill, the whole household was shut in. The door was locked from the outside. A red cross was painted on it, about a foot high. Above it were written the words, Lord have mercy upon us. A watchman stood outside day and night to make sure no one left. Food and other things were passed in through a window. The household stayed shut in for at least forty days. This was meant to stop the disease from spreading. Yet it was terrible for the healthy people trapped inside with the sick. Some escaped through windows or bribed the watchmen. Even so, the rule was not completely without reason. People did understand that the disease could pass between people. Pepys wrote about seeing houses with red crosses for the first time. It made him uneasy, and he bought some tobacco to chew, believing it would protect him.

[long-pause]

{img:76-dog-catcher-empty-lane-1665-quiet}

During the plague, the city ordered thousands of dogs and cats to be killed. In 1665, people did not know what caused the plague. Today we know it was spread mainly by fleas carried by rats, and perhaps also from person to person. In the 1600s, many believed that bad air or dirty animals spread the disease. So the city authorities ordered that dogs and cats be killed. Dog killers were paid for each animal. Thousands were destroyed. Sadly, this may have made things worse. Cats and dogs had helped keep the rat population down. With fewer of them, the rats may have increased. Other measures were also tried. Fires were lit in the streets to purify the air. People carried herbs and sweet-smelling posies. Some smoked tobacco to keep the disease away. Even so, none of these measures stopped the plague. It faded only as the weather grew colder. Yet the people of 1665 were not foolish. They did their best with the knowledge they had.

[long-pause]

{img:77-doctor-plague-house-visit-1665-quiet-room}

Some doctors stayed in London during the plague to care for the sick, at great risk. Many doctors left London when the plague came. Their patients were mostly wealthy, and those patients had fled. Yet some doctors stayed behind. One of them was Nathaniel Hodges. He remained in the city throughout the outbreak and treated many patients. Later he wrote an account of what he had seen. He described how he went out each morning to visit the sick, and how he came home each evening tired and worried. He survived the plague himself. Other doctors were less fortunate and died. The city also appointed special plague doctors. Some were inexperienced or untrained. Even so, the doctors did what they could. Their medicines had little effect, but their presence brought comfort. Nurses and searchers also worked among the sick. These were often poor women who needed the money. Their work was dangerous and unpleasant. Yet without them, the city would have suffered even more.

[long-pause]

{img:78-autumn-frost-london-rooftops-plague-ends-1665}

As the cold winter of 1665 arrived, the plague slowly began to fade. The plague reached its peak in September 1665. In one week, more than seven thousand deaths were recorded. Then, as autumn turned to winter, the number of deaths began to fall. The cold weather slowed the fleas that spread the disease. By December, the city was recovering. People who had fled began to return. Shops opened again, and the streets grew busy. The king returned to London in February 1666. Pepys noted with relief that the town was filling up again. Even so, the plague did not disappear completely. Cases continued in 1666, and the theatres stayed closed for some time. Yet London never again suffered a plague outbreak on that scale. Some people later claimed that the Great Fire had burned the plague out of the city. Historians doubt this. The fire mostly burned the old city, while the plague had been worst in the poor suburbs. Even so, the great plague of 1665 was London's last.

[long-pause]

{img:79-refugees-camp-moorfields-tents-1666}

After the fire, tens of thousands of homeless Londoners camped in the open fields. When the fire ended, many thousands of people had nowhere to live. They fled to the open spaces outside the city walls. The largest camp was at Moorfields, a green area just north of the wall. Families set up tents and shelters made of sheets, boards and anything else they had saved. Some slept under the open sky. Others camped in Islington, Highgate and across the river. The king rode out to visit the camps. He spoke to the people and promised them help. He ordered bread to be brought from the countryside. Markets were set up near the camps. Churches and towns outside London were asked to take people in. Even so, the camps were uncomfortable, and autumn was coming. Many families moved in with relatives or found rooms elsewhere. Some never returned to the city. Yet the speed of the response was remarkable. Within weeks, many people had found shelter and work again.

[long-pause]

{img:80-robert-hubert-watchmaker-courtroom-1666}

A French watchmaker confessed to starting the fire, although he could not have done it. After the fire, many people wanted someone to blame. A young Frenchman named Robert Hubert said that he had started it. He claimed he had thrown a fire grenade into the bakery in Pudding Lane. His story was full of mistakes. Some of the judges doubted him. Some thought he was not in his right mind. Yet he stuck to his confession. In October 1666, he was found guilty and hanged. Only later did the truth become clear. The captain of the ship that had brought Hubert to England said Hubert had not arrived in London until two days after the fire began. Even so, it was too late for him. His case is remembered as one of the sad mistakes of English justice. Historians believe the fire was an accident. Yet for years, many people continued to blame foreigners or Catholics. A plaque blaming Catholics was even put up in Pudding Lane. It was removed much later.

[long-pause]

{img:81-fire-court-judges-papers-dispute-1667}

A special Fire Court was set up to settle disputes between landlords and tenants. After the fire, there was a huge legal problem. Who should pay for rebuilding? Many houses were rented. Leases said that tenants had to repair the houses they lived in. But these houses no longer existed. Landlords and tenants argued about money, rights and responsibility. Ordinary courts would have taken years to settle all the cases. So in 1667, Parliament created a special Fire Court. It was made up of judges who heard cases quickly and fairly. They tried to reach practical agreements that let rebuilding begin. They often split the costs between landlords and tenants. The judges worked without pay. Over several years, they heard more than a thousand cases. Even so, not everyone was happy with the decisions. Yet the court worked remarkably well. It is one reason why London was rebuilt so quickly. The court's records still survive and tell the stories of many ordinary people who lost their homes.

[long-pause]

{img:82-new-brick-houses-rebuilding-street-1670s}

After 1667, new houses in the City had to be built of brick or stone. The king and Parliament wanted to make sure such a fire never happened again. In 1667, they passed the Rebuilding Act. It set strict rules for new houses. They had to be built of brick or stone, not timber. Overhanging upper floors were banned. There were only a few standard types of houses, depending on the width of the street. Larger streets had to have larger houses. Some streets were widened, and new markets were laid out. Work began quickly. Within a few years, thousands of new brick houses had been built. Yet the city kept its old street plan. Several architects, including Wren and Evelyn, had drawn grand new designs. They imagined wide avenues and open squares. But owners wanted their old plots back, and there was no money to buy them out. Even so, the new London was safer. Brick houses with tiled roofs did not burn as easily as the old timber ones.

[long-pause]

{img:83-wren-new-st-pauls-dome-construction-1700}

Christopher Wren designed a new St Paul's Cathedral, which took over thirty years to build. After the fire, Christopher Wren became the most important architect in London. He was asked to design a new St Paul's Cathedral. His first designs were rejected. The church leaders thought they were too unusual. Finally, a design was approved, and work began in 1675. Wren then quietly made many changes as the building rose. The most famous was the great dome. It was built in three layers, one inside the other. The outer dome rose high above the city. The cathedral was declared complete in 1711, when Wren was nearly eighty years old. He had seen it built from start to finish. Even so, it was not his only work. He also designed fifty-one new parish churches for the city. Many of their white spires can still be seen today. Wren was buried in the cathedral he had built. A simple inscription there says, in Latin, if you seek his monument, look around you.

[long-pause]

{img:84-monument-column-golden-flames-top-london}

The Monument to the Great Fire stands two hundred and two feet tall, and two hundred and two feet from the bakery. After the fire, it was decided to build a memorial. The design was by Christopher Wren and Robert Hooke. It is a tall stone column, simply called the Monument. It was finished in 1677. Its height is two hundred and two feet. That is the same as the distance from the column to the place in Pudding Lane where the fire began. At the top is a gilded urn with flames, made of gilded copper. Inside the column, a spiral staircase of three hundred and eleven steps leads up to a viewing platform. Visitors still climb it today to look over the city. Hooke also hoped to use the column for scientific experiments. He thought it could serve as a giant telescope or help with experiments on gravity and air pressure. Even so, traffic vibrations made this difficult. Yet the Monument remains one of the clearest reminders of the fire. It stands quietly among the modern buildings, close to where it all began.

[long-pause]

{img:85-pepys-old-age-house-clapham-garden-evening}

Pepys stopped writing his diary in 1669, fearing he was going blind. For nine and a half years, Pepys wrote in his diary almost every day. Then, in May 1669, he stopped. His eyes had become painful and weak. He feared that writing by candlelight was making him blind. In his final entry, he wrote that he was saying goodbye to his diary, and he felt it was almost like seeing himself go into his grave. In fact, he did not go blind. He lived for another thirty-four years. He became Secretary to the Admiralty, the most important official in the navy. He was briefly imprisoned in the Tower during a political crisis, but he was released. He spent his last years in a house at Clapham, south of London. He died there in 1703, aged seventy. Even so, he never returned to his diary. Yet the pages he had already written were enough. They kept the London of the 1660s alive, with its fires, its music and its everyday joys.
