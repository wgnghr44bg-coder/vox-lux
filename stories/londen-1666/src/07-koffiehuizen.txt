In 1666, there is a new and fashionable kind of place in London: the coffee house.

The first one opened only about fourteen years ago, in a little alley off Cornhill, run by a Greek man named Pasqua Rosée, who had learned to make coffee in the Turkish lands, where it was already popular. Londoners had never tasted anything like it. Soon, there were coffee houses all over the city, dozens of them, more every year.

--

A coffee house is a large, plain room, usually on the ground floor, with long wooden tables and benches, and a big fire at one end, where the coffee is made, in great pots, and kept hot.

For a penny, anyone can come in, and sit down, and drink a dish of coffee, as they call it, because it is served in little bowls without handles, and stay as long as they like. Coffee is hot and black and bitter, and some people think it tastes horrible. But it keeps you awake, and alert, and it does not make you drunk, like beer and wine.

There are newspapers to read, and pamphlets, and news sheets. And above all, there is talk.

--

The coffee houses are the place where London talks.

Men of every kind come in: merchants and lawyers, doctors and scientists, writers and poets, shopkeepers and craftsmen, sea captains and clergymen. They sit down together at the long tables, and talk, about politics, and business, and the war, and the news, and the latest books and plays, and the gossip of the court. Anyone can talk to anyone, whatever their rank. People call the coffee houses penny universities, because for a penny, you can learn more there than at Oxford or Cambridge.

{img:coffee-house-debate-scholars-merchants-clay-pipes}
Many of the coffee houses have their own special customers. In one, the merchants who trade with the East Indies meet. In another, the scientists of the Royal Society. In another, the poets and the writers. Later, some of these coffee houses will grow into great institutions: the Stock Exchange, and the great insurance company, Lloyd's of London, which began in a coffee house.

--

There are also taverns and inns, hundreds of them, much older than the coffee houses.

The taverns are where people go to drink wine, and eat, and meet their friends. They have names like the Mitre, the Globe, the Dolphin, the Sun, the Mermaid, the King's Head, and painted signs hanging over their doors. Inside, there are small rooms, with wooden panelling and fireplaces, where groups of friends can have dinner in private.

{img:tavern-private-room-panelling-fire-friends-dinner}
Pepys loves the taverns. He goes to them often, with his friends and colleagues, for dinner, and to talk, and drink a glass of wine, and sing. He writes about the food, and the wine, and the company, and how much it all cost, and sometimes, about how much he drank, and how ashamed he was the next morning.

--

The great inns are on the main roads in and out of the city, and are the starting points for the coaches and the carriers' wagons that travel to every part of England.

They are built around large courtyards, with galleries around the upper floors, where the bedrooms are, and stables for the horses below. In the courtyards, the coaches arrive and depart, with a great clatter, and the passengers climb in and out, and the ostlers lead the horses to the stables, and the porters carry the luggage.

{img:coaching-inn-courtyard-galleries-coach-departing}
In the old days, before the theatres were built, the actors used to perform their plays in the courtyards of the inns, with the audience standing in the yard, and sitting in the galleries above.

--

And there are the alehouses, small, humble places, often just a room in someone's house, with a few benches and a barrel of ale, where working people go in the evening to drink a pot of ale and talk with their neighbours.

Ale and beer are what almost everyone drinks, all day long, men and women and children, because the water is often not safe to drink. There are brewhouses all over London, and the smell of the brewing drifts through the streets.

--

In the evenings, the coffee houses and the taverns are full of talk and laughter and smoke.

Everyone smokes tobacco, in long white clay pipes, which are sold, already filled, in the taverns and the coffee houses, for a penny. The smoke hangs in clouds under the low ceilings, and the candles flicker, and the voices rise and fall.

{img:evening-coffee-house-candlelight-smoke-conversation}
And in the summer of 1666, the talk is all about the war with the Dutch, and the great sea battles, and the dry weather, and the price of bread, and whether the plague will come back, and the new comet that some people say they have seen in the sky, and what it might mean.
