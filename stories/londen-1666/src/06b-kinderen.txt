The streets and lanes of London in 1666 are full of children.

They play in the streets, and in the churchyards, and in the fields outside the walls, and on the banks of the river. They play with hoops and sticks, and spinning tops, and marbles, and balls. They play games of hide and seek, and leapfrog, and blind man's buff. They fly kites in the fields, and fish from the river stairs, and swim in the river in summer, which their mothers forbid.

--

Children of poor families begin to work very young.

They help their parents at home and in the shop, from the age of five or six. They run errands, and carry water, and mind the babies, and sell things in the streets. At twelve or fourteen, many boys are sent to be apprentices, to learn a trade, and many girls go into service, as maids in the houses of richer families.

But many children, too, go to school, for at least a few years.

--

There are many schools in London.

There are little schools, run by a woman in her own house, where small children learn their letters from a hornbook: a little wooden paddle with a sheet of paper on it, protected by a thin, clear sheet of horn, with the alphabet and the Lord's Prayer printed on it. The children hold it by the handle, and recite the letters, over and over.

{img:little-children-hornbook-dame-school-room}
Then there are the grammar schools, for boys, where they learn Latin and Greek, from morning until evening, six days a week, with a stern master, who keeps a birch rod on his desk. The most famous is Saint Paul's School, beside the cathedral, where Pepys himself went as a boy, and where he learned so much Latin that he could read and write it all his life.

--

Girls of richer families are taught at home, or at little boarding schools, in the villages around London, at Hackney and Chelsea and Putney.

They learn to read and write, and to do sums, and French, and music and singing and dancing, and needlework, and how to run a household. Some of them learn much more: languages, and history, and philosophy, from their fathers or their brothers or their tutors.

{img:girls-needlework-sampler-window-light-school}
One of the things that every girl makes is a sampler: a piece of linen, embroidered with the alphabet, and numbers, and little pictures of flowers and birds and houses, and her name, and the date, to show her skill with the needle. Some of these samplers still survive today, more than three hundred years later, with the little names of the girls who made them stitched in the corner.

--

Children's lives in the seventeenth century are often short, and many families lose children to illness.

But they are loved, and played with, and spoiled, as much as children are today. Their parents make them little toys: wooden dolls with painted faces, and little cradles for them, and tiny pewter dishes and pots for playing house; wooden horses on wheels, and hobby horses; little wooden soldiers and drums and trumpets; whistles and rattles.

On holidays, they are taken to the fairs, at Smithfield and Southwark, where there are puppet shows, and rope dancers, and jugglers, and fire eaters, and gingerbread, and toys, and strange animals to see.

--

In the winter, when it is very cold, the Thames sometimes freezes over, above the bridge, because the narrow arches of London Bridge slow the water down.

Then the children, and the grownups too, go out onto the ice, and slide and skate and play, and there is a frost fair, with booths and stalls on the ice, selling food and drink and toys, and people roast an ox, and play football, and print their names on little cards on a printing press set up on the ice, as souvenirs.

{img:frost-fair-frozen-thames-booths-skaters-children}
In the summer, the children swim in the river, and play in the fields, and pick flowers and blackberries in the hedges, and catch frogs in the ponds, and come home tired and dirty and happy, as the sun goes down over the city.
