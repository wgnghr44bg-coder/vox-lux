On Monday, the third of September, the fire spreads further.

The wind is still blowing from the east, strong and steady, and the fire moves westward and northward through the City, from street to street, faster than anyone can stop it. It reaches Cornhill, and Lombard Street, and the Royal Exchange, the great building where the merchants meet, which burns in the afternoon.

--

The people of the City are leaving.

All day long, the streets are crowded with people, carrying everything they can, and carts and wagons, piled high, trying to get out through the gates of the city, to the fields outside the walls. The price of a cart goes up and up, until people are paying ten times the usual price. The river is crowded with boats.

In the fields to the north of the city, at Moorfields, and in the fields of Islington and Highgate, thousands of families camp in the open, with their belongings around them, under sheets and blankets, and watch the glow of the fire in the sky over the city.

--

Pepys spends the day packing.

In the early morning, at four o'clock, he sends his money and his plate and his best things away, in a cart, to the house of a friend at Bethnal Green, a village outside the city, riding beside the cart in his nightgown. Then he comes back, and goes on packing, all day, with Elizabeth and the servants, and carries more of their things down to the river, to be taken away by boat.

{img:cart-at-dawn-village-road-bethnal-green-belongings}
He is exhausted, and dirty, and worried. But he is still writing his diary, every night.

--

On Monday, the King gives his brother, the Duke of York, the job of fighting the fire.

The Duke sets up posts, all around the edge of the fire, each one commanded by a nobleman or a courtier, with soldiers, and with men to fight the fire, and food and drink and money to pay them. He rides from post to post, all day and all night, encouraging the men, and working with his own hands to pass buckets of water, and pull down houses.

The people of London are amazed. They have never seen a prince working like this, among them, covered in dirt and sweat. They begin to cheer him.

--

On Tuesday, the fourth of September, the fire reaches Saint Paul's Cathedral.

Everyone had thought that the great old cathedral, with its thick stone walls, would be safe. That was why the booksellers had stored their books in the crypt. But there was wooden scaffolding against the walls, for the repairs, and the roof was covered with lead, laid on great wooden beams.

{img:old-cathedral-distant-evening-glow-sky-from-fields}
On Tuesday evening, the scaffolding caught fire, and then the roof. And the great old cathedral, which had stood on its hill for nearly six hundred years, was lost, in a few hours, while the people watched from the fields outside the city, and from the river, and from the hills around London.

--

That same day, John Evelyn, a gentleman and writer, who also kept a diary, walked through the city, and wrote about what he saw.

He wrote that the stones of Saint Paul's flew like grenades, and the lead of the roof melted and ran down the streets in a stream. He wrote that the sky was like the top of a burning oven, and that the light of the fire could be seen forty miles away, and that at night it was as light as day for ten miles around.

{img:evelyn-writing-diary-country-house-window-glow-distant}
And he wrote about the people, thousands of them, camping in the fields, with what little they had saved, in tents and huts, poor and sad, but patient.

--

On Tuesday, too, the fire comes close to Seething Lane, where Pepys lives, and to the Navy Office, and the Tower of London.

Pepys and Sir William Penn, another officer of the Navy Board, who lives next door, dig a pit in the garden, and bury some of their most precious things, to save them: their wine, and Pepys's parmesan cheese, a large, round, hard Italian cheese, which he values very much. It is one of the most famous moments in his diary, because it is so ordinary, and so Pepys.

He sends Elizabeth and Will Hewer away to Woolwich, with his gold, by boat, for safety. And he stays behind, to help to save the Navy Office.

--

To save the Navy Office, and the Tower, Pepys asks for workmen from the royal dockyards at Deptford and Woolwich, who know how to pull down buildings quickly.

They come up the river, and work through the night, pulling down the houses in the path of the fire, and blowing up some of them with gunpowder from the Tower, to make great gaps that the fire cannot cross. That night, Pepys sleeps, for a little while, on the floor of his office, in his clothes.

{img:workmen-pulling-down-timber-house-hooks-ropes-gap}
And outside, in the darkness, the wind blows, and the fire burns, and the city waits.
