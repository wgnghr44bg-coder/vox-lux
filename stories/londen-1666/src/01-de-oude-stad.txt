Tonight we are going more than three hundred and fifty years back, to the old city of London, in the long, warm summer of the year 1666.

--

It is early morning, and the sun is rising over the river Thames.

The light falls on a city of timber and plaster and stone, crowded along the north bank of the river: a great jumble of steep roofs and tall gables, chimneys and church towers, more than a hundred of them, rising above the rooftops. And above them all, on its hill, stands the enormous old cathedral of Saint Paul's, long and grey and ancient, with a great square tower in the middle.

If you enjoy calm journeys into history, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel. Now, let us begin.

--

The river is already busy.

Little boats, called wherries, are rowing across the water, carrying passengers from one bank to the other. Barges loaded with hay and coal and timber drift down with the tide. Tall ships stand at anchor below the bridge, their masts and rigging dark against the morning sky.

And across the river, from the city to the borough of Southwark on the south bank, there stretches London Bridge, the only bridge across the Thames in London: a long, narrow bridge of stone arches, with houses and shops built all along it, on both sides, three and four and five storeys high, hanging out over the water, so that crossing it is like walking down a narrow street.

--

London, in 1666, is one of the largest cities in Europe.

Perhaps four or five hundred thousand people live here, in the old walled City, and in the suburbs that have spread out beyond the walls in every direction: west to Westminster, where the King has his palace, and east toward the docks, and north into the fields.

{img:city-wall-gate-ludgate-carts-travellers-1666}
The old City of London is still surrounded by its ancient wall, built by the Romans more than fourteen hundred years before, and rebuilt many times since, with great gates where the roads come in: Ludgate, Newgate, Aldersgate, Cripplegate, Moorgate, Bishopsgate, Aldgate. At night, the gates are shut.

--

Inside the wall, the streets are narrow and crowded and winding, much as they were in the Middle Ages.

The houses are mostly built of timber, with walls of wattle and plaster between the wooden beams, and many of them are very old. Each storey sticks out a little further over the street than the one below it, so that at the top, the houses on opposite sides of a narrow lane almost touch, and people can shake hands across the street from their top windows. Down below, the lanes are dim and shady, even at midday.

{img:jettied-timber-houses-narrow-lane-overhanging-1666}
In the lanes, there are shops, and taverns, and workshops, and little churches, one in almost every street. There are more than a hundred parish churches in the City, each with its own bells.

--

It is a city of trade.

Ships come up the Thames from all over the world: from Holland and France and Spain, from the Baltic and the Mediterranean, from the new colonies in America and the West Indies, and from the far-off East Indies. They bring wine and silk and spices, sugar and tobacco, timber and tar, and carry away English wool and cloth.

{img:wharves-billingsgate-ships-unloading-casks-1666}
In the City, the merchants buy and sell, in the great courtyard of the Royal Exchange, and in their counting houses and warehouses near the river. The craftsmen make everything that the city needs. The shopkeepers sell it.

--

It has been a hard time for London, lately.

Last year, in 1665, a terrible plague came to the city, and many people were lost. And this year, England is at war with the Dutch, at sea, and the people of London have heard the sound of the guns of the great sea battles, booming far away across the water.

But now, in the summer of 1666, the plague has faded away, and the people who fled to the country have come home, and the city is busy and noisy and full of life again.

--

And it is a dry summer. A very dry summer.

There has been very little rain since the spring. The fields around London are brown and dusty. The wooden houses of the city are as dry as tinder, after months of sunshine. The timber beams, the plaster, the thatched roofs of the poorer houses, the warehouses full of oil and tar and timber and coal and hemp along the river, all of them dry, dry, dry.

{img:dry-summer-london-lane-dust-sunlight-1666}
Nobody worries about it very much. Fires happen in London often, and are put out. The city has survived fires before.

And in a house near the Tower of London, a busy, cheerful, curious man of thirty-three is writing, as he does every night, in his secret diary.
