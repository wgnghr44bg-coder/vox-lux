The City of London is still there today, on the north bank of the Thames, in the same place, on the same hills, within the line of the same old walls.

It is a very different place now. Tall towers of glass and steel rise above the old streets, and the City is one of the greatest centres of banking and business in the world. Hundreds of thousands of people come to work there every day, and in the evening, most of them go home, and the streets grow quiet.

--

But the old City is still there, underneath, if you know how to look.

Many of the streets follow the same lines as they did before the fire, and have the same names: Cheapside, Cornhill, Lombard Street, Eastcheap, Fish Street Hill, Thames Street, Pudding Lane. In some places, the narrow old alleys and courts still wind between the buildings, as they did in Pepys's day.

{img:narrow-old-city-alley-courtyard-lamp-evening-today}
And the churches of Christopher Wren are still there, many of them, scattered among the office buildings, with their white towers and steeples rising up among the glass towers, like old friends among strangers. Their bells still ring. Some of them are open during the day, and people come in from their offices, at lunchtime, to sit quietly for a while, or to listen to a concert.

--

Saint Paul's Cathedral still stands on its hill, with its great white dome, which can be seen from all over London.

For centuries, it was the tallest building in the city. During the Second World War, when London was bombed, and many buildings around it were destroyed, the cathedral survived, and a famous photograph of its dome, rising above the smoke, became a symbol of the courage of the people of London.

{img:st-pauls-interior-dome-mosaics-light-quiet-today}
Visitors can climb up inside the dome, to the Whispering Gallery, where a whisper on one side of the dome can be heard on the other side, more than a hundred feet away. And they can climb higher still, to the Golden Gallery, at the very top, and look out over the whole of London, as Pepys once looked out from the tower of the old cathedral.

--

The Monument still stands at the bottom of Fish Street Hill, near Pudding Lane, tall and white, with its golden flames at the top.

Visitors still climb its three hundred and eleven steps, round and round, up the narrow spiral staircase, to the little balcony at the top, and look out over the river and the City, and get a certificate at the bottom, to say that they have done it.

At the foot of the Monument, on its base, there are carved inscriptions in Latin, describing the fire, and a carved picture, showing King Charles, in Roman armour, coming to help the ruined city.

--

Pudding Lane is still there, a narrow little street running down the hill toward the river.

There is no bakery there now. But there is a small plaque on the wall, which says that near this spot stood the house of Thomas Farriner, the King's baker, in which the Great Fire of London began, on the second of September, 1666.

{img:pudding-lane-today-narrow-street-plaque-quiet}
And Seething Lane is still there, near the Tower of London, where Pepys lived. The Navy Office is gone. But there is a little garden there now, called the Seething Lane Garden, with a bust of Samuel Pepys, and little plaques in the paving, telling the story of his diary. And Saint Olave's church is still there, in Hart Street, with the marble bust of Elizabeth Pepys still looking down from the wall, and Samuel himself buried beneath the floor, beside her.

--

The river Thames still flows past the City, wide and brown and tidal, rising and falling twice a day.

There are many bridges now. London Bridge has been rebuilt several times, and has no houses on it any more. But the little boats of the watermen are gone, and the river is quieter than it was in Pepys's day, except for the tourist boats and the barges.

{img:thames-evening-tower-bridge-lights-calm-water}
On an evening in late summer, when the sun goes down behind Westminster, and the river turns to gold, and the lights come on along the banks, and the bells of the old Wren churches ring out across the City, it is not hard to imagine the old city that Pepys knew, and loved, and wrote about, and watched burning from a little boat on the river, on a warm September night, long ago.

--

So we leave London for tonight, the old city by the river, with its great white dome glowing in the twilight, and its church bells ringing across the quiet evening streets, and the river flowing softly under the bridges, toward the sea.
