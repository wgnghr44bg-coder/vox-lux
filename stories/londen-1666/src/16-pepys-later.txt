Samuel Pepys went on writing his diary for nearly three more years after the fire.

For months afterwards, he had bad dreams about fires, and could not sleep well. But life went on. He worked hard at the Navy Office, and the war with the Dutch came to an end. He went to the theatre, and played his music, and bought books, and quarrelled and made up with Elizabeth, and wrote it all down, every night.

--

But his eyes were getting weaker.

Writing by candlelight, night after night, for years, in his tiny shorthand, had strained them, and he was afraid that he was going blind. So, on the thirty-first of May, 1669, he wrote the last entry in his diary.

He wrote that he must stop, because of his eyes, and that he could not do it any longer. And he ended with words that are still moving to read: And so I betake myself to that course, which is almost as much as to see myself go into my grave. For which, and all the discomforts that will accompany my being blind, the good God prepare me.

He did not go blind. His eyes recovered. But he never wrote his diary again.

--

That autumn, he took Elizabeth on a holiday to France and Holland, which she had always wanted.

But on the way home, she fell ill with a fever. And in November 1669, she passed away, in London, at the age of twenty-nine. Pepys was heartbroken. He put up a beautiful marble monument to her in his parish church, Saint Olave's, in Hart Street, near Seething Lane, which had survived the fire, high on the wall, where he could see it from his seat in the church. He never married again.

{img:st-olaves-church-marble-bust-lady-wall-quiet}
He went on to become one of the greatest officials in the history of the navy. He reformed the way the navy was run, built new ships, and made the navy into one of the greatest in the world. He became a member of Parliament, and the president of the Royal Society, where he helped to publish the great book by Isaac Newton about gravity and the movement of the planets.

--

In his old age, he lived in a house in Clapham, then a village outside London, with his faithful old clerk, Will Hewer, who had become a rich man himself.

He spent his time with his books, which he loved more than anything. He had about three thousand of them, which he kept in twelve beautiful glass-fronted bookcases, made specially for him by the shipwrights of the navy, arranged carefully in order of size, so that they looked neat. He catalogued them, and had them bound, and added to them, until the end of his life.

{img:glass-fronted-bookcases-library-old-books-quiet-room}
He died in 1703, at the age of seventy. He left his library, with all its books and its bookcases, to his old college at Cambridge, Magdalene College, where it is still kept today, exactly as he arranged it.

--

Among the books, there were six volumes, bound in brown leather, written in a strange shorthand that nobody could read.

They were his diary.

For more than a hundred years, the volumes sat on the shelves of the library, unread. Then, in the early nineteenth century, a young student at Magdalene, named John Smith, was given the job of deciphering them. It took him three years, working twelve or fourteen hours a day.

In 1825, a part of the diary was published, at last. People were amazed. Here was a man from the seventeenth century, speaking to them, as if he were in the room: about the Great Fire, and the plague, and the King, and the theatres, and his wife, and his cheese, and his music, and his mistakes. Since then, it has been read by millions of people, all over the world.

--

John Evelyn lived until 1706, and wrote his diary too, for more than sixty years, and planted trees, and wrote books about gardens and forests, and about the smoke of London, which he hated.

Christopher Wren lived until 1723, to the age of ninety. In his old age, it is said, he liked to be driven into London, once a year, and to sit for a while under the great dome of his cathedral, in silence.

{img:old-architect-sitting-under-great-dome-light-quiet}
King Charles the Second reigned until 1685, and is remembered as the Merry Monarch, the king who brought back the theatres and the music and the fun, and who helped to fight the great fire with his own hands.
