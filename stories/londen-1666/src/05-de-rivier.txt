In 1666, the river Thames is the great highway of London.

There are very few bridges, only London Bridge, in the City, and the streets are narrow and crowded and slow. So almost everyone who wants to travel more than a short distance takes a boat. The river is crowded with boats, from early morning until late at night.

--

The boatmen of the Thames are called watermen.

There are thousands of them. They row little boats called wherries: long, narrow, light boats, with a pointed bow, and a cushioned seat in the stern for the passengers, who sit facing forward, under a little awning in the sun or the rain. The waterman sits in the middle, facing backward, and rows with two oars, or sometimes there are two watermen, with four oars, for a faster journey.

All along both banks of the river, there are stairs: stone or wooden steps leading down to the water, at the end of the lanes, where the watermen wait with their boats for passengers. When someone comes down the stairs, the watermen all shout at once: Oars! Oars! Sculls! Sculls! each trying to win the customer.

--

The watermen are famous for their rough language, and their jokes, and their songs.

They have their own company, with a hall, and rules, and apprentices, who learn the river for seven years before they can carry passengers on their own. They know every current and every eddy of the river, and the tides, which rise and fall twice a day, by more than twenty feet, and change the river completely from hour to hour.

{img:thames-at-low-tide-mud-boats-old-london-bridge}
The most dangerous part of the river is London Bridge.

--

London Bridge is a wonder of the world.

It was built about four hundred and fifty years ago, of stone, on nineteen narrow arches, standing on great boat-shaped platforms of timber and stone, called starlings, in the river. The starlings are so big that they block more than half the river, so that the water rushes through the narrow arches like a waterfall, especially when the tide is turning, with a roar that can be heard far away.

Shooting the bridge, passing through the arches in a boat, is very exciting and quite dangerous. Many passengers prefer to get out of the boat before the bridge, and walk around, and get in again on the other side. There is a saying that London Bridge was made for wise men to go over, and fools to go under.

--

On top of the bridge, there are houses and shops, all along both sides, so close together that the roadway between them is only about twelve feet wide, and often jammed with carts and coaches and people.

The shops sell everything: gloves and hats, needles and pins, silks and ribbons. The houses are tall, with overhanging upper storeys, and some of them are built right across the roadway, like a tunnel. In the middle of the bridge, there is an old chapel, and at the southern end, there is a great stone gate.

{img:shops-on-old-london-bridge-narrow-roadway-crowds}
The people who live on the bridge say that they can fish from their windows, and that they are lulled to sleep at night by the roar of the water below.

--

Below the bridge, the river is full of ships.

This part of the river is called the Pool of London, and here the great sailing ships from overseas tie up, or lie at anchor in the middle of the stream, and are unloaded by lighters, small barges, which carry the goods to the wharves along the river bank. Their masts stand like a forest, and their flags of many nations flutter in the wind.

{img:pool-of-london-tall-ships-lighters-17th-century}
Further down the river, at Deptford and Woolwich, there are the royal dockyards, where the King's warships are built and repaired: great wooden ships with three masts and rows of guns, which are sent out to fight the Dutch.

--

Upstream, above the bridge, the river is quieter, and lined with the great houses and gardens of the nobility, along the Strand, between the City and Westminster.

Their gardens run down to the river, with their own stairs and their own boats, and the lords and ladies go up and down the river in their own barges, with liveried oarsmen. The King has a magnificent royal barge, painted and gilded, with a canopy, which is rowed by many oarsmen in red coats.

And on summer evenings, people go out on the river for pleasure, in boats, to listen to music, or to row upriver to the villages and gardens beyond Westminster, among the fields and the meadows.

--

Pepys loves the river.

He writes about it often in his diary: about rowing up to Whitehall on a fine morning, or down to Greenwich on a summer evening, singing with his friends in the boat, and the moon shining on the water. He writes about the watermen, and their stories, and the price of a boat.

{img:summer-evening-boat-music-moonlight-river-friends}
And soon, in a few weeks' time, he will spend many hours on the river, in a little boat, watching his city, and writing about what he sees.
