All through that Sunday morning, the fire spreads along the river, westward, driven by the east wind.

The people living in the lanes near the river begin to flee. They carry their goods down to the river stairs, in their arms and on their backs: beds and chests and chairs, pots and pans, clothes and books, and pile them into boats, or onto barges, and are rowed away across the river, or up the river, to safety. The river is soon crowded with boats, piled high with furniture.

--

Pepys watches for about an hour, from his boat.

He sees that nobody is trying to stop the fire. Everyone is busy saving their own things, and running away. And he sees that the wind is driving the fire into the City, toward the houses and the churches and the warehouses, and that if nothing is done, it will burn the whole of London.

So he tells the boatman to row him up the river to Whitehall, to the King's palace, as fast as he can.

--

At Whitehall, Pepys goes into the chapel, where many of the courtiers are, and tells them what he has seen. They take him to the King.

Pepys tells the King and the Duke of York that unless the King orders houses to be pulled down, to stop the fire, nothing will stop it. The King listens, and agrees, and tells Pepys to go to the Lord Mayor, and tell him, from the King, to spare no houses, but to pull them down before the fire, in every direction. And the Duke of York says that if the Lord Mayor needs soldiers, he shall have them.

{img:king-and-duke-listening-pepys-palace-gallery}
Pepys hurries back to the City, by coach and on foot, to find the Lord Mayor.

--

He finds him at last, in Canning Street, looking exhausted, with a handkerchief around his neck. When Pepys gives him the King's message, the Lord Mayor cries out, like a fainting woman, Pepys writes: Lord, what can I do? I am spent. People will not obey me. I have been pulling down houses, but the fire overtakes us faster than we can do it.

In those days, there was no fire brigade in London. Fighting fires was the job of the people of each parish, with leather buckets, and ladders, and great iron hooks on long poles, to pull down burning houses, and a few simple pumps, called fire engines, which squirted a little water. But against a fire like this, they were useless.

--

All through the afternoon, the fire spreads.

The streets are crowded with people, carrying their goods, and carts, and horses, all trying to get away, and getting in each other's way. The air is full of smoke, and the noise of the crackling flames, and the crash of falling houses, and the shouting of the people.

{img:crowded-street-people-carrying-chairs-bundles-afternoon}
In the late afternoon, Pepys meets Elizabeth, and some friends, and they go out onto the river again, in a boat, and watch the fire from the water. The wind is so strong, and the smoke so thick, that they are showered with sparks, which Pepys calls fire-drops, and must keep away from the shore.

--

As evening comes, they go to a little alehouse on the south bank of the river, in Southwark, and stay there until it is dark, watching the fire.

And Pepys writes about what they saw, in some of the most famous words in his diary. He writes that as it grew darker, the fire appeared more and more, in corners and upon steeples, and between churches and houses, as far as they could see up the hill of the City. And that it was not like the fine flame of an ordinary fire.

They saw the fire as one entire arch, from one end of London Bridge, up the hill, for an arch of above a mile long. It made him weep, he writes, to see it: the churches and the houses, all alight at once, far away across the dark water.

--

Then they go home to Seething Lane, sadly, late at night.

Pepys's house is not yet in danger. But the fire is coming closer. So that night, with the help of his clerk, Will Hewer, and his friends, he carries his money, and his plate, and his best things, and his precious diary, down into his cellar, or packs them up, ready to be carried away.

{img:man-packing-chest-candlelight-books-papers-night}
It is a bright moonlit night, he writes, and very hot, and the light of the fire makes it as bright as day. Nobody in London sleeps much that night.
