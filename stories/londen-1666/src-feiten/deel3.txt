TOPIC: Coffeehouses, taverns and news
===
IMG: coffeehouse-interior-long-table-men-pamphlets-1666 | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a crowded London coffeehouse in 1666 with men in wigs sitting at long wooden tables reading pamphlets and drinking from small cups, a fire and coffee pots, warm lamplight
HEAD: London's first coffeehouse opened in 1652, and within a few years there were dozens.
BODY: Coffee was a new arrival in London. The first coffeehouse opened in 1652, in a small alley near Cornhill. It was run by a man named Pasqua Rosee, who came from the Ottoman lands. He had learned to make coffee there. At first, many people thought the bitter black drink was strange. Some said it tasted like soot. Yet coffeehouses quickly became popular. By the 1660s, there were many of them in the city. They were warm, well lit and full of talk. For a penny, a customer could come in, sit down and join the conversation. Men read newspapers and pamphlets there and argued about politics, trade and science. Because people learned so much in them, coffeehouses were sometimes called penny universities. Even so, they were mostly for men. Women rarely went inside. Yet the coffeehouse changed London life. Business, news and ideas now had a new home.
===
IMG: coffee-pot-fire-small-cups-counter | a row of tall metal coffee pots warming by a fire on the counter of a 17th-century London coffeehouse, small handleless cups lined up, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: Coffee in the 1660s was strong, bitter and served without milk.
BODY: The coffee of 1666 was quite different from today. The beans were roasted over a fire and ground by hand. They were boiled in water in large pots. The drink was thick, strong and bitter. It was served in small dishes without handles. Some people added sugar. Milk was rarely used. Coffeehouses also sold chocolate, which was even more expensive, and tea, which was very new. Some sold sherbet, a sweet drink made with fruit. Alcohol was usually not served. That made coffeehouses different from taverns. People said that coffee kept the mind clear, while ale made it cloudy. Pepys drank coffee often. He visited coffeehouses to hear news and meet colleagues. Even so, not everyone liked them. Some critics claimed they were places of gossip and idleness. Yet their popularity kept growing. Some later coffeehouses became famous institutions, and one of them grew into a great insurance market.
===
IMG: tavern-interior-wooden-booths-ale-1666 | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a cosy London tavern in 1666 with wooden booths, people drinking ale from pewter tankards, a fireplace and low beams, warm firelight
HEAD: Taverns, inns and alehouses were found on almost every street in London.
BODY: London had hundreds of places to drink. There were taverns, which mainly sold wine. There were alehouses, which sold ale and beer to ordinary people. There were inns, which offered rooms for travellers and stabling for horses. Each had its own sign hanging outside. Taverns were places for business as well as pleasure. Merchants signed contracts there. Friends met for dinner. Clubs and societies held their meetings in private rooms. Pepys visited taverns very often. He drank, ate and talked there with colleagues and friends. He also made promises to drink less, and he often broke them. Even so, these places were the social centre of the city. People of all ranks gathered in them. Some old London pubs still stand on the same sites. A few were rebuilt soon after the Great Fire. The Ye Olde Cheshire Cheese in Fleet Street was rebuilt in 1667 and still serves drinks today.
===
IMG: london-gazette-newspaper-printing-1666 | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a printer in a London shop in 1666 lifting a freshly printed single sheet newspaper from a wooden press, stacks of paper and type cases, soft lamplight
HEAD: The London Gazette, first printed in 1665, is still published today.
BODY: News in 1666 travelled more slowly than today. Yet Londoners were hungry for it. In November 1665, a new official newspaper appeared. It was printed in Oxford at first, because the court had fled there from the plague. It was called the Oxford Gazette. A few months later, when the court returned, it moved to the capital and became the London Gazette. It came out twice a week. It printed royal announcements, news from abroad and reports of naval battles. In September 1666, it published an account of the Great Fire. The printing office itself had to move during the fire. Even so, the Gazette was not the only source of news. People also read handwritten newsletters and cheap pamphlets. They heard news in coffeehouses and taverns. Yet the London Gazette was remarkable. It has been published without a break ever since. It is one of the oldest surviving newspapers in the world.
===
IMG: bills-of-mortality-printed-sheet-table | a printed weekly list of deaths in London from the 1660s lying on a wooden table, beside it a candle, spectacles and a quill pen, soft warm candlelight
HEAD: Every week, London printed a list of the deaths in each parish, called the Bills of Mortality.
BODY: Long before modern statistics, London kept count of its dead. Each week, parish clerks reported how many people had died and been baptised in their parish. The figures were collected and printed on a single sheet. These were called the Bills of Mortality. People bought them to see how healthy the city was. They also listed causes of death. Some of these causes sound strange today. They include grief, fright, teeth and being found dead in the street. The causes were often decided by searchers, usually elderly women, who looked at the bodies. They had no medical training. In 1662, a man named John Graunt studied the Bills carefully. He looked for patterns in births and deaths. His small book is now seen as one of the first works of statistics. Even so, the figures were not always accurate. Yet the Bills give a rare window into the lives and deaths of ordinary Londoners.
===
IMG: news-crowd-reading-notice-church-door | a small crowd of Londoners in 17th-century clothes gathered around a printed notice nailed to a church door in 1666, some reading, some listening, soft morning light
HEAD: Most people heard the news by word of mouth rather than reading it.
BODY: Many Londoners in 1666 could read, at least a little. In the city, perhaps more than half of men could sign their names. Yet printed news was still not part of everyone's daily life. Most people heard about events by talking. They shared news in markets, churches, taverns and shops. Proclamations were read aloud in public places and nailed to church doors. Town criers rang their bells and called out announcements. Ballad sellers sang songs about recent events, and people bought the printed words. During the Great Fire, rumours spread very quickly. Some people believed that the French or the Dutch had started it. England was at war with both. Foreigners were attacked in the streets. Even so, the rumours were false. Yet they show how fear could travel faster than the truth. In a city with no radio or telephones, news moved from person to person, one conversation at a time.

TOPIC: Old St Paul's Cathedral
===
IMG: old-st-pauls-exterior-square-tower-no-spire-1666 | Old St Paul's Cathedral in London in 1666, a very long Gothic stone church with a tall square central tower and no spire, a classical columned portico at the west end, surrounded by timber houses, soft evening light
HEAD: Old St Paul's was one of the longest churches in Europe.
BODY: The cathedral that burned in 1666 was not the one people see today. Old St Paul's was a huge Gothic building. It had been built over many years after a fire in 1087. It was finished in the 1300s. The cathedral was about five hundred and eighty feet long. That made it one of the longest churches in Europe. It once had a very tall wooden spire, covered in lead. Some say it was the tallest in England. Yet in 1561, lightning struck the spire, and it burned. It was never rebuilt. By 1666, the cathedral had only a square central tower. The building was old and in poor condition. In the 1630s, the architect Inigo Jones had added a classical portico with tall columns at the west end. Even so, the old church was much loved. For centuries, it had been the great landmark of the city, visible from far across the countryside.
===
IMG: pauls-walk-nave-people-strolling-talking | the long stone nave of Old St Paul's in the 1660s with people strolling and talking in small groups, an indoor scene, enclosed by stone walls, a high vaulted ceiling, soft light from tall windows
HEAD: The nave of Old St Paul's was used as a public walkway and meeting place.
BODY: Inside Old St Paul's, the long central aisle had an unusual use. People treated it almost like a street. It was known as Paul's Walk. Londoners walked up and down it to meet friends, hear news and do business. Lawyers met clients there. Servants looked for new masters. Some people even used the nave as a shortcut, carrying goods through the church. Merchants made deals beside the pillars. Gossips exchanged the latest stories. Fashionable young men strolled there to show off their clothes. The noise sometimes disturbed the services in the choir. Church leaders complained about it many times. Even so, Paul's Walk remained popular for many years. In the plays of the early 1600s, characters often meet in Paul's. It shows how closely the church and city life were bound together. A cathedral was not only a place of prayer. It was also part of the daily life of the town.
===
IMG: wren-evelyn-surveying-old-st-pauls-1666 | two gentlemen in wigs and long coats looking up at the cracked stone walls and old tower of a great Gothic cathedral in 1666, one holding a drawing, soft late summer light
HEAD: Just a week before the fire, Christopher Wren had proposed giving Old St Paul's a dome.
BODY: By the 1660s, the old cathedral was in poor shape. Its walls leaned, and its tower was weak. A group of experts was asked to look at it. One of them was a young scientist and architect named Christopher Wren. Another was John Evelyn, the writer and diarist. On the twenty-seventh of August 1666, they examined the building together. Wren suggested repairing the old church and replacing the tower with a great dome. Others wanted to keep the old style. The group did not agree. Then, only days later, the Great Fire broke out. The cathedral was destroyed. Even so, the plans were not entirely wasted. The idea of a dome stayed in Wren's mind. Years later, he would design a completely new cathedral. Yet at the time, no one could have guessed how quickly the old debate would be decided. The fire settled the question in a single night.
===
IMG: st-faiths-crypt-stone-pillars-dim-light | the dark stone crypt beneath Old St Paul's Cathedral, low round pillars and vaulted ceilings, an indoor scene, enclosed by stone walls, faint light from a small high window
HEAD: The parish church of St Faith was hidden inside the crypt of St Paul's.
BODY: Beneath Old St Paul's lay a large crypt. Part of it served as a separate parish church, dedicated to Saint Faith. Local people worshipped there underground. The Stationers' Company, which controlled the book trade, also used the crypt. It was here that the booksellers stored their books when the fire approached. They believed the thick walls and stone vaults would protect them. When the roof of the cathedral fell in, burning timber crashed into the church above. The heat broke through to the crypt. The books caught fire and burned for about a week. Some accounts say the loss was worth a vast sum of money. Pepys heard about it and wrote of the great loss to the booksellers. Even so, a few things in the cathedral survived. Some monuments were damaged but not destroyed. One statue of the poet John Donne, wrapped in his burial shroud, came through the fire and can still be seen today.
===
IMG: john-donne-shroud-statue-marble-niche | a white marble statue of a standing man wrapped in a burial shroud in a stone niche inside a cathedral, an indoor scene, enclosed by stone walls, soft candlelight
HEAD: The statue of the poet John Donne was one of the few monuments to survive the fire.
BODY: John Donne was one of the great poets of the early 1600s. He was also Dean of St Paul's, the head of the cathedral clergy. He died in 1631. Before his death, he did something unusual. He had a portrait painted of himself wrapped in a burial shroud, standing upright. A sculptor named Nicholas Stone used this painting to carve a marble statue. It was placed in Old St Paul's. During the Great Fire, the cathedral roof fell, and most monuments were destroyed. Yet Donne's statue survived. It fell into the crypt and was found afterwards with only slight damage. Scorch marks can still be seen on its base. Today, it stands in the new St Paul's Cathedral. Even so, it is a strange sight. Donne appears calm and still, as if waiting quietly for the end. For many visitors, it is one of the most moving links between the old cathedral and the new one.

TOPIC: The king, the court and the theatre
===
IMG: charles-ii-return-1660-procession-london | a royal procession through a London street in 1660 with crowds cheering, flowers thrown from windows, a tall man with dark curly hair riding a horse, colourful flags, bright sunshine
HEAD: King Charles the Second returned to London in 1660, after years of exile.
BODY: The 1660s began with a great celebration. For eleven years, England had had no king. Charles the First had been executed in 1649. The country was ruled for a time by Oliver Cromwell. After Cromwell died, the government fell into confusion. In 1660, Parliament invited the old king's son to return. Charles the Second arrived in London on his thirtieth birthday, the twenty-ninth of May. Crowds filled the streets. Flowers were strewn on the road, and fountains ran with wine. Pepys was there. He had sailed with the fleet that brought the king home from the Netherlands. This period is called the Restoration. Even so, the joy did not last forever. The 1660s brought plague, war and fire. Yet the return of the king changed the mood of London. Music, theatre and fashion came back to life after the quieter years under Cromwell.
===
IMG: whitehall-palace-banqueting-house-river-1666 | the long rambling Whitehall Palace beside the River Thames in 1666 with a white stone classical banqueting hall among older brick buildings, boats at the river stairs, soft afternoon light
HEAD: The king lived at Whitehall, the largest palace in Europe at the time.
BODY: Whitehall Palace stood beside the Thames, between the city and Westminster. It was enormous. Some say it was the largest palace in Europe. It had more than a thousand rooms. Yet it was not one grand building. It was a maze of old and new houses, courtyards, gardens and galleries. It had grown over more than a century. Its finest building was the Banqueting House, designed by Inigo Jones in the 1620s. Its ceiling had been painted by Rubens. Courtiers, servants, officials and visitors crowded the palace every day. Pepys came often on Navy business. He walked through the galleries hoping to see the king or the great lords. Even so, Whitehall did not survive. Most of the palace burned in another fire in 1698. Only the Banqueting House remains today. It is one of the few royal buildings in London that still stands from that time.
===
IMG: restoration-theatre-stage-candles-audience-1666 | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, the inside of a London playhouse in the 1660s with a candlelit stage, painted scenery, actors in costume and an audience in the pit and galleries, warm candlelight
HEAD: After 1660, women appeared on the English stage for the first time.
BODY: Under Cromwell, the theatres of London had been closed. When the king returned, they opened again. Two companies were given royal permission to perform. They built new indoor theatres with painted scenery and candlelit stages. Something else changed too. Before, female roles were played by boys. Now, women became actresses. This was a great novelty. Audiences crowded in to see them. One of the most famous was Nell Gwyn. She began by selling oranges in the theatre and became a popular comic actress. She later became a companion of the king. Pepys loved the theatre. He went very often, sometimes several times a week. He wrote about the plays, the actors and the audience. He sometimes made vows to go less, and then broke them. Even so, the theatres were closed again for a long time in 1665 and 1666, because of the plague. Their reopening was a sign that life was returning.
===
IMG: royal-society-meeting-telescope-instruments | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, gentlemen in wigs gathered around a table with a telescope, an air pump and scientific instruments in a London room in the 1660s, soft window light
HEAD: The Royal Society, founded in 1660, brought scientists together to share experiments.
BODY: The 1660s were an exciting time for science. In November 1660, a group of learned men met in London. They decided to form a society to study nature through experiments. The king gave them his support. In 1662, they received a royal charter and became the Royal Society. Its members included Robert Boyle, who studied gases, and Robert Hooke, who studied tiny things with a microscope. Christopher Wren was a founding member too. The society met regularly to watch experiments and discuss new ideas. Pepys joined in 1665. He did not fully understand all the science, but he found it fascinating. Later in his life, he even became its president. Even so, not everyone took the society seriously. Some people laughed at its experiments. Yet its motto expressed a powerful idea. It meant, roughly, take nobody's word for it. The Royal Society still exists and remains one of the world's leading scientific academies.
===
IMG: hooke-micrographia-flea-drawing-book-open | a large old book lying open on a wooden table showing a detailed engraved drawing of a flea seen through a microscope, a brass microscope beside it, soft candlelight
HEAD: In 1665, Robert Hooke published a book with giant drawings of a flea and a louse.
BODY: Robert Hooke was one of the most inventive scientists of his time. He worked for the Royal Society, preparing experiments for its meetings. In 1665, he published a remarkable book called Micrographia. It showed what he had seen through a microscope. It included large, detailed drawings of tiny things. There was a flea, a louse, the eye of a fly and the point of a needle. Some of the drawings folded out to a great size. Readers had never seen anything like them. Hooke also looked at a thin slice of cork. He saw tiny boxes and called them cells. That word is still used in biology today. Pepys bought the book and stayed up until two in the morning reading it. He called it the most ingenious book he had ever read. Even so, Hooke was not only a scientist. After the Great Fire, he helped survey and rebuild the city. He also worked with Wren on many buildings.
===
IMG: dutch-war-ships-battle-distance-calm-sea | a fleet of wooden sailing warships seen far away on a calm sea in 1666, sails and flags against a hazy sky, a low coastline, quiet and still, soft muted light
HEAD: In 1666, England was at war with the Netherlands, the greatest trading nation of the time.
BODY: The year of the fire was also a year of war. England and the Dutch Republic were fighting over trade and the sea. Both nations had large navies and rich merchant fleets. In June 1666, there was a huge sea battle that lasted four days. Pepys heard the guns from London, far away across the land. The English fleet suffered heavy losses. In August, the English struck back by burning Dutch merchant ships. As a Navy official, Pepys was deeply involved. He worked hard to find money, men and supplies for the fleet. The war was expensive, and the navy was often short of cash. Sailors went unpaid for months. Even so, the war had little direct effect on daily life in London until the fire. Then rumours spread that Dutch or French agents had started it. Yet the following year brought real disaster. In 1667, Dutch ships sailed into the Medway and attacked the English fleet in its own harbour.

TOPIC: The plague year of 1665
===
IMG: plague-quiet-street-closed-doors-1665-london | a quiet empty street in London in 1665 with closed doors and shuttered windows, a few people walking at a distance, grass between the cobbles, soft grey light, calm and still
HEAD: A year before the fire, London suffered its last great outbreak of plague.
BODY: The year 1665 is remembered as the year of the Great Plague. The disease had visited London many times before. But this outbreak was especially severe. It began in the spring in the poor suburbs outside the walls. By the summer, it was spreading through the whole city. The Bills of Mortality recorded nearly seventy thousand deaths from plague that year. Many historians think the real number was closer to one hundred thousand. That was perhaps a quarter of the people of London. Those who could afford it left the city. The king and his court moved to Salisbury and then to Oxford. Many wealthy merchants and doctors also fled. Even so, many people stayed. The poor could not leave. Officials, clergy and some doctors remained to help. Pepys stayed in London for much of the year, working at the Navy Office. His diary gives a quiet, sad record of a city falling silent.
===
IMG: red-cross-painted-door-plague-house-1665 | a wooden door of a London house in 1665 with a large red cross painted on it and a small handwritten paper beside it, an empty street, soft muted daylight
HEAD: Houses with plague were shut up, and a red cross was painted on the door.
BODY: The authorities had rules for dealing with plague. When someone in a house fell ill, the whole household was shut in. The door was locked from the outside. A red cross was painted on it, about a foot high. Above it were written the words, Lord have mercy upon us. A watchman stood outside day and night to make sure no one left. Food and other things were passed in through a window. The household stayed shut in for at least forty days. This was meant to stop the disease from spreading. Yet it was terrible for the healthy people trapped inside with the sick. Some escaped through windows or bribed the watchmen. Even so, the rule was not completely without reason. People did understand that the disease could pass between people. Pepys wrote about seeing houses with red crosses for the first time. It made him uneasy, and he bought some tobacco to chew, believing it would protect him.
===
IMG: dog-catcher-empty-lane-1665-quiet | an empty narrow lane in London in 1665, long shadows, a single figure far away at the end of the lane, closed shutters, quiet afternoon light, calm atmosphere
HEAD: During the plague, the city ordered thousands of dogs and cats to be killed.
BODY: In 1665, people did not know what caused the plague. Today we know it was spread mainly by fleas carried by rats, and perhaps also from person to person. In the 1600s, many believed that bad air or dirty animals spread the disease. So the city authorities ordered that dogs and cats be killed. Dog killers were paid for each animal. Thousands were destroyed. Sadly, this may have made things worse. Cats and dogs had helped keep the rat population down. With fewer of them, the rats may have increased. Other measures were also tried. Fires were lit in the streets to purify the air. People carried herbs and sweet-smelling posies. Some smoked tobacco to keep the disease away. Even so, none of these measures stopped the plague. It faded only as the weather grew colder. Yet the people of 1665 were not foolish. They did their best with the knowledge they had.
===
IMG: doctor-plague-house-visit-1665-quiet-room | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a doctor in a long dark coat standing quietly by a simple bed in a London room in 1665, a woman sitting nearby, a candle on a table, soft dim light
HEAD: Some doctors stayed in London during the plague to care for the sick, at great risk.
BODY: Many doctors left London when the plague came. Their patients were mostly wealthy, and those patients had fled. Yet some doctors stayed behind. One of them was Nathaniel Hodges. He remained in the city throughout the outbreak and treated many patients. Later he wrote an account of what he had seen. He described how he went out each morning to visit the sick, and how he came home each evening tired and worried. He survived the plague himself. Other doctors were less fortunate and died. The city also appointed special plague doctors. Some were inexperienced or untrained. Even so, the doctors did what they could. Their medicines had little effect, but their presence brought comfort. Nurses and searchers also worked among the sick. These were often poor women who needed the money. Their work was dangerous and unpleasant. Yet without them, the city would have suffered even more.
===
IMG: autumn-frost-london-rooftops-plague-ends-1665 | the rooftops of old London covered with white frost on a cold clear morning in late 1665, chimneys smoking gently, a pale sun rising, calm and peaceful
HEAD: As the cold winter of 1665 arrived, the plague slowly began to fade.
BODY: The plague reached its peak in September 1665. In one week, more than seven thousand deaths were recorded. Then, as autumn turned to winter, the number of deaths began to fall. The cold weather slowed the fleas that spread the disease. By December, the city was recovering. People who had fled began to return. Shops opened again, and the streets grew busy. The king returned to London in February 1666. Pepys noted with relief that the town was filling up again. Even so, the plague did not disappear completely. Cases continued in 1666, and the theatres stayed closed for some time. Yet London never again suffered a plague outbreak on that scale. Some people later claimed that the Great Fire had burned the plague out of the city. Historians doubt this. The fire mostly burned the old city, while the plague had been worst in the poor suburbs. Even so, the great plague of 1665 was London's last.

TOPIC: After the fire
===
IMG: refugees-camp-moorfields-tents-1666 | families in 17th-century clothes camping in tents and makeshift shelters on open green fields outside London in September 1666, bundles of belongings, cooking fires, hazy evening light
HEAD: After the fire, tens of thousands of homeless Londoners camped in the open fields.
BODY: When the fire ended, many thousands of people had nowhere to live. They fled to the open spaces outside the city walls. The largest camp was at Moorfields, a green area just north of the wall. Families set up tents and shelters made of sheets, boards and anything else they had saved. Some slept under the open sky. Others camped in Islington, Highgate and across the river. The king rode out to visit the camps. He spoke to the people and promised them help. He ordered bread to be brought from the countryside. Markets were set up near the camps. Churches and towns outside London were asked to take people in. Even so, the camps were uncomfortable, and autumn was coming. Many families moved in with relatives or found rooms elsewhere. Some never returned to the city. Yet the speed of the response was remarkable. Within weeks, many people had found shelter and work again.
===
IMG: robert-hubert-watchmaker-courtroom-1666 | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a thin young man standing alone before judges in wigs in a wood-panelled London courtroom in 1666, quiet and serious, soft window light
HEAD: A French watchmaker confessed to starting the fire, although he could not have done it.
BODY: After the fire, many people wanted someone to blame. A young Frenchman named Robert Hubert said that he had started it. He claimed he had thrown a fire grenade into the bakery in Pudding Lane. His story was full of mistakes. Some of the judges doubted him. Some thought he was not in his right mind. Yet he stuck to his confession. In October 1666, he was found guilty and hanged. Only later did the truth become clear. The captain of the ship that had brought Hubert to England said Hubert had not arrived in London until two days after the fire began. Even so, it was too late for him. His case is remembered as one of the sad mistakes of English justice. Historians believe the fire was an accident. Yet for years, many people continued to blame foreigners or Catholics. A plaque blaming Catholics was even put up in Pudding Lane. It was removed much later.
===
IMG: fire-court-judges-papers-dispute-1667 | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a small court with judges in wigs and black robes listening to two men arguing over a property map spread on a table in London in 1667, soft window light
HEAD: A special Fire Court was set up to settle disputes between landlords and tenants.
BODY: After the fire, there was a huge legal problem. Who should pay for rebuilding? Many houses were rented. Leases said that tenants had to repair the houses they lived in. But these houses no longer existed. Landlords and tenants argued about money, rights and responsibility. Ordinary courts would have taken years to settle all the cases. So in 1667, Parliament created a special Fire Court. It was made up of judges who heard cases quickly and fairly. They tried to reach practical agreements that let rebuilding begin. They often split the costs between landlords and tenants. The judges worked without pay. Over several years, they heard more than a thousand cases. Even so, not everyone was happy with the decisions. Yet the court worked remarkably well. It is one reason why London was rebuilt so quickly. The court's records still survive and tell the stories of many ordinary people who lost their homes.
===
IMG: new-brick-houses-rebuilding-street-1670s | new red brick houses being built along a widened street in London in the 1670s, workmen on wooden scaffolding, bricks and timber piled on the ground, bright morning light
HEAD: After 1667, new houses in the City had to be built of brick or stone.
BODY: The king and Parliament wanted to make sure such a fire never happened again. In 1667, they passed the Rebuilding Act. It set strict rules for new houses. They had to be built of brick or stone, not timber. Overhanging upper floors were banned. There were only a few standard types of houses, depending on the width of the street. Larger streets had to have larger houses. Some streets were widened, and new markets were laid out. Work began quickly. Within a few years, thousands of new brick houses had been built. Yet the city kept its old street plan. Several architects, including Wren and Evelyn, had drawn grand new designs. They imagined wide avenues and open squares. But owners wanted their old plots back, and there was no money to buy them out. Even so, the new London was safer. Brick houses with tiled roofs did not burn as easily as the old timber ones.
===
IMG: wren-new-st-pauls-dome-construction-1700 | the great white stone dome of the new St Paul's Cathedral rising above London around 1700, surrounded by wooden scaffolding and cranes, red brick houses below, soft golden morning light
HEAD: Christopher Wren designed a new St Paul's Cathedral, which took over thirty years to build.
BODY: After the fire, Christopher Wren became the most important architect in London. He was asked to design a new St Paul's Cathedral. His first designs were rejected. The church leaders thought they were too unusual. Finally, a design was approved, and work began in 1675. Wren then quietly made many changes as the building rose. The most famous was the great dome. It was built in three layers, one inside the other. The outer dome rose high above the city. The cathedral was declared complete in 1711, when Wren was nearly eighty years old. He had seen it built from start to finish. Even so, it was not his only work. He also designed fifty-one new parish churches for the city. Many of their white spires can still be seen today. Wren was buried in the cathedral he had built. A simple inscription there says, in Latin, if you seek his monument, look around you.
===
IMG: monument-column-golden-flames-top-london | a tall stone column with a gilded flaming urn at the top rising above red brick houses in London in the 1680s, the river glinting nearby, soft afternoon sunshine
HEAD: The Monument to the Great Fire stands two hundred and two feet tall, and two hundred and two feet from the bakery.
BODY: After the fire, it was decided to build a memorial. The design was by Christopher Wren and Robert Hooke. It is a tall stone column, simply called the Monument. It was finished in 1677. Its height is two hundred and two feet. That is the same as the distance from the column to the place in Pudding Lane where the fire began. At the top is a gilded urn with flames, made of gilded copper. Inside the column, a spiral staircase of three hundred and eleven steps leads up to a viewing platform. Visitors still climb it today to look over the city. Hooke also hoped to use the column for scientific experiments. He thought it could serve as a giant telescope or help with experiments on gravity and air pressure. Even so, traffic vibrations made this difficult. Yet the Monument remains one of the clearest reminders of the fire. It stands quietly among the modern buildings, close to where it all began.

HEAD: Pepys stopped writing his diary in 1669, fearing he was going blind.
BODY: For nine and a half years, Pepys wrote in his diary almost every day. Then, in May 1669, he stopped. His eyes had become painful and weak. He feared that writing by candlelight was making him blind. In his final entry, he wrote that he was saying goodbye to his diary, and he felt it was almost like seeing himself go into his grave. In fact, he did not go blind. He lived for another thirty-four years. He became Secretary to the Admiralty, the most important official in the navy. He was briefly imprisoned in the Tower during a political crisis, but he was released. He spent his last years in a house at Clapham, south of London. He died there in 1703, aged seventy. Even so, he never returned to his diary. Yet the pages he had already written were enough. They kept the London of the 1660s alive, with its fires, its music and its everyday joys.
