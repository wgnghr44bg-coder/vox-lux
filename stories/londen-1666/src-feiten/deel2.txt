TOPIC: Samuel Pepys and his diary
===
IMG: pepys-writing-diary-shorthand-candle-study | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a man in a long wig writing in a leather-bound diary by candlelight in a small wood-panelled study in London in 1666, bookshelves behind him, warm candlelight
HEAD: Samuel Pepys wrote his famous diary in a secret shorthand.
BODY: Samuel Pepys kept his diary from January 1660 to May 1669. He wrote almost every day, often late in the evening. He used a system of shorthand, a fast way of writing with special symbols. It was not a true secret code. Many educated people of the time could learn it. Yet it kept his words private from servants and family. Pepys wrote about everything. He described great events, like the return of the king and the Great Fire. He also wrote about his meals, his clothes, his quarrels and his worries. He was very honest, even about his own faults. In the end, the diary filled six volumes and more than a million words. Pepys left it to his old college in Cambridge. It sat unread for more than a century. Then, in the 1820s, a student decoded it. Since then it has become one of the most loved diaries in the English language.
===
IMG: navy-office-clerks-desks-papers-seething-lane | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, clerks in wigs and dark coats working at wooden desks covered with papers and ledgers in the Navy Office in London in 1666, tall windows, soft daylight
HEAD: Pepys worked at the Navy Office, where he helped supply the king's ships.
BODY: Pepys was not born rich. His father was a tailor. Yet Pepys was clever and hard-working, and he had a powerful relative. Through this cousin, he got a job at the Navy Office in 1660. His title was Clerk of the Acts. The office managed the supplies for the Royal Navy. It bought timber, rope, sails, food and guns. It paid the sailors and the shipbuilders. Pepys threw himself into the work. He studied arithmetic so that he could check the accounts. He learned about the different kinds of timber and rope. He visited the dockyards often. He also accepted gifts from merchants who wanted contracts, which was common at the time. Even so, he became one of the most capable officials in the country. Later in his life, he rose to the highest office in the navy administration. Many historians see him as one of the founders of a professional navy.
===
IMG: pepys-wife-elizabeth-portrait-room-window | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a young woman in a silk gown with dark curled hair sitting by a window in a London house in 1666, a small dog at her feet, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Pepys married Elizabeth St Michel when she was only fifteen years old.
BODY: Pepys's wife was named Elizabeth. Her father was a French Protestant who had come to England. She and Samuel married in 1655, when she was fifteen and he was twenty-two. They were poor at first and lived in a single room. Their marriage was loving, but often difficult. The diary records many quarrels and many reconciliations. Pepys could be jealous and controlling. He was also unfaithful to her, and he wrote about it in his diary. Elizabeth was lively and fond of music, dancing and fashion. She kept dogs and caged birds. She sometimes felt lonely, because the couple had no children. Even so, the diary also shows tenderness. Pepys worried when she was ill and enjoyed walks and outings with her. Elizabeth died of a fever in 1669, at only twenty-nine. Pepys never married again. He placed a fine monument to her in their parish church.
===
IMG: seething-lane-house-garden-navy-office-1666 | a brick and timber house with a small walled garden in Seething Lane, London, in 1666, a pear tree and herb beds, a church tower nearby, gentle morning light
HEAD: Pepys lived in a house attached to the Navy Office in Seething Lane.
BODY: The Navy Office stood in Seething Lane, close to the Tower of London. The officials lived in houses that were part of the office buildings. Pepys and his family had rooms there, with a small garden behind. He could walk from his bedroom to his desk in a minute. His neighbours were his colleagues, including Sir William Penn. Penn's son, also called William, later founded the colony of Pennsylvania. The two families visited each other often, played cards and argued. Pepys worshipped at the nearby church of St Olave, Hart Street. He and Elizabeth had their own pew in a gallery. The house was improved over the years as Pepys grew richer. He added new rooms, new furniture and new decorations. Even so, it was a working house, full of papers and visitors. During the Great Fire, it survived, though the flames came very close. The church of St Olave also survived and still stands today.
===
IMG: pepys-playing-flageolet-music-evening-room | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a man in a long wig playing a small wooden flute in a candlelit London parlour in 1666, a lute and sheet music on a table beside him, warm candlelight
HEAD: Pepys loved music and played several instruments.
BODY: Music was one of the great pleasures of Pepys's life. He played the lute, the viol and the flageolet, a small kind of wooden flute. He also sang, and he wrote a few songs of his own. One of them was a setting of words by Shakespeare. Pepys often made music with friends in the evening. He sometimes played on the leads, the flat roof of his house, on warm summer nights. He took his flageolet with him on journeys. In his diary he wrote that music was the thing of the world that he loved most. He paid for singing lessons for himself and his wife. He even took servants partly because they could sing. Even so, he was not a professional. He was an enthusiastic amateur, like many educated people of his time. Yet his love of music fills the pages of the diary. It reminds readers that daily life in 1666 was not only work and worry.
===
IMG: pepys-books-library-glass-cases-shelves | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, tall wooden bookcases with glass doors full of leather-bound books in a quiet room in 1666, a reading desk and a candle, soft window light
HEAD: Pepys built a library of about three thousand books, which still survives.
BODY: Pepys was a passionate collector of books. He bought them throughout his life. He arranged them carefully, ordering them by size so that the shelves looked neat. When his shelves were full, he had special bookcases made. They had glass doors and were built of oak. They are among the earliest bookcases of their kind in England. By the end of his life, he owned around three thousand books. He also collected prints, maps, music and old ballads. When he died in 1703, he left the library to Magdalene College in Cambridge. It is still there, in its original bookcases, in the same order he gave it. His diary is part of the collection. Even so, the library also holds many ordinary things. There are popular songs, cheap pamphlets and little books for children. Pepys saved things that others threw away. Thanks to him, much of the everyday print of the 1600s survives.

TOPIC: A London household in 1666
===
IMG: kitchen-maid-hearth-pots-london-1666 | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a young maid in a linen cap and apron stirring a pot over an open hearth in a London kitchen in 1666, copper pans hanging on the wall, warm firelight
HEAD: Even a middle-class family in London usually had servants.
BODY: In 1666, servants were a normal part of household life. Even fairly modest families employed at least one maid. The Pepys household had several servants over the years. There was usually a cook maid, a chamber maid and a boy. Servants lived in the house and ate with the family or in the kitchen. Their wages were low, but they received food, a bed and sometimes clothing. A maid's work began before dawn. She lit the fires, fetched water, cooked, cleaned and washed. Most servants were young. Many came from the countryside and planned to marry after a few years in service. Pepys wrote a great deal about his servants. Some he liked very much. Others he quarrelled with or dismissed. Even so, the relationship was close in many ways. Servants knew the family's secrets. In the diary, they appear as real people with their own lives and moods.
===
IMG: dinner-table-london-1666-meat-pies-pewter | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a family dinner table in London in 1666 with roast meat, pies and bread on pewter plates, a jug of ale, people in 17th-century clothes, soft daylight from a window
HEAD: The main meal of the day was eaten around midday and was called dinner.
BODY: Londoners in the 1660s ate their main meal at noon or in the early afternoon. It was called dinner. Breakfast was small, often just bread and ale. Supper in the evening was usually light too. Dinner, though, could be large. For those who could afford it, meat was central. There was beef, mutton, pork, chicken, rabbit and fish. Pies were very popular, filled with meat, fish or fruit. Vegetables were eaten too, though less than today. Pepys described many dinners in his diary. Once, for a special occasion, he served a great feast to friends. It included rabbits, a leg of mutton, fowls, a tart and much else. Even so, poorer families ate much more simply. Bread, cheese, pottage and a little meat on Sundays were common. Ale and beer were drunk by everyone, even children, because water was not always safe. Meals were also a time for family and friends to gather and talk.
===
IMG: first-cup-of-tea-1660-table-china-cup | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a single small china cup of tea on a wooden table in a London room in 1660s, a small teapot beside it, soft window light
HEAD: Pepys recorded drinking a cup of tea for the first time in 1660.
BODY: Tea was a very new drink in England in the 1660s. It came from China, carried by Dutch and English ships. It was expensive and rare. On the twenty-fifth of September 1660, Pepys wrote that he sent for a cup of tee, a China drink, which he had never drunk before. This is one of the earliest mentions of tea drinking in England. At first, tea was sold in coffee houses as a kind of medicine. People claimed it helped with headaches and tiredness. Later, Pepys came home to find his wife making tea. An apothecary had told her it was good for her cold. Even so, tea was not yet the national drink. Ale and beer were far more common. Yet tea's popularity grew quickly, especially after Charles the Second married a Portuguese princess who loved it. Within a hundred years, tea was drunk all over the country, by rich and poor alike.
===
IMG: clothes-fashion-silk-coat-wig-1666 | a gentleman in a long curled wig, a knee-length silk coat and a lace cravat standing in a London room in 1666, a tailor holding fabric beside him, soft daylight
HEAD: In the 1660s, fashionable men began wearing large curled wigs.
BODY: Fashion changed quickly after the king returned in 1660. Charles the Second had lived in France for years, and French styles came with him. Fashionable men wore long curled wigs that fell over their shoulders. Pepys bought his first wig in 1663. He worried about it at first, but he soon got used to it. Wigs were made from real human hair and were expensive. They also needed regular care, because they could get lice. In 1666, the king introduced a new style of dress. It was a long coat with a matching waistcoat underneath. This is often seen as the origin of the modern suit. Pepys followed fashion closely. He spent large sums on clothes, and he recorded each new purchase with pleasure. Even so, most people dressed far more simply, in wool and linen. Yet the fashions of the wealthy were visible everywhere on the streets.
===
IMG: washing-day-linen-tub-yard-maid-1666 | a maid washing linen in a large wooden tub in a small yard behind a London house in 1666, wet sheets hanging on a line, steam rising, morning light
HEAD: Washing day came only every few weeks, and it was a great effort.
BODY: Washing clothes in 1666 was hard, slow work. In many households, the big wash happened once a month or even less often. On washing day, maids rose very early. They heated large amounts of water in coppers or pots. The linen was soaked, scrubbed, beaten and rinsed. Lye, made from wood ash, was used to clean it. Soap was also used, but it was expensive. Then everything was wrung out by hand and hung to dry. In bad weather, wet linen hung all over the house for days. Pepys mentions washing days in his diary, often with some annoyance. The house was full of steam and wet cloth. Even so, clean linen was very important. People did not wash their bodies often, but they changed their shirts and shifts. White linen next to the skin was thought to keep the body clean. Clean collars and cuffs were a sign of respect.
===
IMG: apothecary-shop-jars-shelves-london-1666 | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, an apothecary shop in London in 1666 with rows of painted ceramic jars on wooden shelves, a man grinding herbs in a mortar, warm lamplight
HEAD: When people were ill, they often went first to the apothecary.
BODY: Medicine in 1666 was very different from today. Doctors were expensive, and many of their treatments did little good. Most people went first to an apothecary. Apothecaries sold medicines from shops lined with painted jars. They made pills, syrups and ointments from plants, minerals and sometimes animal parts. Many remedies were based on old ideas about four humours in the body. Bleeding, or letting blood, was a common treatment. Pepys himself suffered from a painful bladder stone. In 1658, before the diary began, he had a dangerous operation to remove it. He survived, which was quite lucky. Every year afterwards, on the anniversary, he held a small celebration to give thanks. He kept the stone in a little case. Even so, not all medicine was useless. Some herbal remedies did help. Yet illness was a constant fear. Families relied on prayer, luck and the care of those around them.
===
IMG: children-playing-street-old-london-1666 | children in 17th-century clothes playing with a spinning top and a ball in a narrow London street in 1666, timber houses behind, a cat on a doorstep, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Many London children started work by the age of seven or eight.
BODY: Childhood in London in the 1660s was short for most children. In poor families, children worked from an early age. Boys and girls ran errands, helped in shops and looked after younger brothers and sisters. Some sold small things in the streets. Others swept chimneys, a hard and dangerous job. Around the age of fourteen, many boys became apprentices. They served a master for seven years to learn a trade. Girls often went into service as maids. Children from wealthier families had more time for school. Boys learned Latin and arithmetic. Girls learned to read, write, sew and play music. Yet all children found time for play. They played with tops, balls, hoops and dolls. They played games like blind man's buff and leapfrog. Even so, many children did not survive to adulthood. Disease took many young lives. Families loved their children deeply, and they grieved for those they lost.

TOPIC: The Thames and London Bridge
===
IMG: old-london-bridge-houses-arches-1666-river | old London Bridge in 1666 with many narrow stone arches and tall timber houses built along its whole length, the River Thames rushing between the arches, small boats nearby, soft golden afternoon light
HEAD: Old London Bridge was lined with houses and shops along its whole length.
BODY: In 1666, there was only one bridge across the Thames in London. It had been built of stone in the late 1100s and early 1200s. It stood on nineteen narrow arches. Along the top ran a street lined with houses and shops. Some of the buildings were five or six floors high. They hung out over the river on both sides. The roadway between them was narrow and crowded. Carts, horses and walkers all squeezed through. At the southern end stood a great gatehouse. Near the middle stood a famous building called Nonsuch House. It had been made in the Netherlands, then shipped to London in pieces and put together with wooden pegs. Even so, the bridge was always busy and often in need of repair. Yet it was one of the wonders of London. Visitors from abroad came to see it and to walk across a street that stood above a river.
===
IMG: shooting-the-bridge-boat-rapids-arches | a small wooden rowing boat passing through the narrow stone arch of old London Bridge in 1666, the water rushing and foaming between the piers, two people holding on, soft daylight
HEAD: Going under London Bridge by boat was so dangerous that many passengers got out and walked.
BODY: The arches of old London Bridge were narrow. The stone piers that held them up were wide and bulky. Together, they blocked much of the river. When the tide flowed in or out, the water rushed through the narrow gaps. On one side, the water could be much higher than on the other. The river roared through the arches like a small waterfall. Boatmen called passing through them shooting the bridge. It was exciting and dangerous. Boats sometimes overturned, and people drowned. Many cautious passengers preferred to get out before the bridge. They walked around it and joined their boat again on the other side. There was a saying that London Bridge was made for wise men to go over and fools to go under. Even so, skilled watermen shot the bridge every day. Pepys did it often, though sometimes he chose to walk around instead.
===
IMG: london-bridge-gap-burned-1633-houses-north-end | the northern end of old London Bridge in 1666 with an open gap where houses had once stood, a low wooden fence and empty space between the buildings, the river below, soft evening light
HEAD: An earlier fire in 1633 left a gap on London Bridge that helped save Southwark in 1666.
BODY: London Bridge had suffered from fires before. In 1633, a fire destroyed many houses at the northern end of the bridge. Afterwards, some of those buildings were not rebuilt. An open space remained on the bridge. In 1666, the Great Fire reached the bridge from the city side. It burned the houses at the northern end. But when it reached the gap left by the earlier fire, it could not easily jump across. The open space acted as a firebreak. As a result, the fire did not spread along the whole bridge. Southwark, on the south bank, was largely saved. Even so, the bridge was damaged, and its waterwheels were destroyed. Yet it is a curious twist of history. A disaster in 1633 helped prevent a greater one thirty-three years later. London Bridge was repaired, and houses stood on it until the 1750s.
===
IMG: billingsgate-fish-market-river-boats-1666 | a busy riverside fish market at Billingsgate in London in 1666, fishwives with baskets, boats unloading fish, barrels and crates on the wharf, morning light over the Thames
HEAD: Billingsgate fish market was famous for its loud and colourful language.
BODY: Near London Bridge stood Billingsgate, the city's great landing place for fish. Boats brought in fish from the North Sea, the river and the coasts. Fishwives bought it there and carried it through the streets in baskets on their heads. They sold herring, mackerel, oysters, cod and eels. The market was loud and crowded. The fishwives of Billingsgate were known for their strong voices and their rough words. Their swearing was so famous that the word billingsgate came to mean coarse language. Yet the fishwives were also hard-working women who supported their families. Many were widows. Billingsgate had been a market for centuries, and it continued long after the fire. The fish market stayed near the river until the 1980s, when it moved to a new site. Even so, the old name lives on. People in London still speak of billingsgate when they mean a stream of colourful insults.
===
IMG: ships-moored-pool-of-london-masts-tower | many tall sailing ships moored in the Thames below London Bridge in 1666, a forest of masts and rigging, the Tower of London in the distance, soft morning mist
HEAD: Below London Bridge, the river was crowded with ships from all over the world.
BODY: Large ships could not pass under London Bridge. So they stopped below it, in a stretch of river called the Pool of London. Here hundreds of ships lay at anchor or tied up at wharves. Their masts rose like a forest above the water. They came from the Baltic, the Netherlands, France, Spain, Italy and the Americas. They brought timber, wine, spices, sugar, tobacco and cloth. They carried away English wool, cloth and other goods. Small boats called lighters carried cargo between the ships and the shore. Customs officers inspected the goods and collected taxes. The trade made London rich. Merchants built fine houses and great halls. Even so, 1666 was a difficult year. England was at war with the Dutch, and trade suffered. Yet the Pool of London remained the busy heart of the nation's trade. It would stay that way for nearly three hundred more years.
===
IMG: swans-on-thames-calm-evening-1666 | white swans gliding on the calm River Thames at dusk in 1666, the silhouette of old London Bridge and church towers in the distance, soft pink and gold evening light
HEAD: The swans on the Thames belonged to the king and to two London companies.
BODY: The Thames was full of swans in the 1600s. Visitors often mentioned them. They glided along the river among the boats. In England, swans were special birds. By old law, all unmarked mute swans on open waters belonged to the Crown. Two of the London livery companies, the Vintners and the Dyers, also had the right to own swans. Every year, a ceremony called swan upping took place. Men in boats rowed up the river and caught the young swans. They marked them to show who owned them. Swans were also eaten at grand feasts, though they were a rare and expensive dish. Even so, most Londoners simply enjoyed watching them. The swans were part of the calm beauty of the river. Swan upping still takes place on the Thames every summer. Today, though, it is done to count and protect the birds, not to mark them for the table.

TOPIC: Markets, shops and street cries
===
IMG: cheapside-market-stalls-crowds-1666 | the broad street of Cheapside in London in 1666 with market stalls, shoppers and goldsmiths' shops with painted signs, a stone cross monument in the distance, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Cheapside was the main shopping street of old London.
BODY: Cheapside was the broadest and busiest street in the old city. Its name came from an old English word for market. For centuries, it had been the place to buy and sell. Stalls filled the middle of the street. Along both sides stood shops. The goldsmiths had their shops in a famous row called Goldsmiths' Row. Their windows glittered with silver plate and jewels. Goldsmiths also acted as early bankers. Merchants left money with them for safekeeping. Cheapside was also a place for celebrations. Processions passed along it, and crowds gathered for royal events. Even so, it was not always a pleasant street. It was noisy, crowded and dirty. Yet it was the heart of the city's trade. The fire destroyed Cheapside completely. It was rebuilt in brick, and the street still exists today. Many of its side streets keep the names of the old trades, like Bread Street, Milk Street and Wood Street.
===
IMG: street-seller-crying-wares-basket-1666 | a street seller in a linen cap and apron with a basket of oranges calling out on a London street in 1666, people passing by, warm late afternoon light
HEAD: Street sellers walked the city shouting the names of their goods.
BODY: Shops and markets were not the only places to buy things. The streets were full of travelling sellers. Each had a special cry. Sellers called out to sell oranges, apples, cherries and oysters. Others sold matches, brooms, ballads, ink and milk. Some offered services instead. There were knife grinders, chair menders and rat catchers. Their cries rose above the noise of the street. Many were half sung, with a tune that everyone recognised. People could tell what was for sale before they saw it. Artists later made famous sets of pictures called the Cries of London. They showed these sellers with their baskets and goods. Even so, life for the street sellers was hard. Many were poor women and children. They walked many miles a day for a small profit. Yet their voices were part of the music of old London, from early morning to dusk.
===
IMG: royal-exchange-courtyard-merchants-1666 | the courtyard of the Royal Exchange in London before 1666, merchants in wigs and long coats standing in groups talking, stone arches around the courtyard, soft midday light
HEAD: Merchants from all over Europe met at the Royal Exchange to do business.
BODY: The Royal Exchange was a great building in the heart of the city. It was founded by a rich merchant named Sir Thomas Gresham. It opened in 1571. Its design was based on the exchange in Antwerp. It had a large open courtyard surrounded by covered walkways. Merchants met there every day to buy and sell goods and make deals. Upstairs there were small shops selling fine goods, like lace, gloves and perfume. Different groups of merchants stood in their own areas. There was a place for those who traded with Spain, another for the Baltic and so on. Pepys visited often, to hear news and meet people. Even so, the Exchange was not only for business. It was also a fashionable place to walk and be seen. The Great Fire destroyed the building. It was rebuilt and later burned again in 1838. The present Royal Exchange is the third on the same site.
===
IMG: leadenhall-market-poultry-meat-stalls-1666 | a covered market hall at Leadenhall in London in 1666 with stalls of poultry, game and meat hanging from hooks, traders and shoppers, light falling through high openings
HEAD: Leadenhall Market was one of the largest meat and poultry markets in Europe.
BODY: Leadenhall was one of the great markets of London. It took its name from an old mansion with a lead roof that had once stood on the site. By the 1600s, it was a busy market for meat, poultry, game, leather and wool. Visitors from abroad were amazed by its size. One foreign traveller wrote that he had never seen so much meat in one place. Beef, mutton, chickens, ducks and geese were sold in great quantities. The market survived the Great Fire with some damage. Afterwards, it was rebuilt with three covered courtyards. Even so, Leadenhall's history goes back much further. The site is near the centre of Roman London. Archaeologists have found remains of the great Roman hall beneath it. Today, Leadenhall Market has a beautiful Victorian roof. Yet it is still a market, and people still come to eat and shop there, as they have for centuries.
===
IMG: book-stalls-st-pauls-churchyard-1666 | booksellers' stalls and small shops around the churchyard of Old St Paul's Cathedral in London in 1666, books laid out on tables, people browsing, soft afternoon light
HEAD: St Paul's Churchyard was the centre of the English book trade.
BODY: Around the old cathedral of St Paul's stood many small shops. Most of them belonged to booksellers and printers. For more than a hundred years, this had been the centre of the book trade in England. Each shop had its own sign. Books were sold unbound, as loose printed sheets. The buyer chose a binding later. People came to browse, buy and hear the latest news. New plays, sermons, poems and pamphlets appeared there first. Pepys was a regular visitor. He often stopped to look at books on his way through the city. Sometimes he read a book in the shop without buying it. The booksellers belonged to a guild called the Stationers' Company. It controlled the printing of books in England. Even so, the trade suffered terribly in 1666. When the fire came, the booksellers lost not only their shops but also the stock they had stored in the cathedral. Many were ruined.
===
IMG: goldsmith-banker-counting-coins-scales-1666 | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a goldsmith in a London shop in 1666 weighing gold coins on small brass scales, a strongbox and ledger on the counter, warm candlelight
HEAD: London's goldsmiths also acted as some of the first bankers.
BODY: In the 1600s, there were no banks in London as we know them today. Instead, people turned to goldsmiths. Goldsmiths had strong rooms and safes to protect their gold and silver. Merchants began to leave their money with them for safekeeping. The goldsmith gave them a written receipt. Soon people began passing these receipts to others as payment. In this way, the receipts became an early kind of paper money. Goldsmiths also lent money and paid interest on deposits. Some lent large sums to the king himself. This was risky, because the king did not always repay. Pepys kept some of his money with a goldsmith. In 1667, when Dutch warships sailed into the Medway, he feared for it and sent his gold to be buried in the country. Even so, the goldsmiths played an important role in London's economy. Their work helped lay the foundations for modern banking. Some old London banks can trace their roots back to goldsmiths of this period.

TOPIC: Parks, gardens and Sundays
===
IMG: new-spring-garden-vauxhall-paths-trees-1666 | shady gravel paths between tall trees and hedges in a pleasure garden across the Thames from London in the 1660s, couples strolling, soft golden evening light
HEAD: On summer evenings, Pepys liked to take a boat across the river to the New Spring Garden at Vauxhall.
BODY: Across the Thames, in the fields of Lambeth, lay a pleasure garden. It was called the New Spring Garden. Later it became famous as Vauxhall Gardens. In the 1660s, it was a simple and pleasant place. There were shady walks, hedges and trees. Visitors could buy food and drink and listen to music. Entry was free, and people paid only for what they ate. Pepys went there many times. He usually travelled by boat with his wife or friends. He enjoyed hearing the nightingales and other birds singing in the trees. He also liked to watch the people. Fine ladies, young apprentices and families all came to walk. Even so, the garden could become rowdy late in the evening. Pepys sometimes complained about the behaviour of young gallants. Yet for most visitors, it was a calm escape from the noise of the city. For a few pennies, Londoners could spend a summer evening among trees.
===
IMG: st-james-park-canal-pelicans-ducks-1666 | a long straight canal in St James's Park in London in the 1660s with ducks and two pelicans on the water, rows of young trees on both sides, a few strollers, soft morning light
HEAD: In 1664, the Russian ambassador gave King Charles a pair of pelicans for St James's Park.
BODY: St James's Park lay beside the royal palaces. After 1660, Charles the Second had it redesigned. A long straight canal was dug down the middle. Rows of trees were planted along it. The king loved the park. He walked there almost every day, often very fast, so that his courtiers struggled to keep up. He liked to feed the ducks. He also kept many birds and animals there. In 1664, the Russian ambassador gave the king a pair of pelicans. They lived on the water among the ducks. Visitors came to stare at these strange birds with their great beaks. Even so, the park was open to many ordinary people as well. Londoners strolled there and hoped to see the king. Today, pelicans still live in St James's Park. They are descendants of later gifts, but they continue a tradition that began more than three hundred and fifty years ago.
===
IMG: pall-mall-game-long-alley-mallet-ball | a gentleman in a wig and long coat swinging a long wooden mallet at a wooden ball on a long straight sandy alley lined with trees in London in the 1660s, onlookers watching, soft afternoon light
HEAD: The king played a game called pall mall, which gave its name to a famous London street.
BODY: One of the king's favourite games was called pall mall. It came from France and Italy. Players used a long wooden mallet to hit a wooden ball along a straight alley. The aim was to send the ball through a hoop at the end in as few strokes as possible. It was a little like golf and croquet combined. The alley needed to be long and smooth. A special one was laid out near St James's Park. Its surface was made of crushed shells, which helped the ball roll well. Pepys watched the king and his brother play. He also noted with interest how the alley was kept smooth. Even so, the game was not new in 1660. It had been played in England for some decades. Yet its name lived on. The street that ran beside the old alley is still called Pall Mall. The nearby street called The Mall also takes its name from the game.

HEAD: Pepys saw people skating on the frozen canal in St James's Park, a sport new to England.
BODY: Winters in the 1600s were often colder than today. Historians call this period the Little Ice Age. Sometimes the Thames itself froze over. In December 1662, Pepys walked in St James's Park on a cold day. He saw people sliding on the ice of the new canal with skates. He wrote that it was a very pretty art. Skating had been popular in the Netherlands for a long time. Many courtiers had learned it during their exile there. They brought the sport back with them. John Evelyn also saw the skaters that same week. He described them sliding along with great speed. Even so, skating was still a novelty for most English people. Yet it soon caught on. On cold winters, Londoners gathered on frozen ponds and canals to skate, slide and play. A few years later, in 1683 and 1684, the Thames froze so hard that a whole fair was held on the ice.

IMG: bartholomew-fair-smithfield-booths-puppets | colourful booths, puppet shows and crowds at a summer fair in an open space in London in the 1660s, banners and awnings, children watching a puppet stage, warm afternoon sunshine
HEAD: Every August, Bartholomew Fair filled Smithfield with booths, puppet shows and crowds.
BODY: Bartholomew Fair was one of the great events of the London year. It was held every August in Smithfield, just outside the city wall. It had begun in the Middle Ages as a cloth fair. By the 1600s, it was mostly about entertainment. There were rows of booths selling toys, gingerbread, ribbons and roast pork. There were puppet shows, rope dancers and performing animals. There were people showing strange curiosities, like giants and dwarfs and unusual animals. Pepys went to the fair several times. He enjoyed the puppet plays very much. Once he saw a horse that could count money. People of every rank mixed in the crowds. Even so, the fair had a bad reputation too. Pickpockets and tricksters were common. The authorities tried many times to control or shorten it. Yet it continued for centuries. It finally ended in the 1850s, after more than seven hundred years.

HEAD: Going to church on Sunday was expected of everyone, and sermons could last an hour or more.
BODY: Sunday in 1666 was a day for church. By law, people were expected to attend their parish church. Most did. They dressed in their best clothes and sat in wooden pews. Wealthy families often had their own boxed pews. The service included prayers, psalms and readings. The sermon was the centre of it. A good preacher could hold the attention of the congregation for an hour or more. Pepys went to church most Sundays. He commented on every sermon in his diary. Some he admired. Others he found dull. More than once, he admitted that he fell asleep. He also confessed that he spent some services looking at the pretty women in the congregation. Even so, religion mattered deeply to people of the time. Sunday was also a day of rest from work. In the afternoon, many families walked in the fields or visited friends. It was the quietest day of the week in the busy city.

HEAD: On fine days, Londoners walked out to the villages of Islington and Hackney for cakes and ale.
BODY: Just beyond the edge of London lay open countryside. A short walk north brought people to fields, farms and little villages. Islington was one of the most popular. It was known for its dairies, its inns and its fresh air. Pepys and his wife often walked or rode there on summer afternoons. They visited an old inn where they ate cheesecakes and drank ale. Other Londoners went to Hackney, Hampstead or across the river to the fields of Southwark. Families picnicked, children played, and young couples courted. Archers practised in the fields north of the city. The walk back in the evening was part of the pleasure. Even so, these villages were changing. London was growing quickly, and houses spread steadily into the fields. Today, Islington and Hackney are busy parts of the city. Yet for Londoners of 1666, they were a peaceful escape. A few miles on foot took them from the smoky streets into the green.

IMG: hyde-park-carriages-tour-circle-evening | elegant horse-drawn carriages driving slowly in a circle around a grassy area with trees in Hyde Park in the 1660s, ladies and gentlemen in fine clothes looking out, golden evening light
HEAD: Fashionable Londoners drove their carriages in slow circles around Hyde Park to be seen.
BODY: Hyde Park lay to the west of the city. It had been a royal hunting ground. In the 1600s, it was opened to the public. After the king returned, it became a fashionable place. Wealthy people drove their carriages around a circular track called the Ring, or the Tour. They went round and round slowly, greeting friends and looking at each other's clothes and horses. It was the place to see and be seen. Pepys longed to have his own carriage. For years, he could only watch, or hire a coach. Finally, in 1668, he bought one. He was very proud of it. He drove to the park with his wife and enjoyed being among the fashionable people. Even so, he worried about what others would think. Some might say he was showing off. Yet the habit of the evening drive continued for centuries. Hyde Park remained the favourite promenade of fashionable London for a very long time.
