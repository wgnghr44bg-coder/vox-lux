TOPIC: Pudding Lane, Sunday 2 September 1666
===
IMG: pudding-lane-bakery-night-1666-oven-glow | a small timber bakery in a narrow London lane at night in 1666, the warm glow of a bread oven through a low doorway, overhanging upper floors, quiet and still, soft orange light
HEAD: The Great Fire of London began in a bakery in Pudding Lane.
BODY: The fire started in a small shop near the river. It belonged to a baker named Thomas Farriner. His bakery stood in Pudding Lane, a narrow street close to London Bridge. Farriner was not an ordinary baker. He supplied ship's biscuits to the Royal Navy. On the evening of Saturday the first of September, he finished his work and went to bed. He later swore that he had put out his oven. Yet some time after midnight, the house filled with smoke. The family woke and found the ground floor on fire. Farriner, his daughter and a servant escaped through an upper window and across the roofs. A maid in the house did not get out, and she died. Historians still do not know exactly how the fire began. A spark from the oven is the most likely cause. Whatever started it, that small blaze in Pudding Lane became one of the most famous fires in history.
===
IMG: lord-mayor-woken-night-candle-1666 | a man in a nightshirt holding a candle at the door of a London house at night in 1666, a servant pointing down the street, dark timber houses, faint orange light in the distance
HEAD: The Lord Mayor of London thought the fire was nothing to worry about.
BODY: In the early hours of the morning, the Lord Mayor was woken and told about the fire. His name was Sir Thomas Bloodworth. He came to look at the burning houses. At that point, only a few buildings were alight. Fires were common in London, and most were put out quickly. Bloodworth was not impressed. According to a famous account, he said that a woman could put it out on her own. Then he went back to bed. Officials could have pulled down the houses around the fire to stop it spreading. That was the usual method. But the owners would have to be paid, and Bloodworth hesitated. Even so, it is easy to blame him with hindsight. No one could know that this fire would be different. Yet his slow reaction cost the city precious hours. By the time the sun rose, the fire had already reached the warehouses along the river.
===
IMG: thames-street-warehouses-river-wharf-1666 | old timber warehouses on a narrow street beside the River Thames in London in 1666, barrels and crates stacked outside, wooden wharves and small boats, early morning light
HEAD: The fire quickly reached warehouses full of oil, tallow, coal and timber.
BODY: Pudding Lane ran down towards Thames Street, close to the river. Along Thames Street stood warehouses and cellars packed with goods. There was oil, tar, pitch and tallow for candles. There were stacks of timber, coal and hemp for ropes. There was brandy and wine. These were almost perfect fuel for a fire. Once the flames reached them, the fire grew far too strong to stop with buckets. The wharves along the river also burned. This was a serious problem. The river was the main source of water for fighting fires. With the waterfront in flames, it became very hard to reach it. The fire also destroyed the waterwheels under London Bridge. Those wheels had pumped river water into pipes for part of the city. Even so, many people still did not panic. On Sunday morning, most Londoners thought the fire would soon be under control.
===
IMG: east-wind-sparks-flying-over-rooftops-dusk | sparks and glowing embers carried by a strong wind over dark timber rooftops of old London in 1666, the sky turning orange at dusk, seen from a distance, calm and quiet
HEAD: A strong east wind and a long dry summer helped the fire spread.
BODY: The summer of 1666 had been unusually hot and dry. For weeks there had been very little rain. The timber houses of London were dry through and through. Then, in early September, a strong wind began to blow from the east. It carried sparks and burning pieces of wood over the rooftops. New fires started far from the main blaze. Houses in London were built very close together. Many upper floors leaned out over the street, so that the tops of houses on opposite sides almost touched. Fire could jump easily from one side to the other. The wind kept blowing for three days. Even so, the city had been warned many times. Laws already said that new houses should be built of brick or stone. Yet these rules were often ignored, because timber was cheaper. In September 1666, the price of that cheapness became clear.
===
IMG: samuel-pepys-window-watching-fire-night | a man in a long nightgown and cap looking out of an upstairs window in a London house at night in 1666, a distant orange glow on the horizon reflected in the glass, candlelight in the room
HEAD: Samuel Pepys was woken at three in the morning to look at the fire.
BODY: Much of what we know about the fire comes from a diary. It was written by Samuel Pepys, an official at the Navy Office. In the early hours of Sunday, his maid Jane woke him. She had seen the fire from a window and was worried. Pepys got up, put on his nightgown and looked out. He thought the fire was far enough away. So he went back to bed. A few hours later, he rose again. Jane told him that some three hundred houses had burned during the night. Pepys walked to the Tower of London and climbed up to get a better view. From there he saw a great fire around the end of London Bridge. He took a boat on the river to look more closely. Even so, at first he wrote calmly. Like many Londoners, he did not yet understand what was happening. His diary records how that calm slowly turned to fear.
===
IMG: people-carrying-goods-to-river-boats-1666 | Londoners in 17th-century clothes carrying chests and bundles down to small boats on the Thames in 1666, people loading belongings, a soft orange glow in the hazy sky
HEAD: Many Londoners loaded their belongings into boats on the Thames.
BODY: On Sunday, Pepys watched people trying to save their things. Some threw their goods into lighters and small boats on the river. Others carried them from house to house, moving them again and again as the fire came closer. The poor stayed in their homes until the flames almost touched them. Then they ran to the boats or climbed down the stairs to the water. Pepys noticed something touching. The pigeons did not want to leave their houses. They flew around the windows and balconies until some of them burned their wings and fell. The river became crowded with boats full of furniture, bedding and boxes. Watermen charged high prices to carry goods away. Even so, the river was a place of safety. Fire could not cross water easily. For many families, a small boat on the Thames held everything they still owned.
===
IMG: pepys-boat-river-whitehall-palace-1666 | a small rowing boat on the River Thames in 1666 heading towards a large old palace with many windows by the river, a gentleman in a dark coat sitting in the boat, hazy light
HEAD: Pepys went by boat to Whitehall to tell the king about the fire.
BODY: Later that Sunday morning, Pepys made an important decision. He took a boat up the river to Whitehall Palace, where the king lived. There he told King Charles the Second and his brother James, the Duke of York, what he had seen. He told them that unless houses were pulled down, nothing could stop the fire. The king listened. He ordered Pepys to go to the Lord Mayor with a message. The Lord Mayor should pull down houses in every direction. Pepys found Bloodworth in the street, looking exhausted, with a handkerchief round his neck. The Mayor said that people would not obey him. He said he had been pulling down houses, but the fire was faster. Even so, the royal order gave the work new authority. Yet it was already very late. By Sunday evening, the fire stretched in a great arc along the river.

TOPIC: Four days of fire
===
IMG: fire-arc-across-city-seen-from-bankside-night | the old city of London seen from across the River Thames at night in 1666, a long orange glow on the far bank under a dark sky, small boats on the calm water in the foreground
HEAD: Pepys described the fire at night as a great arch of flame, a mile long.
BODY: On Sunday night, Pepys and his wife watched the fire from a small alehouse on the south bank of the river. As darkness fell, the fire looked bigger and more frightening. Pepys described it as an arch of fire, stretching from one side of the city to the other. He guessed it was about a mile long. He could hear the cracking of houses as they fell. He wrote that it made him weep to see it. From the south bank, the whole city seemed to glow. Smoke rose high into the sky and was carried away by the wind. Even so, few people died in the fire itself. Most had time to escape. The fire moved quickly, but people could see it coming. Yet the loss of homes was enormous. Families watched from the other side of the river as their streets disappeared.
===
IMG: house-being-pulled-down-firehook-crowd-1666 | men in 17th-century clothes using long iron hooks and ropes to pull down a timber house on a London street in 1666, dust rising, a crowd watching, hazy daylight
HEAD: To stop the fire, men tried to pull down houses with long iron hooks.
BODY: London had no fire brigade in 1666. Each parish kept some simple equipment. There were leather buckets, ladders and long poles with large iron hooks at the end. These firehooks were used to pull down houses. The idea was to create a gap that the fire could not cross. Men climbed up, fixed the hooks and pulled with ropes until walls and roofs fell. There were also a few hand-pumped fire engines. They were small and hard to move through the narrow streets. Some of them fell into the river or were lost in the flames. Even so, pulling down houses was the right idea. The problem was speed. The wind blew the fire across the gaps before they were wide enough. Yet the firefighters kept working day and night. Many of them were ordinary citizens, soldiers and sailors who simply joined in.
===
IMG: pepys-burying-cheese-wine-garden-night-1666 | a man in a dark coat digging a hole in a small garden behind a London house at night in 1666, a wheel of cheese and wine bottles beside him, a lantern on the ground, distant orange glow
HEAD: Pepys buried his wine and his Parmesan cheese in his garden.
BODY: By Tuesday the fourth of September, the fire was getting close to Pepys's own house. He lived in Seething Lane, near the Tower of London. He sent most of his valuable things away by cart. Then he did something that many people still remember. With a colleague, Sir William Penn, he dug a pit in the garden. In it they put their wine. Pepys also buried his Parmesan cheese and some other things. Parmesan was an expensive Italian cheese, and he clearly valued it. He also buried official papers from the Navy Office. It sounds amusing today. Yet it was a sensible thing to do. A hole in the earth was one of the safest places during a fire. Gold, silver and papers were often buried in the same way. In the end, Pepys's house survived. Historians still smile at the picture of an important official, digging by lantern light to save his cheese.
===
IMG: old-st-pauls-cathedral-nave-books-stored-1666 | the long stone nave of Old St Paul's Cathedral in London in 1666, an indoor scene, enclosed by stone walls, a high vaulted ceiling, piles of books and bundles stored along the walls, dim light through tall windows
HEAD: The booksellers of London stored their books in St Paul's Cathedral for safety.
BODY: The area around St Paul's Cathedral was the centre of the book trade. Booksellers and printers had their shops in the churchyard. When the fire came closer, they thought of a safe place for their stock. They carried thousands of books down into the crypt of the cathedral. The crypt was underground, with thick stone walls. Surely the fire could not reach it there. Other people also stored their goods inside the great church. The cathedral seemed solid and safe. It was built of stone and had stood for centuries. Yet there was a problem. The cathedral had wooden scaffolding around it, because repairs were under way. Its huge roof was covered with lead, laid on timber. On Tuesday evening, the fire reached St Paul's. The scaffolding caught first. Then the roof began to burn. The booksellers lost almost everything. Their loss was one of the great cultural disasters of the fire.
===
IMG: st-pauls-burning-roof-glow-night-distance | the long silhouette of Old St Paul's Cathedral with its square central tower seen from far away at night in 1666, a deep orange glow behind it, a dark sky, calm water in the foreground
HEAD: Melted lead from the roof of St Paul's ran through the streets like a river.
BODY: The burning of St Paul's was the most dramatic moment of the fire. The roof timbers caught alight and burned fiercely. The lead that covered the roof began to melt. It poured down from the building. Witnesses said it flowed through the streets around the cathedral. Stones cracked and burst in the heat. Parts of the vaulted roof fell into the nave. The writer John Evelyn walked through the ruins a few days later. He described the stones as calcined, burned almost to powder, and the ground still too hot to walk on. He saw that the great church was a ruin. The books in the crypt had burned for days. Even so, some parts of the walls were still standing. At first, people hoped the old cathedral could be repaired. Yet the damage was too great. A completely new cathedral would one day rise in its place.
===
IMG: king-charles-passing-buckets-fire-line-1666 | a tall man with a dark curly wig and a fine coat helping a line of men pass leather buckets of water on a London street in 1666, smoke in the air, hazy orange daylight
HEAD: King Charles the Second and his brother helped fight the fire themselves.
BODY: The king did not stay safely in his palace. Charles the Second and his brother James, the Duke of York, came into the city to help. James was put in charge of the firefighting. He rode from place to place, giving orders and encouraging the men. Soldiers were brought in to help and to keep order. Both brothers were seen working with their own hands. According to several accounts, the king passed buckets of water along a line and stood up to his ankles in mud and water. He also handed out money to the workers to keep them going. People were impressed. Kings did not usually do such things. Even so, the royal brothers also had a practical reason. They feared that the fire might cause disorder or even rebellion. Yet their presence made a real difference to the morale of the city. Many Londoners remembered their courage for years.
===
IMG: gunpowder-firebreak-houses-blown-up-dust | a row of timber houses collapsing in a cloud of dust on a London street in 1666 after a gunpowder blast, workers standing back at a safe distance, hazy afternoon light
HEAD: On the last days of the fire, houses were blown up with gunpowder to make firebreaks.
BODY: Pulling down houses by hand was too slow. So on Tuesday, a new method was tried. Gunpowder was brought from the Tower of London and the navy stores. Houses in the path of the fire were blown up. The explosions brought them down much faster than hooks and ropes. This created wide gaps that the fire could not easily jump. Seamen from the naval dockyards were skilled at this kind of work. Pepys himself suggested bringing men from the dockyards to help. Near the Tower, houses were blown up to protect the fortress, which held a great store of gunpowder. If that store had exploded, the damage would have been terrible. Even so, the method was not without risk. Some people were angry that their houses were destroyed before the fire even reached them. Yet the firebreaks worked. Together with a change in the weather, they finally began to hold the fire back.
===
IMG: wind-dropping-smoke-rising-quiet-dawn-1666 | a quiet grey dawn over the smoking ruins of old London in 1666, thin columns of smoke rising straight up into a still sky, the river calm and grey in front
HEAD: On Tuesday night, the east wind finally dropped.
BODY: For three days, the wind had driven the fire westward. Then, on the night of Tuesday the fourth of September, the wind began to die down. This was the turning point. Without the wind, the fire no longer jumped across the gaps. The firebreaks made with gunpowder now held. By Wednesday, the firefighters were able to put out many smaller fires. Some flames broke out again in a few places, but they were brought under control. By Thursday the sixth of September, the Great Fire was largely over. The ground, however, stayed hot for a long time. Cellars full of coal and goods smouldered for weeks. In some places, smoke still rose months later. Even so, the city could finally breathe again. Pepys climbed the steeple of a church near his house and looked out. He called it the saddest sight of desolation he had ever seen.
===
IMG: ruins-old-london-walls-chimneys-smoke-morning | the ruins of old London in September 1666, blackened brick chimneys and stone walls standing among ashes, thin smoke rising, the river in the distance, soft grey morning light
HEAD: The fire destroyed about thirteen thousand houses and eighty-seven churches.
BODY: When the fire was over, the people of London counted the damage. It was enormous. Around thirteen thousand two hundred houses had burned. Eighty-seven parish churches were destroyed, along with St Paul's Cathedral. The Guildhall, the Royal Exchange and many company halls were gone. Most of the old walled city, the part known as the City of London, was in ruins. Around seventy thousand people had lost their homes. That was most of the people who lived inside the walls. Yet the official number of deaths was very small. Only a handful of deaths were recorded. Some historians think the real number was higher, because the deaths of poor people were not always written down. Even so, there is no proof of thousands dying. The fire was above all a disaster of homes and property. A whole old city had vanished in four days.

TOPIC: The old city before the fire
===
IMG: london-wall-gate-ludgate-timber-houses-1666 | an old stone city gate in the medieval wall of London in 1666 with timber houses crowded on both sides, carts and walkers passing through, soft late afternoon light
HEAD: In 1666, the old City of London was still surrounded by a Roman and medieval wall.
BODY: Before the fire, London was a city of two parts. At its heart was the old City of London. It was still surrounded by a wall, first built by the Romans and later repaired many times. Gates in the wall had names that are still known today, like Ludgate, Newgate, Aldgate, Bishopsgate and Moorgate. Inside the wall, about eighty thousand people lived in crowded streets. Outside the wall, new suburbs had grown in every direction. To the west lay Westminster, where the king, Parliament and the great lords lived. Across the river lay Southwark. Altogether, greater London had perhaps four or five hundred thousand people. It was one of the largest cities in Europe. Even so, the walls no longer had much military purpose. They were mostly a boundary and a place to collect tolls. Yet they still shaped the city, and the fire stopped not far from them in several places.
===
IMG: jettied-timber-houses-narrow-street-overhanging | a narrow street in London in 1666 with tall timber-framed houses whose upper floors lean out over the street, almost touching at the top, hanging shop signs, soft light filtering down
HEAD: The upper floors of many London houses leaned out over the street.
BODY: Many houses in old London were built with timber frames. The spaces between the beams were filled with lath and plaster. To gain more space, builders made each upper floor stick out further than the one below. This was called jettying. In narrow streets, the top floors on opposite sides came very close together. Sometimes neighbours could almost shake hands across the street from their upper windows. The streets below were dark, even at midday. The houses were often four or five floors high. Shops were on the ground floor, and families lived above. Even so, these buildings had a certain charm. Their carved beams and painted plaster were admired by some visitors. Yet they were also dangerous. Wood and plaster burned easily, and the narrow gaps let fire leap across streets. After 1666, new laws banned this way of building in the City.
===
IMG: hanging-shop-signs-cheapside-street-1666 | a busy London street in 1666 with many colourful painted wooden shop signs hanging above the doors, a bell, a swan, a golden key, people walking below, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Houses in London had no street numbers, so people used painted signs.
BODY: In 1666, there were no numbers on London's houses. Instead, shops and many houses had signs. They hung out over the street on iron brackets. Each sign showed a picture, because many people could not read well. There were golden keys, black swans, red lions, bells, anchors and Bibles. An address might be given as "at the sign of the Golden Ball, in Cheapside". People found their way by these pictures. Some signs became very large and heavy. They creaked and swung in the wind. Occasionally one fell and hurt someone below. Even so, the signs made the streets colourful and lively. They also became part of the identity of each business. Many London inns and pubs still carry names that come from these old signs. Yet the system was confusing for strangers. In the 1760s, a law ordered most signs to be taken down, and numbers slowly replaced them.
===
IMG: water-carrier-wooden-tankard-conduit-street | a water carrier in 17th-century clothes with a tall wooden water container on his back standing by a stone conduit fountain on a London street in 1666, morning light
HEAD: Many Londoners bought their water from water carriers who filled tall wooden containers at public conduits.
BODY: Getting water was part of daily life in old London. Some came from wells and pumps. Some came from public conduits, stone fountains fed by pipes from springs outside the city. Water carriers filled tall wooden containers at the conduits. They carried them on their shoulders to houses and sold the water by the load. There were also newer systems. From 1582, waterwheels under London Bridge pumped river water into pipes. In 1613, a long channel called the New River was opened. It brought clean water from springs in Hertfordshire, about forty miles away. Wooden pipes carried it into the houses of people who could pay. Even so, many poor people still relied on the river and on shallow wells. Water from these sources was often dirty. Yet the New River was a remarkable achievement for its time. Parts of it still supply London with water today.
===
IMG: sea-coal-smoke-chimneys-over-roofs-1666 | many chimneys on the roofs of old London in 1666 sending up grey coal smoke into a hazy sky, church towers in the distance, soft muted afternoon light
HEAD: London was already famous for its smoky air in the 1660s.
BODY: Londoners burned a great deal of coal. It came by ship from Newcastle in the north and was called sea coal. Coal was cheaper than wood, and it burned hot. It was used in homes, bakeries, breweries and workshops. The result was smoke. On still days, a grey haze hung over the city. It blackened walls, stained clothes and made people cough. In 1661, the writer John Evelyn wrote a small book about it. He called it Fumifugium. In it he complained to the king about the smoke of London. He suggested moving smoky industries out of the city and planting sweet-smelling gardens around it. His ideas were not carried out. Even so, his little book is remembered as one of the first writings about air pollution. London's smoke would become even worse over the next two centuries. Yet Evelyn's complaint shows that people already understood the problem long ago.
===
IMG: night-lantern-hanging-outside-door-street | a lantern with a candle hanging outside the door of a timber house on a dark London street in 1666, its soft light falling on the cobbles, a quiet night
HEAD: Householders were required to hang lanterns outside their doors on dark nights.
BODY: There were no street lamps in London in 1666. At night, the streets were very dark. To help a little, the city had rules. Householders on main streets were supposed to hang a lantern with a candle outside their doors on dark evenings. This was usually required during the winter months and on nights without a moon. The lanterns gave only a small pool of light. People going out at night often hired a link boy. He was a boy who carried a burning torch and walked ahead to light the way. Watchmen also patrolled with lanterns and called out the hours. Even so, the dark streets could be unsafe. Many people stayed indoors after nightfall. Pepys mentions walking home with a link boy several times in his diary. Yet the little lights at doorways gave the city a quiet, warm look on winter nights.
===
IMG: river-thames-watermen-wherries-steps-1666 | small wooden rowing boats called wherries waiting at stone river steps on the Thames in London in 1666, watermen in caps calling for passengers, the old city behind, morning haze
HEAD: The fastest way across London was often by boat on the Thames.
BODY: The streets of London were narrow and crowded. So the river was the city's main road. Thousands of watermen worked on the Thames. They rowed small boats called wherries. People waited at stone steps along the riverbanks and called for a boat. A waterman would row them up or down the river, or across to the other side. The fares were set by law. Pepys used boats almost every day. He travelled to Whitehall, to the dockyards and to the theatres across the river. Watermen were known for their strong arms and their rough language. They shouted to attract passengers and argued with each other over customers. Even so, travel by river was usually pleasant. It was faster than walking and cleaner than the muddy streets. On a calm evening, the Thames was full of small boats gliding between the lights of the city.
