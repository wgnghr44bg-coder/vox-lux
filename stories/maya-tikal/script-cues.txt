Tonight we go nearly thirteen hundred years back. We go to the great Maya city of Tikal, deep in the rainforest of Central America. The year is 750.

[long-pause]

Above an endless green sea of trees, steep pyramids rise into the morning mist. They are white and red. On top of each one stands a small temple. Smoke of incense drifts from the doorways. Below them lie wide white squares and palaces and ball courts. Thousands of houses stand among gardens and fields. And all around is the forest, full of the calls of monkeys and parrots.

[pause]

It is one of the greatest cities of the ancient Americas. It is at the height of its glory. But it stands in a hard place, and everything depends on one thing. The rain. Tonight we will spend a little time there. We will meet its kings and its writers, its farmers and its potters. And we will meet the forest that surrounds it all.

[pause]

If you enjoy slow journeys into faraway times, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, so that the next one is easy to find.

[long-pause]

{img:peten-rainforest-green-canopy-endless-lakes-low-hills-aerial}
Tikal stands in what is now the north of Guatemala. The land there is flat and low. Under it lies soft white stone called limestone. Thick forest covers it for hundreds of kilometres.

[pause]

It is hot and damp all year. From about May to December it is the rainy season. Big thunderstorms roll across the forest almost every afternoon. The warm rain pours down and the forest steams. From January to April is the dry season. Then it hardly rains at all. The ground cracks, and the water holes dry up.

[long-pause]

There are no rivers near Tikal, and no lakes. When rain falls, it sinks quickly into the stone ground and is gone. So the people must catch the rain and keep it. They store it in big ponds, to last through the dry months. If the ponds run dry, the city is in trouble.

[pause]

And yet, in this hard place, the Maya have built a great city. They have no metal tools and no wheels. They have no horses or oxen to carry loads. Still, perhaps sixty thousand people live here, or even more. And some of their buildings are among the tallest ever built in the Americas, before modern times.

[pause]

{img:maya-city-plaza-white-plaster-temples-people-market-morning}
They have writing and books. They have mathematics and they study the stars. Their calendar is more exact than the one used in Europe at the time. They have art and music and great kings and a long, proud history.

[pause]

Who are they?

[long-pause]

{img:maya-region-map-like-landscape-mountains-lowlands-cities-temples}
The Maya are a people of Central America. They have lived in the same lands for more than three thousand years. Today these lands are parts of Mexico, Guatemala, Belize, Honduras and El Salvador.

[pause]

They are not one empire with one king. They are many peoples, speaking languages that are related. They live in many cities. Each city has its own king, its own gods and its own history. It is a bit like the cities of ancient Greece. They trade with each other and marry each other. They visit each other's festivals. And sometimes they fight each other, for power and glory.

[long-pause]

In the year 750 there are dozens of great Maya cities. And there are hundreds of smaller ones, spread across the forests. There is Tikal. There is Calakmul, its great rival, to the north. There is Palenque in the west, with its beautiful palace. There is Copán in the south, with its wonderful carved stones. And there are many more.

[pause]

Archaeologists call the years from about 250 to about 900 the Classic period. It is the great age of the Maya cities. In these years they made their most beautiful temples and palaces and carvings and books.

[pause]

{img:maya-people-gathering-plaza-festival-feathers-cloth-colourful-crowd}
Tikal is one of the greatest of them all. People have lived here for more than fifteen hundred years. It began as a small village of farmers. By the year 750 it is a huge city. It is the capital of a strong kingdom. The Maya call this kingdom Mutul.

[long-pause]

The Maya call their king the holy lord. The king is a sacred person. He stands between the people and the gods. He leads the great ceremonies that keep the world in order. People believe these ceremonies make the rain fall and the corn grow. So if the rain does not come, people may start to wonder about their king.

[pause]

The Maya wrote down the names of their kings. They wrote when they were born and when they became king. They wrote about their wars and their ceremonies. They carved all this on tall stones that stand in the city squares. These stones are called stelae.

[pause]

{img:carved-stela-maya-king-portrait-glyphs-plaza-tikal-sunlight}
For a long time, nobody could read them. But in the last fifty years, scholars have learned to read most of the Maya writing. So now the stones of Tikal speak again. They tell us the names and stories of people who lived here, nearly thirteen hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:great-plaza-tikal-temple-i-temple-ii-facing-north-acropolis-sunlight}
Let us walk into the heart of Tikal, on a morning in the year 750.

[pause]

At the centre of the city lies the Great Plaza. It is a wide open square, covered with smooth white plaster. It shines so brightly in the sun that it hurts your eyes. On the east and the west side stand two great pyramids. They face each other. Each one is steep and tall, with a stairway up the front to a small temple on top.

[long-pause]

On the east side stands the Temple of the Great Jaguar. Archaeologists call it Temple One. It has nine steep levels and rises forty-seven metres high. On top is a small temple with three narrow rooms. Above that rises a tall carved wall called a roof comb. It shows a king sitting on his throne. It makes the temple look even taller. The temple was built about twenty years ago, over the grave of a great king.

[pause]

On the west side, facing it, stands Temple Two. It is a little smaller. It may have been built for that king's queen.

[pause]

{img:temple-i-great-jaguar-steep-stairway-roof-comb-red-painted}
On the north side of the plaza is a big raised platform with many small temples. They were built one on top of another, over more than a thousand years. The old kings of Tikal are buried there. On the south side is a large group of palaces. The royal family and the nobles live there. And the king holds his court there.

[long-pause]

In front of the northern temples stands a long row of carved stones. Each one shows a king in his rich clothes. The signs on it tell his name and his deeds and the dates. In front of each stone lies a round altar stone.

[pause]

Today the buildings look grey, because they are ruins. But in the year 750 they are covered with smooth plaster and painted. Most of them are a deep rich red. There are details in blue and yellow and green. The whole city glows red and white in the sun. Around it is the green of the forest, and above it the blue sky.

[pause]

{img:central-acropolis-palace-courtyards-rooms-nobles-red-plaster}
From the Great Plaza, wide raised roads run out across the city. They are made of stone and covered with white plaster. The Maya call them white roads. They lead to other groups of temples and palaces, and to the big water ponds. Along them stand more temples, ball courts, markets and the houses of nobles.

[long-pause]

All around the centre, for many kilometres, there are the houses of ordinary people. They stand among the trees and gardens and fields. Each family has a few small houses around a little yard. So the city is not packed tight like a city in Europe. It is spread out, like one big garden in the forest.

[pause]

Today, scientists fly over the forest with a special machine. It sends down beams of laser light that pass between the leaves to the ground. It is called LiDAR. With it they found that Tikal was much bigger than anyone thought. Thousands of houses and fields and roads lie hidden under the trees.

[long-pause]

{img:central-acropolis-throne-room-king-seated-bench-jaguar-cushion-courtiers}
Let us go into the palace, on the south side of the Great Plaza.

[pause]

It is not one big building like a palace in Europe. It is a maze of long low buildings with many rooms. They stand around six courtyards at different levels. Stairways and narrow passages join them. Some buildings have two floors, and some have three.

[long-pause]

The rooms are long and narrow. The walls are thick. The ceilings are high and pointed. The Maya make them by stacking stones so each layer sticks out a little more than the one below. At the top, the two sides almost meet. A flat stone closes the gap. The rooms are cool and dim and quiet, even on the hottest days.

[pause]

In many rooms there are wide stone benches along the walls. They are covered with mats and cushions and jaguar skins. People sit and sleep on them. Painted cotton curtains hang in the doorways, to keep out the light.

[pause]

{img:palace-courtyard-nobles-sitting-mats-servants-chocolate-cups-shade}
In the throne room, the king sits on a wide stone bench covered with a jaguar skin. Behind him is a big cushion, also of jaguar skin. A curtain hangs in front. It can be pulled back to show him to his visitors.

[long-pause]

The nobles sit before him on mats, with their legs crossed. They wear rich clothes and jewels and feathered headdresses. Visitors come from other cities. They bow and lay down their gifts. Bundles of cloth, cacao beans, green jade stones and shining green feathers. Writers sit with their books and brushes. They write down every gift and every word. Servants come and go with food and cups of chocolate.

[pause]

There are musicians and dancers and jesters. There are the king's dogs, and pet parrots.

[long-pause]

In the quieter parts of the palace lives the royal family. The king's wives and his mother. His children, and his brothers and sisters. They have their own rooms and courtyards and kitchens and servants.

[pause]

The young princes learn from the writers and the priests. They learn to read and write the Maya signs. They learn to count the days and watch the stars. They learn to dance and speak well, and to act as a king should. The princesses learn too. They learn to weave the finest cloth and to take part in the ceremonies. Some of them learn to read.

[pause]

{img:palace-wall-graffiti-scratched-drawings-temple-figures-plaster}
On some of the palace walls, archaeologists have found little scratched drawings. People living there drew them long ago. There are small pictures of temples and people and processions. There are ball players and animals. Perhaps children drew them. Or bored servants. Or guards, waiting for something to happen.

[long-pause]

They are not great art. But they bring us close to the people who lived in these rooms. Like people everywhere, they scratched a little drawing on the wall while they waited.

[pause]

In the evenings, torches light up the palace courtyards. The smell of smoke and incense and cooking drifts through the passages. And the sound of music and voices and laughter rises into the night.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-builders-quarry-limestone-blocks-stone-tools-workers}
How did the Maya build such great temples? They had no metal tools and no wheels. They had no animals to pull or carry.

[pause]

They used stone and wood and fire and water. And the strength and skill and patience of thousands of people.

[long-pause]

The ground under Tikal is limestone. The builders cut it out of the ground close by. They use tools made of a very hard stone called flint. Freshly cut limestone is soft and easy to work. It slowly hardens in the air. Many of the holes they dug later became water ponds.

[pause]

The workers carry the blocks on their backs. A strap goes across their forehead to help hold the weight. Or they roll the blocks on logs. They lift them with ropes and ramps of earth and wood.

[pause]

{img:lime-kiln-burning-wood-stones-smoke-workers-forest-edge}
The inside of a pyramid is mostly filled with loose stones and earth. Walls hold it in. Then it is covered with a skin of cut stone blocks. And then with plaster.

[long-pause]

The plaster is made by burning limestone. The builders pile up wood and stones and burn them for a day and a night. The stone turns into white powder. They mix it with water and sand. It becomes a smooth white paste that hardens like stone. But it takes a lot of wood. To make enough plaster for one big temple, whole parts of the forest are cut down. Every new temple makes the forest a little smaller.

[pause]

The plaster covers everything. The pyramids and the temples, the squares and the roads and the floors. It is smoothed and polished. And then it is painted, mostly red, with paint made from red earth.

[long-pause]

On top of each pyramid, the builders make the small temple. Above it they raise the tall roof comb. It has small hollow rooms inside to make it lighter. On the front they shape a big plaster figure of the king on his throne, painted in many colours.

[pause]

The builders work for the king, perhaps a few weeks every year. They come in the dry season, when there is less work in the fields. It is a kind of tax. In return they get food. And they take part in the great festivals.

[pause]

{img:architect-scribe-plan-workers-raising-temple-scaffolding-ropes}
There are master builders and plasterers, carvers and painters. Fathers teach the craft to their sons. They plan the buildings very carefully. Many line up with the rising sun and with the stars. And they line up with each other, across the city.

[long-pause]

The Maya often build on top of old buildings. When a king wants a new temple, he does not tear the old one down. He builds the new one over it, like a shell. So inside many pyramids at Tikal there are older pyramids. And inside those, older ones still. Like the layers of an onion.

[pause]

So the temples of Tikal grew layer by layer, for more than a thousand years. They became the great buildings that still rise above the forest today.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-king-jade-jewelry-feather-headdress-throne-palace-court}
By the year 750, kings have ruled Tikal for more than five hundred years. The stones name more than thirty of them, one after another. The first one lived around the year 90.

[pause]

The kings have wonderful names. They speak of gods and animals and the sky. Great Jaguar Paw. Stormy Sky. Curl Snout. Double Bird.

[long-pause]

The greatest king of Tikal, as people remember in 750, is called Jasaw Chan K'awiil. He ruled for more than fifty years.

[pause]

When he became king, Tikal had been through very hard times. For more than a hundred years, its great rival had ruled over it. That rival was Calakmul, the Snake Kingdom, in the north. Tikal's old stones had been broken. Its kings had been made small. People wondered if the city would ever be great again.

[pause]

{img:king-dancing-ceremony-plaza-feathers-incense-nobles-musicians}
Then, in the year 695, Jasaw Chan K'awiil beat Calakmul in a great battle. He brought Tikal back to glory. He rebuilt the city. He built new temples and set up new stones. He held great festivals. And he was a giant. His skeleton shows he was much taller than most Maya of his time.

[long-pause]

He was buried deep inside the Temple of the Great Jaguar. His grave was full of treasures. There were more than seven kilos of green jade, in necklaces and bracelets and ear rings. There were pearls and shells and painted pots. And there were carved bones. One shows the king's journey to the world of the dead. He travels in a canoe, paddled by gods. Some animals ride along with him. An iguana, a monkey, a parrot and a dog.

[pause]

His son became king after him. He built even bigger temples. One of them is Temple Four. It is about seventy metres high and rises far above the forest. For more than a thousand years, nothing taller was built in the Americas.

[pause]

{img:temple-iv-tallest-rising-above-forest-canopy-view-other-temples}
In the year 750, Tikal is at the height of its power. It is rich and splendid. The kings wear headdresses of shining green feathers, and jaguar skins, and jade. They sit on their thrones in the palace. They welcome visitors and gifts from other cities. And they lead the great ceremonies on top of the temples, in clouds of incense. Below them, the whole city gathers in the squares to watch.

[long-pause]

The queens are important too. Some Maya queens even ruled on their own. Kings often married princesses from other cities, to make friends of them. And the queens appear on the carved stones beside their husbands. They wear rich robes and jewels, and they take part in the ceremonies.

[long-pause]

{img:map-like-view-maya-lowlands-many-cities-forest-rivers-painterly}
Tikal is not the only great city in the Maya world.

[pause]

In the year 750 there are dozens of Maya cities all over the forest. Each one has its own king and its own temples and palaces. Each has its own gods and its own history. They speak more or less the same language. They use the same writing and count the same calendar. But they are not one country. Each city is its own small kingdom.

[long-pause]

Only about twenty kilometres north of Tikal lies an old city called Uaxactun. Long ago it was Tikal's rival, until Tikal took it over. Further away there are other cities. Some are friends of Tikal, some are rivals, and some are ruled by Tikal.

[pause]

And far to the north lies Calakmul, the Snake Kingdom. It is Tikal's great rival.

[pause]

{img:calakmul-great-pyramid-rising-from-forest-north-distance}
For two hundred years, Tikal and Calakmul were the two great powers of the Maya world. Each had its own friends and its own smaller cities under it. They fought over power and trade and glory.

[long-pause]

Calakmul beat Tikal long ago. For more than a hundred years, Tikal stayed down. Then Tikal beat Calakmul and rose again. Now, in 750, Calakmul is weaker and Tikal is strong. But nobody in Tikal forgets that the wheel can turn again.

[pause]

Far to the south lies Copán, in a green valley by a river. It is famous for its carvings. It has a great stairway with more than two thousand signs carved on its steps. They tell the story of its kings. The stones say Copán was founded by a man who may have come from Tikal.

[pause]

{img:palenque-palace-tower-mist-green-hills-temples}
And far to the west lies Palenque, in green hills above the plains. It has beautiful temples and a palace with a tall tower. Its greatest king ruled for sixty-eight years. He was buried in a great stone coffin, deep inside his temple.

[long-pause]

The kings of these cities visit each other. They send gifts and marry each other's daughters. They make friends. And sometimes they quarrel and go to war.

[pause]

Maya wars are mostly small. Warriors from two cities meet with spears and clubs and shields. They wear thick padded cotton for armour. The goal is not to destroy the other city. The goal is to capture its nobles, or even its king. Then the other city must pay. We will leave the battles there, far off in the past, as the stones do, with only a few short words.

[long-pause]

And the ordinary people of all these cities go on with their lives. The farmers and weavers and potters and traders. They grow their corn and raise their children in the forest, whatever the kings are doing.

[long-pause]

{img:rainforest-ceiba-tree-giant-buttress-roots-vines-light-shafts}
All around Tikal is the forest.

[pause]

It is a tropical rainforest. It is tall and thick and green and full of life. The great trees rise thirty, forty, even fifty metres high. Their tops spread out and make a green roof far above. It is so thick that the forest floor below stays dim and green and cool, even at midday. Only a few rays of sunlight fall through onto the ferns.

[long-pause]

The greatest tree of the forest is the ceiba. It has a tall smooth grey trunk. Around its base, big roots stand up like walls. At the very top, its branches spread out like an umbrella. For the Maya, the ceiba is the holy tree, the tree of the world. Its roots reach down to the world below. Its branches reach up to the heavens. It is still the national tree of Guatemala today.

[pause]

There are many other trees too. There are mahogany trees and cedars. There is a tree whose sap is used for chewing gum. There is a tree whose seeds can be eaten when food runs short. There are wild avocados. And there is the copal tree. Its sticky sap is burned as incense in all the temples.

[pause]

{img:howler-monkeys-tree-canopy-dawn-calling-mist-tikal-forest}
And the forest is full of animals.

[long-pause]

At dawn and at evening, the howler monkeys roar from the treetops. It is a deep, loud, booming sound. You can hear it from kilometres away. If you have never heard it before, it sounds like a monster. Spider monkeys swing through the branches with their long arms and tails. There are toucans with huge colourful beaks. There are green parrots flying in pairs and red macaws. There are wild turkeys whose feathers shine blue and green and bronze.

[pause]

There are coatis, small animals with long noses and striped tails held high. They wander through the forest in groups, looking for fruit and insects. There are deer and wild pigs and anteaters.

[pause]

{img:jaguar-resting-forest-branch-spotted-coat-dappled-light-calm}
And there is the jaguar. It is the great spotted cat of the forest, the strongest animal in the Americas. It moves silently and is rarely seen. For the Maya, the jaguar is the king of the forest and the lord of the night. The kings wear its skin and sit on thrones covered with it. Some kings even take its name.

[long-pause]

At night, the forest is full of sounds. Frogs and crickets sing and click and buzz. Owls call. Fireflies flicker among the trees. And the rain drips from the leaves.

[pause]

For the people of Tikal, the forest gives everything. Wood and food and medicine. Colours for cloth, and animals to hunt. But it is also a place of mystery and spirits. It lies at the edge of the human world. And after dark, nobody walks into it alone.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-family-house-thatched-roof-courtyard-dawn-smoke-cooking-fire}
Let us spend a day with an ordinary family of Tikal.

[pause]

They do not live in the centre among the temples. They live out in the countryside, about an hour's walk from the Great Plaza. They have a little group of houses around a small yard. The houses stand on a low platform of earth and stones. That keeps them dry in the rainy season.

[long-pause]

The houses are simple and cool. The walls are wooden poles woven with branches. They are covered with mud and grass and painted white. The roofs are steep and high, made of thick palm leaves. They keep out the rain and the hot sun. And they let the smoke from the fire drift out. Each house has one room and a doorway, but no door and no windows. Inside it is dim and cool. It smells of smoke and earth and corn.

[pause]

There are several houses around the yard. One is for the grandparents. One is for each married son and his family. There is a kitchen house and a store house for corn. Behind them lie gardens and fruit trees and the fields. And behind the fields is the forest.

[pause]

{img:maya-woman-kneeling-grinding-maize-metate-stone-dawn-house}
The day begins in the dark, before dawn. It begins with the sound of the women grinding corn.

[long-pause]

Every morning, every woman of Tikal kneels at her grinding stone. It is a long flat stone on the ground. She holds a second stone in both hands. The corn has soaked all night in water with a little lime. She grinds it back and forth, back and forth, into a soft wet dough. It takes hours every day. It is the sound of every Maya morning, for thousands of years. A soft, steady, scraping sound.

[pause]

Then she shapes the dough into balls and pats them flat. She cooks them on a flat clay plate over the fire. The fire sits between three stones in the middle of the kitchen. Those three stones are holy. They remind people of the three stones the gods set in the sky when they made the world.

[long-pause]

The men and the older boys eat. Then they go out to the fields with their tools and gourds of water. They carry their food wrapped in leaves.

[pause]

The children help. Little girls help their mothers grind the corn and fetch water. They look after the little ones and learn to weave. Little boys go with their fathers. They learn to work in the fields and to hunt. They learn the plants and animals of the forest.

[pause]

{img:maya-women-weaving-backstrap-loom-tree-courtyard-cotton-colours}
In the shade of the yard, the women weave.

[long-pause]

Maya women weave on a simple loom. One end is tied to a tree or a post. The other end is tied to a strap around the weaver's back. When she leans back, the threads get tight. When she leans forward, they get loose. She weaves cotton into cloth. Some is white. Some is dyed red and blue and yellow and purple, with wonderful patterns.

[pause]

Maya women in Guatemala still use the same kind of loom today. Some even weave the same patterns.

[long-pause]

At midday, in the heat, everyone rests in the shade. In the afternoon the men come back from the fields. They wash, sometimes in a little steam bath. Then the family eats the main meal together. There are tamales and flat corn breads, beans and squash with chilli. Sometimes there is a little meat, deer or turkey.

[pause]

{img:maya-family-evening-meal-around-fire-house-tamales-night}
In the evening the sun sets. The howler monkeys roar from the forest. The stars come out. The family sits together around the fire. The old people tell stories. Stories of the gods and how the world was made. Stories of the ancestors and the animals and the spirits of the forest.

[long-pause]

And then they sleep in hammocks or on mats, wrapped in cotton blankets. The house is dim and warm and smoky. And they listen to the sounds of the night. The frogs, the insects, and the rain on the roof.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-children-playing-courtyard-clay-whistles-toy-animals-dog}
Let us think about the children of Tikal.

[pause]

When a Maya baby is born, the family calls a daykeeper. That is a priest who knows the holy calendar. He finds out on which of the two hundred and sixty holy days the baby was born. Each day has its own spirit and its own luck. People believe it will shape the child's life. So the family waits nervously for his words.

[long-pause]

Mothers carry their babies everywhere. They wrap them in a cloth on their back or their hip. Maya mothers still do this today.

[pause]

Some families, especially rich ones, gently shape their babies' heads. For the first months, they bind the head softly between two little boards. Slowly the forehead slopes back, long and smooth, like an ear of corn. It looks like the head of the young corn god. The Maya thought this was very beautiful.

[pause]

{img:mother-carrying-baby-back-cloth-sling-walking-garden-path}
Maya children play.

[long-pause]

Archaeologists have found little clay whistles shaped like animals and birds. Children blew them long ago. They still make a sound today, after more than a thousand years. There are little clay figures that may have been toys. Children played with rubber balls and spinning tops. They played with dogs and pet birds. And they played hide and seek among the houses and the trees, as children do everywhere.

[pause]

But they also work from a young age. By five or six they help in the house and the garden. By twelve or thirteen they are almost grown up.

[long-pause]

Much later, the Spanish wrote about a special day for children of about twelve. The boys and girls gather in a yard with their parents. A priest blesses them and sprinkles them with water. The girls have worn a little shell since they were small. Now their mothers take it off. From that day on, they count as grown up.

[pause]

The children of nobles learn much more. They learn to read and write and count. They learn the calendar and the history of their families. And the sons of kings learn to be kings.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-telling-stories-children-fire-evening-thatched-house}
All the children of Tikal learn the old stories. Stories of the gods and of how the world was made. Stories of why the rabbit has long ears, and why the howler monkey roars. They hear them in the evening by the fire, from their grandparents. And one day they tell them to their own children.

[long-pause]

Some of those stories are still told today in Maya villages in Guatemala. More than twelve hundred years later, they are told to children falling asleep, while the rain falls on the roof.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-cornfield-milpa-maize-beans-squash-forest-edge}
For the Maya, everything begins with corn.

[pause]

Corn is their food, their life and even their god. They eat it every day, at every meal. They eat it as flat breads and tamales, and they drink it too. Nearly three quarters of everything they eat comes from corn.

[long-pause]

And they believe that people were made from corn.

[pause]

There is an old holy book of the Maya called the Popol Vuh. It was written down much later, but it tells very old stories. In it, the gods try to make people several times. First they make them from mud. But the mud people are soft. They fall apart in the water. Then the gods make people from wood. But the wooden people have no hearts. They do not remember who made them. At last the gods make people from yellow and white corn, ground into dough. And these are the true people.

[pause]

The corn god is one of the most loved Maya gods. He is a beautiful young man with a long smooth head, like an ear of corn. His hair is long, like the soft threads of the corn. Every year, like the corn, he dies and is buried in the earth. And then he is born again and grows up and dances, and brings new life to the world.

[long-pause]

The Maya farmer grows his corn in a field cut out of the forest.

[pause]

In the dry season he cuts down the trees and bushes on a piece of land. He lets them dry in the sun. Then, just before the rains come, he burns them. The ash makes the soil rich.

[pause]

When the first rains come in May, he plants the corn. He walks through the field with a pointed stick and a bag of seeds. Every step or so, he pokes a hole in the ground. He drops in a few seeds and covers them with his foot. And then he waits and watches the sky. If the rains come late, the seeds will die in the dry ground.

[pause]

{img:maya-farmer-planting-digging-stick-seed-bag-burnt-field-rain-clouds}
Next to the corn he plants beans. They climb up the corn stalks. He plants squash too. It spreads across the ground with big leaves that keep the soil damp. The three plants grow together and help each other. Later, people will call them the three sisters.

[long-pause]

He grows other plants too. Chilli peppers and tomatoes, sweet potatoes and cotton. Around his house grow avocados and papayas and many other fruits.

[pause]

After two or three years, the soil of a field gets tired. The farmer lets it turn back into forest. And he cuts a new field somewhere else.

[pause]

But a city as big as Tikal needs much more food than this. So the Maya also built steps of land on the hillsides. They built raised fields in the wet lowlands, with little canals between them. And they had gardens everywhere around their houses. The laser maps show that the land around the Maya cities was farmed very carefully.

[pause]

{img:maya-corn-harvest-baskets-ripe-ears-family-field-autumn}
In autumn the corn is ripe. The farmer bends the stalks over so the ears hang down. That way they dry in the field, safe from rain and birds. Then the family picks the corn. They carry it home in big baskets on their backs. They store it in the store house for the whole year.

[long-pause]

Before planting and at harvest, the farmer prays. He gives gifts to the gods of the earth and the rain and the corn. He burns sweet incense and thanks them. Maya farmers still do this today.

[long-pause]

{img:cacao-tree-pods-growing-trunk-shade-forest-red-yellow}
Another plant is very precious to the Maya. It is cacao, the tree that gives us chocolate.

[pause]

The cacao tree is small. It grows in the shade of taller trees, in the warm wet forest. Its fruits grow straight out of the trunk and the branches. They are big oval pods, yellow and orange and red and purple. They look like strange giant fruits.

[long-pause]

Inside each pod there are thirty or forty seeds. These are the cacao beans. They are wrapped in sweet white pulp. The beans are taken out and left for a few days. Then they are dried in the sun and roasted. Then they are ground on a stone into a thick dark paste.

[pause]

And from this paste, the Maya make a drink. Chocolate.

[pause]

{img:maya-woman-pouring-chocolate-drink-vessel-height-foam}
It is not sweet like our chocolate. It is bitter and spicy. It is mixed with water and ground corn and chilli. Sometimes flowers and vanilla and honey are added. To make it foamy, it is poured from high up, from one pot into another. Soon it has a thick rich foam on top. That is the best part.

[long-pause]

Chocolate is a drink for kings and nobles and special days. It is for feasts and weddings and ceremonies. It is offered to the gods. Beautiful painted chocolate cups have been found in the graves of the kings of Tikal. Some still have traces of chocolate inside. On the outside, painted signs say, this is the cup for the cacao of the king.

[pause]

Cacao beans are so precious that people use them as money. With a few beans you can buy a tomato. With a hundred you can buy a turkey or a good piece of cloth.

[pause]

{img:painted-maya-cylinder-vase-court-scene-nobles-cacao-glyphs}
The Maya have a god of cacao and festivals of cacao. Cacao is given to the gods at the great moments of life.

[long-pause]

Much later, the Spanish brought cacao to Europe. There it became the fashionable drink of kings and queens. Later still, people added sugar and milk. And it became the chocolate we know today. The word cacao may come from the Maya word kakaw. It is written in their signs, on their chocolate cups.

[pause]

So every time we drink a cup of hot chocolate before bed, we share something with the kings of Tikal.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-kitchen-house-clay-pots-fire-three-stones-tamales-steaming}
What do the people of Tikal eat?

[pause]

Mostly corn, as we have seen, every day, in many forms. There are flat corn breads cooked on a clay plate. There are tamales, small parcels of corn dough filled with beans or meat. They are wrapped in leaves and steamed in a big pot. And there are warm thick drinks made of corn and water. Sometimes chilli or honey is added. Men carry a little ball of corn dough to the fields. At midday they mix it with water from a stream and drink it.

[long-pause]

The corn is always soaked first, overnight, in water with a little lime. The lime is made from burnt stone or shells. This makes the corn softer and easier to grind. It also makes it much healthier. It is a great discovery, thousands of years old. People in Mexico and Central America still do it today.

[pause]

With the corn there are beans, black and red. There is squash, and its seeds, toasted and ground. There are chilli peppers of many kinds, and tomatoes and avocados.

[pause]

{img:maya-feast-foods-turkey-tamales-squash-fruits-painted-plates-mats}
For meat, the hunters bring deer and wild pigs back from the forest. Families keep tame turkeys in their yards. There are ducks and rabbits and fish from the lakes. There are even iguanas, big lizards, which people roast.

[long-pause]

There is honey from little bees that have no sting. The Maya keep them in hollow logs hung in the shade around their houses. They make a thin sweet honey. Some Maya beekeepers still keep these bees today. But the bees are becoming rare.

[pause]

There are many fruits. Avocados and papayas and guavas. A sweet brown fruit called sapodilla. And a fruit with soft orange flesh called mamey.

[long-pause]

At feasts there is much more. Roasted turkeys and many kinds of tamales. Big pots of chocolate. And a special drink of honey and water and tree bark, left to ferment. It is drunk at ceremonies.

[pause]

{img:maya-woman-cooking-tortillas-clay-griddle-comal-smoke-morning}
The women do the cooking in the kitchen house, over the fire between three stones. The women also know the recipes and the plants and the herbs and the medicines. They pass them on to their daughters and granddaughters.

[long-pause]

The pots are made of clay. Big ones store water and corn. Small ones hold sauces. Gourds are cut in half and dried. They make cups and bowls and bottles.

[pause]

And so every day, in thousands of kitchens all over Tikal, smoke rises through the roofs. The smell of corn and wood smoke and chilli drifts over the city. It mixes with the scent of flowers and the forest.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-scribe-painting-folding-bark-paper-book-brush-shell-inkpot}
The Maya can write.

[pause]

Of all the peoples of the ancient Americas, the Maya had the most complete writing. With it they could write down anything they could say. Names and dates, stories and poems.

[long-pause]

Maya writing is made of small square pictures with rounded corners. We call them glyphs. Each one is like a tiny painting. It might show a face, or an animal, or a hand. Little signs are attached to it. Some glyphs stand for a whole word. A jaguar's head means jaguar. Other glyphs stand for a sound, like ba or la or ma. A writer can write the same word in many ways. Sometimes with a picture, sometimes with sounds, sometimes with both. He chooses what looks most beautiful.

[pause]

There are more than a thousand different glyphs. They are written in pairs of columns, from left to right and from top to bottom.

[pause]

The writers are important people. They are nobles, sometimes even princes. They are artists and writers and star watchers all at once. They even have their own god, a monkey god of writing and art.

[long-pause]

They paint with brushes and with pens made of feathers. Their ink pots are cut from shells. In paintings, writers often have a little bundle of brushes tucked into their headdress.

[pause]

They write on stone, carved on tall stones and altars and stairways. They write on painted pots and on carved jade and bone. And they write books.

[long-pause]

Maya books are made of paper from the inner bark of the fig tree. The bark is beaten flat and covered with a smooth white coat. Then it is folded like a fan, or like an accordion, between covers of wood or jaguar skin. You can open it page by page. Or you can stretch it out to many metres long.

[pause]

The pages are painted on both sides with glyphs and pictures, in black and red and blue. There are books about history and the gods. There are books about the stars, the calendar and medicine. And there are books of songs.

[pause]

{img:maya-codex-pages-unfolded-red-black-glyphs-figures-calendar}
There must have been thousands of these books in every Maya city. But they were fragile. The climate is warm and damp. Almost all of them were lost to time and insects. Much later, Spanish priests burned many more, because they did not understand them.

[pause]

Today only four Maya books survive. They are all from later times, and all from far away from Tikal. They are kept in museums in Germany, Spain, France and Mexico.

[long-pause]

For hundreds of years, nobody could read Maya writing. It was a great puzzle.

[pause]

Then slowly, in the last hundred years, scholars began to understand it. First they worked out the numbers and the dates. Then a Russian scholar showed that many glyphs stood for sounds. Then a woman scholar, Tatiana Proskouriakoff, studied the dates on the stones. She showed that they told the stories of real kings and queens. Their births, their crowning, their wars and their deaths. And then scholars began to read whole sentences.

[pause]

{img:scholar-desk-notebook-glyph-drawings-lamp-twentieth-century}
Today most Maya writing can be read. And the stones of Tikal have begun to speak again. They tell us the names and stories of their kings and their people.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-calendar-stone-carved-day-signs-circle-numbers-bars-dots}
The Maya love time.

[pause]

They watch the sun and the moon and the stars and the planets. They count the days with great care. They write them down on their stones and in their books. And they have not one calendar, but several. They all turn together, like the wheels of a great clock.

[long-pause]

First, the Maya write numbers in a very simple way. A dot is one. A bar is five. So seven is a bar and two dots. Nineteen is three bars and four dots. They also have a sign for zero. It looks like a little shell. They were among the first people in the world to use the idea of zero.

[pause]

They count in twenties, not in tens like we do. Perhaps because people have twenty fingers and toes.

[pause]

{img:bar-dot-numbers-carved-stone-shell-zero-sign}
The first calendar is the holy calendar. It counts two hundred and sixty days.

[long-pause]

It has twenty named days. Each one has its own sign, its own god and its own meaning. They turn together with the numbers one to thirteen. After two hundred and sixty days, the same pair comes round again.

[pause]

This calendar is used to name children. It is used to choose the right days for planting, for weddings and for ceremonies. And it is used to tell the future. Maya daykeepers in the mountains of Guatemala still use it today.

[long-pause]

The second calendar follows the sun. It has three hundred and sixty-five days. There are eighteen months of twenty days each. And at the end there are five extra days. These are unlucky days without names. People stay at home and are careful. Nobody starts anything new. Everyone waits for the new year to begin.

[pause]

The two calendars turn together, like two gears. The same pair of days only comes round once every fifty-two years. For the Maya, this is a great cycle, a bit like a century for us.

[pause]

{img:priest-astronomer-temple-top-dawn-watching-sunrise-horizon}
And then there is the Long Count.

[long-pause]

The Long Count is the great calendar on the Maya stones. It counts every single day from the beginning of the present world. The Maya believed the world was made on a day more than three thousand years before Christ.

[pause]

It counts in days, and twenties of days, and years of three hundred and sixty days. It counts twenties of years, and four hundreds of years. So every date on a stone tells exactly how many days have passed since the world began.

[pause]

Every twenty years there is a great festival at Tikal. The kings build two pyramids facing each other across a square, just for this festival. They set up a carved stone and an altar.

[pause]

{img:twin-pyramid-complex-tikal-katun-ending-stela-enclosure-plaza}
In 2012, many people around the world got worried. They believed the Maya had said the world would end that December. On that day, the Long Count reached the end of a very big cycle.

[long-pause]

But the Maya never said that. For them, it was simply the end of one great cycle and the start of a new one. Like the turning of a great wheel. One Maya stone even talks about a date far in the future, in the year 4772.

[pause]

And the next morning, the sun rose as usual over the temples of Tikal. Thousands of people had gathered there with Maya priests. And they celebrated the new beginning.

[long-pause]

{img:night-sky-milky-way-over-tikal-temples-silhouette-stars}
At night, over Tikal, the sky is full of stars.

[pause]

There are no city lights. There are only the little fires in the houses and the torches on the temples. The sky is black and deep and clear. Thousands and thousands of stars shine. And the glowing band of the Milky Way stretches right across the sky.

[long-pause]

For the Maya, the Milky Way is the tree of the world, rising into the sky. It is also the white road, the road the souls of the dead travel along. In some seasons it stands straight up. In others it lies across the sky like a great canoe.

[pause]

The Maya star watchers sit on top of the temples every night. They know the paths of the sun and the moon and the stars and the planets very well.

[pause]

{img:venus-morning-star-bright-dawn-sky-over-forest-temple}
Most of all, they watch Venus.

[long-pause]

Venus is the brightest of the planets. Sometimes it rises before the sun, as the morning star. Sometimes it shines after sunset, as the evening star. And sometimes it disappears for a while into the light of the sun. Where does it go? The Maya want to know exactly when it will come back.

[pause]

They counted that Venus goes through its whole journey in five hundred and eighty-four days. One old Maya book has tables of Venus. They are correct to within a few hours over many years. It is one of the most amazing things in the history of star watching.

[long-pause]

The Maya could also predict when the sun or the moon would go dark in the middle of the day or night. Their tables show the days when this might happen. That way the priests could warn the people and hold the right ceremonies.

[pause]

They knew the length of the year and the month very exactly. And they watched the longest and the shortest days of the year.

[pause]

{img:maya-astronomers-observing-sky-temple-platform-night-torches-crossed-sticks}
In a city near Tikal, there is a pyramid with three small temples in a row across from it. Stand on the pyramid. On the longest day of the year, the sun rises behind the northern temple. On the shortest day, it rises behind the southern one. And in spring and autumn, it rises right behind the middle one. There is an even older group like this at Tikal itself.

[long-pause]

For the Maya, the sky is not empty. It is full of gods and stories. The sun and the moon and Venus are gods and heroes. They move across the sky by day. At night they travel through the world under the earth. And the kings on top of their temples, at just the right moment, take part in the great movements of the heavens.

[pause]

Tonight the stars over Tikal are the same as they were in the year 750. The Milky Way still rises over the temples. And Venus still shines at dawn over the forest.

[long-pause]

{img:tikal-night-moonlight-great-plaza-temples-torches-quiet}
Let us stay in Tikal for a while, at night.

[pause]

The sun has set behind the temples. The sky over the forest has turned from gold to red to deep blue, and then to black. The heat of the day is gone. The air is soft and warm and damp. It smells of flowers and smoke and wet leaves.

[long-pause]

In the Great Plaza, a few torches burn on the temple stairs. Priests keep watch there. Guards stand at the palace doors. The white floor of the plaza glows faintly in the moonlight. And the great temples rise up, dark against the stars.

[pause]

In the palace, the courtyards are quiet now. The last visitors have gone. Servants are clearing away the cups and plates. Behind the curtains, the royal family is going to sleep on their wide stone benches.

[pause]

{img:village-houses-night-glowing-fires-through-doorways-forest-dark}
Out in the countryside, in thousands of little houses, the fires are burning low. Families lie down in their hammocks, wrapped in blankets. The grandmothers tell one last story to the children, who are already half asleep.

[long-pause]

The dogs are curled up by the doorways. The turkeys are sleeping in the trees. In the gardens, white night flowers open, sweet-smelling, and the moths come to them.

[pause]

And in the forest all around, the night animals are awake. Owls call. Bats flit over the water ponds. Frogs sing, and crickets. Small animals move softly through the branches. And somewhere far away, the jaguar walks silently along a forest path, under the stars.

[pause]

{img:fireflies-over-reservoir-night-water-lilies-temple-silhouette}
Over the ponds, fireflies flicker. Little green lights drift over the still black water. The water lilies have closed their flowers for the night.

[long-pause]

And on top of one temple, a priest sits alone. He watches the sky. He is waiting for Venus to rise before dawn. He counts the days and the stars, while the whole city sleeps below him, in the great dark quiet forest.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-ballcourt-two-sloping-walls-plaza-tikal-afternoon}
In the heart of Tikal, near the Great Plaza, there is a ball court.

[pause]

It is a long narrow lane with a smooth floor. On each side stands a long low building with a sloping wall. The walls face each other. They are painted red and white. Here the Maya play their great game, the ball game.

[long-pause]

The ball game is one of the oldest games in the world. It was played all over Mexico and Central America for more than three thousand years. In some places in Mexico, a version of it is still played today.

[pause]

The ball is made of solid rubber. It is heavy and hard and bouncy, about the size of a big melon. It is made from the sap of the rubber tree. The Maya were among the first people in the world to make rubber. Nobody in Europe had ever seen it.

[pause]

The players must keep the ball in the air. They send it to the other side, bouncing it off the sloping walls. But they may not touch it with their hands or their feet. They hit it with their hips and their thighs and their arms. So they wear thick padded belts around their waists. They wear pads on their knees and arms too. The ball is heavy, and it can hurt.

[long-pause]

It is a fast and difficult game. The ball flies, the players dive, and the crowd holds its breath. Then it cheers. People make bets on their favourite players, with cacao beans and cloth.

[pause]

And the game is also holy. For the Maya, the ball is like the sun and the moon. They move across the sky and through the world below the earth. The game is like the great contest between day and night, between light and darkness.

[pause]

{img:hero-twins-playing-ball-underworld-lords-mythic-scene-painted-vase}
In the old holy book, there is a great story about the ball game.

[long-pause]

Long ago, there were two brothers who loved to play ball. But they played so noisily that they woke the lords of the world below the earth. The lords invited them down to play. And the lords tricked them. The brothers never came back.

[pause]

But one of the brothers had two sons, who were twins. They are called the Hero Twins. And they too loved to play ball. And they too were invited down to the world below.

[long-pause]

But the Hero Twins were clever. The lords set them test after test. They had to spend a night in the House of Darkness. And in the House of Cold. And in the House of Jaguars and the House of Bats. Each time, it seemed they could not escape. And each time, the twins found a trick. They played ball against the lords, and fooled them again and again. In the end, they won.

[pause]

Then the twins rose up into the sky. They became the sun and the moon. And their father, the corn god, was born again from the earth.

[pause]

{img:ballcourt-marker-carved-stone-disc-ballplayer-glyphs}
At Tikal, the game is sometimes played just for fun. Sometimes it is played as a great ceremony. And sometimes, long ago, the stones tell us, prisoners of war had to play. For them, losing had a very high price. But that was rare, and we will leave it there, gently, in the past.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the ball court is quiet again. The players have gone home to rest their bruises. The crowds have gone. The long narrow court lies empty in the fading light. The red walls glow. And the shadows of the temples fall across it.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-jade-mosaic-mask-green-shell-eyes-museum-like-light}
The Maya are wonderful artists.

[pause]

Their most precious treasure is jade. It is a hard smooth green stone. It comes from a river valley far to the south. For the Maya, jade is worth more than gold. Its green is the colour of young corn and of water. It is the colour of life itself.

[long-pause]

The jade carvers work very patiently. They use cords and sand and water and bits of harder stone. They saw and drill and polish for days and weeks and months. They make beads and necklaces and ear rings. They make little figures of gods and kings.

[pause]

And they make masks of hundreds of little pieces of jade. The pieces fit together into a face. The eyes are made of shell and black stone. When a king was buried, such a mask was laid over his face. The Maya hoped he would be born again, like the corn god, with a face of everlasting green.

[pause]

{img:resplendent-quetzal-bird-long-green-tail-feathers-cloud-forest-branch}
After jade, the most precious thing is quetzal feathers.

[long-pause]

The quetzal is a bird of the misty forests in the mountains to the south. It is small, with a red chest and a shining green body. It has two long tail feathers, almost a metre long. In the sun they shine green and blue and gold.

[pause]

Only kings and the greatest nobles may wear them, in big nodding headdresses. The quetzal is still the national bird of Guatemala. Even its money is called the quetzal.

[long-pause]

The Maya use feathers of many other birds too. Macaws and parrots and hummingbirds. They make headdresses and capes and fans in brilliant colours.

[pause]

And they make wonderful pots.

[pause]

{img:maya-potter-shaping-painting-cylinder-vessel-courtyard-brushes}
Maya potters do not use a turning wheel. They build their pots by hand, from long rolls of clay. They smooth them and polish them and bake them in open fires. Then the painters paint them with fine brushes. They use red and orange and black and cream. They paint the gods and the kings, the animals and the old stories. And they paint a line of glyphs around the top.

[long-pause]

The most beautiful are tall round cups for drinking chocolate. They are painted all the way around. As you turn the cup in your hands, the picture unrolls. You see the king on his throne, with his visitors and their gifts. You see his nobles and his musicians and his dogs. Or you see the gods and the Hero Twins.

[pause]

And some are signed. A few Maya painters wrote their names on their pots. So after more than a thousand years, we still know some of their names.

[pause]

{img:carved-wooden-lintel-temple-doorway-king-jaguar-protector-sapodilla}
At Tikal there are also wonderful wood carvings.

[long-pause]

The temple doors high on the pyramids each have a heavy beam across the top. The beams are made of hard red wood. On the underside they are carved with wonderful scenes. The king sits on his throne. Behind him rises a great jaguar or a great snake, to protect him.

[pause]

Some of these beams were taken away about a hundred and fifty years ago. They are now in a museum in Switzerland. But some are still in place, high up on the temples, in the forest.

[long-pause]

And there are stone carvings, and paintings on the walls. On the fronts of the temples there are huge plaster faces of gods. They have big eyes and long teeth and ear rings. They are painted red and white and black. And they look out over the squares and the people and the forest.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-noblewoman-white-huipil-embroidered-jade-necklace-hair-ribbons}
What do the people of Tikal look like? What do they wear?

[pause]

The Maya are not very tall. They have brown skin and black hair and dark eyes. They think a long sloping forehead and a long nose are very beautiful.

[long-pause]

Ordinary men wear a loincloth. It is a long strip of cotton wound around the waist and between the legs. The ends hang down in front and behind. Sometimes the ends are decorated. On cool evenings, men wrap a cape or a blanket around their shoulders. On their feet they wear sandals.

[pause]

Women wear a long wrapped skirt. Over it they wear a loose cotton blouse or dress. It is called a huipil. It can be white or coloured, with woven patterns. They wear a shawl over their shoulders or their heads.

[pause]

{img:maya-man-loincloth-cape-sandals-hair-tied-feather-market}
Men wear their hair long, tied up on top of their heads or in braids. Women wear their hair long too. They braid it and wind it around their heads with coloured ribbons.

[long-pause]

Both men and women love jewellery. Necklaces and bracelets of shell and stone and seeds. Nobles wear jade. Many wear big round ear plugs of jade or shell or wood. The holes in their ears are stretched slowly from childhood.

[pause]

Some people decorate their teeth. They file them into little shapes. Or they drill tiny holes in the front teeth and fill them with shiny bits of jade. Some paint their faces and bodies red and black and blue. Some have tattoos.

[long-pause]

The nobles and the king dress much more richly. They wear fine cotton in deep colours with painted patterns. They wear capes of jaguar skin and feathers. They wear big collars of jade beads. And they wear tall headdresses built on frames of wood. On top are masks of gods and animals and great sprays of green feathers. They nod and shimmer as they move.

[pause]

{img:king-full-regalia-quetzal-feather-headdress-jade-collar-jaguar-kilt-stela}
At the great ceremonies, the king is almost hidden by his clothes. His headdress is as tall as he is. He wears jade and jaguar skins and feathers. He no longer looks like an ordinary man. He looks like a god, standing on top of the temple in clouds of incense, shining in the sun.

[long-pause]

And there is colour everywhere. The deep red of the buildings and the clothes. The green of the jade and the feathers. The white of the plaster and the cotton. And a bright blue paint that archaeologists call Maya blue. It has lasted for more than a thousand years without fading. In the year 750, Tikal is not a grey city of stone. It is a city full of colour.

[long-pause]

{img:world-tree-ceiba-cosmos-roots-underworld-branches-heaven-bird-painted}
The Maya world is full of gods.

[pause]

For the Maya, the world has three levels. Above are the thirteen layers of heaven, where the sun and moon and stars live. In the middle is the earth, where people live. It is flat and square, with four sides. Each side has its own colour. Red for the east, where the sun rises. Black for the west, where it sets. White for the north and yellow for the south. And below are the nine layers of the world under the earth. It is a dark and fearful place, ruled by the lords of death.

[long-pause]

At the centre of the world stands the great world tree. It joins the three levels together. Its roots reach into the world below. Its trunk stands in the middle world. Its branches reach into heaven. And a great bird sits at the very top.

[pause]

The temples of Tikal are like holy mountains, rising toward heaven. Their doorways are like caves, leading into the mountain. And the king, standing in the doorway at the top, is like the world tree. He joins heaven and earth and the world below.

[pause]

{img:chaak-rain-god-axe-lightning-clouds-mask-temple-facade}
There are many gods.

[long-pause]

There is the old wise god, the first priest and the first writer. There is the sun god, with big squinting eyes. By day he travels across the sky. By night he travels through the world below, as a jaguar.

[pause]

There is Chaak, the rain god. He has a long nose and carries an axe. The axe is lightning. He strikes the clouds with it and makes the rain fall. For the farmers, he may be the most important god of all. Without rain, the corn will not grow. There is the young corn god. There is the old moon goddess, who also watches over weaving and babies and healing. And there is a god of lightning and plenty, called K'awiil. His name is part of the names of many kings.

[long-pause]

The gods must be fed and honoured and thanked. People burn sweet incense in clay pots. The white smoke rises toward heaven in big clouds. They give food and chocolate and flowers. They give jade and feathers and songs and dances and prayers.

[pause]

{img:copal-incense-smoke-clay-censer-temple-doorway-offerings-flowers}
On great days, the king and queen give something even more precious. They give a few drops of their own blood. It is put on strips of paper, and the paper is burned. The smoke rises to the gods and the ancestors. It is the most holy gift of all. It is a gift of life itself. And the kings must give it, for their people.

[long-pause]

The Maya also honour their ancestors. Families bury their dead under the floors of their houses, or close by. That way the dead stay near. The great kings are buried in the temples. Their children and grandchildren come to bring gifts and to ask for their help.

[pause]

There are spirits everywhere too. In the mountains and caves, in the springs and the trees and the fields. Every person has an animal spirit that shares their soul and their fate. And the kings have the strongest of all, like jaguars and great snakes.

[pause]

{img:maya-dance-ceremony-plaza-masked-dancers-drums-conch-trumpets}
And there is music at every ceremony. Drums of wood and turtle shell beat. Big sea shells are blown like trumpets. There are flutes and rattles and whistles. People sing and dance in the squares before the temples. The dancers wear masks and feathers and dress as the gods.

[long-pause]

Many of the old beliefs live on today among the Maya people, mixed with Christian beliefs. In villages in Guatemala and Mexico, people still burn sweet incense. They pray to the saints, and to the old spirits of the mountains and the rain and the corn. And they still count the days of the holy calendar.

[long-pause]

{img:katun-ceremony-king-twin-pyramid-plaza-crowds-incense-dawn}
In the year 751, a great day is coming in Tikal.

[pause]

A twenty-year period of the Long Count is coming to an end. For the Maya, it is like the start of a new century for us. At Tikal, the end of every twenty years is celebrated with a great festival. And everyone hopes the new period will be a good one.

[long-pause]

For months before, the people get ready.

[pause]

The king's builders make a new pair of pyramids. They have flat tops and stairs on all four sides. They face each other across a square, one in the east and one in the west. On the north side, there is a small walled space with no roof, open to the sky. Inside stands a new carved stone with the king's portrait, and an altar. On the south side stands a long low building with nine doorways. The nine doors stand for the nine levels of the world below.

[pause]

{img:twin-pyramid-flat-top-four-stairways-plaster-new-white-red}
The stone carvers carve the new stone. It shows the king in his finest clothes. He is scattering little drops from his hand, maybe incense, maybe seeds. The glyphs tell the date and the king's name. They tell who his parents were and what ceremonies he held.

[long-pause]

On the great day, everyone comes. People come from all over the city and from the villages around. Some come from other cities. They wear their best clothes and bring flowers and gifts.

[pause]

At dawn, a long procession starts at the Great Plaza. The king and queen walk along the white road, with the nobles and the priests. They wear their richest clothes and tall headdresses of green feathers. There are drums and trumpets and rattles and singing. And clouds of incense rise into the morning air.

[long-pause]

As the sun rises, the king climbs the eastern pyramid. He makes offerings to the sun and the gods. Then he climbs the western pyramid, where the sun will set. Then he goes into the walled space on the north side. He stands before the new stone and the altar. And he makes the offerings that close the old period. Everyone waits in silence. Has he done it right? Will the gods be pleased?

[pause]

{img:festival-plaza-dancers-feather-costumes-drums-crowd-afternoon}
Then the music starts again. There is dancing and feasting all day and into the night. Dancers in masks dance in the squares, dressed as gods and animals. People eat and drink and talk. The smell of roast turkey and tamales and chocolate and incense fills the air.

[long-pause]

And the people of Tikal feel that the world has been made new. The old period has ended safely. A new one has begun. The king has done what he must. Now the sun will keep rising and the rain will keep falling. And the corn will keep growing.

[pause]

The stone set up for this festival still stands at Tikal today, in its little walled space. Nine of these pairs of pyramids have been found at Tikal. Each one was built for the end of a twenty-year period. They are found almost nowhere else in the Maya world.

[long-pause]

When the festival is over and the people have gone home, the new pyramids stand in the moonlight. They gleam white and red in the silence. And twenty years later, the king's son will build another pair, for the next festival.

[long-pause]

{img:tikal-reservoir-still-water-reflecting-temple-water-lilies-evening}
Tikal has one big problem. Water.

[pause]

It rains a lot in the rainy season, from about June to December. But then for five or six months it hardly rains at all. There are no rivers near Tikal and no lakes, and few springs. The ground is limestone. Rain sinks through it fast, deep underground.

[long-pause]

So the people of Tikal must catch the rain and keep it all year. And they have become masters of water.

[pause]

The whole centre of the city is built to catch the rain. It falls on the big squares and the temples and the courtyards. They are all covered in smooth plaster. The water runs off into channels and along the roads. It flows into big ponds dug into the earth. The ponds are lined with clay to keep the water in.

[pause]

{img:plaster-plaza-rain-running-channels-toward-reservoir-storm}
There are about ten big ponds in the centre of Tikal, and many smaller ones around. Together they can hold millions of litres of water. That is enough for the whole city, through the long dry season. At least, in a normal year.

[long-pause]

The ponds are built and looked after with great care. Some have dams and gates to control the water. Not long ago, archaeologists found something amazing. The people of Tikal cleaned their drinking water. They let it run through layers of sand and a special stone. They brought that stone from many kilometres away. It made the water safe to drink. It is one of the oldest water filters in the world.

[pause]

The water in the ponds is clear and still and green. Water lilies float on it, with round flat leaves and white flowers that open in the morning. The water lily is a holy flower for the Maya. It belongs to the kings. One great king of Tikal even wears a water lily in his headdress.

[pause]

{img:maya-woman-carrying-water-jar-head-path-reservoir-morning}
Every morning, the women and girls of Tikal go down to the ponds with their clay jars. They fill them and carry them home on their heads or their hips. They talk and laugh and share the news of the day.

[long-pause]

The kings are in charge of the water. They build the great ponds next to the temples and palaces. People believe that the king's ceremonies and prayers to the rain god bring the rain.

[pause]

So when the rains fail, the people look to the king.

[long-pause]

And in the years after 750, the rains will begin to fail.

[pause]

But tonight, in the rainy season of the year 750, the ponds are full. The rain is falling softly on the straw roofs and the plaster squares and the forest. It runs in little streams along the channels, into the great still pools. And there, the frogs are singing.

[long-pause]

{img:rain-falling-over-tikal-forest-temples-grey-clouds-thatched-houses}
Let us listen to the rain over Tikal.

[pause]

It is May or June. The long hot dry months are almost over. For weeks the sky has been pale. The air is hazy with smoke from the burning fields. The earth is dry and dusty, and the ponds are low. Everyone is waiting. Then in the afternoons, clouds begin to gather. Great grey towering clouds rise over the forest.

[long-pause]

The air becomes heavy and still and hot. The birds go quiet. Then there is a rumble of thunder far away. The wind rises and shakes the trees. The first drops fall, big and heavy and warm. They land on the dry leaves and the dusty squares and the roofs.

[pause]

And then the rain comes all at once. It pours down and roars on the forest and the roofs and the temples. It is so loud that people must shout to be heard. The squares fill with water. The channels run full. And the water rushes into the ponds.

[pause]

{img:children-standing-doorway-watching-rain-pouring-courtyard}
The children run out into it, laughing. They turn their faces up to the sky. The farmers stand in their doorways and watch. They thank the rain god. Now they can plant their corn.

[long-pause]

Every afternoon for months, the rain comes like this. Then it stops, and the sun comes out again. The forest steams and drips and glitters. It smells of wet earth and leaves and flowers.

[pause]

The forest grows greener and greener. Thousands of frogs come out. They sing all night long in the ponds and the puddles. They croak and peep and trill. The low wet lands around the city fill with water. They become shallow lakes full of herons and ducks.

[long-pause]

In the houses it is cool and dim. The rain drums softly on the straw roof and drips from the edges. The women weave in the shade. The old people sleep in their hammocks. The children play inside. And the dogs curl up by the fire.

[pause]

{img:mist-rising-forest-after-rain-temple-roof-comb-sunbeams}
In the fields, the young corn grows fast, green and tall. The beans climb the stalks. The squash spreads out its big leaves. Between the showers, the farmers go out to pull weeds and chase away the birds.

[long-pause]

And at night the rain falls softly on the roofs of Tikal. It falls on the straw of the houses and the stone of the temples. It falls on the leaves of the great trees. And it falls on the still water of the ponds, making little rings that spread out and fade.

[pause]

And the people of Tikal fall asleep to the sound of it, as people everywhere always have. They are safe and dry and warm under their roofs. And the rain falls and falls, all through the night, on the great dark green forest.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-traders-walking-forest-path-loads-tumplines-backpacks}
Tikal is a great trading city.

[pause]

The Maya have no horses, no donkeys and no carts with wheels. Everything must be carried by people. Porters walk along the forest paths with big loads in baskets on their backs. A strap across their forehead helps hold the weight. Other goods travel in canoes, along the rivers and the coasts.

[long-pause]

And yet the Maya trade over huge distances.

[pause]

From the mountains to the south comes jade. There is also obsidian, a sharp black glass made by volcanoes. It is used for knives. And there are the green quetzal feathers. From the coasts come salt and shells and dried fish and pearls. From the forest lowlands come cacao and cotton and honey and jaguar skins.

[pause]

From far away in central Mexico come green obsidian and fine pots. They also bring new ideas from a great city there.

[long-pause]

In the markets of Tikal, all these things are traded. And so are the things of everyday life. Corn and beans and chillies. Fruit and turkeys. Pots and baskets and mats and cloth. Stone tools and grinding stones and incense.

[pause]

The traders spread their goods on mats on the ground. People walk between them, looking and talking and bargaining. There are no coins. People pay with cacao beans or lengths of cotton cloth. Or they simply swap one thing for another.

[long-pause]

The great traders are important people. They travel far in long lines of porters. The king protects them. They also have their own god. He is an old god with a pack on his back and a walking stick. On the long forest paths, they need him. There are rivers to cross and storms to wait out. And not every city along the way is friendly.

[pause]

{img:river-trade-canoe-dugout-paddlers-goods-jungle-river}
Tikal lies near the middle of the Maya lands. It sits between two great river systems. One flows west to the Gulf of Mexico. The other flows east to the Caribbean Sea. So Tikal can control the trade between them. That is one reason it became so rich and strong.

[long-pause]

And between the Maya cities there are roads.

[pause]

The Maya build wide raised roads. They call them white roads. They are straight and wide and level. They are built up on banks of stone and covered with white plaster. They gleam in the sunlight and in the moonlight. You can follow them even at night. Some run inside the cities. Others run between cities for many kilometres, through the forest and across the swamps.

[pause]

{img:sacbe-white-raised-causeway-straight-through-forest-sunlight}
Messengers run along them between the cities, with news and gifts from one king to another. Princesses travel along them with big escorts, on their way to be married in another city. And pilgrims walk to great temples and holy caves.

[long-pause]

So the people of Tikal are not alone in the forest. They live in a great web of cities, big and small. Trade and roads and rivers join them. So do marriages and friendships and rivalries. They share a language and writing and gods and a calendar. Together, they make up the Maya world.

[long-pause]

{img:tikal-temples-at-sunset-golden-light-forest-quiet-end-of-era}
In the year 750, Tikal is at the height of its glory.

[pause]

But in the hundred and fifty years that follow, something happens. It happens to Tikal and to all the great Maya cities of the south. Slowly, people leave them.

[long-pause]

At Tikal, the building goes on for a while. Temple Four is built. Temple Three, the last of the great temples, is finished around the year 810. The last carved stone with a date is set up in the year 869.

[pause]

And then there are no more dates. No more stones, no more great temples. The kings disappear. The palaces are empty. Year by year there are fewer people, as families move away. By the year 950, only a few people still live among the great buildings. And in the end, they leave too.

[pause]

The same happens in city after city all over the south. Copán and Palenque and Calakmul and many more. Their last stones are set up one after another. And then they fall silent.

[long-pause]

Why?

[pause]

For a long time it was one of the great mysteries of history. Scholars still talk about it. Today they think there was not one single cause, but many, coming together.

[pause]

There were too many people for the land to feed. The forests had been cut down. They were cut for fields and for firewood. And they were cut to make plaster for all the buildings. The soil was tired.

[long-pause]

And then there were droughts.

[pause]

Scientists have studied the mud at the bottom of lakes. They have studied the stone drips that grow slowly in caves. Both tell the same story. Between about 800 and 950, there were several long droughts in the Maya lands. The rains failed year after year. The ponds dried up. The corn died in the fields.

[pause]

{img:dry-cracked-reservoir-bed-drought-sky-pale-temples-distant}
The kings were supposed to bring the rain. But they could not. People lost faith in them. The cities quarrelled more and more over the land and water and food that was left. And trade moved away, to the coasts and the north.

[long-pause]

So slowly and gently, over several generations, the people left. They did not vanish. They moved north, to other Maya cities that grew strong after the southern ones had faded. They moved to the lakes and rivers and mountains and coasts.

[pause]

The Maya did not disappear. Their way of life went on in other places for many hundreds of years. And their children's children still live in Guatemala and Mexico and Belize and Honduras today. There are millions of them.

[pause]

{img:forest-returning-roots-over-stone-stairway-vines-moss-ruins}
But Tikal was left to the forest.

[long-pause]

Slowly, over the centuries, the forest came back. Seeds blew in on the wind. Birds dropped them on the squares and stairways and temple roofs. Little plants grew in the cracks. Then bushes, and then trees. Their roots pushed into the stones and broke them apart. The bright red paint faded and washed away. The straw houses rotted, leaving only low mounds. The ponds filled with mud. The great pyramids became green hills covered with trees. Only the tops of the tallest temples rose above the forest, like grey ghosts.

[pause]

And for nearly a thousand years, the only voices in Tikal were the howler monkeys, the toucans and the parrots. And the soft sound of the rain.

[long-pause]

{img:explorers-1848-forest-clearing-first-sight-temple-overgrown-machetes}
For hundreds of years, Tikal lay hidden in the forest.

[pause]

The local people knew there were ruins somewhere among the trees. The hunters knew, and the men who gathered sap for chewing gum. But almost nobody else did.

[long-pause]

In 1848, two local governors set out into the forest. Local men guided them. They cut their way through the thick green walls of leaves. Would they find anything? Then, through the trees, they saw it. Great temples rising out of the forest, higher than the trees. Carved stones and palaces, all silent, all covered with moss. An artist with them made drawings. A few years later, the drawings were printed in Germany.

[pause]

They were amazed. Nobody had imagined a city like this, so deep in the forest.

[pause]

Some years later, a Swiss doctor visited. He had some of the carved wooden beams taken down from the temples. He sent them to Switzerland, where they still are.

[long-pause]

Then an English explorer called Alfred Maudslay came, with a camera. He took the first photographs of the ruins, on big glass plates. He was a patient and careful man. He spent years taking photographs and making casts of the carved stones in many Maya cities. That way, scholars all over the world could study the glyphs. His photographs of Tikal still look magical today. The great temples sit half buried in the forest.

[pause]

After him came others. They mapped the ruins and drew the stones. And slowly they began to understand the city.

[pause]

{img:archaeologists-1950s-excavation-temple-stairs-workers-field-camp}
Then, in 1956, an American university began a great project at Tikal. It lasted fourteen years.

[long-pause]

There were no roads. So a small airstrip was built in the forest. People and supplies came in by plane. The archaeologists and hundreds of local workers cleared the trees from the temples and squares. They dug and mapped and repaired.

[pause]

Deep under Temple One, they found the grave of the great king, with his jade and his carved bones. They dug into the old northern temples. They found older buildings and older graves, one under another, going back more than a thousand years. And they mapped the land around. It was full of houses. Tikal had been much bigger than anyone thought.

[long-pause]

After them, archaeologists from Guatemala carried on the work. They dug and repaired many more buildings.

[pause]

{img:temple-iv-top-view-over-forest-canopy-mist-temples-poking-through}
And in 1977, a film crew came to Tikal.

[long-pause]

They climbed to the top of Temple Four. They filmed the view over the forest in the early morning mist. The tops of the other temples rose out of the trees in the distance. This view became part of a famous film, Star Wars. In it, it is a secret base on a moon, in a faraway galaxy.

[pause]

So today, many people who have never heard of the Maya have seen the temples of Tikal, without knowing it.

[long-pause]

{img:lake-peten-itza-island-city-nojpeten-temples-canoes-calm-water}
Not far from Tikal, about thirty kilometres to the south, there is a big lake.

[pause]

On a small island in the lake there was once a city. It was the capital of a Maya people called the Itza. Their ancestors had come from the north, they said, long after Tikal was left empty.

[long-pause]

Much later, the Spanish came to Mexico and Central America. They took over the Aztecs and many Maya peoples. But the Itza, on their island in the middle of the forest, stayed free.

[pause]

One day, a Spanish leader marched past the lake on a long journey through the forest. He visited the king of the Itza on his island. He left his horse behind, because it was lame. He promised to come back for it. He never did. The Itza had never seen a horse before. They looked after it. And when it died, they made a statue of it and honoured it.

[pause]

{img:island-city-white-buildings-lake-shore-reeds-morning-mist}
For more than a hundred and seventy years, the Itza kingdom stayed free. It was the last free Maya kingdom. The great forest around it kept it safe.

[long-pause]

Spanish priests came now and then. They tried to change the Itza's faith. Each time, they were sent away. And the Itza went on living as their ancestors had. They kept their temples and their books, their kings and their calendar. But each year, the Spanish came a little closer.

[pause]

At last, in 1697, Spanish soldiers came with boats and took the island. The last Maya kingdom came to an end. We will leave that sad day there, gently, in the past.

[long-pause]

Today the island is a small town called Flores. It has narrow streets and colourful houses. A church stands on top of the island, where the temples once stood. A long road joins it to the shore. Most visitors to Tikal stay here. They set out from here early in the morning to see the temples in the forest.

[pause]

{img:flores-island-town-colourful-houses-lake-sunset-boats}
In the evening, people walk around the edge of the island, beside the lake. They watch the sun set over the water, red and gold and pink. The Itza once watched it from their island city. And the people of Tikal, long before them, watched it from the tops of their temples, over the forest.

[long-pause]

{img:tikal-national-park-visitors-climbing-wooden-stairs-temple-iv-morning}
Today Tikal is a national park in the north of Guatemala.

[pause]

It became a park in 1955. A few decades later it became a World Heritage Site, one of the first in the world. It is protected both for its ruins and for its forest and animals.

[long-pause]

The park covers more than five hundred square kilometres of forest. And it lies inside an even bigger protected forest. That forest covers a fifth of Guatemala.

[pause]

Every year, many thousands of visitors come to Tikal from all over the world. They walk along the paths through the forest, in the shade of the great trees. They walk from one group of temples to the next, just as the people of Tikal once walked along their white roads.

[pause]

{img:sunrise-temple-iv-top-visitors-sitting-mist-forest-howler-calls}
Some visitors climb to the top of Temple Four on wooden stairs before dawn. They sit there in the dark and wait for the sun.

[long-pause]

Slowly the sky grows lighter in the east. Mist rises from the forest. The tops of the other temples appear, grey and silent, above the trees. Then the howler monkeys begin to roar. The toucans and parrots begin to call. And the whole forest wakes up around them, as it has every morning for thousands of years.

[pause]

The animals have come back to Tikal. Coatis wander across the Great Plaza with their tails held high. Spider monkeys swing in the trees above the temples. Wild turkeys with shining feathers walk calmly among the visitors. There are hundreds of kinds of birds. And deep in the forest, rarely seen, there are still jaguars.

[pause]

The Great Plaza is covered with grass now, instead of white plaster. The temples are grey, and the colours have gone. But they are still magnificent.

[long-pause]

In 2018, scientists flew over the forest around Tikal with their laser machine. Under the trees they found more than sixty thousand buildings that nobody knew about. Houses and palaces and pyramids. Roads and fields and walls. In the time of Tikal's glory, millions of people had lived in the Maya lands. Far more than anyone had thought.

[pause]

There is still so much to find. Most of the buildings of Tikal have never been dug up. They still lie under the trees, waiting.

[pause]

{img:modern-maya-woman-weaving-backstrap-loom-colourful-huipil-village}
And the Maya are still here.

[long-pause]

More than six million Maya people live in Guatemala today. That is almost half of the country. Millions more live in Mexico and Belize and Honduras. They speak more than twenty Maya languages. Many still grow corn in their forest fields. Many still weave on the same simple looms. They wear beautiful woven clothes, with patterns that show which village they come from. And some still count the days of the old holy calendar.

[pause]

On special days at Tikal, Maya priests and families come to the Great Plaza. They burn incense and candles in front of the temples. And they pray to their ancestors and the old gods, as their people have done for more than two thousand years.

[long-pause]

So we leave Tikal tonight, as the evening falls.

[pause]

The great temples rise above the forest, grey and silent. The sky turns pink and gold and then deep blue. The howler monkeys roar one last time from the trees. The toucans fly home across the plaza. Fireflies begin to flicker among the stones. And the stars come out one by one over the temples. The great white band of the Milky Way, the tree of the world, rises slowly into the sky. Just as it did over the city of the kings, in the year 750.

[pause]
