Tonight we are going nearly thirteen hundred years back, to the great Maya city of Tikal, deep in the rainforest of Central America, in the year 750 after Christ.

[long-pause]

Above an endless green sea of trees, steep white and red pyramids rise into the morning mist, with temples on their tops, and painted roof combs, and the smoke of incense drifting from their doorways. Below them, there are plazas of white plaster, and palaces, and ball courts, and reservoirs, and thousands of houses, scattered among gardens and fields, and the forest all around, full of the calls of howler monkeys and parrots.

[pause]

It is one of the greatest cities of the ancient Americas, at the height of its glory. And tonight, we will spend a little time there, with its kings and its scribes, its farmers and its potters, and the forest that surrounds it all.

[pause]

If you enjoy slow journeys into faraway times, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, so that the next one is easy to find.

[long-pause]

{img:peten-rainforest-green-canopy-endless-lakes-low-hills-aerial}
Tikal stands in what is now the north of Guatemala, in a region called the Petén: a great, flat, low land of limestone, covered with tropical forest, stretching for hundreds of kilometres, into what is now Mexico, and Belize.

[pause]

It is hot, and humid, all year round. There is a rainy season, from about May to December, when great thunderstorms roll across the forest, almost every afternoon, and the rain pours down, warm, and heavy, and the forest steams. And a dry season, from about January to April, when it hardly rains at all, and the ground cracks, and the water holes dry up, and the trees lose some of their leaves.

[long-pause]

There are no rivers near Tikal, and no lakes. The rain soaks quickly into the limestone ground, and disappears. And so the people of Tikal must catch the rain, and keep it, in great reservoirs, to last them through the dry season.

[pause]

And yet, in this difficult place, in the middle of the forest, without rivers, without metal tools, without wheels, and without horses, or oxen, or any animals to carry loads, the Maya have built a great city, of perhaps fifty, or sixty, or even ninety thousand people, with some of the tallest buildings in the Americas, before the twentieth century.

[pause]

{img:maya-city-plaza-white-plaster-temples-people-market-morning}
They have writing, and books, and mathematics, and astronomy, and a calendar more accurate than the one used in Europe at the time. They have art, and music, and poetry, and great kings, and a long, proud history.

[pause]

Who are they?

[long-pause]

{img:maya-region-map-like-landscape-mountains-lowlands-cities-temples}
The Maya are a people of Central America, who have lived in the same lands, in southern Mexico, and Guatemala, and Belize, and the western parts of Honduras, and El Salvador, for more than three thousand years.

[pause]

They are not one empire, ruled by one king. They are many peoples, speaking related languages, living in many cities, each with its own king, its own dynasty, its own gods, and its own history, like the cities of ancient Greece, or the city-states of medieval Italy. They trade with each other, and marry each other, and visit each other's festivals. And they fight each other, sometimes, for power, and tribute, and glory.

[long-pause]

In the year 750, there are dozens of great Maya cities, and hundreds of smaller ones, scattered across the lowland forests: Tikal, and Calakmul, its great rival, to the north, in Mexico. Palenque, in the west, with its beautiful palace, and its temples, on the edge of the mountains. Copán, in the south east, in Honduras, with its wonderful carved stones. Yaxchilan, on the great river Usumacinta. Caracol, and Naranjo, and Dos Pilas, and many more.

[pause]

The period from about the year 250 to about the year 900 is called, by archaeologists, the Classic period: the great age of the Maya cities, when the most beautiful temples, and palaces, and carvings, and paintings, and books, were made.

[pause]

{img:maya-people-gathering-plaza-festival-feathers-cloth-colourful-crowd}
And Tikal is one of the greatest cities of all. People have lived at Tikal for more than a thousand years, since about 800 before Christ, when it was a little village of farmers. By the year 750, it is an enormous city, the capital of a powerful kingdom, which the Maya call Mutul.

[long-pause]

The Maya call their kings k'uhul ajaw, holy lord. The king is a sacred person, who stands between the people and the gods, and performs the great ceremonies, which keep the world in order, and make the rain fall, and the maize grow.

[pause]

The Maya of the Classic period wrote down the names of their kings, and the dates of their births, and their accessions, and their wars, and their ceremonies, on great carved stone monuments, called stelae, which stand in the plazas of their cities, in front of the temples.

[pause]

{img:carved-stela-maya-king-portrait-glyphs-plaza-tikal-sunlight}
For a long time, nobody could read them. But in the last fifty years, scholars have learned to read most of Maya writing. And now, the stones of Tikal speak again, and tell us the names, and the stories, of the people who lived there, nearly thirteen hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:great-plaza-tikal-temple-i-temple-ii-facing-north-acropolis-sunlight}
Let us walk into the heart of Tikal, on a morning in the year 750.

[pause]

The centre of the city is the Great Plaza: a wide, open square, paved with smooth white plaster, gleaming in the sunshine, so bright that it hurts the eyes. On its east and west sides, facing each other, there are two great temple pyramids, steep, and tall, with stairways running up the front, to small temples on their tops.

[long-pause]

On the east side, there is the Temple of the Great Jaguar, which archaeologists call Temple One: a pyramid of nine steep levels, rising forty-seven metres high, with a small temple on top, with three narrow rooms, and above it, a great roof comb, a tall decorated wall of stone, carved and painted, with the figure of a seated king, which makes the temple look even taller. It was built about twenty years before, over the tomb of a great king, Jasaw Chan K'awiil.

[pause]

On the west side, facing it, there is the Temple of the Masks, Temple Two, a little smaller, built, perhaps, in honour of his queen, Lady Twelve Macaw.

[pause]

{img:temple-i-great-jaguar-steep-stairway-roof-comb-red-painted}
On the north side of the plaza, there is the North Acropolis: a great platform, with many small temples on it, built one on top of another, over more than a thousand years, where the ancient kings of Tikal are buried. And on the south side, there is the Central Acropolis, a great complex of palaces, with courtyards, and rooms, and stairways, where the royal family, and the nobles, live, and the king holds his court.

[long-pause]

In front of the North Acropolis, there is a long row of stelae, carved stone monuments, each with the portrait of a king, in his rich costume, and the glyphs, telling his name, and his deeds, and the dates. And in front of each stela, a round altar stone.

[pause]

The buildings are not grey, as they look today, in the ruins. In 750, they are covered with smooth plaster, and painted, mostly a deep, rich red, with details in blue, and yellow, and green, and black, and white. The whole city glows red, and white, in the sun, against the green of the forest, and the blue of the sky.

[pause]

{img:central-acropolis-palace-courtyards-rooms-nobles-red-plaster}
From the Great Plaza, wide, raised roads, of stone, and plaster, called sacbeob, white roads, run out, across the city, to the other great groups of temples, and palaces, and to the great reservoirs. Along them, there are more temples, and pyramids, and plazas, and ball courts, and markets, and the houses of the nobles.

[long-pause]

And everywhere, around the centre, for many kilometres, among the trees, and the gardens, and the fields, there are the houses of the ordinary people: little groups of houses, around small courtyards, on low platforms, so that the city is not crowded, like a European city, but spread out, like a great garden city, in the forest.

[pause]

Today, archaeologists using a new technology, called LiDAR, which uses laser beams, from aircraft, to see through the trees, to the ground below, have found that the city was much larger than anyone had thought, with thousands of houses, and fields, and roads, and walls, and canals, hidden under the forest.

[long-pause]

{img:central-acropolis-throne-room-king-seated-bench-jaguar-cushion-courtiers}
Let us go into the palace, the Central Acropolis, on the south side of the Great Plaza.

[pause]

It is not one great building, like a European palace, but a maze of long, low buildings, with many rooms, around six courtyards, at different levels, joined by stairways, and narrow passages, and doorways. Some of the buildings have two storeys, and some have three.

[long-pause]

The rooms are narrow, and long, with thick walls, of stone, and plaster, and high, pointed ceilings, made by the Maya arch, the corbel vault, in which each layer of stones sticks out a little further than the one below, until they almost meet, at the top, and are closed with a flat stone. They are cool, and dim, and quiet, even on the hottest days.

[pause]

In many of the rooms, there are wide stone benches, along the walls, covered with mats, and cushions, and jaguar skins, where people sit, and sleep. Curtains of cotton cloth, painted, and embroidered, hang in the doorways, for privacy, and to keep out the light.

[pause]

{img:palace-courtyard-nobles-sitting-mats-servants-chocolate-cups-shade}
In the throne room, the king sits on a wide stone bench, a throne, covered with a jaguar skin, with a great cushion behind him, also covered with jaguar skin, and a curtain, which can be drawn back, to show him to his visitors.

[long-pause]

Before him, the nobles sit, on mats, cross-legged, in their rich clothes, and their jewels, and their headdresses. The ambassadors from other cities come, and bow, and offer their gifts: bundles of cloth, and cacao beans, and jade, and quetzal feathers, and shells, piled up on the floor, before the throne. The scribes sit, with their books, and their brushes, writing down what is given, and said. And the servants come and go, with plates of tamales, and cups of chocolate, and the dwarves, who are thought to be special, and close to the gods, entertain the court.

[pause]

There are musicians, and dancers, and jesters, and the king's dogs, and pet birds, parrots, and macaws.

[long-pause]

In the quieter parts of the palace, the royal family lives: the king's wives, and his mother, and his children, and his brothers, and sisters, with their own rooms, and courtyards, and kitchens, and servants.

[pause]

The young princes are taught by the scribes, and the priests: how to read, and write, the glyphs, and count the days, and watch the stars, and perform the ceremonies, and dance, and speak well, and behave as a king should. And the princesses learn too: to weave the finest cloth, and to take part in the ceremonies, and to read, and perhaps to write.

[pause]

{img:palace-wall-graffiti-scratched-drawings-temple-figures-plaster}
On the plaster walls of some of the palace rooms, archaeologists have found graffiti: drawings, scratched into the plaster, by people living there, long ago. Little pictures of temples, and people, and processions, and ballplayers, and prisoners, and animals. Drawn, perhaps, by children, or by bored servants, or by guards, waiting for something to happen.

[long-pause]

They are not great art. But they bring us close to the ordinary people, who lived in these rooms, more than twelve hundred years ago, and who, like people everywhere, scratched a little drawing, on the wall, while they were waiting.

[pause]

In the evenings, the courtyards of the palace are lit with torches, of pine, and the smell of the smoke, and the copal, and the cooking, drifts through the passages, and the sound of music, and voices, and laughter, rises into the night.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-builders-quarry-limestone-blocks-stone-tools-workers}
How did the Maya build the great temples of Tikal, without metal tools, without wheels, without animals to pull, or carry?

[pause]

With stone, and wood, and fire, and water, and the strength, and skill, and patience, of thousands of people.

[long-pause]

The ground under Tikal is limestone, and the builders quarry it, close by, cutting out the blocks with tools of flint, and harder stone. The limestone is soft, when it is first cut, and easy to work, and it hardens, slowly, in the air. Many of the quarries became the reservoirs, afterwards, so that the holes left by the builders were filled with water.

[pause]

The blocks are carried, on the backs of the workers, with tumplines, across their foreheads, or rolled, on logs, and lifted, with ropes, and ramps, of earth, and wood.

[pause]

{img:lime-kiln-burning-wood-stones-smoke-workers-forest-edge}
The inside of the pyramids is mostly filled with rubble, stones, and earth, held in by walls, and then covered with a skin of cut stone blocks, and then with plaster.

[long-pause]

The plaster is made of lime. The builders burn limestone, in great fires, of wood, piled up, for a day and a night, until it turns into a white powder, quicklime, and then they mix it with water, and sand, and it becomes a smooth, white paste, which hardens, like stone. It needs a great deal of wood: to make enough lime for one great temple, whole areas of forest are cut down.

[pause]

The plaster covers everything: the pyramids, and the temples, and the plazas, and the roads, and the floors, and is smoothed, and polished, and painted, red, mostly, with paint made from iron oxide, red earth, mixed with water, and other things.

[long-pause]

On the top of the pyramids, the builders make the little temples, with thick walls, and narrow rooms, with the Maya arch, and then, above them, the roof combs, tall, decorated walls, of stone, which are made lighter by little rooms, and holes, inside them, and covered with great sculptures, of plaster, of the king, on his throne, painted in many colours.

[pause]

The builders work in teams, for the king, perhaps for some weeks, every year, in the dry season, when there is less work in the fields, as a kind of tax, and in return, they are fed, and honoured, and take part in the great festivals.

[pause]

{img:architect-scribe-plan-workers-raising-temple-scaffolding-ropes}
There are architects, and master masons, and plasterers, and sculptors, and painters, who know their craft, and pass it on, from father to son, and who plan the buildings, so carefully, that they line up with the sun, and the stars, and with each other, across the city.

[long-pause]

And the Maya build on top of the old buildings. When a king wants a new temple, often, he does not tear down the old one, but builds the new one over it, covering it, like a shell, so that, inside many of the pyramids of Tikal, there are older pyramids, and inside them, older ones still, like the layers of an onion, going back hundreds of years.

[pause]

So the temples of Tikal grew, layer by layer, generation by generation, for more than a thousand years, into the great buildings that still rise, above the forest, today.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-king-jade-jewelry-feather-headdress-throne-palace-court}
Tikal has been ruled by kings for more than five hundred years, by the year 750. The stones of Tikal record the names of more than thirty of them, one after another, from the founder, called Yax Ehb Xook, around the year 90, to the kings of the eighth century.

[pause]

The kings have wonderful names, which tell of their gods, and the animals, and the sky: Great Jaguar Paw. Stormy Sky. Curl Snout. Double Bird. Animal Skull.

[long-pause]

The greatest king of Tikal, in the memory of its people in 750, is Jasaw Chan K'awiil, who ruled for more than fifty years, from 682 to about 734.

[pause]

When he came to the throne, Tikal had been through terrible times. For more than a hundred years, it had been defeated, and dominated, by its great rival, Calakmul, the Snake Kingdom, in the north. Its old monuments had been broken, and its kings humbled.

[pause]

{img:king-dancing-ceremony-plaza-feathers-incense-nobles-musicians}
But in the year 695, Jasaw Chan K'awiil defeated Calakmul, in a great battle, and captured its king, and brought Tikal back to glory. He rebuilt the city, and built great new temples, and set up new monuments, and celebrated great festivals. He was a giant: his skeleton shows that he was tall, much taller than most Maya of his time.

[long-pause]

He was buried under the Temple of the Great Jaguar, in a great tomb, deep inside the pyramid, with wonderful treasures: jade, more than sixteen pounds of it, in necklaces, and bracelets, and ear ornaments, and a mosaic vessel, and pearls, and shells, and painted pots, and carved bones, with scenes of the gods, and the king's voyage to the underworld, in a canoe, paddled by gods, with animals, an iguana, a monkey, a parrot, and a dog, as passengers.

[pause]

His son, Yik'in Chan K'awiil, became king after him, and built even greater temples: Temple Four, the tallest of all, about seventy metres high, rising far above the forest, the tallest building in the Americas, before the twentieth century.

[pause]

{img:temple-iv-tallest-rising-above-forest-canopy-view-other-temples}
In the year 750, Tikal is at the height of its power, rich, and splendid, ruled by the descendants of the great king. Its kings wear headdresses of quetzal feathers, and jaguar skins, and jade, and sit on thrones, covered with jaguar skins, in the palaces of the Central Acropolis, and receive ambassadors, and tribute, and gifts, from other cities, and celebrate the great ceremonies of the calendar, on the tops of the temples, in clouds of incense, before the whole people, gathered in the plazas below.

[long-pause]

The queens, too, are important. Some Maya queens ruled in their own right. The wives of the kings came, often, from other royal families, from other cities, to make alliances. And they appear on the monuments, beside their husbands, in their rich robes, and jewels, taking part in the ceremonies.

[long-pause]

{img:map-like-view-maya-lowlands-many-cities-forest-rivers-painterly}
Tikal is not the only great city, in the Maya world.

[pause]

In the year 750, there are dozens of Maya cities, all over the lowlands, in what is now Guatemala, and Mexico, and Belize, and Honduras, each with its own king, its own temples, and palaces, and plazas, its own gods, and its own history. They speak the same language, more or less, and write in the same script, and count the same calendar, and worship the same gods. But they are not one country. Each city is its own little kingdom.

[long-pause]

Close to Tikal, only about twenty kilometres to the north, there is Uaxactun, an old city, which was once Tikal's rival, and was conquered by it, more than three hundred years before. Further away, there are Yaxha, beside its beautiful lake, and Naranjo, and Holmul, and many others, some friends, some rivals, some ruled by Tikal.

[pause]

And, far to the north, there is Calakmul, the Snake Kingdom, Tikal's great rival.

[pause]

{img:calakmul-great-pyramid-rising-from-forest-north-distance}
For two hundred years, Tikal and Calakmul were the two great powers of the Maya world, like two great empires, each with its own allies, and its own vassal cities, competing for power, and trade, and glory.

[long-pause]

Calakmul defeated Tikal, in the year 562, and for more than a hundred years, Tikal was humbled. Then, in 695, Tikal defeated Calakmul, and rose again. By the year 750, Calakmul is weaker, and Tikal is strong again.

[pause]

Far to the southeast, in what is now Honduras, there is Copán, a beautiful city, in a green valley, beside a river, famous for its sculptures, carved almost in the round, and its great Hieroglyphic Stairway, with more than two thousand glyphs, carved on its steps, telling the history of its kings. Copán was founded, the stones say, by a man, K'inich Yax K'uk' Mo', who came, perhaps, from Tikal, or had close ties with it.

[pause]

{img:palenque-palace-tower-mist-green-hills-temples}
And far to the west, in what is now Mexico, there is Palenque, in the green hills, above the plains, with its beautiful temples, and its palace, with its tall tower, and the great tomb of its greatest king, K'inich Janaab Pakal, who ruled for sixty-eight years, and was buried, in a great stone coffin, deep inside his temple, the Temple of the Inscriptions.

[long-pause]

The kings of these cities visit each other, and send ambassadors, and gifts, and marry each other's daughters, and make alliances, and sometimes, they quarrel, and go to war.

[pause]

The wars of the Maya are, mostly, small wars, raids, and battles between the warriors of two cities, with spears, and clubs, edged with obsidian, and shields, and cotton armour, padded and quilted. The aim is not, usually, to destroy the other city, but to capture its nobles, and warriors, and sometimes its king, and to make it pay tribute, and to win glory. We will leave the battles there, far off, in the past, as the stones do, with a few short words.

[long-pause]

And the people of all these cities, the farmers, and the weavers, and the potters, and the traders, go on, mostly, with their lives, growing their corn, and raising their children, in the forest, whatever the kings are doing.

[long-pause]

{img:rainforest-ceiba-tree-giant-buttress-roots-vines-light-shafts}
All around Tikal, there is the forest.

[pause]

It is a tropical rainforest: tall, dense, green, and full of life. The great trees rise thirty, forty, fifty metres high, with their crowns spreading out, high above, into a great green roof, the canopy, so thick that, on the forest floor, below, it is dim, and green, and cool, even at midday, with only a few shafts of sunlight, falling through, onto the ferns, and the leaves.

[long-pause]

The greatest tree of the forest is the ceiba: a huge tree, with a tall, straight, smooth grey trunk, with great buttress roots, like walls, around its base, and branches spreading out, at the very top, like an umbrella. For the Maya, the ceiba is the sacred tree, the world tree, which stands at the centre of the world, with its roots in the underworld, and its branches in the heavens. It is still the national tree of Guatemala.

[pause]

There are mahogany trees, and cedars, and sapodilla trees, whose sap, chicle, is chewing gum, and the ramón, or breadnut tree, whose seeds can be ground, and eaten, in times of hunger, and allspice, and wild avocado, and copal trees, whose resin is burned as incense, in all the temples.

[pause]

{img:howler-monkeys-tree-canopy-dawn-calling-mist-tikal-forest}
And the forest is full of animals.

[long-pause]

At dawn, and at evening, the howler monkeys roar, from the treetops: a deep, loud, booming, roaring sound, which can be heard for kilometres, and sounds, to someone who has never heard it before, like a jaguar, or a monster. Spider monkeys swing through the branches, with their long arms, and tails. There are toucans, with their enormous, brightly coloured beaks, and green parrots, flying in pairs, and scarlet macaws, and wild turkeys, the ocellated turkey, with its feathers shimmering, blue and green and bronze, like a peacock.

[pause]

There are coatis, with their long noses, and striped tails, held up, which wander through the forest, and the ruins, in groups, looking for fruit, and insects. There are deer, and peccaries, wild pigs, and tapirs, and armadillos, and anteaters, and agoutis.

[pause]

{img:jaguar-resting-forest-branch-spotted-coat-dappled-light-calm}
And there is the jaguar: the great spotted cat of the forest, the most powerful animal of the Americas, silent, and secret, and rarely seen. For the Maya, the jaguar is the king of the forest, and the lord of the night, and of the underworld, and the symbol of the power of the kings, who wear its skin, and sit on thrones covered with it, and take its name.

[long-pause]

At night, the forest is full of sounds: the frogs, and the insects, the crickets, and the cicadas, singing, and clicking, and buzzing, and the owls calling, and the fireflies flickering, among the trees, and the rain, dripping from the leaves.

[pause]

For the people of Tikal, the forest is everything: their source of wood, and food, and medicine, and dyes, and fibres, and game, and their home. And it is also a place of mystery, and spirits, and danger, at the edge of the human world.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-family-house-thatched-roof-courtyard-dawn-smoke-cooking-fire}
Let us spend a day with an ordinary family of Tikal, in the year 750.

[pause]

They live, not in the centre of the city, among the temples, but out in the countryside around it, about an hour's walk from the Great Plaza, in a little group of houses, around a small courtyard, on a low platform of earth, and stones, to keep them dry in the rainy season.

[long-pause]

The houses are simple, and cool. The walls are made of wooden poles, set in the ground, and woven with branches, and covered with clay, mud mixed with grass, and plastered, and painted white. The roofs are steep, and high, made of thick thatch, of palm leaves, which keep out the rain, and the heat of the sun, and let the smoke of the fire drift out. Each house has a single room, with a doorway, but no door, and no windows, and inside, it is dim, and cool, and smells of smoke, and earth, and corn.

[pause]

Around the courtyard, there are several houses: one for the grandparents, one for each of their married sons and their families, a kitchen house, and a storehouse for corn. Behind them, there are gardens, and fruit trees, and the family's fields, and the forest.

[pause]

{img:maya-woman-kneeling-grinding-maize-metate-stone-dawn-house}
The day begins in the dark, before dawn, with the sound of the women, grinding corn.

[long-pause]

Every morning, every woman of Tikal kneels at her grinding stone, the metate: a long, flat, slightly hollowed stone, on the ground, and with another stone, the mano, held in both hands, she grinds the corn, softened overnight in water, with lime, back and forth, back and forth, into a soft, wet dough. It takes hours, every day. It is the sound of every Maya morning, for thousands of years: the soft, steady, scraping, rhythmic sound of the stones.

[pause]

Then she shapes the dough into balls, and pats them flat, and cooks them on a flat clay griddle, over the fire, of three stones, in the middle of the kitchen house. The three hearthstones are sacred: they recall the three stones the gods set up at the creation of the world, in the stars, in the sky.

[long-pause]

The men, and the older boys, eat, and then go out to the fields, with their tools, and their gourds of water, and their bundles of food, wrapped in leaves, for the day.

[pause]

The children help. The little girls help their mothers, and grandmothers, to grind the corn, and fetch water, and look after the little ones, and learn to weave. The little boys go with their fathers, and learn to work in the fields, and to hunt, and to know the plants, and the animals, of the forest.

[pause]

{img:maya-women-weaving-backstrap-loom-tree-courtyard-cotton-colours}
In the shade of the courtyard, the women weave.

[long-pause]

Maya women weave on the backstrap loom: a simple loom, with one end tied to a tree, or a post, and the other end to a strap, around the weaver's back, so that, by leaning back, or forward, she can make the threads tight, or loose. They weave cotton, spun by hand, into cloth, white, and dyed in many colours, with dyes from plants, and insects, and shells, red, and blue, and yellow, and purple, with wonderful patterns.

[pause]

The same kind of loom, and some of the same patterns, are still used, by Maya women, in Guatemala, today.

[long-pause]

At midday, in the heat, everyone rests, in the shade. In the afternoon, the men come back from the fields. They bathe, in the water of the reservoir, or the stream, or in a little steam bath, and the family eats the main meal of the day, together, in the late afternoon: tamales, and tortillas, and beans, and squash, with chilli, and perhaps some meat, deer, or turkey, or fish.

[pause]

{img:maya-family-evening-meal-around-fire-house-tamales-night}
And in the evening, as the sun sets, and the howler monkeys roar, from the forest, and the stars come out, the family sits together, around the fire, and the old people tell stories: of the gods, and the creation of the world, and the hero twins, and the ancestors, and the animals, and the spirits of the forest.

[long-pause]

And then, they sleep, in hammocks, or on mats, on low platforms, wrapped in their cotton blankets, in the dim, smoky warmth of the house, listening to the sounds of the night: the frogs, and the insects, and the rain, on the thatched roof.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-children-playing-courtyard-clay-whistles-toy-animals-dog}
Let us think about the children of Tikal.

[pause]

When a Maya baby is born, the family calls a daykeeper, a priest, who knows the sacred calendar, to find out on which day of the two hundred and sixty days the baby was born, and what that day means, for the child's life, and its character. Each day has its own spirit, and its own fortune.

[long-pause]

Babies are carried everywhere, by their mothers, wrapped in a cloth, on their backs, or on their hips, as Maya mothers still do today. They are breastfed for a long time, two or three years, or more.

[pause]

Some Maya families, especially noble families, gently shape the heads of their babies, by binding them, for the first months, between two little boards, so that the forehead slopes back, long and smooth, like an ear of corn, like the head of the young maize god, which they thought very beautiful. And some hang a little bead of wax, or a ball of resin, from the baby's hair, between its eyes, so that it learns to look at it, and becomes a little cross-eyed, which they also thought beautiful.

[pause]

{img:mother-carrying-baby-back-cloth-sling-walking-garden-path}
Maya children play.

[long-pause]

Archaeologists have found little clay whistles, shaped like animals, and people, and birds, which children blew, and which still make a sound, today, after more than a thousand years. And little clay figures, which may have been toys, or dolls. Children played with balls, of rubber, and with spinning tops, and with dogs, and pet birds, and played games of hide and seek, among the houses, and the trees, as children do everywhere.

[pause]

But they also work, from a young age, helping their parents. By the time they are five, or six, they are helping in the house, and the garden, and the fields. By the time they are twelve, or thirteen, they are almost grown up.

[long-pause]

There is a ceremony, the Spanish later wrote, for the children, when they reach the age of about twelve, a little like a coming of age: the children, boys and girls, are gathered together, in a courtyard, with their parents, and their godparents, and a priest blesses them, and sprinkles them with water, and the girls' mothers take off the little shell, or bead, that their daughters have worn, since they were small, as a sign of childhood. After this, they are grown up.

[pause]

The children of the nobles learn much more: to read, and write, and count, and the calendar, and the history of their families, and the ceremonies, and the dances. The sons of kings learn to be kings.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-telling-stories-children-fire-evening-thatched-house}
And all the children of Tikal learn the stories: of the gods, and the hero twins, and the creation of the world, and the corn, and the animals, of why the rabbit has long ears, and why the howler monkey roars, and why the deer has a short tail. They hear them in the evenings, around the fire, from their grandmothers, and grandfathers, and remember them, and tell them, one day, to their own children.

[long-pause]

Some of those stories are still told, today, in the Maya villages of Guatemala, more than twelve hundred years later, to children, falling asleep, as the rain falls on the roof.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-cornfield-milpa-maize-beans-squash-forest-edge}
For the Maya, everything begins with corn.

[pause]

Corn, maize, is their food, their life, and their god. They eat it every day, at every meal, in tortillas, and tamales, and in drinks, and porridges. Nearly three quarters of everything they eat comes from corn.

[long-pause]

And they believe that people were made from corn.

[pause]

In the Popol Vuh, the great sacred book of the K'iche' Maya, written down much later, in the sixteenth century, but telling very old stories, the gods tried to make people several times. First, they made them from mud, but the mud people were soft, and crumbled, and dissolved in the water. Then they made them from wood, but the wooden people had no hearts, and no souls, and did not remember their makers. And at last, they made them from yellow and white corn, ground into dough, and these were the true people, the ancestors of the Maya.

[pause]

The maize god is one of the most beloved of all Maya gods: a beautiful young man, with a long, smooth head, like an ear of corn, and long hair, like corn silk, and jade jewellery. Every year, like the corn, he dies, and is buried in the earth, and is born again, and grows up, and dances, and brings new life to the world.

[long-pause]

The Maya farmer grows his corn in a milpa: a field, cut out of the forest.

[pause]

In the dry season, in the early spring, he cuts down the trees, and the bushes, on a piece of land, with his stone axe, and lets them dry, in the sun. Then, just before the rains come, he burns them. The ashes make the soil rich.

[pause]

Then, when the first rains come, in May, he plants the corn: he walks through the field, with a pointed digging stick, and a bag of seeds, over his shoulder, and every step or so, he makes a hole in the ground, with the stick, and drops in a few seeds, and covers them with his foot.

[pause]

{img:maya-farmer-planting-digging-stick-seed-bag-burnt-field-rain-clouds}
Beside the corn, he plants beans, which climb up the stalks of the corn, and squash, which spreads out on the ground, between them, with its big leaves, shading the soil, and keeping it moist. The three plants grow together, and help each other. Later, they are called the three sisters.

[long-pause]

He grows other plants too: chilli peppers, and tomatoes, and sweet potatoes, and manioc, and cotton, and tobacco, and gourds, and, around his house, avocados, and papayas, and guavas, and many other fruits.

[pause]

After two or three years, the soil of a milpa is tired, and the farmer lets it go back to forest, and cuts a new field, somewhere else.

[pause]

But a city as great as Tikal needs much more food than this. So, the Maya also built terraces, on the hillsides, and raised fields, in the swamps, the bajos, with canals between them, and gardens, everywhere, around their houses. LiDAR has shown that much of the land, around the Maya cities, was carefully farmed, in many different ways.

[pause]

{img:maya-corn-harvest-baskets-ripe-ears-family-field-autumn}
In the autumn, the corn ripens. The farmer bends the stalks over, so that the ears hang down, and dry, in the field, protected from the rain, and the birds. And then, the family harvests it, and carries it home, in great baskets, on their backs, held by a strap, across their foreheads, and stores it, in the storehouse, for the year.

[long-pause]

And before planting, and at the harvest, the farmer prays, and makes offerings, to the gods, of the earth, and the rain, and the corn, and burns copal incense, and thanks them, as Maya farmers still do today.

[long-pause]

{img:cacao-tree-pods-growing-trunk-shade-forest-red-yellow}
Another plant is very precious to the Maya: cacao, the tree that gives us chocolate.

[pause]

The cacao tree is a small tree, which grows in the shade of taller trees, in the warm, wet forests. Its pods grow straight out of its trunk, and its branches: big, oval, ridged pods, yellow, and orange, and red, and purple, like great, strange fruits.

[long-pause]

Inside each pod, there are thirty or forty seeds, the cacao beans, wrapped in a sweet, white pulp. The beans are taken out, and left to ferment, for a few days, and dried, in the sun, and roasted, and ground, on a grinding stone, into a thick, dark paste.

[pause]

And from this paste, the Maya make a drink: chocolate.

[pause]

{img:maya-woman-pouring-chocolate-drink-vessel-height-foam}
It is not sweet, like our chocolate. It is bitter, and spicy, mixed with water, and ground corn, and chilli, and flowers, and vanilla, and honey. And it is made frothy, by pouring it, from a great height, from one vessel into another, so that it is covered with a thick, rich foam, which is the best part.

[long-pause]

Chocolate is a drink for kings, and nobles, and great occasions: for feasts, and weddings, and ceremonies, and offerings to the gods. Beautiful painted vessels, for drinking chocolate, have been found in the tombs of the kings of Tikal, and other Maya cities, and some of them still have traces of chocolate, inside, and glyphs, painted on the outside, which say, this is the cup, for the cacao, of the king.

[pause]

And cacao beans are so valuable that they are used as money. With a few cacao beans, you can buy a tomato, or a tamale. With a hundred, you can buy a turkey, or a good cloth.

[pause]

{img:painted-maya-cylinder-vase-court-scene-nobles-cacao-glyphs}
The Maya have a god of cacao, and festivals of cacao, and cacao is given to the gods, in offerings, and at the great moments of life.

[long-pause]

Much later, when the Aztecs ruled in Mexico, cacao was still money, and chocolate still the drink of kings. The Spanish brought it to Europe, in the sixteenth century, where it became the fashionable drink of kings, and queens, and nobles, and later, with sugar, and milk, the chocolate we know today. The word chocolate comes from the languages of Mexico, and cacao, perhaps, from the Maya word, kakaw, written in their glyphs, on their chocolate cups.

[pause]

So, every time we drink a cup of hot chocolate, before bed, we are sharing something with the kings, and nobles, of Tikal.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-kitchen-house-clay-pots-fire-three-stones-tamales-steaming}
What do the people of Tikal eat?

[pause]

Mostly corn, as we have seen, every day, in many forms: tortillas, cooked on the griddle; and tamales, little parcels of corn dough, filled with beans, or squash seeds, or meat, wrapped in leaves, and steamed, in a big pot, over the fire; and drinks of corn, thick, and warm, made from ground corn, and water, sometimes with chilli, or honey, or cacao, drunk from a gourd, in the morning, and carried to the fields, in a little ball of dough, to be mixed with water, from a stream, at midday.

[long-pause]

The corn is always cooked with lime, or wood ash, first, softened overnight, in a pot of water, with a little lime, made from burnt limestone, or shells. This makes it softer, and easier to grind, and much more nourishing. It is one of the great discoveries of the peoples of Mexico, and Central America, thousands of years old, and it is still done today.

[pause]

With the corn, there are beans, black, and red, and squash, and its seeds, toasted, and ground, and chilli peppers, of many kinds, and tomatoes, and avocados.

[pause]

{img:maya-feast-foods-turkey-tamales-squash-fruits-painted-plates-mats}
For meat, there is the deer, which the hunters bring back, from the forest, and the peccary, and the wild turkey, and the tame turkey, which the families keep, in their courtyards, and ducks, and dogs, a special small breed, which the Maya keep, and fatten, for feasts. There are rabbits, and armadillos, and iguanas, and fish, from the rivers, and the lakes, and snails.

[long-pause]

There is honey, from the stingless bees, little bees, without a sting, which the Maya keep, in hollow logs, hung in the shade, around their houses, and which make a thin, sweet, delicious honey. The Maya beekeepers still keep them, today, in the Yucatan, though they are becoming rare.

[pause]

There are fruits: avocados, and papayas, and guavas, and the sapodilla fruit, sweet, and brown, and the mamey, with its orange flesh, and the custard apple, and the hog plum, and many others, which grow in the gardens, and the forest, around the houses.

[long-pause]

At feasts, there is much more: turkeys, roasted, and tamales, of many kinds, filled with meat, and beans, and decorated, and great pots of chocolate, and a drink, called balché, made of honey, and water, and the bark of a tree, fermented, which is drunk at ceremonies.

[pause]

{img:maya-woman-cooking-tortillas-clay-griddle-comal-smoke-morning}
The cooking is done in the kitchen house, over the fire, of three stones, by the women, who are also the ones who know the recipes, and the plants, and the herbs, and the medicines, and pass them on, to their daughters, and granddaughters.

[long-pause]

The pots are made of clay, big ones, for storing water, and corn, and cooking tamales, and small ones, for sauces, and chillies. The plates, and bowls, are of clay too, and gourds, cut in half, and dried, make cups, and bowls, and dippers, and bottles.

[pause]

And so, every day, in thousands of kitchen houses, all over Tikal, the smoke rises, through the thatch, and the smell of corn, and woodsmoke, and chilli, drifts over the city, mixed with the scent of the flowers, and the forest.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-scribe-painting-folding-bark-paper-book-brush-shell-inkpot}
The Maya can write.

[pause]

Of all the peoples of the ancient Americas, the Maya had the most complete writing system: a script that could write down anything that could be said, in their language, with names, and dates, and stories, and poems.

[long-pause]

Maya writing is made of glyphs: little square pictures, rounded at the corners, each one like a tiny, complicated painting, of a face, or an animal, or a hand, or an object, with little signs attached to it. Some glyphs stand for whole words: a jaguar's head for the word jaguar, balam. Others stand for sounds, syllables, like ba, or la, or ma. And a scribe can write the same word in many different ways, sometimes with a picture, sometimes with syllables, sometimes with both, as he likes, to make his writing beautiful, and interesting.

[pause]

There are more than a thousand different glyphs. They are written in pairs of columns, read from left to right, and from top to bottom.

[pause]

The scribes are important people, nobles, sometimes even princes, highly trained, and greatly respected. They are artists, and writers, and astronomers, and priests, all at once. They have a god of their own, a monkey god, the patron of writing, and art.

[long-pause]

They paint with brushes, and with pens, made of feathers, and reeds, and their inkpots are cut from shells. In paintings, scribes are shown with a little bundle of brushes, or pens, tucked into their headdresses.

[pause]

They write on stone, carved, on stelae, and altars, and lintels, and stairways, and on painted pottery, and on carved jade, and bone, and shell, and wood. And they write books.

[long-pause]

The Maya books are called codices. They are made of paper, from the inner bark of the fig tree, beaten flat, and covered with a smooth white coating of plaster, and folded, like a screen, or an accordion, between covers of wood, or jaguar skin, so that they can be opened, and read, page by page, or spread out, all at once, to many metres long.

[pause]

The pages are painted, on both sides, with glyphs, and pictures, in black and red, and blue, and other colours. There are books of history, and of the gods, and of astronomy, and calendars, and prophecies, and medicine, and songs.

[pause]

{img:maya-codex-pages-unfolded-red-black-glyphs-figures-calendar}
There must have been thousands of these books, in every Maya city, in the libraries of the kings, and the temples. But they were fragile, and the climate is warm, and damp, and almost all of them have been lost, to time, and insects, and decay, and, in the sixteenth century, to the Spanish, who burned many, because they did not understand them.

[pause]

Today, only four Maya books survive, all from later times, and all far from Tikal: the Dresden Codex, in Germany, the Madrid Codex, in Spain, the Paris Codex, in France, and the Maya Codex of Mexico, in Mexico City.

[long-pause]

For hundreds of years, nobody could read the Maya writing.

[pause]

Then, slowly, in the twentieth century, scholars began to understand it. First, the numbers, and the dates. Then, in the 1950s, a Russian scholar, Yuri Knorozov, showed that many of the glyphs stood for sounds. Then, in the 1960s, Tatiana Proskouriakoff, a Russian-born American scholar, studying the dates on the monuments, showed that they told the histories of real kings, and queens: their births, their coming to the throne, their wars, and their deaths. And in the 1970s and 1980s, scholars, meeting together at Palenque, and elsewhere, began to read whole sentences.

[pause]

{img:scholar-desk-notebook-glyph-drawings-lamp-twentieth-century}
Today, most Maya inscriptions can be read. And the stones of Tikal, and the other cities, have begun to speak again, with the names, and the stories, of their kings, and their people.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-calendar-stone-carved-day-signs-circle-numbers-bars-dots}
The Maya are fascinated by time.

[pause]

They watch the sun, and the moon, and the stars, and the planets, and count the days, with wonderful care, and record them, on their monuments, and in their books. And they have not one calendar, but several, all turning together, like the wheels of a great clock.

[long-pause]

First, the Maya count numbers in a very simple way: a dot for one, and a bar for five. So two is two dots, and seven is a bar and two dots, and nineteen is three bars and four dots. And they have a sign for zero, a little shell, one of the first peoples in the world to use the idea of zero.

[pause]

They count in twenties, not in tens, as we do, perhaps because people have twenty fingers and toes.

[pause]

{img:bar-dot-numbers-carved-stone-shell-zero-sign}
The first calendar is the sacred calendar, the count of two hundred and sixty days.

[long-pause]

It has twenty named days, Imix, Ik', Ak'bal, K'an, and so on, each with its own sign, and its own god, and its own meaning, turning together with the numbers one to thirteen. So the days go: 1 Imix, 2 Ik', 3 Ak'bal, and so on, until, after two hundred and sixty days, the same combination comes round again.

[pause]

This calendar is used to name children, and to choose the right days for planting, and for ceremonies, and for weddings, and to tell the future. It is still used today, by Maya daykeepers, in the highlands of Guatemala.

[long-pause]

The second calendar follows the sun: a year of three hundred and sixty five days, divided into eighteen months, of twenty days each, with five extra days, at the end, which are unlucky, nameless days, when people stay at home, and are careful, and do not start anything new.

[pause]

The two calendars turn together, like two gears, and the same combination of a day, in the sacred calendar, and a day, in the solar year, comes round only once every fifty-two years. This is the Calendar Round, a great cycle, like a century, for the Maya.

[pause]

{img:priest-astronomer-temple-top-dawn-watching-sunrise-horizon}
And then, there is the Long Count.

[long-pause]

The Long Count is the great calendar of the Maya monuments. It counts the days, one by one, from the beginning of the present world, which, the Maya believed, was created on a day in the year 3114 BC, in our calendar.

[pause]

It counts them in days, and twenties of days, and years of three hundred and sixty days, called tuns, and twenties of years, called k'atuns, and four hundred years, called b'ak'tuns. So every date, on a Maya monument, tells exactly how many days have passed since the creation: for example, nine b'ak'tuns, fifteen k'atuns, eighteen tuns, and so on.

[pause]

The end of every k'atun, every twenty years, is a great festival, at Tikal. The kings build special groups of buildings, twin pyramids, facing each other, east and west, across a plaza, to celebrate it, with a stela, and an altar, in a little enclosure, on the north side, and a building with nine doorways, on the south.

[pause]

{img:twin-pyramid-complex-tikal-katun-ending-stela-enclosure-plaza}
In 2012, many people around the world believed that the Maya had predicted that the world would end, on the twenty-first of December, because, on that day, the Long Count reached the end of the thirteenth b'ak'tun.

[long-pause]

But the Maya never said that. For them, it was simply the end of a great cycle, and the beginning of a new one, like the turning of a great wheel, or the beginning of a new millennium. And indeed, a Maya inscription, at the city of Palenque, speaks of a date, far in the future, in the year 4772.

[pause]

And on the twenty-second of December, 2012, the sun rose as usual, over the temples of Tikal, where thousands of people, and Maya priests, had gathered, to celebrate the new beginning.

[long-pause]

{img:night-sky-milky-way-over-tikal-temples-silhouette-stars}
At night, over Tikal, the sky is full of stars.

[pause]

There are no city lights, and no streetlamps, only the little fires in the houses, and the torches on the temples, and the sky is black, and deep, and clear, and filled with stars, thousands and thousands of them, and the great, glowing band of the Milky Way, stretching across the sky, from one side to the other.

[long-pause]

For the Maya, the Milky Way is the world tree, the great ceiba, rising up into the sky, and also the road to the underworld, the white road, along which the souls of the dead travel. In some seasons, it stands upright, from north to south. In others, it lies across the sky, like a great canoe, carrying the maize god.

[pause]

The Maya astronomers watch the sky, every night, from the tops of the temples, and from special buildings, and they know the movements of the sun, and the moon, and the stars, and the planets, very well.

[pause]

{img:venus-morning-star-bright-dawn-sky-over-forest-temple}
Most of all, they watch Venus.

[long-pause]

Venus is the brightest of the planets, the morning star, and the evening star, which sometimes rises before the sun, at dawn, and sometimes shines after the sun, in the evening, and sometimes disappears, for a while, into the light of the sun.

[pause]

The Maya astronomers counted that Venus goes through its whole cycle, from morning star to evening star, and back, in five hundred and eighty-four days. In the Dresden Codex, there are tables of the movements of Venus, accurate to within a few hours over many years. They are among the most remarkable achievements of ancient astronomy, anywhere in the world.

[long-pause]

The Maya also predicted eclipses of the sun and the moon. In the Dresden Codex, there are tables that show the days when an eclipse might happen, so that the priests could warn the people, and make the right ceremonies.

[pause]

They knew the length of the year, and of the month of the moon, very accurately. And they watched the solstices, and the equinoxes, when the sun rises at its furthest north, or south, or due east.

[pause]

{img:maya-astronomers-observing-sky-temple-platform-night-torches-crossed-sticks}
At Uaxactun, a city near Tikal, there is a group of buildings, a pyramid, and, opposite, a long platform, with three small temples, arranged so that, standing on the pyramid, one can see the sun rise behind the northern temple, at the summer solstice, behind the middle one, at the equinoxes, and behind the southern one, at the winter solstice. Archaeologists still call groups like this E-Groups, after this one, Group E at Uaxactun, and there is an even older one, in the Lost World group, at Tikal.

[long-pause]

For the Maya, the sky is not empty. It is full of gods, and stories. The sun, and the moon, and Venus, and the stars, are gods, and heroes, moving across the sky, and through the underworld, below the earth, at night. And the kings, on the tops of their temples, in clouds of incense, at the right moment, on the right day, are taking part in the great movements of the heavens.

[pause]

Tonight, the stars over Tikal are the same as they were in 750. The Milky Way still rises over the temples, and Venus still shines, at dawn, over the forest.

[long-pause]

{img:tikal-night-moonlight-great-plaza-temples-torches-quiet}
Let us stay in Tikal for a while, at night.

[pause]

The sun has set, behind the Temple of the Masks, and the sky over the forest has turned from gold, to red, to deep blue, and then to black. The heat of the day has gone, and the air is soft, and warm, and damp, and smells of flowers, and smoke, and wet leaves.

[long-pause]

In the Great Plaza, a few torches are burning, on the stairways of the temples, where the priests keep watch, and the guards stand, at the entrances of the palace. The white plaster of the plaza glows faintly, in the moonlight, and the great temples rise up, dark, against the stars.

[pause]

In the palace, the courtyards are quiet now. The last visitors have gone. The servants are clearing away the cups, and the plates. Behind the curtains, in the narrow, vaulted rooms, the royal family is going to sleep, on their wide stone benches, on their mats, and cushions.

[pause]

{img:village-houses-night-glowing-fires-through-doorways-forest-dark}
Out in the countryside, around the city, in thousands of little houses, the fires are burning low, and the families are lying down, in their hammocks, and on their mats, wrapped in their blankets, and the grandmothers are telling the last story of the evening, to the children, half asleep.

[long-pause]

The dogs are curled up, by the doorways. The turkeys are roosting, in the trees. In the gardens, the night flowers are opening, white, and sweet-smelling, and the moths are coming to them.

[pause]

And in the forest, all around, the night creatures are awake: the owls, and the bats, flitting over the reservoirs, and the frogs, singing, and the crickets, and the kinkajous, and the night monkeys, moving softly through the branches, and, somewhere, far away, the jaguar, walking silently, along a forest path, under the stars.

[pause]

{img:fireflies-over-reservoir-night-water-lilies-temple-silhouette}
Over the reservoirs, the fireflies are flickering, little green lights, drifting over the still, black water, among the water lilies, which have closed their flowers, for the night.

[long-pause]

And on the top of one of the temples, a priest is sitting, alone, watching the sky, waiting for Venus to rise, before the dawn, counting the days, and the stars, while the whole city sleeps, below him, in the great, dark, quiet forest.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-ballcourt-two-sloping-walls-plaza-tikal-afternoon}
In the heart of Tikal, near the Great Plaza, between the Central Acropolis and Temple One, there is a ball court.

[pause]

It is a long, narrow alley, paved with plaster, between two long, low buildings, with sloping walls, facing each other, painted red, and white. And here, the Maya play their great game, the ballgame.

[long-pause]

The ballgame is one of the oldest games in the world. It was played all over Mexico, and Central America, for more than three thousand years, by the Olmecs, and the Maya, and the Aztecs, and many other peoples, and there are still places in Mexico where a version of it is played today.

[pause]

It is played with a ball of solid rubber: heavy, and hard, and bouncy, about the size of a large melon, or a little bigger, made from the sap of the rubber tree. The Maya were among the first people in the world to make rubber, long before anyone in Europe had ever seen it.

[pause]

The players must keep the ball in the air, and send it to the other side of the court, bouncing it off the sloping walls, but they must not touch it with their hands, or their feet. They hit it with their hips, and their thighs, and their arms, and so they wear thick, padded belts, called yokes, around their waists, made of wood, and leather, and cotton, and pads on their knees, and arms, to protect them from the heavy ball.

[long-pause]

It is a fast, and difficult, and exciting game, and the crowds love to watch it, and cheer, and make bets, on their favourite players, with cacao beans, and cloth, and jade, and feathers.

[pause]

And the game is also sacred. For the Maya, the ball is like the sun, and the moon, moving across the sky, and through the underworld, and the game is like the great struggle between day and night, life and death, light and darkness.

[pause]

{img:hero-twins-playing-ball-underworld-lords-mythic-scene-painted-vase}
In the Popol Vuh, there is a great story about the ballgame.

[long-pause]

Long ago, there were two brothers, Hun Hunahpu and Vucub Hunahpu, who loved to play ball. But they played so noisily, that the lords of the underworld, Xibalba, below the earth, were disturbed, and invited them down, to play against them. And the lords of Xibalba tricked them, and they never came back.

[pause]

But the son of Hun Hunahpu, born later, had two sons of his own, twins: Hunahpu and Xbalanque, the Hero Twins. And they, too, loved to play ball. And they, too, were invited down to Xibalba.

[long-pause]

But the Hero Twins were clever. They passed through all the tests, and the tricks, of the lords of Xibalba: the House of Darkness, the House of Cold, the House of Jaguars, the House of Bats. They played ball against the lords, and outwitted them, again and again, and, in the end, they defeated them.

[pause]

And then, the twins rose up into the sky, and became the sun and the moon. And their father, the maize god, was born again, from the earth.

[pause]

{img:ballcourt-marker-carved-stone-disc-ballplayer-glyphs}
The game, at Tikal, is sometimes played for fun, and sometimes as a great ceremony. And sometimes, the stones tell us, the losers were prisoners of war, and the game ended in a sacrifice, but that was rare, and long ago, and we will leave it there, gently, in the past.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the ball court is quiet again. The players have gone home, to rest their bruises, and the crowds have gone, and the long, narrow court lies empty, in the fading light, with the red walls glowing, and the shadows of the temples falling across it.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-jade-mosaic-mask-green-shell-eyes-museum-like-light}
The Maya are wonderful artists.

[pause]

Of all their treasures, the most precious is jade: a hard, smooth, green stone, from far away, from the valley of the Motagua river, in the south of Guatemala. For the Maya, jade is more precious than gold. Its green colour is the colour of the young corn, and the quetzal's feathers, and water, and life itself.

[long-pause]

The Maya jade carvers work the stone, with great patience, using cords, and sand, and water, and bits of harder stone, and bone, sawing, and drilling, and polishing, for days, and weeks, and months. They make beads, and necklaces, and ear ornaments, and pendants, and little figures, of gods, and kings, and plaques, carved with portraits, and glyphs.

[pause]

And they make mosaic masks, of hundreds of little pieces of jade, fitted together, into a face, with eyes of shell, and obsidian, which were placed over the faces of the kings, when they were buried, so that they would be reborn, like the maize god, with a face of everlasting green.

[pause]

{img:resplendent-quetzal-bird-long-green-tail-feathers-cloud-forest-branch}
After jade, the most precious thing is quetzal feathers.

[long-pause]

The resplendent quetzal is a bird of the cloud forests, in the mountains to the south: a small bird, with a red breast, and a body of brilliant, shimmering green, and two long tail feathers, almost a metre long, which shine, in the sunlight, from green to blue, to gold.

[pause]

Only the kings, and the greatest nobles, may wear them, in great, nodding headdresses. The quetzal is still the national bird of Guatemala, and its money is called the quetzal.

[long-pause]

The Maya also work feathers of many other birds, macaws, and parrots, and hummingbirds, and herons, into headdresses, and capes, and fans, and shields, and banners, in brilliant colours.

[pause]

And they make wonderful pottery.

[pause]

{img:maya-potter-shaping-painting-cylinder-vessel-courtyard-brushes}
The Maya potters do not use a potter's wheel. They build their pots by hand, from coils of clay, and smooth them, and polish them, and fire them, in open fires. And the painters paint them, with fine brushes, in red, and orange, and black, and cream, with pictures of the gods, and the kings, and their courts, and the animals, and the stories of the myths, and lines of glyphs, around the rim.

[long-pause]

The most beautiful of all are the cylinder vases: tall, round, straight-sided vessels, for drinking chocolate, painted all around, like a picture that unrolls, as you turn it in your hands. They show scenes from the life of the court: the king on his throne, receiving visitors, and gifts, with his nobles, and his dwarves, and his musicians, and his dogs, and his pots of chocolate. Or the gods, and the hero twins, and the underworld.

[pause]

And they are signed. Some Maya painters wrote their names on their pots, so that, after more than a thousand years, we know the names of a few of them.

[pause]

{img:carved-wooden-lintel-temple-doorway-king-jaguar-protector-sapodilla}
At Tikal, there are also wonderful wood carvings.

[long-pause]

The doorways of the temples, on the tops of the pyramids, have lintels, beams across the top of the doorways, made of the hard, red wood of the sapodilla tree, carved, on the underside, with wonderful scenes: the king, on his throne, with a great jaguar, or a great serpent, rising behind him, to protect him, and lines of glyphs, telling the story.

[pause]

Several of these lintels, from Temples One and Four, were taken away, in the nineteenth century, and are now in a museum in Basel, in Switzerland, where they can still be seen. But some are still in place, high up, on the temples, in the forest.

[long-pause]

And there are carvings in stone, and paintings on the walls, and sculptures of plaster, on the fronts of the temples: great masks, of gods, with huge faces, and eyes, and fangs, and ear ornaments, painted red, and white, and black, which looked out over the plazas, and the people, and the forest.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-noblewoman-white-huipil-embroidered-jade-necklace-hair-ribbons}
How do the people of Tikal look, and dress?

[pause]

The Maya of the year 750 are not very tall, with brown skin, and black hair, and dark eyes, and they think a long, sloping forehead, and a long nose, are very beautiful, so some of them add a little extra bridge to their noses, of clay, or plaster.

[long-pause]

The ordinary men wear a loincloth, a long strip of cotton cloth, wound around the waist, and between the legs, with the ends hanging down, in front, and behind, sometimes embroidered, or decorated, at the ends. Sometimes they wear a cape, or a blanket, around their shoulders, in the cool of the evening, and sandals of leather, or fibre, on their feet.

[pause]

The women wear a long skirt, wrapped around the waist, and a loose blouse, or a long dress, of cotton, the huipil, white, or coloured, and woven, or embroidered, with patterns, and a cloth, or shawl, over their shoulders, or their heads.

[pause]

{img:maya-man-loincloth-cape-sandals-hair-tied-feather-market}
The men wear their hair long, tied up on top of their heads, or in braids, with a bare patch, sometimes, on the top. The women wear their hair long, too, braided, and wound around their heads, with ribbons of coloured cloth.

[long-pause]

Both men and women love jewellery: necklaces, and bracelets, and anklets, of beads, of shell, and stone, and seeds, and, for the nobles, jade. And ear ornaments, called ear flares: great round discs, of jade, or shell, or wood, through holes, in their ear lobes, which are stretched, gradually, from childhood, to hold them.

[pause]

Some people decorate their teeth, filing them into little shapes, or drilling tiny holes, in their front teeth, and filling them with little, shining pieces of jade, or pyrite. Some paint their bodies, and their faces, in red, and black, and blue. And some have tattoos.

[long-pause]

The nobles, and the king, dress much more richly: in fine cotton, dyed in deep colours, and embroidered, and painted, with patterns, and capes, of jaguar skin, and feathers, and belts, with little masks, of jade, and great collars of jade beads, and tall headdresses, built on frames of wood, and wicker, with masks of gods, and animals, and great sprays of quetzal feathers, nodding, and shimmering, as they move.

[pause]

{img:king-full-regalia-quetzal-feather-headdress-jade-collar-jaguar-kilt-stela}
At the great ceremonies, the king is almost hidden by his costume: his headdress, as tall as he is, his jade, his jaguar skins, his feathers, his ornaments, his sceptre. He no longer looks like an ordinary man, but like a god, standing on the top of the temple, in the clouds of incense, shining, in the sun.

[long-pause]

And the colours are everywhere: the deep red of the buildings, and the clothes, the green of the jade, and the feathers, the blue of the paint, made from a clay and indigo, a beautiful, bright blue, which archaeologists call Maya blue, which has lasted, unfaded, for more than a thousand years, the white of the plaster, and the cotton, the black, and the yellow. Tikal, in the year 750, is not a grey city of stone, but a city full of colour.

[long-pause]

{img:world-tree-ceiba-cosmos-roots-underworld-branches-heaven-bird-painted}
The Maya world is full of gods.

[pause]

For the Maya, the world has three levels. Above, there are the thirteen layers of the heavens, where the sun, and the moon, and the stars, and the gods of the sky live. In the middle, there is the earth, where people live, flat, and square, with four corners, and four sides, each with its own colour: red for the east, where the sun rises, black for the west, where it sets, white for the north, and yellow for the south. And below, there are the nine layers of the underworld, Xibalba, the place of fear, ruled by the lords of death.

[long-pause]

And at the centre of the world, joining the three levels together, there is the great world tree, the ceiba, the colour green, with its roots in the underworld, and its trunk in the middle world, and its branches in the heavens, and a great bird, sitting in its top.

[pause]

The temples of Tikal are like sacred mountains, rising toward the heavens. And the doorways of the temples are like caves, leading into the mountain, and the underworld. And the king, standing on the top of the temple, in the doorway, between the heavens and the underworld, is like the world tree, joining them all together.

[pause]

{img:chaak-rain-god-axe-lightning-clouds-mask-temple-facade}
There are many gods.

[long-pause]

There is Itzamnaaj, the old god, the wise one, the first priest, and the first scribe, the lord of the heavens. There is K'inich Ajaw, the sun god, with his great, squinting eyes, and his front teeth filed into a T shape, who travels across the sky, by day, and through the underworld, by night, as a jaguar.

[pause]

There is Chaak, the rain god, with his long nose, and his axe, which is lightning, with which he strikes the clouds, and makes the rain fall. He is perhaps the most important god of all, for the farmers, because, without rain, the corn will not grow. There is the young maize god. There is Ix Chel, the old goddess of the moon, and of weaving, and of childbirth, and medicine. There is K'awiil, the god of lightning, and power, and abundance, with one leg in the shape of a serpent, whose name is in the names of many kings. Jasaw Chan K'awiil means, perhaps, K'awiil who clears the sky.

[long-pause]

The gods must be fed, and honoured, and thanked, with offerings: with incense, of copal resin, burned in clay censers, so that the smoke rises up, to the heavens, in great white clouds, smelling sweet, and resinous; and with food, and chocolate, and flowers, and jade, and feathers, and songs, and dances, and prayers.

[pause]

{img:copal-incense-smoke-clay-censer-temple-doorway-offerings-flowers}
And with blood. The kings, and the queens, on great occasions, offer a few drops of their own blood, on strips of paper, which are burned, so that the smoke rises up, to the ancestors, and the gods. It is the most sacred offering of all, because it is the gift of life itself, and the kings are the ones who must give it, for their people.

[long-pause]

The Maya also honour their ancestors. The dead are buried under the floors of the houses, or near them, so that they stay close to the family. And the great kings are buried in the temples, and their descendants come to make offerings, and to speak with them, and to ask for their help.

[pause]

There are spirits everywhere too: in the mountains, and the caves, and the springs, and the trees, and the fields, and the forest. Every person has a spirit companion, an animal, who shares their soul, and their fate. And the kings have the most powerful of all: jaguars, and serpents, and fiery beings.

[pause]

{img:maya-dance-ceremony-plaza-masked-dancers-drums-conch-trumpets}
And there is music, at every ceremony: the beat of drums, of wood, and turtle shells, and the call of great conch shell trumpets, and long wooden trumpets, and flutes, and rattles, and whistles, and singing, and dancing, in the plazas, before the temples, with the dancers in masks, and feathers, and costumes of the gods.

[long-pause]

Many of the old beliefs live on, today, among the Maya people, mixed with Christianity. In the villages of Guatemala, and Mexico, people still burn copal, and pray to the saints, and to the old spirits, of the mountains, and the earth, and the rain, and the corn, and count the days of the sacred calendar.

[long-pause]

{img:katun-ceremony-king-twin-pyramid-plaza-crowds-incense-dawn}
In the year 751, a great day is coming, in Tikal.

[pause]

It is the end of a k'atun, a period of twenty years, in the Long Count: the day when the count will reach nine b'ak'tuns, sixteen k'atuns, and zero tuns, zero twenties, and zero days. For the Maya, it is like the beginning of a new century, for us. And at Tikal, the end of every k'atun is celebrated, with a great festival.

[long-pause]

For months before, the people prepare.

[pause]

The king's builders build a new twin pyramid complex: two flat-topped pyramids, facing each other, one on the east, and one on the west, across a plaza, with stairways on all four sides. On the north side, there is a small enclosure, with walls, but no roof, open to the sky, with a carved stela, showing the king, and an altar. And on the south side, a long, low building, with nine doorways, for the nine levels of the underworld.

[pause]

{img:twin-pyramid-flat-top-four-stairways-plaster-new-white-red}
The stone carvers carve the new stela, with the portrait of the king, in his finest costume, scattering drops, perhaps of incense, or seeds, or blood, from his hand, and the glyphs, telling the date, and the name of the king, and his parents, and the ceremonies he performed.

[long-pause]

On the great day, everyone comes. People come from all over the city, and from the villages around, and from other cities, in their best clothes, with flowers, and offerings.

[pause]

At dawn, the king, and the queen, and the nobles, and the priests, in their richest costumes, with towering headdresses, of quetzal feathers, and jade, and jaguar skins, come in procession, along the white road, from the Great Plaza, to the new twin pyramids, with music, of drums, and trumpets, and conch shells, and rattles, and singing, and clouds of copal incense, rising into the morning air.

[long-pause]

The king climbs the eastern pyramid, as the sun rises, and makes offerings to the sun, and the gods. And then, he climbs the western pyramid, where the sun will set. He goes into the enclosure, on the north side, and stands before the new stela, and the altar, and makes the offerings that end the k'atun: incense, and blood, and jade, and he scatters offerings, to the gods, and the ancestors.

[pause]

{img:festival-plaza-dancers-feather-costumes-drums-crowd-afternoon}
And then, there is dancing, and feasting, and music, all day long, and into the night. The dancers, in their masks, and costumes of the gods, and animals, dance in the plazas, and the people eat, and drink, and talk, and celebrate, and the smell of roasting turkey, and tamales, and chocolate, and incense, fills the air.

[long-pause]

And the people of Tikal feel that the world has been renewed. The old k'atun has ended, safely, and a new one has begun, and the king has done what he must, to keep the world turning, and the sun rising, and the rain falling, and the corn growing.

[pause]

That stela, put up for this k'atun, still stands at Tikal, today, in its enclosure, at the twin pyramid complex, which archaeologists call Group P. Nine of these twin pyramid complexes have been found, at Tikal, each one built for the end of a k'atun, over about a hundred and thirty years. They are found almost nowhere else in the Maya world.

[long-pause]

When the festival is over, and the people have gone home, the new pyramids stand, gleaming, and white, and red, in the moonlight, in the silence, until, twenty years later, the king's son will build another, for the next k'atun.

[long-pause]

{img:tikal-reservoir-still-water-reflecting-temple-water-lilies-evening}
Tikal has one great problem: water.

[pause]

It rains a great deal, in the rainy season, from May or June until December. But then, for five or six months, from January to May, it hardly rains at all. And there are no rivers, near Tikal, and no lakes, and few springs. The ground is limestone, and the rainwater soaks away through it, quickly, deep underground.

[long-pause]

So the people of Tikal must catch the rain, and keep it, all year round. And they have become masters of water.

[pause]

The whole centre of the city is built so that the rain, falling on the great plazas, and the temples, and the courtyards, all paved with plaster, runs off, into channels, and down the causeways, into great reservoirs, dug into the earth, and lined with clay, to keep the water in.

[pause]

{img:plaster-plaza-rain-running-channels-toward-reservoir-storm}
There are about ten great reservoirs, in the centre of Tikal, and many smaller ones, all around: the Palace Reservoir, the Temple Reservoir, the Causeway Reservoir, and others. Together, they can hold millions of litres of water, enough for the whole city, through the long dry season.

[long-pause]

The reservoirs are carefully built, and carefully kept. Some have dams, and sluice gates, to control the water. And recently, archaeologists found that the people of Tikal even filtered their drinking water, through layers of sand, and of a special mineral, called zeolite, brought from many kilometres away, which cleaned the water, and made it safe to drink. It is one of the oldest water filters known in the world.

[pause]

The water in the reservoirs is clear, and still, and green, and covered with water lilies, with their round, flat leaves, and their white flowers, which open in the morning. And the water lily is a sacred flower, for the Maya, the flower of the waters of the underworld, and of the kings. One of the great kings of Tikal is shown with a water lily, in his headdress, and there is a water lily jaguar, a god.

[pause]

{img:maya-woman-carrying-water-jar-head-path-reservoir-morning}
Every morning, the women of Tikal, and the girls, go down to the reservoirs, with their jars, of clay, or their gourds, and fill them, and carry them home, on their heads, or on their hips, and talk, and laugh, and share the news of the day.

[long-pause]

The kings are responsible for the water. The great reservoirs are built by them, next to the temples, and the palaces, and the king, with his ceremonies, and his prayers to the rain god, Chaak, is believed to bring the rain, and keep the water flowing.

[pause]

So when the rains fail, the people look to the king.

[long-pause]

And in the years after 750, the rains will begin to fail.

[pause]

But tonight, in the rainy season of the year 750, the reservoirs are full, and the rain is falling, softly, on the thatched roofs, and the plastered plazas, and the forest, and running, in little streams, along the channels, into the great, still pools, where the frogs are singing.

[long-pause]

{img:rain-falling-over-tikal-forest-temples-grey-clouds-thatched-houses}
Let us listen to the rain over Tikal.

[pause]

In May, or June, after the long, hot, dry months, when the sky has been pale, and the air hazy with the smoke of the burning fields, and the earth dry, and dusty, and the reservoirs low, the clouds begin to gather, in the afternoons, great, grey, towering clouds, over the forest.

[long-pause]

The air becomes heavy, and still, and hot. The birds fall silent. And then, there is a rumble of thunder, far away, and the wind rises, and shakes the trees, and the first drops fall, big, and heavy, and warm, on the dry leaves, and the dusty plazas, and the thatched roofs.

[pause]

And then the rain comes, all at once, pouring down, roaring, on the forest, and the roofs, and the temples, so loud that people have to shout to be heard, and the plazas are covered with water, and the channels run full, and the water rushes into the reservoirs.

[pause]

{img:children-standing-doorway-watching-rain-pouring-courtyard}
The children run out into it, laughing, and stand with their faces turned up to the sky. The farmers stand in their doorways, watching, and give thanks, to Chaak, the rain god, because now, they can plant their corn.

[long-pause]

Every afternoon, for months, the rain comes like this, and then stops, and the sun comes out again, and the forest steams, and drips, and glitters, and smells of wet earth, and leaves, and flowers.

[pause]

The forest becomes greener, and greener. The frogs come out, in their thousands, and sing, all night long, in the reservoirs, and the swamps, and the puddles, a great chorus, croaking, and peeping, and trilling. The bajos, the low, swampy areas, around the city, fill with water, and become shallow lakes, full of birds, herons, and egrets, and ducks.

[long-pause]

In the houses, it is cool, and dim, and the rain drums softly on the thatch, and drips from the edges of the roof, and runs away, into the gardens. The women weave, in the shade of the porches, and the old people sleep, in their hammocks, and the children play, inside, and the dogs curl up, by the fire.

[pause]

{img:mist-rising-forest-after-rain-temple-roof-comb-sunbeams}
The young corn grows, quickly, in the fields, green, and tall, and the beans climb the stalks, and the squash spreads out its great leaves. The farmers go out, between the showers, to weed, and to keep away the birds, and the animals.

[long-pause]

And at night, the rain falls, softly, on the roofs of Tikal, on the thatch of the houses, and the stone of the temples, and the leaves of the great trees, and on the still water of the reservoirs, making little rings, that spread out, and fade.

[pause]

And the people of Tikal fall asleep, to the sound of it, as people everywhere have always done, safe, and dry, and warm, under their roofs, while the rain falls, and falls, all through the night, on the great, dark, green forest.

[long-pause]

{img:maya-traders-walking-forest-path-loads-tumplines-backpacks}
Tikal is a great trading city.

[pause]

The Maya have no horses, no donkeys, no oxen, no wheeled carts. Everything must be carried, on the backs of people, porters, walking along the forest paths, with great loads, in baskets, and bundles, on their backs, held by a strap, a tumpline, across their foreheads. Or in canoes, along the rivers, and the coasts.

[long-pause]

And yet, the Maya trade over enormous distances.

[pause]

From the highlands of Guatemala, to the south, come jade, from the Motagua valley, and obsidian, the sharp, black, volcanic glass, used for knives, and blades, and quetzal feathers, from the cloud forests. From the coasts, come salt, and shells, and dried fish, and stingray spines, and pearls. From the lowlands, come cacao, and cotton, and honey, and wax, and jaguar skins, and the feathers of tropical birds.

[pause]

From far away, in central Mexico, came the green obsidian, of Pachuca, and fine pottery, and the influence of the great city of Teotihuacan.

[long-pause]

In the markets of Tikal, all of these things are traded, together with the everyday things of life: corn, and beans, and chillies, and squash, and fruit, and turkeys, and dogs, and pots, and baskets, and mats, and cloth, and stone tools, and grinding stones, and copal incense.

[pause]

The traders spread their goods on mats, on the ground, in the market plaza, and people walk among them, looking, and talking, and bargaining. There are no coins. People pay with cacao beans, and with lengths of cotton cloth, and beads of jade, and shell, or simply exchange one thing for another.

[long-pause]

The great traders are important people, and travel long distances, in caravans, with many porters, protected by the king, and by their own god, the god of merchants, an old god, with a pack on his back, and a walking stick.

[pause]

{img:river-trade-canoe-dugout-paddlers-goods-jungle-river}
Tikal stands near the middle of the Maya lowlands, between two great river systems, flowing to the Gulf of Mexico, in the west, and the Caribbean, in the east, so it can control the trade between them. This is one reason why it became so rich, and powerful.

[long-pause]

And between the Maya cities, there are roads.

[pause]

The Maya build great raised roads, the sacbeob, the white roads, straight, and wide, and level, raised above the ground, on banks of stones, and covered with white plaster, which gleam in the sunlight, and in the moonlight, and can be followed even at night. Some run inside the cities, joining the great groups of temples. Others run between cities, for many kilometres, through the forest, and across the swamps.

[pause]

{img:sacbe-white-raised-causeway-straight-through-forest-sunlight}
And there are messengers, and ambassadors, running between the cities, with news, and gifts, and messages, from one king to another, and princesses, travelling, with great escorts, to be married, in other cities, and pilgrims, going to great temples, and caves, and sacred places.

[long-pause]

So the people of Tikal, in the year 750, are not alone, in the forest. They live in a great network of cities, dozens of them, large, and small, joined by trade, and roads, and rivers, and marriages, and alliances, and rivalries, and a shared language, and writing, and gods, and calendar: the Maya world.

[long-pause]

{img:tikal-temples-at-sunset-golden-light-forest-quiet-end-of-era}
In the year 750, Tikal is at the height of its glory.

[pause]

But in the hundred and fifty years that follow, something happens, to Tikal, and to all the great Maya cities of the southern lowlands: they are slowly abandoned.

[long-pause]

At Tikal, the great building goes on, for a while. Temple Four is built. Temple Three, the last of the great temples, is finished around the year 810. The last stela, with a date, at Tikal, is put up in the year 869.

[pause]

And then, there are no more dates, no more monuments, no more great temples. The kings disappear. The palaces are empty. The population falls, year by year, as families move away, or do not have children. By the year 950, only a few people still live, among the great buildings, in the forest, and even they leave, in the end.

[pause]

And the same happens in city after city, all over the southern lowlands: Copán, and Palenque, and Calakmul, and many others. Their last monuments are put up, one after another, in the ninth century, and then they fall silent.

[long-pause]

Why?

[pause]

For a long time, it was one of the great mysteries of history. And scholars still discuss it. But today, they think that there was not one single cause, but many, coming together.

[pause]

There were too many people, for the land to feed. The forests had been cut down, for fields, and for fuel, and for the lime, for the plaster of the buildings, which needs a great deal of wood to make. The soil was tired.

[long-pause]

And then, there were droughts.

[pause]

Scientists, studying the mud at the bottom of lakes, and the layers of stalactites, in caves, have found that, between about 800 and 950, there were several long, terrible droughts, in the Maya lowlands, when the rains failed, year after year. The reservoirs dried up. The corn died, in the fields.

[pause]

{img:dry-cracked-reservoir-bed-drought-sky-pale-temples-distant}
And the kings, who were supposed to bring the rain, could not. People lost their faith in them. There were wars between the cities, more and more, as they competed for the land, and the water, and the food that was left. And the trade routes changed, moving to the coasts, and to the north.

[long-pause]

So, slowly, gently, over several generations, the people left. They did not vanish. They moved away, to the north, to the cities of Yucatan, like Chichen Itza, and Uxmal, which flourished after the southern cities had fallen, and to the lakes, and the rivers, and the highlands, and to the coasts.

[pause]

The Maya did not disappear. Their civilisation went on, in other places, and in other forms, for many hundreds of years, until the coming of the Spanish, and beyond. And their descendants, millions of them, still live in Guatemala, and Mexico, and Belize, and Honduras, today.

[pause]

{img:forest-returning-roots-over-stone-stairway-vines-moss-ruins}
But Tikal was left to the forest.

[long-pause]

Slowly, over the centuries, the forest came back. Seeds blew in, on the wind, and were dropped by birds, onto the plazas, and the stairways, and the roofs of the temples. Little plants grew in the cracks of the plaster, and then bushes, and then trees, whose roots pushed into the stones, and broke them apart. The bright red paint faded, and washed away. The thatched houses of the people rotted, and disappeared, leaving only low mounds. The reservoirs filled up with mud. The great pyramids became green hills, covered with trees, with only their roof combs rising above the forest, like grey ghosts.

[pause]

And for nearly a thousand years, the only voices in Tikal were the roars of the howler monkeys, and the calls of the toucans, and the parrots, and the soft sound of the rain.

[long-pause]

{img:explorers-1848-forest-clearing-first-sight-temple-overgrown-machetes}
For hundreds of years, Tikal lay hidden, in the forest.

[pause]

The local people, the chicle gatherers, and the hunters, and the farmers, of the villages around Lake Petén Itzá, knew that there were ruins, somewhere in the forest. But almost nobody else did.

[long-pause]

In 1848, the governor of the region of Petén, Modesto Méndez, and the governor of the town of Flores, Ambrosio Tut, led an expedition into the forest, guided by local men, and found the ruins. Méndez wrote a report, and an artist, Eusebio Lara, made drawings of the temples, and the carvings, which were published, a few years later, in Berlin.

[pause]

They were astonished: great temples, rising out of the forest, higher than the trees, and carved monuments, and palaces, all silent, and overgrown, and covered with moss.

[pause]

In 1877, a Swiss doctor, Gustav Bernoulli, visited Tikal, and had some of the carved wooden lintels taken down, from the temples, and sent to Basel, where they still are.

[long-pause]

Then, in the 1880s, an English explorer, Alfred Maudslay, came to Tikal, with a camera, and took the first photographs of the ruins, on great glass plates. He was a patient, careful man, who spent years photographing, and making plaster casts, of the monuments, of many Maya cities, so that scholars, all over the world, could study the glyphs. His photographs of Tikal, with the great temples, half buried in the forest, still look magical, today.

[pause]

After him came others: Teobert Maler, a German Austrian photographer, and the American archaeologists of the Peabody Museum, of Harvard, and the Carnegie Institution. They mapped the ruins, and drew the monuments, and began to understand the city.

[pause]

{img:archaeologists-1950s-excavation-temple-stairs-workers-field-camp}
And then, in 1956, the University of Pennsylvania began a great project, of digging, and restoring, at Tikal, which lasted fourteen years.

[long-pause]

A little airstrip was built, in the forest, so that people, and supplies, could come in, by plane, because there were no roads. The archaeologists, and hundreds of local workers, cleared the trees, from the great temples, and the plazas, and dug, and mapped, and studied, and repaired.

[pause]

They found the great tomb of Jasaw Chan K'awiil, deep under Temple One, with its jade, and its carved bones. They dug deep into the North Acropolis, and found the older buildings, and the older tombs, one under another, going back more than a thousand years. And they mapped the city around, with its thousands of houses, and found that Tikal had been much greater than anyone had thought.

[long-pause]

After them, Guatemalan archaeologists continued the work, digging, and restoring, the Lost World group, and the twin pyramid complexes, and Temple Five, and many other buildings.

[pause]

{img:temple-iv-top-view-over-forest-canopy-mist-temples-poking-through}
And in 1977, a film crew came to Tikal.

[long-pause]

They climbed to the top of Temple Four, and filmed the view, out over the forest, with the roof combs of the other temples, rising out of the trees, in the distance, in the early morning mist. And this view became part of a famous film, Star Wars, as the secret base of the rebels, on a moon, far away, in another galaxy.

[pause]

So, today, many people who have never heard of the Maya have seen the temples of Tikal, rising out of the forest, without knowing it.

[long-pause]

{img:lake-peten-itza-island-city-nojpeten-temples-canoes-calm-water}
Not far from Tikal, only about thirty kilometres to the south, there is a great lake: Lake Petén Itzá.

[pause]

And on a little island, in the lake, there was a city, called Nojpetén, the capital of a Maya people, the Itza. Their ancestors had come, they said, from the north, from the Yucatan, from the great city of Chichen Itza, centuries after Tikal was abandoned.

[long-pause]

When the Spanish came to Mexico, and Central America, in the sixteenth century, and conquered the Aztecs, and many of the Maya peoples, the Itza, on their island, in the middle of the forest, remained free.

[pause]

In 1525, the Spanish conqueror Hernán Cortés passed by the lake, on a long march through the forest, and visited the king of the Itza, Kan Ek', on his island. He left behind his horse, which was lame, promising to come back for it. He never did. The Itza, who had never seen a horse before, cared for it, and when it died, they made a statue of it, and honoured it, as a god.

[pause]

{img:island-city-white-buildings-lake-shore-reeds-morning-mist}
For more than a hundred and seventy years after the Spanish conquest of Mexico, the Itza kingdom remained independent, the last independent Maya kingdom, protected by the great forest around it.

[long-pause]

Spanish priests came, from time to time, to try to convert them, and were sent away. And the Itza went on, living as their ancestors had lived, with their temples, and their books, and their kings, and their calendar.

[pause]

At last, in 1697, the Spanish came, with soldiers, and boats, and took the island, and the last Maya kingdom came to an end. We will leave that sad day there, gently, in the past.

[long-pause]

Today, the island city is the little town of Flores, the capital of the region of Petén, with its narrow streets, and its colourful houses, and its church, on the top of the island, where the temples once stood, joined to the shore by a causeway. It is where most visitors to Tikal stay, and from where they set out, early in the morning, to see the temples, in the forest.

[pause]

{img:flores-island-town-colourful-houses-lake-sunset-boats}
In the evening, the people of Flores, and the visitors, walk along the edge of the island, beside the lake, and watch the sun set, over the water, in red, and gold, and pink, as the Itza once watched it, from their island city, and as the people of Tikal, long before them, watched it, from the tops of their temples, over the forest.

[long-pause]

{img:tikal-national-park-visitors-climbing-wooden-stairs-temple-iv-morning}
Today, Tikal is a national park, in the north of Guatemala, in the region of Petén.

[pause]

It was made a national park in 1955, and in 1979, it became a World Heritage Site, one of the first in the world, protected both for its ruins, and for its forest, and its animals.

[long-pause]

The park covers more than five hundred square kilometres, of tropical forest, and it lies inside the great Maya Biosphere Reserve, which covers a fifth of Guatemala, the largest tropical forest in Central America, north of the Amazon.

[pause]

Every year, many thousands of visitors come to Tikal, from Guatemala, and from all over the world. They walk along the paths, through the forest, in the shade of the great trees, from one group of temples to another, as the people of Tikal once walked along their white roads.

[pause]

{img:sunrise-temple-iv-top-visitors-sitting-mist-forest-howler-calls}
Some visitors climb to the top of Temple Four, on wooden stairs, before dawn, and sit, in the dark, waiting for the sunrise.

[long-pause]

And slowly, the sky grows lighter, in the east, and the mist rises from the forest, and the roof combs of Temples One, and Two, and Three, appear, grey, and silent, above the trees. And then, the howler monkeys begin to roar, and the birds begin to call, toucans, and parrots, and the whole forest wakes up, around them, as it has done every morning, for thousands of years.

[pause]

The animals have come back to Tikal. There are coatis, wandering across the Great Plaza, with their tails held high. There are spider monkeys, swinging in the trees, above the temples. There are ocellated turkeys, with their shimmering feathers, walking calmly among the visitors. There are toucans, and parrots, and hundreds of other birds. And, deep in the forest, rarely seen, there are still jaguars.

[pause]

The Great Plaza is covered with grass, now, instead of white plaster, and the temples are grey, and the colours have gone. But they are still magnificent.

[long-pause]

And, in 2018, scientists flew over the forest, around Tikal, with LiDAR, the laser that can see through the trees. And they found, hidden under the forest, more than sixty thousand buildings, houses, and palaces, and pyramids, and roads, and fields, and walls, and canals, that nobody had known about, in the region around Tikal and its neighbours. It showed that the Maya lowlands, in the time of Tikal's glory, had been home to millions of people, far more than anyone had thought.

[pause]

There is still much to discover. Most of the buildings of Tikal have never been dug, and lie, still, under the trees, waiting.

[pause]

{img:modern-maya-woman-weaving-backstrap-loom-colourful-huipil-village}
And the Maya are still here.

[long-pause]

More than six million Maya people live in Guatemala today, almost half of the whole country, and millions more in Mexico, and Belize, and Honduras. They speak more than twenty Maya languages, K'iche', and Q'eqchi', and Kaqchikel, and Mam, and many others. Many of them still grow corn, in their milpas, and weave on backstrap looms, and wear their beautiful, embroidered clothes, the huipiles, with patterns that tell where they come from. And some still count the days of the old sacred calendar.

[pause]

At Tikal, on special days, Maya priests, and families, come to the Great Plaza, and burn copal, and candles, in front of the temples, and pray, to the ancestors, and the old gods, as their people have done, for more than two thousand years.

[long-pause]

So we leave Tikal, tonight, as the evening falls.

[pause]

The great temples rise above the forest, grey, and silent, against a sky turning pink, and gold, and then deep blue. The howler monkeys roar, one last time, from the trees. The toucans fly home, across the plaza. The fireflies begin to flicker, among the stones. And the stars come out, one by one, over the roof combs of the temples, and the great white band of the Milky Way, the world tree, rises, slowly, into the sky, as it did over the city of the kings, in the year 750.
