At night, over Tikal, the sky is full of stars.

There are no city lights, and no streetlamps, only the little fires in the houses, and the torches on the temples, and the sky is black, and deep, and clear, and filled with stars, thousands and thousands of them, and the great, glowing band of the Milky Way, stretching across the sky, from one side to the other.

--

For the Maya, the Milky Way is the world tree, the great ceiba, rising up into the sky, and also the road to the underworld, the white road, along which the souls of the dead travel. In some seasons, it stands upright, from north to south. In others, it lies across the sky, like a great canoe, carrying the maize god.

The Maya astronomers watch the sky, every night, from the tops of the temples, and from special buildings, and they know the movements of the sun, and the moon, and the stars, and the planets, very well.

{img:venus-morning-star-bright-dawn-sky-over-forest-temple}
Most of all, they watch Venus.

--

Venus is the brightest of the planets, the morning star, and the evening star, which sometimes rises before the sun, at dawn, and sometimes shines after the sun, in the evening, and sometimes disappears, for a while, into the light of the sun.

The Maya astronomers counted that Venus goes through its whole cycle, from morning star to evening star, and back, in five hundred and eighty-four days. In the Dresden Codex, there are tables of the movements of Venus, accurate to within a few hours over many years. They are among the most remarkable achievements of ancient astronomy, anywhere in the world.

--

The Maya also predicted eclipses of the sun and the moon. In the Dresden Codex, there are tables that show the days when an eclipse might happen, so that the priests could warn the people, and make the right ceremonies.

They knew the length of the year, and of the month of the moon, very accurately. And they watched the solstices, and the equinoxes, when the sun rises at its furthest north, or south, or due east.

{img:maya-astronomers-observing-sky-temple-platform-night-torches-crossed-sticks}
At Uaxactun, a city near Tikal, there is a group of buildings, a pyramid, and, opposite, a long platform, with three small temples, arranged so that, standing on the pyramid, one can see the sun rise behind the northern temple, at the summer solstice, behind the middle one, at the equinoxes, and behind the southern one, at the winter solstice. Archaeologists still call groups like this E-Groups, after this one, Group E at Uaxactun, and there is an even older one, in the Lost World group, at Tikal.

--

For the Maya, the sky is not empty. It is full of gods, and stories. The sun, and the moon, and Venus, and the stars, are gods, and heroes, moving across the sky, and through the underworld, below the earth, at night. And the kings, on the tops of their temples, in clouds of incense, at the right moment, on the right day, are taking part in the great movements of the heavens.

Tonight, the stars over Tikal are the same as they were in 750. The Milky Way still rises over the temples, and Venus still shines, at dawn, over the forest.
