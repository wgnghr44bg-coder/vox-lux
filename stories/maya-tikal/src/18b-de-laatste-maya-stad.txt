Not far from Tikal, only about thirty kilometres to the south, there is a great lake: Lake Petén Itzá.

And on a little island, in the lake, there was a city, called Nojpetén, the capital of a Maya people, the Itza. Their ancestors had come, they said, from the north, from the Yucatan, from the great city of Chichen Itza, centuries after Tikal was abandoned.

--

When the Spanish came to Mexico, and Central America, in the sixteenth century, and conquered the Aztecs, and many of the Maya peoples, the Itza, on their island, in the middle of the forest, remained free.

In 1525, the Spanish conqueror Hernán Cortés passed by the lake, on a long march through the forest, and visited the king of the Itza, Kan Ek', on his island. He left behind his horse, which was lame, promising to come back for it. He never did. The Itza, who had never seen a horse before, cared for it, and when it died, they made a statue of it, and honoured it, as a god.

{img:island-city-white-buildings-lake-shore-reeds-morning-mist}
For more than a hundred and seventy years after the Spanish conquest of Mexico, the Itza kingdom remained independent, the last independent Maya kingdom, protected by the great forest around it.

--

Spanish priests came, from time to time, to try to convert them, and were sent away. And the Itza went on, living as their ancestors had lived, with their temples, and their books, and their kings, and their calendar.

At last, in 1697, the Spanish came, with soldiers, and boats, and took the island, and the last Maya kingdom came to an end. We will leave that sad day there, gently, in the past.

--

Today, the island city is the little town of Flores, the capital of the region of Petén, with its narrow streets, and its colourful houses, and its church, on the top of the island, where the temples once stood, joined to the shore by a causeway. It is where most visitors to Tikal stay, and from where they set out, early in the morning, to see the temples, in the forest.

{img:flores-island-town-colourful-houses-lake-sunset-boats}
In the evening, the people of Flores, and the visitors, walk along the edge of the island, beside the lake, and watch the sun set, over the water, in red, and gold, and pink, as the Itza once watched it, from their island city, and as the people of Tikal, long before them, watched it, from the tops of their temples, over the forest.
