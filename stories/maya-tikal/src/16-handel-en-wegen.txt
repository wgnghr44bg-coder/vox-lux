Tikal is a great trading city.

The Maya have no horses, no donkeys, no oxen, no wheeled carts. Everything must be carried, on the backs of people, porters, walking along the forest paths, with great loads, in baskets, and bundles, on their backs, held by a strap, a tumpline, across their foreheads. Or in canoes, along the rivers, and the coasts.

--

And yet, the Maya trade over enormous distances.

From the highlands of Guatemala, to the south, come jade, from the Motagua valley, and obsidian, the sharp, black, volcanic glass, used for knives, and blades, and quetzal feathers, from the cloud forests. From the coasts, come salt, and shells, and dried fish, and stingray spines, and pearls. From the lowlands, come cacao, and cotton, and honey, and wax, and jaguar skins, and the feathers of tropical birds.

From far away, in central Mexico, came the green obsidian, of Pachuca, and fine pottery, and the influence of the great city of Teotihuacan.

--

In the markets of Tikal, all of these things are traded, together with the everyday things of life: corn, and beans, and chillies, and squash, and fruit, and turkeys, and dogs, and pots, and baskets, and mats, and cloth, and stone tools, and grinding stones, and copal incense.

The traders spread their goods on mats, on the ground, in the market plaza, and people walk among them, looking, and talking, and bargaining. There are no coins. People pay with cacao beans, and with lengths of cotton cloth, and beads of jade, and shell, or simply exchange one thing for another.

--

The great traders are important people, and travel long distances, in caravans, with many porters, protected by the king, and by their own god, the god of merchants, an old god, with a pack on his back, and a walking stick.

{img:river-trade-canoe-dugout-paddlers-goods-jungle-river}
Tikal stands near the middle of the Maya lowlands, between two great river systems, flowing to the Gulf of Mexico, in the west, and the Caribbean, in the east, so it can control the trade between them. This is one reason why it became so rich, and powerful.

--

And between the Maya cities, there are roads.

The Maya build great raised roads, the sacbeob, the white roads, straight, and wide, and level, raised above the ground, on banks of stones, and covered with white plaster, which gleam in the sunlight, and in the moonlight, and can be followed even at night. Some run inside the cities, joining the great groups of temples. Others run between cities, for many kilometres, through the forest, and across the swamps.

{img:sacbe-white-raised-causeway-straight-through-forest-sunlight}
And there are messengers, and ambassadors, running between the cities, with news, and gifts, and messages, from one king to another, and princesses, travelling, with great escorts, to be married, in other cities, and pilgrims, going to great temples, and caves, and sacred places.

--

So the people of Tikal, in the year 750, are not alone, in the forest. They live in a great network of cities, dozens of them, large, and small, joined by trade, and roads, and rivers, and marriages, and alliances, and rivalries, and a shared language, and writing, and gods, and calendar: the Maya world.
