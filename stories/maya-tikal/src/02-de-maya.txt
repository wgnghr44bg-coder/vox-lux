The Maya are a people of Central America, who have lived in the same lands, in southern Mexico, and Guatemala, and Belize, and the western parts of Honduras, and El Salvador, for more than three thousand years.

They are not one empire, ruled by one king. They are many peoples, speaking related languages, living in many cities, each with its own king, its own dynasty, its own gods, and its own history, like the cities of ancient Greece, or the city-states of medieval Italy. They trade with each other, and marry each other, and visit each other's festivals. And they fight each other, sometimes, for power, and tribute, and glory.

--

In the year 750, there are dozens of great Maya cities, and hundreds of smaller ones, scattered across the lowland forests: Tikal, and Calakmul, its great rival, to the north, in Mexico. Palenque, in the west, with its beautiful palace, and its temples, on the edge of the mountains. Copán, in the south east, in Honduras, with its wonderful carved stones. Yaxchilan, on the great river Usumacinta. Caracol, and Naranjo, and Dos Pilas, and many more.

The period from about the year 250 to about the year 900 is called, by archaeologists, the Classic period: the great age of the Maya cities, when the most beautiful temples, and palaces, and carvings, and paintings, and books, were made.

{img:maya-people-gathering-plaza-festival-feathers-cloth-colourful-crowd}
And Tikal is one of the greatest cities of all. People have lived at Tikal for more than a thousand years, since about 800 before Christ, when it was a little village of farmers. By the year 750, it is an enormous city, the capital of a powerful kingdom, which the Maya call Mutul.

--

The Maya call their kings k'uhul ajaw, holy lord. The king is a sacred person, who stands between the people and the gods, and performs the great ceremonies, which keep the world in order, and make the rain fall, and the maize grow.

The Maya of the Classic period wrote down the names of their kings, and the dates of their births, and their accessions, and their wars, and their ceremonies, on great carved stone monuments, called stelae, which stand in the plazas of their cities, in front of the temples.

{img:carved-stela-maya-king-portrait-glyphs-plaza-tikal-sunlight}
For a long time, nobody could read them. But in the last fifty years, scholars have learned to read most of Maya writing. And now, the stones of Tikal speak again, and tell us the names, and the stories, of the people who lived there, nearly thirteen hundred years ago.
