Let us spend a day with an ordinary family of Tikal, in the year 750.

They live, not in the centre of the city, among the temples, but out in the countryside around it, about an hour's walk from the Great Plaza, in a little group of houses, around a small courtyard, on a low platform of earth, and stones, to keep them dry in the rainy season.

--

The houses are simple, and cool. The walls are made of wooden poles, set in the ground, and woven with branches, and covered with clay, mud mixed with grass, and plastered, and painted white. The roofs are steep, and high, made of thick thatch, of palm leaves, which keep out the rain, and the heat of the sun, and let the smoke of the fire drift out. Each house has a single room, with a doorway, but no door, and no windows, and inside, it is dim, and cool, and smells of smoke, and earth, and corn.

Around the courtyard, there are several houses: one for the grandparents, one for each of their married sons and their families, a kitchen house, and a storehouse for corn. Behind them, there are gardens, and fruit trees, and the family's fields, and the forest.

{img:maya-woman-kneeling-grinding-maize-metate-stone-dawn-house}
The day begins in the dark, before dawn, with the sound of the women, grinding corn.

--

Every morning, every woman of Tikal kneels at her grinding stone, the metate: a long, flat, slightly hollowed stone, on the ground, and with another stone, the mano, held in both hands, she grinds the corn, softened overnight in water, with lime, back and forth, back and forth, into a soft, wet dough. It takes hours, every day. It is the sound of every Maya morning, for thousands of years: the soft, steady, scraping, rhythmic sound of the stones.

Then she shapes the dough into balls, and pats them flat, and cooks them on a flat clay griddle, over the fire, of three stones, in the middle of the kitchen house. The three hearthstones are sacred: they recall the three stones the gods set up at the creation of the world, in the stars, in the sky.

--

The men, and the older boys, eat, and then go out to the fields, with their tools, and their gourds of water, and their bundles of food, wrapped in leaves, for the day.

The children help. The little girls help their mothers, and grandmothers, to grind the corn, and fetch water, and look after the little ones, and learn to weave. The little boys go with their fathers, and learn to work in the fields, and to hunt, and to know the plants, and the animals, of the forest.

{img:maya-women-weaving-backstrap-loom-tree-courtyard-cotton-colours}
In the shade of the courtyard, the women weave.

--

Maya women weave on the backstrap loom: a simple loom, with one end tied to a tree, or a post, and the other end to a strap, around the weaver's back, so that, by leaning back, or forward, she can make the threads tight, or loose. They weave cotton, spun by hand, into cloth, white, and dyed in many colours, with dyes from plants, and insects, and shells, red, and blue, and yellow, and purple, with wonderful patterns.

The same kind of loom, and some of the same patterns, are still used, by Maya women, in Guatemala, today.

--

At midday, in the heat, everyone rests, in the shade. In the afternoon, the men come back from the fields. They bathe, in the water of the reservoir, or the stream, or in a little steam bath, and the family eats the main meal of the day, together, in the late afternoon: tamales, and tortillas, and beans, and squash, with chilli, and perhaps some meat, deer, or turkey, or fish.

{img:maya-family-evening-meal-around-fire-house-tamales-night}
And in the evening, as the sun sets, and the howler monkeys roar, from the forest, and the stars come out, the family sits together, around the fire, and the old people tell stories: of the gods, and the creation of the world, and the hero twins, and the ancestors, and the animals, and the spirits of the forest.

--

And then, they sleep, in hammocks, or on mats, on low platforms, wrapped in their cotton blankets, in the dim, smoky warmth of the house, listening to the sounds of the night: the frogs, and the insects, and the rain, on the thatched roof.
