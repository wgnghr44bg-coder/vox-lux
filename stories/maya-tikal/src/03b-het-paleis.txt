Let us go into the palace, the Central Acropolis, on the south side of the Great Plaza.

It is not one great building, like a European palace, but a maze of long, low buildings, with many rooms, around six courtyards, at different levels, joined by stairways, and narrow passages, and doorways. Some of the buildings have two storeys, and some have three.

--

The rooms are narrow, and long, with thick walls, of stone, and plaster, and high, pointed ceilings, made by the Maya arch, the corbel vault, in which each layer of stones sticks out a little further than the one below, until they almost meet, at the top, and are closed with a flat stone. They are cool, and dim, and quiet, even on the hottest days.

In many of the rooms, there are wide stone benches, along the walls, covered with mats, and cushions, and jaguar skins, where people sit, and sleep. Curtains of cotton cloth, painted, and embroidered, hang in the doorways, for privacy, and to keep out the light.

{img:palace-courtyard-nobles-sitting-mats-servants-chocolate-cups-shade}
In the throne room, the king sits on a wide stone bench, a throne, covered with a jaguar skin, with a great cushion behind him, also covered with jaguar skin, and a curtain, which can be drawn back, to show him to his visitors.

--

Before him, the nobles sit, on mats, cross-legged, in their rich clothes, and their jewels, and their headdresses. The ambassadors from other cities come, and bow, and offer their gifts: bundles of cloth, and cacao beans, and jade, and quetzal feathers, and shells, piled up on the floor, before the throne. The scribes sit, with their books, and their brushes, writing down what is given, and said. And the servants come and go, with plates of tamales, and cups of chocolate, and the dwarves, who are thought to be special, and close to the gods, entertain the court.

There are musicians, and dancers, and jesters, and the king's dogs, and pet birds, parrots, and macaws.

--

In the quieter parts of the palace, the royal family lives: the king's wives, and his mother, and his children, and his brothers, and sisters, with their own rooms, and courtyards, and kitchens, and servants.

The young princes are taught by the scribes, and the priests: how to read, and write, the glyphs, and count the days, and watch the stars, and perform the ceremonies, and dance, and speak well, and behave as a king should. And the princesses learn too: to weave the finest cloth, and to take part in the ceremonies, and to read, and perhaps to write.

{img:palace-wall-graffiti-scratched-drawings-temple-figures-plaster}
On the plaster walls of some of the palace rooms, archaeologists have found graffiti: drawings, scratched into the plaster, by people living there, long ago. Little pictures of temples, and people, and processions, and ballplayers, and prisoners, and animals. Drawn, perhaps, by children, or by bored servants, or by guards, waiting for something to happen.

--

They are not great art. But they bring us close to the ordinary people, who lived in these rooms, more than twelve hundred years ago, and who, like people everywhere, scratched a little drawing, on the wall, while they were waiting.

In the evenings, the courtyards of the palace are lit with torches, of pine, and the smell of the smoke, and the copal, and the cooking, drifts through the passages, and the sound of music, and voices, and laughter, rises into the night.
