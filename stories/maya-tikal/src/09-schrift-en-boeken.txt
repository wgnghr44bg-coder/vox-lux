The Maya can write.

Of all the peoples of the ancient Americas, the Maya had the most complete writing system: a script that could write down anything that could be said, in their language, with names, and dates, and stories, and poems.

--

Maya writing is made of glyphs: little square pictures, rounded at the corners, each one like a tiny, complicated painting, of a face, or an animal, or a hand, or an object, with little signs attached to it. Some glyphs stand for whole words: a jaguar's head for the word jaguar, balam. Others stand for sounds, syllables, like ba, or la, or ma. And a scribe can write the same word in many different ways, sometimes with a picture, sometimes with syllables, sometimes with both, as he likes, to make his writing beautiful, and interesting.

There are more than a thousand different glyphs. They are written in pairs of columns, read from left to right, and from top to bottom.

The scribes are important people, nobles, sometimes even princes, highly trained, and greatly respected. They are artists, and writers, and astronomers, and priests, all at once. They have a god of their own, a monkey god, the patron of writing, and art.

--

They paint with brushes, and with pens, made of feathers, and reeds, and their inkpots are cut from shells. In paintings, scribes are shown with a little bundle of brushes, or pens, tucked into their headdresses.

They write on stone, carved, on stelae, and altars, and lintels, and stairways, and on painted pottery, and on carved jade, and bone, and shell, and wood. And they write books.

--

The Maya books are called codices. They are made of paper, from the inner bark of the fig tree, beaten flat, and covered with a smooth white coating of plaster, and folded, like a screen, or an accordion, between covers of wood, or jaguar skin, so that they can be opened, and read, page by page, or spread out, all at once, to many metres long.

The pages are painted, on both sides, with glyphs, and pictures, in black and red, and blue, and other colours. There are books of history, and of the gods, and of astronomy, and calendars, and prophecies, and medicine, and songs.

{img:maya-codex-pages-unfolded-red-black-glyphs-figures-calendar}
There must have been thousands of these books, in every Maya city, in the libraries of the kings, and the temples. But they were fragile, and the climate is warm, and damp, and almost all of them have been lost, to time, and insects, and decay, and, in the sixteenth century, to the Spanish, who burned many, because they did not understand them.

Today, only four Maya books survive, all from later times, and all far from Tikal: the Dresden Codex, in Germany, the Madrid Codex, in Spain, the Paris Codex, in France, and the Maya Codex of Mexico, in Mexico City.

--

For hundreds of years, nobody could read the Maya writing.

Then, slowly, in the twentieth century, scholars began to understand it. First, the numbers, and the dates. Then, in the 1950s, a Russian scholar, Yuri Knorozov, showed that many of the glyphs stood for sounds. Then, in the 1960s, Tatiana Proskouriakoff, a Russian-born American scholar, studying the dates on the monuments, showed that they told the histories of real kings, and queens: their births, their coming to the throne, their wars, and their deaths. And in the 1970s and 1980s, scholars, meeting together at Palenque, and elsewhere, began to read whole sentences.

{img:scholar-desk-notebook-glyph-drawings-lamp-twentieth-century}
Today, most Maya inscriptions can be read. And the stones of Tikal, and the other cities, have begun to speak again, with the names, and the stories, of their kings, and their people.
