In the year 750, Tikal is at the height of its glory.

But in the hundred and fifty years that follow, something happens. It happens to Tikal and to all the great Maya cities of the south. Slowly, people leave them.

--

At Tikal, the building goes on for a while. Temple Four is built. Temple Three, the last of the great temples, is finished around the year 810. The last carved stone with a date is set up in the year 869.

And then there are no more dates. No more stones, no more great temples. The kings disappear. The palaces are empty. Year by year there are fewer people, as families move away. By the year 950, only a few people still live among the great buildings. And in the end, they leave too.

The same happens in city after city all over the south. Copán and Palenque and Calakmul and many more. Their last stones are set up one after another. And then they fall silent.

--

Why?

For a long time it was one of the great mysteries of history. Scholars still talk about it. Today they think there was not one single cause, but many, coming together.

There were too many people for the land to feed. The forests had been cut down. They were cut for fields and for firewood. And they were cut to make plaster for all the buildings. The soil was tired.

--

And then there were droughts.

Scientists have studied the mud at the bottom of lakes. They have studied the stone drips that grow slowly in caves. Both tell the same story. Between about 800 and 950, there were several long droughts in the Maya lands. The rains failed year after year. The ponds dried up. The corn died in the fields.

{img:dry-cracked-reservoir-bed-drought-sky-pale-temples-distant}
The kings were supposed to bring the rain. But they could not. People lost faith in them. The cities quarrelled more and more over the land and water and food that was left. And trade moved away, to the coasts and the north.

--

So slowly and gently, over several generations, the people left. They did not vanish. They moved north, to other Maya cities that grew strong after the southern ones had faded. They moved to the lakes and rivers and mountains and coasts.

The Maya did not disappear. Their way of life went on in other places for many hundreds of years. And their children's children still live in Guatemala and Mexico and Belize and Honduras today. There are millions of them.

{img:forest-returning-roots-over-stone-stairway-vines-moss-ruins}
But Tikal was left to the forest.

--

Slowly, over the centuries, the forest came back. Seeds blew in on the wind. Birds dropped them on the squares and stairways and temple roofs. Little plants grew in the cracks. Then bushes, and then trees. Their roots pushed into the stones and broke them apart. The bright red paint faded and washed away. The straw houses rotted, leaving only low mounds. The ponds filled with mud. The great pyramids became green hills covered with trees. Only the tops of the tallest temples rose above the forest, like grey ghosts.

And for nearly a thousand years, the only voices in Tikal were the howler monkeys, the toucans and the parrots. And the soft sound of the rain.
