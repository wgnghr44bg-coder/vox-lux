In the year 750, Tikal is at the height of its glory.

But in the hundred and fifty years that follow, something happens, to Tikal, and to all the great Maya cities of the southern lowlands: they are slowly abandoned.

--

At Tikal, the great building goes on, for a while. Temple Four is built. Temple Three, the last of the great temples, is finished around the year 810. The last stela, with a date, at Tikal, is put up in the year 869.

And then, there are no more dates, no more monuments, no more great temples. The kings disappear. The palaces are empty. The population falls, year by year, as families move away, or do not have children. By the year 950, only a few people still live, among the great buildings, in the forest, and even they leave, in the end.

And the same happens in city after city, all over the southern lowlands: Copán, and Palenque, and Calakmul, and many others. Their last monuments are put up, one after another, in the ninth century, and then they fall silent.

--

Why?

For a long time, it was one of the great mysteries of history. And scholars still discuss it. But today, they think that there was not one single cause, but many, coming together.

There were too many people, for the land to feed. The forests had been cut down, for fields, and for fuel, and for the lime, for the plaster of the buildings, which needs a great deal of wood to make. The soil was tired.

--

And then, there were droughts.

Scientists, studying the mud at the bottom of lakes, and the layers of stalactites, in caves, have found that, between about 800 and 950, there were several long, terrible droughts, in the Maya lowlands, when the rains failed, year after year. The reservoirs dried up. The corn died, in the fields.

{img:dry-cracked-reservoir-bed-drought-sky-pale-temples-distant}
And the kings, who were supposed to bring the rain, could not. People lost their faith in them. There were wars between the cities, more and more, as they competed for the land, and the water, and the food that was left. And the trade routes changed, moving to the coasts, and to the north.

--

So, slowly, gently, over several generations, the people left. They did not vanish. They moved away, to the north, to the cities of Yucatan, like Chichen Itza, and Uxmal, which flourished after the southern cities had fallen, and to the lakes, and the rivers, and the highlands, and to the coasts.

The Maya did not disappear. Their civilisation went on, in other places, and in other forms, for many hundreds of years, until the coming of the Spanish, and beyond. And their descendants, millions of them, still live in Guatemala, and Mexico, and Belize, and Honduras, today.

{img:forest-returning-roots-over-stone-stairway-vines-moss-ruins}
But Tikal was left to the forest.

--

Slowly, over the centuries, the forest came back. Seeds blew in, on the wind, and were dropped by birds, onto the plazas, and the stairways, and the roofs of the temples. Little plants grew in the cracks of the plaster, and then bushes, and then trees, whose roots pushed into the stones, and broke them apart. The bright red paint faded, and washed away. The thatched houses of the people rotted, and disappeared, leaving only low mounds. The reservoirs filled up with mud. The great pyramids became green hills, covered with trees, with only their roof combs rising above the forest, like grey ghosts.

And for nearly a thousand years, the only voices in Tikal were the roars of the howler monkeys, and the calls of the toucans, and the parrots, and the soft sound of the rain.
