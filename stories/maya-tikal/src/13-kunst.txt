The Maya are wonderful artists.

Of all their treasures, the most precious is jade: a hard, smooth, green stone, from far away, from the valley of the Motagua river, in the south of Guatemala. For the Maya, jade is more precious than gold. Its green colour is the colour of the young corn, and the quetzal's feathers, and water, and life itself.

--

The Maya jade carvers work the stone, with great patience, using cords, and sand, and water, and bits of harder stone, and bone, sawing, and drilling, and polishing, for days, and weeks, and months. They make beads, and necklaces, and ear ornaments, and pendants, and little figures, of gods, and kings, and plaques, carved with portraits, and glyphs.

And they make mosaic masks, of hundreds of little pieces of jade, fitted together, into a face, with eyes of shell, and obsidian, which were placed over the faces of the kings, when they were buried, so that they would be reborn, like the maize god, with a face of everlasting green.

{img:resplendent-quetzal-bird-long-green-tail-feathers-cloud-forest-branch}
After jade, the most precious thing is quetzal feathers.

--

The resplendent quetzal is a bird of the cloud forests, in the mountains to the south: a small bird, with a red breast, and a body of brilliant, shimmering green, and two long tail feathers, almost a metre long, which shine, in the sunlight, from green to blue, to gold.

Only the kings, and the greatest nobles, may wear them, in great, nodding headdresses. The quetzal is still the national bird of Guatemala, and its money is called the quetzal.

--

The Maya also work feathers of many other birds, macaws, and parrots, and hummingbirds, and herons, into headdresses, and capes, and fans, and shields, and banners, in brilliant colours.

And they make wonderful pottery.

{img:maya-potter-shaping-painting-cylinder-vessel-courtyard-brushes}
The Maya potters do not use a potter's wheel. They build their pots by hand, from coils of clay, and smooth them, and polish them, and fire them, in open fires. And the painters paint them, with fine brushes, in red, and orange, and black, and cream, with pictures of the gods, and the kings, and their courts, and the animals, and the stories of the myths, and lines of glyphs, around the rim.

--

The most beautiful of all are the cylinder vases: tall, round, straight-sided vessels, for drinking chocolate, painted all around, like a picture that unrolls, as you turn it in your hands. They show scenes from the life of the court: the king on his throne, receiving visitors, and gifts, with his nobles, and his dwarves, and his musicians, and his dogs, and his pots of chocolate. Or the gods, and the hero twins, and the underworld.

And they are signed. Some Maya painters wrote their names on their pots, so that, after more than a thousand years, we know the names of a few of them.

{img:carved-wooden-lintel-temple-doorway-king-jaguar-protector-sapodilla}
At Tikal, there are also wonderful wood carvings.

--

The doorways of the temples, on the tops of the pyramids, have lintels, beams across the top of the doorways, made of the hard, red wood of the sapodilla tree, carved, on the underside, with wonderful scenes: the king, on his throne, with a great jaguar, or a great serpent, rising behind him, to protect him, and lines of glyphs, telling the story.

Several of these lintels, from Temples One and Four, were taken away, in the nineteenth century, and are now in a museum in Basel, in Switzerland, where they can still be seen. But some are still in place, high up, on the temples, in the forest.

--

And there are carvings in stone, and paintings on the walls, and sculptures of plaster, on the fronts of the temples: great masks, of gods, with huge faces, and eyes, and fangs, and ear ornaments, painted red, and white, and black, which looked out over the plazas, and the people, and the forest.
