Tikal has one great problem: water.

It rains a great deal, in the rainy season, from May or June until December. But then, for five or six months, from January to May, it hardly rains at all. And there are no rivers, near Tikal, and no lakes, and few springs. The ground is limestone, and the rainwater soaks away through it, quickly, deep underground.

--

So the people of Tikal must catch the rain, and keep it, all year round. And they have become masters of water.

The whole centre of the city is built so that the rain, falling on the great plazas, and the temples, and the courtyards, all paved with plaster, runs off, into channels, and down the causeways, into great reservoirs, dug into the earth, and lined with clay, to keep the water in.

{img:plaster-plaza-rain-running-channels-toward-reservoir-storm}
There are about ten great reservoirs, in the centre of Tikal, and many smaller ones, all around: the Palace Reservoir, the Temple Reservoir, the Causeway Reservoir, and others. Together, they can hold millions of litres of water, enough for the whole city, through the long dry season.

--

The reservoirs are carefully built, and carefully kept. Some have dams, and sluice gates, to control the water. And recently, archaeologists found that the people of Tikal even filtered their drinking water, through layers of sand, and of a special mineral, called zeolite, brought from many kilometres away, which cleaned the water, and made it safe to drink. It is one of the oldest water filters known in the world.

The water in the reservoirs is clear, and still, and green, and covered with water lilies, with their round, flat leaves, and their white flowers, which open in the morning. And the water lily is a sacred flower, for the Maya, the flower of the waters of the underworld, and of the kings. One of the great kings of Tikal is shown with a water lily, in his headdress, and there is a water lily jaguar, a god.

{img:maya-woman-carrying-water-jar-head-path-reservoir-morning}
Every morning, the women of Tikal, and the girls, go down to the reservoirs, with their jars, of clay, or their gourds, and fill them, and carry them home, on their heads, or on their hips, and talk, and laugh, and share the news of the day.

--

The kings are responsible for the water. The great reservoirs are built by them, next to the temples, and the palaces, and the king, with his ceremonies, and his prayers to the rain god, Chaak, is believed to bring the rain, and keep the water flowing.

So when the rains fail, the people look to the king.

--

And in the years after 750, the rains will begin to fail.

But tonight, in the rainy season of the year 750, the reservoirs are full, and the rain is falling, softly, on the thatched roofs, and the plastered plazas, and the forest, and running, in little streams, along the channels, into the great, still pools, where the frogs are singing.
