Tikal is not the only great city, in the Maya world.

In the year 750, there are dozens of Maya cities, all over the lowlands, in what is now Guatemala, and Mexico, and Belize, and Honduras, each with its own king, its own temples, and palaces, and plazas, its own gods, and its own history. They speak the same language, more or less, and write in the same script, and count the same calendar, and worship the same gods. But they are not one country. Each city is its own little kingdom.

--

Close to Tikal, only about twenty kilometres to the north, there is Uaxactun, an old city, which was once Tikal's rival, and was conquered by it, more than three hundred years before. Further away, there are Yaxha, beside its beautiful lake, and Naranjo, and Holmul, and many others, some friends, some rivals, some ruled by Tikal.

And, far to the north, there is Calakmul, the Snake Kingdom, Tikal's great rival.

{img:calakmul-great-pyramid-rising-from-forest-north-distance}
For two hundred years, Tikal and Calakmul were the two great powers of the Maya world, like two great empires, each with its own allies, and its own vassal cities, competing for power, and trade, and glory.

--

Calakmul defeated Tikal, in the year 562, and for more than a hundred years, Tikal was humbled. Then, in 695, Tikal defeated Calakmul, and rose again. By the year 750, Calakmul is weaker, and Tikal is strong again.

Far to the southeast, in what is now Honduras, there is Copán, a beautiful city, in a green valley, beside a river, famous for its sculptures, carved almost in the round, and its great Hieroglyphic Stairway, with more than two thousand glyphs, carved on its steps, telling the history of its kings. Copán was founded, the stones say, by a man, K'inich Yax K'uk' Mo', who came, perhaps, from Tikal, or had close ties with it.

{img:palenque-palace-tower-mist-green-hills-temples}
And far to the west, in what is now Mexico, there is Palenque, in the green hills, above the plains, with its beautiful temples, and its palace, with its tall tower, and the great tomb of its greatest king, K'inich Janaab Pakal, who ruled for sixty-eight years, and was buried, in a great stone coffin, deep inside his temple, the Temple of the Inscriptions.

--

The kings of these cities visit each other, and send ambassadors, and gifts, and marry each other's daughters, and make alliances, and sometimes, they quarrel, and go to war.

The wars of the Maya are, mostly, small wars, raids, and battles between the warriors of two cities, with spears, and clubs, edged with obsidian, and shields, and cotton armour, padded and quilted. The aim is not, usually, to destroy the other city, but to capture its nobles, and warriors, and sometimes its king, and to make it pay tribute, and to win glory. We will leave the battles there, far off, in the past, as the stones do, with a few short words.

--

And the people of all these cities, the farmers, and the weavers, and the potters, and the traders, go on, mostly, with their lives, growing their corn, and raising their children, in the forest, whatever the kings are doing.
