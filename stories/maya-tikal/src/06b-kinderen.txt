Let us think about the children of Tikal.

When a Maya baby is born, the family calls a daykeeper, a priest, who knows the sacred calendar, to find out on which day of the two hundred and sixty days the baby was born, and what that day means, for the child's life, and its character. Each day has its own spirit, and its own fortune.

--

Babies are carried everywhere, by their mothers, wrapped in a cloth, on their backs, or on their hips, as Maya mothers still do today. They are breastfed for a long time, two or three years, or more.

Some Maya families, especially noble families, gently shape the heads of their babies, by binding them, for the first months, between two little boards, so that the forehead slopes back, long and smooth, like an ear of corn, like the head of the young maize god, which they thought very beautiful. And some hang a little bead of wax, or a ball of resin, from the baby's hair, between its eyes, so that it learns to look at it, and becomes a little cross-eyed, which they also thought beautiful.

{img:mother-carrying-baby-back-cloth-sling-walking-garden-path}
Maya children play.

--

Archaeologists have found little clay whistles, shaped like animals, and people, and birds, which children blew, and which still make a sound, today, after more than a thousand years. And little clay figures, which may have been toys, or dolls. Children played with balls, of rubber, and with spinning tops, and with dogs, and pet birds, and played games of hide and seek, among the houses, and the trees, as children do everywhere.

But they also work, from a young age, helping their parents. By the time they are five, or six, they are helping in the house, and the garden, and the fields. By the time they are twelve, or thirteen, they are almost grown up.

--

There is a ceremony, the Spanish later wrote, for the children, when they reach the age of about twelve, a little like a coming of age: the children, boys and girls, are gathered together, in a courtyard, with their parents, and their godparents, and a priest blesses them, and sprinkles them with water, and the girls' mothers take off the little shell, or bead, that their daughters have worn, since they were small, as a sign of childhood. After this, they are grown up.

The children of the nobles learn much more: to read, and write, and count, and the calendar, and the history of their families, and the ceremonies, and the dances. The sons of kings learn to be kings.

{img:grandmother-telling-stories-children-fire-evening-thatched-house}
And all the children of Tikal learn the stories: of the gods, and the hero twins, and the creation of the world, and the corn, and the animals, of why the rabbit has long ears, and why the howler monkey roars, and why the deer has a short tail. They hear them in the evenings, around the fire, from their grandmothers, and grandfathers, and remember them, and tell them, one day, to their own children.

--

Some of those stories are still told, today, in the Maya villages of Guatemala, more than twelve hundred years later, to children, falling asleep, as the rain falls on the roof.
