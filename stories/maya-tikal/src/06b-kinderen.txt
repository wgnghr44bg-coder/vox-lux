Let us think about the children of Tikal.

When a Maya baby is born, the family calls a daykeeper. That is a priest who knows the holy calendar. He finds out on which of the two hundred and sixty holy days the baby was born. Each day has its own spirit and its own luck. People believe it will shape the child's life. So the family waits nervously for his words.

--

Mothers carry their babies everywhere. They wrap them in a cloth on their back or their hip. Maya mothers still do this today.

Some families, especially rich ones, gently shape their babies' heads. For the first months, they bind the head softly between two little boards. Slowly the forehead slopes back, long and smooth, like an ear of corn. It looks like the head of the young corn god. The Maya thought this was very beautiful.

{img:mother-carrying-baby-back-cloth-sling-walking-garden-path}
Maya children play.

--

Archaeologists have found little clay whistles shaped like animals and birds. Children blew them long ago. They still make a sound today, after more than a thousand years. There are little clay figures that may have been toys. Children played with rubber balls and spinning tops. They played with dogs and pet birds. And they played hide and seek among the houses and the trees, as children do everywhere.

But they also work from a young age. By five or six they help in the house and the garden. By twelve or thirteen they are almost grown up.

--

Much later, the Spanish wrote about a special day for children of about twelve. The boys and girls gather in a yard with their parents. A priest blesses them and sprinkles them with water. The girls have worn a little shell since they were small. Now their mothers take it off. From that day on, they count as grown up.

The children of nobles learn much more. They learn to read and write and count. They learn the calendar and the history of their families. And the sons of kings learn to be kings.

{img:grandmother-telling-stories-children-fire-evening-thatched-house}
All the children of Tikal learn the old stories. Stories of the gods and of how the world was made. Stories of why the rabbit has long ears, and why the howler monkey roars. They hear them in the evening by the fire, from their grandparents. And one day they tell them to their own children.

--

Some of those stories are still told today in Maya villages in Guatemala. More than twelve hundred years later, they are told to children falling asleep, while the rain falls on the roof.
