The Maya love time.

They watch the sun and the moon and the stars and the planets. They count the days with great care. They write them down on their stones and in their books. And they have not one calendar, but several. They all turn together, like the wheels of a great clock.

--

First, the Maya write numbers in a very simple way. A dot is one. A bar is five. So seven is a bar and two dots. Nineteen is three bars and four dots. They also have a sign for zero. It looks like a little shell. They were among the first people in the world to use the idea of zero.

They count in twenties, not in tens like we do. Perhaps because people have twenty fingers and toes.

{img:bar-dot-numbers-carved-stone-shell-zero-sign}
The first calendar is the holy calendar. It counts two hundred and sixty days.

--

It has twenty named days. Each one has its own sign, its own god and its own meaning. They turn together with the numbers one to thirteen. After two hundred and sixty days, the same pair comes round again.

This calendar is used to name children. It is used to choose the right days for planting, for weddings and for ceremonies. And it is used to tell the future. Maya daykeepers in the mountains of Guatemala still use it today.

--

The second calendar follows the sun. It has three hundred and sixty-five days. There are eighteen months of twenty days each. And at the end there are five extra days. These are unlucky days without names. People stay at home and are careful. Nobody starts anything new. Everyone waits for the new year to begin.

The two calendars turn together, like two gears. The same pair of days only comes round once every fifty-two years. For the Maya, this is a great cycle, a bit like a century for us.

{img:priest-astronomer-temple-top-dawn-watching-sunrise-horizon}
And then there is the Long Count.

--

The Long Count is the great calendar on the Maya stones. It counts every single day from the beginning of the present world. The Maya believed the world was made on a day more than three thousand years before Christ.

It counts in days, and twenties of days, and years of three hundred and sixty days. It counts twenties of years, and four hundreds of years. So every date on a stone tells exactly how many days have passed since the world began.

Every twenty years there is a great festival at Tikal. The kings build two pyramids facing each other across a square, just for this festival. They set up a carved stone and an altar.

{img:twin-pyramid-complex-tikal-katun-ending-stela-enclosure-plaza}
In 2012, many people around the world got worried. They believed the Maya had said the world would end that December. On that day, the Long Count reached the end of a very big cycle.

--

But the Maya never said that. For them, it was simply the end of one great cycle and the start of a new one. Like the turning of a great wheel. One Maya stone even talks about a date far in the future, in the year 4772.

And the next morning, the sun rose as usual over the temples of Tikal. Thousands of people had gathered there with Maya priests. And they celebrated the new beginning.
