The Maya are fascinated by time.

They watch the sun, and the moon, and the stars, and the planets, and count the days, with wonderful care, and record them, on their monuments, and in their books. And they have not one calendar, but several, all turning together, like the wheels of a great clock.

--

First, the Maya count numbers in a very simple way: a dot for one, and a bar for five. So two is two dots, and seven is a bar and two dots, and nineteen is three bars and four dots. And they have a sign for zero, a little shell, one of the first peoples in the world to use the idea of zero.

They count in twenties, not in tens, as we do, perhaps because people have twenty fingers and toes.

{img:bar-dot-numbers-carved-stone-shell-zero-sign}
The first calendar is the sacred calendar, the count of two hundred and sixty days.

--

It has twenty named days, Imix, Ik', Ak'bal, K'an, and so on, each with its own sign, and its own god, and its own meaning, turning together with the numbers one to thirteen. So the days go: 1 Imix, 2 Ik', 3 Ak'bal, and so on, until, after two hundred and sixty days, the same combination comes round again.

This calendar is used to name children, and to choose the right days for planting, and for ceremonies, and for weddings, and to tell the future. It is still used today, by Maya daykeepers, in the highlands of Guatemala.

--

The second calendar follows the sun: a year of three hundred and sixty five days, divided into eighteen months, of twenty days each, with five extra days, at the end, which are unlucky, nameless days, when people stay at home, and are careful, and do not start anything new.

The two calendars turn together, like two gears, and the same combination of a day, in the sacred calendar, and a day, in the solar year, comes round only once every fifty-two years. This is the Calendar Round, a great cycle, like a century, for the Maya.

{img:priest-astronomer-temple-top-dawn-watching-sunrise-horizon}
And then, there is the Long Count.

--

The Long Count is the great calendar of the Maya monuments. It counts the days, one by one, from the beginning of the present world, which, the Maya believed, was created on a day in the year 3114 BC, in our calendar.

It counts them in days, and twenties of days, and years of three hundred and sixty days, called tuns, and twenties of years, called k'atuns, and four hundred years, called b'ak'tuns. So every date, on a Maya monument, tells exactly how many days have passed since the creation: for example, nine b'ak'tuns, fifteen k'atuns, eighteen tuns, and so on.

The end of every k'atun, every twenty years, is a great festival, at Tikal. The kings build special groups of buildings, twin pyramids, facing each other, east and west, across a plaza, to celebrate it, with a stela, and an altar, in a little enclosure, on the north side, and a building with nine doorways, on the south.

{img:twin-pyramid-complex-tikal-katun-ending-stela-enclosure-plaza}
In 2012, many people around the world believed that the Maya had predicted that the world would end, on the twenty-first of December, because, on that day, the Long Count reached the end of the thirteenth b'ak'tun.

--

But the Maya never said that. For them, it was simply the end of a great cycle, and the beginning of a new one, like the turning of a great wheel, or the beginning of a new millennium. And indeed, a Maya inscription, at the city of Palenque, speaks of a date, far in the future, in the year 4772.

And on the twenty-second of December, 2012, the sun rose as usual, over the temples of Tikal, where thousands of people, and Maya priests, had gathered, to celebrate the new beginning.
