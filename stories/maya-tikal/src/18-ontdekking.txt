For hundreds of years, Tikal lay hidden, in the forest.

The local people, the chicle gatherers, and the hunters, and the farmers, of the villages around Lake Petén Itzá, knew that there were ruins, somewhere in the forest. But almost nobody else did.

--

In 1848, the governor of the region of Petén, Modesto Méndez, and the governor of the town of Flores, Ambrosio Tut, led an expedition into the forest, guided by local men, and found the ruins. Méndez wrote a report, and an artist, Eusebio Lara, made drawings of the temples, and the carvings, which were published, a few years later, in Berlin.

They were astonished: great temples, rising out of the forest, higher than the trees, and carved monuments, and palaces, all silent, and overgrown, and covered with moss.

In 1877, a Swiss doctor, Gustav Bernoulli, visited Tikal, and had some of the carved wooden lintels taken down, from the temples, and sent to Basel, where they still are.

--

Then, in the 1880s, an English explorer, Alfred Maudslay, came to Tikal, with a camera, and took the first photographs of the ruins, on great glass plates. He was a patient, careful man, who spent years photographing, and making plaster casts, of the monuments, of many Maya cities, so that scholars, all over the world, could study the glyphs. His photographs of Tikal, with the great temples, half buried in the forest, still look magical, today.

After him came others: Teobert Maler, a German Austrian photographer, and the American archaeologists of the Peabody Museum, of Harvard, and the Carnegie Institution. They mapped the ruins, and drew the monuments, and began to understand the city.

{img:archaeologists-1950s-excavation-temple-stairs-workers-field-camp}
And then, in 1956, the University of Pennsylvania began a great project, of digging, and restoring, at Tikal, which lasted fourteen years.

--

A little airstrip was built, in the forest, so that people, and supplies, could come in, by plane, because there were no roads. The archaeologists, and hundreds of local workers, cleared the trees, from the great temples, and the plazas, and dug, and mapped, and studied, and repaired.

They found the great tomb of Jasaw Chan K'awiil, deep under Temple One, with its jade, and its carved bones. They dug deep into the North Acropolis, and found the older buildings, and the older tombs, one under another, going back more than a thousand years. And they mapped the city around, with its thousands of houses, and found that Tikal had been much greater than anyone had thought.

--

After them, Guatemalan archaeologists continued the work, digging, and restoring, the Lost World group, and the twin pyramid complexes, and Temple Five, and many other buildings.

{img:temple-iv-top-view-over-forest-canopy-mist-temples-poking-through}
And in 1977, a film crew came to Tikal.

--

They climbed to the top of Temple Four, and filmed the view, out over the forest, with the roof combs of the other temples, rising out of the trees, in the distance, in the early morning mist. And this view became part of a famous film, Star Wars, as the secret base of the rebels, on a moon, far away, in another galaxy.

So, today, many people who have never heard of the Maya have seen the temples of Tikal, rising out of the forest, without knowing it.
