For the Maya, everything begins with corn.

Corn is their food, their life and even their god. They eat it every day, at every meal. They eat it as flat breads and tamales, and they drink it too. Nearly three quarters of everything they eat comes from corn.

--

And they believe that people were made from corn.

There is an old holy book of the Maya called the Popol Vuh. It was written down much later, but it tells very old stories. In it, the gods try to make people several times. First they make them from mud. But the mud people are soft. They fall apart in the water. Then the gods make people from wood. But the wooden people have no hearts. They do not remember who made them. At last the gods make people from yellow and white corn, ground into dough. And these are the true people.

The corn god is one of the most loved Maya gods. He is a beautiful young man with a long smooth head, like an ear of corn. His hair is long, like the soft threads of the corn. Every year, like the corn, he dies and is buried in the earth. And then he is born again and grows up and dances, and brings new life to the world.

--

The Maya farmer grows his corn in a field cut out of the forest.

In the dry season he cuts down the trees and bushes on a piece of land. He lets them dry in the sun. Then, just before the rains come, he burns them. The ash makes the soil rich.

When the first rains come in May, he plants the corn. He walks through the field with a pointed stick and a bag of seeds. Every step or so, he pokes a hole in the ground. He drops in a few seeds and covers them with his foot. And then he waits and watches the sky. If the rains come late, the seeds will die in the dry ground.

{img:maya-farmer-planting-digging-stick-seed-bag-burnt-field-rain-clouds}
Next to the corn he plants beans. They climb up the corn stalks. He plants squash too. It spreads across the ground with big leaves that keep the soil damp. The three plants grow together and help each other. Later, people will call them the three sisters.

--

He grows other plants too. Chilli peppers and tomatoes, sweet potatoes and cotton. Around his house grow avocados and papayas and many other fruits.

After two or three years, the soil of a field gets tired. The farmer lets it turn back into forest. And he cuts a new field somewhere else.

But a city as big as Tikal needs much more food than this. So the Maya also built steps of land on the hillsides. They built raised fields in the wet lowlands, with little canals between them. And they had gardens everywhere around their houses. The laser maps show that the land around the Maya cities was farmed very carefully.

{img:maya-corn-harvest-baskets-ripe-ears-family-field-autumn}
In autumn the corn is ripe. The farmer bends the stalks over so the ears hang down. That way they dry in the field, safe from rain and birds. Then the family picks the corn. They carry it home in big baskets on their backs. They store it in the store house for the whole year.

--

Before planting and at harvest, the farmer prays. He gives gifts to the gods of the earth and the rain and the corn. He burns sweet incense and thanks them. Maya farmers still do this today.
