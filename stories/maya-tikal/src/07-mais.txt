For the Maya, everything begins with corn.

Corn, maize, is their food, their life, and their god. They eat it every day, at every meal, in tortillas, and tamales, and in drinks, and porridges. Nearly three quarters of everything they eat comes from corn.

--

And they believe that people were made from corn.

In the Popol Vuh, the great sacred book of the K'iche' Maya, written down much later, in the sixteenth century, but telling very old stories, the gods tried to make people several times. First, they made them from mud, but the mud people were soft, and crumbled, and dissolved in the water. Then they made them from wood, but the wooden people had no hearts, and no souls, and did not remember their makers. And at last, they made them from yellow and white corn, ground into dough, and these were the true people, the ancestors of the Maya.

The maize god is one of the most beloved of all Maya gods: a beautiful young man, with a long, smooth head, like an ear of corn, and long hair, like corn silk, and jade jewellery. Every year, like the corn, he dies, and is buried in the earth, and is born again, and grows up, and dances, and brings new life to the world.

--

The Maya farmer grows his corn in a milpa: a field, cut out of the forest.

In the dry season, in the early spring, he cuts down the trees, and the bushes, on a piece of land, with his stone axe, and lets them dry, in the sun. Then, just before the rains come, he burns them. The ashes make the soil rich.

Then, when the first rains come, in May, he plants the corn: he walks through the field, with a pointed digging stick, and a bag of seeds, over his shoulder, and every step or so, he makes a hole in the ground, with the stick, and drops in a few seeds, and covers them with his foot.

{img:maya-farmer-planting-digging-stick-seed-bag-burnt-field-rain-clouds}
Beside the corn, he plants beans, which climb up the stalks of the corn, and squash, which spreads out on the ground, between them, with its big leaves, shading the soil, and keeping it moist. The three plants grow together, and help each other. Later, they are called the three sisters.

--

He grows other plants too: chilli peppers, and tomatoes, and sweet potatoes, and manioc, and cotton, and tobacco, and gourds, and, around his house, avocados, and papayas, and guavas, and many other fruits.

After two or three years, the soil of a milpa is tired, and the farmer lets it go back to forest, and cuts a new field, somewhere else.

But a city as great as Tikal needs much more food than this. So, the Maya also built terraces, on the hillsides, and raised fields, in the swamps, the bajos, with canals between them, and gardens, everywhere, around their houses. LiDAR has shown that much of the land, around the Maya cities, was carefully farmed, in many different ways.

{img:maya-corn-harvest-baskets-ripe-ears-family-field-autumn}
In the autumn, the corn ripens. The farmer bends the stalks over, so that the ears hang down, and dry, in the field, protected from the rain, and the birds. And then, the family harvests it, and carries it home, in great baskets, on their backs, held by a strap, across their foreheads, and stores it, in the storehouse, for the year.

--

And before planting, and at the harvest, the farmer prays, and makes offerings, to the gods, of the earth, and the rain, and the corn, and burns copal incense, and thanks them, as Maya farmers still do today.
