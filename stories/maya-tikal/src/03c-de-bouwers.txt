How did the Maya build the great temples of Tikal, without metal tools, without wheels, without animals to pull, or carry?

With stone, and wood, and fire, and water, and the strength, and skill, and patience, of thousands of people.

--

The ground under Tikal is limestone, and the builders quarry it, close by, cutting out the blocks with tools of flint, and harder stone. The limestone is soft, when it is first cut, and easy to work, and it hardens, slowly, in the air. Many of the quarries became the reservoirs, afterwards, so that the holes left by the builders were filled with water.

The blocks are carried, on the backs of the workers, with tumplines, across their foreheads, or rolled, on logs, and lifted, with ropes, and ramps, of earth, and wood.

{img:lime-kiln-burning-wood-stones-smoke-workers-forest-edge}
The inside of the pyramids is mostly filled with rubble, stones, and earth, held in by walls, and then covered with a skin of cut stone blocks, and then with plaster.

--

The plaster is made of lime. The builders burn limestone, in great fires, of wood, piled up, for a day and a night, until it turns into a white powder, quicklime, and then they mix it with water, and sand, and it becomes a smooth, white paste, which hardens, like stone. It needs a great deal of wood: to make enough lime for one great temple, whole areas of forest are cut down.

The plaster covers everything: the pyramids, and the temples, and the plazas, and the roads, and the floors, and is smoothed, and polished, and painted, red, mostly, with paint made from iron oxide, red earth, mixed with water, and other things.

--

On the top of the pyramids, the builders make the little temples, with thick walls, and narrow rooms, with the Maya arch, and then, above them, the roof combs, tall, decorated walls, of stone, which are made lighter by little rooms, and holes, inside them, and covered with great sculptures, of plaster, of the king, on his throne, painted in many colours.

The builders work in teams, for the king, perhaps for some weeks, every year, in the dry season, when there is less work in the fields, as a kind of tax, and in return, they are fed, and honoured, and take part in the great festivals.

{img:architect-scribe-plan-workers-raising-temple-scaffolding-ropes}
There are architects, and master masons, and plasterers, and sculptors, and painters, who know their craft, and pass it on, from father to son, and who plan the buildings, so carefully, that they line up with the sun, and the stars, and with each other, across the city.

--

And the Maya build on top of the old buildings. When a king wants a new temple, often, he does not tear down the old one, but builds the new one over it, covering it, like a shell, so that, inside many of the pyramids of Tikal, there are older pyramids, and inside them, older ones still, like the layers of an onion, going back hundreds of years.

So the temples of Tikal grew, layer by layer, generation by generation, for more than a thousand years, into the great buildings that still rise, above the forest, today.
