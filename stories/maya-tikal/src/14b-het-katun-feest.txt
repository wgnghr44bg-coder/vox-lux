In the year 751, a great day is coming, in Tikal.

It is the end of a k'atun, a period of twenty years, in the Long Count: the day when the count will reach nine b'ak'tuns, sixteen k'atuns, and zero tuns, zero twenties, and zero days. For the Maya, it is like the beginning of a new century, for us. And at Tikal, the end of every k'atun is celebrated, with a great festival.

--

For months before, the people prepare.

The king's builders build a new twin pyramid complex: two flat-topped pyramids, facing each other, one on the east, and one on the west, across a plaza, with stairways on all four sides. On the north side, there is a small enclosure, with walls, but no roof, open to the sky, with a carved stela, showing the king, and an altar. And on the south side, a long, low building, with nine doorways, for the nine levels of the underworld.

{img:twin-pyramid-flat-top-four-stairways-plaster-new-white-red}
The stone carvers carve the new stela, with the portrait of the king, in his finest costume, scattering drops, perhaps of incense, or seeds, or blood, from his hand, and the glyphs, telling the date, and the name of the king, and his parents, and the ceremonies he performed.

--

On the great day, everyone comes. People come from all over the city, and from the villages around, and from other cities, in their best clothes, with flowers, and offerings.

At dawn, the king, and the queen, and the nobles, and the priests, in their richest costumes, with towering headdresses, of quetzal feathers, and jade, and jaguar skins, come in procession, along the white road, from the Great Plaza, to the new twin pyramids, with music, of drums, and trumpets, and conch shells, and rattles, and singing, and clouds of copal incense, rising into the morning air.

--

The king climbs the eastern pyramid, as the sun rises, and makes offerings to the sun, and the gods. And then, he climbs the western pyramid, where the sun will set. He goes into the enclosure, on the north side, and stands before the new stela, and the altar, and makes the offerings that end the k'atun: incense, and blood, and jade, and he scatters offerings, to the gods, and the ancestors.

{img:festival-plaza-dancers-feather-costumes-drums-crowd-afternoon}
And then, there is dancing, and feasting, and music, all day long, and into the night. The dancers, in their masks, and costumes of the gods, and animals, dance in the plazas, and the people eat, and drink, and talk, and celebrate, and the smell of roasting turkey, and tamales, and chocolate, and incense, fills the air.

--

And the people of Tikal feel that the world has been renewed. The old k'atun has ended, safely, and a new one has begun, and the king has done what he must, to keep the world turning, and the sun rising, and the rain falling, and the corn growing.

That stela, put up for this k'atun, still stands at Tikal, today, in its enclosure, at the twin pyramid complex, which archaeologists call Group P. Nine of these twin pyramid complexes have been found, at Tikal, each one built for the end of a k'atun, over about a hundred and thirty years. They are found almost nowhere else in the Maya world.

--

When the festival is over, and the people have gone home, the new pyramids stand, gleaming, and white, and red, in the moonlight, in the silence, until, twenty years later, the king's son will build another, for the next k'atun.
