In the heart of Tikal, near the Great Plaza, between the Central Acropolis and Temple One, there is a ball court.

It is a long, narrow alley, paved with plaster, between two long, low buildings, with sloping walls, facing each other, painted red, and white. And here, the Maya play their great game, the ballgame.

--

The ballgame is one of the oldest games in the world. It was played all over Mexico, and Central America, for more than three thousand years, by the Olmecs, and the Maya, and the Aztecs, and many other peoples, and there are still places in Mexico where a version of it is played today.

It is played with a ball of solid rubber: heavy, and hard, and bouncy, about the size of a large melon, or a little bigger, made from the sap of the rubber tree. The Maya were among the first people in the world to make rubber, long before anyone in Europe had ever seen it.

The players must keep the ball in the air, and send it to the other side of the court, bouncing it off the sloping walls, but they must not touch it with their hands, or their feet. They hit it with their hips, and their thighs, and their arms, and so they wear thick, padded belts, called yokes, around their waists, made of wood, and leather, and cotton, and pads on their knees, and arms, to protect them from the heavy ball.

--

It is a fast, and difficult, and exciting game, and the crowds love to watch it, and cheer, and make bets, on their favourite players, with cacao beans, and cloth, and jade, and feathers.

And the game is also sacred. For the Maya, the ball is like the sun, and the moon, moving across the sky, and through the underworld, and the game is like the great struggle between day and night, life and death, light and darkness.

{img:hero-twins-playing-ball-underworld-lords-mythic-scene-painted-vase}
In the Popol Vuh, there is a great story about the ballgame.

--

Long ago, there were two brothers, Hun Hunahpu and Vucub Hunahpu, who loved to play ball. But they played so noisily, that the lords of the underworld, Xibalba, below the earth, were disturbed, and invited them down, to play against them. And the lords of Xibalba tricked them, and they never came back.

But the son of Hun Hunahpu, born later, had two sons of his own, twins: Hunahpu and Xbalanque, the Hero Twins. And they, too, loved to play ball. And they, too, were invited down to Xibalba.

--

But the Hero Twins were clever. They passed through all the tests, and the tricks, of the lords of Xibalba: the House of Darkness, the House of Cold, the House of Jaguars, the House of Bats. They played ball against the lords, and outwitted them, again and again, and, in the end, they defeated them.

And then, the twins rose up into the sky, and became the sun and the moon. And their father, the maize god, was born again, from the earth.

{img:ballcourt-marker-carved-stone-disc-ballplayer-glyphs}
The game, at Tikal, is sometimes played for fun, and sometimes as a great ceremony. And sometimes, the stones tell us, the losers were prisoners of war, and the game ended in a sacrifice, but that was rare, and long ago, and we will leave it there, gently, in the past.

--

In the evening, the ball court is quiet again. The players have gone home, to rest their bruises, and the crowds have gone, and the long, narrow court lies empty, in the fading light, with the red walls glowing, and the shadows of the temples falling across it.
