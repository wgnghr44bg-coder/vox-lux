Today, Tikal is a national park, in the north of Guatemala, in the region of Petén.

It was made a national park in 1955, and in 1979, it became a World Heritage Site, one of the first in the world, protected both for its ruins, and for its forest, and its animals.

--

The park covers more than five hundred square kilometres, of tropical forest, and it lies inside the great Maya Biosphere Reserve, which covers a fifth of Guatemala, the largest tropical forest in Central America, north of the Amazon.

Every year, many thousands of visitors come to Tikal, from Guatemala, and from all over the world. They walk along the paths, through the forest, in the shade of the great trees, from one group of temples to another, as the people of Tikal once walked along their white roads.

{img:sunrise-temple-iv-top-visitors-sitting-mist-forest-howler-calls}
Some visitors climb to the top of Temple Four, on wooden stairs, before dawn, and sit, in the dark, waiting for the sunrise.

--

And slowly, the sky grows lighter, in the east, and the mist rises from the forest, and the roof combs of Temples One, and Two, and Three, appear, grey, and silent, above the trees. And then, the howler monkeys begin to roar, and the birds begin to call, toucans, and parrots, and the whole forest wakes up, around them, as it has done every morning, for thousands of years.

The animals have come back to Tikal. There are coatis, wandering across the Great Plaza, with their tails held high. There are spider monkeys, swinging in the trees, above the temples. There are ocellated turkeys, with their shimmering feathers, walking calmly among the visitors. There are toucans, and parrots, and hundreds of other birds. And, deep in the forest, rarely seen, there are still jaguars.

The Great Plaza is covered with grass, now, instead of white plaster, and the temples are grey, and the colours have gone. But they are still magnificent.

--

And, in 2018, scientists flew over the forest, around Tikal, with LiDAR, the laser that can see through the trees. And they found, hidden under the forest, more than sixty thousand buildings, houses, and palaces, and pyramids, and roads, and fields, and walls, and canals, that nobody had known about, in the region around Tikal and its neighbours. It showed that the Maya lowlands, in the time of Tikal's glory, had been home to millions of people, far more than anyone had thought.

There is still much to discover. Most of the buildings of Tikal have never been dug, and lie, still, under the trees, waiting.

{img:modern-maya-woman-weaving-backstrap-loom-colourful-huipil-village}
And the Maya are still here.

--

More than six million Maya people live in Guatemala today, almost half of the whole country, and millions more in Mexico, and Belize, and Honduras. They speak more than twenty Maya languages, K'iche', and Q'eqchi', and Kaqchikel, and Mam, and many others. Many of them still grow corn, in their milpas, and weave on backstrap looms, and wear their beautiful, embroidered clothes, the huipiles, with patterns that tell where they come from. And some still count the days of the old sacred calendar.

At Tikal, on special days, Maya priests, and families, come to the Great Plaza, and burn copal, and candles, in front of the temples, and pray, to the ancestors, and the old gods, as their people have done, for more than two thousand years.

--

So we leave Tikal, tonight, as the evening falls.

The great temples rise above the forest, grey, and silent, against a sky turning pink, and gold, and then deep blue. The howler monkeys roar, one last time, from the trees. The toucans fly home, across the plaza. The fireflies begin to flicker, among the stones. And the stars come out, one by one, over the roof combs of the temples, and the great white band of the Milky Way, the world tree, rises, slowly, into the sky, as it did over the city of the kings, in the year 750.
