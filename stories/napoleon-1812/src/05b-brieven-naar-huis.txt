Every evening in camp, in the summer of 1812, some soldiers sit by the fire and write letters home.

They write on whatever paper they can find, with pencils or ink, using their packs or their drums as desks. Those who cannot write ask a friend or a sergeant to write for them.

--

The army has its own post. Riders on fast horses carry the letters along the roads, from station to station, between the army and Paris, day and night. A letter from Russia can reach Paris in two or three weeks.

So all through the summer and autumn, letters travel back and forth between the soldiers and their families.

Many of these letters still exist. Some were captured later by Russian horsemen, and kept in archives in Moscow for two hundred years, never delivered. In recent years, historians have studied and published them, so we can still read them.

--

They are mostly simple letters from ordinary young men to their mothers and fathers, their wives and their sweethearts.

They write about the long marches, the heat and the dust and the bad roads. About the strange country with its wooden villages and onion-domed churches. About the food, or the lack of it. About their friends in the regiment. And later, about Moscow.

{img:mother-reading-letter-french-village-kitchen-window-light}
And they ask about home. How is the harvest? How is the cow? Is the new baby well? Give my love to grandmother. Tell my sweetheart I think of her every day. Pray for me. I hope to be home by winter.

--

The officers write longer letters. Napoleon himself writes almost every day to his wife. Short, loving letters. He asks about her health and their little son. He tells her he is well and the weather is fine, and asks her to give the little boy a kiss from his father.

At home, the families wait for the letters. They read them again and again, out loud by the fire, to the whole family and the neighbours. They keep them carefully in a box for the rest of their lives.

{img:old-letters-tied-ribbon-wooden-box-candle-quiet-room}
In the winter, the letters stopped coming. Many families waited for months and years for news that never came. Some kept a candle burning in the window every night, in case their son came home.

And some sons did come home, one day, long after everyone had given up hope. Thin and changed, they walked up the village street in spring or summer. And then there was great joy.
