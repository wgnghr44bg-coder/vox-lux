Let us spend an ordinary day with a soldier of the great army, on the march in the summer of 1812.

He is a young foot soldier, perhaps twenty years old, from a village in France or Germany. He was called up two or three years ago, and has been in the army ever since. He has fought in one or two battles. He is tough and used to hardship. He grumbles, as soldiers do. But he is proud of his regiment and of his Emperor.

--

The day begins before dawn. A drum beats in the dark, and the soldiers wake up on the ground where they slept, wrapped in their coats beside last night's fire.

They roll up their coats and strap them to their packs. They put on their tall black hats with a brass eagle on the front. They eat a little, a piece of hard biscuit or bread if there is any, and drink some water.

{img:soldier-packing-knapsack-greatcoat-musket-dawn-campfire}
Then they line up in their companies. The drums beat, and they set off along the road in long columns, four men side by side.

--

A soldier carries a heavy load, perhaps twenty-five or thirty kilos.

There is his long, heavy musket with its bayonet. There is a box of cartridges, little paper tubes filled with gunpowder and a lead ball. There is his pack, with spare shirts and shoes, a comb, a sewing kit, and perhaps a few letters from home. On top is his rolled coat. And he carries food for several days, biscuit, flour and rice.

In the summer of 1812 it is very hot. The sun beats down on the long columns. The dust rises from the road in thick white clouds. It is so thick that the soldiers cannot see the men in front of them. It fills their eyes, their mouths and their noses. And there is very little water. The village wells are few, and soon drunk dry.

--

The soldiers march for hours, twenty or thirty kilometres a day, with a short rest every hour or so. Then they sit down by the road, take off their packs and rest their feet.

They sing as they march, to keep their spirits up and to keep in step. Old marching songs, songs from home, and funny songs about their officers and their girls. When the Emperor rides past, they cheer: long live the Emperor!

{img:soldiers-resting-roadside-shade-birch-trees-canteens-singing}
Jakob Walter, the young German, later writes about the heat and the thirst and the dust. He tells how the soldiers drank from puddles and ditches, and even from the hoof prints of the horses. And how many of them fell ill.

--

In the afternoon, the march goes on. Feet are sore and blistered. Shoes wear out on the long roads. The soldiers must mend them, or find new ones, or march barefoot.

Then, in the late afternoon, the order comes to stop and make camp for the night. They stop beside the road, in a field or at the edge of a forest, near a village or a stream.

{img:column-halting-evening-meadow-forest-edge-soldiers-stacking-muskets}
The soldiers stand their muskets together in little pyramids, take off their packs and stretch. And then, tired as they are, they must find food, water and wood for their fires before they can rest. Each evening, it is a little harder to find any food at all.
