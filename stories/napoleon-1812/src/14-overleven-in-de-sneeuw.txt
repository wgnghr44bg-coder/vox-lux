How did anyone survive the retreat from Moscow?

Those who did, and wrote about it later, all say the same thing. They survived because of their friends. Their little groups stayed together, shared everything and helped each other through it all.

--

The soldiers learned many things in those weeks, just to stay alive.

They learned to keep moving during the day. Never sit down in the snow to rest, they told each other. If you sit down and fall asleep in the cold, you may never wake up. They learned to keep their feet dry if they could, and wrap them in rags and straw. And they learned not to hold frozen hands too close to the fire, but to warm them slowly.

{img:soldier-wrapping-feet-rags-straw-fireside-snow-careful}
They learned to cover their faces against the wind, leaving only the eyes. And they watched each other's faces. If a friend's nose or cheeks turned white from the cold, they told him at once, so he could rub them and bring the blood back.

--

They learned to find food anywhere. In frozen fields they dug under the snow for potatoes and cabbages. In ruined villages they searched cellars and barns for grain. They made a thin porridge of flour and melted snow. And they ate the horses that had fallen. For many soldiers, that was their main food on the retreat. Bourgogne writes that nothing in his whole life ever tasted so good as a piece of horse meat, roasted on the end of his sword, after days of hunger.

They shared what they had. A soldier who found food and ate it alone was despised. A soldier who shared was loved.

The women who followed the army were among the bravest of all. Many stayed with their regiments all through the retreat. They gave the soldiers food and care, and nursed the sick. Bourgogne tells of one of them, Mother Dubois, who gave birth to a baby in the snow during the retreat. The soldiers gave her their cloaks. The colonel gave her his own cloak, to keep her and the baby warm. And mother and baby both survived.

--

Others survived because of kindness from people they did not expect.

Some Russian farmers took pity on the frozen, starving soldiers. They gave them bread and let them warm themselves by the stove, and let them go. Some Jewish families in the towns gave them food and warmth, and hid them. Some Russian officers treated their prisoners kindly.

{img:russian-peasant-woman-giving-bread-frozen-soldier-izba-door-snow}
And some survived simply by luck. A barn found at the right moment. A fire, a friend, a piece of bread, a horse or a place on a sledge.

--

Jakob Walter, the young German, survived. He tells how he lost his friends one by one, and his strength too. He went on alone, step by step, through the snow. Then he found a little Russian pony and rode it, and it saved his life. He was ill with fever and hardly knew where he was. But at last he reached Poland. And then he came home to his village in Germany, thin and ill, and changed forever.

{img:german-village-winter-1813-church-snow-soldier-returning-home}
He lived to be an old man. Later he moved to America, to Kansas. There, many years afterwards, he wrote down his memories for his children. His little book is one of the most moving accounts of the war ever written.
