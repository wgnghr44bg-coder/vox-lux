For five weeks, from the middle of September to the middle of October, 1812, the Grande Armée stays in Moscow.

It is a strange time. The weather is beautiful: a golden autumn, warm and sunny, day after day. The soldiers say that it is warmer than in Paris. Napoleon says that the Russian winter is a fable, invented to frighten people.

--

The soldiers find lodging where they can, in the houses and palaces that did not burn, and in churches, and cellars. They find food in the cellars, and storehouses, that survived the fire: flour, and preserved fruit, and sugar, and tea, and fish, and wine, and brandy, and liqueurs. For a while, after the hungry months of the march, they eat, and drink, very well.

They also find treasure: silver, and gold, and furs, and silk, and jewels, and paintings, and fine clothes, in the empty palaces, and churches, and shops. And many of them fill their knapsacks, and their wagons, with them, to take home.

Sergeant Bourgogne describes his own knapsack, when the army left Moscow: a few pounds of sugar, some rice, some biscuit, a bottle of liqueur, a woman's Chinese silk dress, embroidered in gold and silver, several gold and silver ornaments, a piece of the cross of Ivan the Great, from the bell tower, a woman's cloak, lined with green velvet, two silver pictures, in relief, of the judgment of Paris, a crucifix, and a little Chinese porcelain vase. He was very proud of it. Most of it, he would leave by the road.

--

There is even entertainment. Some French actors, who had been living in Moscow before the war, stay in the city, and the officers arrange performances, in a theatre, in one of the palaces, with plays, and music, to pass the time. Napoleon attends some of them.

Napoleon works hard, in the Kremlin, as always: dictating letters, and orders, and reading reports from Paris, and even drawing up new rules for the Comédie-Française, the great theatre of Paris.

And he waits for the Tsar's answer. He sends messages to Alexander, offering peace. But no answer comes. The Tsar will not talk to him, as long as he is in Russia.

--

Meanwhile, outside Moscow, the Russian army is resting, and growing stronger, with new soldiers, and supplies, in a camp south of the city, at Tarutino. And the Cossacks, and the peasant partisans, are attacking the French foragers, who go out of the city to look for food, and for hay for the horses.

The army's horses are starving. There is no hay, and no oats. Every day, more of them are lost.

{img:cossack-patrol-watching-from-forest-edge-autumn-misty-field}
Some of Napoleon's officers urge him to leave Moscow, before it is too late, and march back to Poland, for the winter. Others urge him to stay, for the winter, in Moscow. Napoleon cannot decide. He waits, and hopes, day after day, for the Tsar's answer.

--

And then, on the thirteenth of October, the first snow falls, lightly, on the ruins of Moscow. It melts at once. But it is a warning.

At last, Napoleon decides to leave. On the nineteenth of October, 1812, five weeks after he entered the city, the Grande Armée marches out of Moscow, heading south west, with about a hundred thousand men, and a huge, long train of wagons, and carriages, and carts, loaded with food, and treasure, and the wounded, and the families of French people who had lived in Moscow, and were afraid to stay.

{img:long-column-leaving-moscow-wagons-carriages-autumn-road-domes-behind}
The column is so long that it takes many hours to pass any one point. And the soldiers look back, at the golden domes of Moscow, in the autumn sunshine, for the last time.
