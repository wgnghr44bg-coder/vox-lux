Every regiment of the Grande Armée carries an eagle.

It is a golden eagle, of gilded bronze, with its wings half spread, standing on a little platform, on top of a long pole, with the regiment's flag, the colour, hanging below it, in blue, and white, and red, embroidered in gold, with the names of the regiment's battles.

--

Napoleon gave the eagles to the regiments himself, in a great ceremony, in Paris, in 1804, on the Champ de Mars, and the soldiers swore to defend them with their lives. The eagle is the soul of the regiment. It is carried, in battle, and on the march, by a chosen soldier, the eagle bearer, with two brave sergeants beside him, to protect it. To lose the eagle is the greatest shame. To capture an enemy's eagle is the greatest glory.

During the retreat from Moscow, many regiments, which had almost ceased to exist, still kept their eagles. Some soldiers took the eagles off their poles, and carried them inside their coats, or their knapsacks, through the snow, so that they would not be lost. Some buried them, secretly, in the snow, or in the earth, or threw them into rivers, rather than let the Cossacks take them.

And there was music. Every regiment had its band, and its drummers, and its fifers, and its trumpeters.

--

The drummers were often young boys, of twelve or thirteen, or fourteen. They beat the drums for the march, and for the reveille in the morning, and for the commands in battle. The drum beats were like a language: one beat meant advance, another meant halt, another meant gather round. Over the noise of battle, the soldiers could hear the drums, when they could not hear their officers' voices.

On the march, in the summer, the bands played marches, and songs, to keep the soldiers' spirits up, and their feet moving. And in the evenings, at the camps, they played for the officers, and the soldiers, sometimes dances, sometimes old songs from home.

{img:young-drummer-boy-drum-sticks-column-marching-summer-road}
Sergeant Bourgogne remembers that, when the army entered Moscow, the bands of the Guard played a march, called the Victory is Ours, as the soldiers marched through the empty streets.

--

And in the winter, on the retreat, there was little music. The instruments froze, and the bandsmen's fingers were too cold to play, and the drums were abandoned, by the road, with the wagons, and the cannons.

But sometimes, in the bivouac, at night, around the fire, a soldier would sing, quietly, an old song, from home, from his village, in France, or Germany, or Italy, or Holland. And the others would listen, and some would join in, softly. And for a few minutes, they would be far away, at home, in the summer, with their families, in the warm.
