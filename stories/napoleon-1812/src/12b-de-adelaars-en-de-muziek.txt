Every regiment of the great army carries an eagle.

It is a golden eagle made of bronze, with its wings half spread. It stands on top of a long pole. Below it hangs the regiment's flag, in blue, white and red, with the names of its battles embroidered in gold.

--

Napoleon gave the eagles to the regiments himself, at a great ceremony in Paris. The soldiers swore to defend them with their lives. The eagle is the soul of the regiment. A chosen soldier carries it, with two brave sergeants beside him to protect it. Losing the eagle is the greatest shame of all.

During the retreat from Moscow, some regiments almost disappeared. But many still kept their eagles. Some soldiers took the eagle off its pole and carried it inside their coat or their pack, through the snow. Some buried their eagle secretly in the snow, or threw it in a river, rather than let the enemy take it.

{img:soldier-hiding-eagle-under-greatcoat-snow-march-quiet-resolve}
And there was music. Every regiment had its band, its drummers and its trumpeters.

--

The drummers were often young boys of twelve or thirteen. They beat the drums for the march, for waking up in the morning and for orders in battle. The drum beats were like a language. One beat meant forward, another meant stop, another meant gather round. In the noise of battle, the soldiers could hear the drums when they could not hear their officers.

On the summer march, the bands played marches and songs, to keep the soldiers' spirits up and their feet moving. In the evenings at camp they played old songs from home.

{img:young-drummer-boy-drum-sticks-column-marching-summer-road}
Sergeant Bourgogne remembers that when the army entered Moscow, the bands of the Guard played a march called Victory Is Ours, as the soldiers walked through the empty streets.

--

In the winter, on the retreat, there was little music. The instruments froze. The bandsmen's fingers were too cold to play. The drums were left beside the road with the wagons.

But sometimes at night, around the fire, a soldier would quietly sing an old song from his village, in France or Germany or Italy. The others would listen, and some would softly join in. And for a few minutes they were far away, at home in the summer, with their families, in the warm.
