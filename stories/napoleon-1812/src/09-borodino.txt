At the beginning of September, 1812, about a hundred and twenty kilometres west of Moscow, the Russian army stops retreating, and turns to fight.

Kutuzov has chosen a place near a little village called Borodino, with a white church, beside a little river, the Kolocha, among rolling fields and woods and ravines, across the great road to Moscow. And there, the Russians dig earthworks, and wait.

--

On the evening before the battle, the sixth of September, the two armies are camped close to each other, on the fields, with their fires burning, all night, for miles.

In the Russian camp, the priests carry a holy icon, the Black Virgin of Smolensk, which was rescued from the burning city, along the lines of the army. And the soldiers kneel, and take off their caps, and pray, and the priests bless them, and sprinkle them with holy water.

In the French camp, that evening, a messenger arrives from Paris, with a portrait of Napoleon's little son, the King of Rome. Napoleon has it placed outside his tent, on a chair, so that the soldiers of the Guard can see it. And the old soldiers come, and look at the picture of the little boy, and some of them are moved to tears.

--

And then, on the seventh of September, at dawn, the battle begins.

We will not go into the battle. We will stand far away, on a little hill, at the edge of the forest, and watch from a great distance. From there, we would have seen only the smoke, great clouds of white smoke, from tens of thousands of muskets, and more than a thousand cannons, drifting across the fields, in the autumn sunshine, and heard the endless thunder of the guns, all day long, from dawn until dark.

It was one of the greatest and hardest battles in history. By the evening, very many men, of both sides, had been lost, or wounded. Neither side had really won. The Russian army retreated, in good order, during the night, toward Moscow. And the French held the field.

--

Dominique Larrey, the chief surgeon of the Grande Armée, worked through the day, and the night, and the next day, without stopping, caring for the wounded, French and Russian alike, in field hospitals, in barns, and in the open fields. He was a kind and brave man, who had invented a system of light, fast carriages, with springs, called flying ambulances, to carry the wounded from the battlefield quickly to the surgeons. He treated everyone, he said, whatever their rank, or their nation.

{img:surgeon-larrey-tending-wounded-field-hospital-barn-lanterns-calm}
Napoleon himself, that day, was not well. He had a bad cold, and a fever, and could hardly speak. He did not use his Guard, his reserve of the finest soldiers, in the battle, because, he said, he was eight hundred leagues from Paris, and could not risk it.

--

After the battle, Kutuzov held a council of war, in a peasant's hut, in a village called Fili, just outside Moscow, with his generals, sitting around a table, under the icons, while a little girl, the peasant's granddaughter, watched them from the top of the great stove.

They argued about whether to fight another battle, to defend Moscow. And at last, Kutuzov said that if they fought, they might lose the army, and then Russia would be lost. But if they gave up Moscow, they would keep the army, and Russia would be saved. And so, he ordered the army to retreat, through Moscow, and beyond, and leave the city to the French.

{img:peasant-hut-council-generals-table-icons-child-on-stove-candle}
It was a terrible decision. Moscow was the ancient, holy heart of Russia. But Kutuzov believed it was right.
