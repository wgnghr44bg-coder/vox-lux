Tonight we are going more than two hundred years back, to the wide plains of Russia, in the summer and the winter of the year 1812.

--

The greatest army the world has ever seen is marching east, along dusty roads, through birch forests and wheat fields, toward Moscow. More than half a million men, from all over Europe, with their horses and their wagons and their cannons, and the drums and the bands playing. Their leader is the Emperor of the French, Napoleon Bonaparte.

They set out in the summer sunshine, full of hope. Very few of them will come home. And tonight, gently, and from a distance, we will follow them, and see how they lived, on the long road to Moscow, and back again, through the Russian winter.

If you like to fall asleep to slow stories from the past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, and to come back for the next one.

--

Let us imagine Europe, in the year 1812.

For almost twenty years, since the French Revolution, Europe has been at war. And for most of those years, France has been winning. Under Napoleon, the French armies have marched across Europe, and defeated Austria, and Prussia, and Russia, and Spain, and many smaller states, again and again.

--

By 1812, Napoleon rules, directly or indirectly, almost the whole of Western and Central Europe. France itself is much larger than today, stretching from Hamburg to Rome. His brothers and his marshals are kings of Spain, and Naples, and Westphalia, and Holland. The kings of Prussia and Austria are his unwilling allies. Most of Germany, and Italy, and Poland, are under his control.

Only Britain, safe on its island, behind its great navy, still fights against him. And in Spain, the Spanish people, and a British army, under the Duke of Wellington, are fighting a long, bitter war against the French.

And in the east, there is Russia, the great empire of the Tsar, Alexander the First.

--

Napoleon and Alexander were once friends, of a kind. Five years before, in 1807, after Napoleon defeated the Russians, the two emperors met, on a raft, in the middle of the river Niemen, at Tilsit, and made peace, and divided Europe between them, and talked for hours, and charmed each other.

But the friendship did not last. Russia did not want to stop trading with Britain, as Napoleon demanded. The two emperors quarrelled, about Poland, and about trade, and about power. And in 1812, Napoleon decided to invade Russia, and force the Tsar to obey him.

{img:raft-pavilion-river-niemen-two-emperors-meeting-1807}
He thought it would be a short war: a few weeks, a great battle, a victory, and then the Tsar would ask for peace, as all his enemies had done before. He was wrong.

--

The story of Napoleon's campaign in Russia, in 1812, is one of the most famous, and the saddest, stories in the history of war.

But it is also a story of ordinary people: of young soldiers, and old soldiers, and drummer boys, and officers, and surgeons, and the women who followed the army, and the Russian peasants in their villages, and the horses, and the long roads, and the camp fires, and the snow. Many of the soldiers kept diaries, or wrote down their memories, many years later. And thanks to them, we can still follow the road to Moscow, step by step, and see it through their eyes.

{img:soldier-writing-diary-campfire-night-shako-candle}
We will not watch the battles. Tonight, we will walk beside the soldiers, and see how they lived, day by day, and night by night, in the summer, and the autumn, and the winter of 1812.
