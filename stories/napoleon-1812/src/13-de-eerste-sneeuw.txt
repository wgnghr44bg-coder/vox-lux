On the sixth of November, 1812, the weather changes.

The sky turns grey and heavy. A cold wind begins to blow from the north. And then the snow begins to fall: thick, heavy, silent snow, falling and falling, all day, and all night, covering the road, and the fields, and the forests, and the soldiers, in a deep white blanket.

--

The temperature falls, below freezing, and keeps falling. The Russian winter, which Napoleon had called a fable, has come.

For the soldiers on the road, everything becomes harder. The road disappears under the snow. The soldiers sink in it, up to their knees. The horses slip, and fall, on the icy road, because their shoes are not made for ice, and many of them cannot get up again. The wagons, and the cannons, are left behind, by the side of the road, in the snow.

The soldiers' clothes are not made for the Russian winter. They wear the same uniforms that they wore in the summer: thin trousers, and jackets, and shakos, and shoes, and their greatcoats. Nothing more. Their shoes are worn out, and their feet are wrapped in rags.

--

So they wrap themselves in anything they can find: the furs, and silks, and shawls, and cloaks, and carpets, and blankets, that they took from Moscow. Women's dresses, and priests' robes, and sheepskins, and fur hats, and the cloaks of their fallen comrades.

The army no longer looks like an army. Sergeant Bourgogne describes himself, at this time: wearing a woman's fur-lined cloak, of yellow silk, and a cap of fur, with a scarf tied around his head, and his face black with the smoke of camp fires, and his beard and moustache full of icicles.

He writes that the soldiers looked like a masquerade, a carnival, a crowd of strange figures, in every kind of garment, walking slowly, silently, through the snow, with their heads down, against the wind.

--

The nights are the hardest.

There is no shelter: the villages along the road have been burned. The soldiers must sleep in the open, in the snow, around their fires. They cut down trees, and pull down fences, and the ruins of houses, to make their fires. And they sit, or lie, as close to the fire as they can, wrapped in everything they have, and try to sleep.

{img:night-snow-bivouac-many-small-fires-soldiers-huddled-stars-cold}
Bourgogne writes about a night when he and his friends found an old barn, still standing, and crowded into it, dozens of men, with their fire in the middle, and slept, warm, for the first time in days. And how happy they were, for a few hours.

--

Napoleon himself walks, much of the time, in the snow, with his Guard, wearing a fur coat, and a fur hat, and carrying a stick, to keep warm, and to encourage his soldiers.

The soldiers of the Guard still march in order, in their ranks, with their bearskins, and their muskets. Napoleon's presence among them gives them courage. When they see him, walking beside them, in the snow, they still cheer.

{img:napoleon-walking-snow-fur-coat-stick-guard-beside-him-quiet}
On the ninth of November, the army reaches Smolensk, where there are supply stores, and the soldiers hope for food, and rest, and shelter. But the stores are not enough for everyone, and they are quickly emptied, and the army must go on, west, through the snow, toward the river Berezina, and Poland.
