On the sixth of November 1812, the weather changes.

The sky turns grey and heavy. A cold wind starts to blow from the north. And then the snow begins to fall. Thick, heavy, silent snow, falling all day and all night. It covers the road, the fields, the forests and the soldiers in a deep white blanket.

--

The temperature drops below freezing, and keeps dropping. The Russian winter, which Napoleon called a fairy tale, has come.

For the soldiers on the road, everything gets harder. The road vanishes under the snow. The soldiers sink in up to their knees. The horses slip on the ice, because their shoes are not made for it. Many fall and cannot get up again. The wagons and the cannons are left beside the road, in the snow.

The soldiers' clothes are not made for this. They still wear their summer uniforms, thin trousers and jackets, and their coats. Nothing more. Their shoes are worn out, and their feet are wrapped in rags.

--

So they wrap themselves in anything they can find. The furs and silks and shawls they took from Moscow. Women's dresses, priests' robes, sheepskins, blankets and carpets.

The army no longer looks like an army. Sergeant Bourgogne describes himself at this time. He wore a woman's yellow silk cloak lined with fur, and a fur cap with a scarf tied around his head. His face was black with smoke, and his beard was full of icicles.

He writes that the soldiers looked like a carnival. A crowd of strange figures in every kind of clothing, walking slowly and silently through the snow, heads down against the wind.

--

The nights are the hardest.

There is no shelter. The villages along the road were burned. The soldiers must sleep in the open, in the snow, around their fires. They cut down trees and pull down fences to burn. They sit or lie as close to the fire as they can, wrapped in everything they have, and try to sleep.

{img:night-snow-bivouac-many-small-fires-soldiers-huddled-stars-cold}
Bourgogne writes about one night when he and his friends found an old barn still standing. Dozens of men crowded into it, with their fire in the middle. They slept warm for the first time in days. For a few hours, they were happy.

--

Napoleon himself walks much of the time in the snow with his Guard. He wears a fur coat and a fur hat, and carries a stick, to keep warm and to encourage his soldiers.

The soldiers of the Guard still march in their ranks, with their tall fur hats and their muskets. When they see the Emperor walking beside them in the snow, they still cheer.

{img:napoleon-walking-snow-fur-coat-stick-guard-beside-him-quiet}
On the ninth of November, the army reaches Smolensk. There are food stores there, and the soldiers hope for food and rest and shelter. But the stores are not enough for everyone. They are quickly emptied. And the army must go on west through the snow, toward a river called the Berezina. Nobody has heard of it yet. Soon, everyone will.
