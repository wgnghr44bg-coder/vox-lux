Among all the people who marched into Russia in 1812, one of the most remarkable is the chief surgeon of the Grande Armée, Dominique Jean Larrey.

He is forty-six years old. He was born in a little village in the Pyrenees, in the south of France, the son of a shoemaker. His father passed away when he was a boy, and he was brought up by his uncle, a surgeon, in Toulouse, and learned medicine from him.

--

He has been an army surgeon for twenty years, and has followed Napoleon everywhere: to Italy, and Egypt, and Syria, and Austria, and Prussia, and Spain. He has cared for tens of thousands of wounded soldiers, in battles, and in hospitals, and in the open fields.

Larrey is a kind, honest, brave, and tireless man. He believes that a wounded soldier is a wounded soldier, whatever his rank, and whatever his nation. He treats the wounded in the order of their need, not their rank: a general must wait, if a private is more badly hurt. And he treats enemy wounded with the same care as his own.

In the early years of his career, he saw that wounded soldiers were often left on the battlefield for a day or more, before they were taken to the surgeons, because the hospitals were far behind the army, and the wagons were slow. And many soldiers were lost, who could have been saved.

So he invented a new kind of carriage: light, fast, with springs, pulled by two horses, with room for two or four wounded men, lying down, which could go right onto the battlefield, and bring the wounded quickly back to the surgeons. He called them flying ambulances. It was the beginning of the modern ambulance service.

--

He also organized teams of surgeons, and nurses, and stretcher bearers, who worked close behind the fighting, so that the wounded could be helped within minutes, or hours, instead of days.

In Russia, Larrey works without rest. Before the campaign, he has prepared everything: surgeons, and medicines, and bandages, and instruments, and wagons. On the march, he cares for the sick, who are many, in the heat, and the dust. After the battles, he works for days, without sleep.

{img:surgeon-writing-notes-candle-field-hospital-night-quiet}
And on the retreat, in the cold, he goes on caring for the soldiers. He teaches them how to protect themselves against the cold, and how to treat frozen hands and feet: not by warming them quickly, by the fire, which does great harm, but slowly, by rubbing them gently with snow, or cold water, and then warming them, little by little. This was new knowledge, in his time, and it saved many lives.

--

The soldiers love him. At the Berezina, it is said, when Larrey was trying to cross the crowded bridge, carrying his instruments, the soldiers recognized him, and passed him over their heads, from hand to hand, across the crowd, and set him down safely on the other side.

Larrey survived the campaign, and wrote about it, in his memoirs, carefully, and honestly, describing everything he had seen, and learned, so that other doctors could learn from it.

{img:soldiers-lifting-man-hand-to-hand-over-crowd-bridge-snow-gentle}
He lived until 1842. And in 1992, his remains were moved to the Invalides, in Paris, to rest near the Emperor he had served for so long. And in the courtyard of the Val-de-Grâce, the military hospital in Paris, there is a statue of him, looking down kindly on the doctors and nurses who pass by.
