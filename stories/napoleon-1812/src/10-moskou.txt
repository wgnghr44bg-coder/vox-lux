On the fourteenth of September 1812, the first soldiers of the great army come over the top of a hill west of Moscow. And for the first time, they see the city spread out below them.

They stop and stare, and then they cheer. Moscow! Moscow!

--

It is a wonderful sight. The city lies in a wide valley along a winding river, in the golden September sun. It is one of the largest cities in Europe. And it is full of churches, hundreds of them, with domes shaped like onions. They are painted green and blue and red, covered with gold, and crowned with golden crosses glittering in the sun.

Sergeant Bourgogne writes that the soldiers forgot all their hardships at the sight of it. They clapped their hands and shouted and danced.

In the middle of the city, on a hill above the river, stands the Kremlin. It is the old fortress of the Tsars, with great red walls and towers. Inside are palaces and churches with golden domes, and a tall white bell tower.

--

Napoleon waits on the hill for the leaders of the city to come out and bring him the keys, as the leaders of other capitals have done before.

But nobody comes. Hour after hour he waits. At last his officers come back from the city and tell him something strange. The city is empty.

Almost everyone in Moscow has gone. The nobles in their carriages, the merchants, and the ordinary people with whatever they could carry. Only a few remain. The governor of the city has ordered everyone to leave.

--

The French march into the city through the empty streets, with their bands playing. The houses are shuttered and the shops are closed. The only sounds are the drums and the tramp of feet, echoing from the walls. Bourgogne writes that the silence was strange, and frightening.

Napoleon moves into the palace of the Tsars in the Kremlin. He has taken Moscow. Surely now, he thinks, the Tsar will ask for peace.

{img:kremlin-palace-rooms-gilded-napoleon-window-looking-out-evening}
But that night, fires begin to break out in different parts of the city.

--

We will watch from far away again. From a hill outside the city, on that night and the nights after, you would have seen a glow in the sky over Moscow. It grew bigger and bigger as the fires spread from street to street. A strong wind drove them through the old city, which was built mostly of wood.

For four days the fires burned. Napoleon had to leave the Kremlin and stay in a palace outside the city until they died down.

{img:distant-glow-sky-over-city-night-hill-silhouette-calm-far}
Who started the fires? Most historians believe the governor of Moscow ordered them, so the French would find no shelter and no food. Some fires were perhaps accidents, started by soldiers searching empty houses by candlelight. We will never know for sure.

--

When the fires were over, about three quarters of the city was gone. The wooden houses had vanished. Only the stone churches, the stone palaces and the Kremlin still stood among the ashes and chimneys.

And the great army settled down in what was left of Moscow, to wait for the Tsar's answer. The days were still warm. But every week, the autumn was moving on.
