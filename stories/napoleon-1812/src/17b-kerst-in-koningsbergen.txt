In the last days of December 1812, the survivors of the great army reach Königsberg, in Prussia, on the shore of the Baltic Sea.

It is a city of red brick churches and tall old houses, with a castle and a cathedral on an island in the river. Along the frozen harbour stand the warehouses. For the soldiers who reach it, it is like coming back to life.

--

They arrive a few at a time, in little groups or alone, on foot or in sledges. They are ragged, thin and frozen, in their strange clothes of furs and shawls and blankets. The people of the city stare at them in pity and astonishment. Some do not even recognise them as soldiers.

But in Königsberg there are houses and warm rooms, stoves and beds, clean clothes and food. Bread and soup, meat and beer. There are hospitals where the sick and frostbitten can be cared for. And there is rest.

{img:warm-room-tiled-stove-soldiers-sleeping-beds-clean-quiet}
Many soldiers later describe the joy of that first night. A warm room and a real bed, after six months on the ground. Washing in hot water. Eating a real meal at a table. Taking off their boots. And sleeping for a whole day and night without waking.

--

And it is Christmas.

In the churches of Königsberg the bells ring and the candles are lit. People sing the old Christmas hymns in German. In some houses there are little trees decorated with candles, apples and nuts. It is a German custom that is just beginning to spread. And some families invite the poor soldiers into their homes, to share their Christmas dinner by the warm stove.

{img:german-family-christmas-candles-tree-inviting-ragged-soldier-table}
For many of the soldiers, it is the most moving Christmas of their lives. They are alive. They are warm and fed. And in a few weeks or months, they will be home.

But they also think of their friends who did not come back, lying quietly under the snow along the long road from Moscow. And they raise their glasses to them, in silence.
