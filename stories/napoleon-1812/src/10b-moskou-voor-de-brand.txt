What was Moscow like before the French came, in 1812?

It was a city unlike any other in Europe. It was not as grand or as neat as Saint Petersburg, the new capital by the sea, with its straight avenues and stone palaces. Moscow was older and more Russian. It was like a great, sprawling village.

--

It was a city of contrasts. Great palaces of the nobles, painted yellow and white and pale green, stood beside little wooden houses with carved windows, vegetable gardens and chickens. There were wide boulevards and narrow winding lanes. And everywhere there were churches, more than three hundred of them. People said there were forty times forty churches in Moscow.

On great feast days, all the bells of Moscow rang together, in a wave of sound that could be heard for miles across the countryside.

The great noble families lived in Moscow in winter, in palaces with hundreds of servants. They held balls and concerts and plays in their own private theatres. They spoke French, wore the latest Paris fashions, and some could hardly speak Russian at all.

--

And there were the merchants, with long beards and long coats and tall boots. In their shops near Red Square, they sold silk and tea and furs from China and Persia.

There were markets where farmers came in from the countryside. In winter they came on sledges loaded with frozen meat and fish, butter and eggs, wood and honey.

{img:red-square-1811-trading-rows-merchants-saint-basil-domes-snow}
And on Red Square, beside the Kremlin, stood the wonderful cathedral of Saint Basil. Its many domes were all different, twisted and striped in red, green, blue and gold, like a bunch of flowers, or something from a fairy tale.

--

When the news came that the French were coming, and that Moscow would not be defended, the people left.

The nobles packed their carriages with furniture, paintings and silver, and left for the countryside. The merchants closed their shops. Ordinary people walked out along the roads to the east with their bundles, their children and their cows. For days the roads were full of carts and carriages and people. Some families had to leave everything behind. Would there be anything left when they came back?

{img:families-leaving-moscow-carts-carriages-road-east-september-dust}
In Tolstoy's novel War and Peace, there is a famous scene about this. A family is packing to leave Moscow, with all their carts loaded. Then the young daughter, Natasha, sees wounded soldiers in the courtyard. She persuades her parents to unload the carts, leave their treasures behind, and take the wounded with them instead. It is one of the most beautiful scenes in the book.

--

Most of the people of Moscow came back in the winter and spring, after the French had gone. They found their city in ruins, their houses gone and their churches blackened.

But they began to rebuild at once. Within a few years, Moscow was alive again, with new houses, palaces and markets. And the bells rang out again on feast days, across the city.
