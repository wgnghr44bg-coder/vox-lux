Tonight we are going more than two hundred years back, to the wide plains of Russia, in the summer and the winter of the year 1812.

[long-pause]

The biggest army the world has ever seen is marching east. It moves along dusty roads, through birch forests and wheat fields, toward Moscow. More than half a million men, from all over Europe, march with their horses, their wagons and their cannons. The drums are beating and the bands are playing. Their leader is the Emperor of the French, Napoleon Bonaparte.

[pause]

They set out in the summer sunshine, full of hope. Very few of them will come home. Tonight we will follow them, gently and from a distance. We will see how they lived on the long road to Moscow, and back again through the Russian winter.

[pause]

If you like to fall asleep to slow stories from the past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, and to come back for the next one.

[long-pause]

Let us picture Europe in the year 1812.

[pause]

For almost twenty years, Europe has been at war. And for most of those years, France has been winning. Under Napoleon, the French armies have marched across Europe and beaten one enemy after another.

[long-pause]

By 1812, Napoleon controls almost all of Western and Central Europe. France itself is much bigger than today. It stretches from Hamburg to Rome. His brothers and his generals have become kings of Spain, Naples and Holland. The kings of Prussia and Austria are his allies, but not because they want to be.

[pause]

Only Britain, safe on its island behind its great navy, still fights him. And in Spain, the Spanish people and a British army are fighting a long, bitter war against the French.

[pause]

And in the east there is Russia, the huge empire of the Tsar, Alexander the First.

[long-pause]

Napoleon and Alexander were once friends, of a kind. Five years before, the two emperors met on a raft in the middle of a river. They made peace, divided Europe between them, and talked for hours, charming each other.

[pause]

But the friendship did not last. Russia would not stop trading with Britain, as Napoleon demanded. The two emperors quarrelled about trade and about power. And in 1812, Napoleon decided to invade Russia, and force the Tsar to obey him.

[pause]

{img:raft-pavilion-river-niemen-two-emperors-meeting-1807}
He thought it would be a short war. A few weeks, one great battle, a victory, and then the Tsar would ask for peace, as all his enemies had done before. He was wrong.

[long-pause]

The story of Napoleon's march into Russia is one of the most famous and saddest stories in the history of war.

[pause]

But it is also a story of ordinary people. Young soldiers and old soldiers, drummer boys and doctors. The women who followed the army. The Russian farmers in their villages. And the horses, the long roads, the campfires and the snow. Many soldiers kept diaries, or wrote down their memories years later. Thanks to them, we can still follow the road to Moscow, step by step, through their eyes.

[pause]

{img:soldier-writing-diary-campfire-night-shako-candle}
We will not watch the battles. Tonight we will walk beside the soldiers, day by day and night by night, through the summer, the autumn and the winter of 1812.

[long-pause]

{img:napoleon-grey-greatcoat-bicorne-hat-thoughtful-campfire-1812}
In 1812, Napoleon Bonaparte is forty-two years old.

[pause]

He is not as short as people later believed. He is of average height for a Frenchman of his time. He is getting a little stout. He has short dark hair, grey eyes and a pale face. In the field he wears a simple green or blue uniform. Over it he wears his famous grey coat, and his black hat turned sideways.

[long-pause]

He was born on the island of Corsica, in the Mediterranean, into a poor noble family. As a boy he was sent to a military school in France. The other boys laughed at his accent and at how poor he was. He was lonely, and he read books about history and great generals.

[pause]

He became an officer in charge of cannons. During the French Revolution, many of the old officers fled. Young men with talent could rise quickly, and Napoleon rose faster than anyone. At twenty-four he was a general. At thirty he ruled France. At thirty-five he crowned himself Emperor of the French.

[pause]

Everyone agrees he is a genius. He can work eighteen hours a day, and sleep anywhere for a few hours, even on horseback. He remembers everything. The names of officers, the size of every regiment, the details of laws. He made new laws for France that are still the basis of the laws of many countries today.

[long-pause]

His soldiers love him. He knows how to talk to them and make them feel part of something great. He walks among them at their campfires, pulls their ears fondly, and remembers their faces. They call him the little corporal. They would follow him anywhere.

[pause]

But he is also proud and impatient. He believes in his lucky star. He has won so many battles that he thinks he cannot lose.

[pause]

{img:napoleon-walking-among-soldiers-bivouac-fires-grenadiers-cheering}
Two years ago he married a young Austrian princess, Marie Louise. Last year she gave birth to a son. Napoleon loves the little boy very much. He has brought a portrait of him to Russia, and shows it proudly to his officers.

[long-pause]

In the spring of 1812, Napoleon leaves Paris and travels east to Dresden, in Germany. All the kings and princes of his empire come to meet him there, and bow to him, at a great court with feasts and balls.

[pause]

Then he goes on to the river Niemen, the border of the Russian Empire, where his great army is waiting.

[pause]

{img:dresden-1812-palace-court-ball-kings-princes-chandeliers}
His advisers have warned him. Russia is enormous, they say. The roads are bad and the distances are vast. An army cannot feed itself there. And the Russian winter is terrible. But Napoleon does not listen. He plans to win one great battle quickly, in the summer, and be home before the snow falls.

[long-pause]

{img:empress-marie-louise-baby-king-of-rome-cradle-tuileries-1811}
In Paris, in the summer of 1812, in a royal palace, there is a little boy of one year old. He has fair curly hair and blue eyes. He does not know that his father has gone to war.

[pause]

He is Napoleon's son. At his birth he was given the title King of Rome. When he was born, a hundred and one cannons were fired in Paris, to tell everyone that the Emperor had a son. And the people cheered in the streets.

[long-pause]

Napoleon loves him dearly. Before leaving for Russia, he played with him on the floor, carried him around, and let him play with his sword and his hat. He has asked a painter to make a picture of the boy, to be sent to him in Russia.

[pause]

The boy's mother, the Empress Marie Louise, is twenty years old. As a child in Austria, she was taught to fear Napoleon as the enemy of her family. But when she married him, she found him kind and gentle with her. Now she writes to him often in Russia about their son. He has new teeth. He is learning to walk. He is learning to say papa.

[pause]

{img:small-boy-toddler-walking-garden-saint-cloud-nurse-summer}
The boy is looked after by a kind, wise governess, whom he calls Maman Quiou. Every evening she teaches him to say his prayers, and to pray for his papa, far away. Every evening, far away in Russia, his papa is marching further from home.

[long-pause]

Two years later, when Napoleon has lost his throne, the little boy is taken by his mother to Vienna, to the court of his Austrian grandfather. There he is given a new name and raised as an Austrian prince. He never sees his father again.

[pause]

He grows up into a tall, gentle, serious young man, who loves to read about his father's battles. He dies of an illness at only twenty-one. People call him the little eagle.

[pause]

{img:young-man-palace-window-vienna-reading-books-thoughtful-1830}
More than a hundred years later, his remains are brought to Paris, and laid to rest near his father.

[long-pause]

{img:soldiers-many-nations-uniforms-camp-poland-1812-colourful}
In June 1812, the great army is gathered along the river Niemen, on the border of the Russian Empire.

[pause]

It is the biggest army Europe has ever seen. About four hundred and fifty thousand men cross into Russia in the first days, and more come later. In all, more than six hundred thousand men will march into Russia. With them come more than a hundred and fifty thousand horses, more than a thousand cannons and thousands of wagons.

[long-pause]

Only about half of the soldiers are French. The others come from all over Napoleon's empire and from his allies. There are Germans and Poles, Italians and Austrians, Dutch, Belgians, Swiss and Spaniards. The Poles hope that Napoleon will give them back their own country.

[pause]

They speak a dozen languages. Some people call it the army of twenty nations.

[pause]

{img:polish-lancers-red-white-pennants-horses-forest-road-summer}
The soldiers are of every kind. There is the Imperial Guard, the very best of the army. They are tall, experienced veterans with big fur hats and moustaches. Napoleon calls them his old grumblers, because they grumble, but always follow him. There are the ordinary foot soldiers, many of them only eighteen or nineteen, called up from the villages of France and Germany.

[long-pause]

There are the horsemen. The heavy cavalry, big men on big horses, with steel breastplates and long plumes on their helmets. The light cavalry, with fur-trimmed jackets slung over one shoulder. And the Polish lancers, with red and white flags fluttering on their lances. There are the gunners with their cannons, each pulled by a team of horses.

[pause]

There are engineers who build bridges, doctors and bakers, carpenters and blacksmiths.

[pause]

One of the soldiers is a young German stonemason called Jakob Walter. He will write down his memories years later, in a simple, honest little book. Another is a French sergeant of the Guard called Adrien Bourgogne. He will write a famous book too. And there is the chief doctor of the army, Dominique Larrey, a kind and brilliant man, who will care for the wounded of both sides with great courage.

[long-pause]

And there are women.

[pause]

With every regiment there are a few women who sell food, drink and tobacco to the soldiers from little carts. Most are soldiers' wives. They wash and mend clothes, and nurse the sick and the wounded. They are brave and tough, and the soldiers love them.

[pause]

{img:cantiniere-woman-small-cart-barrels-soldiers-buying-camp}
Many others follow the army too. Servants, cooks, merchants, and even some officers' families in carriages. Altogether it is like a great moving city, stretching for miles along the roads.

[long-pause]

On the evening of the twenty-third of June 1812, Napoleon rides along the bank of the river Niemen to look at the place where the army will cross.

[pause]

As he rides, a hare runs out under his horse's feet. The horse rears, and Napoleon falls to the ground. He gets up at once, unhurt, and climbs back into the saddle without a word. But some officers who saw it think it is a bad sign. One of them whispers: a Roman general would turn back.

[pause]

{img:quiet-riverbank-evening-niemen-forest-far-side-summer}
That night, engineers build three bridges of boats across the river. And in the morning, the twenty-fourth of June 1812, the great army begins to cross into Russia. On the far bank, the forest is silent. There is no enemy in sight. Where are the Russians?

[long-pause]

{img:surgeon-larrey-portrait-uniform-kind-face-1812-tent}
Among all the people who marched into Russia in 1812, one of the most remarkable is the chief doctor of the army, Dominique Larrey.

[pause]

He is forty-six years old. He was born in a small village in the mountains of southern France, the son of a shoemaker. His father died when he was a boy. He was raised by his uncle, a surgeon, who taught him medicine.

[long-pause]

He has been an army doctor for twenty years. He has followed Napoleon everywhere, to Italy and Egypt, Austria and Spain. He has cared for tens of thousands of wounded soldiers.

[pause]

Larrey is kind, honest, brave and tireless. He believes a wounded soldier is a wounded soldier, whatever his rank or his country. He treats the wounded in order of need, not rank. A general must wait if a private is hurt worse. And he treats enemy wounded just as carefully as his own.

[pause]

When he was young, he saw that wounded soldiers were often left lying on the battlefield for a day or more. The hospitals were far behind the army, and the wagons were slow. Many men died who could have been saved.

[pause]

So he invented a new kind of carriage. It was light and fast, with springs, pulled by two horses, with room for a few wounded men lying down. It could drive right onto the battlefield and bring the wounded quickly to the doctors. He called them flying ambulances. It was the beginning of the ambulance as we know it.

[long-pause]

He also set up teams of doctors and stretcher bearers who worked close behind the fighting. That way, the wounded got help within hours instead of days.

[pause]

In Russia, Larrey works without rest. Before the march, he has prepared doctors, medicines, bandages and wagons. On the march, he cares for the many sick in the heat and the dust.

[pause]

{img:surgeon-writing-notes-candle-field-hospital-night-quiet}
And later, in the cold, he will teach the soldiers how to care for frozen hands and feet. Not by warming them fast by the fire, which does great harm. But slowly, by rubbing them gently with snow or cold water, and then warming them little by little. This was new knowledge in his time, and it saved many lives.

[long-pause]

The soldiers love him. There is a story that, at a crowded river crossing later on, the soldiers recognised Larrey. They lifted him up and passed him over their heads from hand to hand, and set him down safely on the other side.

[pause]

Larrey survived the campaign. Afterwards he wrote down everything he had seen and learned, so that other doctors could learn from it.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-lifting-man-hand-to-hand-over-crowd-bridge-snow-gentle}
He lived to be an old man. Today his remains rest in Paris, near the Emperor he served for so long. And in the courtyard of a military hospital in Paris, there is a statue of him, looking down kindly on the doctors and nurses who pass by.

[long-pause]

{img:pontoon-bridges-river-niemen-army-crossing-sunrise-bands-playing}
On the morning of the twenty-fourth of June 1812, the sun rises in a clear blue sky over the river Niemen, and the great army begins to cross.

[pause]

For three days, the columns march over the bridges of boats without stopping, day and night. Foot soldiers, horsemen, cannons and wagons pass in an endless stream. Their flags are flying and their bands are playing. They cheer as they pass the Emperor, who sits on a little hill by the bridges, watching them.

[long-pause]

On the other side of the river lies Russia. And there is nothing there.

[pause]

No Russian army. No soldiers. Nobody to fight. Only forests and fields, and an empty road leading east. The Russian army has pulled back deep into its huge country. It is waiting.

[pause]

The soldiers are surprised, and a little disappointed. They expected a battle. Instead there is only the road. And nobody knows where it will end.

[long-pause]

At first they march through Lithuania. The land is flat or gently rolling, with great forests of pine and birch, wide fields of rye and wheat, marshes and lakes. The villages are small and poor. The little wooden houses have thatched roofs and fences of wooden stakes. There are wooden churches with onion-shaped domes.

[pause]

The roads are not paved. They are tracks of earth. In the summer heat they turn to deep, soft dust, and in the rain to thick mud.

[pause]

On the second day after the crossing, a great thunderstorm breaks. Rain pours down, the air turns suddenly cold and the roads become rivers of mud. Thousands of horses, already worn out and sick from eating wet green rye, collapse and do not get up. It is a warning of what is to come. But few people want to see it that way yet.

[long-pause]

At the end of June, the army reaches the city of Vilnius, the capital of Lithuania. Only a few days before, the Tsar himself was there. He was at a ball at a country house nearby when he heard that Napoleon had crossed the river.

[pause]

Napoleon stays in Vilnius for more than two weeks, organising his army and his supplies. Many Poles and Lithuanians in the city welcome him. They hope he will free them from Russian rule.

[pause]

{img:vilnius-1812-baroque-churches-old-town-streets-soldiers-arriving}
Vilnius is a beautiful city, with many churches painted white, pink and yellow, and a castle on a hill. The soldiers rest there, eat and drink, and write letters home.

[long-pause]

Then the army marches on, east, deeper into Russia. They follow the Russian armies, which always seem to be one day ahead, always just out of reach.

[pause]

The Russian generals have decided not to fight a big battle near the border against Napoleon's enormous army. Instead they retreat, and retreat again. They draw the French further and further into the vast spaces of Russia, away from their supplies and their homes.

[pause]

{img:long-dusty-road-east-columns-soldiers-horizon-heat-haze}
And as they retreat, they burn the villages, the crops and the stores behind them, so the French will find nothing to eat. Every day, the smoke rises ahead of the army. And every day, the road is emptier.

[long-pause]

{img:lithuanian-summer-landscape-storks-nest-wooden-farm-lake-forest}
In the first weeks, in the summer of 1812, the soldiers march through the countryside of Lithuania and Belarus. Many of them write about its quiet beauty in their letters.

[pause]

It is a land of forests, lakes and meadows, with little rivers winding through the reeds. The meadows are full of cornflowers, poppies and clover. On the barn roofs and the tops of old trees, white storks have built big nests. They stand on one leg, clatter their beaks, and fly slowly over the fields looking for frogs.

[long-pause]

In the forests there are bears, wolves, elk and wild boar, and beavers in the rivers. In the evenings, nightingales sing in the bushes by the water, and frogs call from the marshes.

[pause]

The villages are small and quiet and poor. There are little wooden houses with steep roofs, gardens with sunflowers and beehives made of hollow logs. At the crossroads stand carved and painted wooden crosses.

[pause]

{img:small-market-town-wooden-houses-synagogue-square-carts-summer}
There are little market towns where many Jewish families live, as merchants, craftsmen and innkeepers. They have wooden synagogues, markets and schools. The soldiers buy food from them and sleep in their inns. Later, in the winter, many of these families will take in frozen, starving soldiers, and give them food and warmth and shelter.

[long-pause]

On long summer evenings, the soldiers sometimes swim in the lakes and rivers, to wash off the dust and cool down. They pick wild strawberries and blueberries in the forest. They fish. They lie in the grass, smoke their pipes and watch the storks and the clouds.

[pause]

For a little while it feels almost like a holiday. Many of the young soldiers come from cities, or from the vineyards of France, or the mountains of Switzerland. They have never seen such wide, quiet, empty country. Some of them write home that it is beautiful.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-swimming-lake-summer-evening-uniforms-on-grass-laughing}
But the land is poor, and it cannot feed so many men. And day by day, the road leads them further from home. The older soldiers look at the long, empty road ahead, and say nothing.

[long-pause]

{img:infantry-column-dawn-march-drums-shakos-mist-fields}
Let us spend an ordinary day with a soldier of the great army, on the march in the summer of 1812.

[pause]

He is a young foot soldier, perhaps twenty years old, from a village in France or Germany. He was called up two or three years ago, and has been in the army ever since. He has fought in one or two battles. He is tough and used to hardship. He grumbles, as soldiers do. But he is proud of his regiment and of his Emperor.

[long-pause]

The day begins before dawn. A drum beats in the dark, and the soldiers wake up on the ground where they slept, wrapped in their coats beside last night's fire.

[pause]

They roll up their coats and strap them to their packs. They put on their tall black hats with a brass eagle on the front. They eat a little, a piece of hard biscuit or bread if there is any, and drink some water.

[pause]

{img:soldier-packing-knapsack-greatcoat-musket-dawn-campfire}
Then they line up in their companies. The drums beat, and they set off along the road in long columns, four men side by side.

[long-pause]

A soldier carries a heavy load, perhaps twenty-five or thirty kilos.

[pause]

There is his long, heavy musket with its bayonet. There is a box of cartridges, little paper tubes filled with gunpowder and a lead ball. There is his pack, with spare shirts and shoes, a comb, a sewing kit, and perhaps a few letters from home. On top is his rolled coat. And he carries food for several days, biscuit, flour and rice.

[pause]

In the summer of 1812 it is very hot. The sun beats down on the long columns. The dust rises from the road in thick white clouds. It is so thick that the soldiers cannot see the men in front of them. It fills their eyes, their mouths and their noses. And there is very little water. The village wells are few, and soon drunk dry.

[long-pause]

The soldiers march for hours, twenty or thirty kilometres a day, with a short rest every hour or so. Then they sit down by the road, take off their packs and rest their feet.

[pause]

They sing as they march, to keep their spirits up and to keep in step. Old marching songs, songs from home, and funny songs about their officers and their girls. When the Emperor rides past, they cheer: long live the Emperor!

[pause]

{img:soldiers-resting-roadside-shade-birch-trees-canteens-singing}
Jakob Walter, the young German, later writes about the heat and the thirst and the dust. He tells how the soldiers drank from puddles and ditches, and even from the hoof prints of the horses. And how many of them fell ill.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, the march goes on. Feet are sore and blistered. Shoes wear out on the long roads. The soldiers must mend them, or find new ones, or march barefoot.

[pause]

Then, in the late afternoon, the order comes to stop and make camp for the night. They stop beside the road, in a field or at the edge of a forest, near a village or a stream.

[pause]

{img:column-halting-evening-meadow-forest-edge-soldiers-stacking-muskets}
The soldiers stand their muskets together in little pyramids, take off their packs and stretch. And then, tired as they are, they must find food, water and wood for their fires before they can rest. Each evening, it is a little harder to find any food at all.

[long-pause]

{img:soldier-writing-letter-drum-as-desk-camp-summer-evening}
Every evening in camp, in the summer of 1812, some soldiers sit by the fire and write letters home.

[pause]

They write on whatever paper they can find, with pencils or ink, using their packs or their drums as desks. Those who cannot write ask a friend or a sergeant to write for them.

[long-pause]

The army has its own post. Riders on fast horses carry the letters along the roads, from station to station, between the army and Paris, day and night. A letter from Russia can reach Paris in two or three weeks.

[pause]

So all through the summer and autumn, letters travel back and forth between the soldiers and their families.

[pause]

Many of these letters still exist. Some were captured later by Russian horsemen, and kept in archives in Moscow for two hundred years, never delivered. In recent years, historians have studied and published them, so we can still read them.

[long-pause]

They are mostly simple letters from ordinary young men to their mothers and fathers, their wives and their sweethearts.

[pause]

They write about the long marches, the heat and the dust and the bad roads. About the strange country with its wooden villages and onion-domed churches. About the food, or the lack of it. About their friends in the regiment. And later, about Moscow.

[pause]

{img:mother-reading-letter-french-village-kitchen-window-light}
And they ask about home. How is the harvest? How is the cow? Is the new baby well? Give my love to grandmother. Tell my sweetheart I think of her every day. Pray for me. I hope to be home by winter.

[long-pause]

The officers write longer letters. Napoleon himself writes almost every day to his wife. Short, loving letters. He asks about her health and their little son. He tells her he is well and the weather is fine, and asks her to give the little boy a kiss from his father.

[pause]

At home, the families wait for the letters. They read them again and again, out loud by the fire, to the whole family and the neighbours. They keep them carefully in a box for the rest of their lives.

[pause]

{img:old-letters-tied-ribbon-wooden-box-candle-quiet-room}
In the winter, the letters stopped coming. Many families waited for months and years for news that never came. Some kept a candle burning in the window every night, in case their son came home.

[pause]

And some sons did come home, one day, long after everyone had given up hope. Thin and changed, they walked up the village street in spring or summer. And then there was great joy.

[long-pause]

{img:bivouac-evening-many-campfires-soldiers-cooking-pots-forest-edge}
The soldiers of the great army do not sleep in tents. Napoleon believes tents slow an army down. So every night, the soldiers sleep in the open air, around their campfires. This is called a bivouac.

[pause]

In summer, it is not so bad. In autumn and winter, it will be very hard indeed.

[long-pause]

When the army stops for the night, the soldiers split into little groups of six or eight. They share their food, their fire and their cooking pot, and look after each other. These little groups are like families. The men in them are friends, and they stay together through everything.

[pause]

Some go to fetch wood from the forest, or from the fences of the nearest village. Some fetch water. And some go looking for food.

[pause]

{img:foraging-soldiers-village-searching-barns-hens-sacks-evening}
Finding food is the most important job of all. The army cannot carry enough for so many men. The wagons with bread and flour are far behind on the bad roads. So the soldiers must find food for themselves in the villages and farms near the road. Grain and potatoes, cabbages and chickens, anything at all. But the Russian farmers have often fled into the forests with their animals. More and more often, the villages are empty.

[long-pause]

Back at the fire, the group's cook makes soup in the big iron pot.

[pause]

A soldier's soup is made of whatever they can find. Water, and some meat if they are lucky. Flour or rice, cabbage or potatoes, and salt if they have any. It boils for a long time over the fire. Then the soldiers sit around the pot and eat it with their spoons. They carry their spoons stuck in a buttonhole, or in their hats.

[pause]

When there is grain but no flour, they grind it themselves between stones, and bake flat little cakes in the ashes.

[long-pause]

After supper, the soldiers sit by the fires. They smoke their pipes, talk and sing. They play cards, mend their clothes and clean their muskets.

[pause]

The old soldiers tell stories of their old campaigns, of Egypt and the pyramids, and of great victories long ago. The young soldiers listen, and dream of glory.

[pause]

{img:old-grenadier-telling-stories-young-conscripts-listening-fire-pipe}
Sergeant Bourgogne remembers these summer evenings. The fires stretched away for miles across the plain, like stars in the sky. He could hear thousands of voices talking and singing in many languages, and the bands playing at the generals' tents.

[long-pause]

At last the soldiers lie down to sleep on the ground. They lie on straw if they can find any, wrapped in their coats, with their packs as pillows, close to the fire and close to each other.

[pause]

Guards walk up and down at the edge of the camp, looking out into the dark and listening. The fires burn low. The horses stamp and shift. And the great army sleeps under the stars, in the middle of Russia.

[pause]

{img:sleeping-soldiers-greatcoats-around-embers-stars-sentry-silhouette}
In summer, the nights are short and warm. Dawn comes at three or four in the morning. Then the drum beats again, and another day of marching begins. Nobody thinks much about how long the nights will be in winter.

[long-pause]

{img:artillery-horses-pulling-cannon-muddy-road-drivers-1812}
An army in 1812 runs on horses.

[pause]

There are horses for the cavalry. There are horses for the officers and the messengers. There are teams of six or eight horses to pull each cannon. And there are thousands of horses and oxen to pull the supply wagons, full of bread and flour, gunpowder and medicine.

[long-pause]

More than a hundred and fifty thousand horses march into Russia with the great army.

[pause]

They come from all over Europe. Big, strong horses from Normandy and Flanders for the heavy cavalry and the cannons. Light, quick horses from Hungary and Poland for the light cavalry.

[pause]

A horseman's horse is his most precious possession and his friend. He must feed it, water it and brush it before he looks after himself. A good horseman, the old soldiers say, will go hungry himself before he lets his horse go hungry.

[long-pause]

But in Russia there is a terrible problem. There is not enough food for the horses.

[pause]

A horse needs a lot of hay and oats and water every day. The army cannot carry enough. So the horses must graze along the way. But there are so many of them that the fields beside the road are soon eaten bare. And they are given green, unripe rye from the fields, which makes them ill.

[pause]

After the great thunderstorm, the heat, the bad food and the long marches, thousands of horses fall ill and cannot go on. By the time the army reaches Moscow, a great many horses have been lost. And this will matter very much later, in the winter. Without horses, how will the army carry its food home?

[long-pause]

The wagons have problems too. The roads are terrible. Deep sand and dust in summer, deep mud when it rains. The wagons sink in up to their axles and must be pushed and dug out. They break down and fall behind. So the soldiers at the front often have no bread, while the bread is far away, stuck on the roads.

[pause]

The Russian farmers have their own little carts, light and simple, pulled by one small horse. They are much better on Russian roads. Many soldiers buy or take them for their baggage.

[pause]

{img:small-russian-peasant-cart-kibitka-shaggy-horse-sandy-road}
The little Russian horses are shaggy and tough. They are used to the climate. They can eat almost anything and live through the winter. The big horses from the West cannot.

[long-pause]

And still the army marches east, through the heat of July and August.

[pause]

They reach the town of Vitebsk. Napoleon stops there for two weeks. For a while he thinks about staying there for the winter, and going on in spring. It would have been a wise choice. But he decides to go on. On to Smolensk, an old city with great red walls on the river Dnieper. There, in the middle of August, there is a battle, and the city burns.

[pause]

{img:smolensk-red-walls-river-dnieper-domes-distance-haze}
The Russians retreat again, toward Moscow. And Napoleon follows them along the great road, as summer comes to an end and the nights grow cooler.

[long-pause]

{img:cossack-village-don-river-steppe-houses-horses-summer}
Who are the Cossacks, the horsemen who will follow the great army all the way from Moscow to the border?

[pause]

They are free horsemen from the great southern grasslands of Russia, along rivers like the Don and the Volga. Hundreds of years ago, their ancestors were farmers and runaways who fled from their lords to the wild open lands of the south. There they lived freely as hunters, fishermen and farmers.

[long-pause]

They live in their own villages along the rivers. They choose their own leaders. In return for their freedom and their land, every Cossack man must serve the Tsar as a soldier, on his own horse, with his own weapons.

[pause]

They are wonderful riders. A Cossack boy is put on a horse at the age of three. They can ride for days and sleep in the saddle. They can pick things up from the ground at full gallop. Their horses are small and shaggy and tough. They can find food under the snow, and survive the winter when the big Western horses cannot.

[pause]

{img:cossack-horseman-lance-fur-hat-long-coat-snow-plain-calm}
They wear long dark coats and tall fur hats. They carry long lances and curved swords. As they ride, they sing long, sad, beautiful songs about the grasslands, the river Don, their mothers and their horses.

[long-pause]

In 1812, the Cossacks do not fight big battles. Instead they scout and raid. They follow the French army on both sides of the road, day and night. They swoop on soldiers who wander off to look for food, and on wagons and messengers. Then they vanish into the forest before the French can catch them. The French soldiers fear them more than anything. Every time a soldier steps away from the column, he wonders if the Cossacks are watching.

[pause]

Their leader is an old Cossack general called Platov. He is brave, clever and cheerful. He loves his Cossacks, and they love him.

[pause]

{img:cossack-camp-night-fire-singing-horses-tethered-stars}
After the war, the Cossacks ride all the way to Paris with the Russian army. They camp right in the middle of the city, and Parisians come to stare at them. There is a story that the Cossacks banged on café tables and shouted bystro, which means quickly in Russian. Some say that is where the French word bistro comes from, for a little café. It is probably not true. But it is a good story.

[long-pause]

{img:tsar-alexander-first-tall-fair-uniform-epaulettes-palace-window}
Who are the people on the other side of this war?

[pause]

The ruler of Russia is the Tsar, Alexander the First. He is thirty-four years old, tall, fair, handsome and charming, with blue eyes and gentle manners. He is a little deaf in one ear. He speaks French better than Russian, as all Russian nobles do.

[long-pause]

He is a complicated man. He dreams of great ideals and he is deeply religious. But he can also be clever, stubborn and secretive. And in 1812 he makes one decision, and keeps it, whatever happens. He will never make peace with Napoleon as long as one French soldier stands on Russian soil. Even if he has to retreat all the way to Siberia, he says, and grow a beard and live on potatoes with his farmers.

[pause]

The Russian army is a great army too, though smaller than Napoleon's.

[pause]

{img:russian-infantry-green-coats-shakos-marching-birch-forest}
Its soldiers are mostly farmers who belong to their lords. They are taken from their villages for twenty-five years, almost their whole lives. They are tough, patient and brave. They fight for their Tsar and their church. Before battle, priests carry holy paintings along the lines, and the soldiers kneel and pray.

[long-pause]

The best-known Russian soldiers are the Cossacks, the wild horsemen of the south, who appear out of nowhere and disappear again.

[pause]

At first, the Russian armies are led by a careful general called Barclay de Tolly. His plan is to retreat and wear out the French. But the Russians hate retreating. Many of them do not trust Barclay, because his family came from Scotland and Germany. So in August, the Tsar chooses a new commander, Mikhail Kutuzov.

[pause]

Kutuzov is sixty-seven. He is fat and old and has lost an eye in a long-ago battle. His enemies say he is lazy. He sleeps a lot, reads French novels and loves good food. But he is wise, patient and cunning. The soldiers love him, because he is one of them, and he cares about them. They call him grandfather. And grandfather has a plan. He is going to let time and winter do the fighting.

[long-pause]

And then there are the Russian farmers in their villages along the road.

[pause]

Most of them belong to their lords and live very hard lives. They live in little wooden houses, and grow rye, potatoes and cabbages. They keep a cow and a few chickens. In the corner of every room there is a holy painting with a little lamp burning in front of it. They wear long shirts, shoes woven from birch bark, and sheepskin coats in winter.

[pause]

{img:russian-peasant-family-izba-icon-corner-lamp-stove-evening}
When the French army comes, most of them flee into the forests with their animals and their food. As the war goes on, many take up axes, pitchforks and old muskets. In little bands in the forests, they attack the French who wander off looking for food.

[long-pause]

The great Russian writer Leo Tolstoy, born sixteen years after 1812, will write a famous novel about this war, called War and Peace. In it he describes the Russian people rising up together, like a great wave, to drive the invader out.

[pause]

{img:birch-forest-autumn-gold-leaves-quiet-path-russia}
And in the summer of 1812, as the French march toward Moscow, the people of Russia are getting ready.

[long-pause]

{img:russian-izba-winter-snow-smoke-chimney-sledge-horse-blue-dusk}
How do the Russians themselves live through their long, hard winters?

[pause]

For them, winter is not a terrible enemy. It is simply part of life, as it has been for hundreds of years. They know exactly how to live with it. The soldiers from the West do not.

[long-pause]

The heart of every Russian farmhouse is the stove.

[pause]

It is enormous. It is a great block of brick and clay, painted white, taking up a quarter of the room. It has an oven for cooking and baking bread. It is lit once a day, in the morning, with birch logs. Its thick walls soak up the heat and give it out slowly, all day and all night. So the house stays warm even when it is thirty degrees below zero outside.

[pause]

{img:russian-stove-interior-family-sleeping-on-top-warm-icons-lamp}
On top of the stove there is a flat shelf where the old people and the children sleep, snug on blankets and sheepskins. It is the warmest and best place in the house.

[long-pause]

The houses are built of thick round logs laid on top of each other. The gaps are filled with moss to keep out the wind. In winter, snow is piled up against the walls outside, to keep the warmth in.

[pause]

The farmers wear thick clothes made for the cold. Long sheepskin coats with the wool inside, down to the ankles. Fur hats with flaps over the ears. Woollen mittens. And on their feet, thick boots of felted wool, made in one piece, which keep the feet warm and dry in the dry frozen snow.

[pause]

{img:peasants-sheepskin-coats-fur-hats-felt-boots-village-snow-sun}
If the soldiers of the great army had had clothes like these, many more of them would have come home.

[long-pause]

In winter, the Russians travel by sledge. The snow makes the roads impossible for wagons, but perfect for sledges. The rivers freeze hard and become roads. People travel quickly across the snowy land in sledges pulled by small shaggy horses, with bells on their harness jingling in the frosty air.

[pause]

And every week, many families go to the bath house. It is a little wooden hut by the river, with a stove and hot stones. They throw water on the stones to make clouds of steam, and sit in the heat. Then they run outside and roll in the snow, or jump into a hole in the river ice, and run back in, laughing.

[pause]

{img:wooden-banya-bathhouse-steam-snow-birch-twigs-riverbank}
The long winter evenings are spent around the stove. The women spin and sew by the light of a little lamp. The men mend their tools. The old people tell fairy tales. Stories about Baba Yaga, the witch who lives in a hut on chicken legs deep in the forest. About Father Frost, the firebird, and brave young Ivan.

[long-pause]

And there are winter festivals. At Epiphany, the priest blesses the water through a hole cut in the river ice in the shape of a cross. At the end of winter there is butter week. Everyone eats pancakes and goes sledging down hills. They have snowball fights, and burn a straw figure of winter to welcome the spring.

[pause]

The Russians love their winter. Their poets write about the white silence of the forests, and frost on the windows like silver ferns. They write about the bright sun on the snow, and the sound of sledge bells far away across the white fields.

[long-pause]

{img:borodino-fields-september-dawn-mist-village-church-distance}
At the beginning of September 1812, about a hundred and twenty kilometres west of Moscow, the Russian army stops retreating. It turns to fight.

[pause]

Kutuzov has chosen a place near a little village called Borodino. There is a white church beside a little river, among rolling fields and woods, right across the great road to Moscow. There the Russians dig earth walls, and wait.

[long-pause]

On the evening before the battle, the two armies camp close to each other in the fields. Their fires burn all night, for miles.

[pause]

In the Russian camp, priests carry a holy painting of the Virgin Mary along the lines. It was rescued from the burning city of Smolensk. The soldiers kneel, take off their caps and pray, and the priests bless them.

[pause]

In the French camp that evening, a messenger arrives from Paris with a portrait of Napoleon's little son. Napoleon has it placed on a chair outside his tent, so the soldiers of the Guard can see it. The old soldiers come and look at the picture of the little boy. Some of them have tears in their eyes. Tomorrow many of them will fight their hardest day.

[long-pause]

At dawn on the seventh of September, the battle begins.

[pause]

We will not go into the battle. We will stand far away, on a little hill at the edge of the forest. From there we would see only smoke. Great clouds of white smoke drifting across the fields in the autumn sunshine. And we would hear the endless thunder of more than a thousand cannons, all day long, from dawn until dark.

[pause]

It was one of the greatest and hardest battles in history. By evening, very many men on both sides had been lost or wounded. Neither side had really won. During the night the Russian army pulled back in good order toward Moscow. And the French held the field.

[long-pause]

The doctor Larrey worked all that day, all night and all the next day without stopping. He cared for the wounded, French and Russian alike, in barns and in open fields.

[pause]

{img:surgeon-larrey-tending-wounded-field-hospital-barn-lanterns-calm}
Napoleon himself was not well that day. He had a bad cold and a fever, and could hardly speak. He did not send in his Guard, his best soldiers. He said he was too far from Paris to risk them.

[long-pause]

After the battle, Kutuzov held a meeting with his generals in a farmer's hut just outside Moscow. They sat around a table under the holy paintings. The farmer's little granddaughter watched them from the top of the big stove.

[pause]

They argued about whether to fight another battle to defend Moscow. At last Kutuzov spoke. If we fight, he said, we may lose the army, and then Russia is lost. But if we give up Moscow, we keep the army, and Russia is saved. So he ordered the army to retreat, through Moscow and beyond, and leave the city to the French.

[pause]

{img:peasant-hut-council-generals-table-icons-child-on-stove-candle}
It was a terrible decision. Moscow was the ancient, holy heart of Russia. But Kutuzov believed it was right. And now Napoleon would get what he wanted. Or so he thought.

[long-pause]

{img:moscow-1812-golden-domes-kremlin-towers-view-from-hill-september}
On the fourteenth of September 1812, the first soldiers of the great army come over the top of a hill west of Moscow. And for the first time, they see the city spread out below them.

[pause]

They stop and stare, and then they cheer. Moscow! Moscow!

[long-pause]

It is a wonderful sight. The city lies in a wide valley along a winding river, in the golden September sun. It is one of the largest cities in Europe. And it is full of churches, hundreds of them, with domes shaped like onions. They are painted green and blue and red, covered with gold, and crowned with golden crosses glittering in the sun.

[pause]

Sergeant Bourgogne writes that the soldiers forgot all their hardships at the sight of it. They clapped their hands and shouted and danced.

[pause]

In the middle of the city, on a hill above the river, stands the Kremlin. It is the old fortress of the Tsars, with great red walls and towers. Inside are palaces and churches with golden domes, and a tall white bell tower.

[long-pause]

Napoleon waits on the hill for the leaders of the city to come out and bring him the keys, as the leaders of other capitals have done before.

[pause]

But nobody comes. Hour after hour he waits. At last his officers come back from the city and tell him something strange. The city is empty.

[pause]

Almost everyone in Moscow has gone. The nobles in their carriages, the merchants, and the ordinary people with whatever they could carry. Only a few remain. The governor of the city has ordered everyone to leave.

[long-pause]

The French march into the city through the empty streets, with their bands playing. The houses are shuttered and the shops are closed. The only sounds are the drums and the tramp of feet, echoing from the walls. Bourgogne writes that the silence was strange, and frightening.

[pause]

Napoleon moves into the palace of the Tsars in the Kremlin. He has taken Moscow. Surely now, he thinks, the Tsar will ask for peace.

[pause]

{img:kremlin-palace-rooms-gilded-napoleon-window-looking-out-evening}
But that night, fires begin to break out in different parts of the city.

[long-pause]

We will watch from far away again. From a hill outside the city, on that night and the nights after, you would have seen a glow in the sky over Moscow. It grew bigger and bigger as the fires spread from street to street. A strong wind drove them through the old city, which was built mostly of wood.

[pause]

For four days the fires burned. Napoleon had to leave the Kremlin and stay in a palace outside the city until they died down.

[pause]

{img:distant-glow-sky-over-city-night-hill-silhouette-calm-far}
Who started the fires? Most historians believe the governor of Moscow ordered them, so the French would find no shelter and no food. Some fires were perhaps accidents, started by soldiers searching empty houses by candlelight. We will never know for sure.

[long-pause]

When the fires were over, about three quarters of the city was gone. The wooden houses had vanished. Only the stone churches, the stone palaces and the Kremlin still stood among the ashes and chimneys.

[pause]

And the great army settled down in what was left of Moscow, to wait for the Tsar's answer. The days were still warm. But every week, the autumn was moving on.

[long-pause]

{img:moscow-1811-street-merchants-sledges-churches-colourful-houses}
What was Moscow like before the French came, in 1812?

[pause]

It was a city unlike any other in Europe. It was not as grand or as neat as Saint Petersburg, the new capital by the sea, with its straight avenues and stone palaces. Moscow was older and more Russian. It was like a great, sprawling village.

[long-pause]

It was a city of contrasts. Great palaces of the nobles, painted yellow and white and pale green, stood beside little wooden houses with carved windows, vegetable gardens and chickens. There were wide boulevards and narrow winding lanes. And everywhere there were churches, more than three hundred of them. People said there were forty times forty churches in Moscow.

[pause]

On great feast days, all the bells of Moscow rang together, in a wave of sound that could be heard for miles across the countryside.

[pause]

The great noble families lived in Moscow in winter, in palaces with hundreds of servants. They held balls and concerts and plays in their own private theatres. They spoke French, wore the latest Paris fashions, and some could hardly speak Russian at all.

[long-pause]

And there were the merchants, with long beards and long coats and tall boots. In their shops near Red Square, they sold silk and tea and furs from China and Persia.

[pause]

There were markets where farmers came in from the countryside. In winter they came on sledges loaded with frozen meat and fish, butter and eggs, wood and honey.

[pause]

{img:red-square-1811-trading-rows-merchants-saint-basil-domes-snow}
And on Red Square, beside the Kremlin, stood the wonderful cathedral of Saint Basil. Its many domes were all different, twisted and striped in red, green, blue and gold, like a bunch of flowers, or something from a fairy tale.

[long-pause]

When the news came that the French were coming, and that Moscow would not be defended, the people left.

[pause]

The nobles packed their carriages with furniture, paintings and silver, and left for the countryside. The merchants closed their shops. Ordinary people walked out along the roads to the east with their bundles, their children and their cows. For days the roads were full of carts and carriages and people. Some families had to leave everything behind. Would there be anything left when they came back?

[pause]

{img:families-leaving-moscow-carts-carriages-road-east-september-dust}
In Tolstoy's novel War and Peace, there is a famous scene about this. A family is packing to leave Moscow, with all their carts loaded. Then the young daughter, Natasha, sees wounded soldiers in the courtyard. She persuades her parents to unload the carts, leave their treasures behind, and take the wounded with them instead. It is one of the most beautiful scenes in the book.

[long-pause]

Most of the people of Moscow came back in the winter and spring, after the French had gone. They found their city in ruins, their houses gone and their churches blackened.

[pause]

But they began to rebuild at once. Within a few years, Moscow was alive again, with new houses, palaces and markets. And the bells rang out again on feast days, across the city.

[long-pause]

{img:french-soldiers-camping-moscow-mansion-courtyard-autumn-leaves}
For five weeks, from the middle of September to the middle of October 1812, the great army stays in Moscow.

[pause]

It is a strange time. The weather is beautiful, a golden autumn, warm and sunny day after day. The soldiers say it is warmer than Paris. Napoleon says the Russian winter is a fairy tale, made up to frighten people.

[long-pause]

The soldiers find shelter where they can, in houses and palaces that did not burn, in churches and in cellars. In the cellars and storehouses that survived the fire they find food. Flour, sugar, tea, fish, wine and brandy. After the hungry months of the march, they eat and drink very well for a while.

[pause]

They also find treasure in the empty palaces and shops. Silver and gold, furs and silk, paintings and fine clothes. Many fill their packs and wagons with it, to take home.

[pause]

Sergeant Bourgogne later lists what was in his pack when the army left Moscow. A few pounds of sugar, some rice and biscuit, and a bottle of sweet liqueur. A Chinese silk dress embroidered with gold. A woman's cloak lined with green velvet. Silver pictures, a crucifix and a little porcelain vase. He was very proud of it all. Most of it he would leave beside the road.

[long-pause]

There is even entertainment. Some French actors who lived in Moscow before the war stay on. The officers arrange plays and music in one of the palaces to pass the time. Napoleon goes to some of them.

[pause]

He works hard in the Kremlin, as always. He dictates letters and orders, and reads reports from Paris.

[pause]

And he waits for the Tsar's answer. He sends messages to Alexander, offering peace. But no answer comes. The Tsar will not talk to him while he is in Russia. Day after day, Napoleon waits, and the silence grows heavier.

[long-pause]

Meanwhile, outside Moscow, the Russian army is resting in a camp south of the city. It is getting stronger, with new soldiers and supplies. And the Cossacks and the farmers in the forests attack the French soldiers who go out of the city to look for food and hay.

[pause]

The army's horses are starving. There is no hay and no oats. Every day more horses are lost.

[pause]

{img:cossack-patrol-watching-from-forest-edge-autumn-misty-field}
Some of Napoleon's officers beg him to leave Moscow now, before it is too late, and march back to Poland for the winter. Others tell him to stay the winter in Moscow. Napoleon cannot decide. He waits, and hopes, day after day.

[long-pause]

Then, on the thirteenth of October, the first snow falls, lightly, on the ruins of Moscow. It melts at once. But it is a warning.

[pause]

At last Napoleon decides to leave. On the nineteenth of October 1812, five weeks after he entered the city, the great army marches out of Moscow. About a hundred thousand men leave, with a huge, long line of wagons and carriages. They are loaded with food and treasure, with the wounded, and with French families who lived in Moscow and are afraid to stay.

[pause]

{img:long-column-leaving-moscow-wagons-carriages-autumn-road-domes-behind}
The line is so long that it takes many hours to pass any one point. And the soldiers look back at the golden domes of Moscow in the autumn sunshine, for the last time. Ahead of them lies more than a thousand kilometres of road. And somewhere behind them, the winter is coming.

[long-pause]

{img:retreat-column-autumn-road-birch-trees-grey-sky-wagons}
Napoleon's plan is to march southwest, through country the war has not touched, where there will still be food. Then he will turn west toward Smolensk and Poland.

[pause]

But at a town called Maloyaroslavets, the Russian army blocks the road. There is a battle, and the town burns. Napoleon thinks for a long time. Then he decides not to push through. Instead he turns the army north, back onto the great road to Smolensk. It is the same road the army marched along in summer, on the way to Moscow.

[long-pause]

It is a fateful choice. That road has already been stripped bare. The villages along it are burned, the fields are eaten and the people have fled. There is nothing left to eat.

[pause]

So at the end of October, the long column marches back the way it came, through empty, ruined country. It passes the field of Borodino, where the great battle was fought seven weeks before. The soldiers pass it in silence.

[pause]

At first the weather is still fine, cool and clear, with frost at night. But the food is running out. The horses are failing. When a horse falls, its wagon must be left behind, or its cannon, or its treasure. The soldiers carry what they can.

[long-pause]

Every day, the Cossacks ride around the column like wolves around a flock. They attack anyone who falls behind, and then disappear into the forest. So the soldiers must stay together in their little groups. Being alone is dangerous.

[pause]

The column grows longer and more spread out each day. At the front marches Napoleon with his Guard, still in good order. Then come the other parts of the army. And among them, and behind them, there is a growing crowd of stragglers. These are men who have lost their regiment and their weapons, and walk alone or in small groups, looking for food and shelter.

[pause]

{img:imperial-guard-marching-good-order-bearskins-autumn-wind}
At the very back marches the rear guard. Its job is to protect the whole army from the Russians following behind. It is the hardest job of all.

[long-pause]

Jakob Walter, the young German, writes about these days. Every evening he and his friends searched the burned villages for food. They found a few frozen potatoes, a little grain or a few cabbage leaves. They cooked them in their pot over a tiny fire. They shared what they found, and helped each other, and stayed together.

[pause]

He writes about the kindness of his friends. And about strangers who sometimes gave him a piece of bread, or a place by their fire, just when he needed it most.

[pause]

{img:small-group-soldiers-sharing-bread-tiny-fire-ruined-barn-dusk}
In all the stories of the retreat from Moscow there are terrible things. But there are also stories of friendship, loyalty, courage and kindness. They shine out like little fires in the dark. And the soldiers will need every one of them, because the real cold has not even begun.

[long-pause]

{img:regimental-eagle-standard-golden-eagle-pole-flag-guard-proud}
Every regiment of the great army carries an eagle.

[pause]

It is a golden eagle made of bronze, with its wings half spread. It stands on top of a long pole. Below it hangs the regiment's flag, in blue, white and red, with the names of its battles embroidered in gold.

[long-pause]

Napoleon gave the eagles to the regiments himself, at a great ceremony in Paris. The soldiers swore to defend them with their lives. The eagle is the soul of the regiment. A chosen soldier carries it, with two brave sergeants beside him to protect it. Losing the eagle is the greatest shame of all.

[pause]

During the retreat from Moscow, some regiments almost disappeared. But many still kept their eagles. Some soldiers took the eagle off its pole and carried it inside their coat or their pack, through the snow. Some buried their eagle secretly in the snow, or threw it in a river, rather than let the enemy take it.

[pause]

{img:soldier-hiding-eagle-under-greatcoat-snow-march-quiet-resolve}
And there was music. Every regiment had its band, its drummers and its trumpeters.

[long-pause]

The drummers were often young boys of twelve or thirteen. They beat the drums for the march, for waking up in the morning and for orders in battle. The drum beats were like a language. One beat meant forward, another meant stop, another meant gather round. In the noise of battle, the soldiers could hear the drums when they could not hear their officers.

[pause]

On the summer march, the bands played marches and songs, to keep the soldiers' spirits up and their feet moving. In the evenings at camp they played old songs from home.

[pause]

{img:young-drummer-boy-drum-sticks-column-marching-summer-road}
Sergeant Bourgogne remembers that when the army entered Moscow, the bands of the Guard played a march called Victory Is Ours, as the soldiers walked through the empty streets.

[long-pause]

In the winter, on the retreat, there was little music. The instruments froze. The bandsmen's fingers were too cold to play. The drums were left beside the road with the wagons.

[pause]

But sometimes at night, around the fire, a soldier would quietly sing an old song from his village, in France or Germany or Italy. The others would listen, and some would softly join in. And for a few minutes they were far away, at home in the summer, with their families, in the warm.

[long-pause]

{img:first-snow-falling-forest-road-column-soldiers-grey-november}
On the sixth of November 1812, the weather changes.

[pause]

The sky turns grey and heavy. A cold wind starts to blow from the north. And then the snow begins to fall. Thick, heavy, silent snow, falling all day and all night. It covers the road, the fields, the forests and the soldiers in a deep white blanket.

[long-pause]

The temperature drops below freezing, and keeps dropping. The Russian winter, which Napoleon called a fairy tale, has come.

[pause]

For the soldiers on the road, everything gets harder. The road vanishes under the snow. The soldiers sink in up to their knees. The horses slip on the ice, because their shoes are not made for it. Many fall and cannot get up again. The wagons and the cannons are left beside the road, in the snow.

[pause]

The soldiers' clothes are not made for this. They still wear their summer uniforms, thin trousers and jackets, and their coats. Nothing more. Their shoes are worn out, and their feet are wrapped in rags.

[long-pause]

So they wrap themselves in anything they can find. The furs and silks and shawls they took from Moscow. Women's dresses, priests' robes, sheepskins, blankets and carpets.

[pause]

The army no longer looks like an army. Sergeant Bourgogne describes himself at this time. He wore a woman's yellow silk cloak lined with fur, and a fur cap with a scarf tied around his head. His face was black with smoke, and his beard was full of icicles.

[pause]

He writes that the soldiers looked like a carnival. A crowd of strange figures in every kind of clothing, walking slowly and silently through the snow, heads down against the wind.

[long-pause]

The nights are the hardest.

[pause]

There is no shelter. The villages along the road were burned. The soldiers must sleep in the open, in the snow, around their fires. They cut down trees and pull down fences to burn. They sit or lie as close to the fire as they can, wrapped in everything they have, and try to sleep.

[pause]

{img:night-snow-bivouac-many-small-fires-soldiers-huddled-stars-cold}
Bourgogne writes about one night when he and his friends found an old barn still standing. Dozens of men crowded into it, with their fire in the middle. They slept warm for the first time in days. For a few hours, they were happy.

[long-pause]

Napoleon himself walks much of the time in the snow with his Guard. He wears a fur coat and a fur hat, and carries a stick, to keep warm and to encourage his soldiers.

[pause]

The soldiers of the Guard still march in their ranks, with their tall fur hats and their muskets. When they see the Emperor walking beside them in the snow, they still cheer.

[pause]

{img:napoleon-walking-snow-fur-coat-stick-guard-beside-him-quiet}
On the ninth of November, the army reaches Smolensk. There are food stores there, and the soldiers hope for food and rest and shelter. But the stores are not enough for everyone. They are quickly emptied. And the army must go on west through the snow, toward a river called the Berezina. Nobody has heard of it yet. Soon, everyone will.

[long-pause]

{img:small-group-soldiers-around-fire-snow-sharing-soup-pot-friends}
How did anyone survive the retreat from Moscow?

[pause]

Those who did, and wrote about it later, all say the same thing. They survived because of their friends. Their little groups stayed together, shared everything and helped each other through it all.

[long-pause]

The soldiers learned many things in those weeks, just to stay alive.

[pause]

They learned to keep moving during the day. Never sit down in the snow to rest, they told each other. If you sit down and fall asleep in the cold, you may never wake up. They learned to keep their feet dry if they could, and wrap them in rags and straw. And they learned not to hold frozen hands too close to the fire, but to warm them slowly.

[pause]

{img:soldier-wrapping-feet-rags-straw-fireside-snow-careful}
They learned to cover their faces against the wind, leaving only the eyes. And they watched each other's faces. If a friend's nose or cheeks turned white from the cold, they told him at once, so he could rub them and bring the blood back.

[long-pause]

They learned to find food anywhere. In frozen fields they dug under the snow for potatoes and cabbages. In ruined villages they searched cellars and barns for grain. They made a thin porridge of flour and melted snow. And they ate the horses that had fallen. For many soldiers, that was their main food on the retreat. Bourgogne writes that nothing in his whole life ever tasted so good as a piece of horse meat, roasted on the end of his sword, after days of hunger.

[pause]

They shared what they had. A soldier who found food and ate it alone was despised. A soldier who shared was loved.

[pause]

The women who followed the army were among the bravest of all. Many stayed with their regiments all through the retreat. They gave the soldiers food and care, and nursed the sick. Bourgogne tells of one of them, Mother Dubois, who gave birth to a baby in the snow during the retreat. The soldiers gave her their cloaks. The colonel gave her his own cloak, to keep her and the baby warm. And mother and baby both survived.

[long-pause]

Others survived because of kindness from people they did not expect.

[pause]

Some Russian farmers took pity on the frozen, starving soldiers. They gave them bread and let them warm themselves by the stove, and let them go. Some Jewish families in the towns gave them food and warmth, and hid them. Some Russian officers treated their prisoners kindly.

[pause]

{img:russian-peasant-woman-giving-bread-frozen-soldier-izba-door-snow}
And some survived simply by luck. A barn found at the right moment. A fire, a friend, a piece of bread, a horse or a place on a sledge.

[long-pause]

Jakob Walter, the young German, survived. He tells how he lost his friends one by one, and his strength too. He went on alone, step by step, through the snow. Then he found a little Russian pony and rode it, and it saved his life. He was ill with fever and hardly knew where he was. But at last he reached Poland. And then he came home to his village in Germany, thin and ill, and changed forever.

[pause]

{img:german-village-winter-1813-church-snow-soldier-returning-home}
He lived to be an old man. Later he moved to America, to Kansas. There, many years afterwards, he wrote down his memories for his children. His little book is one of the most moving accounts of the war ever written.

[long-pause]

{img:marshal-ney-red-hair-musket-rear-guard-snow-calm-determined}
Of all the heroes of the retreat from Moscow, the most famous is Marshal Michel Ney.

[pause]

Ney is forty-three years old. He is the son of a barrel maker from the border of France and Germany. He joined the army as a young horseman, and rose by courage and skill to become one of Napoleon's top generals. He has red hair and a hot temper, and he is the bravest man in the army. Napoleon calls him the bravest of the brave.

[long-pause]

During the retreat, Ney commands the rear guard. That is the very last part of the army. Its job is to hold back the Russians following behind, day after day, so the rest of the army can get away.

[pause]

It is the hardest and most dangerous job of all. The rear guard is always the last to leave and the first to be attacked. And every day it grows smaller.

[pause]

Ney does not just give orders. He fights himself, with a musket in his hand, like an ordinary soldier, beside his men in the snow. His soldiers say he seems to be made of iron. He never seems tired, or cold, or afraid.

[long-pause]

In the middle of November, Ney and his rear guard find themselves cut off. The Russian army has blocked the road ahead of them. The Russians send an officer with a white flag, to ask him to surrender. A marshal of France does not surrender, Ney answers.

[pause]

That night, he leads his men away north, through the dark forest, to the river Dnieper. The river is frozen, but the ice is thin and cracking. In the darkness, Ney and his men cross on the ice, a few at a time. They test the ice with every step, while it creaks and groans beneath them. Will it hold?

[pause]

It holds. And for three days they march west along the river, through the forests, with the Cossacks following them, fighting them off by day and by night. At last they reach the rest of the army.

[long-pause]

When Napoleon hears that Ney is safe, he is overjoyed. He says he would have given all the gold in his palace in Paris to save Ney.

[pause]

When the soldiers see Ney and his little band arriving, they cheer and hug them, and weep for joy. Ney's crossing of the frozen river becomes one of the great legends of the retreat.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-cheering-embracing-arriving-comrades-snowy-camp-joy}
And Ney goes on commanding the rear guard all the way to the end, to the border of Russia. There, it is said, he was the very last French soldier to leave Russia. He crossed the bridge with a musket in his hand.

[long-pause]

A few days later, in a town in Prussia, a French general was sitting at breakfast. A strange man walked in. He wore a brown coat and had a long beard, red eyes and a face blackened by smoke, and a musket in his hand. The general did not recognise him, and asked who he was.

[pause]

And the man said: I am the rear guard of the great army. I am Michel Ney.

[pause]

{img:inn-room-prussia-breakfast-table-weary-bearded-man-doorway}
Ney's own end, three years later, will be a sad one. But in the winter of 1812, he was the hero of the army.

[long-pause]

{img:berezina-river-banks-marshes-snow-grey-november-1812}
At the end of November 1812, the army comes to the river Berezina, in what is now Belarus.

[pause]

The Berezina is not a very wide river. But it is cold and deep, and its banks are marshy. The bridge at the town nearby has been destroyed by the Russians. And Russian armies are coming from the north, the south and the east. The great army is trapped.

[long-pause]

The army must cross the river. But how?

[pause]

A few kilometres away, a cavalry officer finds a shallow place where the river can be crossed. The engineers decide to build bridges there, quickly, before the Russians arrive.

[pause]

The men who build them are the bridge builders of the army, about four hundred of them, Dutch and French. Their commander is General Éblé, a quiet, kind old soldier of fifty-four. His men love him.

[long-pause]

Éblé had disobeyed an order. Earlier in the retreat, Napoleon had ordered all the bridge-building gear to be burned, to save the horses. But Éblé secretly kept six wagons of tools, nails and iron clamps. And now, those wagons save the army.

[pause]

On the twenty-sixth of November, the bridge builders go into the river.

[pause]

{img:bridge-builders-standing-in-cold-water-placing-trestles-dawn-mist}
They wade into the icy water up to their shoulders, among floating blocks of ice. They drive wooden legs into the riverbed, and lay beams and planks on top. They work in shifts in the freezing water, for hours, through the day and the night. They build two bridges, one for the soldiers on foot and on horseback, and a stronger one for the cannons and wagons.

[pause]

Many of these men later died from the cold. They are among the greatest heroes of the war. Today there is a memorial to them where the bridges stood.

[long-pause]

For three days the army crosses, while the rear guard on both banks holds back the Russians.

[pause]

The bridges break several times under the weight. Each time, the bridge builders go back into the water and mend them.

[pause]

We will not watch the last day of the crossing, when the Russians came close, and the crowds who had waited too long pressed onto the bridges. It is enough to say that it was one of the saddest days of the whole retreat. Even today, in French, the word Bérézina means a terrible disaster.

[long-pause]

On the morning of the twenty-ninth of November, General Éblé set fire to the bridges, as he had been ordered, so the Russians could not follow. He waited as long as he could, giving the people on the far bank every chance to cross.

[pause]

But most of the army had made it across. Napoleon, the Guard, Ney and many thousands of soldiers. And they marched on west, toward Poland.

[pause]

{img:quiet-river-winter-morning-mist-snow-banks-memorial-calm}
General Éblé, the kind old bridge builder, worn out by the cold and his work, died a month later.

[long-pause]

{img:sleigh-night-snow-forest-road-small-escort-lanterns-december}
After the Berezina, in December 1812, the cold gets even worse.

[pause]

The temperature falls to twenty, thirty, even thirty-five degrees below zero. The air is so cold it hurts to breathe. The dry snow squeaks under your feet. The trees crack in the frost with sounds like pistol shots. On clear days the sky is pale blue, and the low sun shines without any warmth on the white land.

[long-pause]

On the fifth of December, in a little town, Napoleon leaves the army.

[pause]

He has heard that in Paris, someone tried to overthrow his government while he was away. And he knows that after this disaster, his enemies all over Europe will rise against him. He must get back to Paris quickly to raise a new army.

[pause]

{img:emperor-leaving-in-sleigh-officers-saluting-snowy-village-dusk}
So he hands command to his brother-in-law, and leaves at night in a sleigh, with a few companions. He travels in disguise, day and night, across Poland and Germany. Thirteen days later, at midnight, he arrives at his palace in Paris, so muddy and bearded that the guards do not recognise him.

[pause]

On the journey, it is said, he spoke a famous sentence: from the sublime to the ridiculous, there is only one step.

[long-pause]

The army goes on without him to Vilnius, where there are big stores of food and clothes. The soldiers arrive there frozen and starving. For a day or two they eat and warm themselves in the houses of the city.

[pause]

But the Russians are close behind. So the army must go on again, west, to the river Niemen and the border.

[pause]

In the middle of December, the last survivors of the great army cross the river Niemen, out of Russia. It is the same river they crossed so proudly in the summer sunshine, with their bands playing, six months before.

[pause]

Of the more than six hundred thousand men who marched into Russia, only a small part came back. Many tens of thousands were taken prisoner, and some of them came home years later. Some stayed in Russia and made new lives there. But very many were lost.

[long-pause]

It was one of the greatest disasters in the history of war. And it was the beginning of the end for Napoleon.

[pause]

In 1813, all of Europe rose against him, as he had feared. He raised a new army of young recruits and fought brilliantly. But in October 1813 he was beaten in a great battle at Leipzig. In 1814 the allied armies, with Tsar Alexander at their head, entered Paris. Napoleon gave up his throne and was sent away to the little island of Elba.

[pause]

{img:allied-sovereigns-entering-paris-1814-spring-crowds-boulevard}
A year later he escaped, came back to France and ruled again for a hundred days. But in June 1815, at Waterloo in Belgium, he was beaten for the last time. He was sent far away to the lonely island of Saint Helena, in the middle of the South Atlantic Ocean. He died there six years later.

[long-pause]

Marshal Ney fought for him again at Waterloo. Afterwards, the new king of France had him put on trial for treason. Ney met his end bravely in Paris. Today there is a statue of him in Paris, with his sword raised.

[pause]

And the Tsar, who had refused to make peace, became for a while the most powerful man in Europe. He died mysteriously some years later. Some people believed he did not really die, but became a hermit in Siberia, and lived there in secret as a holy man.

[pause]

{img:saint-helena-island-cliffs-ocean-lonely-house-clouds-calm}
And Moscow was rebuilt, slowly, more beautiful than before, with new houses and churches of stone. Once again, it became the great heart of Russia.

[long-pause]

{img:konigsberg-1812-winter-old-town-churches-snow-river-ice}
In the last days of December 1812, the survivors of the great army reach Königsberg, in Prussia, on the shore of the Baltic Sea.

[pause]

It is a city of red brick churches and tall old houses, with a castle and a cathedral on an island in the river. Along the frozen harbour stand the warehouses. For the soldiers who reach it, it is like coming back to life.

[long-pause]

They arrive a few at a time, in little groups or alone, on foot or in sledges. They are ragged, thin and frozen, in their strange clothes of furs and shawls and blankets. The people of the city stare at them in pity and astonishment. Some do not even recognise them as soldiers.

[pause]

But in Königsberg there are houses and warm rooms, stoves and beds, clean clothes and food. Bread and soup, meat and beer. There are hospitals where the sick and frostbitten can be cared for. And there is rest.

[pause]

{img:warm-room-tiled-stove-soldiers-sleeping-beds-clean-quiet}
Many soldiers later describe the joy of that first night. A warm room and a real bed, after six months on the ground. Washing in hot water. Eating a real meal at a table. Taking off their boots. And sleeping for a whole day and night without waking.

[long-pause]

And it is Christmas.

[pause]

In the churches of Königsberg the bells ring and the candles are lit. People sing the old Christmas hymns in German. In some houses there are little trees decorated with candles, apples and nuts. It is a German custom that is just beginning to spread. And some families invite the poor soldiers into their homes, to share their Christmas dinner by the warm stove.

[pause]

{img:german-family-christmas-candles-tree-inviting-ragged-soldier-table}
For many of the soldiers, it is the most moving Christmas of their lives. They are alive. They are warm and fed. And in a few weeks or months, they will be home.

[pause]

But they also think of their friends who did not come back, lying quietly under the snow along the long road from Moscow. And they raise their glasses to them, in silence.

[long-pause]

{img:old-veteran-writing-memoirs-desk-candle-medals-1830s}
In the years after 1812, the survivors of the Russian campaign went home to their villages and towns all over Europe.

[pause]

Many never spoke about what they had seen. Others told their stories again and again to their families, by the fire on long winter evenings, for the rest of their lives. And some wrote them down.

[long-pause]

Sergeant Bourgogne came home after years as a prisoner. He lived a long life and became an officer. In his old age he wrote his memories. They were published long after his death. They are full of life and humour, horror and kindness, and people still read them today.

[pause]

Jakob Walter wrote his little book in simple German, for his family. Many years later, it was found in America by his great-grandchildren, and published.

[pause]

Many of the Dutch bridge builders of the Berezina never came home. But in the Netherlands they are still remembered. And in Switzerland there is an old song, sung by Swiss soldiers on the morning of the crossing. It is about life as a journey through the night. It is still sung today.

[pause]

Larrey, the great doctor, came home too and wrote his memories. He went on caring for soldiers, even at Waterloo. Napoleon later called him the most honest man he had ever known. And it is said that at Waterloo, the British commander saw Larrey working among the wounded. He told his gunners not to fire in that direction, and raised his hat to salute him.

[long-pause]

And the story became a legend, told in books, paintings and music.

[pause]

Leo Tolstoy was born sixteen years after the campaign. As a boy he heard stories about it from old people in his family and from old soldiers. Later he wrote War and Peace, one of the greatest novels ever written. It tells of the war of 1812, of Russian families and soldiers, of the battle of Borodino and the burning of Moscow.

[pause]

{img:tolstoy-writing-study-estate-window-snow-birches-quiet}
In 1880, the Russian composer Tchaikovsky wrote a piece of music called the 1812 Overture. At the start you hear the French national song. Slowly it is drowned out by Russian hymns, the bells of Moscow and the boom of cannons. It is still played all over the world, sometimes with real cannons.

[long-pause]

And in 1869, a retired French engineer called Minard made a famous chart of the campaign.

[pause]

It shows the army as a broad band, wide at the start, at the river Niemen in the summer. As it moves east toward Moscow, it gets narrower and narrower. Then a thin black band comes back west through the winter, thinner still. Below it, a line shows the temperature falling, day by day.

[pause]

{img:old-chart-map-band-narrowing-army-route-paper-desk-lamp}
Many people say it is the best chart ever drawn. A whole sad story told in one picture, without words.

[long-pause]

But perhaps the most important stories are the small ones. The soldiers who shared their last piece of bread. The women who gave away their cloaks. The farmers who opened their doors. The friends who carried each other through the snow. And the old men, many years later by the fire, who remembered them.

[long-pause]

{img:borodino-field-today-monuments-meadows-birch-autumn}
Today, more than two hundred years later, you can still follow the road of 1812, from the river Niemen to Moscow and back.

[pause]

In Kaunas, in Lithuania, the river Niemen still flows past the old town, wide and calm between green hills. On a hill above the river, near the place where the army crossed, there is a little monument. In summer the river is full of swimmers and boats.

[long-pause]

In Vilnius, the old town with its white, pink and yellow churches is one of the most beautiful in Europe. In 2001, workers building new houses at the edge of the city found the resting place of many soldiers of the great army, who had died there in the winter of 1812. Scientists studied them carefully and learned a lot about them, their ages, their health and where they came from. Then they were buried again with honour, with soldiers of many nations present.

[pause]

{img:vilnius-old-town-baroque-churches-snow-quiet-evening}
At the Berezina, in Belarus, there are memorials on the riverbank where the bridges stood. The river flows quietly past, between marshy meadows and woods. People come from France, Holland, Switzerland and Russia to lay flowers there.

[long-pause]

At Borodino, the battlefield is now a great open-air museum. It is a peaceful place of fields, woods and birch trees, with dozens of monuments to the regiments of both sides. There is also a small monastery. It was built by the widow of a Russian general, at the place where he fell.

[pause]

Every year in September, thousands of people come to Borodino. Some dress in the uniforms of 1812, with muskets and horses and cannons. They act out the battle as a festival of remembering, while crowds watch from the hillsides.

[pause]

In Moscow there is a great round building with an enormous circular painting of the battle inside, more than a hundred metres long. Visitors stand in the middle and see the battle all around them.

[long-pause]

In Moscow there is also a great cathedral with a golden dome by the river. It was built to give thanks for the victory of 1812. It was destroyed in 1931, and rebuilt exactly as it was in the 1990s.

[pause]

The Kremlin still stands on its hill above the river, with its red walls and golden domes. In one of its courtyards, French cannons captured in 1812 are lined up along the walls.

[pause]

{img:moscow-kremlin-red-walls-golden-domes-river-winter-evening}
And in Paris, Napoleon rests in a great tomb of red stone, under the golden dome of a home for old soldiers. Around him the names of his victories are carved in the walls. And on the great triumphal arch in Paris, the names of his battles and his generals are carved too, among them Borodino, and Ney, and Éblé.

[long-pause]

So the long road of 1812 comes to an end tonight, with the snow falling softly on the quiet fields of Borodino, and the river Niemen flowing calmly past the place where the great army crossed in the summer sunshine.

[pause]

The young soldiers and the old grumblers, the women with their little carts, the bridge builders in the icy river, and the friends who shared their last bread by a small fire in the snow.

[pause]
