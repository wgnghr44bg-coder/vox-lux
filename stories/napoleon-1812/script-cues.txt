Tonight we are going more than two hundred years back, to the wide plains of Russia, in the summer and the winter of the year 1812.

[long-pause]

The greatest army the world has ever seen is marching east, along dusty roads, through birch forests and wheat fields, toward Moscow. More than half a million men, from all over Europe, with their horses and their wagons and their cannons, and the drums and the bands playing. Their leader is the Emperor of the French, Napoleon Bonaparte.

[pause]

They set out in the summer sunshine, full of hope. Very few of them will come home. And tonight, gently, and from a distance, we will follow them, and see how they lived, on the long road to Moscow, and back again, through the Russian winter.

[pause]

If you like to fall asleep to slow stories from the past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, and to come back for the next one.

[long-pause]

Let us imagine Europe, in the year 1812.

[pause]

For almost twenty years, since the French Revolution, Europe has been at war. And for most of those years, France has been winning. Under Napoleon, the French armies have marched across Europe, and defeated Austria, and Prussia, and Russia, and Spain, and many smaller states, again and again.

[long-pause]

By 1812, Napoleon rules, directly or indirectly, almost the whole of Western and Central Europe. France itself is much larger than today, stretching from Hamburg to Rome. His brothers and his marshals are kings of Spain, and Naples, and Westphalia, and Holland. The kings of Prussia and Austria are his unwilling allies. Most of Germany, and Italy, and Poland, are under his control.

[pause]

Only Britain, safe on its island, behind its great navy, still fights against him. And in Spain, the Spanish people, and a British army, under the Duke of Wellington, are fighting a long, bitter war against the French.

[pause]

And in the east, there is Russia, the great empire of the Tsar, Alexander the First.

[long-pause]

Napoleon and Alexander were once friends, of a kind. Five years before, in 1807, after Napoleon defeated the Russians, the two emperors met, on a raft, in the middle of the river Niemen, at Tilsit, and made peace, and divided Europe between them, and talked for hours, and charmed each other.

[pause]

But the friendship did not last. Russia did not want to stop trading with Britain, as Napoleon demanded. The two emperors quarrelled, about Poland, and about trade, and about power. And in 1812, Napoleon decided to invade Russia, and force the Tsar to obey him.

[pause]

{img:raft-pavilion-river-niemen-two-emperors-meeting-1807}
He thought it would be a short war: a few weeks, a great battle, a victory, and then the Tsar would ask for peace, as all his enemies had done before. He was wrong.

[long-pause]

The story of Napoleon's campaign in Russia, in 1812, is one of the most famous, and the saddest, stories in the history of war.

[pause]

But it is also a story of ordinary people: of young soldiers, and old soldiers, and drummer boys, and officers, and surgeons, and the women who followed the army, and the Russian peasants in their villages, and the horses, and the long roads, and the camp fires, and the snow. Many of the soldiers kept diaries, or wrote down their memories, many years later. And thanks to them, we can still follow the road to Moscow, step by step, and see it through their eyes.

[pause]

{img:soldier-writing-diary-campfire-night-shako-candle}
We will not watch the battles. Tonight, we will walk beside the soldiers, and see how they lived, day by day, and night by night, in the summer, and the autumn, and the winter of 1812.

[long-pause]

{img:napoleon-grey-greatcoat-bicorne-hat-thoughtful-campfire-1812}
In 1812, Napoleon Bonaparte is forty-two years old.

[pause]

He is short, but not as short as people later believed: about one metre sixty-eight, average for a Frenchman of his time. He is becoming a little stout. He has dark hair, cut short, and grey eyes, and a pale face, and small, fine hands. In the field, he wears a simple uniform, the green coat of a colonel of the light cavalry of his Guard, or the blue of the grenadiers, and over it, his famous grey greatcoat, and his black bicorne hat, worn sideways.

[long-pause]

He was born in 1769, on the island of Corsica, in the Mediterranean, into a poor noble family, the second of eight children. As a boy, he was sent to a military school in France, where the other boys laughed at his Corsican accent, and his poverty. He was lonely, and read books, about history, and mathematics, and great generals.

[pause]

He became an officer of artillery, the cannons. And during the French Revolution, when many of the old officers fled, or lost their places, young officers of talent could rise quickly. Napoleon rose faster than anyone. At twenty-four, he was a general. At thirty, he was the ruler of France. At thirty-five, in 1804, he crowned himself Emperor of the French, in the cathedral of Notre Dame, in Paris.

[pause]

He is a genius. Everyone agrees on that. He can work for eighteen hours a day, and sleep for a few hours, anywhere, even on horseback, and wake up fresh. He remembers everything: the names of officers, and the numbers of men in every regiment, and the positions of armies, and the details of laws. He dictates letters to four secretaries at once. He has made new laws for France, the Code Napoléon, which are still the basis of the laws of many countries today.

[long-pause]

His soldiers love him. He knows how to speak to them, and make them feel that they are part of something great. He walks among them, at their camp fires, and pulls their ears, affectionately, and remembers their faces, and their battles. They call him the little corporal. And they would follow him anywhere.

[pause]

But he is also proud, and ambitious, and impatient, and he believes in his own star, his destiny. He has won so many battles, that he believes that he cannot lose.

[pause]

{img:napoleon-walking-among-soldiers-bivouac-fires-grenadiers-cheering}
In 1810, he married a new wife, Marie Louise, the young daughter of the Emperor of Austria, after divorcing his first wife, Joséphine, because she could not give him a son. And in 1811, Marie Louise gave birth to a son, the little King of Rome, whom Napoleon loves very much. He has a portrait of the little boy with him, in Russia, and shows it to his officers, and his Guard.

[long-pause]

In the spring of 1812, Napoleon leaves Paris, and travels east, to Dresden, in Germany, where all the kings and princes of his empire come to meet him, and bow to him, in a great court, with feasts, and balls, and plays.

[pause]

And then he goes on, to Poland, to the river Niemen, the border of the Russian Empire, where his great army is waiting for him.

[pause]

{img:dresden-1812-palace-court-ball-kings-princes-chandeliers}
His advisers have warned him. Russia is enormous. The roads are bad. The distances are vast. The Russian winter is terrible. An army cannot feed itself there. But Napoleon does not listen. He plans to win a great battle, quickly, in the summer, and be home before the winter comes.

[long-pause]

{img:empress-marie-louise-baby-king-of-rome-cradle-tuileries-1811}
In Paris, in the summer of 1812, at the palace of the Tuileries, and at the palace of Saint-Cloud, outside the city, there is a little boy of one year old, with fair curly hair, and blue eyes, who does not know that his father has gone to war.

[pause]

He is Napoleon's son, Napoléon François Joseph Charles Bonaparte, born in March 1811. He was given the title King of Rome, at his birth. And when he was born, a hundred and one cannons were fired, in Paris, to announce that the Emperor had a son, and the people of Paris cheered in the streets.

[long-pause]

Napoleon loves him very much. Before he left for Russia, he played with him on the floor, and carried him in his arms, and let him play with his sword, and his hat. He has commissioned a painter, François Gérard, to paint a portrait of the little boy, playing with a cup and ball, to send to him in Russia.

[pause]

The little king's mother is the Empress Marie Louise, who is twenty years old, the daughter of the Emperor of Austria. She had been taught, as a child, to fear and hate Napoleon, the enemy of her family. But when she married him, at eighteen, she found him kind, and gentle, with her. And she writes to him, often, while he is in Russia, about their son: that he is growing, and has new teeth, and is learning to walk, and to say papa.

[pause]

{img:small-boy-toddler-walking-garden-saint-cloud-nurse-summer}
The little boy is looked after by his governess, Madame de Montesquiou, a kind, wise lady, whom he calls Maman Quiou. She teaches him to say his prayers, every evening, and to pray for his papa, far away.

[long-pause]

After the fall of Napoleon, in 1814, the little boy was taken by his mother to Vienna, in Austria, to the court of his grandfather, the Emperor of Austria. There, he was given a new name, the Duke of Reichstadt, and brought up as an Austrian prince. He never saw his father again.

[pause]

He grew up to be a tall, gentle, serious young man, who loved to read about his father's battles. He passed away of an illness, at the age of twenty-one, in 1832, in the palace of Schönbrunn, in Vienna. People called him l'Aiglon, the little eagle.

[pause]

{img:young-man-palace-window-vienna-reading-books-thoughtful-1830}
In 1940, more than a hundred years later, his remains were brought to Paris, and laid in the Invalides, near his father.

[long-pause]

{img:soldiers-many-nations-uniforms-camp-poland-1812-colourful}
In June 1812, the Grande Armée, the great army, is gathered along the river Niemen, in Poland and Prussia, on the border of the Russian Empire.

[pause]

It is the largest army that Europe has ever seen. About four hundred and fifty thousand men cross the river into Russia in the first days, and more come later, so that, in all, more than six hundred thousand men will march into Russia. With them, there are more than a hundred and fifty thousand horses, and more than a thousand cannons, and thousands of wagons.

[long-pause]

Only about half of the soldiers are French. The others come from all over Napoleon's empire, and his allied states: Germans, from Bavaria, and Saxony, and Württemberg, and Westphalia, and Baden. Poles, who hope that Napoleon will give them back their country. Italians. Austrians, and Prussians, who are allies, unwillingly. Dutch, and Belgians, and Swiss, and Croats, and Spaniards, and Portuguese, and even Illyrians, from the coast of the Adriatic.

[pause]

They speak a dozen languages. Some people call it the army of twenty nations.

[pause]

{img:polish-lancers-red-white-pennants-horses-forest-road-summer}
The soldiers are of every kind. There is the Imperial Guard, the elite of the army, the tallest and bravest and most experienced veterans, with their tall bearskin hats, and their moustaches, the old grumblers, as Napoleon calls them affectionately, because they grumble, but always follow him. There are the line infantry, young men, many of them only eighteen or nineteen, conscripts, called up by the law, from the villages of France and Germany. There are the light infantry, the voltigeurs, and the skirmishers.

[long-pause]

There are the cavalry: the heavy cuirassiers, big men on big horses, with steel breastplates, and helmets with long horsehair plumes. The dragoons. The hussars, with their fur-trimmed jackets, slung over one shoulder, and their braided hair, the dashing light cavalry. The Polish lancers, with their red and white pennants fluttering on their lances. And the artillery, with their cannons, pulled by teams of horses.

[pause]

And there are the engineers, and the pontonniers, who build bridges. And the doctors, and the surgeons, and the bakers, and the carpenters, and the blacksmiths, and the clerks.

[pause]

One of the soldiers is a young German, a stonemason from Württemberg, called Jakob Walter, aged twenty-four, a private in the infantry, who will write down his memories of the campaign, years later, in a simple, honest, unforgettable little book.

[pause]

Another is a Frenchman, a sergeant of the Imperial Guard, Adrien Bourgogne, aged twenty-six, who will also write a famous memoir. Another is the chief surgeon of the army, Dominique Larrey, a kind and brilliant man, who will look after the wounded, of both sides, with great courage and skill, all through the campaign.

[long-pause]

And there are the women.

[pause]

With every regiment, there are a few women, called cantinières, or vivandières: the wives of soldiers, who travel with the army, and sell food, and drink, and tobacco, and other things, to the soldiers, from little carts, or from barrels on their backs. They wash and mend the soldiers' clothes, and nurse the sick and the wounded. They are brave, and tough, and greatly loved by the soldiers.

[pause]

{img:cantiniere-woman-small-cart-barrels-soldiers-buying-camp}
There are also many other people following the army: servants, and cooks, and merchants, and some families of officers, with their carriages. All together, a great moving city of hundreds of thousands of people, stretching for many miles along the roads.

[long-pause]

On the evening of the twenty-third of June, 1812, Napoleon rides along the bank of the river Niemen, near the town of Kovno, to look at the place where the army will cross.

[pause]

As he rides, a hare runs out from under his horse's feet, and the horse rears, and Napoleon falls to the ground. He gets up at once, unhurt, and gets back on his horse, without a word. But some of the officers who saw it think it is a bad omen. One of them says: a Roman general would turn back.

[pause]

{img:quiet-riverbank-evening-niemen-forest-far-side-summer}
That night, the engineers build three bridges, of boats, across the river. And in the morning, the twenty-fourth of June, 1812, the Grande Armée begins to cross into Russia.

[long-pause]

{img:surgeon-larrey-portrait-uniform-kind-face-1812-tent}
Among all the people who marched into Russia in 1812, one of the most remarkable is the chief surgeon of the Grande Armée, Dominique Jean Larrey.

[pause]

He is forty-six years old. He was born in a little village in the Pyrenees, in the south of France, the son of a shoemaker. His father passed away when he was a boy, and he was brought up by his uncle, a surgeon, in Toulouse, and learned medicine from him.

[long-pause]

He has been an army surgeon for twenty years, and has followed Napoleon everywhere: to Italy, and Egypt, and Syria, and Austria, and Prussia, and Spain. He has cared for tens of thousands of wounded soldiers, in battles, and in hospitals, and in the open fields.

[pause]

Larrey is a kind, honest, brave, and tireless man. He believes that a wounded soldier is a wounded soldier, whatever his rank, and whatever his nation. He treats the wounded in the order of their need, not their rank: a general must wait, if a private is more badly hurt. And he treats enemy wounded with the same care as his own.

[pause]

In the early years of his career, he saw that wounded soldiers were often left on the battlefield for a day or more, before they were taken to the surgeons, because the hospitals were far behind the army, and the wagons were slow. And many soldiers were lost, who could have been saved.

[pause]

So he invented a new kind of carriage: light, fast, with springs, pulled by two horses, with room for two or four wounded men, lying down, which could go right onto the battlefield, and bring the wounded quickly back to the surgeons. He called them flying ambulances. It was the beginning of the modern ambulance service.

[long-pause]

He also organized teams of surgeons, and nurses, and stretcher bearers, who worked close behind the fighting, so that the wounded could be helped within minutes, or hours, instead of days.

[pause]

In Russia, Larrey works without rest. Before the campaign, he has prepared everything: surgeons, and medicines, and bandages, and instruments, and wagons. On the march, he cares for the sick, who are many, in the heat, and the dust. After the battles, he works for days, without sleep.

[pause]

{img:surgeon-writing-notes-candle-field-hospital-night-quiet}
And on the retreat, in the cold, he goes on caring for the soldiers. He teaches them how to protect themselves against the cold, and how to treat frozen hands and feet: not by warming them quickly, by the fire, which does great harm, but slowly, by rubbing them gently with snow, or cold water, and then warming them, little by little. This was new knowledge, in his time, and it saved many lives.

[long-pause]

The soldiers love him. At the Berezina, it is said, when Larrey was trying to cross the crowded bridge, carrying his instruments, the soldiers recognized him, and passed him over their heads, from hand to hand, across the crowd, and set him down safely on the other side.

[pause]

Larrey survived the campaign, and wrote about it, in his memoirs, carefully, and honestly, describing everything he had seen, and learned, so that other doctors could learn from it.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-lifting-man-hand-to-hand-over-crowd-bridge-snow-gentle}
He lived until 1842. And in 1992, his remains were moved to the Invalides, in Paris, to rest near the Emperor he had served for so long. And in the courtyard of the Val-de-Grâce, the military hospital in Paris, there is a statue of him, looking down kindly on the doctors and nurses who pass by.

[long-pause]

{img:pontoon-bridges-river-niemen-army-crossing-sunrise-bands-playing}
On the morning of the twenty-fourth of June, 1812, the sun rises in a clear blue sky, over the river Niemen, and the great army begins to cross.

[pause]

For three days, the columns march over the pontoon bridges, without stopping, day and night: infantry, and cavalry, and artillery, and wagons, and horses, in an endless stream, with their flags flying, and their bands playing, and the soldiers cheering, as they pass the Emperor, who sits on a little hill, by the bridges, watching them.

[long-pause]

On the other side of the river, there is Russia. And there is nothing there.

[pause]

No Russian army. No soldiers. No one to fight. Only the forests, and the fields, and the empty road, leading east. The Russian army has retreated, inland, into the vast country, and is waiting.

[pause]

The soldiers are surprised, and a little disappointed. They expected a battle. Instead, there is only the road.

[long-pause]

The country they march through, at first, is Lithuania, and then Belarus. It is flat, or gently rolling, with great forests, of pine, and birch, and fir, and wide fields of rye and wheat, and marshes, and rivers, and lakes. The villages are small and poor: little wooden houses, called izbas, with thatched roofs, and fences of wooden stakes, and wells with long sweeping poles, and little wooden churches, with onion domes.

[pause]

The roads are not paved. They are tracks of earth, which in the summer heat turn to deep, soft dust, and in the rain, to deep, thick mud.

[pause]

On the second day after the crossing, there is a great thunderstorm, with torrents of rain, and the temperature drops suddenly, and the roads turn to mud, and thousands of horses, exhausted, and sick from eating wet green rye, collapse, and do not get up. It is a warning of what is to come.

[long-pause]

At the end of June, the army reaches the city of Vilna, which we call Vilnius, the capital of Lithuania. The Tsar himself was staying there, a few days before, and was at a ball, at a country house nearby, when he heard the news that Napoleon had crossed the Niemen.

[pause]

Napoleon stays in Vilna for more than two weeks, organizing his army, and his supplies, and the government of Lithuania. The Poles and Lithuanians of the city welcome him, hoping that he will give them back their freedom from Russia.

[pause]

{img:vilnius-1812-baroque-churches-old-town-streets-soldiers-arriving}
Vilna is a beautiful city, with many baroque churches, white and pink and yellow, and a castle on a hill, and old streets. The soldiers rest there, and eat, and drink, and write letters home.

[long-pause]

Then the army marches on, east, deeper into Russia, following the retreating Russian armies, which always seem to be one day ahead, always out of reach.

[pause]

The Russian generals, at first under Barclay de Tolly, a quiet, careful, unpopular general of Scottish descent, have decided not to fight a great battle, near the border, against Napoleon's huge army. Instead, they retreat, and retreat, drawing the French further and further into the vast spaces of Russia, away from their supplies, and their homes.

[pause]

{img:long-dusty-road-east-columns-soldiers-horizon-heat-haze}
And as they retreat, they burn the villages, and the crops, and the stores, behind them, so that the French will find nothing to eat.

[long-pause]

{img:lithuanian-summer-landscape-storks-nest-wooden-farm-lake-forest}
In the first weeks of the campaign, in the summer of 1812, the soldiers march through the countryside of Lithuania and Belarus, and many of them, in their letters, and their memories, write about its quiet beauty.

[pause]

It is a land of forests, and lakes, and meadows, and little rivers, winding through the reeds. In the meadows, there are wild flowers: cornflowers, and poppies, and daisies, and clover. On the roofs of the barns, and the tops of the old trees, there are the great nests of the white storks, who stand on one leg, and clatter their beaks, and fly slowly over the fields, looking for frogs.

[long-pause]

In the forests, there are bears, and wolves, and elk, and wild boar, and lynx, and beavers in the rivers. And in the evenings, the nightingales sing, in the bushes by the water, and the frogs call from the marshes, and the cuckoo calls from the woods.

[pause]

The villages are small, and quiet, and poor: little wooden houses, with steep roofs of thatch, or wooden shingles, and gardens with sunflowers, and beehives, made of hollow logs, and wooden crosses, carved and painted, at the crossroads, and little wooden churches.

[pause]

{img:small-market-town-wooden-houses-synagogue-square-carts-summer}
And there are little market towns, called shtetls, where many Jewish families live, as merchants, and craftsmen, and innkeepers, and tailors, with their wooden synagogues, and their markets, and their schools. The soldiers buy food, and drink, from them, and lodge in their inns, and their houses. And many of the Jewish families, in the winter, on the retreat, will take in the frozen, starving soldiers, and give them food, and warmth, and shelter.

[long-pause]

In the long summer evenings, the soldiers sometimes swim in the lakes and rivers, to wash off the dust of the road, and cool themselves. They pick wild strawberries, and raspberries, and blueberries, in the forests. They fish. They lie in the grass, and smoke their pipes, and watch the storks, and the clouds.

[pause]

For a little while, it is almost like a holiday. The young soldiers, many of them from cities, or from the vineyards of France, or the mountains of Switzerland, have never seen such wide, quiet, empty country. And some of them write home that it is beautiful.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-swimming-lake-summer-evening-uniforms-on-grass-laughing}
But the land is poor, and cannot feed so many. And day by day, the road leads them further from home.

[long-pause]

{img:infantry-column-dawn-march-drums-shakos-mist-fields}
Let us spend an ordinary day with a soldier of the Grande Armée, on the march, in the summer of 1812.

[pause]

He is a young infantryman, a fusilier, perhaps twenty years old, from a village in France, or Germany. He was called up by the conscription, two or three years ago, and has been in the army ever since. He has fought in one or two battles. He is tough, and used to hardship, and he grumbles, as soldiers do, but he is proud of his regiment, and of his Emperor.

[long-pause]

The day begins before dawn. A drum beats, the reveille, in the darkness, and the soldiers wake up, on the ground, where they slept, wrapped in their greatcoats, beside the embers of last night's fire.

[pause]

They roll up their greatcoats, and strap them to their packs, and put on their shakos, tall black hats, with a brass plate on the front, with the eagle and the number of their regiment, and a little woollen pompom, in the colour of their company. They eat a little: a piece of hard biscuit, or bread, if they have any, and drink some water, or a little brandy, from the cantinière.

[pause]

{img:soldier-packing-knapsack-greatcoat-musket-dawn-campfire}
And then they fall in, in their companies, and their battalions, and the drums beat, and they set off, along the road, in long columns, four abreast.

[long-pause]

A soldier carries a heavy load: perhaps twenty-five or thirty kilograms.

[pause]

His musket, long and heavy, with its bayonet. His cartridge box, full of cartridges, little paper tubes, filled with gunpowder and a lead ball. His knapsack, of calfskin, with the hair left on, with his spare shirts, and shoes, and stockings, and his little bag of tools for cleaning his musket, and his brush, and his comb, and his sewing kit, and perhaps a few letters from home, and a few coins. On top, his rolled greatcoat. And food for several days: biscuit, and flour, and rice, and sometimes a share of the cooking pot, which the soldiers take turns to carry.

[pause]

In the summer of 1812, it is very hot. The sun beats down on the long columns, and the dust rises from the road, thick and white, in great clouds, so thick that the soldiers cannot see the men in front of them, and it fills their eyes, and their mouths, and their noses, and covers their uniforms. And there is very little water. The wells in the villages are few, and soon drunk dry, or spoiled.

[long-pause]

The soldiers march for hours, ten or twenty or thirty kilometres a day, or more, with short rests, every hour or so, when they sit down by the road, and take off their packs, and rest their feet.

[pause]

They sing, as they march, to keep their spirits up, and to keep in step: old marching songs, and songs from home, and funny songs, about their officers, and their girls. The drummers beat the rhythm. When the Emperor rides past, they cheer: Vive l'Empereur!

[pause]

{img:soldiers-resting-roadside-shade-birch-trees-canteens-singing}
Jakob Walter, the young German, writes about the heat, and the thirst, and the dust, and how the soldiers drank water from the puddles, and the ditches, and even from the footprints of the horses. And how many of them fell ill.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, the march goes on. The soldiers' feet are sore, and blistered. Their shoes wear out, on the long roads, and they must mend them, or find new ones, or march barefoot.

[pause]

And then, in the late afternoon, or evening, the order comes to halt, and make camp, for the night, beside the road, in a field, or at the edge of a forest, near a village, or a stream.

[pause]

{img:column-halting-evening-meadow-forest-edge-soldiers-stacking-muskets}
The soldiers stack their muskets, in little pyramids, three together, and take off their packs, and stretch, and sit down on the grass. And then, tired as they are, they must find food, and water, and wood for their fires, before they can rest.

[long-pause]

{img:soldier-writing-letter-drum-as-desk-camp-summer-evening}
Every evening, in the bivouac, in the summer of 1812, some of the soldiers sit by the fire, and write letters home.

[pause]

They write on whatever paper they can find, with pencils, or with ink, carried in little bottles, using their knapsacks, or their drums, as desks. Those who cannot write ask a friend, or a sergeant, to write for them.

[long-pause]

The Grande Armée has its own postal service, called the estafette: riders, on fast horses, who carry the letters, and the dispatches, along the roads, from relay to relay, between the army and Paris, day and night. A letter from the army in Russia can reach Paris in about two or three weeks.

[pause]

So, all through the summer, and the autumn, letters travel back and forth, between the soldiers in Russia, and their families, in France, and Germany, and Italy, and Holland, and Poland.

[pause]

Many of these letters still exist. Some of them were captured by the Cossacks, on the retreat, and kept in the archives in Moscow, for two hundred years, never delivered. In recent years, historians have studied them, and published them, so that we can still read them.

[long-pause]

They are simple letters, mostly, from ordinary young men, to their mothers, and fathers, and wives, and sweethearts, and brothers, and sisters.

[pause]

They write about the long marches, and the heat, and the dust, and the bad roads. About the strange country, and the forests, and the wooden villages, and the onion-domed churches. About the food, or the lack of it. About their health. About their friends in the regiment. About the battles, sometimes, briefly. About Moscow, and its golden domes, and its palaces, and its fire.

[pause]

{img:mother-reading-letter-french-village-kitchen-window-light}
And they ask about home. How is the harvest? How is the cow? Is the new baby well? Has my little brother grown? Give my love to my grandmother. Tell my sweetheart that I think of her every day. Pray for me. I hope to be home by the winter.

[long-pause]

The officers write longer letters, to their wives, describing everything. Napoleon himself writes almost every day to his wife, Marie Louise, short, affectionate letters, asking about her health, and their little son, and telling her that he is well, and that the weather is fine, and that she should give the King of Rome a kiss from his father.

[pause]

And at home, the families wait for the letters, and read them, again and again, aloud, by the fire, to the whole family, and the neighbours. And keep them, carefully, in a box, or a drawer, for the rest of their lives.

[pause]

{img:old-letters-tied-ribbon-wooden-box-candle-quiet-room}
In the winter, the letters stopped coming. And many families waited, for months, and years, for news that never came. Some kept a candle burning in the window, every night, for a long time, in case their son should come home.

[pause]

And some sons did come home, one day, unexpectedly, thin and changed, walking up the village street, in the spring, or the summer, long after everyone had given up hope. And there was great joy.

[long-pause]

{img:bivouac-evening-many-campfires-soldiers-cooking-pots-forest-edge}
The soldiers of the Grande Armée do not sleep in tents, as later armies will. Napoleon believes that tents slow an army down. So, every night, the soldiers sleep in the open, in a bivouac, around their camp fires.

[pause]

In the summer, it is not so bad. In the autumn, and the winter, it will be very hard indeed.

[long-pause]

When the army halts for the night, the soldiers divide up into little groups, of six or eight men, called an ordinary, or a mess, who share their food, and their fire, and their cooking pot, and look after each other. These little groups are like families. The men in them are friends, and stay together, through everything.

[pause]

Some of them go to fetch wood, from the forest, or from the fences, and roofs, of the nearest village. Some go to fetch water, from the stream, or the well. And some go foraging: looking for food.

[pause]

{img:foraging-soldiers-village-searching-barns-hens-sacks-evening}
Foraging is the most important job of all. Because the army cannot carry enough food for so many men. The wagons, with the bread and the biscuit and the flour, are far behind, on the bad roads. So the soldiers must find food for themselves, in the villages, and the farms, around the road: grain, and flour, and potatoes, and cabbages, and chickens, and pigs, and cattle, and anything else they can find. But the Russian peasants have often fled, with their animals, into the forests, and the villages are empty.

[long-pause]

Back at the fire, the cook of the group makes soup, in the big iron pot.

[pause]

A soldier's soup is made of whatever they can find: water, and some meat, if they are lucky, beef, or a chicken, or mutton, and flour, or rice, or bread, and vegetables, cabbage, or potatoes, or onions, or beans, and salt, if they have any. It is boiled for a long time, over the fire. And then the soldiers sit around the pot, and eat it, with their spoons, which they carry in their buttonholes, or their shakos, and their bread.

[pause]

When there is grain, but no flour, the soldiers grind it themselves, with little hand mills, if they have them, or between stones, and make a kind of flat bread, or cakes, or porridge, cooked in the ashes.

[long-pause]

After supper, the soldiers sit around the fires, and smoke their pipes, and talk, and tell stories, and sing, and play cards, and mend their clothes, and clean their muskets, and write letters home, by the light of the fire, if they can write, or ask a friend to write for them.

[pause]

The old soldiers tell stories of their old campaigns: of Egypt, and the pyramids, and Austerlitz, and Jena, and Friedland, and Spain. The young soldiers listen, and dream of glory.

[pause]

{img:old-grenadier-telling-stories-young-conscripts-listening-fire-pipe}
Sergeant Bourgogne, of the Imperial Guard, remembers the evenings in the bivouac, in the summer, with the fires stretching away, for miles, across the plain, like the stars in the sky, and the sound of thousands of voices, talking, and singing, in many languages, and the bands playing, at the headquarters of the generals.

[long-pause]

And then, at last, the soldiers lie down to sleep, on the ground, on straw, if they can find it, or branches, wrapped in their greatcoats, with their packs as pillows, close to the fire, and close to each other, for warmth, under the open sky.

[pause]

The sentries walk up and down, at the edge of the camp, looking out into the darkness, and listening. The fires burn low. The horses stamp and shift, tied to their picket lines. And the great army sleeps, under the stars, in the middle of Russia.

[pause]

{img:sleeping-soldiers-greatcoats-around-embers-stars-sentry-silhouette}
In the summer, the nights are short, and warm, and the dawn comes early, at three or four in the morning. And then the drum beats again, and another day of marching begins.

[long-pause]

{img:artillery-horses-pulling-cannon-muddy-road-drivers-1812}
An army in 1812 runs on horses.

[pause]

There are horses for the cavalry, the hussars and the dragoons and the cuirassiers and the lancers. There are horses for the officers, and the generals, and the messengers. There are teams of horses, six or eight to each cannon, to pull the artillery. And there are thousands of horses, and oxen, to pull the supply wagons, carrying bread, and biscuit, and flour, and ammunition, and medicines, and baggage.

[long-pause]

More than a hundred and fifty thousand horses march into Russia with the Grande Armée.

[pause]

The horses come from all over Europe: big, strong horses from Normandy, and Flanders, and Holstein, for the heavy cavalry, and the artillery. Light, quick horses from Hungary and Poland, for the light cavalry. They are cared for by their riders, and by the drivers of the artillery and the wagons, and by farriers, and veterinary surgeons.

[pause]

A cavalryman's horse is his most important possession, and his friend. He must feed it, and water it, and brush it, and look after its feet, and its back, and its saddle, before he looks after himself. A good cavalryman, the old soldiers say, will go hungry himself, before he lets his horse go hungry.

[long-pause]

But in Russia, there is a terrible problem: there is not enough food for the horses.

[pause]

A horse needs a great deal of food: hay, and oats, and water, every day. The army cannot carry enough. So the horses must graze, along the way, in the fields, and the meadows. But there are so many of them, that the fields along the road are soon eaten bare. And the horses are given green, unripe rye, from the fields, which makes them ill.

[pause]

In the first weeks of the campaign, after the great thunderstorm, and the heat, and the bad food, and the long marches, thousands and thousands of horses fall ill, and cannot go on. By the time the army reaches Moscow, a great many of the horses have been lost. And this will matter very much, later, in the winter.

[long-pause]

The wagons, too, have problems. The roads are terrible: deep sand, and dust, in the summer, and deep mud, when it rains. The wagons sink in, up to their axles, and must be pushed, and pulled, and dug out. They break down. They fall behind. And so the soldiers at the front of the army often have no bread, while the bread is far away, stuck on the roads.

[pause]

The Russian peasants have their own little carts, called kibitkas, light, and simple, on four wheels, pulled by a single little Russian horse, which are much better on the Russian roads. Many soldiers and officers buy or take them, and use them for their baggage.

[pause]

{img:small-russian-peasant-cart-kibitka-shaggy-horse-sandy-road}
The little Russian horses, shaggy, and tough, and small, are used to the climate, and can eat anything, and live through the winter. The big horses of Western Europe are not.

[long-pause]

And still the army marches on, east, through the heat of July, and August.

[pause]

Through the town of Vitebsk, where Napoleon stops for two weeks, and thinks, for a while, about stopping there, for the winter, and going on in the spring. But he decides to go on. And on, to Smolensk, an old city, with great red walls, on the river Dnieper, the gateway to Russia proper, where, in the middle of August, there is a battle, and the city burns.

[pause]

{img:smolensk-red-walls-river-dnieper-domes-distance-haze}
The Russians retreat again, toward Moscow. And Napoleon follows them, along the great road, with the summer coming to an end, and the nights growing cooler.

[long-pause]

{img:cossack-village-don-river-steppe-houses-horses-summer}
Who are the Cossacks, who follow the Grande Armée all the way from Moscow to the Niemen?

[pause]

They are free horsemen, from the great southern steppes of Russia, along the rivers Don, and Dnieper, and Volga, and Ural. Their ancestors were peasants, and runaways, and adventurers, who fled from their lords, hundreds of years ago, to the wild, open frontier lands of the south, and lived there freely, as hunters, and fishermen, and farmers, and horsemen, and fought the Tatars, and the Turks.

[long-pause]

They live in their own villages, called stanitsas, along the rivers, and govern themselves, with their own elected leaders, called atamans. In return for their freedom, and their land, every Cossack man must serve the Tsar, as a soldier, on horseback, with his own horse, and his own weapons.

[pause]

They are wonderful riders. A Cossack boy is put on a horse at the age of three. They can ride for days, without rest, and sleep in the saddle, and pick things up from the ground, at full gallop, and stand on their horses' backs. Their horses are small, and shaggy, and tough, and can find food under the snow, and live through the winter, when the big horses of Western Europe cannot.

[pause]

{img:cossack-horseman-lance-fur-hat-long-coat-snow-plain-calm}
They wear long dark coats, and wide trousers, with a coloured stripe, and tall fur hats, and they carry long lances, and curved swords, and pistols, and whips. They have beards, and moustaches, and they sing, as they ride, long, sad, beautiful songs, about the steppe, and the river Don, and their mothers, and their horses.

[long-pause]

In 1812, the Cossacks do not fight great battles. Instead, they scout, and raid, and harass. They follow the French army, on both sides of the road, day and night, and attack the foragers, and the stragglers, and the wagons, and the messengers, and then vanish into the forests, before the French can catch them. The French soldiers fear them, more than anything.

[pause]

Their leader is the great Cossack ataman, Matvei Platov, a brave, cunning, cheerful old soldier, who loves his Cossacks, and whom they love.

[pause]

{img:cossack-camp-night-fire-singing-horses-tethered-stars}
In 1814, after the war, the Cossacks ride all the way to Paris, with the Russian army, and camp on the Champs-Élysées, in the middle of the city, and the Parisians come to stare at them, in wonder. There is a story that the Cossacks, in the cafés of Paris, would bang on the tables, and shout bystro, which means quickly, in Russian, because they were in a hurry. And that this is the origin of the French word bistro, for a little café. It is probably not true. But it is a good story.

[long-pause]

{img:tsar-alexander-first-tall-fair-uniform-epaulettes-palace-window}
Who are the people on the other side, in this war?

[pause]

The ruler of Russia is the Tsar, Alexander the First. He is thirty-four years old, tall, and fair, and handsome, and charming, with blue eyes, and a gentle manner. He is a little deaf in one ear. He was brought up by a Swiss tutor, who taught him the ideas of the Enlightenment, of liberty and reason. He speaks French better than Russian, as all Russian nobles do.

[long-pause]

He is a strange, complicated man: idealistic, and religious, and mystical, and also cunning, and stubborn, and secretive. Napoleon once called him a Byzantine Greek. But in 1812, Alexander makes one decision, and keeps it, whatever happens: he will never make peace with Napoleon, as long as there is a single French soldier on Russian soil. Even if he has to retreat to Siberia, he says, and let his beard grow, and live on potatoes with his peasants.

[pause]

The Russian army is a great army too, though smaller than Napoleon's.

[pause]

{img:russian-infantry-green-coats-shakos-marching-birch-forest}
Its soldiers are mostly peasants, serfs, taken from their villages, for twenty-five years of service, which is almost all their lives. They are tough, and patient, and brave, and religious, and they fight for their Tsar, and their Holy Russia, and their Orthodox church. Before battle, the priests carry the icons, the holy pictures, along the lines, and the soldiers kneel, and pray, and cross themselves.

[long-pause]

The Cossacks are the most famous of the Russian soldiers: horsemen, from the free communities of the southern steppes, along the rivers Don and Volga, wild, and brave, and wonderful riders, with long lances, and shaggy little horses, and fur caps, who scout, and raid, and appear out of nowhere, and disappear again, and harass the French columns, day and night.

[pause]

The commander of the Russian armies, at the beginning of the war, is Barclay de Tolly, a careful general, whose plan is to retreat, and wear out the French. But the Russians hate retreating, and many of them do not trust Barclay, because his family came from Scotland, and Germany. So, in August, the Tsar appoints a new commander: Mikhail Kutuzov.

[pause]

Kutuzov is sixty-seven years old. He is fat, and old, and lazy, his enemies say, and has lost an eye, from a wound, long ago, in a battle against the Turks. He sleeps a great deal, and reads French novels, and likes good food. But he is wise, and patient, and cunning, and the soldiers love him, because he is Russian, and old, and cares about them. They call him grandfather.

[long-pause]

And there are the Russian peasants, in their villages, along the road.

[pause]

Most of them are serfs, who belong to their lords, and live very hard lives, in little wooden houses, growing rye, and potatoes, and cabbages, and keeping a cow, and some chickens. They are deeply religious, with icons in the corner of every room, and a little lamp burning in front of them. They wear long shirts, and bark shoes, made of woven birch bark, and sheepskin coats, in winter.

[pause]

{img:russian-peasant-family-izba-icon-corner-lamp-stove-evening}
When the French army comes, most of them flee into the forests, with their animals, and their food. And as the war goes on, many of them take up arms, with axes, and pitchforks, and old muskets, and fight against the invaders, in little bands, in the forests, and attack the French foragers, and stragglers. They become partisans.

[long-pause]

The great Russian writer Leo Tolstoy, who was born sixteen years after 1812, will write a famous novel, War and Peace, about the war, and about the Russian families, and soldiers, and peasants, who lived through it. In it, he describes the Russian people rising up, all together, like a great wave, to drive the invader out of their land.

[pause]

{img:birch-forest-autumn-gold-leaves-quiet-path-russia}
And in the summer of 1812, as the French march toward Moscow, the people of Russia are getting ready.

[long-pause]

{img:russian-izba-winter-snow-smoke-chimney-sledge-horse-blue-dusk}
How do the Russians themselves live through their long, hard winters?

[pause]

For the people of Russia, the winter is not a terrible enemy. It is simply part of life, as it has been for hundreds of years. And they know how to live with it.

[long-pause]

The heart of every Russian peasant house, the izba, is the stove.

[pause]

It is enormous: a great block of brick and clay, whitewashed, taking up a quarter of the room, with an oven inside, for cooking, and baking bread. It is lit once a day, in the morning, with a fire of birch logs, and the thick walls of the stove soak up the heat, and give it out slowly, all day, and all night, so that the house stays warm, even when it is thirty degrees below zero outside.

[pause]

{img:russian-stove-interior-family-sleeping-on-top-warm-icons-lamp}
On top of the stove, there is a flat shelf, where the old people, and the children, sleep at night, warm and snug, on blankets and sheepskins. It is the warmest place in the house, and the best.

[long-pause]

The izba is built of logs, thick, round logs, of pine, or fir, laid one on top of the other, and the gaps between them filled with moss, to keep out the wind. The windows are small, and double, in winter. Snow is piled up against the walls, outside, to keep in the warmth.

[pause]

The peasants wear thick clothes, made for the cold. Long coats of sheepskin, with the wool inside, called tulups, reaching to the ankles. Fur hats, with flaps to cover the ears. Mittens of wool and fur. And on their feet, valenki: thick, warm boots, made of felted wool, all in one piece, which keep the feet warm and dry in the dry, frozen snow.

[pause]

{img:peasants-sheepskin-coats-fur-hats-felt-boots-village-snow-sun}
If only the soldiers of the Grande Armée had had such clothes, very many more of them would have come home.

[long-pause]

In winter, the Russians travel by sledge. The snow, which makes the roads impossible for wagons, makes them perfect for sledges. The rivers freeze hard, and become roads. And the peasants, and the merchants, and the nobles, travel quickly and easily, across the snowy country, in sledges, pulled by little shaggy horses, with bells on their harness, which jingle in the frosty air.

[pause]

And every week, on Saturday, many Russian families go to the banya: a little wooden bath house, by the river, or the pond, heated by a stove, with hot stones, on which water is thrown, to make clouds of steam. They sit in the steam, and beat themselves gently with bundles of birch twigs, to make the blood flow. And then they run out, and roll in the snow, or jump into a hole in the ice of the river, and run back in, to the heat, laughing.

[pause]

{img:wooden-banya-bathhouse-steam-snow-birch-twigs-riverbank}
The long winter evenings are spent around the stove, with the women spinning, and weaving, and sewing, by the light of a burning splinter of birch wood, or a little oil lamp, in front of the icons. And the men mending tools, and harness, and making bast shoes. And the old people telling stories, and fairy tales, about Baba Yaga, the witch, who lives in a hut on chicken legs, deep in the forest, and Father Frost, and the firebird, and the brave young Ivan.

[long-pause]

And in winter, there are festivals: Christmas, and the New Year, and the Epiphany, when the priest blesses the waters, through a hole cut in the ice of the river, in the shape of a cross. And Maslenitsa, at the end of winter, before Lent, the butter week, when everyone eats pancakes, blini, and goes sledging down hills, and has snowball fights, and builds snow fortresses, and burns a straw figure of winter, to welcome the spring.

[pause]

The Russians love their winter. Their poets write about its beauty: the white silence of the forests, the frost on the windows, like silver ferns, the bright sun on the snow, and the sound of sledge bells, far off, across the white fields.

[long-pause]

{img:borodino-fields-september-dawn-mist-village-church-distance}
At the beginning of September, 1812, about a hundred and twenty kilometres west of Moscow, the Russian army stops retreating, and turns to fight.

[pause]

Kutuzov has chosen a place near a little village called Borodino, with a white church, beside a little river, the Kolocha, among rolling fields and woods and ravines, across the great road to Moscow. And there, the Russians dig earthworks, and wait.

[long-pause]

On the evening before the battle, the sixth of September, the two armies are camped close to each other, on the fields, with their fires burning, all night, for miles.

[pause]

In the Russian camp, the priests carry a holy icon, the Black Virgin of Smolensk, which was rescued from the burning city, along the lines of the army. And the soldiers kneel, and take off their caps, and pray, and the priests bless them, and sprinkle them with holy water.

[pause]

In the French camp, that evening, a messenger arrives from Paris, with a portrait of Napoleon's little son, the King of Rome. Napoleon has it placed outside his tent, on a chair, so that the soldiers of the Guard can see it. And the old soldiers come, and look at the picture of the little boy, and some of them are moved to tears.

[long-pause]

And then, on the seventh of September, at dawn, the battle begins.

[pause]

We will not go into the battle. We will stand far away, on a little hill, at the edge of the forest, and watch from a great distance. From there, we would have seen only the smoke, great clouds of white smoke, from tens of thousands of muskets, and more than a thousand cannons, drifting across the fields, in the autumn sunshine, and heard the endless thunder of the guns, all day long, from dawn until dark.

[pause]

It was one of the greatest and hardest battles in history. By the evening, very many men, of both sides, had been lost, or wounded. Neither side had really won. The Russian army retreated, in good order, during the night, toward Moscow. And the French held the field.

[long-pause]

Dominique Larrey, the chief surgeon of the Grande Armée, worked through the day, and the night, and the next day, without stopping, caring for the wounded, French and Russian alike, in field hospitals, in barns, and in the open fields. He was a kind and brave man, who had invented a system of light, fast carriages, with springs, called flying ambulances, to carry the wounded from the battlefield quickly to the surgeons. He treated everyone, he said, whatever their rank, or their nation.

[pause]

{img:surgeon-larrey-tending-wounded-field-hospital-barn-lanterns-calm}
Napoleon himself, that day, was not well. He had a bad cold, and a fever, and could hardly speak. He did not use his Guard, his reserve of the finest soldiers, in the battle, because, he said, he was eight hundred leagues from Paris, and could not risk it.

[long-pause]

After the battle, Kutuzov held a council of war, in a peasant's hut, in a village called Fili, just outside Moscow, with his generals, sitting around a table, under the icons, while a little girl, the peasant's granddaughter, watched them from the top of the great stove.

[pause]

They argued about whether to fight another battle, to defend Moscow. And at last, Kutuzov said that if they fought, they might lose the army, and then Russia would be lost. But if they gave up Moscow, they would keep the army, and Russia would be saved. And so, he ordered the army to retreat, through Moscow, and beyond, and leave the city to the French.

[pause]

{img:peasant-hut-council-generals-table-icons-child-on-stove-candle}
It was a terrible decision. Moscow was the ancient, holy heart of Russia. But Kutuzov believed it was right.

[long-pause]

{img:moscow-1812-golden-domes-kremlin-towers-view-from-hill-september}
On the fourteenth of September, 1812, the vanguard of the Grande Armée comes over the top of a hill, west of Moscow, called Poklonnaya Gora, the Hill of Salutation, and sees, for the first time, the city spread out below.

[pause]

The soldiers stop, and stare, and cheer. Moscow! Moscow!

[long-pause]

It is a wonderful sight. The city lies in a wide valley, along a winding river, in the golden September sunshine. It is enormous, one of the largest cities in Europe, with perhaps two hundred and fifty thousand people. And it is full of churches, hundreds and hundreds of them, with domes shaped like onions, painted green, and blue, and red, and covered with gold, and crowned with golden crosses, glittering in the sun.

[pause]

Sergeant Bourgogne writes that the soldiers forgot all their hardships, and their fatigue, at the sight of it, and clapped their hands, and shouted, and danced.

[pause]

In the middle of the city, on a hill above the river, there is the Kremlin: the ancient fortress of the Tsars, with its great red walls, and towers, and inside, palaces, and cathedrals, with golden domes, and the great bell tower of Ivan the Great.

[long-pause]

Napoleon waits, on the hill, for the leaders of the city to come out, and bring him the keys, and surrender, as the leaders of Vienna, and Berlin, and other capitals, have done before.

[pause]

But nobody comes. Hour after hour, he waits. And at last, his officers come back from the city, and tell him that it is empty.

[pause]

Almost all the people of Moscow have left: the nobles, in their carriages, and the merchants, and the ordinary people, with whatever they could carry. Only a few remain: the poor, and the old, and the sick, and some foreigners, and some criminals, released from the prisons. The governor of the city, Count Rostopchin, has ordered everyone to leave.

[long-pause]

The French march into the city, through the empty streets, with their bands playing. The houses are shuttered. The shops are closed. The only sounds are the drums, and the tramp of the soldiers' feet, echoing from the walls. Bourgogne writes that the silence was strange, and frightening.

[pause]

Napoleon goes to the Kremlin, and moves into the palace of the Tsars. He has taken Moscow. He believes that now, surely, the Tsar will ask for peace.

[pause]

{img:kremlin-palace-rooms-gilded-napoleon-window-looking-out-evening}
But that night, fires begin to break out, in different parts of the city.

[long-pause]

We will watch, again, from far away. From a hill outside the city, on the night of the fifteenth of September, and the nights that followed, you would have seen the glow, in the sky, over Moscow, growing larger and larger, as the fires spread, from street to street, driven by a strong wind, through the old city, which was built mostly of wood.

[pause]

For four days, the fires burned. Napoleon had to leave the Kremlin, and move to a palace outside the city, the Petrovsky Palace, until they died down.

[pause]

{img:distant-glow-sky-over-city-night-hill-silhouette-calm-far}
Who started the fires? Most historians believe that they were started on the orders of Count Rostopchin, the governor, so that the French would find no shelter, and no supplies, in Moscow. Some were perhaps accidents, by French soldiers, looting the empty houses, by candlelight. We will never know for certain.

[long-pause]

When the fires were over, about three quarters of the city had been destroyed. The wooden houses were gone. Only the stone churches, and the stone palaces, and the Kremlin, stood, among the ashes, and the chimneys, and the blackened walls.

[pause]

And the Grande Armée settled down, in what was left of Moscow, to wait for the Tsar's answer.

[long-pause]

{img:moscow-1811-street-merchants-sledges-churches-colourful-houses}
What was Moscow like, before the French came, in the year 1812?

[pause]

It was a city unlike any other in Europe. Not as grand, or as regular, as Saint Petersburg, the new capital, by the sea, built by Peter the Great, a hundred years before, with its straight avenues, and stone palaces, in the European style. Moscow was older, and more Russian, and more like a great, sprawling village.

[long-pause]

It was a city of contrasts. Great palaces of the nobles, painted yellow, and white, and pale green, with columns, and gardens, and parks, stood beside little wooden houses, with carved windows, and vegetable gardens, and cows, and chickens. There were wide boulevards, and narrow, winding lanes. And everywhere, there were churches, more than three hundred of them, with their onion domes, and their bell towers. People said that there were forty times forty churches in Moscow.

[pause]

On great feast days, all the bells of Moscow rang together, in a great wave of sound, that could be heard for miles, across the countryside.

[pause]

The great noble families of Russia lived in Moscow, in the winter, in their palaces, with hundreds of servants, many of them serfs, from their estates, and gave balls, and dinners, and concerts, and plays, in their own private theatres, with serf actors and musicians. They spoke French, and dressed in the latest fashions from Paris, and read French books, and some of them could hardly speak Russian. Their children had French tutors.

[long-pause]

And there were the merchants, with their long beards, and their long coats, and their tall boots, in their shops, in the great trading rows, near Red Square, selling silk, and tea, and furs, and spices, from China, and Persia, and Central Asia, and cloth, and iron, and leather, from all over Russia.

[pause]

There were markets, where the peasants came in from the countryside, with their sledges, in winter, loaded with frozen meat, and fish, and butter, and eggs, and grain, and wood, and hay, and honey, and mushrooms.

[pause]

{img:red-square-1811-trading-rows-merchants-saint-basil-domes-snow}
And on Red Square, beside the Kremlin, there was the strange, wonderful cathedral of Saint Basil, with its many domes, all different, twisted, and striped, and patterned, in red, and green, and blue, and gold, like a bunch of flowers, or a fairy tale.

[long-pause]

When the news came, in August 1812, that the French were coming, and in September, that Kutuzov would not defend the city, the people of Moscow left.

[pause]

The nobles packed their carriages, and their carts, with their furniture, and their paintings, and their silver, and their servants, and left for their estates in the country, or for other cities. The merchants closed their shops. The ordinary people walked out, along the roads to the east, with their bundles, and their children, and their cows. Long lines of carts and carriages and people filled the roads, for days. Some families had to leave everything behind.

[pause]

{img:families-leaving-moscow-carts-carriages-road-east-september-dust}
In War and Peace, Tolstoy describes the Rostov family, packing to leave Moscow, with all their carts loaded with their possessions, when the young daughter, Natasha, sees the wounded soldiers in the courtyard, and persuades her parents to unload the carts, and leave their furniture, and their treasures, behind, and take the wounded with them, instead. It is one of the most beautiful scenes in the book.

[long-pause]

Most of the people of Moscow came back, in the winter, and the spring, after the French had gone, to find their city in ruins, their houses gone, their churches blackened.

[pause]

But they began, at once, to rebuild. And within a few years, Moscow was alive again, with new houses, and palaces, and churches, and markets, and the bells ringing out again, on the feast days, across the city.

[long-pause]

{img:french-soldiers-camping-moscow-mansion-courtyard-autumn-leaves}
For five weeks, from the middle of September to the middle of October, 1812, the Grande Armée stays in Moscow.

[pause]

It is a strange time. The weather is beautiful: a golden autumn, warm and sunny, day after day. The soldiers say that it is warmer than in Paris. Napoleon says that the Russian winter is a fable, invented to frighten people.

[long-pause]

The soldiers find lodging where they can, in the houses and palaces that did not burn, and in churches, and cellars. They find food in the cellars, and storehouses, that survived the fire: flour, and preserved fruit, and sugar, and tea, and fish, and wine, and brandy, and liqueurs. For a while, after the hungry months of the march, they eat, and drink, very well.

[pause]

They also find treasure: silver, and gold, and furs, and silk, and jewels, and paintings, and fine clothes, in the empty palaces, and churches, and shops. And many of them fill their knapsacks, and their wagons, with them, to take home.

[pause]

Sergeant Bourgogne describes his own knapsack, when the army left Moscow: a few pounds of sugar, some rice, some biscuit, a bottle of liqueur, a woman's Chinese silk dress, embroidered in gold and silver, several gold and silver ornaments, a piece of the cross of Ivan the Great, from the bell tower, a woman's cloak, lined with green velvet, two silver pictures, in relief, of the judgment of Paris, a crucifix, and a little Chinese porcelain vase. He was very proud of it. Most of it, he would leave by the road.

[long-pause]

There is even entertainment. Some French actors, who had been living in Moscow before the war, stay in the city, and the officers arrange performances, in a theatre, in one of the palaces, with plays, and music, to pass the time. Napoleon attends some of them.

[pause]

Napoleon works hard, in the Kremlin, as always: dictating letters, and orders, and reading reports from Paris, and even drawing up new rules for the Comédie-Française, the great theatre of Paris.

[pause]

And he waits for the Tsar's answer. He sends messages to Alexander, offering peace. But no answer comes. The Tsar will not talk to him, as long as he is in Russia.

[long-pause]

Meanwhile, outside Moscow, the Russian army is resting, and growing stronger, with new soldiers, and supplies, in a camp south of the city, at Tarutino. And the Cossacks, and the peasant partisans, are attacking the French foragers, who go out of the city to look for food, and for hay for the horses.

[pause]

The army's horses are starving. There is no hay, and no oats. Every day, more of them are lost.

[pause]

{img:cossack-patrol-watching-from-forest-edge-autumn-misty-field}
Some of Napoleon's officers urge him to leave Moscow, before it is too late, and march back to Poland, for the winter. Others urge him to stay, for the winter, in Moscow. Napoleon cannot decide. He waits, and hopes, day after day, for the Tsar's answer.

[long-pause]

And then, on the thirteenth of October, the first snow falls, lightly, on the ruins of Moscow. It melts at once. But it is a warning.

[pause]

At last, Napoleon decides to leave. On the nineteenth of October, 1812, five weeks after he entered the city, the Grande Armée marches out of Moscow, heading south west, with about a hundred thousand men, and a huge, long train of wagons, and carriages, and carts, loaded with food, and treasure, and the wounded, and the families of French people who had lived in Moscow, and were afraid to stay.

[pause]

{img:long-column-leaving-moscow-wagons-carriages-autumn-road-domes-behind}
The column is so long that it takes many hours to pass any one point. And the soldiers look back, at the golden domes of Moscow, in the autumn sunshine, for the last time.

[long-pause]

{img:retreat-column-autumn-road-birch-trees-grey-sky-wagons}
Napoleon's plan is to march south west, through country that the war has not touched, where there will be food, and then turn west, toward Smolensk, and Poland.

[pause]

But at a town called Maloyaroslavets, the Russian army blocks the road. There is a battle, and the town burns. And Napoleon, after thinking for a long time, decides not to try to push through. Instead, he turns the army north, back onto the great road to Smolensk: the same road that the army had marched along, in the summer, on the way to Moscow.

[long-pause]

It is a fateful decision. Because that road has already been stripped bare. The villages along it have been burned, and the fields eaten, and the people have fled. There is nothing left to eat.

[pause]

And so, at the end of October, the long column marches back along the road it came by, through the empty, ruined country, past the field of Borodino, where the battle was fought, seven weeks before, in the summer. The soldiers pass it in silence.

[pause]

The weather is still fine, at first: cool, and clear, with frost at night. But the food is running out. The horses are failing, and as they fail, the wagons must be left behind, and the cannons, and the treasure, and the soldiers must carry what they can.

[long-pause]

Every day, the Cossacks ride around the column, like wolves around a flock, and attack the stragglers, and the wagons, and the foragers, and then disappear into the forests. The soldiers must stay together, in their groups, for safety.

[pause]

The column grows longer, and more spread out, every day. At the front, there is Napoleon, and the Imperial Guard, still in good order. Then the different corps of the army. And behind them, and among them, a growing crowd of stragglers, men who have lost their regiments, and their weapons, and walk along alone, or in little groups, looking for food, and shelter.

[pause]

{img:imperial-guard-marching-good-order-bearskins-autumn-wind}
And at the very back, the rear guard, which must protect the whole army from the pursuing Russians.

[long-pause]

Jakob Walter, the young German soldier, writes about these days: how he and his friends searched for food, every evening, in the burned villages, and found a few frozen potatoes, or a little grain, or a few cabbage leaves, and cooked them in their pot, over a little fire. And how they shared what they found with each other, and helped each other, and stayed together.

[pause]

He writes about the kindness of his friends, and the generosity of strangers, and how, sometimes, a stranger would give him a piece of bread, or a place at a fire, when he most needed it.

[pause]

{img:small-group-soldiers-sharing-bread-tiny-fire-ruined-barn-dusk}
In all the stories of the retreat from Moscow, there are terrible things. But there are also stories of friendship, and loyalty, and courage, and kindness, among the soldiers, which shine out, like little fires, in the darkness.

[long-pause]

{img:regimental-eagle-standard-golden-eagle-pole-flag-guard-proud}
Every regiment of the Grande Armée carries an eagle.

[pause]

It is a golden eagle, of gilded bronze, with its wings half spread, standing on a little platform, on top of a long pole, with the regiment's flag, the colour, hanging below it, in blue, and white, and red, embroidered in gold, with the names of the regiment's battles.

[long-pause]

Napoleon gave the eagles to the regiments himself, in a great ceremony, in Paris, in 1804, on the Champ de Mars, and the soldiers swore to defend them with their lives. The eagle is the soul of the regiment. It is carried, in battle, and on the march, by a chosen soldier, the eagle bearer, with two brave sergeants beside him, to protect it. To lose the eagle is the greatest shame. To capture an enemy's eagle is the greatest glory.

[pause]

During the retreat from Moscow, many regiments, which had almost ceased to exist, still kept their eagles. Some soldiers took the eagles off their poles, and carried them inside their coats, or their knapsacks, through the snow, so that they would not be lost. Some buried them, secretly, in the snow, or in the earth, or threw them into rivers, rather than let the Cossacks take them.

[pause]

{img:soldier-hiding-eagle-under-greatcoat-snow-march-quiet-resolve}
And there was music. Every regiment had its band, and its drummers, and its fifers, and its trumpeters.

[long-pause]

The drummers were often young boys, of twelve or thirteen, or fourteen. They beat the drums for the march, and for the reveille in the morning, and for the commands in battle. The drum beats were like a language: one beat meant advance, another meant halt, another meant gather round. Over the noise of battle, the soldiers could hear the drums, when they could not hear their officers' voices.

[pause]

On the march, in the summer, the bands played marches, and songs, to keep the soldiers' spirits up, and their feet moving. And in the evenings, at the camps, they played for the officers, and the soldiers, sometimes dances, sometimes old songs from home.

[pause]

{img:young-drummer-boy-drum-sticks-column-marching-summer-road}
Sergeant Bourgogne remembers that, when the army entered Moscow, the bands of the Guard played a march, called the Victory is Ours, as the soldiers marched through the empty streets.

[long-pause]

And in the winter, on the retreat, there was little music. The instruments froze, and the bandsmen's fingers were too cold to play, and the drums were abandoned, by the road, with the wagons, and the cannons.

[pause]

But sometimes, in the bivouac, at night, around the fire, a soldier would sing, quietly, an old song, from home, from his village, in France, or Germany, or Italy, or Holland. And the others would listen, and some would join in, softly. And for a few minutes, they would be far away, at home, in the summer, with their families, in the warm.

[long-pause]

{img:first-snow-falling-forest-road-column-soldiers-grey-november}
On the sixth of November, 1812, the weather changes.

[pause]

The sky turns grey and heavy. A cold wind begins to blow from the north. And then the snow begins to fall: thick, heavy, silent snow, falling and falling, all day, and all night, covering the road, and the fields, and the forests, and the soldiers, in a deep white blanket.

[long-pause]

The temperature falls, below freezing, and keeps falling. The Russian winter, which Napoleon had called a fable, has come.

[pause]

For the soldiers on the road, everything becomes harder. The road disappears under the snow. The soldiers sink in it, up to their knees. The horses slip, and fall, on the icy road, because their shoes are not made for ice, and many of them cannot get up again. The wagons, and the cannons, are left behind, by the side of the road, in the snow.

[pause]

The soldiers' clothes are not made for the Russian winter. They wear the same uniforms that they wore in the summer: thin trousers, and jackets, and shakos, and shoes, and their greatcoats. Nothing more. Their shoes are worn out, and their feet are wrapped in rags.

[long-pause]

So they wrap themselves in anything they can find: the furs, and silks, and shawls, and cloaks, and carpets, and blankets, that they took from Moscow. Women's dresses, and priests' robes, and sheepskins, and fur hats, and the cloaks of their fallen comrades.

[pause]

The army no longer looks like an army. Sergeant Bourgogne describes himself, at this time: wearing a woman's fur-lined cloak, of yellow silk, and a cap of fur, with a scarf tied around his head, and his face black with the smoke of camp fires, and his beard and moustache full of icicles.

[pause]

He writes that the soldiers looked like a masquerade, a carnival, a crowd of strange figures, in every kind of garment, walking slowly, silently, through the snow, with their heads down, against the wind.

[long-pause]

The nights are the hardest.

[pause]

There is no shelter: the villages along the road have been burned. The soldiers must sleep in the open, in the snow, around their fires. They cut down trees, and pull down fences, and the ruins of houses, to make their fires. And they sit, or lie, as close to the fire as they can, wrapped in everything they have, and try to sleep.

[pause]

{img:night-snow-bivouac-many-small-fires-soldiers-huddled-stars-cold}
Bourgogne writes about a night when he and his friends found an old barn, still standing, and crowded into it, dozens of men, with their fire in the middle, and slept, warm, for the first time in days. And how happy they were, for a few hours.

[long-pause]

Napoleon himself walks, much of the time, in the snow, with his Guard, wearing a fur coat, and a fur hat, and carrying a stick, to keep warm, and to encourage his soldiers.

[pause]

The soldiers of the Guard still march in order, in their ranks, with their bearskins, and their muskets. Napoleon's presence among them gives them courage. When they see him, walking beside them, in the snow, they still cheer.

[pause]

{img:napoleon-walking-snow-fur-coat-stick-guard-beside-him-quiet}
On the ninth of November, the army reaches Smolensk, where there are supply stores, and the soldiers hope for food, and rest, and shelter. But the stores are not enough for everyone, and they are quickly emptied, and the army must go on, west, through the snow, toward the river Berezina, and Poland.

[long-pause]

{img:small-group-soldiers-around-fire-snow-sharing-soup-pot-friends}
How did anyone survive the retreat from Moscow?

[pause]

Those who did, and wrote about it later, all say the same thing: they survived because of their friends, and their little groups, who stayed together, and shared everything, and helped each other, through everything.

[long-pause]

The soldiers learned many things, in those weeks, to stay alive.

[pause]

They learned to keep moving, during the day, and never to sit down to rest in the snow, because if you sat down, and fell asleep, in the cold, you might never wake up. They learned to keep their feet dry, if they could, and to wrap them in rags, and straw, and fur. They learned not to come too close to a fire, with frozen hands and feet, too quickly, but to warm them slowly, or to rub them with snow.

[pause]

{img:soldier-wrapping-feet-rags-straw-fireside-snow-careful}
They learned to cover their faces, against the wind, leaving only their eyes. And to look after each other's faces, and to tell a friend if his nose, or his cheeks, were turning white, from the cold, so that he could rub them, and bring back the blood.

[long-pause]

They learned to find food anywhere. In the frozen fields, they dug under the snow, for frozen potatoes, and cabbages. In the ruined villages, they searched the cellars, and the barns, for grain, and flour. They made a kind of porridge, from flour, and melted snow, cooked over the fire, which they called rata. They ate the horses that had fallen, which, for many soldiers, was the main food of the retreat. Sergeant Bourgogne writes that he never ate anything so good, in his whole life, as a piece of horse meat, roasted on the end of his sword, over a fire, in the snow, after days of hunger.

[pause]

They shared what they had. A soldier who found food, and ate it alone, was despised. A soldier who shared it was loved.

[pause]

The cantinières, the women who followed the army, were among the bravest of all. Many of them stayed with their regiments, all through the retreat, and gave the soldiers food, and drink, and care, and nursed the sick, and kept their little carts going, through the snow. Bourgogne tells of one cantinière, Mother Dubois, of his regiment, who gave birth to a baby, in the snow, in a bivouac, during the retreat, and how the soldiers gave her their cloaks, and the colonel his own cloak, to keep her and the baby warm. And she and the baby both survived.

[long-pause]

Others survived because of kindness, from unexpected people.

[pause]

Some Russian peasants, in the villages, took pity on the frozen, starving soldiers, and gave them bread, and shelter, by their stoves, and let them go. Some Jewish families, in the towns of Lithuania and Belarus, gave them food, and warmth, and hid them. Some Russian officers treated their prisoners kindly.

[pause]

{img:russian-peasant-woman-giving-bread-frozen-soldier-izba-door-snow}
And some survived simply by luck: a barn found at the right moment, a fire, a friend, a piece of bread, a horse, a sledge, a place in a wagon.

[long-pause]

Jakob Walter, the young German, survived. He tells how he lost his friends, one by one, and his strength, and how he went on, alone, step by step, through the snow. How he found a little horse, a Russian pony, and rode it, and it saved his life. How he was ill with fever, and hardly knew where he was. And how, at last, he came to Poland, and then home, to his village in Württemberg, thin, and ill, and changed forever.

[pause]

{img:german-village-winter-1813-church-snow-soldier-returning-home}
He lived to be an old man, and emigrated, later, to America, to Kansas, where he wrote down his memories, for his children, many years later. His little book is one of the most moving accounts of the war ever written.

[long-pause]

{img:marshal-ney-red-hair-musket-rear-guard-snow-calm-determined}
Of all the heroes of the retreat from Moscow, the most famous is Marshal Michel Ney.

[pause]

Ney is forty-three years old. He is the son of a barrel maker, from Saarlouis, on the border of France and Germany. He joined the army as a young hussar, before the Revolution, and rose, by courage and skill, to be one of Napoleon's marshals, and a duke, and later a prince. He has red hair, and a hot temper, and is the bravest man in the army. Napoleon calls him the bravest of the brave.

[long-pause]

During the retreat, Ney commands the rear guard: the last part of the army, which must protect all the rest from the pursuing Russians, holding them back, day after day, so that the rest of the army, and the stragglers, can get away.

[pause]

It is the hardest and most dangerous job of all. The rear guard is always the last to leave every place, and the first to be attacked. And every day, it grows smaller.

[pause]

Ney does not just command. He fights, himself, with a musket in his hand, like an ordinary soldier, beside his men, in the snow. His soldiers say that he seems to be made of iron. He never seems tired, or cold, or afraid.

[long-pause]

In the middle of November, after Smolensk, near a town called Krasnoi, Ney and his rear guard find themselves cut off, behind the Russian army, which has blocked the road ahead of them. The Russians send an officer, under a flag of truce, to ask him to surrender. A marshal of France does not surrender, Ney answers.

[pause]

That night, he leads his men away, north, through the dark forest, in the snow, to the river Dnieper. The river is frozen, but the ice is thin, and cracking. In the darkness, Ney and his men cross the river, on the ice, a few at a time, carefully, testing the ice as they go, while it creaks and groans under them.

[pause]

Then, for three days, they march west, along the river, through the forests, followed by the Cossacks, fighting them off, by day and by night. And at last, they reach the rest of the army, near the town of Orsha.

[long-pause]

When Napoleon hears that Ney is safe, he is overjoyed. He says that he has two hundred million francs in the cellars of the Tuileries, in Paris, and he would have given them all, to save Ney.

[pause]

The soldiers of the army, when they see Ney and his little band arriving, cheer, and embrace them, and weep for joy. The story of Ney's crossing of the Dnieper becomes one of the great legends of the retreat.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-cheering-embracing-arriving-comrades-snowy-camp-joy}
And Ney goes on, commanding the rear guard, all the way to the end, to the border of Russia, at the river Niemen. And there, it is said, he was the last French soldier to leave Russia, crossing the bridge at Kovno, with a musket in his hand, and turning, at the end, to fire one last shot, at the pursuing Cossacks.

[long-pause]

A few days later, in Gumbinnen, in Prussia, a French general, Mathieu Dumas, was sitting at his breakfast, when a strange man came in: in a brown coat, with a long beard, and red eyes, and a face blackened by smoke, with a musket in his hand. Dumas did not recognize him, and asked who he was.

[pause]

And the man said: I am the rear guard of the Grande Armée. I am Michel Ney.

[pause]

{img:inn-room-prussia-breakfast-table-weary-bearded-man-doorway}
Ney's end, three years later, will be a sad one. But in the winter of 1812, he was the hero of the army.

[long-pause]

{img:berezina-river-banks-marshes-snow-grey-november-1812}
At the end of November, 1812, the army comes to the river Berezina, in what is now Belarus.

[pause]

The Berezina is not a very wide river. But it is cold, and deep, and the banks are marshy, and the bridge across it, at the town of Borisov, has been destroyed by the Russians. And Russian armies are coming, from the north, and the south, and the east. The Grande Armée is trapped.

[long-pause]

The army must cross the river. But how?

[pause]

A few kilometres north of Borisov, at a village called Studienka, a cavalry officer finds a ford, a place where the river is shallow enough to cross. And the engineers decide to build bridges there, quickly, before the Russians arrive.

[pause]

The men who build them are the pontonniers, the bridge builders of the army, about four hundred of them, Dutch and French, under their commander, General Jean-Baptiste Éblé, a quiet, kind, old soldier, of fifty-four, who is greatly loved by his men.

[long-pause]

Éblé had disobeyed an order. Earlier in the retreat, Napoleon had ordered all the bridging equipment to be burned, to save the horses. But Éblé had secretly kept six wagons of tools, and nails, and iron clamps, and two field forges. And now, these save the army.

[pause]

On the twenty-sixth of November, the pontonniers go into the river.

[pause]

{img:bridge-builders-standing-in-cold-water-placing-trestles-dawn-mist}
They wade into the icy water, up to their shoulders, among the floating blocks of ice, and drive the wooden trestles, the legs of the bridges, into the bed of the river, and lay the beams, and the planks, on top, working in shifts, in the freezing water, for hours, through the day and the night. They build two bridges: one for the infantry and the cavalry, and one, stronger, for the cannons and the wagons.

[pause]

Many of them gave their lives, from the cold, in the days that followed. They are among the greatest heroes of the war. Today, at the place where the bridges stood, there is a memorial to them.

[long-pause]

For three days, from the twenty-sixth to the twenty-ninth of November, the army crosses the bridges, while the soldiers of the rear guard, on both banks, hold back the Russians.

[pause]

The bridges break, several times, under the weight, and each time, the pontonniers go back into the water, and mend them.

[pause]

We will not watch the last day of the crossing, when the Russians came close, and the crowds of stragglers, who had waited too long, pressed forward onto the bridges. It is enough to say that it was one of the saddest days of the whole retreat, and that the French word Bérézina, still today, means a terrible disaster.

[long-pause]

On the morning of the twenty-ninth of November, General Éblé, following his orders, set fire to the bridges, so that the Russians could not follow. He waited as long as he could, giving the people on the far bank every chance to cross.

[pause]

But most of the army was across. Napoleon, and the Guard, and Ney, and many thousands of soldiers. And they marched on, west, toward Vilna, and Poland.

[pause]

{img:quiet-river-winter-morning-mist-snow-banks-memorial-calm}
General Éblé, the kind old bridge builder, exhausted by the cold, and his work, passed away a month later, in Königsberg, in Prussia.

[long-pause]

{img:sleigh-night-snow-forest-road-small-escort-lanterns-december}
After the Berezina, in December 1812, the cold becomes even worse.

[pause]

The temperature falls to twenty, thirty, even thirty-five degrees below zero. The air is so cold that it hurts to breathe. The snow is dry, and squeaks under the feet. The trees crack in the frost, with sounds like pistol shots. And the sky, on clear days, is pale and blue, and the sun shines, low, without warmth, on the white land.

[long-pause]

On the fifth of December, at the little town of Smorgoni, Napoleon leaves the army.

[pause]

He has heard that, in Paris, there has been an attempt to overthrow his government, while he was away. And he knows that, after the disaster in Russia, his enemies all over Europe will rise against him. He must get back to Paris, quickly, to raise a new army.

[pause]

{img:emperor-leaving-in-sleigh-officers-saluting-snowy-village-dusk}
So he gives the command of the army to his brother-in-law, Joachim Murat, the King of Naples, and leaves, at night, in a sleigh, with a few companions, and a small escort, travelling in disguise, as the secretary of his friend Caulaincourt, and rides west, through the snow, day and night, across Poland, and Germany, to Paris. He arrives at the Tuileries palace, in Paris, thirteen days later, at midnight, on the eighteenth of December, so muddy and bearded that the guards do not recognize him.

[pause]

On the journey, he talks for hours with Caulaincourt, who writes it all down. And he says, it is reported: from the sublime to the ridiculous, there is only one step.

[long-pause]

The army goes on, without him, to Vilna, where there are great stores of food, and clothes, and shelter. The soldiers arrive there, in the second week of December, frozen and starving. And for a day or two, they eat, and warm themselves, in the houses of the city.

[pause]

But the Russians are close behind. And the army must go on, again, west, to the river Niemen, and the border.

[pause]

In the middle of December, the last survivors of the Grande Armée cross the river Niemen, at Kovno, out of Russia: the same river that they had crossed, so proudly, with their bands playing, in the summer sunshine, six months before.

[pause]

Of the more than six hundred thousand men who had marched into Russia, only a small part came back. Many tens of thousands had been taken prisoner, and some of them, in the end, came home, years later. Many others had stayed behind, in Russia, and made new lives there. But very many were lost.

[long-pause]

It was one of the greatest disasters in the history of war. And it was the beginning of the end for Napoleon.

[pause]

In 1813, all of Europe rose against him, as he had feared: Russia, and Prussia, and Austria, and Sweden, and Britain. He raised a new army, of young conscripts, and fought brilliantly. But at Leipzig, in October 1813, he was defeated, in a great battle. And in 1814, the allied armies, with the Tsar Alexander at their head, entered Paris. And Napoleon gave up his throne, and was sent into exile, on the little island of Elba, in the Mediterranean.

[pause]

{img:allied-sovereigns-entering-paris-1814-spring-crowds-boulevard}
He escaped, a year later, and came back to France, and ruled again, for a hundred days. But in June 1815, at Waterloo, in Belgium, he was defeated, for the last time. And he was sent far away, to the little island of Saint Helena, in the middle of the South Atlantic Ocean, where he passed away, in 1821, at the age of fifty-one.

[long-pause]

Marshal Ney fought for him again, at Waterloo, where he led the great charges of the French cavalry. After Waterloo, the restored king of France had him tried, and condemned, for treason. Ney met his end bravely, in Paris, in December 1815. He refused a blindfold. Today, there is a statue of him, in Paris, near the place where he fell, with his sword raised.

[pause]

And the Tsar, Alexander, who had refused to make peace, became, for a while, the most powerful man in Europe. He passed away, mysteriously, in 1825, in the south of Russia. Some people believed that he did not really die, but became a hermit, in Siberia, and lived there, in secret, as a holy man, for many years.

[pause]

{img:saint-helena-island-cliffs-ocean-lonely-house-clouds-calm}
And Moscow was rebuilt, slowly, more beautiful than before, with new houses, and palaces, and churches, of stone, and became once again the great heart of Russia.

[long-pause]

{img:konigsberg-1812-winter-old-town-churches-snow-river-ice}
In the last days of December, 1812, the survivors of the Grande Armée arrive in Königsberg, in Prussia, on the shore of the Baltic Sea, the old city of the philosopher Immanuel Kant.

[pause]

It is a city of red brick churches, and gabled houses, and a castle, and a cathedral, on an island in the river, and warehouses, along the frozen harbour. And for the soldiers who reach it, it is like coming back to life.

[long-pause]

They come into the city, a few at a time, in little groups, or alone, on foot, or in sledges, ragged, and thin, and frozen, in their strange clothes, their furs and shawls and blankets. The people of Königsberg stare at them, in pity, and astonishment. Some of them do not even recognize them as soldiers.

[pause]

But in Königsberg, there are houses, and warm rooms, and stoves, and beds, and clean clothes, and food: bread, and soup, and meat, and beer. There are hospitals, where the sick and the frostbitten can be cared for. And there is rest.

[pause]

{img:warm-room-tiled-stove-soldiers-sleeping-beds-clean-quiet}
Many of the soldiers, writing later, describe the joy of their first night in a warm room, in a bed, after six months of sleeping on the ground. Of washing, in hot water, for the first time in months. Of eating a real meal, at a table, with a knife and fork. Of taking off their boots. Of sleeping, and sleeping, for a day and a night, without waking.

[long-pause]

And it is Christmas.

[pause]

In the churches of Königsberg, the bells ring, and the candles are lit, and the people sing the old Christmas hymns, in German. In the houses, there are little trees, decorated with candles, and apples, and nuts, a German custom which is just beginning to spread. And some of the families of Königsberg invite the poor soldiers, French and German and Italian and Dutch and Polish, into their homes, to share their Christmas dinner, by the warm stove.

[pause]

{img:german-family-christmas-candles-tree-inviting-ragged-soldier-table}
For many of the soldiers, it is the most moving Christmas of their lives. They are alive. They are warm. They are fed. And, in a few weeks, or months, they will be home.

[pause]

But many of them think, too, of their friends, who did not come back, and who are lying, quietly, under the snow, along the long road from Moscow. And they raise their glasses to them, in silence.

[long-pause]

{img:old-veteran-writing-memoirs-desk-candle-medals-1830s}
In the years after 1812, the survivors of the Russian campaign went home, to their villages, and their towns, all over Europe: to France, and Germany, and Italy, and Poland, and Holland, and Switzerland.

[pause]

Many of them never spoke about what they had seen. Others told their stories, again and again, to their families, and their friends, by the fire, on long winter evenings, for the rest of their lives. And some of them wrote them down.

[long-pause]

Sergeant Bourgogne came home, after years as a prisoner and a soldier, and lived a long life, and became an officer, and in his old age, wrote his memoirs, which were published in 1898, long after his death. They are full of life, and humour, and horror, and kindness, and are still read today.

[pause]

Jakob Walter wrote his little book, in simple German, for his family, and it was found, many years later, in America, by his descendants, and published.

[pause]

Many of the Dutch pontonniers, who built the bridges at the Berezina, never came home. But in the Netherlands, they are still remembered. And in Switzerland, there is a famous old song, the Berezina song, which the Swiss soldiers sang on the morning of the crossing, about life as a journey through the night, and which is still sung today.

[pause]

Dominique Larrey, the great surgeon, came home too, and wrote his memoirs, and went on caring for soldiers, at Waterloo, and afterwards. Napoleon, at Saint Helena, called him the most virtuous man he had ever known. The Duke of Wellington, at Waterloo, it is said, saw him working among the wounded, under fire, and ordered his gunners not to fire in his direction, and raised his hat to salute him.

[long-pause]

And the story became a legend, told in books, and paintings, and music.

[pause]

Leo Tolstoy, who was born in 1828, sixteen years after the campaign, and grew up hearing stories of it from the old people of his family, and the old soldiers, wrote War and Peace, in the 1860s, one of the greatest novels ever written, about the war of 1812, and the lives of Russian families, and soldiers, and the great battle of Borodino, and the burning of Moscow, and the retreat of the French.

[pause]

{img:tolstoy-writing-study-estate-window-snow-birches-quiet}
In 1880, the Russian composer Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky wrote a piece of music, the 1812 Overture, to celebrate the Russian victory, with the French national anthem, the Marseillaise, at the beginning, slowly drowned out by Russian hymns, and the bells of Moscow, and cannons. It is still played, at concerts, and celebrations, all over the world, sometimes with real cannons.

[long-pause]

And in 1869, a retired French engineer, Charles Joseph Minard, made a famous map, or chart, of the campaign.

[pause]

It shows the army as a broad band, beginning wide, at the river Niemen, in the summer, with the number of soldiers written on it, and becoming narrower, and narrower, as it moves east, to Moscow. And then a thin black band, coming back, west, through the winter, becoming thinner still, with the temperature, below, day by day, falling, as the band shrinks.

[pause]

{img:old-chart-map-band-narrowing-army-route-paper-desk-lamp}
Many people say that it is the best chart ever drawn: a whole tragic story, told in a single picture, without words.

[long-pause]

But perhaps the most important stories are the small ones: of the soldiers who shared their last piece of bread, and the cantinières who gave their cloaks, and the peasants who opened their doors, and the friends who carried each other, through the snow, and the old men, many years later, by the fire, who remembered them.

[long-pause]

{img:borodino-field-today-monuments-meadows-birch-autumn}
Today, more than two hundred years later, you can still follow the road of 1812, from the river Niemen to Moscow, and back.

[pause]

At Kaunas, in Lithuania, which was once Kovno, the river Niemen still flows past the old town, wide and calm, between green hills. On a hill above the river, near the place where the army crossed, there is a little monument. And in the summer, the river is full of swimmers, and boats.

[long-pause]

In Vilnius, the capital of Lithuania, the old town, with its baroque churches, white and pink and yellow, is a World Heritage Site, and one of the most beautiful old towns in Europe. In 2001, when workers were building new houses on the edge of the city, they found the resting place of many soldiers of the Grande Armée, who had passed away in Vilna, in the winter of 1812. The scientists studied them carefully, and learned a great deal about the soldiers: their ages, and their health, and where they came from. And then they were buried again, with honour, in a cemetery in the city, in 2003, with soldiers of many nations present.

[pause]

{img:vilnius-old-town-baroque-churches-snow-quiet-evening}
At the Berezina, near the village of Studienka, in Belarus, there are memorials, on the riverbank, at the place where the bridges stood. The river flows quietly past, between marshy meadows and woods. People come to lay flowers there, from France, and Holland, and Switzerland, and Russia.

[long-pause]

At Borodino, west of Moscow, the battlefield is a great open-air museum, a peaceful landscape of fields, and woods, and birch trees, and little villages, with dozens of monuments, to the regiments of both sides, scattered across it, and a museum, and a monastery, built by the widow of a Russian general who fell in the battle, at the place where he fell.

[pause]

Every year, on the first Sunday of September, thousands of people come to Borodino, and some of them, dressed in uniforms of 1812, French and Russian, with muskets, and horses, and cannons, act out the battle, as a festival of memory, with crowds watching from the hillsides.

[pause]

In Moscow itself, there is a great round building, a panorama, which contains an enormous circular painting of the battle of Borodino, a hundred and fifteen metres long, painted in 1912, for the hundredth anniversary, so that visitors stand in the middle, and see the battle all around them.

[long-pause]

And in Moscow, there is the great Cathedral of Christ the Saviour, with its golden dome, by the river, which was built as a thanksgiving for the victory of 1812. It was destroyed in 1931, by the Soviet government, and rebuilt, exactly as it was, in the 1990s.

[pause]

The Kremlin still stands, on its hill above the river, with its red walls, and its towers, and its golden domes. In one of its buildings, there is a collection of French cannons, captured in 1812, lined up along the walls.

[pause]

{img:moscow-kremlin-red-walls-golden-domes-river-winter-evening}
And in Paris, Napoleon rests, in a great tomb of red stone, under the golden dome of the Invalides, a home for old soldiers, which he loved. And around him, carved on the walls, are the names of his victories. And in the city, the great Arc de Triomphe, which he ordered to be built, bears the names of his battles, and his generals, including Borodino, which the French call the battle of the Moskova, and Ney, and Éblé.

[long-pause]

So the long road of 1812 comes to an end tonight, with the snow falling softly on the quiet fields of Borodino, and the river Niemen flowing calmly past the place where the great army crossed in the summer sunshine.

[pause]

The young soldiers and the old grumblers, the women with their little carts, the bridge builders in the icy river, and the friends who shared their last bread by a small fire in the snow.
