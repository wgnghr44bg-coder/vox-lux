Fort Bridger is not really a fort. It is a small group of log cabins, surrounded by a log fence, standing in a green valley where several mountain streams come together.

It was built three years ago by Jim Bridger, one of the best-known of the old fur trappers. He has spent more than twenty years in these mountains, and he knows them better than almost anyone alive.

He is in his early forties now. He cannot read or write, but he can draw a map of the whole West in the dust with a stick, and tell you where every river goes.

--

At Fort Bridger, the travellers can rest their animals for a few days on the good grass by the streams. The blacksmith repairs their wagons. They can trade their worn-out oxen for fresh ones, at a price.

The Shoshone people come here to trade too, and their lodges stand along the creeks near the fort, with horses grazing all around them.

It is a pleasant, quiet place. Many of the travellers stay longer than they meant to.

--

{img:two-roads-wagons-dividing}
It is here, or a little before, that the companies going to California and the companies going to Oregon say goodbye to each other.

For more than a thousand miles they have travelled the same road. They have camped side by side, helped each other across rivers, shared their supper and their music.

Now the Oregon families will turn north, toward Fort Hall and the Snake River. The California families will go on toward the southwest.

There are many farewells. People who were strangers in Missouri have become close friends, and they know they will probably never see each other again.

--

This summer, there is a new idea being talked about around the fires.

A man named Lansford Hastings has written a book, praising California, and he has described a new shortcut. Instead of going the long way north and around, he says, the wagons could go straight west from Fort Bridger, through the mountains and across the great salt desert, and save several hundred miles.

Hastings himself has ridden the route on horseback. But no wagons have ever travelled it.

--

{img:reed-donner-wagons-turning}
At Fort Bridger, at the end of July, the Reed and Donner families have to decide which way to go.

Some people on the trail have warned them against the new route. But they are tired, and the shortcut sounds good, and they are told that Hastings himself will guide them.

They choose the shortcut.

On the last day of July, their wagons roll out of Fort Bridger toward the southwest, into the Wasatch Mountains. Their journey will be much longer and harder than they expect, and we will hear a little more about it later.

--

{img:bear-river-valley-wagons}
The Oregon families, meanwhile, turn northwest.

The trail leads them over a range of hills and down into the valley of the Bear River, one of the loveliest places on the whole journey.

The valley is wide and green, with tall grass up to the oxen's shoulders, and clear streams running down from the hills, full of trout. There are wild currants and gooseberries along the creeks, and the women and children pick them by the bucketful.

For a few days, the travelling is easy, and everyone is cheerful.

--

{img:soda-springs-bubbling}
Farther along the Bear River, the travellers come to a remarkable place.

Here, the ground is full of springs, and the water that bubbles out of them is fizzy, like soda water. Some springs are cool and taste a little of iron. Others are warm.

The travellers call the place Soda Springs. They mix the water with a little sugar and a few drops of vinegar, and drink it like lemonade.

One spring, close beside the river, makes a strange, regular puffing sound, like a small steam engine. They call it Steamboat Spring.

The children love it here. They sit by the springs and drink until they feel quite sick, and then they go and drink some more.
