For the families who have arrived in the valley, the first thing to do is to find land.

There is no government office here yet to hand it out. Instead, the settlers have made their own rules. A married couple may claim six hundred and forty acres, a whole square mile of land, as long as they live on it and farm it.

So the men ride out across the valley, looking for a good place. They want a stream for water, a stand of trees for timber and firewood, and a stretch of open prairie for the plough.

When they find it, they mark the corners with piles of stones or blazes cut into the trees, and write a short description in the claims book at Oregon City.

--

{img:building-log-cabin-autumn}
Then they build a cabin.

The neighbours come to help, as they do back home. The trees are cut and trimmed, notched at the ends, and laid one on top of another, until the walls are high enough. The gaps between the logs are filled with mud and moss.

The roof is made of split cedar boards. There is a chimney of sticks and clay at one end, and a door made of planks, hung on wooden hinges.

There is usually only one room, with a dirt floor, and one small window covered with oiled paper.

But it is dry, and it is warm, and after five months of sleeping in a wagon, it feels like a palace.

--

{img:winter-rain-cabin-fireside}
The first winter is wet.

In the Willamette Valley, it does not snow very much, but it rains, softly and steadily, for weeks on end. The clouds lie low over the hills, and the creeks run full.

The families spend the dark evenings by the fire, mending clothes and making furniture, a table, a bench, a bedstead with a mattress stuffed with straw or dry grass.

Food is short in this first winter, because there was no time to plant a crop. They live on wheat bought from the older settlers, on salmon and venison, on potatoes and peas, and on whatever is left in the wagon.

--

During that winter, a piece of news arrives that the settlers have been waiting for.

Back in June, far away in Washington and London, the governments of the United States and Britain have signed a treaty. The border between them has been drawn along the forty-ninth parallel, from the Rocky Mountains to the sea.

Everything south of that line, including the Willamette Valley, now belongs to the United States.

The news has travelled very slowly. But when it arrives, the settlers are delighted. The country they walked to is now, officially, part of their own.

--

{img:snowy-sierra-mountains-distance}
Far to the south, in the high mountains of California, the Reed and Donner families are spending the same winter in a very different way.

The shortcut through the mountains turned out to be much slower than they had been told. They had to cut their own road through thick forest, and cross a great salt desert, and they lost many weeks.

By the time they reached the last mountains before California, at the end of October, it was too late. The snow came early that year, and it closed the pass in front of them.

They were trapped there for the whole winter, in cabins and tents beside a frozen lake, while rescue parties struggled to reach them from the valley below.

--

It was a very hard winter, and many of them did not survive it. It became one of the saddest stories of the whole western trail.

But some did come through. In the spring, Virginia Reed, who had ridden her pony so happily beside the wagons in Missouri, reached the warm valleys of California with her family.

Not long afterwards, she wrote a long letter to her cousin back home, telling the whole story. At the end of it, she gave some simple advice for anyone thinking of making the journey.

Never take a cutoff, she wrote, and hurry along as fast as you can.

--

{img:spring-ploughing-new-farm}
In the Willamette Valley, the spring comes early, and it comes softly.

By February, the grass is green again. By March, the first wildflowers are blooming in the meadows, and the oak trees are putting out their leaves.

The men hitch up the same oxen that pulled the wagons across the continent, and plough the first furrows in the deep, dark soil. The women plant gardens beside the cabins, with seeds they carried all the way from home, wrapped in little paper packets.

Peas, beans, onions, cabbages. And, in many gardens, a few small apple trees, grown from seeds or cuttings brought over the mountains.

--

{img:summer-wheat-harvest-valley}
That summer, the first harvest is good.

The wheat stands tall and golden. The gardens are full. The cattle are fat again on the valley grass.

In the evenings, the families sit outside their cabins and look out over the fields, toward the blue hills to the west, and the white cone of Mount Hood far to the east, catching the last of the sunlight.

And somewhere back along the trail, two thousand miles away, a new year's wagons are already rolling west across the prairie.
