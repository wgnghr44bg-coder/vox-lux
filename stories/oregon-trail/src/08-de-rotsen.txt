West of the place where the Platte divides into two rivers, the country begins to change.

The wagons follow the North Platte now. The low green hills give way to drier land, with sand and sagebrush and pale rocks, and the grass grows shorter and greyer.

And then, one day, the first of the great landmarks appears on the horizon.

--

It looks, from a long way off, like a large building standing alone on the plain. Some say it looks like a courthouse, and so it has been given the name Courthouse Rock.

It is a huge mass of clay and sandstone, rising out of the flat land, and it seems to be much closer than it really is. In the clear dry air of the plains, distances are hard to judge.

Some of the young men decide to walk over to it after supper. They walk for hours, and it never seems to come any closer, and they arrive back in camp long after dark, tired and laughing.

--

{img:chimney-rock-evening}
A day or two farther on, there is something even stranger.

A tall, thin spire of rock rises straight up from a cone-shaped hill, pointing at the sky like a finger. It can be seen for days before the wagons reach it, and for days after they pass it.

The travellers call it Chimney Rock, and nearly every one of them mentions it in their diary. Some draw little pictures of it in the margins.

It is made of soft stone, worn away by wind and rain over thousands of years. Some people climb up the base of it, and carve their names into the rock with a knife, beside hundreds of other names from the years before.

--

{img:scotts-bluff-wagons-below}
Beyond Chimney Rock, the trail comes to a long line of high bluffs, cut into towers and walls by the weather, like the ruins of some great old city.

This is Scotts Bluff, named after a fur trader who died near here many years ago.

In 1846, the wagons do not go through the gap in the bluffs, as later travellers will. They go around them, through a valley a little to the south, where there is a spring of cold, clear water and a few cedar trees.

In the evening light, the bluffs turn gold and red, and then purple, and the swallows fly in and out of their nests in the cliffs.

--

{img:fort-laramie-adobe-walls}
About a week later, the wagons come to Fort Laramie.

It is not a military fort, at least not yet. It is a trading post, built by the American Fur Company, a square of high adobe walls standing on a low rise above the Laramie River, near the place where it joins the North Platte.

Inside the walls there are storerooms and living quarters, a courtyard, and a pair of watch towers at the corners.

For the travellers, Fort Laramie is the first sign of anything like a town for more than a month. Here they can buy a few supplies, at very high prices, have their wagons repaired, and leave letters to be carried back east.

--

Francis Parkman reaches Fort Laramie in the middle of June, and stays for a while.

He describes the fort as a busy, cheerful place. The traders and their families live inside the walls, and around it, on the plain, stand the lodges of the Lakota people, who have come here to trade buffalo robes for cloth, beads, iron kettles and knives.

Later in the summer, Parkman goes to live for several weeks with a band of the Oglala Lakota, travelling with them and writing down everything he sees.

--

{img:lakota-lodges-near-fort}
The emigrant families watch the Lakota camps with great curiosity, and the Lakota watch them in the same way.

Sometimes they visit each other. The travellers trade flour and sugar and cloth for moccasins and buffalo robes. Children look at each other shyly across the grass, and sometimes they play together.

For most of the travellers, this is the first time they have ever met the people who live on the plains. Their diaries are full of notes about the painted lodges, the horses, the beadwork and the clothes.

--

The wagons rest near Fort Laramie for a day or two. The oxen graze. The women wash clothes in the clear water of the Laramie River.

Behind them lie six hundred and fifty miles of plains.

Ahead of them, on the western horizon, a dark line of hills is rising. And beyond those hills are the Rocky Mountains.
