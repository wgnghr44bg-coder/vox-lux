In the years that followed, the trail grew busier and busier.

In 1846, perhaps a thousand or so people made the journey to Oregon. A few years later, when gold was found in California, tens of thousands came along the same road every summer.

Over about twenty-five years, something like three or four hundred thousand people walked west along the Oregon Trail and the trails that branched from it.

--

Fort Laramie was bought by the army in 1849 and became a real military fort. Fort Kearny was built on the Platte. Ferries were built at the river crossings, and bridges, and trading posts sprang up along the road.

Francis Parkman went home to Boston at the end of 1846, ill and exhausted, but full of what he had seen. His account of the journey was published as a book, which he called The Oregon Trail. It is still in print today.

He went on to become one of the most famous historians in America, writing many books about the early history of the continent.

--

{img:railroad-across-plains}
Then, in 1869, the railroad was finished across the continent.

Now, a family could travel from the Missouri River to the Pacific coast in about a week, sitting comfortably in a railway carriage, looking out of the window at the plains and the mountains.

Slowly, the wagons stopped coming. The trail grew quiet. The grass grew back over the road in many places.

But not everywhere.

--

{img:deep-wagon-ruts-sandstone}
Near the town of Guernsey, in Wyoming, not far from Fort Laramie, the trail crosses a ridge of soft sandstone.

Here, the iron wheels of tens of thousands of wagons wore a deep groove into the rock, in some places almost as deep as a person is tall.

The ruts are still there today. Visitors can walk along the bottom of them, between the walls of pale stone, and put their hands on the marks left by the wheels.

In many other places too, across the plains and the mountains, the two faint lines of the old wagon road can still be seen, running through the sagebrush, if you know where to look.

--

{img:independence-rock-today}
Independence Rock is still there, beside the Sweetwater River, and many of the old names are still on it, though the wind and the weather have worn some of them away.

Chimney Rock still stands, a little shorter than it was. Scotts Bluff is a national monument. The walls of Fort Laramie have been restored, and visitors can walk through its rooms.

At South Pass, the wide, empty plateau looks almost exactly as it did in 1846. There are no towns there even now, only the wind, the sage, and the far-off snowy mountains.

--

{img:oregon-farm-valley-today}
And in the Willamette Valley, the farms are still there.

Many of the families who live there today are descended from the people who came over the trail in those years. Some of them still own the same land that their great-great-grandparents claimed, and some still have a chest, or a clock, or a quilt that travelled across the continent in a covered wagon.

In Forest Grove, the college that grew out of Tabitha Brown's little school still holds its classes. And on the Barlow Road, high on the slopes of Mount Hood, it is still possible to walk part of the old route through the forest.

--

{img:wagon-sunset-prairie-slot}
The journey along the Oregon Trail took about five months. It was long and slow and often very hard.

But for most of the people who made it, it was simply a part of life. They walked, and cooked, and washed, and sang around the fires. They watched the land change slowly around them, day after day, from the green prairie to the mountains to the forests of the Pacific.

And when they reached the end, they built a home, and planted a garden, and began again.

--

That is where we will leave them tonight, in a green valley in Oregon, under the soft grey rain, with the smoke rising from the chimneys.
