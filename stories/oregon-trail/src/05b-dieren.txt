For five months, the travellers live closer to their animals than they ever have before.

They walk beside them all day, and sleep near them all night. They know the sound of each one's breathing, the way each one walks, and which one is likely to wander off in the morning.

--

Every ox has a name. Back home, a farmer might not have bothered, but on the trail, the oxen are called by name a hundred times a day, and they learn to answer.

There are oxen called Buck and Bright, which are the most common names of all. There are oxen called Duke and Dime, Tom and Jerry, Lion and Brindle, Old Ball and Star.

Each ox has its own place in the team. The strongest and steadiest pair is yoked nearest the wagon, where the hardest work is done. The cleverest pair goes in front, where they must follow the trail and listen for the commands.

--

The oxen are patient animals, but they have their own opinions.

On hot afternoons, if the trail passes near a river, the whole team may decide, all at once, that it is time for a drink, and turn off the road toward the water, wagon and all, no matter what the driver says.

In the evenings, after they are unyoked, they lie down in the grass with long sighs, and chew, and look at the sunset.

Many of the travellers write about their oxen with great affection. When one of them grows too weak to go on, and has to be left behind at a fort or a good meadow, the children cry.

--

{img:family-cow-tied-behind-wagon}
Most families also bring a cow or two, walking at the back of the wagon on a rope, or with the loose herd.

The cow is precious. She gives milk every morning and evening, for the children, and for the coffee, and for the butter that churns itself in the jolting wagon.

On the long dry stretches, when the grass is poor, the milk grows less and less. But the cow keeps walking, all the way across the continent, and at the end of the journey, she will be the start of a new herd in Oregon.

Some families bring chickens too, in wooden coops tied to the side of the wagon. The hens complain loudly about the road, but they go on laying eggs.

--

{img:dog-walking-beside-wagon}
And nearly every company has dogs.

There are farm dogs and hunting dogs, big dogs and small dogs, and they run alongside the wagons all day, exploring the grass, chasing rabbits and barking at the cattle.

At night, they sleep under the wagons, and they are the first to hear anything moving in the darkness.

Some companies make rules about the dogs, because there are so many of them and they bark so much. But the children will not hear of leaving them behind.

James Reed's family has five dogs, and the children love them all. The smallest one is called Cash.

--

{img:prairie-dog-town-children}
On the plains, there are animals that none of the travellers have ever seen before.

The children are fascinated by the prairie dogs. These are not really dogs at all, but small brown animals, a little like squirrels, which live together in great colonies, with thousands of burrows dug into the ground.

When the wagons pass, the prairie dogs sit up on the little mounds outside their holes, watching. They give a sharp little bark, flick their tails, and then dive down out of sight, and a moment later pop up again somewhere else.

The colonies are called towns, and some of them stretch for miles.

--

There are antelope too, the swiftest animals on the plains. They are tan and white, with short black horns, and they stand in small groups far away, watching the wagons with great curiosity.

The hunters say that if you lie down in the grass and wave a handkerchief tied to a stick, the antelope will come closer and closer to see what it is.

There are jackrabbits with enormous ears. There are hawks circling high above, and flocks of little birds rising out of the grass. There are wolves too, mostly heard rather than seen, singing far away in the night.

--

{img:horses-grazing-evening-camp}
Some of the men have horses, and they ride ahead of the wagons, scouting the road, looking for water and grass, or going out to hunt.

A good horse on the trail is a great treasure. It needs better grass than an ox, and more care, and in the evenings its owner rubs it down and checks its feet, and talks to it quietly while it eats.

Francis Parkman has a horse he calls Pontiac, a strong and lively animal, and later, a gentle mare he calls Pauline, whom he describes as the best horse he ever had.

In the evening light, the horses graze at the edge of the camp, swishing their tails, their coats shining gold in the last of the sun.
