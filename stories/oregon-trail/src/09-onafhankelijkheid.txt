Beyond Fort Laramie, the trail climbs slowly into higher, drier country.

The grass grows thin. There is sagebrush everywhere now, grey-green and fragrant, and the wagon wheels crush it, so that the whole company travels in a cloud of its sharp, clean smell.

The days are hot, and the nights are suddenly cold. In the mornings there is sometimes a thin skin of ice on the water buckets, even in the middle of summer.

--

This is the hardest stretch for the animals. The water in some of the springs is bitter with alkali, and the oxen must be kept away from it. The grass is poor, and the road is rough and stony.

Some families begin to lighten their wagons. They leave a heavy chest of drawers beside the trail, or a cast-iron stove, or a barrel of something they brought from home and never used.

The road is lined with these things now. Later travellers will walk past them and wonder whose they were.

--

{img:north-platte-crossing-ferry}
Near the place where the trail finally leaves the North Platte, the wagons must cross the river.

Here it is deep and fast, and in 1846 there is no proper ferry. The men build rafts from logs, or they take the wheels off a wagon, caulk its bed with pitch, and float it across like a boat.

It takes a long time, and everyone is careful. The oxen swim across with the men beside them, and on the far bank, the wagons are put back together, one by one.

Then the trail turns away from the river, and climbs over a stretch of dry, empty land toward the Sweetwater.

--

{img:independence-rock-wagons}
And then, rising out of the plain beside the Sweetwater River, there is a great rounded rock, grey and smooth, like an enormous turtle lying asleep in the grass.

This is Independence Rock.

It got its name many years ago, from a group of fur trappers who camped here on the Fourth of July. And the travellers have a saying about it. If you reach Independence Rock by the Fourth of July, you will reach Oregon before the snow.

So the companies try hard to arrive here by then.

--

{img:names-carved-on-rock}
Independence Rock is covered with names.

Thousands of travellers have climbed onto it, and painted or carved their names and the date into the stone, with a knife, or a chisel, or with axle grease mixed with gunpowder.

Some of the names are big and carefully made. Others are just initials, scratched quickly before the wagons moved on.

The children love to climb it. From the top, they can see the whole valley of the Sweetwater, green along the river and brown and gold beyond, and the white wagon covers of their own company far below them, small as toys.

--

{img:fourth-of-july-celebration}
If a company is at Independence Rock on the Fourth of July, there is a celebration.

The men fire their rifles into the air, a flag is raised on a wagon pole, and someone makes a speech about America, and about the new country in the west that will one day be part of it.

There is a special supper, with whatever can be found: fresh antelope, a few dried peaches, perhaps a cake made with the last of the sugar. A bottle that has been carefully saved for two months is opened and shared.

In the evening, there is music and dancing in the circle of wagons, and the young people stay up late.

--

{img:devils-gate-sweetwater}
A few miles beyond Independence Rock, the Sweetwater River does something surprising.

Instead of going around a ridge of granite, it cuts straight through it, in a narrow gorge with walls three hundred feet high. The gap is called Devil's Gate.

The wagons cannot go through it. The trail goes around, through a low pass just to the south. But many of the travellers walk over to look at it, and stand at the edge of the gorge, watching the clear water rushing between the rocks far below.

--

From here, the trail follows the Sweetwater River westward, crossing it again and again, for more than a week.

The water is cold and clear and good, the best water since Missouri. The valley is green and narrow, and on both sides the hills rise higher and higher.

And far ahead, to the northwest, on the clearest mornings, there is a line of white along the horizon. It is snow, lying on the high peaks of the Wind River Mountains.
