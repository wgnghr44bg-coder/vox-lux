Not everyone reaches the valley so easily this year.

Tabitha Brown, the sixty-six-year-old widow from Massachusetts, and her family are travelling with a group of wagons that left the main trail at Fort Hall, to try another new route.

--

That summer, a few men from the Willamette Valley, led by two brothers named Jesse and Lindsay Applegate, have ridden east to look for a southern road into Oregon. They hope it will avoid the dangerous river journey down the Columbia.

They found a way, of sorts. It leads southwest from the Snake River, across the deserts of what is now Nevada, through the mountains, and then north, up the long valleys of southern Oregon, into the Willamette Valley from the south.

They meet the wagons at Fort Hall, and some of the families decide to follow them.

--

{img:desert-wagons-dusty-autumn}
The new road is much longer and harder than anyone expected.

The wagons cross long stretches of desert, with very little water and grass. The oxen grow weak. In the mountains of southern Oregon, there are rivers to cross and steep canyons to climb through, with no proper road at all.

The autumn comes. And then, in the forests, the rain begins.

It rains day after day. The road turns into deep mud. The wagons sink up to their axles, and some of them have to be left behind.

--

Mrs Brown later wrote a long letter to her family back east, describing this part of the journey. She wrote it calmly, the way she did everything.

She tells how her wagon had to be abandoned, and how she rode on horseback through the forests, with her old brother-in-law, Captain Brown, beside her.

One evening, the two of them fell behind the others. Night came, in the rain, in the forest, and they were alone.

--

{img:night-in-rain-forest-fire}
The old captain was very tired, and not entirely clear in his mind. So Mrs Brown took charge.

She found a sheltered place under the trees. She got down from her horse and helped him down from his. She made a little shelter with a saddle blanket, and wrapped him up as warmly as she could.

Then she sat beside him through the night, in the dark and the rain, listening to the drops falling from the trees, and waited for the morning.

In her letter, she wrote that she was not afraid. She simply waited, and thought, and prayed.

--

{img:morning-forest-reunion}
At first light, she got the captain back onto his horse, and they rode on along the trail.

After a while, they smelled wood smoke. Then they heard voices, and came around a bend, and there was the camp, with the rest of the family, and the fires burning.

They had been only a short way behind all along.

Everyone was very glad to see them. And the next morning, the slow journey went on again, through the rain and the mud, toward the north.

--

{img:salem-cabin-christmas-rain}
Tabitha Brown reached the Willamette Valley on Christmas Day, 1846, at a little settlement called Salem.

She had been travelling for nearly nine months. She was sixty-six years old, and had almost nothing left. Her wagon and most of her belongings were gone.

A Methodist minister's family took her in, and gave her a warm place by their fire.

--

{img:picayune-coin-in-glove}
In her letter, she tells a little story about that first winter.

One day, she put her hand into the finger of an old glove, and found a single small coin. It was a picayune, worth about six and a quarter cents. It was all the money she had in the world.

She used it to buy three needles. She traded some of her old clothes for some cloth, and began to make gloves, and sell them to the settlers, and slowly, she began to earn a living again.

By the spring, she had earned enough to look after herself again, and she was very proud of it.

--

But she was not finished.

In the town of Forest Grove, a little to the north, she saw that there were many children whose parents had died on the trail, or who had no one to look after them. So she took them in.

She gave them a home, and cooked for them, and taught them to read and write, the way she had taught school as a young widow back east.

Her little home for orphans grew into a school. And the school grew into a college, which is still there today, more than a hundred and seventy years later.

In Oregon today, Tabitha Brown is remembered as the mother of Oregon.
