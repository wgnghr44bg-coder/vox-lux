Until this year, there has been only one way to go on from The Dalles. And that is by water.

The families take their wagons apart. They build rafts out of logs, or they hire canoes and boats from the local people or the Hudson's Bay Company, and they load everything they own onto them.

Then they drift down the Columbia River, through the gorge, into the heart of the Cascade Mountains.

--

It is one of the most beautiful parts of the whole journey. The river runs between enormous cliffs, covered with dark green forest, with waterfalls pouring down them from hundreds of feet above.

Mist hangs over the water in the mornings. The air is soft and damp, and smells of moss and rain, quite different from the dry plains.

But it is also cold and slow and expensive, and the river has rapids that must be carried around. And the cattle cannot travel on the rafts. They have to be driven along a narrow trail over the mountains beside the river.

--

{img:sam-barlow-forest-road}
Last year, a farmer named Samuel Barlow decided that there must be a better way.

He was in his fifties, a stubborn and practical man from Kentucky, and when he reached The Dalles in the autumn of 1845, he said that God never made a mountain without a way over it, and he set off south, around the side of Mount Hood, with a few wagons, to look for one.

He did not quite make it before the snow came. The families had to leave their wagons in the mountains for the winter and walk out.

But now, in the summer of 1846, Barlow has come back with his men. They have cut a narrow road through the forest, around the southern side of Mount Hood, and down the other side into the Willamette Valley. It is the first wagon road over the Cascades.

He charges five dollars a wagon to use it, and ten cents for each animal.

--

{img:mount-hood-above-forest}
So this year, for the first time, many families do not have to leave their wagons at The Dalles. They pay their toll at a little gate in the forest and drive on.

The road climbs through tall forests of fir and cedar. The trees are enormous, far bigger than any the travellers have ever seen, with trunks so wide that three people holding hands cannot reach around them.

Above them, through gaps in the forest, stands Mount Hood, white and perfect, with its snow shining in the sun.

At night, they camp in small meadows high in the mountains, with the stars above them and the great dark forest all around.

--

{img:laurel-hill-steep-descent}
The worst part of the Barlow Road comes near the end.

At a place called Laurel Hill, the road goes down a slope so steep that the wagons cannot simply be driven. The men tie ropes around them, wrap the ropes around the trunks of trees, and let the wagons down slowly, a little at a time.

Some families cut down a whole tree and tie it to the back of the wagon, so that it drags behind and acts as a brake.

It takes all day for a company to get down the hill. But at the bottom, the worst is over.

--

{img:willamette-valley-first-view}
And then, one day in the autumn, the forest opens up, and the travellers come down into the valley they have been walking toward since April.

The Willamette Valley lies in front of them, wide and green, with a broad river winding through it. There are oak trees standing in the grassy meadows, and forests of fir along the hills. Here and there, smoke rises from the chimney of a log cabin.

It is raining softly. The clouds hang low over the hills.

For a long moment, the families simply stand and look at it.

--

{img:oregon-city-falls-cabins}
At the northern end of the valley, beside a great waterfall on the Willamette River, there is a little town called Oregon City.

It has perhaps a few hundred people, a few streets of wooden houses, two churches, some stores and a sawmill and a flour mill beside the falls. There is even a newspaper, which started printing this year.

To the travellers, after five months on the trail, it looks like a city.

They have walked two thousand miles. They have crossed the plains, the mountains and the deserts. Their clothes are worn out, their wagons are broken, and their oxen are thin.

But they are here.
