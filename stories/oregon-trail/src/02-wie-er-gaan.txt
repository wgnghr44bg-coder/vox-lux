Why would a family sell their farm, load everything they own onto a wagon, and walk two thousand miles into country they have never seen?

The reasons are different for every family, but many of them begin in the same way, with a letter.

--

Someone's cousin, or an old neighbour, went west a few years earlier. Now a letter has arrived, passed from hand to hand across the country, and it is read aloud at the kitchen table in the evening.

The letter talks about a place called the Willamette Valley. The soil there is deep and black, it says. The winters are mild, and the cattle can graze outside all year long. The rivers are full of salmon, and the forests go on for ever.

And there is land. A great deal of land, free to anyone who will go there and farm it.

--

Back home in the Mississippi valley, the years have not been easy. Prices for corn and pork have been low. Many families are in debt. Every summer, the fevers come, and the children fall sick with the ague.

So the letter is read again, and then again. People begin to talk about Oregon at church, at the mill, at the store. Newspapers print long articles about it.

Some people call it Oregon fever.

--

At this moment, Oregon does not quite belong to anyone.

For many years, Britain and the United States have agreed to share the country between them. The British Hudson's Bay Company has its trading posts there, and a small number of American settlers have built farms in the valleys.

In the spring of 1846, the two governments are still talking about where the border should be. That summer they will agree on it, but the families leaving Independence do not know that yet.

--

{img:tabitha-brown-elderly-woman}
Among the people setting out this year is a woman named Tabitha Brown.

She is sixty-six years old, small and slight, and she walks with a cane. She was born in Massachusetts, the daughter of a doctor, and she married a minister who died many years ago, leaving her with three young children.

She raised them by teaching school, and now her children are grown. One of her sons went to Oregon two years ago, and he has come back east to fetch the rest of the family.

Everyone tells her she is too old for a journey like this. She goes anyway.

With her travels her husband's brother, Captain John Brown, who is seventy-seven and was once a sea captain. Mrs Brown has her own wagon, and a young man to drive it.

--

{img:young-parkman-st-louis-levee}
Another traveller this spring is not an emigrant at all.

His name is Francis Parkman. He is twenty-two years old, from a wealthy family in Boston, and he has just finished studying law at Harvard.

What he really loves, though, is history, and the forests and the open country. He wants to see the West while it is still wild, and to meet the Native American peoples of the plains, because he hopes to write about them one day.

With his cousin, Quincy Adams Shaw, he has come by steamboat from St. Louis. They have hired a guide called Henry Chatillon, a quiet, skilled hunter who has spent many years on the plains.

Parkman keeps a notebook in his pocket, and every evening he writes down what he has seen.

--

{img:reed-family-wagon-illinois}
From Springfield, in Illinois, two large farming families are on the road as well.

They are the family of James Reed, a businessman who runs a furniture factory, and the families of the brothers George and Jacob Donner.

James Reed has had a special wagon built for this journey. It has two decks, with a little stove inside, a set of beds, and a ladder up to a sleeping loft. His wife Margret is not well, and he wants her to travel in comfort.

His stepdaughter Virginia is nearly thirteen. She has a pony of her own, and she rides it alongside the wagons, often far ahead of them.

Their journey will turn out to be a long and difficult one, and we will come back to it later, gently and from a distance.

--

{img:emigrant-families-gathering}
But most of the people on the trail this year are not famous at all.

They are ordinary farming families. A father, a mother, five or six children, perhaps a grandmother, and a young hired man who will help with the cattle in return for his food and the journey.

Some of them are travelling with their brothers and sisters, their cousins, the people from their church. Whole neighbourhoods are moving west together.

Altogether, in this one year, perhaps two or three thousand people will set out for Oregon and California.

They will spend the next five months walking.
