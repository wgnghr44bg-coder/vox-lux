Life on the trail is not only work. There is a great deal of music too.

Almost every company has at least one fiddle, carefully wrapped in a blanket in the back of a wagon. Some have a flute, or a harmonica, or a little accordion, and one or two families have even brought a small melodeon, a kind of little organ.

--

In the evenings, when the work is done, the musicians sit on the wagon tongues and play.

They play the tunes everyone knows from home. Old reels and jigs from Scotland and Ireland, songs about rivers and girls and faraway places, and hymns on a Sunday evening.

The young people dance on the flattened grass inside the circle of wagons, by the light of the fires. The older people sit and watch, tapping their feet, and remember when they were young.

The music carries out across the dark prairie, and sometimes a company camped a mile away hears it, and walks over to join in.

--

{img:wedding-on-trail-meadow}
Life goes on along the trail, in all its ordinary ways.

Young people who meet on the journey fall in love. And if there is a minister in the company, or a man who has been a justice of the peace back home, sometimes there is a wedding.

The bride wears the best dress she has, which has been folded at the bottom of a trunk since Missouri. Someone picks wildflowers from the prairie for her to hold.

The couple stand together in front of the wagons, in the open air, with the whole company gathered around them, and say their vows. Afterwards, there is music and dancing, and a supper with whatever special things people have been saving.

The next morning, the newly married couple yoke their oxen and roll on west with everyone else.

--

Babies are born on the trail too, quite often.

When the time comes, the company may stop for a day, or the wagon simply falls out of line, and some of the older women gather around to help.

In the diaries, it is often mentioned in a single line, between the weather and the distance travelled. A fine little girl born in the next wagon this morning. Mother and child doing well. Made twelve miles.

And so, some of the first new Oregonians are born under a wagon cover, somewhere on the open prairie, and arrive in their new home without remembering the journey at all.

--

{img:children-lessons-wagon-tongue}
Some mothers try to keep up the children's schooling.

In the evenings, or during the long noon rest, they sit with the younger ones on a quilt in the shade, and go through their letters and their numbers, or read to them from a book.

The children practise writing with a stick in the dust, or with a pencil on the back of an old letter. They learn the names of the rivers they cross, and the landmarks they pass, and count the miles on the trail guide.

Many of them will remember, for the rest of their lives, that this was the best schooling they ever had.

--

Friendships grow quickly on the trail.

People who were strangers in April are sharing their bread and their coffee by June. They help each other with broken wheels and lost cattle, look after each other's children, and nurse each other when someone falls ill.

Families who have walked together for a thousand miles become something like one large family.

--

{img:quilting-in-wagon-shade}
On a slow afternoon in camp, a few of the women sit together in the shade of a wagon, with a quilt spread out across their knees.

Each of them has brought scraps of fabric from home, pieces of old dresses and shirts, and they stitch them together, square by square, while they talk.

Some of these quilts have a name embroidered on every square, one for each family in the company. They are called friendship quilts, and they are made so that, wherever people end up in Oregon, they will have something to remember each other by.

--

{img:evening-hymn-firelight}
And on many evenings, before going to bed, the company sings together.

Someone begins a hymn, and the others join in, one fire after another, until the whole camp is singing, softly, in the dark.

The sound rises up into the enormous sky, and fades away across the plain.

Then the fires are covered, and the guards walk out to the cattle, and everyone goes to sleep, a little farther west than they were the night before.
