{img:worn-wagon-repairs-camp}
By the time the wagons reach the Snake River, they have been on the road for more than three months, and everything is wearing out.

The wagon covers are stained and patched. The wooden wheels have shrunk in the dry air, so that the iron tyres grow loose, and rattle, and sometimes roll right off.

So in the evenings, there is always something to mend.

--

When the tyres come loose, the men take the wheels off and soak them overnight in the river, so that the wood swells again and fits tightly. Or they drive thin wedges of wood between the tyre and the rim.

A broken axle is a more serious matter. If the company is lucky, there is a spare one, carried under the wagon bed since Missouri. If not, the men must search for a suitable tree, which can be very hard in country where there are no trees at all.

There are men in every company who turn out to be very good at this kind of work. They become known as the people to go to, and they are busy every evening.

--

{img:mending-clothes-by-lantern}
The clothes are wearing out too.

The men's trousers are patched at the knees, and patched again. The boots are coming apart, and are mended with rawhide and pegs. Some of the travellers, by the end, are walking in moccasins bought from the Shoshone or the Lakota, which they find softer and more comfortable than their boots.

The women's skirts are shorter now, cut off at the hem, because the long ones dragged in the dust and the mud.

In the evenings, by the light of a lantern, the mending goes on, stitch after stitch, while the children sleep.

--

{img:trail-guidebook-reading}
Many of the travellers carry a little guidebook with them.

There are only a few of these books so far. Some families carry the report of John Frémont, an army explorer who travelled the route a few years ago and described it day by day. Others carry the book written by Lansford Hastings, or notes copied by hand from a letter.

The guides list the camping places, the springs and the river crossings, and how many miles lie between them.

In the evenings, the men sit by the fire with the book open on their knees, working out how far it is to the next good water, and how long it will take.

--

The guides are not always right. Rivers change their course, springs dry up, good grass is eaten by the companies in front.

So the travellers also learn to rely on what they hear from others.

They read the notes left for them along the trail, written on pieces of paper tucked into the cracks of rocks, or on buffalo skulls by the side of the road. Good grass two miles to the left. Water at the foot of the hill. The Smith family passed here on the ninth, all well.

The trail is a kind of long, slow letter, with each company writing a few lines for the ones that follow.

--

{img:trading-with-shoshone-camp}
And all along the way, there is trading.

At the forts and the trading posts, the travellers trade with the clerks of the fur companies. Along the rivers, they trade with the people who live there: the Lakota, the Shoshone, the Cayuse and the Nez Perce.

The things that are most wanted are often surprising. A cotton shirt is worth a great deal. So are needles, fishhooks, buttons, ribbons, and small mirrors. A good shirt might buy a horse, or a large salmon, or a sack of camas roots, or a pair of beautifully made moccasins.

The travellers learn a few words of the local languages, and of the trade language that is used across the Northwest, and the rest is done with smiles and gestures, and holding things up for the other person to see.

--

Very often, the people who live along the trail are helpful to the travellers. They show them the best places to cross the rivers, help them to find lost cattle, and guide them over the mountains.

Many of the diaries record a kindness of this kind, sometimes just in a single sentence. A man of the Shoshone helped us across the river today, and would take nothing for it but a little tobacco.

--

{img:campfire-stories-old-trapper}
Sometimes, at a camp along the Snake, an old trapper or a guide rides in and sits down by the fire.

He is weather-beaten, with a long beard and clothes made of buckskin, and he has spent most of his life in the mountains, and has seen the country before any wagons came.

The children sit around him in a circle as he tells his stories, about the winters of years ago, and the beaver streams, and the great gatherings of trappers in the mountains, which are now nearly gone.

The fire burns down. The stories go on late into the night.

And in the morning, he rides off again, alone, into the hills, and the wagons roll on toward Oregon.
