{img:independence-square-spring}
Tonight we are going about a hundred and eighty years back, to a busy little town on the western edge of Missouri, where the farmland ends and the open prairie begins.

[long-pause]

The town is called Independence, and it is the spring of 1846.

[pause]

The roads around the square are soft with mud after the April rain, and the air smells of wood smoke, wet leather and new-cut timber.

[pause]

In the middle of the square stands a plain brick courthouse. Around it are shops and stables, a few taverns, a bank, a church or two, and a great many wagons.

[pause]

There are wagons everywhere this spring. Wagons waiting outside the stores, wagons standing in the side streets, wagons parked in long rows along the edge of town, with their white covers moving gently in the wind.

[long-pause]

For a few weeks every year, Independence is one of the busiest places in America.

[pause]

Families arrive from Illinois and Indiana, from Kentucky and Ohio and Tennessee, some by steamboat up the Missouri River, some by wagon along the country roads. They all come here for the same reason.

[pause]

From this town, a long trail leads west. It crosses the plains, follows the rivers into the Rocky Mountains, and goes on, for about two thousand miles, until it reaches a green valley near the Pacific Ocean.

[pause]

People call it the road to Oregon.

[long-pause]

{img:outfitters-store-counter}
Inside the general stores, the shelves are full and the shopkeepers are tired.

[pause]

A man in a dusty coat leans over the counter with a list in his hand, written carefully in pencil on the back of a letter.

[pause]

Flour, two hundred pounds for every grown person. Bacon, packed in barrels with bran around it, so that it will not melt in the heat. Coffee, sugar, salt, dried apples, rice, a sack of beans.

[pause]

A keg of vinegar. A little saleratus, for making the bread rise. Tea for the women who prefer it. A few pounds of dried peaches, as a treat for later in the journey.

[pause]

The shopkeeper writes it all down, and his boy carries the sacks out to the wagon, one at a time.

[long-pause]

There are other things on the list too. A rifle and powder and lead. An axe and a spade. Rope, chains, a spare wagon tongue. Needles and thread, candles, soap, a lantern.

[pause]

A cast-iron skillet, a coffee pot, a Dutch oven with a heavy lid, tin plates and tin cups.

[pause]

Most families also bring a few things from home that they cannot leave behind. A clock, a rocking chair, a box of books, a set of good dishes wrapped in quilts.

[pause]

Many of these things will be left beside the trail later on, when the oxen grow tired. But for now, everything goes in.

[long-pause]

{img:blacksmith-wagon-wheel}
Down the street, the blacksmiths are working from early morning until dark.

[pause]

The ring of hammers on iron carries across the town. Men bring their wagons to have the wheels checked, new iron tyres fitted, and the axles greased.

[pause]

A good wagon for the journey is not very large. It is a farm wagon, about ten feet long and four feet wide, made of seasoned hardwood, with the bed shaped a little like a shallow boat.

[pause]

Over the top, hoops of bent hickory hold up a cover of heavy cotton cloth, rubbed with linseed oil so that the rain will run off it.

[pause]

From a distance, a line of these wagons moving through the long grass looks like a line of small sailing ships, and so people have started to call them prairie schooners.

[long-pause]

{img:oxen-trading-pen}
At the edge of town there are pens full of cattle, and here the traders are busy too.

[pause]

Most families choose oxen to pull their wagons. Oxen are slow, perhaps two miles an hour, but they are strong and patient, they can live on prairie grass, and they are less likely to be taken in the night than horses or mules.

[pause]

A family will usually buy three or four yoke of them, so that the animals can take turns.

[pause]

The young men walk among the cattle, looking at their feet and their shoulders, and asking how many seasons they have worked. The oxen stand quietly, chewing, and look back at them with large, calm eyes.

[long-pause]

{img:camp-outside-independence}
Outside the town, the prairie is already full of tents.

[pause]

The families who have bought what they need are waiting here for the grass to grow. There is no point in leaving too early, because the animals will need fresh grass to eat along the way, and on the plains the grass comes late.

[pause]

So they wait, for a week, or two weeks, or three.

[pause]

The children run between the wagons. The women wash clothes in the creek and hang them on ropes between the wagon wheels. In the evenings, small fires burn across the meadow, and someone always has a fiddle.

[pause]

Every day a few more wagons arrive. And every few days, another little company decides that the grass is high enough, and rolls slowly out onto the trail.

[long-pause]

{img:farm-family-reading-letter}
Why would a family sell their farm, load everything they own onto a wagon, and walk two thousand miles into country they have never seen?

[pause]

The reasons are different for every family, but many of them begin in the same way, with a letter.

[long-pause]

Someone's cousin, or an old neighbour, went west a few years earlier. Now a letter has arrived, passed from hand to hand across the country, and it is read aloud at the kitchen table in the evening.

[pause]

The letter talks about a place called the Willamette Valley. The soil there is deep and black, it says. The winters are mild, and the cattle can graze outside all year long. The rivers are full of salmon, and the forests go on for ever.

[pause]

And there is land. A great deal of land, free to anyone who will go there and farm it.

[long-pause]

Back home in the Mississippi valley, the years have not been easy. Prices for corn and pork have been low. Many families are in debt. Every summer, the fevers come, and the children fall sick with the ague.

[pause]

So the letter is read again, and then again. People begin to talk about Oregon at church, at the mill, at the store. Newspapers print long articles about it.

[pause]

Some people call it Oregon fever.

[long-pause]

At this moment, Oregon does not quite belong to anyone.

[pause]

For many years, Britain and the United States have agreed to share the country between them. The British Hudson's Bay Company has its trading posts there, and a small number of American settlers have built farms in the valleys.

[pause]

In the spring of 1846, the two governments are still talking about where the border should be. That summer they will agree on it, but the families leaving Independence do not know that yet.

[long-pause]

{img:tabitha-brown-elderly-woman}
Among the people setting out this year is a woman named Tabitha Brown.

[pause]

She is sixty-six years old, small and slight, and she walks with a cane. She was born in Massachusetts, the daughter of a doctor, and she married a minister who died many years ago, leaving her with three young children.

[pause]

She raised them by teaching school, and now her children are grown. One of her sons went to Oregon two years ago, and he has come back east to fetch the rest of the family.

[pause]

Everyone tells her she is too old for a journey like this. She goes anyway.

[pause]

With her travels her husband's brother, Captain John Brown, who is seventy-seven and was once a sea captain. Mrs Brown has her own wagon, and a young man to drive it.

[long-pause]

{img:young-parkman-st-louis-levee}
Another traveller this spring is not an emigrant at all.

[pause]

His name is Francis Parkman. He is twenty-two years old, from a wealthy family in Boston, and he has just finished studying law at Harvard.

[pause]

What he really loves, though, is history, and the forests and the open country. He wants to see the West while it is still wild, and to meet the Native American peoples of the plains, because he hopes to write about them one day.

[pause]

With his cousin, Quincy Adams Shaw, he has come by steamboat from St. Louis. They have hired a guide called Henry Chatillon, a quiet, skilled hunter who has spent many years on the plains.

[pause]

Parkman keeps a notebook in his pocket, and every evening he writes down what he has seen.

[long-pause]

{img:reed-family-wagon-illinois}
From Springfield, in Illinois, two large farming families are on the road as well.

[pause]

They are the family of James Reed, a businessman who runs a furniture factory, and the families of the brothers George and Jacob Donner.

[pause]

James Reed has had a special wagon built for this journey. It has two decks, with a little stove inside, a set of beds, and a ladder up to a sleeping loft. His wife Margret is not well, and he wants her to travel in comfort.

[pause]

His stepdaughter Virginia is nearly thirteen. She has a pony of her own, and she rides it alongside the wagons, often far ahead of them.

[pause]

Their journey will turn out to be a long and difficult one, and we will come back to it later, gently and from a distance.

[long-pause]

{img:emigrant-families-gathering}
But most of the people on the trail this year are not famous at all.

[pause]

They are ordinary farming families. A father, a mother, five or six children, perhaps a grandmother, and a young hired man who will help with the cattle in return for his food and the journey.

[pause]

Some of them are travelling with their brothers and sisters, their cousins, the people from their church. Whole neighbourhoods are moving west together.

[pause]

Altogether, in this one year, perhaps two or three thousand people will set out for Oregon and California.

[pause]

They will spend the next five months walking.

[long-pause]

{img:wagons-leaving-into-prairie}
One morning in early May, a small company of wagons leaves the camp outside Independence and turns west.

[pause]

There are perhaps twenty wagons in this company, and a herd of loose cattle following behind, driven by boys on horseback.

[pause]

The road leads first through green, rolling country, with patches of woodland along the creeks and farms here and there on the hillsides.

[pause]

The children walk beside the wagons. Almost everyone walks. The wagon is for food and tools and bedding, and there is no room inside for passengers, except the very young, the very old, and anyone who is ill.

[long-pause]

The pace is slow. An ox walks at about the speed of a person strolling through a park, and a good day on the trail is fifteen miles.

[pause]

At this speed, the land changes very gradually. A hill that appears on the horizon in the morning is still there in the afternoon, a little closer.

[pause]

In the first few days, everything is new. People laugh at the oxen, which seem to understand the commands better than some of the drivers. Gee means turn to the right, and haw means turn to the left, and whoa means stop.

[pause]

The drivers walk on the left side of the team with a long whip, which they mostly crack in the air, rather than use.

[long-pause]

{img:company-meeting-electing-captain}
On the third or fourth evening, the men of the company gather around a fire to hold a meeting.

[pause]

They have to decide how they will travel together, and so, in the American way, they hold an election.

[pause]

They choose a captain, who will decide when to start in the morning and where to camp at night. They choose a few lieutenants to help him, and they write down a few simple rules.

[pause]

Every man between sixteen and sixty will take his turn standing guard at night. No one is to fire a gun inside the camp. Sundays will be a day of rest, if possible.

[pause]

The rules are read out and agreed to with a show of hands. Then the meeting ends, and people go back to their own fires.

[long-pause]

These companies do not always last very long. On the trail, people quarrel about small things: whose cattle are slowing everyone down, whose dog barked all night, whether to stop on a Sunday or press on.

[pause]

When the disagreements grow too large, a company simply divides. Some wagons go ahead, and the others follow a day behind.

[pause]

No one is in charge of the trail as a whole. It is just a great many families, travelling in small groups, all going the same way.

[long-pause]

{img:ferry-across-kansas-river}
After about a week, the trail comes down to the Kansas River.

[pause]

The river is wide and brown, moving steadily between low banks lined with cottonwood trees.

[pause]

Here there is a ferry, run by local families of French and Kansa descent, who have lived along this river for many years. The ferry is a simple flat boat, pulled across the river on a rope.

[pause]

One wagon at a time goes over. The oxen are unhitched and made to swim, with men in a small boat beside them, calling out and guiding them toward the far bank.

[pause]

It takes all day for the company to cross. In the evening, they camp on the other side, among the trees, and dry their clothes by the fire.

[long-pause]

{img:open-prairie-tall-grass}
Beyond the Kansas River, the farms come to an end.

[pause]

From here on, there are no towns, no fences, no shops and no roads. There is only the trail itself, worn into the grass by the wagons of the years before.

[pause]

The country opens up. The trees grow fewer, and then there are only a few, along the streams. In every direction there is grass, waving in the wind, and the sky seems much bigger than it did at home.

[pause]

In May, the prairie is full of flowers. There are blue spiderworts, and yellow coneflowers, and wild roses, and something that smells a little like mint when the wagon wheels crush it.

[long-pause]

{img:wagon-ruts-rolling-hills}
In the afternoons, the clouds pile up over the plains, very high and white.

[pause]

Sometimes, in the distance, there is a grey curtain of rain moving slowly across the land, while the sun shines on the wagons.

[pause]

The trail goes on ahead of them, two pale lines through the grass, over one long gentle rise after another.

[pause]

Each evening, the company stops a little farther west. And Independence, with its shops and its blacksmiths and its muddy square, begins to feel like a place they visited a long time ago.

[long-pause]

{img:dawn-camp-wagons-circle}
A day on the Oregon Trail begins in the dark.

[pause]

At about four o'clock in the morning, while the stars are still out, the last men on guard fire their rifles into the air. It is the signal that the night is over.

[pause]

Within a few minutes, the camp is awake. Canvas flaps are pushed open, and people climb out of the wagons and out of the tents, stiff and cold, pulling on their boots.

[pause]

Small fires are lit again from the ashes of last night's fires. The smoke rises straight up into the still, grey air.

[long-pause]

During the night, the wagons were drawn up in a large circle, each wagon's tongue resting against the back of the wagon in front of it. Inside the circle, the oxen have been kept safe, or else they have been grazing outside it, watched by the night guard.

[pause]

Now the young men and the boys walk out among the cattle in the half-light. Each family has to find its own animals among hundreds of others, and bring them back to be yoked.

[pause]

It is not always easy. Some of the oxen have wandered off. Others do not wish to be found.

[long-pause]

{img:breakfast-campfire-family}
Meanwhile, the women are making breakfast.

[pause]

There is coffee, always coffee, boiled in a tin pot over the fire. There is bacon frying in the skillet, and bread, or biscuits baked in the Dutch oven with coals heaped on the lid.

[pause]

Sometimes there is a little milk, from the family cow walking at the back of the wagon. And if the cream is put in a churn and hung inside the wagon in the morning, the jolting of the road has turned it into a small lump of butter by the evening.

[pause]

People eat quickly, standing or sitting on boxes. Then the dishes are washed, the bedding is rolled up, and everything is packed back into the wagon in its proper place.

[long-pause]

{img:yoking-oxen-morning}
By about seven o'clock, the teams are yoked and the wagons are ready.

[pause]

The captain looks along the line, and when all is well, he gives the signal. The drivers crack their whips, the oxen lean into their yokes, and with a long creaking of wood and iron, the first wagon begins to move.

[pause]

The others follow, one after another, and the company stretches out along the trail.

[pause]

The wagons take turns at the front. The one that leads today will be at the back tomorrow, eating the dust of all the others, and so it goes on, day after day, so that it is fair for everyone.

[long-pause]

{img:nooning-wagons-shade}
In the middle of the day, the company stops for an hour or two. This is called the nooning.

[pause]

The oxen are unhitched and allowed to graze. People sit in the shade of the wagons and eat a cold meal from the morning: bread, a piece of bacon, perhaps some dried apples.

[pause]

Some of the men lie down with their hats over their faces and sleep. The children go looking along the creek for anything interesting: a turtle, a snake skin, a smooth white stone.

[pause]

The women mend clothes, or write a few lines in a diary.

[pause]

Then the oxen are yoked again, and the afternoon march begins.

[long-pause]

{img:evening-corral-sunset}
In the late afternoon, the captain rides on ahead to look for a good place to camp. He needs water, grass for the animals, and if possible, a little wood for the fires.

[pause]

When he has found a place, the wagons are drawn up again into their great circle, and the day's work is almost done.

[pause]

The oxen are unhitched and driven out to graze. The fires are lit. Supper is cooked, and eaten, and the dishes are washed once more.

[pause]

And then, for an hour or two, there is time to rest.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, people visit each other's fires. The men talk about the road ahead, and the condition of the cattle, and how many miles they have made. The women talk about their children, and about home.

[pause]

Someone plays a fiddle, and a few of the young people dance on the trampled grass inside the circle of wagons.

[pause]

As the light fades, the families go back to their own wagons. The first guard of the night walks out to the cattle.

[pause]

And soon, apart from the low voices of the guards and the sound of the oxen chewing, the camp is quiet.

[long-pause]

{img:mother-dutch-oven-baking}
For the women of the trail, the work does not stop when the wagons stop.

[pause]

At home, they had a kitchen, with a table and a stove, a well in the yard and a garden behind the house. Here, they have a fire on the open ground, a few iron pots and a wagon.

[pause]

And yet, three times a day, there is food to cook for the whole family.

[long-pause]

Bread is the hardest part. The dough is mixed in a pan on the tailboard of the wagon in the evening, and left to rise overnight, wrapped in a cloth to keep it warm.

[pause]

In the morning, it goes into the Dutch oven, which stands right in the embers. More hot coals are piled onto the lid, so that the heat comes from above and below at the same time.

[pause]

It takes practice. The first loaves on the trail are often burned on the bottom and pale on top. But after a few weeks, most of the women can bake very good bread, in the wind, in the rain, on the open prairie.

[long-pause]

{img:children-gathering-buffalo-chips}
Farther out on the plains, there is a new problem. There is no firewood.

[pause]

For days at a time, there is not a single tree to be seen. So the travellers learn to use what the buffalo have left behind.

[pause]

The dried droppings of the buffalo, lying in the grass in great numbers, burn quite well. They make a hot, steady fire with very little smoke, and almost no smell.

[pause]

At first, some of the women are not very happy about this. But soon, gathering buffalo chips becomes one of the ordinary jobs of the day, and the children go out with sacks and baskets every afternoon, competing to see who can collect the most.

[long-pause]

The children have other jobs too.

[pause]

They carry water from the creek. They look after the younger children. They help to drive the loose cattle, and they go looking for lost oxen in the morning.

[pause]

The older girls cook and wash and sew beside their mothers. The older boys stand guard at night with the men, holding a rifle that is almost as tall as they are, and feeling very grown up.

[pause]

And in between, they play. They chase each other between the wagons, pick wild strawberries, look for arrowheads and fossils, and run ahead to be the first to see whatever lies over the next hill.

[long-pause]

{img:laundry-day-by-creek}
Every week or two, if the company stops near a good stream for a day, the women hold a washing day.

[pause]

Great kettles of water are heated over the fires. The clothes are scrubbed on washboards, rinsed in the creek and spread out on the grass and over the bushes to dry.

[pause]

It is hard work, but it is also one of the few times on the journey when the women can sit together and talk for a whole day.

[pause]

By the evening, the clothes are dry and folded, and smell of sunshine and wind. They will stay clean for a day or two, until the dust of the road settles on them again.

[long-pause]

{img:sunday-prayer-on-prairie}
On Sundays, many of the companies try to rest.

[pause]

Not every company manages it. Some of the men worry that they are falling behind, and that the snow will reach the mountains before they do. But when they can, the families stay in camp for the day.

[pause]

In the morning, they gather in the shade of a wagon, or out in the open grass, and someone who knows the Bible well leads a short service. They read a passage, and pray, and sing a few hymns together.

[pause]

The voices carry a long way across the prairie on a still morning.

[pause]

In the afternoon, the oxen rest and graze, and the people mend their shoes and their harness, and write letters that they hope someone travelling east will carry back home.

[long-pause]

{img:girl-writing-diary-wagon}
Many of the travellers keep a diary.

[pause]

They write in little notebooks, in pencil or with ink made from powder and water, sitting on the wagon tongue in the evening light.

[pause]

Most of the entries are short. The distance travelled today, fifteen miles. The weather, warm and windy. The camping place, good grass but no wood. A note about a river crossed, a wagon broken, a baby born in the next wagon.

[pause]

Thousands of these little notebooks have survived. Today they are kept in libraries and archives, and they tell us, day by day, what the journey was really like.

[pause]

They are full of the small details of ordinary life: a lost cow, a good supper, a song around the fire, and the long, quiet line of wagons going on toward the west.

[long-pause]

{img:oxen-team-with-names}
For five months, the travellers live closer to their animals than they ever have before.

[pause]

They walk beside them all day, and sleep near them all night. They know the sound of each one's breathing, the way each one walks, and which one is likely to wander off in the morning.

[long-pause]

Every ox has a name. Back home, a farmer might not have bothered, but on the trail, the oxen are called by name a hundred times a day, and they learn to answer.

[pause]

There are oxen called Buck and Bright, which are the most common names of all. There are oxen called Duke and Dime, Tom and Jerry, Lion and Brindle, Old Ball and Star.

[pause]

Each ox has its own place in the team. The strongest and steadiest pair is yoked nearest the wagon, where the hardest work is done. The cleverest pair goes in front, where they must follow the trail and listen for the commands.

[long-pause]

The oxen are patient animals, but they have their own opinions.

[pause]

On hot afternoons, if the trail passes near a river, the whole team may decide, all at once, that it is time for a drink, and turn off the road toward the water, wagon and all, no matter what the driver says.

[pause]

In the evenings, after they are unyoked, they lie down in the grass with long sighs, and chew, and look at the sunset.

[pause]

Many of the travellers write about their oxen with great affection. When one of them grows too weak to go on, and has to be left behind at a fort or a good meadow, the children cry.

[long-pause]

{img:family-cow-tied-behind-wagon}
Most families also bring a cow or two, walking at the back of the wagon on a rope, or with the loose herd.

[pause]

The cow is precious. She gives milk every morning and evening, for the children, and for the coffee, and for the butter that churns itself in the jolting wagon.

[pause]

On the long dry stretches, when the grass is poor, the milk grows less and less. But the cow keeps walking, all the way across the continent, and at the end of the journey, she will be the start of a new herd in Oregon.

[pause]

Some families bring chickens too, in wooden coops tied to the side of the wagon. The hens complain loudly about the road, but they go on laying eggs.

[long-pause]

{img:dog-walking-beside-wagon}
And nearly every company has dogs.

[pause]

There are farm dogs and hunting dogs, big dogs and small dogs, and they run alongside the wagons all day, exploring the grass, chasing rabbits and barking at the cattle.

[pause]

At night, they sleep under the wagons, and they are the first to hear anything moving in the darkness.

[pause]

Some companies make rules about the dogs, because there are so many of them and they bark so much. But the children will not hear of leaving them behind.

[pause]

James Reed's family has five dogs, and the children love them all. The smallest one is called Cash.

[long-pause]

{img:prairie-dog-town-children}
On the plains, there are animals that none of the travellers have ever seen before.

[pause]

The children are fascinated by the prairie dogs. These are not really dogs at all, but small brown animals, a little like squirrels, which live together in great colonies, with thousands of burrows dug into the ground.

[pause]

When the wagons pass, the prairie dogs sit up on the little mounds outside their holes, watching. They give a sharp little bark, flick their tails, and then dive down out of sight, and a moment later pop up again somewhere else.

[pause]

The colonies are called towns, and some of them stretch for miles.

[long-pause]

There are antelope too, the swiftest animals on the plains. They are tan and white, with short black horns, and they stand in small groups far away, watching the wagons with great curiosity.

[pause]

The hunters say that if you lie down in the grass and wave a handkerchief tied to a stick, the antelope will come closer and closer to see what it is.

[pause]

There are jackrabbits with enormous ears. There are hawks circling high above, and flocks of little birds rising out of the grass. There are wolves too, mostly heard rather than seen, singing far away in the night.

[long-pause]

{img:horses-grazing-evening-camp}
Some of the men have horses, and they ride ahead of the wagons, scouting the road, looking for water and grass, or going out to hunt.

[pause]

A good horse on the trail is a great treasure. It needs better grass than an ox, and more care, and in the evenings its owner rubs it down and checks its feet, and talks to it quietly while it eats.

[pause]

Francis Parkman has a horse he calls Pontiac, a strong and lively animal, and later, a gentle mare he calls Pauline, whom he describes as the best horse he ever had.

[pause]

In the evening light, the horses graze at the edge of the camp, swishing their tails, their coats shining gold in the last of the sun.

[long-pause]

{img:platte-river-wide-shallow}
After about three weeks of travel, the trail comes over a low ridge, and below it lies a wide, flat valley, with a river running through it.

[pause]

This is the Platte River, and for the next several weeks, the wagons will follow it west.

[long-pause]

The Platte is a strange river. It is very wide, often more than a mile from bank to bank, but in most places it is only a few inches deep.

[pause]

The water is brown and full of sand, and it spreads out in dozens of little channels around low sandy islands, some of them covered in willows and cottonwood trees.

[pause]

The travellers have a saying about it. The Platte, they say, is a mile wide and an inch deep. It is too thick to drink and too thin to plough.

[pause]

When they want water for cooking, they fill a bucket and let it stand for an hour, until the sand settles at the bottom.

[long-pause]

{img:wagon-train-platte-valley}
The trail along the Platte is easy to follow. The valley is flat and wide, and the wagons can spread out side by side, instead of travelling in a single line, so that not everyone has to walk in the dust of the others.

[pause]

On some days, from a small rise, it is possible to see other companies ahead and behind, little lines of white wagon covers, strung out along the river for many miles.

[pause]

At night, their campfires can be seen in the distance, scattered along the valley like a few small stars that have fallen on the plain.

[long-pause]

The wind blows nearly all the time on the Platte. It blows the ashes out of the fires, it blows the hats off the men's heads, it blows sand into the bread.

[pause]

The women tie their bonnets down tightly. The wagon covers flap and snap all day.

[pause]

Sometimes, in the afternoon, the wind drops, and for an hour the whole valley is warm and still and silent, except for the creak of the wheels and the larks singing in the grass.

[long-pause]

{img:distant-buffalo-herd}
And then, one morning, someone sees the buffalo.

[pause]

At first there are only a few, dark shapes on the far side of the river, grazing slowly. Then, over the next few days, there are more and more.

[pause]

Some of the travellers will later write that they saw the plains black with buffalo, as far as the eye could reach. A herd can take hours to cross the trail, and the wagons have to stop and wait for them to pass.

[pause]

The buffalo are enormous, heavy animals, with great shaggy heads and small horns, and they move with a slow, rolling walk.

[long-pause]

The men go out hunting, of course. Fresh buffalo meat is a welcome change from bacon, and the hump and the tongue are thought to be the best parts.

[pause]

Francis Parkman, who is travelling along the Platte this same spring, rides out with his guide Henry Chatillon to hunt. He finds it the most exciting thing he has ever done.

[pause]

But for the families in the wagons, the buffalo are mostly something to look at. The children stand on the wagon seats to see them better, and the herd moves slowly away across the plain, raising a long cloud of dust behind it.

[long-pause]

{img:thunderstorm-over-plains}
The weather on the plains can change very quickly.

[pause]

On a hot afternoon, a great dark cloud will rise in the west, taller than any mountain. The light turns strange and yellow, and the wind suddenly grows cold.

[pause]

Then the storm arrives. Lightning flickers across the whole sky at once, and the thunder rolls on and on. The rain comes down so hard that it bounces on the ground.

[pause]

The families shelter inside their wagons and wait. The cattle turn their backs to the wind and stand very still.

[long-pause]

{img:rainbow-after-storm-wagons}
And then, after half an hour or so, the storm moves on, rumbling away to the east.

[pause]

The sun comes out again, low and golden. Every blade of grass is shining with water, and the air smells fresh and clean, of wet earth and crushed sage.

[pause]

Often there is a rainbow, standing over the river.

[pause]

The families climb out of their wagons, hang their wet blankets over the wheels to dry, and light the evening fires with whatever dry chips they managed to keep under the wagon covers.

[pause]

Tomorrow the trail will be muddy for a few hours. But by noon, under the prairie sun, it will be dry and dusty again, as if the storm had never been.

[long-pause]

{img:night-camp-under-stars}
Let us stay with one company of wagons for a single night, somewhere along the Platte, in the first days of June.

[long-pause]

The sun has gone down behind the low hills to the west, and the sky above them is turning from orange to pale green to deep blue.

[pause]

The wagons stand in their great circle on a grassy flat near the river. Inside the circle, a dozen small fires are burning, and around each of them sits a family.

[pause]

The supper is finished. A kettle of water is heating for the dishes. Somewhere a baby is crying, and then stops.

[long-pause]

{img:family-around-campfire-night}
At one fire, a father is sitting on an upturned bucket, mending a piece of harness by the light of the flames. His needle goes in and out of the leather, slowly and carefully.

[pause]

Beside him, his wife is knitting a sock, without looking at it. She has knitted so many socks that her hands can do it on their own.

[pause]

Their two youngest children are already asleep in the wagon, under a pile of quilts. The oldest boy is sitting with his back against a wheel, cleaning his rifle, because he will take the second watch tonight.

[pause]

The fire crackles and settles. Every now and then, a little shower of sparks rises into the dark.

[long-pause]

From the next fire, someone is singing an old song from home, very quietly, and a few other voices join in for the chorus.

[pause]

Farther off, a fiddle is playing a slow tune, and then a faster one, and then stops, and people laugh.

[pause]

The talk around the fires grows quieter as the evening goes on. People talk about the distance they have come, and the distance still ahead, and what the land will look like in Oregon.

[long-pause]

{img:night-guard-cattle-moonlight}
Outside the circle of wagons, out in the darkness, the cattle are grazing.

[pause]

Two men are walking slowly around the herd, with rifles over their shoulders. This is the first watch, and it lasts until midnight.

[pause]

The men walk in opposite directions, so that they meet each other twice on every round. When they meet, they stop for a moment and speak quietly, and then walk on.

[pause]

The moon is rising over the river, large and pale. The grass is silver in its light, and the oxen are dark, still shapes, lying down now, chewing slowly.

[long-pause]

Far off, across the prairie, a coyote begins to call. Its voice rises and falls, and then a second one answers it, from farther away.

[pause]

The guards hardly notice. They have heard the coyotes every night for weeks. It is simply one of the sounds of the plains, like the wind and the river.

[pause]

An owl flies low over the herd without a sound.

[long-pause]

{img:stars-over-wagon-covers}
Inside the circle, the fires are burning down to embers now.

[pause]

One by one, the families climb into their wagons, or crawl into their tents beside them. The canvas covers glow faintly for a while from the candles inside, and then, one by one, the candles are put out.

[pause]

The night is very clear. Without any towns, without any lights at all for hundreds of miles, the stars are brighter than anyone back home has ever seen them.

[pause]

The Milky Way stretches right across the sky, from one side of the prairie to the other, like a pale river of light above the dark river below.

[long-pause]

At midnight, the first guard comes back to the wagons and wakes the second guard. The boy with the rifle climbs down, rubbing his eyes, and walks out into the cool air to take his turn.

[pause]

The camp sleeps.

[pause]

A horse shifts its weight and sighs. The river murmurs over its sandbars. The wind moves softly in the wagon covers.

[long-pause]

{img:first-light-over-river}
And then, very slowly, the sky in the east begins to grow pale.

[pause]

The stars fade, one by one. A thin line of light appears along the edge of the world, first grey, then pink, then gold.

[pause]

A meadowlark sings somewhere in the grass.

[pause]

At four o'clock, the guard raises his rifle and fires it into the air, and another day on the Oregon Trail begins.

[long-pause]

{img:fiddle-dance-inside-circle}
Life on the trail is not only work. There is a great deal of music too.

[pause]

Almost every company has at least one fiddle, carefully wrapped in a blanket in the back of a wagon. Some have a flute, or a harmonica, or a little accordion, and one or two families have even brought a small melodeon, a kind of little organ.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, when the work is done, the musicians sit on the wagon tongues and play.

[pause]

They play the tunes everyone knows from home. Old reels and jigs from Scotland and Ireland, songs about rivers and girls and faraway places, and hymns on a Sunday evening.

[pause]

The young people dance on the flattened grass inside the circle of wagons, by the light of the fires. The older people sit and watch, tapping their feet, and remember when they were young.

[pause]

The music carries out across the dark prairie, and sometimes a company camped a mile away hears it, and walks over to join in.

[long-pause]

{img:wedding-on-trail-meadow}
Life goes on along the trail, in all its ordinary ways.

[pause]

Young people who meet on the journey fall in love. And if there is a minister in the company, or a man who has been a justice of the peace back home, sometimes there is a wedding.

[pause]

The bride wears the best dress she has, which has been folded at the bottom of a trunk since Missouri. Someone picks wildflowers from the prairie for her to hold.

[pause]

The couple stand together in front of the wagons, in the open air, with the whole company gathered around them, and say their vows. Afterwards, there is music and dancing, and a supper with whatever special things people have been saving.

[pause]

The next morning, the newly married couple yoke their oxen and roll on west with everyone else.

[long-pause]

Babies are born on the trail too, quite often.

[pause]

When the time comes, the company may stop for a day, or the wagon simply falls out of line, and some of the older women gather around to help.

[pause]

In the diaries, it is often mentioned in a single line, between the weather and the distance travelled. A fine little girl born in the next wagon this morning. Mother and child doing well. Made twelve miles.

[pause]

And so, some of the first new Oregonians are born under a wagon cover, somewhere on the open prairie, and arrive in their new home without remembering the journey at all.

[long-pause]

{img:children-lessons-wagon-tongue}
Some mothers try to keep up the children's schooling.

[pause]

In the evenings, or during the long noon rest, they sit with the younger ones on a quilt in the shade, and go through their letters and their numbers, or read to them from a book.

[pause]

The children practise writing with a stick in the dust, or with a pencil on the back of an old letter. They learn the names of the rivers they cross, and the landmarks they pass, and count the miles on the trail guide.

[pause]

Many of them will remember, for the rest of their lives, that this was the best schooling they ever had.

[long-pause]

Friendships grow quickly on the trail.

[pause]

People who were strangers in April are sharing their bread and their coffee by June. They help each other with broken wheels and lost cattle, look after each other's children, and nurse each other when someone falls ill.

[pause]

Families who have walked together for a thousand miles become something like one large family.

[long-pause]

{img:quilting-in-wagon-shade}
On a slow afternoon in camp, a few of the women sit together in the shade of a wagon, with a quilt spread out across their knees.

[pause]

Each of them has brought scraps of fabric from home, pieces of old dresses and shirts, and they stitch them together, square by square, while they talk.

[pause]

Some of these quilts have a name embroidered on every square, one for each family in the company. They are called friendship quilts, and they are made so that, wherever people end up in Oregon, they will have something to remember each other by.

[long-pause]

{img:evening-hymn-firelight}
And on many evenings, before going to bed, the company sings together.

[pause]

Someone begins a hymn, and the others join in, one fire after another, until the whole camp is singing, softly, in the dark.

[pause]

The sound rises up into the enormous sky, and fades away across the plain.

[pause]

Then the fires are covered, and the guards walk out to the cattle, and everyone goes to sleep, a little farther west than they were the night before.

[long-pause]

{img:courthouse-rock-distance}
West of the place where the Platte divides into two rivers, the country begins to change.

[pause]

The wagons follow the North Platte now. The low green hills give way to drier land, with sand and sagebrush and pale rocks, and the grass grows shorter and greyer.

[pause]

And then, one day, the first of the great landmarks appears on the horizon.

[long-pause]

It looks, from a long way off, like a large building standing alone on the plain. Some say it looks like a courthouse, and so it has been given the name Courthouse Rock.

[pause]

It is a huge mass of clay and sandstone, rising out of the flat land, and it seems to be much closer than it really is. In the clear dry air of the plains, distances are hard to judge.

[pause]

Some of the young men decide to walk over to it after supper. They walk for hours, and it never seems to come any closer, and they arrive back in camp long after dark, tired and laughing.

[long-pause]

{img:chimney-rock-evening}
A day or two farther on, there is something even stranger.

[pause]

A tall, thin spire of rock rises straight up from a cone-shaped hill, pointing at the sky like a finger. It can be seen for days before the wagons reach it, and for days after they pass it.

[pause]

The travellers call it Chimney Rock, and nearly every one of them mentions it in their diary. Some draw little pictures of it in the margins.

[pause]

It is made of soft stone, worn away by wind and rain over thousands of years. Some people climb up the base of it, and carve their names into the rock with a knife, beside hundreds of other names from the years before.

[long-pause]

{img:scotts-bluff-wagons-below}
Beyond Chimney Rock, the trail comes to a long line of high bluffs, cut into towers and walls by the weather, like the ruins of some great old city.

[pause]

This is Scotts Bluff, named after a fur trader who died near here many years ago.

[pause]

In 1846, the wagons do not go through the gap in the bluffs, as later travellers will. They go around them, through a valley a little to the south, where there is a spring of cold, clear water and a few cedar trees.

[pause]

In the evening light, the bluffs turn gold and red, and then purple, and the swallows fly in and out of their nests in the cliffs.

[long-pause]

{img:fort-laramie-adobe-walls}
About a week later, the wagons come to Fort Laramie.

[pause]

It is not a military fort, at least not yet. It is a trading post, built by the American Fur Company, a square of high adobe walls standing on a low rise above the Laramie River, near the place where it joins the North Platte.

[pause]

Inside the walls there are storerooms and living quarters, a courtyard, and a pair of watch towers at the corners.

[pause]

For the travellers, Fort Laramie is the first sign of anything like a town for more than a month. Here they can buy a few supplies, at very high prices, have their wagons repaired, and leave letters to be carried back east.

[long-pause]

Francis Parkman reaches Fort Laramie in the middle of June, and stays for a while.

[pause]

He describes the fort as a busy, cheerful place. The traders and their families live inside the walls, and around it, on the plain, stand the lodges of the Lakota people, who have come here to trade buffalo robes for cloth, beads, iron kettles and knives.

[pause]

Later in the summer, Parkman goes to live for several weeks with a band of the Oglala Lakota, travelling with them and writing down everything he sees.

[long-pause]

{img:lakota-lodges-near-fort}
The emigrant families watch the Lakota camps with great curiosity, and the Lakota watch them in the same way.

[pause]

Sometimes they visit each other. The travellers trade flour and sugar and cloth for moccasins and buffalo robes. Children look at each other shyly across the grass, and sometimes they play together.

[pause]

For most of the travellers, this is the first time they have ever met the people who live on the plains. Their diaries are full of notes about the painted lodges, the horses, the beadwork and the clothes.

[long-pause]

The wagons rest near Fort Laramie for a day or two. The oxen graze. The women wash clothes in the clear water of the Laramie River.

[pause]

Behind them lie six hundred and fifty miles of plains.

[pause]

Ahead of them, on the western horizon, a dark line of hills is rising. And beyond those hills are the Rocky Mountains.

[long-pause]

{img:dry-hills-sagebrush-trail}
Beyond Fort Laramie, the trail climbs slowly into higher, drier country.

[pause]

The grass grows thin. There is sagebrush everywhere now, grey-green and fragrant, and the wagon wheels crush it, so that the whole company travels in a cloud of its sharp, clean smell.

[pause]

The days are hot, and the nights are suddenly cold. In the mornings there is sometimes a thin skin of ice on the water buckets, even in the middle of summer.

[long-pause]

This is the hardest stretch for the animals. The water in some of the springs is bitter with alkali, and the oxen must be kept away from it. The grass is poor, and the road is rough and stony.

[pause]

Some families begin to lighten their wagons. They leave a heavy chest of drawers beside the trail, or a cast-iron stove, or a barrel of something they brought from home and never used.

[pause]

The road is lined with these things now. Later travellers will walk past them and wonder whose they were.

[long-pause]

{img:north-platte-crossing-ferry}
Near the place where the trail finally leaves the North Platte, the wagons must cross the river.

[pause]

Here it is deep and fast, and in 1846 there is no proper ferry. The men build rafts from logs, or they take the wheels off a wagon, caulk its bed with pitch, and float it across like a boat.

[pause]

It takes a long time, and everyone is careful. The oxen swim across with the men beside them, and on the far bank, the wagons are put back together, one by one.

[pause]

Then the trail turns away from the river, and climbs over a stretch of dry, empty land toward the Sweetwater.

[long-pause]

{img:independence-rock-wagons}
And then, rising out of the plain beside the Sweetwater River, there is a great rounded rock, grey and smooth, like an enormous turtle lying asleep in the grass.

[pause]

This is Independence Rock.

[pause]

It got its name many years ago, from a group of fur trappers who camped here on the Fourth of July. And the travellers have a saying about it. If you reach Independence Rock by the Fourth of July, you will reach Oregon before the snow.

[pause]

So the companies try hard to arrive here by then.

[long-pause]

{img:names-carved-on-rock}
Independence Rock is covered with names.

[pause]

Thousands of travellers have climbed onto it, and painted or carved their names and the date into the stone, with a knife, or a chisel, or with axle grease mixed with gunpowder.

[pause]

Some of the names are big and carefully made. Others are just initials, scratched quickly before the wagons moved on.

[pause]

The children love to climb it. From the top, they can see the whole valley of the Sweetwater, green along the river and brown and gold beyond, and the white wagon covers of their own company far below them, small as toys.

[long-pause]

{img:fourth-of-july-celebration}
If a company is at Independence Rock on the Fourth of July, there is a celebration.

[pause]

The men fire their rifles into the air, a flag is raised on a wagon pole, and someone makes a speech about America, and about the new country in the west that will one day be part of it.

[pause]

There is a special supper, with whatever can be found: fresh antelope, a few dried peaches, perhaps a cake made with the last of the sugar. A bottle that has been carefully saved for two months is opened and shared.

[pause]

In the evening, there is music and dancing in the circle of wagons, and the young people stay up late.

[long-pause]

{img:devils-gate-sweetwater}
A few miles beyond Independence Rock, the Sweetwater River does something surprising.

[pause]

Instead of going around a ridge of granite, it cuts straight through it, in a narrow gorge with walls three hundred feet high. The gap is called Devil's Gate.

[pause]

The wagons cannot go through it. The trail goes around, through a low pass just to the south. But many of the travellers walk over to look at it, and stand at the edge of the gorge, watching the clear water rushing between the rocks far below.

[long-pause]

From here, the trail follows the Sweetwater River westward, crossing it again and again, for more than a week.

[pause]

The water is cold and clear and good, the best water since Missouri. The valley is green and narrow, and on both sides the hills rise higher and higher.

[pause]

And far ahead, to the northwest, on the clearest mornings, there is a line of white along the horizon. It is snow, lying on the high peaks of the Wind River Mountains.

[long-pause]

{img:sweetwater-valley-snowy-peaks}
For weeks now, the travellers have been climbing.

[pause]

They have hardly noticed it, because the slope is so gentle. But the air has grown thinner and cooler, the sky has become a deeper blue, and the nights are very cold.

[pause]

The Sweetwater River grows smaller and smaller, until it is only a little stream that a child could jump across.

[long-pause]

Many people imagine that crossing the Rocky Mountains will mean climbing steep, rocky passes between high peaks. They have been dreading it for the whole journey.

[pause]

But the trail has found a secret.

[pause]

Here, in the middle of the mountains, there is a wide, open saddle of land, twenty miles across, where the ground rises so gradually that a wagon can roll over it without any difficulty at all.

[pause]

It was found by fur trappers, more than twenty years ago, and it is called South Pass.

[long-pause]

{img:south-pass-open-plateau}
The wagons climb slowly up onto the pass on a cold, bright morning.

[pause]

It does not look like a mountain pass at all. It looks like a high, wide meadow, with grey sage and short grass stretching away on every side, and the snowy peaks of the Wind River Mountains standing far off to the north.

[pause]

The wind blows steadily across it. There are no trees.

[pause]

Many of the travellers cannot believe that this is really the summit of the Rockies. They keep looking ahead, expecting the real climb to begin.

[long-pause]

But it is the summit. Somewhere on this wide plateau, without any sign to mark it, the wagons cross the Continental Divide.

[pause]

Behind them, all the rivers flow east, toward the Missouri and the Mississippi and the Gulf of Mexico. Ahead of them, all the rivers flow west, toward the Pacific Ocean.

[pause]

When the travellers understand this, they are quietly moved. They have come almost a thousand miles from Independence. They have left the waters of home behind.

[long-pause]

{img:pacific-springs-meadow}
A few miles beyond the summit, there is a marshy meadow with a cold spring, where the water seeps out of the ground and begins to flow west.

[pause]

The travellers call it Pacific Springs. The water here, they say to each other, will eventually reach the Pacific Ocean, just as they hope to do.

[pause]

Some of them kneel down and drink from it, and some fill a bottle to keep.

[pause]

The children run around the edge of the marsh, trying to find the exact place where the water begins.

[long-pause]

That evening, the company camps beside the spring. It is cold, and the fires are built up high.

[pause]

Around the fires, people talk about how far they have come, and someone says that they are nearly halfway to Oregon. It is true, more or less. But they also know that the second half will be harder than the first.

[pause]

The oxen are thinner now. The wagons are battered. The supplies of flour and bacon have grown smaller. And the season is moving on.

[long-pause]

{img:little-sandy-camp-dusk}
West of South Pass, the land is high and dry and empty. The trail runs across a wide sage plain, broken by low bluffs and dry stream beds.

[pause]

There are a few small rivers here, the Dry Sandy, the Little Sandy, the Big Sandy, with a little grass and a few willows along their banks, and the companies camp beside them, one after another.

[pause]

Somewhere along this stretch, the trail divides. One branch goes straight on to the west, across forty miles of desert without water, a shortcut that saves several days but is very hard on the oxen.

[pause]

The other branch turns south, toward a trading post in the valley of the Green River.

[long-pause]

{img:green-river-crossing}
Most companies in 1846 take the longer, southern road.

[pause]

It brings them down to the Green River, a beautiful, wide river of clear green water, flowing between banks of cottonwoods and willows.

[pause]

It is too deep to cross on foot, and once again the wagons are floated across, one by one, with ropes and rafts and a great deal of shouting.

[pause]

On the far side, the trail turns southwest, toward a small fort at the foot of the Uinta Mountains, where a famous old mountain man is waiting to trade with them.

[long-pause]

{img:fort-bridger-log-post}
Fort Bridger is not really a fort. It is a small group of log cabins, surrounded by a log fence, standing in a green valley where several mountain streams come together.

[pause]

It was built three years ago by Jim Bridger, one of the best-known of the old fur trappers. He has spent more than twenty years in these mountains, and he knows them better than almost anyone alive.

[pause]

He is in his early forties now. He cannot read or write, but he can draw a map of the whole West in the dust with a stick, and tell you where every river goes.

[long-pause]

At Fort Bridger, the travellers can rest their animals for a few days on the good grass by the streams. The blacksmith repairs their wagons. They can trade their worn-out oxen for fresh ones, at a price.

[pause]

The Shoshone people come here to trade too, and their lodges stand along the creeks near the fort, with horses grazing all around them.

[pause]

It is a pleasant, quiet place. Many of the travellers stay longer than they meant to.

[long-pause]

{img:two-roads-wagons-dividing}
It is here, or a little before, that the companies going to California and the companies going to Oregon say goodbye to each other.

[pause]

For more than a thousand miles they have travelled the same road. They have camped side by side, helped each other across rivers, shared their supper and their music.

[pause]

Now the Oregon families will turn north, toward Fort Hall and the Snake River. The California families will go on toward the southwest.

[pause]

There are many farewells. People who were strangers in Missouri have become close friends, and they know they will probably never see each other again.

[long-pause]

This summer, there is a new idea being talked about around the fires.

[pause]

A man named Lansford Hastings has written a book, praising California, and he has described a new shortcut. Instead of going the long way north and around, he says, the wagons could go straight west from Fort Bridger, through the mountains and across the great salt desert, and save several hundred miles.

[pause]

Hastings himself has ridden the route on horseback. But no wagons have ever travelled it.

[long-pause]

{img:reed-donner-wagons-turning}
At Fort Bridger, at the end of July, the Reed and Donner families have to decide which way to go.

[pause]

Some people on the trail have warned them against the new route. But they are tired, and the shortcut sounds good, and they are told that Hastings himself will guide them.

[pause]

They choose the shortcut.

[pause]

On the last day of July, their wagons roll out of Fort Bridger toward the southwest, into the Wasatch Mountains. Their journey will be much longer and harder than they expect, and we will hear a little more about it later.

[long-pause]

{img:bear-river-valley-wagons}
The Oregon families, meanwhile, turn northwest.

[pause]

The trail leads them over a range of hills and down into the valley of the Bear River, one of the loveliest places on the whole journey.

[pause]

The valley is wide and green, with tall grass up to the oxen's shoulders, and clear streams running down from the hills, full of trout. There are wild currants and gooseberries along the creeks, and the women and children pick them by the bucketful.

[pause]

For a few days, the travelling is easy, and everyone is cheerful.

[long-pause]

{img:soda-springs-bubbling}
Farther along the Bear River, the travellers come to a remarkable place.

[pause]

Here, the ground is full of springs, and the water that bubbles out of them is fizzy, like soda water. Some springs are cool and taste a little of iron. Others are warm.

[pause]

The travellers call the place Soda Springs. They mix the water with a little sugar and a few drops of vinegar, and drink it like lemonade.

[pause]

One spring, close beside the river, makes a strange, regular puffing sound, like a small steam engine. They call it Steamboat Spring.

[pause]

The children love it here. They sit by the springs and drink until they feel quite sick, and then they go and drink some more.

[long-pause]

{img:fort-hall-white-walls}
In the middle of August, the Oregon wagons arrive at Fort Hall.

[pause]

It stands on a wide, flat plain beside the Snake River, a square of whitewashed walls with a flag flying over the gate. The flag is British, because Fort Hall belongs to the Hudson's Bay Company.

[pause]

The man in charge is a big, friendly Scotsman named Richard Grant. He is polite and helpful to the American travellers, and he sells them flour and sugar, at high prices, because everything here has been carried a very long way.

[long-pause]

Grant has seen many wagons pass by in the last few years. Not so long ago, he used to tell travellers that wagons could not get any farther than Fort Hall, and that they should leave them here and go on with pack horses.

[pause]

But the emigrants did not listen, and they took their wagons on anyway, and now there is a rough road all the way to the Columbia River.

[pause]

The families rest for a day or two, mend their wagons again, and then set out along the Snake River, heading west.

[long-pause]

{img:snake-river-canyon-sagebrush}
The Snake River is nothing like the Platte.

[pause]

It runs through a great dark plain of old lava rock, and in many places, it flows at the bottom of a deep canyon, with black cliffs on either side.

[pause]

The trail follows along the top, high above the river. The travellers can see the water far below them, blue and white and full of fish, but they often cannot reach it. Some days, the oxen have to go all day without a drink, though the river is right there, at the bottom of the cliffs.

[pause]

The road is dusty and stony. The sun is hot. The sagebrush goes on and on.

[long-pause]

{img:shoshone-salmon-fishing-falls}
But there are wonderful places along the Snake as well.

[pause]

At a place called Salmon Falls, the river tumbles over a series of rocky ledges, and here the Shoshone people have their summer fishing camps.

[pause]

They stand on the rocks with long spears and nets, catching the great silver salmon as they leap up through the white water. Along the bank, the fish are split and hung on wooden racks to dry in the sun.

[pause]

The travellers stop here to trade. A shirt, a needle and thread, or a little powder will buy a fine salmon. That evening, every fire in the camp has fresh fish roasting over it, and the whole valley smells of wood smoke and cooking.

[long-pause]

{img:springs-pouring-from-cliffs}
Not far from Salmon Falls, there is another sight that everyone remembers.

[pause]

Out of the black cliffs on the far side of the canyon, dozens of springs come pouring straight out of the solid rock, in white streams and falls, down into the river below.

[pause]

The water has travelled underground for many miles, through the old lava, and here it suddenly bursts out into the light.

[pause]

The travellers stand at the edge of the canyon and watch it for a long time. The sound of falling water fills the air, and there are small rainbows in the spray.

[long-pause]

{img:three-island-crossing-river}
Farther down the river, the trail comes to Three Island Crossing.

[pause]

Here, the Snake River spreads out around a chain of small islands, and it is possible to cross it, from island to island, to the north bank, where the road is shorter and there is more grass.

[pause]

It is a difficult crossing. The water is deep and fast between the islands, and the wagons are pulled across by teams of oxen chained together, with men on horseback alongside them.

[pause]

Some companies decide that it is too dangerous, and stay on the south side, following the long, dry road around.

[long-pause]

{img:fort-boise-river-evening}
Beyond the crossing, the land becomes a little kinder. There are warm springs, where the women can wash clothes in hot water for the first time in months. There is a little more grass.

[pause]

And at the end of August, or early in September, the wagons come to Fort Boise, another small Hudson's Bay Company post, at the place where the Boise River flows into the Snake.

[pause]

The man in charge here is a Frenchman named François Payette. He keeps a garden, and sometimes he has fresh vegetables to sell, which the travellers have not tasted for months.

[pause]

Here the trail crosses the Snake River one last time, and turns away from it, toward the mountains of Oregon.

[long-pause]

{img:worn-wagon-repairs-camp}
By the time the wagons reach the Snake River, they have been on the road for more than three months, and everything is wearing out.

[pause]

The wagon covers are stained and patched. The wooden wheels have shrunk in the dry air, so that the iron tyres grow loose, and rattle, and sometimes roll right off.

[pause]

So in the evenings, there is always something to mend.

[long-pause]

When the tyres come loose, the men take the wheels off and soak them overnight in the river, so that the wood swells again and fits tightly. Or they drive thin wedges of wood between the tyre and the rim.

[pause]

A broken axle is a more serious matter. If the company is lucky, there is a spare one, carried under the wagon bed since Missouri. If not, the men must search for a suitable tree, which can be very hard in country where there are no trees at all.

[pause]

There are men in every company who turn out to be very good at this kind of work. They become known as the people to go to, and they are busy every evening.

[long-pause]

{img:mending-clothes-by-lantern}
The clothes are wearing out too.

[pause]

The men's trousers are patched at the knees, and patched again. The boots are coming apart, and are mended with rawhide and pegs. Some of the travellers, by the end, are walking in moccasins bought from the Shoshone or the Lakota, which they find softer and more comfortable than their boots.

[pause]

The women's skirts are shorter now, cut off at the hem, because the long ones dragged in the dust and the mud.

[pause]

In the evenings, by the light of a lantern, the mending goes on, stitch after stitch, while the children sleep.

[long-pause]

{img:trail-guidebook-reading}
Many of the travellers carry a little guidebook with them.

[pause]

There are only a few of these books so far. Some families carry the report of John Frémont, an army explorer who travelled the route a few years ago and described it day by day. Others carry the book written by Lansford Hastings, or notes copied by hand from a letter.

[pause]

The guides list the camping places, the springs and the river crossings, and how many miles lie between them.

[pause]

In the evenings, the men sit by the fire with the book open on their knees, working out how far it is to the next good water, and how long it will take.

[long-pause]

The guides are not always right. Rivers change their course, springs dry up, good grass is eaten by the companies in front.

[pause]

So the travellers also learn to rely on what they hear from others.

[pause]

They read the notes left for them along the trail, written on pieces of paper tucked into the cracks of rocks, or on buffalo skulls by the side of the road. Good grass two miles to the left. Water at the foot of the hill. The Smith family passed here on the ninth, all well.

[pause]

The trail is a kind of long, slow letter, with each company writing a few lines for the ones that follow.

[long-pause]

{img:trading-with-shoshone-camp}
And all along the way, there is trading.

[pause]

At the forts and the trading posts, the travellers trade with the clerks of the fur companies. Along the rivers, they trade with the people who live there: the Lakota, the Shoshone, the Cayuse and the Nez Perce.

[pause]

The things that are most wanted are often surprising. A cotton shirt is worth a great deal. So are needles, fishhooks, buttons, ribbons, and small mirrors. A good shirt might buy a horse, or a large salmon, or a sack of camas roots, or a pair of beautifully made moccasins.

[pause]

The travellers learn a few words of the local languages, and of the trade language that is used across the Northwest, and the rest is done with smiles and gestures, and holding things up for the other person to see.

[long-pause]

Very often, the people who live along the trail are helpful to the travellers. They show them the best places to cross the rivers, help them to find lost cattle, and guide them over the mountains.

[pause]

Many of the diaries record a kindness of this kind, sometimes just in a single sentence. A man of the Shoshone helped us across the river today, and would take nothing for it but a little tobacco.

[long-pause]

{img:campfire-stories-old-trapper}
Sometimes, at a camp along the Snake, an old trapper or a guide rides in and sits down by the fire.

[pause]

He is weather-beaten, with a long beard and clothes made of buckskin, and he has spent most of his life in the mountains, and has seen the country before any wagons came.

[pause]

The children sit around him in a circle as he tells his stories, about the winters of years ago, and the beaver streams, and the great gatherings of trappers in the mountains, which are now nearly gone.

[pause]

The fire burns down. The stories go on late into the night.

[pause]

And in the morning, he rides off again, alone, into the hills, and the wagons roll on toward Oregon.

[long-pause]

{img:grande-ronde-valley}
After Fort Boise, the trail climbs again, over dry hills and along small rivers, until one evening the wagons come over a ridge and see something they have almost forgotten.

[pause]

Below them lies a round green valley, ringed by mountains, with a river winding through it and tall grass waving in the wind. Along the edges of the valley, there are pine trees.

[pause]

It is called the Grande Ronde, and after the sagebrush of the Snake River plain, it looks to the travellers like a garden.

[long-pause]

The Cayuse and Nez Perce peoples come to this valley every year, to dig the camas roots that grow in its wet meadows, and to graze their horses, which are famous for their strength and beauty.

[pause]

They are good traders, and the travellers are glad to exchange clothing and tools for fresh vegetables, potatoes, and sometimes a good horse.

[pause]

The wagons rest for a day or two. The oxen eat their fill of grass, for what feels like the first time in weeks.

[long-pause]

{img:blue-mountains-pine-forest}
And then comes the climb over the Blue Mountains.

[pause]

This is the first real forest the travellers have seen since Missouri. Tall pines and firs grow thick on the steep slopes, and the air is cool and smells of resin.

[pause]

The road is very hard. It goes straight up the side of the mountains, and the oxen have to be doubled up, two teams to a wagon, to pull it to the top. Going down, the wheels are chained so that they cannot turn, and the wagons slide down the slopes, held back by ropes tied around the trees.

[pause]

In some places, the men have to cut a path through the fallen timber with their axes.

[long-pause]

But even here, there are moments of great beauty.

[pause]

At the top, in the clearings, the families can look out over range after range of blue, forested mountains, fading away into the distance. The evenings are cold, and there is plenty of firewood at last, and the campfires are the biggest of the whole journey.

[pause]

The children gather pine cones. The smell of the forest, after months of dust and sage, makes many of the travellers think of home.

[long-pause]

{img:whitman-mission-house-orchard}
On the far side of the Blue Mountains, in a broad valley near the Walla Walla River, there is a mission.

[pause]

It was founded ten years ago by a doctor named Marcus Whitman and his wife Narcissa, who came here from New York to live among the Cayuse people and teach them.

[pause]

The mission is a cluster of adobe buildings, with a mill, a blacksmith shop, fields of wheat and corn, and an orchard of young apple trees. For many tired travellers, it is the first real farm they have seen since Missouri.

[pause]

Dr Whitman sells them flour and potatoes, and some families who are too ill or too worn out to go on stay here for a few weeks to rest.

[long-pause]

{img:columbia-river-gorge-cliffs}
From the Whitman mission, the trail leads west, down to the Columbia River.

[pause]

This is the great river of the West, which the travellers have heard about for their whole journey. It is wide and powerful, and it flows through a deep gorge between dark cliffs of basalt, carved by the river over countless years.

[pause]

On the dry hills above it, there are no trees at all. The wind blows hard, always from the west, raising the sand into little clouds.

[pause]

The wagons follow the river along its southern bank, through the dust and the wind, for many days.

[long-pause]

{img:the-dalles-mission-camp}
In the middle of September, the first wagons reach a place called The Dalles.

[pause]

Here, the Columbia rushes through a series of narrow channels between walls of rock, in a great roar of white water. It is one of the most famous fishing places in all of the West, and for thousands of years, people have come here from many miles around to catch salmon and to trade.

[pause]

There is a small Methodist mission here too, on a hill above the river.

[pause]

And here, the road comes to an end. Ahead lie the Cascade Mountains, covered in thick forest, and the Columbia cuts straight through them in a canyon where no wagon can go.

[pause]

The travellers have one last decision to make. How will they get to the Willamette Valley, which lies on the other side?

[long-pause]

{img:rafts-on-columbia-river}
Until this year, there has been only one way to go on from The Dalles. And that is by water.

[pause]

The families take their wagons apart. They build rafts out of logs, or they hire canoes and boats from the local people or the Hudson's Bay Company, and they load everything they own onto them.

[pause]

Then they drift down the Columbia River, through the gorge, into the heart of the Cascade Mountains.

[long-pause]

It is one of the most beautiful parts of the whole journey. The river runs between enormous cliffs, covered with dark green forest, with waterfalls pouring down them from hundreds of feet above.

[pause]

Mist hangs over the water in the mornings. The air is soft and damp, and smells of moss and rain, quite different from the dry plains.

[pause]

But it is also cold and slow and expensive, and the river has rapids that must be carried around. And the cattle cannot travel on the rafts. They have to be driven along a narrow trail over the mountains beside the river.

[long-pause]

{img:sam-barlow-forest-road}
Last year, a farmer named Samuel Barlow decided that there must be a better way.

[pause]

He was in his fifties, a stubborn and practical man from Kentucky, and when he reached The Dalles in the autumn of 1845, he said that God never made a mountain without a way over it, and he set off south, around the side of Mount Hood, with a few wagons, to look for one.

[pause]

He did not quite make it before the snow came. The families had to leave their wagons in the mountains for the winter and walk out.

[pause]

But now, in the summer of 1846, Barlow has come back with his men. They have cut a narrow road through the forest, around the southern side of Mount Hood, and down the other side into the Willamette Valley. It is the first wagon road over the Cascades.

[pause]

He charges five dollars a wagon to use it, and ten cents for each animal.

[long-pause]

{img:mount-hood-above-forest}
So this year, for the first time, many families do not have to leave their wagons at The Dalles. They pay their toll at a little gate in the forest and drive on.

[pause]

The road climbs through tall forests of fir and cedar. The trees are enormous, far bigger than any the travellers have ever seen, with trunks so wide that three people holding hands cannot reach around them.

[pause]

Above them, through gaps in the forest, stands Mount Hood, white and perfect, with its snow shining in the sun.

[pause]

At night, they camp in small meadows high in the mountains, with the stars above them and the great dark forest all around.

[long-pause]

{img:laurel-hill-steep-descent}
The worst part of the Barlow Road comes near the end.

[pause]

At a place called Laurel Hill, the road goes down a slope so steep that the wagons cannot simply be driven. The men tie ropes around them, wrap the ropes around the trunks of trees, and let the wagons down slowly, a little at a time.

[pause]

Some families cut down a whole tree and tie it to the back of the wagon, so that it drags behind and acts as a brake.

[pause]

It takes all day for a company to get down the hill. But at the bottom, the worst is over.

[long-pause]

{img:willamette-valley-first-view}
And then, one day in the autumn, the forest opens up, and the travellers come down into the valley they have been walking toward since April.

[pause]

The Willamette Valley lies in front of them, wide and green, with a broad river winding through it. There are oak trees standing in the grassy meadows, and forests of fir along the hills. Here and there, smoke rises from the chimney of a log cabin.

[pause]

It is raining softly. The clouds hang low over the hills.

[pause]

For a long moment, the families simply stand and look at it.

[long-pause]

{img:oregon-city-falls-cabins}
At the northern end of the valley, beside a great waterfall on the Willamette River, there is a little town called Oregon City.

[pause]

It has perhaps a few hundred people, a few streets of wooden houses, two churches, some stores and a sawmill and a flour mill beside the falls. There is even a newspaper, which started printing this year.

[pause]

To the travellers, after five months on the trail, it looks like a city.

[pause]

They have walked two thousand miles. They have crossed the plains, the mountains and the deserts. Their clothes are worn out, their wagons are broken, and their oxen are thin.

[pause]

But they are here.

[long-pause]

{img:applegate-party-southern-road}
Not everyone reaches the valley so easily this year.

[pause]

Tabitha Brown, the sixty-six-year-old widow from Massachusetts, and her family are travelling with a group of wagons that left the main trail at Fort Hall, to try another new route.

[long-pause]

That summer, a few men from the Willamette Valley, led by two brothers named Jesse and Lindsay Applegate, have ridden east to look for a southern road into Oregon. They hope it will avoid the dangerous river journey down the Columbia.

[pause]

They found a way, of sorts. It leads southwest from the Snake River, across the deserts of what is now Nevada, through the mountains, and then north, up the long valleys of southern Oregon, into the Willamette Valley from the south.

[pause]

They meet the wagons at Fort Hall, and some of the families decide to follow them.

[long-pause]

{img:desert-wagons-dusty-autumn}
The new road is much longer and harder than anyone expected.

[pause]

The wagons cross long stretches of desert, with very little water and grass. The oxen grow weak. In the mountains of southern Oregon, there are rivers to cross and steep canyons to climb through, with no proper road at all.

[pause]

The autumn comes. And then, in the forests, the rain begins.

[pause]

It rains day after day. The road turns into deep mud. The wagons sink up to their axles, and some of them have to be left behind.

[long-pause]

Mrs Brown later wrote a long letter to her family back east, describing this part of the journey. She wrote it calmly, the way she did everything.

[pause]

She tells how her wagon had to be abandoned, and how she rode on horseback through the forests, with her old brother-in-law, Captain Brown, beside her.

[pause]

One evening, the two of them fell behind the others. Night came, in the rain, in the forest, and they were alone.

[long-pause]

{img:night-in-rain-forest-fire}
The old captain was very tired, and not entirely clear in his mind. So Mrs Brown took charge.

[pause]

She found a sheltered place under the trees. She got down from her horse and helped him down from his. She made a little shelter with a saddle blanket, and wrapped him up as warmly as she could.

[pause]

Then she sat beside him through the night, in the dark and the rain, listening to the drops falling from the trees, and waited for the morning.

[pause]

In her letter, she wrote that she was not afraid. She simply waited, and thought, and prayed.

[long-pause]

{img:morning-forest-reunion}
At first light, she got the captain back onto his horse, and they rode on along the trail.

[pause]

After a while, they smelled wood smoke. Then they heard voices, and came around a bend, and there was the camp, with the rest of the family, and the fires burning.

[pause]

They had been only a short way behind all along.

[pause]

Everyone was very glad to see them. And the next morning, the slow journey went on again, through the rain and the mud, toward the north.

[long-pause]

{img:salem-cabin-christmas-rain}
Tabitha Brown reached the Willamette Valley on Christmas Day, 1846, at a little settlement called Salem.

[pause]

She had been travelling for nearly nine months. She was sixty-six years old, and had almost nothing left. Her wagon and most of her belongings were gone.

[pause]

A Methodist minister's family took her in, and gave her a warm place by their fire.

[long-pause]

{img:picayune-coin-in-glove}
In her letter, she tells a little story about that first winter.

[pause]

One day, she put her hand into the finger of an old glove, and found a single small coin. It was a picayune, worth about six and a quarter cents. It was all the money she had in the world.

[pause]

She used it to buy three needles. She traded some of her old clothes for some cloth, and began to make gloves, and sell them to the settlers, and slowly, she began to earn a living again.

[pause]

By the spring, she had earned enough to look after herself again, and she was very proud of it.

[long-pause]

But she was not finished.

[pause]

In the town of Forest Grove, a little to the north, she saw that there were many children whose parents had died on the trail, or who had no one to look after them. So she took them in.

[pause]

She gave them a home, and cooked for them, and taught them to read and write, the way she had taught school as a young widow back east.

[pause]

Her little home for orphans grew into a school. And the school grew into a college, which is still there today, more than a hundred and seventy years later.

[pause]

In Oregon today, Tabitha Brown is remembered as the mother of Oregon.

[long-pause]

{img:choosing-land-claim-meadow}
For the families who have arrived in the valley, the first thing to do is to find land.

[pause]

There is no government office here yet to hand it out. Instead, the settlers have made their own rules. A married couple may claim six hundred and forty acres, a whole square mile of land, as long as they live on it and farm it.

[pause]

So the men ride out across the valley, looking for a good place. They want a stream for water, a stand of trees for timber and firewood, and a stretch of open prairie for the plough.

[pause]

When they find it, they mark the corners with piles of stones or blazes cut into the trees, and write a short description in the claims book at Oregon City.

[long-pause]

{img:building-log-cabin-autumn}
Then they build a cabin.

[pause]

The neighbours come to help, as they do back home. The trees are cut and trimmed, notched at the ends, and laid one on top of another, until the walls are high enough. The gaps between the logs are filled with mud and moss.

[pause]

The roof is made of split cedar boards. There is a chimney of sticks and clay at one end, and a door made of planks, hung on wooden hinges.

[pause]

There is usually only one room, with a dirt floor, and one small window covered with oiled paper.

[pause]

But it is dry, and it is warm, and after five months of sleeping in a wagon, it feels like a palace.

[long-pause]

{img:winter-rain-cabin-fireside}
The first winter is wet.

[pause]

In the Willamette Valley, it does not snow very much, but it rains, softly and steadily, for weeks on end. The clouds lie low over the hills, and the creeks run full.

[pause]

The families spend the dark evenings by the fire, mending clothes and making furniture, a table, a bench, a bedstead with a mattress stuffed with straw or dry grass.

[pause]

Food is short in this first winter, because there was no time to plant a crop. They live on wheat bought from the older settlers, on salmon and venison, on potatoes and peas, and on whatever is left in the wagon.

[long-pause]

During that winter, a piece of news arrives that the settlers have been waiting for.

[pause]

Back in June, far away in Washington and London, the governments of the United States and Britain have signed a treaty. The border between them has been drawn along the forty-ninth parallel, from the Rocky Mountains to the sea.

[pause]

Everything south of that line, including the Willamette Valley, now belongs to the United States.

[pause]

The news has travelled very slowly. But when it arrives, the settlers are delighted. The country they walked to is now, officially, part of their own.

[long-pause]

{img:snowy-sierra-mountains-distance}
Far to the south, in the high mountains of California, the Reed and Donner families are spending the same winter in a very different way.

[pause]

The shortcut through the mountains turned out to be much slower than they had been told. They had to cut their own road through thick forest, and cross a great salt desert, and they lost many weeks.

[pause]

By the time they reached the last mountains before California, at the end of October, it was too late. The snow came early that year, and it closed the pass in front of them.

[pause]

They were trapped there for the whole winter, in cabins and tents beside a frozen lake, while rescue parties struggled to reach them from the valley below.

[long-pause]

It was a very hard winter, and many of them did not survive it. It became one of the saddest stories of the whole western trail.

[pause]

But some did come through. In the spring, Virginia Reed, who had ridden her pony so happily beside the wagons in Missouri, reached the warm valleys of California with her family.

[pause]

Not long afterwards, she wrote a long letter to her cousin back home, telling the whole story. At the end of it, she gave some simple advice for anyone thinking of making the journey.

[pause]

Never take a cutoff, she wrote, and hurry along as fast as you can.

[long-pause]

{img:spring-ploughing-new-farm}
In the Willamette Valley, the spring comes early, and it comes softly.

[pause]

By February, the grass is green again. By March, the first wildflowers are blooming in the meadows, and the oak trees are putting out their leaves.

[pause]

The men hitch up the same oxen that pulled the wagons across the continent, and plough the first furrows in the deep, dark soil. The women plant gardens beside the cabins, with seeds they carried all the way from home, wrapped in little paper packets.

[pause]

Peas, beans, onions, cabbages. And, in many gardens, a few small apple trees, grown from seeds or cuttings brought over the mountains.

[long-pause]

{img:summer-wheat-harvest-valley}
That summer, the first harvest is good.

[pause]

The wheat stands tall and golden. The gardens are full. The cattle are fat again on the valley grass.

[pause]

In the evenings, the families sit outside their cabins and look out over the fields, toward the blue hills to the west, and the white cone of Mount Hood far to the east, catching the last of the sunlight.

[pause]

And somewhere back along the trail, two thousand miles away, a new year's wagons are already rolling west across the prairie.

[long-pause]

{img:many-wagons-later-years}
In the years that followed, the trail grew busier and busier.

[pause]

In 1846, perhaps a thousand or so people made the journey to Oregon. A few years later, when gold was found in California, tens of thousands came along the same road every summer.

[pause]

Over about twenty-five years, something like three or four hundred thousand people walked west along the Oregon Trail and the trails that branched from it.

[long-pause]

Fort Laramie was bought by the army in 1849 and became a real military fort. Fort Kearny was built on the Platte. Ferries were built at the river crossings, and bridges, and trading posts sprang up along the road.

[pause]

Francis Parkman went home to Boston at the end of 1846, ill and exhausted, but full of what he had seen. His account of the journey was published as a book, which he called The Oregon Trail. It is still in print today.

[pause]

He went on to become one of the most famous historians in America, writing many books about the early history of the continent.

[long-pause]

{img:railroad-across-plains}
Then, in 1869, the railroad was finished across the continent.

[pause]

Now, a family could travel from the Missouri River to the Pacific coast in about a week, sitting comfortably in a railway carriage, looking out of the window at the plains and the mountains.

[pause]

Slowly, the wagons stopped coming. The trail grew quiet. The grass grew back over the road in many places.

[pause]

But not everywhere.

[long-pause]

{img:deep-wagon-ruts-sandstone}
Near the town of Guernsey, in Wyoming, not far from Fort Laramie, the trail crosses a ridge of soft sandstone.

[pause]

Here, the iron wheels of tens of thousands of wagons wore a deep groove into the rock, in some places almost as deep as a person is tall.

[pause]

The ruts are still there today. Visitors can walk along the bottom of them, between the walls of pale stone, and put their hands on the marks left by the wheels.

[pause]

In many other places too, across the plains and the mountains, the two faint lines of the old wagon road can still be seen, running through the sagebrush, if you know where to look.

[long-pause]

{img:independence-rock-today}
Independence Rock is still there, beside the Sweetwater River, and many of the old names are still on it, though the wind and the weather have worn some of them away.

[pause]

Chimney Rock still stands, a little shorter than it was. Scotts Bluff is a national monument. The walls of Fort Laramie have been restored, and visitors can walk through its rooms.

[pause]

At South Pass, the wide, empty plateau looks almost exactly as it did in 1846. There are no towns there even now, only the wind, the sage, and the far-off snowy mountains.

[long-pause]

{img:oregon-farm-valley-today}
And in the Willamette Valley, the farms are still there.

[pause]

Many of the families who live there today are descended from the people who came over the trail in those years. Some of them still own the same land that their great-great-grandparents claimed, and some still have a chest, or a clock, or a quilt that travelled across the continent in a covered wagon.

[pause]

In Forest Grove, the college that grew out of Tabitha Brown's little school still holds its classes. And on the Barlow Road, high on the slopes of Mount Hood, it is still possible to walk part of the old route through the forest.

[long-pause]

{img:wagon-sunset-prairie-slot}
The journey along the Oregon Trail took about five months. It was long and slow and often very hard.

[pause]

But for most of the people who made it, it was simply a part of life. They walked, and cooked, and washed, and sang around the fires. They watched the land change slowly around them, day after day, from the green prairie to the mountains to the forests of the Pacific.

[pause]

And when they reached the end, they built a home, and planted a garden, and began again.

[long-pause]

That is where we will leave them tonight, in a green valley in Oregon, under the soft grey rain, with the smoke rising from the chimneys.

[pause]

If you enjoyed this journey, you are very welcome to subscribe to the channel, so that you can find the next one.

[pause]

There should be another quiet story waiting on the screen now, if you are still awake.

[pause]

Sleep well, and good night.
