Saint Benedict wrote that the care of the sick must come before everything else, and above everything else, so that they are truly served, as if they were Christ himself.

So every monastery has an infirmary: a separate building, for the sick, and the old, and the weak, with its own little kitchen, and chapel, and garden, and its own monk in charge, the infirmarian.

--

At Rievaulx, the infirmary stands to the east of the cloister, in a quiet place, away from the noise of the workshops. It is a long, warm hall, with beds along the walls, and a great fireplace, where a fire burns all day, in winter. It is the only place in the monastery, besides the warming house and the kitchen, where there is a fire.

In the infirmary, the rules are gentler. The sick monks may eat meat, to make them strong again. They may sleep longer, and miss the night office. They may talk a little. They are looked after with great care.

{img:infirmarian-monk-giving-warm-drink-sick-brother-bed}
The old monks, who are too weak to keep the full life of the monastery, live in the infirmary too, in their last years, and pray, and rest, and are cared for by their brothers, until they pass away, peacefully, surrounded by the community, singing psalms around their beds.

--

The infirmarian knows a great deal about medicine.

He has books of medicine, copied from the old Greek and Roman doctors, and from the Arabic doctors, which are just beginning to be translated into Latin in these years. He knows about herbs, and their powers, and grows them in the infirmary garden.

In the herb garden, there are neat little beds, each with a different plant: sage, for sore throats. Mint, for the stomach. Rue, and fennel, and lovage, and rosemary, and lavender, and camomile, and feverfew, for headaches. Comfrey, which the monks call knitbone, for healing broken bones and wounds. Marigolds, and lilies, and roses, for medicines, and for beauty.

--

The infirmarian makes medicines, from the herbs: drinks, and ointments, and poultices, and syrups. He mixes them with honey, and wine, and vinegar. He prescribes rest, and warmth, and good food, and baths.

He also bleeds the monks, a few times a year, which the doctors of that time believe keeps people healthy. After the bleeding, the monks are allowed a few days of rest, in the infirmary, with better food, and a little conversation, and walks in the garden. Some monks look forward to it, as a little holiday.

{img:monks-resting-infirmary-garden-bench-sunshine-quiet}
Aelred himself spends much of his later life in pain, from his illness, and in his last years, he cannot walk, and lives in a little room, built for him, next to the infirmary. But his monks come to him there, every day, twenty or thirty at a time, and sit around him, on his bed, and on the floor, and talk with him, freely, like children with their mother, as Walter Daniel says. And he talks with them, and teaches them, and listens to them.

--

The monastery also cares for the sick of the countryside around.

Poor people who are ill come to the gate, and are given food, and medicine, and sometimes a bed, in the guest house. The monks are among the few people in the countryside who know anything about medicine.

{img:monk-at-gate-giving-medicine-poor-woman-child-morning}
And the monasteries also keep bees, for honey, which is used in medicines, and for sweetening, and for wax, for the candles in the church. The beehives, of woven straw, called skeps, stand in a row, in a sheltered corner of the garden, humming in the summer sun.
