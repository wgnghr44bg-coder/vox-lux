Let us spend a day with the lay brothers, on one of the granges of Rievaulx, high on the edge of the moors, in the spring of 1150.

The grange is a little group of buildings, of stone and timber, with thatched roofs: a long house, where the lay brothers live, with a hall, and a kitchen, and a dormitory above. A great barn, for the corn. Sheepfolds, built of stone. Stables, for the horses and the oxen. And a little chapel, where the lay brothers say their prayers.

--

There are perhaps ten or twelve lay brothers here, under the master of the grange, an older lay brother, who is in charge. And there are some hired servants, and shepherds, from the villages around, who work for the abbey for wages.

The day begins early, before dawn, with prayers, in the little chapel, said together, by heart, in the dark: the Our Father, and the Hail Mary, and the Creed, repeated many times, instead of the long psalms that the monks sing at the abbey.

Then breakfast: bread, and pottage, and ale. The lay brothers, who work hard in the fields, are allowed more food than the monks.

--

Then everyone goes out to work.

It is the lambing season. Out on the hillside, around the grange, hundreds of ewes are giving birth, and the shepherds are watching them, day and night. The lambs are born in the cold spring wind, wet and wobbly, and the shepherds help them, if they are in trouble, and carry the weak ones into the shelter of the sheepfolds, and the barn, and rub them dry, with straw, and help them to suckle.

{img:shepherd-carrying-newborn-lamb-hillside-spring-wind}
The shepherds have dogs, to help them gather the sheep, and they know every ewe in the flock, and call them by their marks. They sleep in little huts, on the hillside, during lambing, to be near the flock.

--

Other lay brothers are ploughing, in the fields below the grange, with teams of oxen, eight oxen to a plough, walking slowly, up and down the long strips of land, turning over the dark earth. A boy walks in front of the oxen, calling to them, and guiding them, with a long stick. Behind them, the gulls and the rooks follow, picking up the worms.

Others are sowing oats and barley, walking across the ploughed fields, scattering the seed from a basket, with a wide sweep of the arm, left and right, in a steady rhythm.

{img:oxen-team-ploughing-strip-field-boy-guiding-rooks}
Others are mending the stone walls, which keep the sheep out of the crops. Others are cutting wood, in the copse, and carting it to the grange, for the fires. Others are working in the forge, making and mending tools: ploughshares, and sickles, and spades, and horseshoes.

--

At midday, the lay brothers stop, and say a short prayer, in the field, wherever they are, when they hear the little bell of the grange chapel. And they eat a meal: bread, and cheese, and ale, sitting in the shelter of a wall.

Then they go back to work, until the evening.

In the evening, they come back to the grange, tired, and muddy, and wet, and wash, and eat their supper, in the hall, in silence, while one of them, who can read a little, or a monk visiting from the abbey, reads aloud. And then the evening prayers, in the chapel. And then to bed, in the dormitory, in the long, low room under the thatch, with the wind blowing outside, across the moor, and the sheep calling in the darkness.

--

On Saturday evening, the lay brothers of the granges walk back to the abbey, across the moors and down into the valley, to spend Sunday there, with the community.

They go to Mass, in the abbey church, at the back, behind the screen, and hear the monks singing. They go to the chapter, where the abbot speaks to them, in English, or in French, and gives them news, and advice, and listens to their troubles. They eat in their refectory, and sleep in their dormitory, in the west range of the cloister.

{img:lay-brothers-walking-home-across-moor-evening-sunset}
And on Monday morning, before dawn, they walk back to the granges, across the moors, to begin another week of work, among the sheep, and the fields, and the wind.
