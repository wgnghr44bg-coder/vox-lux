The monks of Rievaulx have left the world. But the world does not leave them alone.

In 1150, England is in the middle of a long and miserable civil war, which later historians will call the Anarchy.

--

When King Henry the First died, in 1135, he left no son. He wanted his daughter, Matilda, to be queen. But many of the great lords did not want a woman to rule. And so his nephew, Stephen, seized the throne.

For nearly twenty years, Stephen and Matilda, and their supporters, fight each other for the crown. Lords build castles everywhere, without permission, and rob the countryside. Villages are burned. Crops are destroyed. People go hungry.

{img:countryside-burned-fields-distance-grey-sky-quiet-village}
A monk at the abbey of Peterborough, writing the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, wrote that in those years, men said openly that Christ and his saints were asleep.

--

The north of England suffers too. In 1138, King David of Scotland, Aelred's old friend and master, invaded Yorkshire, to support Matilda, his niece, and there was a great battle, the Battle of the Standard, near Northallerton, not far from Rievaulx. Walter Espec, the founder of Rievaulx, was one of the English leaders. The Scots were defeated.

Aelred later wrote an account of the battle, with a long, sad speech for Walter Espec, and a gentle portrait of King David, whom he loved.

The monasteries, in those years, were places of peace, in a violent world. People fled to them, for safety. Lords, on both sides, respected them, mostly, and gave them land, to pay for prayers for their souls. And Walter Espec, in his old age, became a monk himself, at Rievaulx, among the white monks he had brought to the valley, and passed away there, a few years later.

--

The world comes to Rievaulx in other ways too.

There are guests: bishops, and abbots, and lords, and messengers, and travellers, and pilgrims, and merchants, who stay in the guest house, near the gate. The Rule of Saint Benedict says that all guests should be received as if they were Christ. The abbot, or a monk, welcomes them, and washes their hands, and gives them food, and a bed.

{img:guest-house-monastery-travellers-cloaks-welcomed-evening}
There are the poor, who come every day to the gate, for food. There are the people of the villages around, who work for the monastery, or sell things to it, or buy wool from it, or come to ask for help.

And there are letters, from Clairvaux, and Cîteaux, and Rome, and from other monasteries, and from friends. Every year, the abbot travels to Cîteaux, in Burgundy, for the General Chapter, a long journey, across England, and the Channel, and France, which takes weeks.

--

And in the wider world, in 1150, there is much happening.

Bernard of Clairvaux has just preached the Second Crusade, which ended in failure, in 1148. In Paris, the great new cathedral schools are growing, where teachers like Peter Abelard have taught, and which will soon become the first universities. Great new churches are being built, in a new style, with pointed arches, and tall windows, which we now call Gothic, at the abbey of Saint-Denis, near Paris, and soon in many other places.

{img:hildegard-rupertsberg-nun-writing-visions-scriptorium-rhine}
And in Germany, on the river Rhine, in the year 1150, a remarkable Benedictine nun, named Hildegard of Bingen, moves with her nuns to a new convent of her own, at Rupertsberg. She is fifty-two. She writes books about her visions, and about medicine, and plants, and composes beautiful music, which is still sung today. And she writes letters to Bernard, and to popes and emperors, who listen to her.

--

So the quiet valley of the Rye is not as far from the world as it seems.

The monks pray for the world, every day, and every night: for the king, and for peace, and for the sick, and the poor, and the dead, and for all people. That, they believe, is their most important work.
