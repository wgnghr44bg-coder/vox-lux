One of the most remarkable things about a Cistercian monastery is the silence.

The monks of Rievaulx do not speak to each other, except when it is absolutely necessary. Not in the church, except to sing and pray. Not in the cloister. Not in the refectory, at meals. Not in the dormitory. Not at work. And never during the Great Silence, from Compline in the evening until the morning.

--

Saint Benedict wrote that monks should love silence, and should speak rarely, and only of useful things, and gently, and humbly, without laughing, and without many words. He wrote that in much speaking, you cannot avoid sin.

The Cistercians follow this very strictly. They believe that silence helps the monks to listen: to God, and to their own hearts. In the silence, they can pray all the time, even while they work.

{img:monks-hoeing-garden-rows-silent-misty-morning-valley}
A monk who wants to speak to the abbot, or to one of the officials of the monastery, about something necessary, may do so, briefly, in a quiet voice, in the parlour, or in a special place. But otherwise, there is no conversation.

--

So how do the monks communicate?

They use signs. Over the centuries, the monks of Europe developed a sign language, with hundreds of signs, made with the hands, so that they could say simple things to each other, without breaking the silence.

There is a sign for bread: you make a circle with your thumbs and forefingers, because the loaves are round. A sign for fish: you move your hand like a fish's tail, swimming in the water. A sign for milk: you put your little finger to your lips, like a baby sucking. A sign for honey: you put your tongue out a little, and touch it with your fingers. A sign for a book: you move your hand as if turning a page. A sign for the abbot: you touch your forehead, and pull your fingers away, as if pulling a hood.

--

Lists of these signs were written down, in the monasteries, so that new monks could learn them. Some of these lists still survive. One, from an English monastery, has more than a hundred and twenty signs.

The signs are only for necessary things: pass me the bread, please. Where is the key? The abbot wants you. It is not supposed to be used for chatting. But of course, some monks, being human, did use the signs to chat, and to joke, and the abbots complained about it.

{img:refectory-monks-eating-silently-one-reading-pulpit-lamp}
The Cistercian monasteries were so quiet that visitors were amazed. A writer of the time describes walking into a Cistercian abbey, with hundreds of monks and lay brothers, at work, all over the valley, and hearing nothing but the sound of their tools, and the singing in the church.

--

What is it like, to live in silence, for years, for a whole lifetime?

For the monks, it was not empty. The silence was full: of prayer, and of the sounds of nature, the birds, and the wind, and the river, and the bells, and the singing in the church. And full of the presence of God, they believed.

{img:river-rye-quiet-evening-abbey-reflections-birdsong}
Aelred himself wrote, in one of his books, about the peace of Rievaulx: everywhere peace, everywhere serenity, and a marvellous freedom from the tumult of the world. Such harmony among the brothers, that each thing seems to belong to all, and all to each.

--

And yet, in the silence, the monks were not alone. They lived together, every day, for their whole lives, side by side, in the church, and the cloister, and the refectory, and the dormitory, and the fields. They knew each other very well, without words: their faces, and their footsteps, and their voices when they sang, and their ways.

And when they did speak, with Aelred, in the rare moments of conversation that he allowed, as in his book on friendship, every word was precious.
