Every Cistercian monastery is built to the same plan, so that a monk from any Cistercian abbey, in any country, would feel at home, and know where everything was, in any other.

Let us walk through Rievaulx, as it is in 1150, and see the buildings.

--

The most important building is the church.

It stands on the north side of the monastery, the highest and the largest building. It is long and narrow, built of pale, creamy sandstone, cut from the quarries in the valley. Inside, it is very plain. The Cistercians believe that a church should be simple, without decoration, so that nothing distracts the monks from their prayers.

There are no paintings on the walls, no statues, no carvings, no stained glass in the windows, only plain, clear, or greyish glass. There are no tall towers, only a small bell tower, for one or two bells. The crosses are made of painted wood, not gold. The candlesticks are of iron. The robes of the priests are of plain linen or wool, not silk.

Bernard of Clairvaux wrote that the walls of the great churches glittered, while the poor went hungry. He wanted the Cistercian churches to be beautiful only in their simplicity, and their proportions, and the light.

--

Inside the church, the monks sit in two rows of wooden stalls, facing each other, across the choir, in the eastern part of the church, near the altar. The lay brothers have their own place, at the western end of the church, separated from the monks by a screen.

On the south side of the church, there is the cloister.

{img:cloister-square-garth-grass-covered-walkways-arches-light}
The cloister is the heart of the monastery. It is a square open space, with grass, or a garden, in the middle, and a well, or a fountain. Around it, on all four sides, there are covered walkways, with a roof, open to the garden, through rows of arches, held up by little columns. Here, the monks walk, and read, and meditate, and work, sheltered from the rain.

--

Along the east side of the cloister, there is the chapter house, a beautiful hall, where the monks meet every morning, to hear a chapter of the Rule of Saint Benedict read aloud, and to discuss the business of the monastery, and confess their faults. The early abbots of the monastery are buried under its floor.

Beside it, there is a narrow passage, the parlour, the only place in the monastery where monks are allowed to speak to each other, briefly, about necessary things.

{img:chapter-house-stone-benches-monks-seated-listening-abbot}
Above the chapter house, and along the east side of the cloister, on the upper floor, there is the dormitory, a long hall, where all the monks sleep together, on simple beds, in their robes. A night stair leads straight down from the dormitory into the church, so that the monks can go down to pray in the night, without going outside.

--

Along the south side of the cloister, there is the refectory, the great dining hall, where the monks eat, at long tables, in silence, while one monk reads aloud from a pulpit, built into the wall.

Next to it, there is the kitchen, and the warming house, the only room in the monastery, besides the kitchen and the infirmary, where a fire is allowed, in winter, so that the monks can come and warm themselves, for a little while, on the coldest days.

Along the west side of the cloister, there is the range of the lay brothers: their own refectory, below, and their own dormitory, above, and storerooms, and cellars.

--

Further away, to the east, there is the infirmary, for the sick and the old monks, with its own little kitchen, and chapel, and garden. And the abbot's lodging. And the novices' quarters.

And all around, there are the other buildings: the guest house, for travellers and visitors, near the gate. The mill, on the river, where the grain is ground. The bakehouse, and the brewhouse. The tannery, where leather is made. The forge, where iron tools are made. Barns, and stables, and workshops, and gardens, and orchards, and fish ponds.

{img:monastery-mill-wheel-river-stream-stone-building-morning}
And around everything, there is a wall, with a gatehouse, where the porter sits, and welcomes visitors, and gives food to the poor.

--

The whole monastery is like a little town, a little world, complete in itself, with everything the monks need, so that they never have to go out into the world.

Saint Benedict wrote that a monastery should, if possible, be built so that everything necessary, water, a mill, a garden, and the various crafts, can be found within its walls, so that the monks do not need to wander outside, which is not good for their souls.
