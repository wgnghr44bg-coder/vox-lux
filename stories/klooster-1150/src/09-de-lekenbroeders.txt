The Cistercian monasteries have two kinds of members: the monks, and the lay brothers.

The monks are usually from noble or well-off families. They can read and write Latin, and they spend much of their time in the church, singing the services, and in reading, and prayer, as well as work.

--

The lay brothers, called the conversi, are usually from poor families, peasants and labourers, who cannot read or write, and do not know Latin. They make the same vows as the monks, and live a religious life, in the monastery. But their life is mostly work.

They wear robes of brown or grey, and have beards, unlike the monks, who are shaved. They have their own part of the monastery, at the west side of the cloister, with their own refectory and dormitory, and their own place, at the back of the church. They say simple prayers, which they learn by heart, the Our Father, and the Hail Mary, instead of singing the long services.

{img:lay-brothers-praying-simple-back-of-church-dim-light}
The lay brothers do most of the heavy work of the monastery: farming, and building, and looking after the animals, and working in the mill, and the tannery, and the forge. Without them, the great Cistercian monasteries could not exist.

--

Many of the lay brothers live and work on the granges.

A grange is a farm, belonging to the abbey, some distance away, in the hills, or in the valleys, around the monastery. Rievaulx has many granges, some of them miles away, on the moors, and in the valleys, and on the coast. Each one has a farmhouse, and barns, and a little chapel, and is run by a group of lay brothers, who live there, and come back to the abbey only on Sundays and great feasts.

On the granges, the lay brothers grow wheat, and oats, and barley, and beans, and peas. They keep cattle, and pigs, and horses. And above all, they keep sheep.

--

The Cistercians of Yorkshire become the greatest sheep farmers in England.

On the wide, empty moors and hills around the monasteries, they graze great flocks, thousands and thousands of sheep. Rievaulx, a century later, will have about fourteen thousand sheep. And the wool of the Cistercian abbeys of Yorkshire is the finest in England, and is sold to merchants from Flanders and Italy, who take it to the great cloth-making cities of Europe, like Bruges and Ghent and Florence.

{img:shepherd-lay-brother-flock-sheep-heather-moorland-wind}
The money from the wool makes the Cistercian monasteries rich, although the monks themselves live in poverty. It pays for the great churches, and the buildings, and the books.

--

The life of a lay brother is hard.

He works long hours, in all weathers, in the fields, and on the moors, with the sheep, in the rain and the wind and the snow. In the lambing season, in the spring, he is out day and night, with the ewes. At harvest, he works from dawn to dusk. He eats simple food, and sleeps in a dormitory, on a straw bed.

{img:lay-brothers-lambing-shelter-moor-spring-dawn-lantern}
But for many poor men of the twelfth century, the life of a lay brother is better than the life they would have had outside. They have enough to eat, and a bed to sleep in, and clothes, and care when they are ill or old, and a place in a great community. And they believe that they are working for God, and for the salvation of their souls.

--

Aelred, it is said, loves the lay brothers, and cares for them, as much as for the monks. He knows many of them by name, and visits them, and talks with them, and listens to their troubles.

Among the lay brothers, there are some simple, holy men, who cannot read a single word, but who pray all day long, as they work, in the fields, and on the moors. Aelred respects them greatly, and often says that they are closer to God than many learned men.
