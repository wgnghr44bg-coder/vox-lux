Every monastery needs books.

The monks need books for the services in the church: great books of psalms, and hymns, and readings, and music, big enough to be read by several monks at once, from the choir stalls. They need Bibles, and the writings of the Church Fathers, and the lives of the saints, for their holy reading. And the Rule of Saint Benedict. And books of history, and medicine, and law, and grammar.

--

In 1150, there are no printed books. Printing will not be invented in Europe for another three hundred years. Every book must be copied by hand, letter by letter, word by word, page by page. And much of this copying is done by monks.

At Rievaulx, the monks copy books in the cloister, sitting at desks in the walkway along the north side, next to the church, where the light is best, because it faces south, into the sun.

{img:parchment-making-skins-stretched-frames-scraping-monk}
First, they need parchment, to write on. Parchment is made from the skins of calves, or sheep, or goats. The skins are soaked in lime, and scraped clean of hair and flesh, and stretched on wooden frames, and dried, and scraped again, and rubbed smooth with pumice stone, until they are thin, and white, and smooth. One large Bible might need the skins of two hundred animals.

--

Then the parchment is cut into sheets, and folded, and the lines are ruled on it, with a sharp point, or a little lead, so that the writing will be straight.

The monks write with quills, made from the feathers of geese or swans, cut to a fine point with a penknife. They use ink made from oak galls, the little round growths on oak trees, crushed, and mixed with iron, and gum, and water, which makes a deep black ink, that turns brown with age.

They write slowly, carefully, in a beautiful, clear, rounded script, in Latin. A good scribe might copy two or three pages a day. A large book might take a year, or more, to copy.

--

The work is hard. The scribe sits for hours, bent over his desk, in the cold cloister, with cold fingers. Sometimes, at the end of a book, the scribes write little notes, in the margins, which still exist: thank God it will soon be dark. Or: my hand is tired. Or: three fingers write, but the whole body labours. Or: now I have written the whole thing, for Christ's sake give me a drink.

The scribes believe that copying books is a holy work, a way of praying with their hands. Every letter they write, it is said, is a blow against the devil.

--

In the great Benedictine monasteries, the books are often decorated with beautiful pictures, called illuminations, in bright colours, red and blue and green, and gold leaf, which shines in the candlelight. Great initial letters, at the beginning of chapters, filled with flowers, and animals, and dragons, and little scenes.

But the Cistercians, in the spirit of Bernard, decide that their books should be simple too. In about 1150, the General Chapter at Cîteaux orders that the initial letters in Cistercian books should be of a single colour, without gold, and without pictures. Bernard had complained about the strange monsters and fantastic creatures in the books and the cloisters of other monasteries, which distracted the monks from their prayers.

{img:simple-red-initial-letter-manuscript-page-clear-script}
So the books of Rievaulx are beautiful, but plain: clear black writing, with red or blue initial letters, and nothing else.

--

The library of Rievaulx grows, over the years, to more than two hundred books, which is a great library for the time.

A list of the books, made in the late twelfth century, still exists. There are Bibles, and commentaries, and the works of the great Church Fathers, Augustine, and Jerome, and Gregory, and Ambrose. There are the writings of Bernard, and of Aelred himself. There are books of history, by the English monk Bede, who lived nearby, four hundred years before, and books of medicine, and grammar.

{img:monastery-book-cupboard-cloister-wall-chained-volumes}
The books are kept in a cupboard, built into the wall of the cloister, near the door of the church, called the armarium. Once a year, at the beginning of Lent, each monk is given a book from the library, to read through the year. The Rule of Saint Benedict says so.

--

Some of the books of Rievaulx still exist today, in libraries in England, in Cambridge and Oxford, and London. When the monastery was closed, in the sixteenth century, most of its books were scattered, or lost, or destroyed. But a few were saved, by collectors, and we can still turn their pages, and see the writing of the monks, nearly nine hundred years ago.
