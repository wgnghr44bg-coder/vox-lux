Today, the ruins of Rievaulx Abbey still stand in the valley of the Rye, among the woods and the meadows, in the North York Moors National Park.

They are looked after by English Heritage, and every year, many thousands of people come to visit them.

--

You come down into the valley, along a narrow country lane, between hedges and stone walls, past little cottages, of golden stone, with gardens full of flowers. And suddenly, through the trees, you see them: the great arches of the church, rising high above the meadow, against the green hillside, pale gold and grey, in the sunlight.

The eastern part of the church, the part that was rebuilt in the thirteenth century, is still standing, almost to its full height: three storeys of tall pointed arches, one above the other, open to the sky. Birds fly in and out of the empty windows. The grass grows where the floor once was.

You can walk through the ruins, slowly, and imagine the life of the monastery. Here is the church, where the monks sang the night office, by candlelight. Here is the cloister, where they walked, and read, and copied books. Here is the chapter house, where they met every morning, and where the tomb of the first abbot, William, can still be seen, and where Aelred was buried.

--

Here is the refectory, a great tall hall, with its tall windows, and the stairs, built into the wall, that led up to the reader's pulpit. Here is the stone basin, where the monks washed their hands before meals. Here is the warming house, with its great fireplace. Here is the infirmary, and the little room where Aelred lived his last years.

And in the little museum, by the entrance, you can see things found in the ruins: carved stones, and floor tiles, and pottery, and coins, and tools, and the pieces of a bell, and little things that the monks used, every day, and left behind.

{img:refectory-ruin-tall-walls-windows-grass-floor-sun}
On the hillside above, the Rievaulx Terrace, with its two little temples, is still there, looked after by the National Trust. You can walk along it, on the soft grass, and look down at the ruins, through gaps in the trees, as the visitors did, more than two hundred and fifty years ago.

--

The valley itself is still beautiful, and quiet.

The river Rye still flows past the abbey, clear and fast, over its stones, in the channel that the monks dug for it. The woods still cover the hillsides, oak and ash and beech, green in summer, gold in autumn. The meadows are full of sheep, grazing, as they did in the days of the lay brothers.

{img:river-rye-meadow-sheep-abbey-ruins-background-evening}
In the spring, the woods are full of bluebells, and primroses, and wild garlic. In the summer, the heather blooms on the moors above, purple, as far as the eye can see. In the autumn, the leaves turn gold. In the winter, sometimes, there is snow, and the ruins stand white and silent, in the white valley.

--

And not far away, in the same hills, there are other ruined abbeys, of the Cistercians and the Benedictines: Byland, and Fountains, the largest and most beautiful of all, a World Heritage Site, and Jervaulx, and Whitby, high on its cliff above the sea.

And there are still monks in Yorkshire. At Ampleforth, a few miles from Rievaulx, there is a Benedictine abbey, founded in the nineteenth century, where the monks still sing the services, every day, in the abbey church, and run a school.

On some days, visitors to Rievaulx find that there is a quiet hush in the ruins, especially early in the morning, or in the evening, when the crowds have gone, and the light is low and golden, and the only sounds are the river, and the birds, and the wind in the trees.

--

So the story of the white monks of Rievaulx comes to an end tonight, in the green valley, with the river running over its stones, and the great arches standing open to the evening sky.

The bell in the night, the singing in the dark church, the silence of the cloister, and a kind abbot who believed that friendship was a way to God.
