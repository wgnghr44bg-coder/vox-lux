At the gate of Rievaulx, there is a gatehouse, and in it, the porter.

Saint Benedict wrote that the porter should be a wise old monk, who knows how to receive and give a message, and whose age keeps him from wandering about. He should have a little room by the gate, so that visitors always find someone there to answer them. And as soon as anyone knocks, or a poor man calls, he should answer: thanks be to God, or, a blessing on you, and go to them at once, gently, and warmly.

--

All kinds of people come to the gate of Rievaulx.

There are travellers, on their way through the valley, who need a meal, and a bed for the night. There are pilgrims, on their way to the great shrines: to York, and Durham, where Saint Cuthbert is buried, and Canterbury, and even to Rome, or Santiago de Compostela, in Spain, or Jerusalem. There are merchants, coming to buy wool. There are messengers, with letters. There are monks from other monasteries, and abbots, and bishops, and lords, and their servants.

And there are the poor, who come every day, for bread, and soup, and old clothes, and help. The porter gives them what the monastery has, and the leftovers from the refectory, and in hard winters, the monks may feed hundreds of people, every day.

--

The guests are lodged in the guest house, near the gate, a large hall, with beds, and a fire, and its own kitchen, so that the guests do not disturb the silence of the monks.

Saint Benedict wrote that all guests should be received as Christ, because Christ himself will say, I was a stranger, and you welcomed me. The abbot, or a monk chosen for the purpose, greets them, and bows to them, and prays with them, and washes their hands, and their feet. They are given food, and drink, and a bed, and, if they need it, care for their horses.

{img:guest-hall-fire-travellers-eating-monk-serving-bread}
The rich guests, and the poor, are treated with the same courtesy. In fact, Benedict wrote, the poor and the pilgrims should be given the greatest care, because in them, Christ is more truly welcomed. The rich, he said dryly, demand respect by their very presence.

--

The guests may stay for a few days, or longer. Some come to rest, and pray, and think, for a while, in the peace of the valley, away from their troubles. Some come to talk to the abbot, and ask his advice. Aelred's wisdom, and his kindness, become famous, and many people come to Rievaulx just to see him.

Some guests, after staying a while, ask to become monks themselves.

{img:visitor-talking-with-abbot-garden-path-trees-afternoon}
And when they leave, the monks give them food for the journey, and their blessing, and the porter opens the gate, and they go on their way, up the steep track, out of the valley, and back into the world.
