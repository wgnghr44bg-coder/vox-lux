The Cistercians are not only men of prayer. They are also some of the best engineers and farmers of the Middle Ages.

Water is very important to them. Every Cistercian monastery is built near a river, or a stream, and the monks use the water in many clever ways.

--

At Rievaulx, the monks dig channels, from the river Rye, and from the streams that run down the hillsides, to bring water to every part of the monastery.

One channel turns the great wheel of the mill, which grinds the corn into flour, for the bread. Another runs under the kitchen, and the latrines, carrying away the waste, like a sewer, and washing everything clean. Another fills the fish ponds. Fresh, clean water for drinking and washing is brought from springs, in pipes of lead, or of hollowed wood, to the great basin in the cloister, and to the kitchen.

A monk of the time, describing the Cistercian abbey of Clairvaux, wrote a beautiful passage about its river, and how it served the monks: turning the mill, and the fulling mill, which beats the cloth, and helping the brewers, and the tanners, and the gardeners, and then, having done all its work, flowing away, cleaning everything as it goes, back to the main river.

--

The monks of Rievaulx also become ironmakers.

In the hills around the valley, there is iron ore, in the rocks. And there is plenty of wood, for charcoal. So the lay brothers dig the ore, and burn the wood into charcoal, in great slow-burning piles, covered with earth, in the woods. And they smelt the iron, in small furnaces of clay and stone, blown by bellows, and hammer it into bars, and tools.

{img:charcoal-burners-earth-mound-smoke-woods-lay-brother}
Much later, by the early sixteenth century, the monks of Rievaulx have an iron furnace at a place called Laskill, a few miles up the valley. Archaeologists who studied the slag from Laskill believe that it may have been one of the most advanced furnaces in Europe, and that the monks may have been close to making cast iron, before it became common in England.

--

The monks use the iron for their own tools: ploughshares, and spades, and axes, and nails, and hinges, and locks, and horseshoes. And they sell it, to people around, along with their wool, and their corn.

They also drain the marshes, in the valley bottom, with ditches, to make good meadows and fields. They improve the breeds of their sheep, and their cattle, and their horses. They plant orchards, and teach the people around how to graft fruit trees.

{img:orchard-monk-grafting-apple-tree-spring-blossom}
All over Europe, the Cistercians are pioneers, clearing new land, and draining marshes, and building mills, and improving farming. They believe that work is prayer. And their work changes the landscape of Europe, in ways that can still be seen today.
