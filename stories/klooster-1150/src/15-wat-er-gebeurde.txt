Aelred lives for another seventeen years after 1150, as abbot of Rievaulx.

They are years of peace, and growth. The civil war ends, in 1153, when Stephen agrees that Matilda's son, Henry, will be king after him. Henry the Second becomes king in 1154, and brings order back to England.

--

Rievaulx grows, and prospers. New buildings are added. The church is made larger. Aelred travels to Scotland, and to the daughter houses of Rievaulx, and to Cîteaux, and writes his books, and preaches, and cares for his monks.

But his illness grows worse. In his last years, he cannot walk, and lives in his little room, next to the infirmary, surrounded by his monks.

And on the twelfth of January, 1167, Aelred passes away, peacefully, at Rievaulx, at the age of fifty-six, with his monks around him. Walter Daniel says that, at the end, Aelred said, again and again, in Latin and in English: hasten, hasten. For the love of Christ, hasten. He was buried in the chapter house, beside the first abbot, William.

--

After Aelred, Rievaulx goes on, for nearly four hundred years.

In the thirteenth century, the monks rebuild the eastern part of the church, in the new Gothic style, much larger and grander than before, with tall pointed arches, and great windows, reaching up toward the sky. The abbey becomes very rich, from its wool. It has thousands of sheep, on its granges, all over Yorkshire.

{img:gothic-abbey-church-tall-pointed-arches-thirteenth-century}
But slowly, things change. The number of lay brothers falls, as fewer poor men want to live that hard life, and the monks begin to rent out their granges to farmers, instead of farming them themselves. In 1349, the Black Death comes to England, and many of the monks and lay brothers pass away. After that, the monastery is much smaller.

--

By the 1530s, there are only about twenty-two monks at Rievaulx.

And in those years, King Henry the Eighth of England breaks away from the Church of Rome, and makes himself head of the Church in England. And he decides to close all the monasteries in England, and take their lands and their wealth for himself.

{img:abbey-quiet-empty-cloister-last-monks-leaving-1538}
In December 1538, Rievaulx Abbey is closed. The monks are sent away, with small pensions. The buildings are given to a nobleman, the Earl of Rutland. And his men take the lead from the roofs, and the glass from the windows, and the bells, and the stones, and sell them. And the great church, and the cloister, and all the buildings of the monastery, slowly fall into ruin.

--

It is a sad end, to four hundred years of prayer and work.

But it is not quite the end. Because the ruins of Rievaulx are so beautiful, that they became, in later centuries, one of the most famous and most visited places in England.

In the eighteenth century, a rich landowner, Thomas Duncombe, built a long grassy terrace, on the hillside above the abbey, with two little classical temples, at either end, so that his guests could walk along it, and look down at the ruins, through the trees, as a romantic view. Artists came, like J. M. W. Turner, and painted them. Poets came, and wrote about them.

--

And the Cistercians themselves did not disappear.

The order survived the closing of the English monasteries, and the French Revolution, and still exists today, all over the world. There are Cistercian monasteries in France, and Italy, and Spain, and Germany, and Belgium, and the Netherlands, and Ireland, and America, and Africa, and Asia. Some of them, called Trappists, after the abbey of La Trappe, in France, follow a stricter rule, like the first Cistercians.

{img:modern-cistercian-monks-singing-choir-simple-church}
And every night, in those monasteries, the monks still rise in the darkness, and go down to their churches, and sing the psalms, in the same way, as the monks of Rievaulx did, nearly nine hundred years ago.
