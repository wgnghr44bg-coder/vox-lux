The monks of Rievaulx eat simply.

The Rule of Saint Benedict says that the monks should have one or two meals a day, depending on the season, with two cooked dishes at each meal, so that if a monk cannot eat one, he can eat the other. And a pound of bread a day. And a measure of wine, or, in England, of ale. And fruit or vegetables, when they are available.

--

The Cistercians follow this strictly. They eat no meat at all, except for the sick, in the infirmary. Not even fish, at first, although later they will allow it. No eggs, or cheese, or milk, during the fasting seasons. No spices, or rich sauces. No white bread, only coarse brown bread, made of rye, or barley, or wheat, with the bran left in.

The main food is bread, and pottage: a thick soup, or stew, of beans, or peas, or lentils, cooked with vegetables from the garden, cabbages, and leeks, and onions, and herbs, and seasoned with a little salt.

From the middle of September until Easter, the monks eat only one meal a day, in the afternoon. And during Lent, the forty days before Easter, the meal is in the evening, after Vespers. From Easter until September, there are two meals: dinner at midday, and supper in the evening.

--

The meals are eaten in the refectory, in silence.

Before the meal, the monks wash their hands, at a long stone basin, with running water, in the cloister, outside the refectory door. Then they go in, and stand at their places, at the long wooden tables, along the walls, and the abbot says a blessing.

{img:stone-lavatorium-basin-running-water-monks-washing-hands}
The tables are covered with white linen cloths. Each monk has a wooden bowl, and a spoon, and a knife, and a cup, and a napkin, and a loaf of bread. The food is brought from the kitchen by the monks who are serving that week, in turns, and set before each monk.

--

While the monks eat, one monk reads aloud, from a pulpit, built high in the wall of the refectory, with a little staircase inside the wall. He reads from the Bible, or from the writings of the saints, or from the Rule, in Latin, in a clear, steady voice.

The monks listen, and eat, in silence. If a monk needs something, salt, or bread, or water, he makes a sign, and his neighbour passes it to him. They must not look around, or make noise with their spoons, or eat greedily, or leave food on their plates.

{img:reader-monk-refectory-pulpit-book-candle-evening-meal}
After the meal, the monks say grace together, and leave the refectory, in procession, singing a psalm, and go to the church, to give thanks.

--

Occasionally, on feast days, or when a benefactor has given money for it, there is a little extra: a pittance, a special dish, of eggs, or cheese, or fish, or a little better bread, or a cake made with honey, or better ale. The monks look forward to these very much.

The lay brothers, who do heavy work in the fields, eat more, and earlier, and more often, than the monks, because they need the strength.

The bread is baked in the monastery bakehouse, in great ovens, every day. The ale is brewed in the brewhouse, from barley grown on the abbey's fields. Vegetables and herbs and fruit come from the gardens and orchards. And beans and peas from the fields. The monastery produces almost all its own food.

--

The Cistercians eat so simply, and fast so often, that some people of the time think they are too strict.

But Aelred, in his kindness, makes sure that the sick, and the old, and the weak, are given better food, in the infirmary, and that no one is made ill by too much fasting. He himself eats very little, because of his own illness, and gives his own portion to the poor.

{img:abbot-giving-bread-to-poor-at-gate-winter-morning}
And every day, at the gate of the monastery, the porter gives out bread, and the leftovers from the refectory, to the poor who come to the gate. In hard times, when there is famine, hundreds of poor people come, and the monks feed them all, as best they can.
