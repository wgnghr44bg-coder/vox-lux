Tonight we are going nearly nine hundred years back, to a quiet green valley in the north of England, in the year 1150.

--

At the bottom of the valley, beside a little river, among the woods and the meadows, there stands a great church of pale stone, with long grey buildings around it. Inside live more than a hundred monks in white robes, and many more lay brothers, who have given up everything in the world to live a life of prayer, and work, and silence.

The place is called Rievaulx Abbey. And tonight, we will spend a little time with them.

If you enjoy quiet evenings in the distant past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, and come back for another one soon.

--

The valley lies in the hills of North Yorkshire, about thirty miles north of the city of York, at the edge of the great high moors, which stretch away to the north and east, toward the sea.

The moors are wide and empty and wild, covered with heather and bracken and grass, purple in late summer, and brown in winter, with the wind blowing across them, and the curlews calling. But down in the valleys, it is sheltered and green, with woods of oak and ash and alder, and little streams, and meadows, and fields.

--

The river in this valley is called the Rye. It is a small, clear, fast river, running over stones, between steep, wooded hillsides.

The monks named their abbey after it. Rievaulx means the valley of the Rye, in the Norman French that the monks and the great lords speak. The ordinary people of the countryside, who speak English, call it something like Rivers.

{img:clear-river-stones-woods-steep-banks-dappled-light}
When the first monks came here, eighteen years ago, in 1132, it was a wild place, with thick woods, and marshes, and no houses, and no roads. The monks had chosen it on purpose. They wanted to be far from the world, and its noise, and its temptations. A place of solitude, where they could live for God alone.

--

Now, in 1150, the valley has changed.

The monks have cleared the woods, and drained the marshes, and planted fields, and orchards, and gardens. They have even moved the river, a little, digging a new channel for it, to make more room for their buildings. They have built a mill, and fish ponds, and a great church, and the buildings of the monastery around it, of stone, cut from the hillsides of the valley.

{img:monastery-buildings-construction-masons-scaffolding-stone}
And the monastery is growing fast. In 1150, there are already more than a hundred monks, and hundreds of lay brothers, living and working here, and on the abbey's farms, in the hills around. It is one of the largest monasteries in England.

--

Let us imagine that we are standing on the hillside, above the abbey, on a spring morning, in the year 1150.

The sun is rising over the hills to the east, and the mist is lifting from the river. Below us, in the valley, the long roof of the great church shines in the first light, with its tall, plain walls of creamy stone, and its narrow windows. Around it, there are the other buildings: the cloister, a square garden, surrounded by covered walkways, and the long halls where the monks eat, and sleep, and meet, and work.

A bell rings, from the church. Clear and simple, across the quiet valley. And from the buildings, figures in white robes come out, walking slowly, in silence, toward the church.

--

What is a monastery?

A monastery is a place where men, or women, live together, apart from the world, to devote their whole lives to God. The men are called monks, and the women are called nuns. They make promises, called vows, to stay in their monastery for the rest of their lives, to obey their abbot, and to live in poverty, owning nothing of their own, and to live in chastity, never marrying.

{img:monks-walking-cloister-white-habits-silent-morning}
They spend their days in prayer, and in work, and in study, following a strict rule, which tells them exactly how to live, every hour of every day.

In 1150, there are thousands of monasteries all over Europe, with tens of thousands of monks and nuns. They are among the most important places in the medieval world: centres of prayer, and of learning, and of farming, and of charity, and of art.

--

And the monks of Rievaulx belong to a new and special kind of monastery, which is spreading all over Europe in these years, faster than any before: the Cistercians, the white monks.
