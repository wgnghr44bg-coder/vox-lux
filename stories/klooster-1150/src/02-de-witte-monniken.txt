The story of the monks of Rievaulx begins six hundred years earlier, in Italy, with a man called Benedict.

Benedict was born around the year 480, in the little town of Nursia, in the mountains of central Italy, at the time when the Roman empire in the west was falling apart. As a young man, he went to Rome to study. But he was disgusted by the wickedness of the city, and went away, into the mountains, to live alone, in a cave, as a hermit, praying.

--

Other men came to join him. And in the end, he founded a monastery, on a mountain called Monte Cassino, between Rome and Naples. And there, he wrote a little book of rules, for his monks, telling them how to live together.

It is called the Rule of Saint Benedict. It is a short, wise, gentle, practical book, with seventy-three short chapters. It tells the monks how to pray, and when, and what to eat, and how much, and when to sleep, and how to work, and how to treat each other, and their guests, and the sick, and the old, and the young. It asks them to live a balanced life, with time for prayer, and for work, and for reading, and for rest.

Benedict says, in the beginning, that he hopes to set down nothing harsh, nothing burdensome. He calls the monastery a school for the Lord's service. And he says that the monks should listen, with the ear of the heart.

--

For the next six hundred years, almost all the monks of Western Europe follow the Rule of Saint Benedict. They are called Benedictines, or black monks, because they wear black robes.

Over the centuries, many of the Benedictine monasteries became rich, and powerful, with great lands, given to them by kings and lords, in return for their prayers. The greatest of them was the abbey of Cluny, in Burgundy, in France, with a huge church, the largest in Christendom, and hundreds of monks, and beautiful services, with music, and candles, and incense, and gold and silver, and long processions, that took up most of the day.

{img:cluny-great-abbey-church-burgundy-towers-golden-light}
Some monks began to feel that the monasteries had become too rich, and too comfortable, and had forgotten the simple life of Benedict's Rule.

--

So, in the year 1098, a group of monks left their monastery, in Burgundy, and went into a wild, marshy forest, south of the city of Dijon, at a place called Cîteaux, to start a new monastery. They wanted to live by the Rule of Saint Benedict, exactly as it was written, simply and strictly, with nothing added.

They wore robes of plain, undyed wool, greyish white, instead of black, so they became known as the white monks. Their monastery was called the New Monastery. And their order, after the place, was called the Cistercians.

At first, life at Cîteaux was very hard, and the new monastery was poor, and almost failed. Very few men came to join.

--

Then, in 1113, a young nobleman from Burgundy, called Bernard, came to Cîteaux, with thirty of his friends and relatives, including four of his brothers and an uncle, all asking to become monks.

Bernard was twenty-two. He was thin, and pale, and intense, and brilliant, and he had a wonderful gift for speaking and writing, and for persuading people. Two years later, he was sent to found a new monastery, in a wild valley, called Clairvaux, the valley of light. And as abbot of Clairvaux, Bernard became the most famous and powerful churchman in Europe.

{img:bernard-of-clairvaux-thin-monk-white-habit-preaching}
In 1150, Bernard is still alive, an old man of about sixty, worn out by fasting and work, but still writing letters to popes and kings, and preaching, and travelling. Everyone listens to him.

--

Under Bernard, the Cistercians spread all over Europe.

Young men flocked to join them. New monasteries were founded, one after another: in France, and Germany, and Italy, and Spain, and England, and Scotland, and Ireland, and Scandinavia. Each new monastery was a daughter of an older one, and the abbots of all the monasteries met, every year, at Cîteaux, in a great meeting, called the General Chapter, to make sure that all the monasteries were following the Rule in the same way.

By 1150, there are more than three hundred Cistercian monasteries. By the end of the century, there will be more than five hundred.

--

And in 1132, Bernard sent a group of twelve monks, and an abbot, named William, from Clairvaux to England, to the north of Yorkshire, to found a new monastery.

They came with a letter from Bernard to the king of England, Henry the First. And a great lord of Yorkshire, Walter Espec, a tall, generous, old knight, who lived in a castle nearby, at Helmsley, gave them the land in the valley of the Rye.

{img:twelve-monks-walking-yorkshire-road-wooded-valley-1132}
And so, in the spring of 1132, the white monks came to Rievaulx.
