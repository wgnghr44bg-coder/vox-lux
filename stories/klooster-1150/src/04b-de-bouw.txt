In 1150, Rievaulx is still being built. In fact, it will be built, and rebuilt, and enlarged, for more than a hundred years.

When the first monks arrived, in 1132, they lived in simple wooden huts, and prayed in a little wooden chapel. Then, slowly, they began to build in stone. By 1150, they have built a great stone church, one of the first Cistercian churches in England, and are building the cloister, and the other buildings around it.

--

The stone comes from the valley itself.

On the hillsides above the abbey, there are quarries, where the masons cut the stone: a pale, creamy, golden sandstone, which is easy to cut when it is fresh, and hardens in the air. The lay brothers, and hired workmen, split the great blocks from the rock, with iron wedges, and hammers, and crowbars.

To bring the stone down to the building site, the monks dig a canal, a little channel, from the river, along the valley, so that the stone can be floated on rafts, or small boats, from the quarries to the abbey. Archaeologists have found the traces of these channels, in the valley, still today.

--

At the building site, the master mason is in charge.

He is a skilled craftsman, often a layman, hired by the abbey, who has learned his trade over many years, travelling from one great building to another. He draws the plans, on the ground, or on a floor of plaster, with compasses and measuring rods, and cuts templates, of wood, for the shapes of the stones.

{img:master-mason-drawing-plan-plaster-floor-compass-rods}
The masons cut each stone carefully, with axes, and chisels, to the right shape, square and smooth, and each mason carves his own little mark on his stones, so that he can be paid for them. These masons' marks can still be seen on the stones of Rievaulx today: little crosses, and arrows, and stars, and letters.

--

The walls are built of two faces of cut stone, with a core of rubble and mortar in between. The mortar is made of lime, which is burned in kilns, near the site, from limestone, and mixed with sand and water.

As the walls rise, the carpenters build scaffolding, of wooden poles, tied together with ropes, and fixed into holes in the walls, which can still be seen, in many old buildings, as little square holes, in rows. The stones are lifted up, by cranes, worked by men walking inside great wooden wheels, like hamster wheels, called treadwheels.

{img:treadwheel-crane-lifting-stone-scaffolding-church-wall}
The arches are built over wooden frames, called centring, which hold up the stones until the mortar sets, and the arch can stand by itself. Then the frame is taken away, and moved to the next arch.

--

The roofs are made of great oak beams, cut from the woods of the valley, and covered with lead sheets, or with stone slates, or with tiles. The floors are of stone, or of beaten earth, and later, of tiles, made in the abbey's own kilns, glazed in green and yellow, laid in simple patterns.

It is slow work. A great church can take thirty or forty years, or more, to build. The masons can only work in the warmer months. In winter, the walls are covered with straw and turf, to protect the new mortar from the frost.

The monks, it seems, do not mind. They are building, they believe, not for themselves, but for God, and for the monks who will come after them, for hundreds of years.

--

And there are other builders in the valley too.

The monks build their mill, on the river, with a great wooden wheel, turned by the water, and millstones, to grind the grain. They build fish ponds, where they keep fish, carp and trout and eels, for the infirmary, and for the guests. They build the walls of the precinct, and the gatehouse, and the barns, and the bridges over the river.

{img:monastery-fishponds-terraced-water-reeds-valley-calm}
Little by little, year by year, stone by stone, the monks turn their wild valley into one of the great places of medieval England.
