Tonight we are going nearly nine hundred years back, to a quiet green valley in the north of England, in the year 1150.

[long-pause]

At the bottom of the valley, beside a little river, among the woods and the meadows, there stands a great church of pale stone, with long grey buildings around it. Inside live more than a hundred monks in white robes, and many more lay brothers, who have given up everything in the world to live a life of prayer, and work, and silence.

[pause]

The place is called Rievaulx Abbey. And tonight, we will spend a little time with them.

[pause]

If you enjoy quiet evenings in the distant past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, and come back for another one soon.

[long-pause]

The valley lies in the hills of North Yorkshire, about thirty miles north of the city of York, at the edge of the great high moors, which stretch away to the north and east, toward the sea.

[pause]

The moors are wide and empty and wild, covered with heather and bracken and grass, purple in late summer, and brown in winter, with the wind blowing across them, and the curlews calling. But down in the valleys, it is sheltered and green, with woods of oak and ash and alder, and little streams, and meadows, and fields.

[long-pause]

The river in this valley is called the Rye. It is a small, clear, fast river, running over stones, between steep, wooded hillsides.

[pause]

The monks named their abbey after it. Rievaulx means the valley of the Rye, in the Norman French that the monks and the great lords speak. The ordinary people of the countryside, who speak English, call it something like Rivers.

[pause]

{img:clear-river-stones-woods-steep-banks-dappled-light}
When the first monks came here, eighteen years ago, in 1132, it was a wild place, with thick woods, and marshes, and no houses, and no roads. The monks had chosen it on purpose. They wanted to be far from the world, and its noise, and its temptations. A place of solitude, where they could live for God alone.

[long-pause]

Now, in 1150, the valley has changed.

[pause]

The monks have cleared the woods, and drained the marshes, and planted fields, and orchards, and gardens. They have even moved the river, a little, digging a new channel for it, to make more room for their buildings. They have built a mill, and fish ponds, and a great church, and the buildings of the monastery around it, of stone, cut from the hillsides of the valley.

[pause]

{img:monastery-buildings-construction-masons-scaffolding-stone}
And the monastery is growing fast. In 1150, there are already more than a hundred monks, and hundreds of lay brothers, living and working here, and on the abbey's farms, in the hills around. It is one of the largest monasteries in England.

[long-pause]

Let us imagine that we are standing on the hillside, above the abbey, on a spring morning, in the year 1150.

[pause]

The sun is rising over the hills to the east, and the mist is lifting from the river. Below us, in the valley, the long roof of the great church shines in the first light, with its tall, plain walls of creamy stone, and its narrow windows. Around it, there are the other buildings: the cloister, a square garden, surrounded by covered walkways, and the long halls where the monks eat, and sleep, and meet, and work.

[pause]

A bell rings, from the church. Clear and simple, across the quiet valley. And from the buildings, figures in white robes come out, walking slowly, in silence, toward the church.

[long-pause]

What is a monastery?

[pause]

A monastery is a place where men, or women, live together, apart from the world, to devote their whole lives to God. The men are called monks, and the women are called nuns. They make promises, called vows, to stay in their monastery for the rest of their lives, to obey their abbot, and to live in poverty, owning nothing of their own, and to live in chastity, never marrying.

[pause]

{img:monks-walking-cloister-white-habits-silent-morning}
They spend their days in prayer, and in work, and in study, following a strict rule, which tells them exactly how to live, every hour of every day.

[pause]

In 1150, there are thousands of monasteries all over Europe, with tens of thousands of monks and nuns. They are among the most important places in the medieval world: centres of prayer, and of learning, and of farming, and of charity, and of art.

[long-pause]

And the monks of Rievaulx belong to a new and special kind of monastery, which is spreading all over Europe in these years, faster than any before: the Cistercians, the white monks.

[long-pause]

{img:saint-benedict-writing-rule-monte-cassino-old-manuscript}
The story of the monks of Rievaulx begins six hundred years earlier, in Italy, with a man called Benedict.

[pause]

Benedict was born around the year 480, in the little town of Nursia, in the mountains of central Italy, at the time when the Roman empire in the west was falling apart. As a young man, he went to Rome to study. But he was disgusted by the wickedness of the city, and went away, into the mountains, to live alone, in a cave, as a hermit, praying.

[long-pause]

Other men came to join him. And in the end, he founded a monastery, on a mountain called Monte Cassino, between Rome and Naples. And there, he wrote a little book of rules, for his monks, telling them how to live together.

[pause]

It is called the Rule of Saint Benedict. It is a short, wise, gentle, practical book, with seventy-three short chapters. It tells the monks how to pray, and when, and what to eat, and how much, and when to sleep, and how to work, and how to treat each other, and their guests, and the sick, and the old, and the young. It asks them to live a balanced life, with time for prayer, and for work, and for reading, and for rest.

[pause]

Benedict says, in the beginning, that he hopes to set down nothing harsh, nothing burdensome. He calls the monastery a school for the Lord's service. And he says that the monks should listen, with the ear of the heart.

[long-pause]

For the next six hundred years, almost all the monks of Western Europe follow the Rule of Saint Benedict. They are called Benedictines, or black monks, because they wear black robes.

[pause]

Over the centuries, many of the Benedictine monasteries became rich, and powerful, with great lands, given to them by kings and lords, in return for their prayers. The greatest of them was the abbey of Cluny, in Burgundy, in France, with a huge church, the largest in Christendom, and hundreds of monks, and beautiful services, with music, and candles, and incense, and gold and silver, and long processions, that took up most of the day.

[pause]

{img:cluny-great-abbey-church-burgundy-towers-golden-light}
Some monks began to feel that the monasteries had become too rich, and too comfortable, and had forgotten the simple life of Benedict's Rule.

[long-pause]

So, in the year 1098, a group of monks left their monastery, in Burgundy, and went into a wild, marshy forest, south of the city of Dijon, at a place called Cîteaux, to start a new monastery. They wanted to live by the Rule of Saint Benedict, exactly as it was written, simply and strictly, with nothing added.

[pause]

They wore robes of plain, undyed wool, greyish white, instead of black, so they became known as the white monks. Their monastery was called the New Monastery. And their order, after the place, was called the Cistercians.

[pause]

At first, life at Cîteaux was very hard, and the new monastery was poor, and almost failed. Very few men came to join.

[long-pause]

Then, in 1113, a young nobleman from Burgundy, called Bernard, came to Cîteaux, with thirty of his friends and relatives, including four of his brothers and an uncle, all asking to become monks.

[pause]

Bernard was twenty-two. He was thin, and pale, and intense, and brilliant, and he had a wonderful gift for speaking and writing, and for persuading people. Two years later, he was sent to found a new monastery, in a wild valley, called Clairvaux, the valley of light. And as abbot of Clairvaux, Bernard became the most famous and powerful churchman in Europe.

[pause]

{img:bernard-of-clairvaux-thin-monk-white-habit-preaching}
In 1150, Bernard is still alive, an old man of about sixty, worn out by fasting and work, but still writing letters to popes and kings, and preaching, and travelling. Everyone listens to him.

[long-pause]

Under Bernard, the Cistercians spread all over Europe.

[pause]

Young men flocked to join them. New monasteries were founded, one after another: in France, and Germany, and Italy, and Spain, and England, and Scotland, and Ireland, and Scandinavia. Each new monastery was a daughter of an older one, and the abbots of all the monasteries met, every year, at Cîteaux, in a great meeting, called the General Chapter, to make sure that all the monasteries were following the Rule in the same way.

[pause]

By 1150, there are more than three hundred Cistercian monasteries. By the end of the century, there will be more than five hundred.

[long-pause]

And in 1132, Bernard sent a group of twelve monks, and an abbot, named William, from Clairvaux to England, to the north of Yorkshire, to found a new monastery.

[pause]

They came with a letter from Bernard to the king of England, Henry the First. And a great lord of Yorkshire, Walter Espec, a tall, generous, old knight, who lived in a castle nearby, at Helmsley, gave them the land in the valley of the Rye.

[pause]

{img:twelve-monks-walking-yorkshire-road-wooded-valley-1132}
And so, in the spring of 1132, the white monks came to Rievaulx.

[long-pause]

{img:abbot-aelred-white-habit-kind-face-cloister-walking}
In 1150, the abbot of Rievaulx is a man called Aelred.

[pause]

He is forty years old. He has been abbot for three years. And he will become one of the most loved and most famous abbots in the history of England. Many years later, he will be honoured as a saint.

[long-pause]

We know a great deal about Aelred, because one of his monks, Walter Daniel, who lived with him for seventeen years, wrote a book about his life, soon after his death. And because Aelred himself wrote many books, and letters, and sermons, which still exist.

[pause]

Aelred was born in 1110, in the town of Hexham, in Northumberland, in the far north of England, near the Scottish border. His father was a priest, as his grandfather had been, because in those days, before the rules were made stricter, some English priests still married and had families.

[pause]

{img:hexham-abbey-town-northumberland-1110s-river-tyne}
As a boy, Aelred was clever, and gentle, and full of charm, and everyone liked him. When he was about fourteen, he was sent to the court of the king of Scotland, King David the First, at Roxburgh, to be brought up with the king's son, Henry, as a companion.

[long-pause]

At the Scottish court, Aelred did very well. King David liked him, and trusted him, and made him his steward, in charge of the royal household and the king's table, when he was still a young man. He could have had a great career at court, and become a bishop, or a great official.

[pause]

But he was not happy. He felt that the life of the court was empty, and that he wanted something more. He later wrote that, in those years, he was outwardly cheerful, but inwardly troubled, and that he longed for a real friend, and for God.

[pause]

In 1134, when he was twenty-four, King David sent him on business to the archbishop of York. On the way, he stayed with Walter Espec, the lord of Helmsley, who told him about the new monastery of the white monks, in the valley nearby, at Rievaulx, which had been founded two years earlier.

[long-pause]

Aelred went to visit. And the next day, as he was riding home, past the monastery, on the hillside above the valley, he stopped, and looked down at it, for a long time. And then he turned his horse around, and rode down into the valley, and asked to become a monk.

[pause]

He gave up everything: his place at court, his career, his wealth, his friends. And he put on the plain white robe of a novice, and began a new life.

[pause]

At Rievaulx, Aelred was a model monk. He was humble, and hard-working, and kind, and patient. He was not strong, physically. He suffered from illness for much of his life, from painful stones in his kidneys, and later from arthritis. But he never complained.

[long-pause]

He was soon given important work. He was sent to Rome, on business for the abbey, in 1142. He became master of the novices, teaching the young men who came to join. He was sent to found a new monastery, at Revesby, in Lincolnshire, as its first abbot. And in 1147, when he was thirty-seven, the monks of Rievaulx chose him as their abbot.

[pause]

As abbot, he is kind, and wise, and gentle. He is famous for his patience, and for never losing his temper, and for his love of his monks. Walter Daniel writes that Aelred never sent anyone away from the monastery, in all his years as abbot, even the most difficult monks. He said that it was the glory of the house of Rievaulx that it was a home for the weak as well as the strong, a mother of mercy for all.

[pause]

{img:abbot-listening-to-young-monk-garden-bench-gentle}
Under Aelred, Rievaulx grows larger than ever. Men come from all over England and Scotland, and even from abroad, to join, because they have heard of his kindness. By the end of his life, there are, Walter Daniel says, a hundred and forty monks and five hundred lay brothers, so many that on feast days, the church is as crowded as a hive of bees.

[long-pause]

Aelred writes books too, many of them, in Latin.

[pause]

The most famous is called On Spiritual Friendship. It is a book about friendship, written as a conversation between Aelred and some of his monks, sitting together, talking. Aelred says that friendship is one of the greatest gifts of God, and that a true friend is a treasure. He says that God himself is friendship, and that whoever lives in friendship lives in God.

[pause]

{img:three-monks-sitting-talking-cloister-garden-sunlight}
This was a new and unusual idea, for a monk, in the twelfth century. Many monastic rules warned the monks against having special friendships. But Aelred believed that friendship, between people who love God, was a way to God. His book is still read today, all over the world.

[long-pause]

Walter Daniel describes him in 1150: a slight, slender man, with a gentle face, and a quiet voice, walking in the cloister, among his monks, or sitting in his little room, reading, or writing, or talking with a friend.

[pause]

He still has twenty more years to live, at Rievaulx. And they will be the happiest years of the abbey's history.

[long-pause]

{img:monk-writing-at-desk-cell-window-valley-thoughtful-quill}
Much of what we know about Rievaulx in the time of Aelred comes from one monk, Walter Daniel.

[pause]

Walter came to Rievaulx as a young man, in about 1150, the very year of our story. He was clever, and educated, and had trained as a doctor, it seems, before he became a monk. He was a bit proud, and quick-tempered, and liked to argue. But he loved Aelred deeply, and Aelred loved him, and was patient with him.

[long-pause]

Walter lived at Rievaulx for the rest of his life. He was with Aelred for seventeen years, and was with him when he passed away. And soon afterwards, he wrote a book about Aelred's life, in Latin, called The Life of Aelred.

[pause]

It is a wonderful book, full of little details about life at Rievaulx: how Aelred talked, and walked, and prayed, and worked. How he treated his monks. How he endured his illness. What happened when difficult monks came. And many little stories, some funny, some sad, some strange.

[pause]

Some people at the time complained that Walter had exaggerated, and made Aelred sound too perfect, like a saint in a story. So Walter wrote a letter, to defend his book, and added more details, and the names of people who had seen what he described.

[long-pause]

Walter tells us, for example, that Aelred had a little room built, next to the infirmary, when he became too ill to walk, with a little bath, or pool, beneath the floor, filled from a spring, where he would sit in cold water, in his youth, to calm his passions.

[pause]

He tells us that, in his little room, Aelred would let the monks come in, freely, every day, and sit around him, and talk with him, and that nobody else in the monastery was allowed to talk so freely, except in Aelred's room.

[pause]

{img:old-monks-and-young-gathered-sick-abbot-room-warm-light}
He tells us that Aelred was so gentle, that once, when a difficult monk shouted at him, and insulted him, in front of the others, Aelred simply waited, until the man had finished, and then answered him quietly and kindly, and the man was ashamed, and asked his forgiveness.

[long-pause]

And he tells us about Aelred's last days, in January 1167, in great detail: how he gave his last advice to his monks, and gave them his books, and his little cross, and his relics. How he lay on the floor, on a hair cloth and ashes, as monks did when they were dying. How the monks gathered around him, and sang. And how, at the very end, Walter himself held Aelred's head in his hands.

[pause]

{img:winter-dawn-valley-abbey-snow-quiet-bell-tower}
Thanks to Walter Daniel, and his book, the monks of Rievaulx, who lived and died nearly nine hundred years ago, are not just names, or ruins. We can still almost see them, and hear them, in the valley, in the cloister, around their abbot's bed, talking, and laughing, and praying.

[long-pause]

{img:cistercian-abbey-plan-cloister-church-buildings-aerial-view}
Every Cistercian monastery is built to the same plan, so that a monk from any Cistercian abbey, in any country, would feel at home, and know where everything was, in any other.

[pause]

Let us walk through Rievaulx, as it is in 1150, and see the buildings.

[long-pause]

The most important building is the church.

[pause]

It stands on the north side of the monastery, the highest and the largest building. It is long and narrow, built of pale, creamy sandstone, cut from the quarries in the valley. Inside, it is very plain. The Cistercians believe that a church should be simple, without decoration, so that nothing distracts the monks from their prayers.

[pause]

There are no paintings on the walls, no statues, no carvings, no stained glass in the windows, only plain, clear, or greyish glass. There are no tall towers, only a small bell tower, for one or two bells. The crosses are made of painted wood, not gold. The candlesticks are of iron. The robes of the priests are of plain linen or wool, not silk.

[pause]

Bernard of Clairvaux wrote that the walls of the great churches glittered, while the poor went hungry. He wanted the Cistercian churches to be beautiful only in their simplicity, and their proportions, and the light.

[long-pause]

Inside the church, the monks sit in two rows of wooden stalls, facing each other, across the choir, in the eastern part of the church, near the altar. The lay brothers have their own place, at the western end of the church, separated from the monks by a screen.

[pause]

On the south side of the church, there is the cloister.

[pause]

{img:cloister-square-garth-grass-covered-walkways-arches-light}
The cloister is the heart of the monastery. It is a square open space, with grass, or a garden, in the middle, and a well, or a fountain. Around it, on all four sides, there are covered walkways, with a roof, open to the garden, through rows of arches, held up by little columns. Here, the monks walk, and read, and meditate, and work, sheltered from the rain.

[long-pause]

Along the east side of the cloister, there is the chapter house, a beautiful hall, where the monks meet every morning, to hear a chapter of the Rule of Saint Benedict read aloud, and to discuss the business of the monastery, and confess their faults. The early abbots of the monastery are buried under its floor.

[pause]

Beside it, there is a narrow passage, the parlour, the only place in the monastery where monks are allowed to speak to each other, briefly, about necessary things.

[pause]

{img:chapter-house-stone-benches-monks-seated-listening-abbot}
Above the chapter house, and along the east side of the cloister, on the upper floor, there is the dormitory, a long hall, where all the monks sleep together, on simple beds, in their robes. A night stair leads straight down from the dormitory into the church, so that the monks can go down to pray in the night, without going outside.

[long-pause]

Along the south side of the cloister, there is the refectory, the great dining hall, where the monks eat, at long tables, in silence, while one monk reads aloud from a pulpit, built into the wall.

[pause]

Next to it, there is the kitchen, and the warming house, the only room in the monastery, besides the kitchen and the infirmary, where a fire is allowed, in winter, so that the monks can come and warm themselves, for a little while, on the coldest days.

[pause]

Along the west side of the cloister, there is the range of the lay brothers: their own refectory, below, and their own dormitory, above, and storerooms, and cellars.

[long-pause]

Further away, to the east, there is the infirmary, for the sick and the old monks, with its own little kitchen, and chapel, and garden. And the abbot's lodging. And the novices' quarters.

[pause]

And all around, there are the other buildings: the guest house, for travellers and visitors, near the gate. The mill, on the river, where the grain is ground. The bakehouse, and the brewhouse. The tannery, where leather is made. The forge, where iron tools are made. Barns, and stables, and workshops, and gardens, and orchards, and fish ponds.

[pause]

{img:monastery-mill-wheel-river-stream-stone-building-morning}
And around everything, there is a wall, with a gatehouse, where the porter sits, and welcomes visitors, and gives food to the poor.

[long-pause]

The whole monastery is like a little town, a little world, complete in itself, with everything the monks need, so that they never have to go out into the world.

[pause]

Saint Benedict wrote that a monastery should, if possible, be built so that everything necessary, water, a mill, a garden, and the various crafts, can be found within its walls, so that the monks do not need to wander outside, which is not good for their souls.

[long-pause]

{img:masons-cutting-stone-blocks-quarry-hillside-valley-1150}
In 1150, Rievaulx is still being built. In fact, it will be built, and rebuilt, and enlarged, for more than a hundred years.

[pause]

When the first monks arrived, in 1132, they lived in simple wooden huts, and prayed in a little wooden chapel. Then, slowly, they began to build in stone. By 1150, they have built a great stone church, one of the first Cistercian churches in England, and are building the cloister, and the other buildings around it.

[long-pause]

The stone comes from the valley itself.

[pause]

On the hillsides above the abbey, there are quarries, where the masons cut the stone: a pale, creamy, golden sandstone, which is easy to cut when it is fresh, and hardens in the air. The lay brothers, and hired workmen, split the great blocks from the rock, with iron wedges, and hammers, and crowbars.

[pause]

To bring the stone down to the building site, the monks dig a canal, a little channel, from the river, along the valley, so that the stone can be floated on rafts, or small boats, from the quarries to the abbey. Archaeologists have found the traces of these channels, in the valley, still today.

[long-pause]

At the building site, the master mason is in charge.

[pause]

He is a skilled craftsman, often a layman, hired by the abbey, who has learned his trade over many years, travelling from one great building to another. He draws the plans, on the ground, or on a floor of plaster, with compasses and measuring rods, and cuts templates, of wood, for the shapes of the stones.

[pause]

{img:master-mason-drawing-plan-plaster-floor-compass-rods}
The masons cut each stone carefully, with axes, and chisels, to the right shape, square and smooth, and each mason carves his own little mark on his stones, so that he can be paid for them. These masons' marks can still be seen on the stones of Rievaulx today: little crosses, and arrows, and stars, and letters.

[long-pause]

The walls are built of two faces of cut stone, with a core of rubble and mortar in between. The mortar is made of lime, which is burned in kilns, near the site, from limestone, and mixed with sand and water.

[pause]

As the walls rise, the carpenters build scaffolding, of wooden poles, tied together with ropes, and fixed into holes in the walls, which can still be seen, in many old buildings, as little square holes, in rows. The stones are lifted up, by cranes, worked by men walking inside great wooden wheels, like hamster wheels, called treadwheels.

[pause]

{img:treadwheel-crane-lifting-stone-scaffolding-church-wall}
The arches are built over wooden frames, called centring, which hold up the stones until the mortar sets, and the arch can stand by itself. Then the frame is taken away, and moved to the next arch.

[long-pause]

The roofs are made of great oak beams, cut from the woods of the valley, and covered with lead sheets, or with stone slates, or with tiles. The floors are of stone, or of beaten earth, and later, of tiles, made in the abbey's own kilns, glazed in green and yellow, laid in simple patterns.

[pause]

It is slow work. A great church can take thirty or forty years, or more, to build. The masons can only work in the warmer months. In winter, the walls are covered with straw and turf, to protect the new mortar from the frost.

[pause]

The monks, it seems, do not mind. They are building, they believe, not for themselves, but for God, and for the monks who will come after them, for hundreds of years.

[long-pause]

And there are other builders in the valley too.

[pause]

The monks build their mill, on the river, with a great wooden wheel, turned by the water, and millstones, to grind the grain. They build fish ponds, where they keep fish, carp and trout and eels, for the infirmary, and for the guests. They build the walls of the precinct, and the gatehouse, and the barns, and the bridges over the river.

[pause]

{img:monastery-fishponds-terraced-water-reeds-valley-calm}
Little by little, year by year, stone by stone, the monks turn their wild valley into one of the great places of medieval England.

[long-pause]

{img:dark-dormitory-monks-sleeping-row-simple-beds-moonlight}
The day of a Cistercian monk begins in the middle of the night.

[pause]

At about two o'clock in the morning, in winter, a little later in summer, the monk whose job it is to wake the others rings a small bell, in the dark dormitory. The monks wake up, and get up, at once, from their simple beds of straw, with a blanket and a pillow.

[long-pause]

They do not need to dress, because they sleep in their robes. They put on their night shoes, and pull their hoods over their heads, and walk, silently, in a line, down the night stair, into the dark, cold church.

[pause]

The church is lit only by a few little lamps, and candles. It is very cold, especially in winter, when the stone walls and floors are icy, and the monks' breath steams in the air. The monks go to their places in the choir, and bow to the altar, and wait.

[pause]

{img:monks-descending-night-stair-candles-dark-church-cold}
Then the first service of the day begins: Vigils, the night office, also called Matins.

[long-pause]

The monks sing the psalms: the hundred and fifty songs of the Bible, which they know by heart. They sing them in Latin, in Gregorian chant, a simple, flowing melody, sung by all the monks together, in unison, without instruments, one side of the choir answering the other, back and forth, verse by verse.

[pause]

Between the psalms, there are readings, from the Bible, and from the writings of the Church Fathers, read aloud by one of the monks, by the light of a candle.

[pause]

The night office is long. It lasts for an hour or more, sometimes two, on great feasts. Some of the monks are sleepy. One of the monks walks up and down the choir, with a little lantern, and if he finds a monk asleep, he gently wakes him up.

[long-pause]

After Vigils, in winter, there is a long time before dawn. The monks may go back to bed, for a little while, or stay in the church, or in the cloister, reading, and praying, by the light of a lamp.

[pause]

Then, at first light, there is the second service, Lauds, the morning praise, when the monks sing the psalms of praise, as the sun rises, and the light begins to come through the plain windows of the church, grey, and then gold.

[pause]

{img:first-light-through-plain-church-windows-monks-praying-dawn}
For the monks, the night office is the most beautiful time of the day. Everything is dark, and silent, and still. The world outside is asleep. And only the monks are awake, keeping watch, and singing, in the darkness, for all the people who are sleeping, and for all the world.

[long-pause]

Saint Benedict wrote that the monks should rise at night to praise God, because the psalm says: at midnight I rose to give thanks to you.

[pause]

And so, every night, in every monastery in Europe, in the darkness, the monks rise, and go down to their churches, and sing the same psalms, in the same way. As they have done for six hundred years. And as some monks still do today.

[pause]

{img:abbey-at-night-valley-stars-faint-light-church-windows}
If you had been standing on the hillside above Rievaulx, on a winter night in 1150, in the darkness, you would have seen the faint glow of the candles, in the windows of the church, far below. And perhaps, if the wind was right, you would have heard, very faintly, the sound of the monks singing, rising and falling, in the cold night air.

[long-pause]

{img:monk-watching-stars-cloister-night-sacristan-lantern}
How do the monks know when to get up, in the middle of the night, in the year 1150, when there are no clocks?

[pause]

Mechanical clocks, with weights and gears, will not be invented for another hundred and fifty years. So the monks must find other ways to tell the time.

[long-pause]

The monk responsible for waking the others is the sacristan, who looks after the church. He must be careful, and reliable, and he does not sleep much.

[pause]

On clear nights, he watches the stars. The old monastery books explain which stars rise at which times, in different months, and how to tell the hour by looking at them, through a certain window, or over a certain roof. When a particular star appears over the roof of the dormitory, it is time to ring the bell.

[pause]

{img:clear-winter-night-stars-over-monastery-roof-orion}
On cloudy nights, he uses candles, marked with lines, which burn down at a steady rate, so many lines an hour. Or a water clock: a vessel of water, with a little hole, through which the water drips slowly, into another vessel, marked with the hours. Or he simply recites psalms, in the dark, a fixed number of them, knowing how long each one takes.

[long-pause]

In the daytime, there are sundials, on the south wall of the church, simple ones, with lines carved in the stone, and a stick, casting a shadow, to show the hours of the services.

[pause]

And there is the bell. Everything in the monastery is ruled by the bell. When the bell rings, every monk stops what he is doing, at once, even in the middle of writing a letter, Benedict says, and goes to the church.

[pause]

{img:simple-sundial-carved-church-wall-shadow-stick-sunlight}
The monks do not think of time as we do, in minutes and seconds. They think of it in the hours of prayer, and the seasons, and the feasts, and the light and the dark. Their time is measured not by machines, but by the sun, and the stars, and the singing.

[long-pause]

Sometimes, of course, the sacristan makes a mistake, and wakes the monks too early, or too late.

[pause]

The old monastic books tell what to do then. If the monks are woken too late, the night office must be shortened, so that the morning service can begin at dawn. And the sacristan must confess his fault, at the chapter, the next morning, in front of everyone.

[long-pause]

{img:monks-chapter-meeting-morning-light-stone-benches-reading}
After the services of the night and the dawn, the day of the monks goes on, in a fixed rhythm, which is the same every day, all year round, with small changes for the seasons, and the feasts.

[pause]

The day is divided by the services in the church, which the monks call the Divine Office, or the Work of God, the opus Dei. Seven times a day, and once in the night, the bell rings, and the monks stop whatever they are doing, and go to the church, to pray and sing.

[long-pause]

After Lauds, at sunrise, there is Prime, the first hour. And then the monks go to the chapter house, for the daily meeting, the chapter.

[pause]

They sit on stone benches, around the walls, with the abbot in his seat, at the end. A monk reads a chapter of the Rule of Saint Benedict, aloud. Then the abbot speaks, about the Rule, or about the life of the monastery. Then the names of monks who have passed away, in other monasteries, are read, and the monks pray for them.

[pause]

And then comes the chapter of faults. Any monk who has broken one of the rules, by being late for a service, or breaking a dish, or speaking when he should be silent, comes forward, and kneels, and confesses it, and receives a small penance, from the abbot. Other monks may also point out, kindly, the faults of their brothers. It is a way of keeping the community honest, and humble.

[long-pause]

Then, in the morning, there is work.

[pause]

The Cistercians believe, like Saint Benedict, that work is a kind of prayer, and that monks should work with their hands, as the apostles did. Idleness, Benedict wrote, is the enemy of the soul.

[pause]

{img:monks-working-fields-hoes-white-habits-tucked-up-sunny}
So the monks go out to work, in silence: in the gardens, and the orchards, and the fields, digging, and planting, and weeding, and harvesting. Or in the workshops, mending shoes, and clothes, and tools. Or in the kitchen, or the bakehouse. Or cleaning the buildings. At harvest time, everyone, even the abbot, goes out to help bring in the crops.

[long-pause]

At about nine o'clock, the bell rings for Terce, the third hour. Then there is the Mass, the most important service of the day, when the monks gather around the altar, and the priest offers the bread and the wine.

[pause]

At midday, there is Sext, the sixth hour. And then, in summer, the main meal of the day, dinner, in the refectory. In winter, during the fasting season, dinner is later, in the afternoon.

[pause]

After dinner, in summer, the monks may rest for a while, on their beds, in the dormitory, because they rose so early, and the days are long. It is called the meridian, the midday rest.

[long-pause]

In the early afternoon, there is None, the ninth hour. And then more work, or reading.

[pause]

Every monk must spend some hours each day in lectio divina, holy reading. He sits in the cloister, on a stone bench, or in a little carrel, a small wooden desk, against the wall, with a book from the library, and reads, slowly, aloud, in a low murmur, a few lines at a time, from the Bible, or the writings of the saints, and thinks about them, and prays.

[pause]

{img:monk-reading-book-cloister-carrel-afternoon-sun-quiet}
It is not like our reading, fast, for information. It is slow, and repeated, like chewing, the monks say, so that the words sink deep into the heart, and nourish the soul.

[long-pause]

In the late afternoon, as the sun goes down, there is Vespers, the evening service, with psalms, and the beautiful song of Mary, the Magnificat, sung by all the monks, as the light fades in the church.

[pause]

Then a light supper, in summer. In winter, during the fast, there is no supper, only a drink, and a little bread.

[pause]

And then the monks gather in the cloister, or the chapter house, and one of them reads aloud, from a book of the lives of the saints, or the sayings of the old monks of the desert, by the light of a lamp. This is called the collation.

[long-pause]

And last of all, as darkness falls, there is Compline, the last service of the day, the completion, short and quiet, with the psalms of the night. The monks pray for protection, during the night, and sing a hymn to Mary, the Salve Regina, hail holy queen, softly, in the dark church.

[pause]

And then the abbot sprinkles each monk with holy water, as they leave the church. And the Great Silence begins, which lasts all night, until the morning. Nobody speaks, at all, for any reason.

[pause]

{img:monks-climbing-stairs-dormitory-candle-evening-silence}
The monks climb the stairs to the dormitory, and lie down on their beds, in their robes, and go to sleep, at about seven or eight o'clock in the evening, in winter. A little lamp burns all night, in the dormitory. And outside, the river flows, and the owls call, in the woods of the valley.

[long-pause]

{img:large-choir-book-on-lectern-monks-gathered-singing-chant}
The monks of Rievaulx spend many hours a day singing. And their singing is one of the most beautiful things about their life.

[pause]

They sing Gregorian chant, which is named after Pope Gregory the Great, who lived about five hundred years before, although the chant itself is probably older, and grew slowly, over the centuries, in the churches and the monasteries of Europe.

[long-pause]

Gregorian chant is very simple. It is sung by all the monks together, in unison, all singing the same note, at the same time, without harmony, and without any instruments: no organ, no harps, no bells, only the human voice.

[pause]

The melodies are long, and flowing, and calm, rising and falling gently, like waves, or like breathing. There is no steady beat, like in modern music. The music follows the rhythm of the Latin words. Sometimes, one syllable is sung on many notes, in a long, winding melody, especially on the word Alleluia.

[pause]

{img:chant-manuscript-square-notes-four-line-staff-red-lines}
The music is written in the great choir books, in little square notes, on four lines, in black ink, with the words underneath. But most of the monks know the chant by heart, because they sing it every day, for years.

[long-pause]

The Cistercians wanted their chant to be pure, and simple, and correct, like everything else in their life.

[pause]

So, in Bernard's time, they sent monks to Metz, in France, which was said to have the most authentic chant, to copy the books. And later, Bernard himself oversaw a revision of the chant, removing what he thought were later additions, and too elaborate melodies, so that the chant would be simpler, and closer to what he believed was the original.

[pause]

In the church, the monks stand in their stalls, in two rows, facing each other across the choir. One side sings one verse of a psalm, and the other side answers with the next. Back and forth, back and forth, for hours, like a long, gentle conversation.

[long-pause]

Aelred himself wrote about singing, in one of his books, The Mirror of Charity.

[pause]

He complained about some churches, where the singers showed off, with strange voices, and trills, and high notes, and twisted melodies, and dramatic gestures, so that the people listening forgot to pray, and only admired the singers. He said that the singing should be simple, and humble, and help the heart to pray, not distract it.

[pause]

{img:empty-abbey-church-choir-stalls-soft-light-dust}
At Rievaulx, the chant is sung slowly, and quietly, and simply, in the plain stone church, which echoes the voices softly, so that the sound seems to float in the air, and fill the whole building.

[long-pause]

For a monk, the singing is not a performance. It is prayer.

[pause]

Saint Benedict wrote that the monks should sing in such a way that their mind is in harmony with their voice. And that nothing should be put before the Work of God.

[pause]

{img:monk-alone-church-dawn-singing-quietly-candle}
Many people today, who are not monks, and not religious, still listen to Gregorian chant, on recordings, to help them relax, or to sleep. Something in its long, calm, flowing melodies, sung by many voices as one, seems to quiet the mind, as it did for the monks of Rievaulx, nine hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:silent-cloister-monk-walking-alone-shadows-arches-afternoon}
One of the most remarkable things about a Cistercian monastery is the silence.

[pause]

The monks of Rievaulx do not speak to each other, except when it is absolutely necessary. Not in the church, except to sing and pray. Not in the cloister. Not in the refectory, at meals. Not in the dormitory. Not at work. And never during the Great Silence, from Compline in the evening until the morning.

[long-pause]

Saint Benedict wrote that monks should love silence, and should speak rarely, and only of useful things, and gently, and humbly, without laughing, and without many words. He wrote that in much speaking, you cannot avoid sin.

[pause]

The Cistercians follow this very strictly. They believe that silence helps the monks to listen: to God, and to their own hearts. In the silence, they can pray all the time, even while they work.

[pause]

{img:monks-hoeing-garden-rows-silent-misty-morning-valley}
A monk who wants to speak to the abbot, or to one of the officials of the monastery, about something necessary, may do so, briefly, in a quiet voice, in the parlour, or in a special place. But otherwise, there is no conversation.

[long-pause]

So how do the monks communicate?

[pause]

They use signs. Over the centuries, the monks of Europe developed a sign language, with hundreds of signs, made with the hands, so that they could say simple things to each other, without breaking the silence.

[pause]

There is a sign for bread: you make a circle with your thumbs and forefingers, because the loaves are round. A sign for fish: you move your hand like a fish's tail, swimming in the water. A sign for milk: you put your little finger to your lips, like a baby sucking. A sign for honey: you put your tongue out a little, and touch it with your fingers. A sign for a book: you move your hand as if turning a page. A sign for the abbot: you touch your forehead, and pull your fingers away, as if pulling a hood.

[long-pause]

Lists of these signs were written down, in the monasteries, so that new monks could learn them. Some of these lists still survive. One, from an English monastery, has more than a hundred and twenty signs.

[pause]

The signs are only for necessary things: pass me the bread, please. Where is the key? The abbot wants you. It is not supposed to be used for chatting. But of course, some monks, being human, did use the signs to chat, and to joke, and the abbots complained about it.

[pause]

{img:refectory-monks-eating-silently-one-reading-pulpit-lamp}
The Cistercian monasteries were so quiet that visitors were amazed. A writer of the time describes walking into a Cistercian abbey, with hundreds of monks and lay brothers, at work, all over the valley, and hearing nothing but the sound of their tools, and the singing in the church.

[long-pause]

What is it like, to live in silence, for years, for a whole lifetime?

[pause]

For the monks, it was not empty. The silence was full: of prayer, and of the sounds of nature, the birds, and the wind, and the river, and the bells, and the singing in the church. And full of the presence of God, they believed.

[pause]

{img:river-rye-quiet-evening-abbey-reflections-birdsong}
Aelred himself wrote, in one of his books, about the peace of Rievaulx: everywhere peace, everywhere serenity, and a marvellous freedom from the tumult of the world. Such harmony among the brothers, that each thing seems to belong to all, and all to each.

[long-pause]

And yet, in the silence, the monks were not alone. They lived together, every day, for their whole lives, side by side, in the church, and the cloister, and the refectory, and the dormitory, and the fields. They knew each other very well, without words: their faces, and their footsteps, and their voices when they sang, and their ways.

[pause]

And when they did speak, with Aelred, in the rare moments of conversation that he allowed, as in his book on friendship, every word was precious.

[long-pause]

{img:refectory-long-tables-bread-bowls-pottery-monks-seated}
The monks of Rievaulx eat simply.

[pause]

The Rule of Saint Benedict says that the monks should have one or two meals a day, depending on the season, with two cooked dishes at each meal, so that if a monk cannot eat one, he can eat the other. And a pound of bread a day. And a measure of wine, or, in England, of ale. And fruit or vegetables, when they are available.

[long-pause]

The Cistercians follow this strictly. They eat no meat at all, except for the sick, in the infirmary. Not even fish, at first, although later they will allow it. No eggs, or cheese, or milk, during the fasting seasons. No spices, or rich sauces. No white bread, only coarse brown bread, made of rye, or barley, or wheat, with the bran left in.

[pause]

The main food is bread, and pottage: a thick soup, or stew, of beans, or peas, or lentils, cooked with vegetables from the garden, cabbages, and leeks, and onions, and herbs, and seasoned with a little salt.

[pause]

From the middle of September until Easter, the monks eat only one meal a day, in the afternoon. And during Lent, the forty days before Easter, the meal is in the evening, after Vespers. From Easter until September, there are two meals: dinner at midday, and supper in the evening.

[long-pause]

The meals are eaten in the refectory, in silence.

[pause]

Before the meal, the monks wash their hands, at a long stone basin, with running water, in the cloister, outside the refectory door. Then they go in, and stand at their places, at the long wooden tables, along the walls, and the abbot says a blessing.

[pause]

{img:stone-lavatorium-basin-running-water-monks-washing-hands}
The tables are covered with white linen cloths. Each monk has a wooden bowl, and a spoon, and a knife, and a cup, and a napkin, and a loaf of bread. The food is brought from the kitchen by the monks who are serving that week, in turns, and set before each monk.

[long-pause]

While the monks eat, one monk reads aloud, from a pulpit, built high in the wall of the refectory, with a little staircase inside the wall. He reads from the Bible, or from the writings of the saints, or from the Rule, in Latin, in a clear, steady voice.

[pause]

The monks listen, and eat, in silence. If a monk needs something, salt, or bread, or water, he makes a sign, and his neighbour passes it to him. They must not look around, or make noise with their spoons, or eat greedily, or leave food on their plates.

[pause]

{img:reader-monk-refectory-pulpit-book-candle-evening-meal}
After the meal, the monks say grace together, and leave the refectory, in procession, singing a psalm, and go to the church, to give thanks.

[long-pause]

Occasionally, on feast days, or when a benefactor has given money for it, there is a little extra: a pittance, a special dish, of eggs, or cheese, or fish, or a little better bread, or a cake made with honey, or better ale. The monks look forward to these very much.

[pause]

The lay brothers, who do heavy work in the fields, eat more, and earlier, and more often, than the monks, because they need the strength.

[pause]

The bread is baked in the monastery bakehouse, in great ovens, every day. The ale is brewed in the brewhouse, from barley grown on the abbey's fields. Vegetables and herbs and fruit come from the gardens and orchards. And beans and peas from the fields. The monastery produces almost all its own food.

[long-pause]

The Cistercians eat so simply, and fast so often, that some people of the time think they are too strict.

[pause]

But Aelred, in his kindness, makes sure that the sick, and the old, and the weak, are given better food, in the infirmary, and that no one is made ill by too much fasting. He himself eats very little, because of his own illness, and gives his own portion to the poor.

[pause]

{img:abbot-giving-bread-to-poor-at-gate-winter-morning}
And every day, at the gate of the monastery, the porter gives out bread, and the leftovers from the refectory, to the poor who come to the gate. In hard times, when there is famine, hundreds of poor people come, and the monks feed them all, as best they can.

[long-pause]

{img:monastery-water-channel-stone-culvert-stream-flowing-mill}
The Cistercians are not only men of prayer. They are also some of the best engineers and farmers of the Middle Ages.

[pause]

Water is very important to them. Every Cistercian monastery is built near a river, or a stream, and the monks use the water in many clever ways.

[long-pause]

At Rievaulx, the monks dig channels, from the river Rye, and from the streams that run down the hillsides, to bring water to every part of the monastery.

[pause]

One channel turns the great wheel of the mill, which grinds the corn into flour, for the bread. Another runs under the kitchen, and the latrines, carrying away the waste, like a sewer, and washing everything clean. Another fills the fish ponds. Fresh, clean water for drinking and washing is brought from springs, in pipes of lead, or of hollowed wood, to the great basin in the cloister, and to the kitchen.

[pause]

A monk of the time, describing the Cistercian abbey of Clairvaux, wrote a beautiful passage about its river, and how it served the monks: turning the mill, and the fulling mill, which beats the cloth, and helping the brewers, and the tanners, and the gardeners, and then, having done all its work, flowing away, cleaning everything as it goes, back to the main river.

[long-pause]

The monks of Rievaulx also become ironmakers.

[pause]

In the hills around the valley, there is iron ore, in the rocks. And there is plenty of wood, for charcoal. So the lay brothers dig the ore, and burn the wood into charcoal, in great slow-burning piles, covered with earth, in the woods. And they smelt the iron, in small furnaces of clay and stone, blown by bellows, and hammer it into bars, and tools.

[pause]

{img:charcoal-burners-earth-mound-smoke-woods-lay-brother}
Much later, by the early sixteenth century, the monks of Rievaulx have an iron furnace at a place called Laskill, a few miles up the valley. Archaeologists who studied the slag from Laskill believe that it may have been one of the most advanced furnaces in Europe, and that the monks may have been close to making cast iron, before it became common in England.

[long-pause]

The monks use the iron for their own tools: ploughshares, and spades, and axes, and nails, and hinges, and locks, and horseshoes. And they sell it, to people around, along with their wool, and their corn.

[pause]

They also drain the marshes, in the valley bottom, with ditches, to make good meadows and fields. They improve the breeds of their sheep, and their cattle, and their horses. They plant orchards, and teach the people around how to graft fruit trees.

[pause]

{img:orchard-monk-grafting-apple-tree-spring-blossom}
All over Europe, the Cistercians are pioneers, clearing new land, and draining marshes, and building mills, and improving farming. They believe that work is prayer. And their work changes the landscape of Europe, in ways that can still be seen today.

[long-pause]

{img:lay-brothers-brown-habits-beards-working-farm-grange}
The Cistercian monasteries have two kinds of members: the monks, and the lay brothers.

[pause]

The monks are usually from noble or well-off families. They can read and write Latin, and they spend much of their time in the church, singing the services, and in reading, and prayer, as well as work.

[long-pause]

The lay brothers, called the conversi, are usually from poor families, peasants and labourers, who cannot read or write, and do not know Latin. They make the same vows as the monks, and live a religious life, in the monastery. But their life is mostly work.

[pause]

They wear robes of brown or grey, and have beards, unlike the monks, who are shaved. They have their own part of the monastery, at the west side of the cloister, with their own refectory and dormitory, and their own place, at the back of the church. They say simple prayers, which they learn by heart, the Our Father, and the Hail Mary, instead of singing the long services.

[pause]

{img:lay-brothers-praying-simple-back-of-church-dim-light}
The lay brothers do most of the heavy work of the monastery: farming, and building, and looking after the animals, and working in the mill, and the tannery, and the forge. Without them, the great Cistercian monasteries could not exist.

[long-pause]

Many of the lay brothers live and work on the granges.

[pause]

A grange is a farm, belonging to the abbey, some distance away, in the hills, or in the valleys, around the monastery. Rievaulx has many granges, some of them miles away, on the moors, and in the valleys, and on the coast. Each one has a farmhouse, and barns, and a little chapel, and is run by a group of lay brothers, who live there, and come back to the abbey only on Sundays and great feasts.

[pause]

On the granges, the lay brothers grow wheat, and oats, and barley, and beans, and peas. They keep cattle, and pigs, and horses. And above all, they keep sheep.

[long-pause]

The Cistercians of Yorkshire become the greatest sheep farmers in England.

[pause]

On the wide, empty moors and hills around the monasteries, they graze great flocks, thousands and thousands of sheep. Rievaulx, a century later, will have about fourteen thousand sheep. And the wool of the Cistercian abbeys of Yorkshire is the finest in England, and is sold to merchants from Flanders and Italy, who take it to the great cloth-making cities of Europe, like Bruges and Ghent and Florence.

[pause]

{img:shepherd-lay-brother-flock-sheep-heather-moorland-wind}
The money from the wool makes the Cistercian monasteries rich, although the monks themselves live in poverty. It pays for the great churches, and the buildings, and the books.

[long-pause]

The life of a lay brother is hard.

[pause]

He works long hours, in all weathers, in the fields, and on the moors, with the sheep, in the rain and the wind and the snow. In the lambing season, in the spring, he is out day and night, with the ewes. At harvest, he works from dawn to dusk. He eats simple food, and sleeps in a dormitory, on a straw bed.

[pause]

{img:lay-brothers-lambing-shelter-moor-spring-dawn-lantern}
But for many poor men of the twelfth century, the life of a lay brother is better than the life they would have had outside. They have enough to eat, and a bed to sleep in, and clothes, and care when they are ill or old, and a place in a great community. And they believe that they are working for God, and for the salvation of their souls.

[long-pause]

Aelred, it is said, loves the lay brothers, and cares for them, as much as for the monks. He knows many of them by name, and visits them, and talks with them, and listens to their troubles.

[pause]

Among the lay brothers, there are some simple, holy men, who cannot read a single word, but who pray all day long, as they work, in the fields, and on the moors. Aelred respects them greatly, and often says that they are closer to God than many learned men.

[long-pause]

{img:grange-farm-moorland-edge-stone-barn-sheepfold-dawn}
Let us spend a day with the lay brothers, on one of the granges of Rievaulx, high on the edge of the moors, in the spring of 1150.

[pause]

The grange is a little group of buildings, of stone and timber, with thatched roofs: a long house, where the lay brothers live, with a hall, and a kitchen, and a dormitory above. A great barn, for the corn. Sheepfolds, built of stone. Stables, for the horses and the oxen. And a little chapel, where the lay brothers say their prayers.

[long-pause]

There are perhaps ten or twelve lay brothers here, under the master of the grange, an older lay brother, who is in charge. And there are some hired servants, and shepherds, from the villages around, who work for the abbey for wages.

[pause]

The day begins early, before dawn, with prayers, in the little chapel, said together, by heart, in the dark: the Our Father, and the Hail Mary, and the Creed, repeated many times, instead of the long psalms that the monks sing at the abbey.

[pause]

Then breakfast: bread, and pottage, and ale. The lay brothers, who work hard in the fields, are allowed more food than the monks.

[long-pause]

Then everyone goes out to work.

[pause]

It is the lambing season. Out on the hillside, around the grange, hundreds of ewes are giving birth, and the shepherds are watching them, day and night. The lambs are born in the cold spring wind, wet and wobbly, and the shepherds help them, if they are in trouble, and carry the weak ones into the shelter of the sheepfolds, and the barn, and rub them dry, with straw, and help them to suckle.

[pause]

{img:shepherd-carrying-newborn-lamb-hillside-spring-wind}
The shepherds have dogs, to help them gather the sheep, and they know every ewe in the flock, and call them by their marks. They sleep in little huts, on the hillside, during lambing, to be near the flock.

[long-pause]

Other lay brothers are ploughing, in the fields below the grange, with teams of oxen, eight oxen to a plough, walking slowly, up and down the long strips of land, turning over the dark earth. A boy walks in front of the oxen, calling to them, and guiding them, with a long stick. Behind them, the gulls and the rooks follow, picking up the worms.

[pause]

Others are sowing oats and barley, walking across the ploughed fields, scattering the seed from a basket, with a wide sweep of the arm, left and right, in a steady rhythm.

[pause]

{img:oxen-team-ploughing-strip-field-boy-guiding-rooks}
Others are mending the stone walls, which keep the sheep out of the crops. Others are cutting wood, in the copse, and carting it to the grange, for the fires. Others are working in the forge, making and mending tools: ploughshares, and sickles, and spades, and horseshoes.

[long-pause]

At midday, the lay brothers stop, and say a short prayer, in the field, wherever they are, when they hear the little bell of the grange chapel. And they eat a meal: bread, and cheese, and ale, sitting in the shelter of a wall.

[pause]

Then they go back to work, until the evening.

[pause]

In the evening, they come back to the grange, tired, and muddy, and wet, and wash, and eat their supper, in the hall, in silence, while one of them, who can read a little, or a monk visiting from the abbey, reads aloud. And then the evening prayers, in the chapel. And then to bed, in the dormitory, in the long, low room under the thatch, with the wind blowing outside, across the moor, and the sheep calling in the darkness.

[long-pause]

On Saturday evening, the lay brothers of the granges walk back to the abbey, across the moors and down into the valley, to spend Sunday there, with the community.

[pause]

They go to Mass, in the abbey church, at the back, behind the screen, and hear the monks singing. They go to the chapter, where the abbot speaks to them, in English, or in French, and gives them news, and advice, and listens to their troubles. They eat in their refectory, and sleep in their dormitory, in the west range of the cloister.

[pause]

{img:lay-brothers-walking-home-across-moor-evening-sunset}
And on Monday morning, before dawn, they walk back to the granges, across the moors, to begin another week of work, among the sheep, and the fields, and the wind.

[long-pause]

{img:monk-copying-manuscript-quill-parchment-cloister-desk-light}
Every monastery needs books.

[pause]

The monks need books for the services in the church: great books of psalms, and hymns, and readings, and music, big enough to be read by several monks at once, from the choir stalls. They need Bibles, and the writings of the Church Fathers, and the lives of the saints, for their holy reading. And the Rule of Saint Benedict. And books of history, and medicine, and law, and grammar.

[long-pause]

In 1150, there are no printed books. Printing will not be invented in Europe for another three hundred years. Every book must be copied by hand, letter by letter, word by word, page by page. And much of this copying is done by monks.

[pause]

At Rievaulx, the monks copy books in the cloister, sitting at desks in the walkway along the north side, next to the church, where the light is best, because it faces south, into the sun.

[pause]

{img:parchment-making-skins-stretched-frames-scraping-monk}
First, they need parchment, to write on. Parchment is made from the skins of calves, or sheep, or goats. The skins are soaked in lime, and scraped clean of hair and flesh, and stretched on wooden frames, and dried, and scraped again, and rubbed smooth with pumice stone, until they are thin, and white, and smooth. One large Bible might need the skins of two hundred animals.

[long-pause]

Then the parchment is cut into sheets, and folded, and the lines are ruled on it, with a sharp point, or a little lead, so that the writing will be straight.

[pause]

The monks write with quills, made from the feathers of geese or swans, cut to a fine point with a penknife. They use ink made from oak galls, the little round growths on oak trees, crushed, and mixed with iron, and gum, and water, which makes a deep black ink, that turns brown with age.

[pause]

They write slowly, carefully, in a beautiful, clear, rounded script, in Latin. A good scribe might copy two or three pages a day. A large book might take a year, or more, to copy.

[long-pause]

The work is hard. The scribe sits for hours, bent over his desk, in the cold cloister, with cold fingers. Sometimes, at the end of a book, the scribes write little notes, in the margins, which still exist: thank God it will soon be dark. Or: my hand is tired. Or: three fingers write, but the whole body labours. Or: now I have written the whole thing, for Christ's sake give me a drink.

[pause]

The scribes believe that copying books is a holy work, a way of praying with their hands. Every letter they write, it is said, is a blow against the devil.

[long-pause]

In the great Benedictine monasteries, the books are often decorated with beautiful pictures, called illuminations, in bright colours, red and blue and green, and gold leaf, which shines in the candlelight. Great initial letters, at the beginning of chapters, filled with flowers, and animals, and dragons, and little scenes.

[pause]

But the Cistercians, in the spirit of Bernard, decide that their books should be simple too. In about 1150, the General Chapter at Cîteaux orders that the initial letters in Cistercian books should be of a single colour, without gold, and without pictures. Bernard had complained about the strange monsters and fantastic creatures in the books and the cloisters of other monasteries, which distracted the monks from their prayers.

[pause]

{img:simple-red-initial-letter-manuscript-page-clear-script}
So the books of Rievaulx are beautiful, but plain: clear black writing, with red or blue initial letters, and nothing else.

[long-pause]

The library of Rievaulx grows, over the years, to more than two hundred books, which is a great library for the time.

[pause]

A list of the books, made in the late twelfth century, still exists. There are Bibles, and commentaries, and the works of the great Church Fathers, Augustine, and Jerome, and Gregory, and Ambrose. There are the writings of Bernard, and of Aelred himself. There are books of history, by the English monk Bede, who lived nearby, four hundred years before, and books of medicine, and grammar.

[pause]

{img:monastery-book-cupboard-cloister-wall-chained-volumes}
The books are kept in a cupboard, built into the wall of the cloister, near the door of the church, called the armarium. Once a year, at the beginning of Lent, each monk is given a book from the library, to read through the year. The Rule of Saint Benedict says so.

[long-pause]

Some of the books of Rievaulx still exist today, in libraries in England, in Cambridge and Oxford, and London. When the monastery was closed, in the sixteenth century, most of its books were scattered, or lost, or destroyed. But a few were saved, by collectors, and we can still turn their pages, and see the writing of the monks, nearly nine hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:monastery-infirmary-hall-beds-fire-old-monks-resting}
Saint Benedict wrote that the care of the sick must come before everything else, and above everything else, so that they are truly served, as if they were Christ himself.

[pause]

So every monastery has an infirmary: a separate building, for the sick, and the old, and the weak, with its own little kitchen, and chapel, and garden, and its own monk in charge, the infirmarian.

[long-pause]

At Rievaulx, the infirmary stands to the east of the cloister, in a quiet place, away from the noise of the workshops. It is a long, warm hall, with beds along the walls, and a great fireplace, where a fire burns all day, in winter. It is the only place in the monastery, besides the warming house and the kitchen, where there is a fire.

[pause]

In the infirmary, the rules are gentler. The sick monks may eat meat, to make them strong again. They may sleep longer, and miss the night office. They may talk a little. They are looked after with great care.

[pause]

{img:infirmarian-monk-giving-warm-drink-sick-brother-bed}
The old monks, who are too weak to keep the full life of the monastery, live in the infirmary too, in their last years, and pray, and rest, and are cared for by their brothers, until they pass away, peacefully, surrounded by the community, singing psalms around their beds.

[long-pause]

The infirmarian knows a great deal about medicine.

[pause]

He has books of medicine, copied from the old Greek and Roman doctors, and from the Arabic doctors, which are just beginning to be translated into Latin in these years. He knows about herbs, and their powers, and grows them in the infirmary garden.

[pause]

In the herb garden, there are neat little beds, each with a different plant: sage, for sore throats. Mint, for the stomach. Rue, and fennel, and lovage, and rosemary, and lavender, and camomile, and feverfew, for headaches. Comfrey, which the monks call knitbone, for healing broken bones and wounds. Marigolds, and lilies, and roses, for medicines, and for beauty.

[long-pause]

The infirmarian makes medicines, from the herbs: drinks, and ointments, and poultices, and syrups. He mixes them with honey, and wine, and vinegar. He prescribes rest, and warmth, and good food, and baths.

[pause]

He also bleeds the monks, a few times a year, which the doctors of that time believe keeps people healthy. After the bleeding, the monks are allowed a few days of rest, in the infirmary, with better food, and a little conversation, and walks in the garden. Some monks look forward to it, as a little holiday.

[pause]

{img:monks-resting-infirmary-garden-bench-sunshine-quiet}
Aelred himself spends much of his later life in pain, from his illness, and in his last years, he cannot walk, and lives in a little room, built for him, next to the infirmary. But his monks come to him there, every day, twenty or thirty at a time, and sit around him, on his bed, and on the floor, and talk with him, freely, like children with their mother, as Walter Daniel says. And he talks with them, and teaches them, and listens to them.

[long-pause]

The monastery also cares for the sick of the countryside around.

[pause]

Poor people who are ill come to the gate, and are given food, and medicine, and sometimes a bed, in the guest house. The monks are among the few people in the countryside who know anything about medicine.

[pause]

{img:monk-at-gate-giving-medicine-poor-woman-child-morning}
And the monasteries also keep bees, for honey, which is used in medicines, and for sweetening, and for wax, for the candles in the church. The beehives, of woven straw, called skeps, stand in a row, in a sheltered corner of the garden, humming in the summer sun.

[long-pause]

{img:young-man-knocking-monastery-gate-porter-opening-morning}
How does a man become a monk at Rievaulx?

[pause]

In the older Benedictine monasteries, many monks were given to the monastery as little children, by their parents, and grew up there. But the Cistercians do not accept children. A man must be at least fifteen, and later eighteen, and must come of his own free will.

[long-pause]

When a young man comes to the gate, and asks to become a monk, he is not welcomed at once.

[pause]

Saint Benedict wrote that the newcomer should not be given an easy entrance. He should be made to wait, at the gate, for four or five days, and treated rather roughly, to see if he really means it, and is patient. Then, if he persists, he is allowed into the guest house, for a few days.

[pause]

And then, if he still wishes to stay, he is taken to the novices' quarters, and becomes a novice.

[long-pause]

The novice spends a year learning the life of the monastery, under the care of an older monk, the master of novices. Aelred himself was master of novices at Rievaulx, before he became abbot.

[pause]

The novice learns the Rule of Saint Benedict, which is read to him three times during the year. He learns the psalms by heart, all hundred and fifty of them. He learns the chant, and the services, and the customs of the monastery, and the sign language. He learns to read Latin, if he does not already know it. He learns to keep silence, and to obey, and to work.

[pause]

{img:master-of-novices-teaching-young-monks-psalms-room}
And at the end of each part of the year, he is asked again: do you still wish to stay? He may leave at any time, and many do. The life is hard.

[long-pause]

At the end of the year, if the novice still wishes to stay, and the monks agree that he is suitable, he makes his profession.

[pause]

In the church, in front of all the monks, he promises stability, to stay in this monastery for the rest of his life. Conversion of life, to live as a monk. And obedience, to his abbot. He writes his promise on a piece of parchment, in his own hand, or, if he cannot write, someone writes it for him, and he makes a cross. And he lays it on the altar.

[pause]

{img:young-monk-kneeling-altar-profession-parchment-candles}
Then he sings, three times, a verse from the psalms: receive me, O Lord, according to your word, and I shall live, and do not disappoint me in my hope. And all the monks sing it after him. And he prostrates himself, at the feet of each of the monks, one by one, and asks them to pray for him.

[long-pause]

Then he is given the white robe, the cowl, of a Cistercian monk. And from that day, he belongs to the monastery, for the rest of his life.

[pause]

Everything he owned before, he must give away, to the poor, or to the monastery. He keeps nothing. He has no money, no property, nothing that is his own, not even a book, or a pen. Everything he needs is given to him by the abbot: his clothes, his bed, his food, his tools.

[pause]

What does a monk own? The Rule says: a cowl, and a tunic, and shoes, and stockings, and a belt, and a knife, and a pen, and a writing tablet, and a needle, and a handkerchief. And a mattress, a blanket, a cover, and a pillow. Nothing more.

[long-pause]

Why do men choose this life? For many reasons.

[pause]

Some, like Aelred, feel that the world is empty, and long for God. Some want to save their souls, and to make up for their sins. Some are looking for peace, and quiet, and safety, in a violent world. Some are poor, and want security. Some are drawn by the beauty of the life, the singing, and the silence, and the order. Some come because their friends are coming, or because they have heard a great preacher, like Bernard, and been moved.

[pause]

{img:young-monks-walking-cloister-together-silent-spring}
And once they have come, most of them stay, for the rest of their lives, in the valley of the Rye, among their brothers.

[long-pause]

{img:cistercian-monk-habit-white-wool-cowl-black-scapular-standing}
The monks of Rievaulx wear a habit of plain, undyed wool, the natural greyish white colour of the sheep.

[pause]

They wear a long tunic, down to the ankles, with long sleeves, tied with a belt of leather or cord. Over it, for work, a scapular: a long, narrow piece of cloth, hanging down in front and behind, over the shoulders, like an apron, with a hood. And over everything, in the church, and in cold weather, the cowl: a great loose robe, with very wide sleeves, and a hood, which falls in long, soft folds, almost to the ground.

[long-pause]

The cloth is woven from the wool of the abbey's own sheep, by the lay brothers, and fulled, and cut and sewn, in the monastery's own workshops. It is rough, and plain, and warm, and lasts for years.

[pause]

Under the tunic, the monks wear nothing much: the Cistercians, at first, do not even wear underclothes, because Saint Benedict does not mention them. On their feet, they wear shoes or boots of leather, made in the monastery's tannery, and woollen stockings. In winter, in the cold church, at night, their feet are frozen.

[pause]

{img:monastery-laundry-river-washing-habits-wooden-tubs}
Each monk has two tunics, and two cowls, so that one can be washed while the other is worn. The clothes are washed in the monastery's laundry, by the river, in great wooden tubs, with water, and lye, made from wood ash. And when they are worn out, they are given to the poor.

[long-pause]

The monks' hair is cut short, and the top of the head is shaved, in a circle, called the tonsure, leaving a ring of hair around the head, like a crown. It is a sign that the monk has given his life to God.

[pause]

The monks are shaved, all together, by one of the brothers, about once every few weeks, in the cloister, sitting in rows, while they sing psalms. The water is heated in the kitchen, and carried out in basins. In winter, it is a cold business.

[pause]

The lay brothers do not shave their beards. They keep them, as a sign that they are lay brothers, not monks.

[long-pause]

How often do the monks wash?

[pause]

They wash their hands, and their faces, every morning, and before every meal, at the great stone basin in the cloister, with running water, piped from a spring. They wash their feet, often, especially on Saturdays, when the monks whose turn it has been to serve in the kitchen wash the feet of all the brothers, as Christ washed the feet of his disciples.

[pause]

{img:saturday-foot-washing-cloister-monks-humble-basins}
But full baths are rare, in the twelfth century, for monks. Saint Benedict wrote that baths should be allowed to the sick, as often as necessary, but to the healthy, and especially to the young, more rarely. So the healthy monks bathe only a few times a year, before the great feasts, like Christmas and Easter.

[long-pause]

The monks sleep in their habits, in the dormitory, on simple beds: a wooden frame, with a mattress filled with straw, a pillow, a woollen blanket, and a cover. They sleep in rows, along the walls, a little apart from each other, with a lamp burning all night, at the end of the room.

[pause]

In winter, the dormitory is bitterly cold. There is no fire. The wind blows through the cracks. Sometimes snow drifts in, under the doors. The monks sleep wrapped in their cowls and their blankets, and wake at two in the morning, to go down to the church.

[pause]

{img:dormitory-winter-night-lamp-rows-beds-frost-window}
It is a hard life. But many monks of Rievaulx live to be old, in the clean air of the valley, with their simple food, and their regular hours, and their peace of mind.

[long-pause]

{img:abbey-valley-four-seasons-autumn-trees-river-mist}
The life of the monastery follows not only the hours of the day, but the seasons of the year, and the great feasts of the Church.

[pause]

The monks' year begins in the late autumn, with Advent, the four weeks before Christmas, a time of quiet waiting, and preparation, and fasting. The days grow short and dark. The monks rise in the darkness, and go to bed in the darkness. The services are long, and the church is cold.

[long-pause]

Then Christmas comes, the great feast of the birth of Christ.

[pause]

On Christmas Eve, the monks sing the night office, the most solemn of the year. At midnight, there is the first Mass of Christmas, by candlelight. Then a second Mass at dawn, and a third in the day. The church is lit with extra candles. There is a better meal, in the refectory, with a pittance, perhaps of fish, or cheese, or eggs. The monks may rest a little.

[pause]

After Christmas, the winter goes on. Snow falls on the moors, and in the valley. The river freezes, at the edges. The monks work indoors, in the workshops, and the scriptorium, and the cloister, copying books, and mending clothes, and making tools. The lay brothers look after the animals, in the barns, and the sheep on the snowy hills.

[long-pause]

At the beginning of February, there is Candlemas, the feast of the Purification of Mary, when all the candles for the year are blessed, and the monks walk in procession, around the cloister, each carrying a lighted candle.

[pause]

Then comes Lent, the forty days of fasting before Easter.

[pause]

{img:candlemas-procession-cloister-monks-lighted-candles-snow}
In Lent, the monks eat only one meal a day, in the evening, of bread and vegetables. They keep even stricter silence. They pray more, and read more. At the beginning of Lent, each monk is given a book from the library, to read through the year. The church is bare. The crosses are covered with cloth.

[long-pause]

And then, at the end of Lent, comes Holy Week, and Easter, the greatest feast of all.

[pause]

On the Thursday before Easter, the abbot washes the feet of the monks, and of poor men, brought in from the gate, as Christ washed the feet of his disciples. On Good Friday, the monks walk barefoot, and kneel before the cross. And on Easter night, a new fire is lit, outside the church, and a great candle, and the light is carried into the dark church, and the monks sing, for the first time since the beginning of Lent, the joyful word: Alleluia.

[pause]

{img:easter-new-fire-church-doorway-night-monks-great-candle}
Easter brings the spring. The days grow longer. The monks go out to work in the fields and gardens again. There are two meals a day again. The lambs are born, on the hills. The birds sing in the woods. The primroses and the bluebells come out, under the trees, on the banks of the river.

[long-pause]

In summer, the days are long, and the work is hard.

[pause]

The monks rise at about one in the morning, because the night is so short, and the services must be finished before dawn. They work in the fields, haymaking, in June, and weeding, and the lay brothers shear the sheep, on the granges, and pack the wool, in great sacks, for the merchants. After the midday meal, the monks rest, in the dormitory.

[pause]

Then, in August and September, the harvest. Everyone works, monks and lay brothers together, even the abbot, from dawn to dusk, cutting the corn with sickles, and binding it into sheaves, and carrying it to the barns. Sometimes, at harvest, the monks even sing the services in the fields, so as not to lose the good weather.

[long-pause]

After the harvest, in the autumn, the apples and pears are picked, in the orchards. The pigs are taken into the woods, to eat the acorns and the beechnuts. The new ale is brewed.

[pause]

On the fourteenth of September, the feast of the Holy Cross, the winter timetable begins again, with one meal a day.

[pause]

{img:orchard-apple-picking-monks-baskets-autumn-light-ladder}
And on the first of November, there is All Saints, and on the second, All Souls, when the monks pray for all the dead: their own brothers, and their families, and their benefactors, and all the souls of the faithful departed. They go out to the monks' cemetery, east of the church, and pray beside the graves.

[pause]

And then Advent comes again. And the year begins once more, the same as before, and as it will be next year, and the year after, for as long as the monastery stands.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-yorkshire-castle-helmsley-1150-stone-keep-village}
The monks of Rievaulx have left the world. But the world does not leave them alone.

[pause]

In 1150, England is in the middle of a long and miserable civil war, which later historians will call the Anarchy.

[long-pause]

When King Henry the First died, in 1135, he left no son. He wanted his daughter, Matilda, to be queen. But many of the great lords did not want a woman to rule. And so his nephew, Stephen, seized the throne.

[pause]

For nearly twenty years, Stephen and Matilda, and their supporters, fight each other for the crown. Lords build castles everywhere, without permission, and rob the countryside. Villages are burned. Crops are destroyed. People go hungry.

[pause]

{img:countryside-burned-fields-distance-grey-sky-quiet-village}
A monk at the abbey of Peterborough, writing the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, wrote that in those years, men said openly that Christ and his saints were asleep.

[long-pause]

The north of England suffers too. In 1138, King David of Scotland, Aelred's old friend and master, invaded Yorkshire, to support Matilda, his niece, and there was a great battle, the Battle of the Standard, near Northallerton, not far from Rievaulx. Walter Espec, the founder of Rievaulx, was one of the English leaders. The Scots were defeated.

[pause]

Aelred later wrote an account of the battle, with a long, sad speech for Walter Espec, and a gentle portrait of King David, whom he loved.

[pause]

The monasteries, in those years, were places of peace, in a violent world. People fled to them, for safety. Lords, on both sides, respected them, mostly, and gave them land, to pay for prayers for their souls. And Walter Espec, in his old age, became a monk himself, at Rievaulx, among the white monks he had brought to the valley, and passed away there, a few years later.

[long-pause]

The world comes to Rievaulx in other ways too.

[pause]

There are guests: bishops, and abbots, and lords, and messengers, and travellers, and pilgrims, and merchants, who stay in the guest house, near the gate. The Rule of Saint Benedict says that all guests should be received as if they were Christ. The abbot, or a monk, welcomes them, and washes their hands, and gives them food, and a bed.

[pause]

{img:guest-house-monastery-travellers-cloaks-welcomed-evening}
There are the poor, who come every day to the gate, for food. There are the people of the villages around, who work for the monastery, or sell things to it, or buy wool from it, or come to ask for help.

[pause]

And there are letters, from Clairvaux, and Cîteaux, and Rome, and from other monasteries, and from friends. Every year, the abbot travels to Cîteaux, in Burgundy, for the General Chapter, a long journey, across England, and the Channel, and France, which takes weeks.

[long-pause]

And in the wider world, in 1150, there is much happening.

[pause]

Bernard of Clairvaux has just preached the Second Crusade, which ended in failure, in 1148. In Paris, the great new cathedral schools are growing, where teachers like Peter Abelard have taught, and which will soon become the first universities. Great new churches are being built, in a new style, with pointed arches, and tall windows, which we now call Gothic, at the abbey of Saint-Denis, near Paris, and soon in many other places.

[pause]

{img:hildegard-rupertsberg-nun-writing-visions-scriptorium-rhine}
And in Germany, on the river Rhine, in the year 1150, a remarkable Benedictine nun, named Hildegard of Bingen, moves with her nuns to a new convent of her own, at Rupertsberg. She is fifty-two. She writes books about her visions, and about medicine, and plants, and composes beautiful music, which is still sung today. And she writes letters to Bernard, and to popes and emperors, who listen to her.

[long-pause]

So the quiet valley of the Rye is not as far from the world as it seems.

[pause]

The monks pray for the world, every day, and every night: for the king, and for peace, and for the sick, and the poor, and the dead, and for all people. That, they believe, is their most important work.

[long-pause]

{img:monastery-gatehouse-porter-small-window-travellers-arriving}
At the gate of Rievaulx, there is a gatehouse, and in it, the porter.

[pause]

Saint Benedict wrote that the porter should be a wise old monk, who knows how to receive and give a message, and whose age keeps him from wandering about. He should have a little room by the gate, so that visitors always find someone there to answer them. And as soon as anyone knocks, or a poor man calls, he should answer: thanks be to God, or, a blessing on you, and go to them at once, gently, and warmly.

[long-pause]

All kinds of people come to the gate of Rievaulx.

[pause]

There are travellers, on their way through the valley, who need a meal, and a bed for the night. There are pilgrims, on their way to the great shrines: to York, and Durham, where Saint Cuthbert is buried, and Canterbury, and even to Rome, or Santiago de Compostela, in Spain, or Jerusalem. There are merchants, coming to buy wool. There are messengers, with letters. There are monks from other monasteries, and abbots, and bishops, and lords, and their servants.

[pause]

And there are the poor, who come every day, for bread, and soup, and old clothes, and help. The porter gives them what the monastery has, and the leftovers from the refectory, and in hard winters, the monks may feed hundreds of people, every day.

[long-pause]

The guests are lodged in the guest house, near the gate, a large hall, with beds, and a fire, and its own kitchen, so that the guests do not disturb the silence of the monks.

[pause]

Saint Benedict wrote that all guests should be received as Christ, because Christ himself will say, I was a stranger, and you welcomed me. The abbot, or a monk chosen for the purpose, greets them, and bows to them, and prays with them, and washes their hands, and their feet. They are given food, and drink, and a bed, and, if they need it, care for their horses.

[pause]

{img:guest-hall-fire-travellers-eating-monk-serving-bread}
The rich guests, and the poor, are treated with the same courtesy. In fact, Benedict wrote, the poor and the pilgrims should be given the greatest care, because in them, Christ is more truly welcomed. The rich, he said dryly, demand respect by their very presence.

[long-pause]

The guests may stay for a few days, or longer. Some come to rest, and pray, and think, for a while, in the peace of the valley, away from their troubles. Some come to talk to the abbot, and ask his advice. Aelred's wisdom, and his kindness, become famous, and many people come to Rievaulx just to see him.

[pause]

Some guests, after staying a while, ask to become monks themselves.

[pause]

{img:visitor-talking-with-abbot-garden-path-trees-afternoon}
And when they leave, the monks give them food for the journey, and their blessing, and the porter opens the gate, and they go on their way, up the steep track, out of the valley, and back into the world.

[long-pause]

{img:aged-abbot-small-room-monks-gathered-around-bed-lamplight}
Aelred lives for another seventeen years after 1150, as abbot of Rievaulx.

[pause]

They are years of peace, and growth. The civil war ends, in 1153, when Stephen agrees that Matilda's son, Henry, will be king after him. Henry the Second becomes king in 1154, and brings order back to England.

[long-pause]

Rievaulx grows, and prospers. New buildings are added. The church is made larger. Aelred travels to Scotland, and to the daughter houses of Rievaulx, and to Cîteaux, and writes his books, and preaches, and cares for his monks.

[pause]

But his illness grows worse. In his last years, he cannot walk, and lives in his little room, next to the infirmary, surrounded by his monks.

[pause]

And on the twelfth of January, 1167, Aelred passes away, peacefully, at Rievaulx, at the age of fifty-six, with his monks around him. Walter Daniel says that, at the end, Aelred said, again and again, in Latin and in English: hasten, hasten. For the love of Christ, hasten. He was buried in the chapter house, beside the first abbot, William.

[long-pause]

After Aelred, Rievaulx goes on, for nearly four hundred years.

[pause]

In the thirteenth century, the monks rebuild the eastern part of the church, in the new Gothic style, much larger and grander than before, with tall pointed arches, and great windows, reaching up toward the sky. The abbey becomes very rich, from its wool. It has thousands of sheep, on its granges, all over Yorkshire.

[pause]

{img:gothic-abbey-church-tall-pointed-arches-thirteenth-century}
But slowly, things change. The number of lay brothers falls, as fewer poor men want to live that hard life, and the monks begin to rent out their granges to farmers, instead of farming them themselves. In 1349, the Black Death comes to England, and many of the monks and lay brothers pass away. After that, the monastery is much smaller.

[long-pause]

By the 1530s, there are only about twenty-two monks at Rievaulx.

[pause]

And in those years, King Henry the Eighth of England breaks away from the Church of Rome, and makes himself head of the Church in England. And he decides to close all the monasteries in England, and take their lands and their wealth for himself.

[pause]

{img:abbey-quiet-empty-cloister-last-monks-leaving-1538}
In December 1538, Rievaulx Abbey is closed. The monks are sent away, with small pensions. The buildings are given to a nobleman, the Earl of Rutland. And his men take the lead from the roofs, and the glass from the windows, and the bells, and the stones, and sell them. And the great church, and the cloister, and all the buildings of the monastery, slowly fall into ruin.

[long-pause]

It is a sad end, to four hundred years of prayer and work.

[pause]

But it is not quite the end. Because the ruins of Rievaulx are so beautiful, that they became, in later centuries, one of the most famous and most visited places in England.

[pause]

In the eighteenth century, a rich landowner, Thomas Duncombe, built a long grassy terrace, on the hillside above the abbey, with two little classical temples, at either end, so that his guests could walk along it, and look down at the ruins, through the trees, as a romantic view. Artists came, like J. M. W. Turner, and painted them. Poets came, and wrote about them.

[long-pause]

And the Cistercians themselves did not disappear.

[pause]

The order survived the closing of the English monasteries, and the French Revolution, and still exists today, all over the world. There are Cistercian monasteries in France, and Italy, and Spain, and Germany, and Belgium, and the Netherlands, and Ireland, and America, and Africa, and Asia. Some of them, called Trappists, after the abbey of La Trappe, in France, follow a stricter rule, like the first Cistercians.

[pause]

{img:modern-cistercian-monks-singing-choir-simple-church}
And every night, in those monasteries, the monks still rise in the darkness, and go down to their churches, and sing the psalms, in the same way, as the monks of Rievaulx did, nearly nine hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:rievaulx-abbey-ruins-tall-arches-green-valley-today}
Today, the ruins of Rievaulx Abbey still stand in the valley of the Rye, among the woods and the meadows, in the North York Moors National Park.

[pause]

They are looked after by English Heritage, and every year, many thousands of people come to visit them.

[long-pause]

You come down into the valley, along a narrow country lane, between hedges and stone walls, past little cottages, of golden stone, with gardens full of flowers. And suddenly, through the trees, you see them: the great arches of the church, rising high above the meadow, against the green hillside, pale gold and grey, in the sunlight.

[pause]

The eastern part of the church, the part that was rebuilt in the thirteenth century, is still standing, almost to its full height: three storeys of tall pointed arches, one above the other, open to the sky. Birds fly in and out of the empty windows. The grass grows where the floor once was.

[pause]

You can walk through the ruins, slowly, and imagine the life of the monastery. Here is the church, where the monks sang the night office, by candlelight. Here is the cloister, where they walked, and read, and copied books. Here is the chapter house, where they met every morning, and where the tomb of the first abbot, William, can still be seen, and where Aelred was buried.

[long-pause]

Here is the refectory, a great tall hall, with its tall windows, and the stairs, built into the wall, that led up to the reader's pulpit. Here is the stone basin, where the monks washed their hands before meals. Here is the warming house, with its great fireplace. Here is the infirmary, and the little room where Aelred lived his last years.

[pause]

And in the little museum, by the entrance, you can see things found in the ruins: carved stones, and floor tiles, and pottery, and coins, and tools, and the pieces of a bell, and little things that the monks used, every day, and left behind.

[pause]

{img:refectory-ruin-tall-walls-windows-grass-floor-sun}
On the hillside above, the Rievaulx Terrace, with its two little temples, is still there, looked after by the National Trust. You can walk along it, on the soft grass, and look down at the ruins, through gaps in the trees, as the visitors did, more than two hundred and fifty years ago.

[long-pause]

The valley itself is still beautiful, and quiet.

[pause]

The river Rye still flows past the abbey, clear and fast, over its stones, in the channel that the monks dug for it. The woods still cover the hillsides, oak and ash and beech, green in summer, gold in autumn. The meadows are full of sheep, grazing, as they did in the days of the lay brothers.

[pause]

{img:river-rye-meadow-sheep-abbey-ruins-background-evening}
In the spring, the woods are full of bluebells, and primroses, and wild garlic. In the summer, the heather blooms on the moors above, purple, as far as the eye can see. In the autumn, the leaves turn gold. In the winter, sometimes, there is snow, and the ruins stand white and silent, in the white valley.

[long-pause]

And not far away, in the same hills, there are other ruined abbeys, of the Cistercians and the Benedictines: Byland, and Fountains, the largest and most beautiful of all, a World Heritage Site, and Jervaulx, and Whitby, high on its cliff above the sea.

[pause]

And there are still monks in Yorkshire. At Ampleforth, a few miles from Rievaulx, there is a Benedictine abbey, founded in the nineteenth century, where the monks still sing the services, every day, in the abbey church, and run a school.

[pause]

On some days, visitors to Rievaulx find that there is a quiet hush in the ruins, especially early in the morning, or in the evening, when the crowds have gone, and the light is low and golden, and the only sounds are the river, and the birds, and the wind in the trees.

[long-pause]

So the story of the white monks of Rievaulx comes to an end tonight, in the green valley, with the river running over its stones, and the great arches standing open to the evening sky.

[pause]

The bell in the night, the singing in the dark church, the silence of the cloister, and a kind abbot who believed that friendship was a way to God.

[pause]
