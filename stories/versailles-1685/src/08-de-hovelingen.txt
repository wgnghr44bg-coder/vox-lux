The courtiers of Versailles are the nobles of France. Princes, dukes, counts and marquises, with their wives and children. They have come from their castles all over the country, to live at the palace, close to the king. Some have rooms in the palace itself. Others have houses in the town. And all of them spend their days at court, following the king, from morning till night.

--

Life at court is very expensive. The courtiers must wear the finest clothes, which cost a fortune. The men wear long coats of silk or velvet, embroidered with gold and silver thread, over long waistcoats. They wear tight breeches to the knee, silk stockings and shoes with buckles. They wear huge curly wigs, like the king, and lace at their necks and wrists. And they carry a sword at their side, and a hat with feathers under their arm.

The ladies wear gowns of rich silk, with tight bodices and wide skirts, often with a long train behind. Their gowns are decorated with lace, ribbons and jewels. Their hair is curled and piled up high, with ribbons and lace. They carry fans, which they use not only to cool themselves, but also to send secret signals. A slow wave, a quick snap, the fan held over the lips. Every movement has a meaning.

{img:lady-with-fan-curtsy-court-etiquette-gallery-sunlight-silk-gown}
At court, there are rules for everything. They are called etiquette. Who may sit, and on what kind of seat. Who may enter a room first. Who may wear a hat in front of the king. Who may ride in the king's carriage. How deep to bow, and to whom. Only duchesses may sit on a little stool in front of the queen. Everyone else must stand. Courtiers spend a lot of time and energy on these little rules.

There is also a special way of knocking on doors. At Versailles, you never knock on a door in the king's apartments. Instead, you gently scratch on it, with the little finger of your left hand. Some courtiers let the nail of that finger grow long, just for this purpose. It sounds strange to us. But at Versailles, these little things showed that you knew how to behave.

--

{img:courtiers-playing-cards-gaming-table-candles-coins-evening-salon}
The courtiers have little to do during the day. They do not work. So they fill their time with other things. They follow the king to Mass, to dinner and on his walks. They gossip. They play cards, and often lose huge amounts of money. They go hunting with the king. They write letters. Some of the best letters ever written in French were written by courtiers at Versailles, describing the life of the court to their friends and relatives.

But life at court is also tiring. There are always crowds. The palace is noisy and full of people, day and night. The rooms are often small and uncomfortable, even for the great nobles. In winter, the big rooms are freezing cold. And there is no proper plumbing. People use chamber pots, which servants empty. Some visitors even complain that the corridors smell, because not everyone bothers to find a chamber pot in time.

{img:tired-courtier-small-attic-room-palace-candle-writing-letter-night}
Some courtiers are unhappy at Versailles. They miss their castles and their estates. They are tired of the rules, the gossip and the endless competition. One famous courtier, the Duke of Saint-Simon, wrote that the king used every little favour, every little sign of attention, to keep the courtiers busy and dependent. He wrote it all down in his memoirs, many years later, and they are some of the best descriptions of court life we have.

--

Jeanne watches the courtiers with great curiosity. To her, they look like birds of paradise, all colours and feathers. They walk so slowly, and talk so softly, and laugh behind their fans. They never seem to hurry, and never seem to work. Her mother says that she would not want their life for all the gold in France. Too many rules, she says. Too many eyes watching. Jeanne thinks about it, and decides that her mother is probably right. But she would still like to try on one of those silk dresses, just once.
