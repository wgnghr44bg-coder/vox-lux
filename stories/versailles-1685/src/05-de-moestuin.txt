Not far from the palace, there is a special garden. It is not a garden of statues and fountains, but a garden of fruit and vegetables. It is called the Potager du Roi, the King's Kitchen Garden. It was finished just a couple of years ago. And it grows food for the king's own table, the finest fruit and vegetables in France.

--

The kitchen garden covers about nine hectares. It was built on a marsh, which first had to be drained and filled with good soil, brought in by thousands of carts. In the middle, there is a big square garden, with a round pool and a fountain. Around it, there are terraces, and many smaller gardens, each surrounded by high stone walls. The walls protect the plants from the wind. And they hold the warmth of the sun, so the fruit ripens faster.

The garden is run by a remarkable man. His name is Jean-Baptiste de La Quintinie. He was a lawyer, who fell in love with gardening, and taught himself everything. Now he is the most famous gardener in Europe. The king admires him greatly, and often comes to visit him in the garden, to talk about pears and melons. La Quintinie is about sixty years old, with a serious face and very careful hands.

{img:espalier-pear-trees-trained-flat-against-stone-wall-sunlight-ripe-fruit}
Along the walls, there are fruit trees, trained to grow flat against the stone, with their branches spread out like the fingers of a hand. This is called espalier. It saves space, and the trees get all the warmth of the sunny wall. There are pears, apples, peaches, apricots, plums, cherries and figs. La Quintinie grows more than five hundred different kinds of pears alone. The king's favourite is a pear called the Bon Chrétien, the Good Christian.

La Quintinie is very clever. He knows how to make plants grow earlier or later than usual, so that the king can eat fresh fruit and vegetables for as many months of the year as possible. He uses glass bells, called cloches, to cover the young plants and keep them warm. He uses beds of warm horse manure, which give off heat as they rot. And so he can serve the king strawberries in March and asparagus in December.

--

{img:glass-cloches-rows-young-plants-garden-beds-gardeners-early-morning}
The king loves his kitchen garden. And at the moment, the whole court is mad about one vegetable in particular. Green peas. Fresh, young, sweet green peas, which were quite new at the French court. The ladies and gentlemen of Versailles eat them in enormous amounts. Some of them, people say, eat a big dish of peas after a full supper with the king, and then go home and eat another dish before going to bed.

Jeanne works in the kitchen garden with her father. Every morning, she helps to pick the peas, the strawberries and the cherries, while they are still cool and fresh with dew. She lays them carefully in shallow baskets lined with leaves. Then she and the other children carry the baskets to the palace kitchens. The fruit must not be bruised. La Quintinie checks every basket himself.

{img:girl-carrying-basket-strawberries-cherries-walking-to-palace-morning}
Sometimes, the king himself comes to the kitchen garden. He enters through a special gate, with a small group of courtiers. He walks along the paths, looking at the trees and the plants. He asks La Quintinie questions. He tastes a pear, or a fig, straight from the tree. Once, he even took a pruning knife and cut a branch himself, under the gardener's careful eye.

--

One morning in July, Jeanne is picking figs, high up on a ladder, when the king comes into her part of the garden. She is so surprised that she almost drops her basket. She does not know what to do. Her father is too far away to help. So she just stays very still on her ladder, holding her basket. The king looks up at her, and smiles a little. He asks if the figs are good this year. Jeanne says, yes, Sire, very good. The king nods, and walks on. Jeanne's knees are shaking for the rest of the day.
