Tonight we travel back about three hundred and forty years, to the summer of 1685. We go to France, to a small town about twenty kilometres west of Paris. It is called Versailles. And here, on a low hill, there stands the biggest and most splendid palace in the world. It is the home of the king of France, Louis the Fourteenth, the Sun King.

[long-pause]

It is early morning in July. The sun is just rising over the gardens. A soft mist lies over the long lawns and the still water of the canal. The rows of clipped trees stand dark and neat, like soldiers. The statues of gods and goddesses glow pale in the first light. And the great palace, with its hundreds of windows, is beginning to shine gold, as the sun touches its walls.

[pause]

The gardens are quiet. But not empty. Already, dozens of gardeners are at work, raking the gravel paths, trimming the hedges and watering the flowers. Men are sweeping the stone terraces. A boy is pushing a wheelbarrow full of fresh soil. Somewhere, a bird is singing. And from the stables, there comes the sound of horses, and the clatter of hooves on cobblestones.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:louis-xiv-1685-portrait-feeling-wig-red-heels-gentle-dignified-palace}
Inside the palace, the most powerful man in Europe is still asleep. In a little while, he will be woken, in a grand ceremony, with dozens of people watching. Then he will spend the day ruling his kingdom, walking in his gardens, hunting in his forests, eating his dinner in front of an audience, and dancing, playing cards and listening to music until late at night. Every hour of his day follows a careful plan.

[pause]

Around him live thousands of people. Princes and princesses, dukes and duchesses, ministers and bishops, soldiers and servants. Thousands of people live in and around the palace. They all want to be near the king. Because at Versailles, being close to the king is everything. A smile from him can make your fortune. A frown can ruin your life.

[pause]

{img:gardener-girl-thirteen-straw-hat-basket-vegetables-palace-garden-morning}
But tonight, we will not only follow the king and his court. We will also follow an ordinary family who works at Versailles. A gardener, his wife, and their two children. A girl of thirteen, called Jeanne, and her brother Pierre, who is fifteen. They live in the town, below the palace. And they work in the gardens, which they know better than any duke or princess.

[long-pause]

With them, we will walk through the famous gardens, and watch the fountains play. We will visit the king's kitchen garden, where the sweetest pears and the earliest peas in France are grown. We will see the great Hall of Mirrors, finished only a year ago. We will glide on the canal in a Venetian gondola. We will hear the music of the king's violins. And we will watch the fireworks light up the summer night.

[pause]

{img:hall-of-mirrors-versailles-chandeliers-golden-light-summer-afternoon}
Life at Versailles was not perfect. It was a world of great wealth, while many people in France were poor. It was full of rules and gossip and envy. But it was also a world of extraordinary beauty, of art and music and gardens, which people still come from all over the world to see. So let us begin, on a summer morning in 1685, as the sun rises over the gardens of Versailles.

[long-pause]

{img:small-hunting-lodge-louis-xiii-brick-stone-woods-1630s-versailles}
Versailles was not always a great palace. About sixty years before our story, it was just a small village, surrounded by forests and marshes. The forests were full of deer and wild boar. And the king of France at that time, Louis the Thirteenth, loved to hunt there. So he built a small hunting lodge on the hill, made of red brick and white stone, with a slate roof. It was a simple, comfortable house, where he could rest after hunting.

[long-pause]

His son, Louis the Fourteenth, became king in 1643, when he was only four years old. As a boy, he sometimes came to the little hunting lodge with his father's old friends. He loved it. It was quiet and peaceful, far from Paris. And when he grew up, he decided to make it his own special place.

[pause]

At first, he just added gardens and held parties there. In 1664, he held a famous festival at Versailles, called the Pleasures of the Enchanted Island. It lasted for a whole week. There were plays, ballets, music, banquets and fireworks. The young king himself danced in the ballets. The whole court talked about it for months.

[pause]

{img:versailles-construction-1670s-workers-scaffolding-stone-carts-palace-growing}
Then the king began to build. He did not pull down his father's little hunting lodge. He loved it too much. Instead, he wrapped new buildings around it, on three sides. The architect Louis Le Vau designed an elegant new palace of white stone, with long rows of tall windows. Later, another architect, Jules Hardouin-Mansart, made it even bigger, adding two enormous wings, to the north and to the south.

[pause]

The building went on for decades. At times, more than thirty thousand workers were busy at Versailles. Stonemasons, carpenters, painters, sculptors, gilders, glassmakers and gardeners. They worked from dawn to dusk. They drained the marshes, moved hills, dug lakes and planted whole forests of trees. Many of them came from all over France. It was one of the biggest building projects in the history of Europe.

[long-pause]

{img:royal-court-arriving-versailles-1682-carriages-procession-courtyard}
In May 1682, three years before our story, the king made a great decision. He moved his whole court and his government from Paris to Versailles. From now on, Versailles was the centre of France. The king lived here. His ministers worked here. The nobles of France had to live here too, if they wanted to stay in the king's favour.

[pause]

Why did the king do this? Partly because he loved Versailles. But also for a clever reason. When Louis was a boy, the great nobles of France had rebelled against the crown. Louis never forgot it. By bringing them all to Versailles, he could keep an eye on them. Instead of plotting in their castles, they spent their time and money at court, competing for his attention. Versailles was a golden cage.

[pause]

{img:versailles-marble-courtyard-black-white-floor-gilded-balcony-morning}
In 1685, the palace is still not finished. There is still building going on, in many places. But it is already enormous. It has hundreds of rooms, and more than two thousand windows. In the middle, there is a courtyard paved with black and white marble, called the Marble Courtyard. Around it stands the old red brick hunting lodge of Louis the Thirteenth, now covered with gold decorations. And around it, on the first floor, are the private rooms of the king.

[long-pause]

Behind the palace, on the garden side, everything is white stone and tall windows, looking out over the gardens. From the windows, you can see the gardens stretching away into the distance, for kilometres, all the way to the horizon. The king designed it so. When he looks out of his windows, he wants to see nothing but his own beautiful creation, going on forever.

[pause]

Many people thought the king was mad to build a palace here. The ground was marshy, the water was poor, and there was no river. One courtier later wrote that it was the saddest and most barren of places, without a view, without water and without woods. But the king did not listen. He wanted to show that he could make beauty anywhere, even where nature had not. And in the end, he did.

[long-pause]

{img:louis-xiv-walking-gardens-cane-courtiers-following-summer-morning}
Louis the Fourteenth is forty-six years old in the summer of 1685. He has been king for more than forty years, since he was a little boy. And he has ruled by himself, without a chief minister, for almost twenty-five years. He is the most powerful king in Europe. And he is the most admired, and the most feared.

[long-pause]

He is not very tall. But he holds himself so straight and so proudly that everyone thinks he is tall. He wears a huge wig of dark curls, which comes down over his shoulders. He wears shoes with high red heels, which make him a little taller, and which only the most important people at court are allowed to copy. He moves slowly and gracefully, like a dancer. As a young man, he danced in ballets, and he still walks as if he were on a stage.

[pause]

He is very polite. He takes off his hat to every woman he meets, even the maids in the corridors. He speaks calmly and briefly, and rarely loses his temper. He has a good memory for faces and names. He is a great lover of music, gardens, architecture and painting. He eats enormous amounts of food. And he works hard. Every day, he spends hours with his ministers, reading reports and making decisions.

[pause]

{img:sun-emblem-golden-apollo-face-rays-gate-decoration-versailles}
Louis chose the sun as his symbol. Just as the sun gives light and life to the whole world, he believed, the king gives light and life to his kingdom. Everything turns around him, like the planets around the sun. So at Versailles, the sun is everywhere. On the gates, on the ceilings, on the furniture, in the gardens. And the god of the sun, Apollo, appears in statues and paintings all over the palace. That is why Louis is called the Sun King.

[pause]

In the summer of 1685, the king's life has changed. His wife, Queen Maria Theresa, a Spanish princess, passed away two years ago. And not long after, the king secretly married another woman. Her name is Françoise, but everyone calls her Madame de Maintenon. She was once the governess of some of his children. She is calm, clever and very religious. The marriage is never officially announced. But everyone at court knows about it.

[long-pause]

{img:madame-de-maintenon-dark-dress-reading-quiet-room-window-light}
Madame de Maintenon has a quiet influence on the king. Since he married her, the court has become more serious. There are fewer wild parties, and more church services. The king, who was once a great lover of pleasure, now goes to Mass every day, and listens to sermons. Some courtiers find it a little boring. They miss the old, merry days. But the king seems content.

[pause]

The king has a large family. His eldest son, the Dauphin, is twenty-three years old, and loves hunting and music. The Dauphin has two little sons, the king's grandsons. And there are several other children of the king, by other mothers, who have been given titles and grand marriages. The king loves his children and grandchildren. He likes to walk with them in the gardens, and show them the fountains.

[pause]

{img:king-with-small-grandsons-garden-fountain-pointing-sunlit-afternoon}
Every day, the king follows exactly the same timetable. A courtier once said that with a calendar and a clock, you could know exactly what the king was doing, even from three hundred leagues away. He gets up at the same time. He goes to Mass at the same time. He eats at the same time. He walks in the gardens at the same time. And he goes to bed at the same time. This routine gives the whole court its rhythm.

[long-pause]

For the people of France, the king is almost like a god. But Louis is also a man. He has toothache, and a bad back. He loves his dogs, and feeds them biscuits with his own hands. He likes to walk in his gardens when it is quiet, and look at the flowers. And he is never happier than when he is planning a new fountain, or a new avenue of trees, with his gardener.

[long-pause]

{img:versailles-town-1685-street-houses-workers-palace-in-distance-morning}
Below the palace, a whole new town has grown up. Twenty years ago, Versailles was a tiny village. Now, thousands of people live here. There are big houses for the nobles and the ministers, who do not have room in the palace. There are inns, shops and markets. And there are many small houses for the workers. The builders, the craftsmen, the servants and the gardeners, who keep the great palace running.

[long-pause]

In one of these small houses, on a quiet street, lives the family of Jacques Martin. He is a gardener. He works in the king's kitchen garden, the Potager du Roi, growing fruit and vegetables for the king's table. He is about forty years old, with a brown, weathered face, big hands and a slow, kind smile. He knows everything about pears, peaches, melons and figs. He has worked in the king's gardens for fifteen years.

[pause]

His wife is called Marguerite. She works as a laundress, washing linen for one of the great households of the palace. It is hard work. She stands at the washing tubs for hours, with her sleeves rolled up and her hands red from the hot water and the soap. She is a cheerful, practical woman, who always has a song on her lips and an opinion about everything.

[pause]

{img:laundress-washing-linen-tubs-courtyard-steam-sleeves-rolled-sunlight}
Their son Pierre is fifteen. He has just started working with the fontainiers, the men who look after the fountains of Versailles. It is a very important job, because the king loves his fountains more than almost anything. Pierre is tall and thin, with curly hair and a quick mind. He loves machines, pipes and water. He spends hours watching how the fountains work.

[pause]

And their daughter Jeanne is thirteen. She helps her father in the kitchen garden. She picks fruit, weeds the beds and carries baskets of vegetables to the palace kitchens. She has dark braids, freckles from the sun, and sharp brown eyes that notice everything. She has been inside the palace many times, carrying baskets through the back corridors. And she has seen the king, more than once, from behind a hedge.

[long-pause]

{img:small-house-family-supper-table-soup-bread-candle-evening-1685}
The family's house is small but comfortable. It has two rooms downstairs and one upstairs, with a little garden at the back. There is a fireplace for cooking, a table and benches, a big bed with curtains for the parents, and a smaller bed upstairs for the children. In the garden, Jacques grows his own vegetables and a few flowers. And there is a pear tree, which he grafted himself, which gives the best pears on the street.

[pause]

In the evenings, the family eats supper together. Soup, bread, a little cheese, and vegetables from the garden. Sometimes, when the king's kitchen has too much, Jacques brings home a few peaches or a melon, which the head gardener has allowed him to take. Then it is a feast. They talk about their day. Marguerite tells the gossip from the laundry. Pierre talks about pipes and pumps. And Jeanne tells what she saw at the palace.

[pause]

{img:girl-peeking-through-hedge-watching-courtiers-walk-garden-path}
Jeanne's stories are the family's favourite. She has seen a duchess slip on the gravel and land on her bottom. She has seen the king feed biscuits to his dogs. She has seen an ambassador from a faraway country, in a robe of gold silk. She has seen a little prince crying because he lost his kite. Her father always warns her to be careful, and not to get in anyone's way. But he laughs at her stories all the same.

[long-pause]

Life is not easy for a gardener's family. They work very long hours, especially in summer. They are not rich. But they are proud of their work. Every peach that Jacques grows, every fountain that Pierre helps to run, every shirt that Marguerite washes, is part of the most beautiful palace in the world. And in a way, they feel that Versailles belongs to them too.

[pause]

On Sundays, the family goes to church in the town, in their best clothes. After Mass, they walk in the streets, greet their neighbours and buy a little treat at the market. Sometimes, in the afternoon, they walk up to the palace gardens, which are open to respectable people. Then they walk along the paths like everyone else, looking at the fountains and the flowers, and for a few hours, they are not workers, but visitors.

[long-pause]

{img:potager-du-roi-walled-kitchen-garden-terraces-fruit-trees-vegetable-beds}
Not far from the palace, there is a special garden. It is not a garden of statues and fountains, but a garden of fruit and vegetables. It is called the Potager du Roi, the King's Kitchen Garden. It was finished just a couple of years ago. And it grows food for the king's own table, the finest fruit and vegetables in France.

[long-pause]

The kitchen garden covers about nine hectares. It was built on a marsh, which first had to be drained and filled with good soil, brought in by thousands of carts. In the middle, there is a big square garden, with a round pool and a fountain. Around it, there are terraces, and many smaller gardens, each surrounded by high stone walls. The walls protect the plants from the wind. And they hold the warmth of the sun, so the fruit ripens faster.

[pause]

The garden is run by a remarkable man. His name is Jean-Baptiste de La Quintinie. He was a lawyer, who fell in love with gardening, and taught himself everything. Now he is the most famous gardener in Europe. The king admires him greatly, and often comes to visit him in the garden, to talk about pears and melons. La Quintinie is about sixty years old, with a serious face and very careful hands.

[pause]

{img:espalier-pear-trees-trained-flat-against-stone-wall-sunlight-ripe-fruit}
Along the walls, there are fruit trees, trained to grow flat against the stone, with their branches spread out like the fingers of a hand. This is called espalier. It saves space, and the trees get all the warmth of the sunny wall. There are pears, apples, peaches, apricots, plums, cherries and figs. La Quintinie grows more than five hundred different kinds of pears alone. The king's favourite is a pear called the Bon Chrétien, the Good Christian.

[pause]

La Quintinie is very clever. He knows how to make plants grow earlier or later than usual, so that the king can eat fresh fruit and vegetables for as many months of the year as possible. He uses glass bells, called cloches, to cover the young plants and keep them warm. He uses beds of warm horse manure, which give off heat as they rot. And so he can serve the king strawberries in March and asparagus in December.

[long-pause]

{img:glass-cloches-rows-young-plants-garden-beds-gardeners-early-morning}
The king loves his kitchen garden. And at the moment, the whole court is mad about one vegetable in particular. Green peas. Fresh, young, sweet green peas, which were quite new at the French court. The ladies and gentlemen of Versailles eat them in enormous amounts. Some of them, people say, eat a big dish of peas after a full supper with the king, and then go home and eat another dish before going to bed.

[pause]

Jeanne works in the kitchen garden with her father. Every morning, she helps to pick the peas, the strawberries and the cherries, while they are still cool and fresh with dew. She lays them carefully in shallow baskets lined with leaves. Then she and the other children carry the baskets to the palace kitchens. The fruit must not be bruised. La Quintinie checks every basket himself.

[pause]

{img:girl-carrying-basket-strawberries-cherries-walking-to-palace-morning}
Sometimes, the king himself comes to the kitchen garden. He enters through a special gate, with a small group of courtiers. He walks along the paths, looking at the trees and the plants. He asks La Quintinie questions. He tastes a pear, or a fig, straight from the tree. Once, he even took a pruning knife and cut a branch himself, under the gardener's careful eye.

[long-pause]

One morning in July, Jeanne is picking figs, high up on a ladder, when the king comes into her part of the garden. She is so surprised that she almost drops her basket. She does not know what to do. Her father is too far away to help. So she just stays very still on her ladder, holding her basket. The king looks up at her, and smiles a little. He asks if the figs are good this year. Jeanne says, yes, Sire, very good. The king nods, and walks on. Jeanne's knees are shaking for the rest of the day.

[long-pause]

{img:kings-bedchamber-versailles-gilded-bed-curtains-morning-light-courtiers-waiting}
Every morning at Versailles begins with a ceremony. It is called the lever, which means the rising. At about eight o'clock, the king's first valet, who has slept on a little bed in the king's room, wakes him gently. He says the words, Sire, it is time. And from that moment, the day of the king, and of the whole court, begins.

[long-pause]

The king's doctor and his old nurse come in first. They check his health. The nurse kisses him good morning, as she has done since he was a baby. Then, in groups, the courtiers are allowed to enter the bedroom. First come the king's closest family and the highest officials. Then, in waves, more and more people. Each group has a right to enter at a certain moment. It is a great honour to be allowed in early.

[pause]

The king washes his hands and face with a little spirit of wine. He says a short prayer. A barber combs his hair and shaves him, every other day. The king chooses his wig for the day, from a box of different wigs. Then he gets dressed, piece by piece, helped by his officials. Each piece of clothing is handed to him by a particular person, according to their rank. Handing the king his shirt is one of the greatest honours of all.

[pause]

{img:courtiers-crowded-bedchamber-bowing-king-dressing-ceremony-morning}
The bedroom is crowded. There may be a hundred people in the room, all men, all standing, all watching the king get dressed. They wait for a word or a glance from him. Some have come to ask a favour. Some just want to be seen. Some are hoping to be noticed. The king speaks to a few of them, briefly. Most of them, he ignores. But they come back every morning anyway.

[pause]

Why do the nobles put up with all this? Because at Versailles, everything depends on the king. Money, honours, jobs for your sons, good marriages for your daughters. All of it comes from the king. And the king notices who is there, and who is not. If a nobleman is absent too often, the king will say, I do not know him, I never see him. And that nobleman's career is over.

[long-pause]

{img:royal-chapel-mass-king-in-gallery-music-choir-candles-morning}
After he is dressed, the king goes to Mass in the chapel. Every day, at about ten o'clock. The court follows him. The king's musicians and singers fill the chapel with beautiful music. Then the king goes to his council, to work with his ministers, for two or three hours. Nobody is allowed to disturb him there, except in an emergency.

[pause]

The same ceremony happens in reverse at night. It is called the coucher, the going to bed. At about midnight, the king goes to his bedroom. The courtiers gather again. The king takes off his clothes, piece by piece, with the help of his officials. One lucky courtier is chosen to hold the candlestick while the king undresses. It is a little honour, but courtiers fight for it.

[pause]

{img:courtier-holding-candlestick-honour-night-ceremony-bedroom-glow}
For Jeanne and her family, all of this seems very strange. When Jeanne's father gets up in the morning, at five o'clock, he simply gets up, washes his face in the bucket, puts on his clothes and goes to work. Nobody watches him. Nobody hands him his shirt. Jeanne thinks that the king must be very tired of all these people, every morning and every night. Her mother says that perhaps he is used to it. He has done it all his life.

[long-pause]

But Pierre has heard something interesting from the fontainiers. They say that sometimes, after the official ceremony of going to bed, when all the courtiers have left, the king gets up again, quietly. He goes to a smaller room, to talk with his family, play with his dogs, or simply rest, without anyone watching. Jeanne likes that thought. Even the Sun King, it seems, needs a little quiet time alone.

[pause]

While the king is being dressed, the gardens outside are already busy. The gardeners have been working for hours. The paths have been raked. The flowers have been watered. The fountains have been checked. Everything must be perfect, before the king takes his walk. Jeanne has already carried two baskets of strawberries to the kitchens, and eaten one strawberry on the way, when nobody was looking.

[long-pause]

{img:hall-of-mirrors-1685-silver-furniture-chandeliers-tall-windows-gardens}
In the middle of the palace, on the garden side, there is a room that is like nothing else in the world. It is called the Hall of Mirrors. It was finished only a year ago, in 1684. And it has already become the most famous room in Europe. Everyone who comes to Versailles wants to see it.

[long-pause]

The hall is very long, about seventy-three metres, and quite narrow. On one side, there are seventeen tall arched windows, looking out over the gardens. And on the other side, facing each window, there is an arch filled with mirrors. Seventeen arches, with three hundred and fifty-seven mirrors in all. The mirrors reflect the windows, the gardens and the sky. So the hall seems to be twice as wide, and full of light.

[pause]

At that time, mirrors were incredibly expensive. The best mirrors in the world were made in Venice, and the Venetians kept their methods secret. But the king's minister, Colbert, wanted France to make its own mirrors. So he brought some Venetian glassmakers secretly to France, and started a French mirror factory. All the mirrors in the Hall of Mirrors were made in France. It was a way of showing the world that France could do anything.

[pause]

{img:painted-ceiling-hall-of-mirrors-king-victories-gold-frames-colours}
The ceiling is covered with huge paintings, framed in gold. They were painted by the king's first painter, Charles Le Brun. They show the great deeds of Louis the Fourteenth. His wars, his victories and his wise government. The king is shown as a hero, or as a god, surrounded by figures from Greek myths. It took Le Brun and his assistants four years to paint them all.

[pause]

From the ceiling hang great chandeliers of crystal. And along the walls, there are tall candlesticks, statues and orange trees in silver tubs. And at this time, there is something even more astonishing. Much of the furniture in the hall is made of solid silver. Silver tables, silver benches, silver vases and silver candlesticks. When the candles are lit at night, the whole hall glitters like a treasure chest.

[long-pause]

{img:doge-of-genoa-visit-1685-red-robes-bowing-before-king-throne-mirrors}
In May of this year, 1685, something very special happened in the Hall of Mirrors. The Doge of Genoa came to Versailles. The Doge was the ruler of the Republic of Genoa, a rich trading city in Italy. Genoa had angered the king, by helping his enemies. So the king's fleet had bombarded the city. And now, the Doge had to come to Versailles in person, to apologise.

[pause]

The king received him in the Hall of Mirrors, sitting on a silver throne at the end of the hall. The whole court was there, in their finest clothes. The Doge, an old man in red robes, walked slowly down the long hall, past hundreds of courtiers, and bowed before the king. Afterwards, the courtiers showed him the palace and the gardens. And someone asked him what he found most remarkable at Versailles. The Doge replied, with a sad smile, that the most remarkable thing was to see himself there.

[pause]

{img:courtiers-strolling-hall-of-mirrors-afternoon-light-reflections-gossip}
On ordinary days, the Hall of Mirrors is used as a passage. It connects the apartments of the king with those of the queen. Courtiers walk up and down it all day, waiting to see the king pass. They stand in groups, talking and gossiping, looking at themselves in the mirrors. Ordinary visitors can also come in, if they are properly dressed, to watch the king walk by on his way to Mass.

[long-pause]

Jeanne has been in the Hall of Mirrors only once. She was carrying a basket of flowers for the decoration of a party, with some other girls. They walked quickly along the side, trying not to be noticed. But Jeanne could not help looking around. The light, the gold, the mirrors, the silver. She saw herself reflected a hundred times, a small girl in a plain dress, with a basket of flowers. And she thought that it was like walking inside a dream.

[long-pause]

{img:courtiers-1685-silk-coats-lace-wigs-ladies-gowns-gallery-versailles}
The courtiers of Versailles are the nobles of France. Princes, dukes, counts and marquises, with their wives and children. They have come from their castles all over the country, to live at the palace, close to the king. Some have rooms in the palace itself. Others have houses in the town. And all of them spend their days at court, following the king, from morning till night.

[long-pause]

Life at court is very expensive. The courtiers must wear the finest clothes, which cost a fortune. The men wear long coats of silk or velvet, embroidered with gold and silver thread, over long waistcoats. They wear tight breeches to the knee, silk stockings and shoes with buckles. They wear huge curly wigs, like the king, and lace at their necks and wrists. And they carry a sword at their side, and a hat with feathers under their arm.

[pause]

The ladies wear gowns of rich silk, with tight bodices and wide skirts, often with a long train behind. Their gowns are decorated with lace, ribbons and jewels. Their hair is curled and piled up high, with ribbons and lace. They carry fans, which they use not only to cool themselves, but also to send secret signals. A slow wave, a quick snap, the fan held over the lips. Every movement has a meaning.

[pause]

{img:lady-with-fan-curtsy-court-etiquette-gallery-sunlight-silk-gown}
At court, there are rules for everything. They are called etiquette. Who may sit, and on what kind of seat. Who may enter a room first. Who may wear a hat in front of the king. Who may ride in the king's carriage. How deep to bow, and to whom. Only duchesses may sit on a little stool in front of the queen. Everyone else must stand. Courtiers spend a lot of time and energy on these little rules.

[pause]

There is also a special way of knocking on doors. At Versailles, you never knock on a door in the king's apartments. Instead, you gently scratch on it, with the little finger of your left hand. Some courtiers let the nail of that finger grow long, just for this purpose. It sounds strange to us. But at Versailles, these little things showed that you knew how to behave.

[long-pause]

{img:courtiers-playing-cards-gaming-table-candles-coins-evening-salon}
The courtiers have little to do during the day. They do not work. So they fill their time with other things. They follow the king to Mass, to dinner and on his walks. They gossip. They play cards, and often lose huge amounts of money. They go hunting with the king. They write letters. Some of the best letters ever written in French were written by courtiers at Versailles, describing the life of the court to their friends and relatives.

[pause]

But life at court is also tiring. There are always crowds. The palace is noisy and full of people, day and night. The rooms are often small and uncomfortable, even for the great nobles. In winter, the big rooms are freezing cold. And there is no proper plumbing. People use chamber pots, which servants empty. Some visitors even complain that the corridors smell, because not everyone bothers to find a chamber pot in time.

[pause]

{img:tired-courtier-small-attic-room-palace-candle-writing-letter-night}
Some courtiers are unhappy at Versailles. They miss their castles and their estates. They are tired of the rules, the gossip and the endless competition. One famous courtier, the Duke of Saint-Simon, wrote that the king used every little favour, every little sign of attention, to keep the courtiers busy and dependent. He wrote it all down in his memoirs, many years later, and they are some of the best descriptions of court life we have.

[long-pause]

Jeanne watches the courtiers with great curiosity. To her, they look like birds of paradise, all colours and feathers. They walk so slowly, and talk so softly, and laugh behind their fans. They never seem to hurry, and never seem to work. Her mother says that she would not want their life for all the gold in France. Too many rules, she says. Too many eyes watching. Jeanne thinks about it, and decides that her mother is probably right. But she would still like to try on one of those silk dresses, just once.

[long-pause]

{img:versailles-gardens-1685-parterres-gravel-paths-clipped-yews-statues-vista}
Behind the palace, the gardens of Versailles stretch away into the distance. They are enormous. With the park around them, they cover thousands of hectares. They are the largest and most famous formal gardens in the world. And for the king, they are even more important than the palace itself.

[long-pause]

The gardens were designed by a man called André Le Nôtre. In 1685, he is seventy-two years old. He is the son of a gardener, and he grew up in the gardens of the Tuileries palace in Paris. He studied painting and architecture. And then he became the greatest garden designer in history. He has worked for the king for almost twenty-five years. The king loves him like an old friend. Le Nôtre is the only man at court who is allowed to hug the king.

[pause]

Le Nôtre's gardens are very different from the wild, natural gardens that came later. They are formal gardens, with straight lines, perfect shapes and exact symmetry. Everything is planned. The paths are straight. The hedges are clipped into walls. The trees are cut into cones and balls. The flower beds are laid out in patterns, like embroidery on a carpet. They are called parterres.

[pause]

{img:parterre-embroidery-pattern-box-hedges-colored-gravel-flowers-above-view}
Right behind the palace, there are two great water parterres. They are big, flat pools of water, with bronze statues around their edges, representing the great rivers of France. The pools reflect the palace and the sky, like mirrors. From the windows of the Hall of Mirrors, you look out over the water, and then down a long, wide avenue, toward the horizon. It is one of the most beautiful views in the world.

[pause]

From the terrace, wide stone stairs lead down into the gardens. There is a main avenue, straight as an arrow, going west, toward the setting sun. On both sides of it, there are thick groves of trees, called bosquets. And hidden inside each grove, there is a surprise. A secret garden, with a fountain, a theatre of green hedges, a colonnade of marble, or a ballroom with water cascades.

[long-pause]

{img:bosquet-hidden-grove-marble-colonnade-fountain-trees-surrounding}
Walking through the gardens is like walking through a book of stories. There are statues everywhere. Hundreds of them, in marble and bronze. Gods and goddesses, heroes and nymphs, the four seasons, the four continents, the hours of the day. Many of them tell the story of Apollo, the god of the sun, which is also the story of the king. In the morning, the sun rises in the east, over the palace. In the evening, it sets in the west, at the end of the great canal.

[pause]

The gardens need an army of gardeners. Hundreds of men work in them every day. They clip the hedges with big shears, standing on tall ladders. They rake the gravel paths into perfect lines. They plant flowers, millions of them, every year. When the king wants a different colour in a flower bed, the gardeners change all the flowers overnight. Once, people say, the flowers in one garden were changed twice in a single day.

[pause]

{img:gardeners-on-ladders-clipping-tall-hedges-shears-morning-gardens}
The king loves to walk in his gardens. A few years from now, he will even write a little guidebook, in his own handwriting. It will be called The Way to Present the Gardens of Versailles. It tells visitors exactly which way to walk, where to stop, and what to look at. Leave the palace by the door of the vestibule of the Marble Court, it begins. Go onto the terrace, stop at the top of the steps, and look at the arrangement of the parterres. Already now, the king often guides important visitors himself, along exactly the route he likes best.

[long-pause]

Jeanne knows the gardens very well, but in a different way from the courtiers. She knows the hidden paths, which the gardeners use. She knows which statue has a crack in its nose. She knows where the frogs live, in the pools. And she knows where the old gardeners sit in the shade at midday, to eat their bread and cheese, and talk about the weather, the king and the price of bread. To her, the gardens are not a book of stories. They are home.

[pause]

In the hot summer afternoons, the groves are cool and shady. The tall trees close over the paths like the roof of a green church. The fountains splash softly. The birds sing in the branches. Courtiers sit on stone benches in the shade, fanning themselves and reading letters. And sometimes, in a quiet corner, an old gardener is fast asleep on a pile of cut grass, with his hat over his face.

[long-pause]

{img:latona-fountain-versailles-golden-frogs-water-jets-tiers-sunlight}
The fountains are the greatest wonder of Versailles. There are more than a thousand water jets in the gardens. When they all play together, the gardens are filled with sparkling water, leaping, falling, splashing and shining in the sun. Visitors from all over Europe come to see them. And the king loves them more than anything.

[long-pause]

One of the most famous is the Latona Fountain. It tells an old story from Greek myths. The goddess Latona, the mother of Apollo, was insulted by some peasants. So the god Jupiter turned them into frogs. The fountain shows Latona at the top, with her children, and all around her, on the steps below, the peasants turning into frogs and lizards. And from the mouths of all the frogs, water spouts up into the air. Children love it.

[pause]

At the other end of the main avenue, there is the Apollo Fountain. It shows the god of the sun, rising out of the water in his chariot, pulled by four horses, with sea monsters around him. The statue is made of lead, covered with gold. When the fountain plays, the water rises up around the chariot, and the horses seem to be galloping out of the waves. It is the symbol of the king himself, rising like the sun each morning.

[pause]

{img:apollo-fountain-chariot-horses-rising-water-gilded-lead-morning-spray}
But there is a problem. Versailles does not have enough water. The palace stands on a hill, far from any big river. To run all the fountains at once, an enormous amount of water is needed. So the king's engineers have built a huge system of ponds, reservoirs, canals and pipes. They collect rainwater from all the land around Versailles. And just recently, they have built a gigantic machine on the river Seine.

[pause]

It is called the Machine of Marly. It has fourteen enormous water wheels, turned by the river. They drive more than two hundred pumps, which push the water of the Seine up a hill, more than a hundred and fifty metres high. From there, the water flows through an aqueduct toward Versailles. It is one of the most complicated machines in the world. It makes a terrible creaking and groaning noise, which can be heard from far away.

[long-pause]

{img:fontainiers-whistle-signal-opening-valve-underground-pipes-lanterns}
Even with all this, there is still not enough water to run all the fountains all the time. So the fontainiers have a clever trick. When the king walks in the gardens, they turn on only the fountains near him. They stand at their valves, hidden behind the hedges, and wait for a signal. When the king is coming, a boy blows a whistle. The fontainiers open the valves, and the fountains come to life, just as the king arrives. When he has passed, another whistle, and they close them again.

[pause]

This is now Pierre's job. He is one of the boys who run ahead of the king, through the hidden paths, blowing whistles to the fontainiers. He must be fast, and he must know every path and every valve. He must never be seen by the king or the courtiers. It is exciting work. Pierre runs like the wind, through the groves, with his whistle in his mouth, and the fountains bursting into life behind him.

[pause]

{img:boy-running-hidden-garden-path-whistle-fountain-bursting-behind}
Underneath the gardens, there is a hidden world of pipes. Kilometres of pipes, made of lead and cast iron, carry the water from the reservoirs to every fountain. The fontainiers must know them all by heart. They walk through the underground tunnels with lanterns, checking for leaks. When a pipe breaks, they must fix it quickly, before the king notices that a fountain is weak.

[long-pause]

On special days, when there are important visitors, all the fountains play at once. Then the whole garden is alive with water. Jets shooting high into the air, cascades tumbling down marble steps, water spouting from the mouths of frogs and dolphins and gods. The sunlight makes rainbows in the spray. The sound of the water fills the air. And everyone, courtiers and gardeners alike, stops to look and listen, and holds their breath.

[long-pause]

{img:grand-canal-versailles-cross-shape-long-water-boats-summer-evening}
At the end of the main avenue, beyond the Apollo Fountain, there is a great sheet of water. It is called the Grand Canal. It is shaped like a huge cross. The long arm is more than one and a half kilometres long, stretching away toward the horizon. The short arm crosses it in the middle. On a calm day, the water is perfectly still, and reflects the sky like a giant mirror.

[long-pause]

The Grand Canal is not only beautiful. It is also a place for fun. On the water, there is a whole little fleet of boats. There are small sailing ships, models of real warships, with tiny cannons and colourful flags. There are rowing boats and little galleys. And there are gondolas, the long black boats of Venice, with their curved prows. The Republic of Venice gave the king two gondolas, as a gift. And with them came real Venetian gondoliers, to row them.

[pause]

The gondoliers live in a little group of houses at the end of the canal, which people call Little Venice. They wear their Venetian costumes, and sing Italian songs as they row. There are also sailors, carpenters and boatbuilders, who look after the boats. All together, there are dozens of men who work on the canal. It is like a little harbour, in the middle of the countryside.

[pause]

{img:venetian-gondola-on-grand-canal-courtiers-ladies-parasol-gondolier-singing}
On summer evenings, the king and the court often go out on the canal. The ladies and gentlemen climb into the gondolas and the little boats. They glide slowly over the water, in the cool evening air. Musicians play on a boat nearby. Violins and flutes, and sometimes singers. The music floats over the water. The sun sets at the end of the canal, turning the water red and gold. It is one of the most romantic things at Versailles.

[pause]

Sometimes, there are little mock sea battles on the canal, with the model warships firing their tiny cannons, to entertain the court. And sometimes, in the evening, the canal is lit up with hundreds of lanterns, on the boats and along the banks, and there is music and fireworks. The reflections of the lights dance on the water. People say it looks like a fairy tale.

[long-pause]

{img:canal-lit-lanterns-night-boats-music-reflections-fairy-tale}
In winter, when the canal freezes, the courtiers go skating on it. Or they ride in little sledges, pulled by horses, decorated with bells and furs. But now it is summer, and the canal is a place of cool water and evening breezes. Along the banks, there are long rows of trees, which give shade. People walk there in the late afternoon, when the heat of the day is fading.

[pause]

Jeanne and Pierre love the canal too. On Sunday afternoons, when they are free, they walk along it, far away from the palace, where the courtiers rarely go. They watch the boats, and the swans, and the fish jumping. They sit on the grass under the trees, eating bread and cherries. Sometimes, the gondoliers wave to them, and sing a little song in Italian, just for them.

[pause]

{img:two-siblings-sitting-canal-bank-trees-eating-cherries-swans-afternoon}
One Sunday, one of the gondoliers, an old man called Zuane, invites them into his gondola, when nobody is looking. He rows them slowly along the canal, standing at the back, with his long oar. He sings a song from Venice, about the moon and the sea. Jeanne trails her fingers in the cool water. Pierre asks a hundred questions about how the gondola is built. And for half an hour, the gardener's children feel like a prince and princess.

[long-pause]

When they get out at the bank, Zuane puts a finger to his lips and winks. It is their secret. Jeanne thanks him in her best manners. Then the two children walk home through the gardens, in the evening light. Behind them, the canal shines like gold, all the way to the horizon. And Jeanne thinks that she will remember this afternoon for the rest of her life.

[long-pause]

{img:versailles-orangery-arched-gallery-orange-trees-silver-tubs-summer-parterre}
Below the south terrace of the palace, there is a special garden for orange trees. It is called the Orangery. In 1685, a great new building for it is being finished, designed by the architect Mansart. It is a long, vaulted hall, built into the hillside, with tall arched windows facing south, to catch the sun. And in front of it, there is a big flat garden, where the orange trees stand in summer.

[long-pause]

Orange trees come from warm countries, like Spain, Portugal and Italy. They cannot survive the cold winters of northern France outside. So in winter, they must be kept indoors, protected from the frost. The new Orangery is like a huge greenhouse. Its walls are about four metres thick, to keep out the cold. Inside, even in the hardest winter, the temperature never falls below about five degrees. The trees are safe.

[pause]

In summer, the trees are brought outside, in their big wooden tubs. There are more than a thousand orange trees, and also lemon trees, pomegranates, oleanders and palms. They stand in neat rows around the parterre, in square boxes painted green. Some of them are very old. A few of them came from the gardens of a minister who fell out of favour with the king, many years ago. One tree was already said to be more than a hundred and fifty years old.

[pause]

{img:gardeners-moving-orange-tree-tub-wooden-cart-team-of-men-spring}
Moving the trees is a big job. Each tub is heavy, full of earth, and some trees are taller than a man. In spring, when the frost is over, teams of gardeners move them outside, one by one, with special wooden carts and lifting machines. In autumn, before the first frost, they move them all back inside again. It takes many days. Jacques, Jeanne's father, sometimes helps with the moving, when the Orangery needs extra hands.

[pause]

The king loves orange trees. In the seventeenth century, they are a sign of great wealth and luxury. The king has them everywhere. In the gardens, on the terraces, and even inside the palace, in silver tubs, in the Hall of Mirrors and the grand apartments. Their white flowers smell sweet and strong, and fill the rooms with their scent. The king likes that smell very much.

[long-pause]

{img:orange-blossoms-white-flowers-close-glossy-leaves-sunlight-bees}
In the summer evenings, the Orangery garden is a lovely place. The air is warm and still. The orange trees are in flower, and the scent is so strong that you can smell it from the terrace above. The courtiers walk between the rows of trees, slowly, talking quietly. The fountains in the middle play gently. And the sun sets behind the hills, turning the white stone of the Orangery pink and gold.

[pause]

Jeanne sometimes helps the Orangery gardeners water the trees, on hot summer mornings. She carries water in a big watering can, from tree to tree. The gardeners teach her how to tell if a tree is thirsty, by looking at its leaves. They show her how to pick off the dead flowers, and how to look for insects under the leaves. One old gardener gives her a small orange, from one of the trees. It is a little sour. But Jeanne eats it slowly, savouring every bit. She has never eaten an orange before.

[pause]

{img:girl-holding-small-orange-smiling-watering-can-orangery-garden}
Beyond the Orangery garden, there is a large lake, called the Lake of the Swiss Guards. It was dug by the soldiers of the king's Swiss regiment, in a marshy field. It is long and quiet, with trees around it. People go fishing there, and walk along its banks. In the evening, the mist rises from it, and the frogs sing in the reeds.

[long-pause]

Today, the Orangery of Versailles still stands, and still shelters its orange trees in winter, more than three hundred years later. Every spring, the gardeners still carry them out into the sunshine, in their green boxes, just as they did in the time of the Sun King. And every autumn, they carry them back inside again, to sleep through the winter, safe and warm.

[long-pause]

{img:grand-couvert-king-dining-alone-table-courtiers-watching-candlelight}
At Versailles, even the king's meals are a kind of show. Every evening at ten o'clock, the king eats his supper in public. It is called the grand couvert. He sits at a table in one of the rooms of his apartment. And around him, people stand and watch him eat. Courtiers, but also ordinary visitors, who have come to Versailles especially to see it.

[long-pause]

The king sits alone at his table, or with members of his family. Nobody else is allowed to sit, except the princes and princesses of the royal blood. Duchesses may sit on little stools, a little way back. Everyone else stands, in silence, around the walls. The king's musicians play softly. And the food is brought in, dish after dish, in a grand procession.

[pause]

The food has a long way to travel. The kitchens are not in the palace itself. They are in a separate building, across the street, to keep away the smell and the danger of fire. So the dishes must be carried through the streets, up the stairs and through the corridors, by a long line of servants, guarded by soldiers. It is called the king's meat. When it passes, everyone must bow, and the men take off their hats, as if the king himself were passing.

[pause]

{img:procession-of-servants-carrying-covered-dishes-through-palace-corridor}
By the time it arrives, the food is often a little cold. But there is a lot of it. The king has an enormous appetite. A meal might have four or five courses, each with many dishes. Soups, pies, roast meats, game birds, fish, salads, vegetables, fruit and sweets. His sister-in-law once wrote that she had seen the king eat four plates of soup, a whole pheasant, a partridge, a big plate of salad, two slices of ham, some mutton with garlic, a plate of pastry, and then fruit and hard-boiled eggs.

[pause]

The king eats with his fingers, mostly. Forks are known, and some courtiers use them. But the king prefers his fingers and his knife, as kings always have. He does not like forks very much. And he drinks his wine mixed with water, from a glass that is handed to him by an official, after it has been tasted for poison.

[long-pause]

{img:palace-kitchen-1685-cooks-roasting-spits-copper-pots-busy-steam}
The king's kitchens are huge. Hundreds of cooks, bakers, pastry chefs, sauce makers and kitchen boys work there. They prepare not only the king's meals, but meals for hundreds of members of the royal household. There are separate kitchens for meat, for pastry, for bread and for fruit. And there is a special officer, responsible for the king's fruit, who receives the baskets that Jeanne carries from the kitchen garden.

[pause]

The most exciting new thing in the kitchens is ice. In deep pits near the palace, called ice houses, ice from the frozen ponds is stored all winter, packed in straw. In summer, it is used to cool drinks, and to make something wonderful. Frozen sweets, made from fruit juice, sugar and ice. Sorbets and water ices, flavoured with lemon, strawberry or orange blossom. On hot summer evenings, they are served to the court in little glass cups.

[pause]

{img:sorbet-in-small-glass-cups-silver-tray-fruit-ice-summer-evening}
Jeanne has never tasted a sorbet. But one evening, a kitchen boy who is a friend of Pierre's saves a little cup of lemon ice that was not eaten. He gives it to Pierre, who runs all the way home with it, as fast as he can. By the time he arrives, it has half melted. But Jeanne tastes it anyway, with a little spoon. It is cold, and sweet, and sour, all at once. She closes her eyes. It is the best thing she has ever tasted.

[long-pause]

At home, the family eats much more simply. Bread is the most important food, as it is for all ordinary people in France. A worker eats about one kilo of bread a day. With the bread, there is soup, made from vegetables and a little bacon. Cheese, eggs, beans, and fruit from the garden. Meat only on Sundays, if they are lucky. And a little wine, mixed with water. It is simple food. But Marguerite is a good cook. And after a long day in the garden, Jacques says that the king himself does not eat better.

[long-pause]

{img:lully-conducting-violins-orchestra-palace-salon-candlelight-courtiers}
Versailles is full of music. Every day, from morning till night, there is music somewhere in the palace. Music at Mass in the chapel. Music during the king's dinner. Music for dancing, for walks in the gardens, for boat trips on the canal. Music for plays, ballets and operas. The king loves music, and he has the best musicians in Europe.

[long-pause]

The most famous musician at Versailles is Jean-Baptiste Lully. He was born in Florence, in Italy, the son of a miller. As a boy, he came to France to work as a servant, and to teach Italian to a princess. But he was so talented that he soon became a musician at court. He played the violin, danced, and composed music. When the king was young, they danced together in the ballets. Now Lully is the most powerful musician in France.

[pause]

Lully writes operas, ballets, church music and music for dancing. He has invented a new kind of French opera, with beautiful songs, grand choruses and graceful dances. His music is elegant, proud and full of rhythm, like the king himself. Everyone at court can hum his tunes. And he leads the king's famous orchestra, the Twenty-Four Violins of the King.

[pause]

{img:twenty-four-violins-musicians-playing-gallery-wigs-bows-raised}
The Twenty-Four Violins play at all the important events at court. Lully trains them very strictly. They must all play exactly together, starting and stopping at the same moment, with their bows moving in the same direction. Other orchestras in Europe are amazed by their discipline and precision. Lully beats the time with a long, heavy staff, which he bangs on the floor.

[pause]

Music is also played in the gardens. Little groups of musicians are hidden in the groves, behind the hedges. When the king and his guests walk through the gardens, they suddenly hear music, coming from nowhere. Flutes, oboes, violins. It sounds magical. And in the evenings, on the canal, there are musicians on the boats, playing as the gondolas glide over the water.

[long-pause]

{img:courtiers-dancing-minuet-ballroom-candles-mirrors-musicians-gallery}
Dancing is very important at Versailles. Every courtier must know how to dance well. They take lessons from dancing masters, from the time they are small children. The favourite dance is the minuet. It is slow and graceful, with small, delicate steps, bows and curtsies. The dancers move in patterns, like the figures in a garden. To dance badly at court is a great embarrassment.

[pause]

There are also great balls, especially in winter. The rooms are lit with thousands of candles. The courtiers wear their finest clothes and jewels. The musicians play in a gallery. And the dancers move across the floor, in pairs and in groups, for hours. The king used to dance at every ball. Now that he is older, he mostly watches. But his son and his grandchildren dance in his place.

[pause]

{img:girl-listening-to-music-from-behind-hedge-garden-evening-light}
Jeanne loves the music of Versailles. When she works in the gardens in the evening, she sometimes hears the violins from the palace windows, or from a grove where a party is being held. She stops what she is doing, and listens. She does not know the names of the pieces. But the music makes her feel happy and sad at the same time. She tries to remember the tunes, and hums them on her way home.

[long-pause]

Lully will pass away in 1687, two years after our story, after a strange accident. While conducting, he hit his own foot with his heavy staff. The wound became infected, and he would not let the doctors treat it properly. But his music lived on. It was played at Versailles for many years after his death. And it is still played today, in concerts all over the world, and in the palace of Versailles itself, where it first filled the rooms with sound.

[pause]

Some evenings, Jeanne and Pierre sit on the wall of the kitchen garden, close to the palace, and listen. The music drifts over the gardens in the warm air. Violins, then a flute, then a whole orchestra. They do not move. They do not talk. They just listen, with the stars coming out above them, and the smell of ripe fruit around them. And Pierre says that it is almost as beautiful as the sound of a fountain.

[long-pause]

{img:porcelain-trianon-blue-white-tiles-small-pavilion-flower-garden-1685}
Sometimes, even the Sun King needs to get away from his own court. The palace is always crowded, always noisy, always full of people watching him. So, at the far end of the gardens, near the arm of the Grand Canal, the king has built himself a small, private retreat. It is called the Trianon. And in 1685, it is one of the most charming little buildings in France.

[long-pause]

The Trianon is made to look like porcelain. Its walls and its roof are covered with tiles of white and blue earthenware, made in the style of Chinese porcelain. At that time, porcelain from China was very rare and very precious in Europe. Everyone admired it. So the king wanted a little palace that looked like it was made of porcelain. People call it the Porcelain Trianon.

[pause]

It is not a big building. There is a small main house, and four little pavilions around a courtyard. Inside, the rooms are decorated in blue and white, like the outside. There are vases, painted furniture and silk curtains. It is light, gay and pretty. Very different from the heavy gold and marble of the big palace. The king comes here to rest, to eat a light meal with a few friends, or simply to enjoy the flowers.

[pause]

{img:trianon-flower-garden-tulips-hyacinths-jasmine-beds-colourful}
Because the most famous thing about the Trianon is its flower garden. It is called the palace of Flora, the goddess of flowers. The garden is filled with flowers, all year round, in every colour. Tulips, hyacinths, anemones, narcissus, carnations, jasmine and tuberoses. In summer, the scent is so strong that people say it is almost overwhelming. Once, the king and his guests had to leave the garden, because the smell of the tuberoses was too strong.

[pause]

The flowers are grown in pots, by the thousands. They are kept in greenhouses and nurseries nearby. And when a flower begins to fade, the gardeners simply take away the pot and replace it with a fresh one, in full bloom. So the flower beds are always perfect. When the king walks in the garden, he never sees a single faded flower. The gardeners work at night, so that nobody sees them changing the pots.

[long-pause]

{img:gardeners-at-night-lanterns-replacing-flower-pots-trianon-beds}
Jeanne's father sometimes works at the Trianon, when they need extra gardeners. And one summer night, Jeanne is allowed to come with him. They work by the light of lanterns, carrying pots of flowers from the nursery to the garden, and taking away the faded ones. Jeanne carries pot after pot of carnations, red and white and pink. The garden smells wonderful in the night air.

[pause]

When the work is done, it is almost dawn. The sky is turning pale in the east. The birds are beginning to sing. Jeanne stands at the edge of the garden, looking at the flower beds, all fresh and perfect in the grey light. In a few hours, the king might walk here, and see only flowers, and never know that a little girl spent the whole night carrying them.

[pause]

{img:dawn-over-trianon-garden-fresh-flowers-mist-birds-quiet-pastel}
The Porcelain Trianon was very beautiful, but it had one big problem. The tiles did not like the cold, wet winters of northern France. They cracked and fell off. Every year, they had to be repaired. And so, two years after our story, in 1687, the king had it pulled down. In its place, he built a new Trianon, of pink marble, with a long open gallery of columns. It is called the Grand Trianon. It still stands today.

[long-pause]

Later, the kings who came after Louis also loved the Trianon area. A new little palace was built nearby, called the Petit Trianon. And there, almost a hundred years after our story, a young queen called Marie Antoinette would make her own little world, with a pretend farm village, where she could escape the rules of the court, just as the Sun King once escaped them in his little porcelain house among the flowers.

[long-pause]

{img:versailles-menagerie-octagonal-pavilion-animal-courtyards-birds-1685}
At the other end of the Grand Canal, on the south side, there is another surprise. A zoo. The Romans had animal collections, and so did some kings before Louis. But the Menagerie of Versailles is something new. It is a beautiful, elegant place, designed not just to keep animals, but to show them, like works of art.

[long-pause]

In the middle, there is a small, eight-sided pavilion, with a balcony around the top floor. From this balcony, visitors can look down into seven courtyards, arranged around the pavilion like the slices of a pie. Each courtyard has different animals. And each has a little house for them, a pond, and a fence of iron railings. So from one place, the king and his guests can see all the animals at once.

[pause]

The Menagerie is full of wonderful birds. There are flamingos, pink and long-legged. There are pelicans, cranes, storks and ostriches. There are peacocks, with their shining tails. There are rare ducks and geese from faraway lands, and a whole flock of exotic pigeons and parrots. Most of the animals at the Menagerie are birds. They are beautiful, peaceful and easy to keep.

[pause]

{img:flamingos-pelicans-crane-pond-menagerie-courtyard-sunlight}
But there are also bigger animals. There are gazelles, with their graceful legs and big dark eyes. There is a camel, from North Africa. There are monkeys, who are very popular with the visitors, because they play and chatter. There is a pair of ostriches. And until a few years ago, there was the most famous animal of all. An elephant, sent as a gift from the king of Portugal. It lived at the Menagerie for thirteen years, and everyone at court came to see it.

[pause]

The animals come from all over the world. Some are gifts from foreign kings and ambassadors. Others are brought back by French ships, from Africa, the Middle East and the Americas. The king pays for special expeditions to collect rare animals. And many of them do not survive the long, hard journey. Those who do are looked after very carefully at Versailles.

[long-pause]

{img:scientists-drawing-animals-sketchbooks-menagerie-artist-studying-bird}
The Menagerie is not only for fun. It is also for science. The scientists of the king's new Academy of Sciences come here to study the animals. They watch how they live, how they move and what they eat. They make careful drawings and notes. When an animal dies, they study its body, to understand how it works. Their work is published in big books, with beautiful pictures.

[pause]

Artists also come to the Menagerie, to paint and draw the animals. Some of the most beautiful animal paintings of the time were made here. And the animals appear everywhere in the decoration of Versailles. In the statues and the fountains, in the paintings and the tapestries. Many people in France have never seen a flamingo or a camel. But at Versailles, they can see them, painted or real.

[pause]

{img:children-watching-monkeys-railing-menagerie-laughing-summer-day}
One Sunday, Jeanne and Pierre walk to the Menagerie. Ordinary people are sometimes allowed to visit, if they are respectable and polite. They walk around the outside of the courtyards, and look through the railings. Jeanne loves the flamingos, standing on one leg in the pond. Pierre loves the monkeys, who climb up the railings and stretch out their little hands. One monkey grabs Pierre's hat and runs off with it. The keeper has to chase it around the courtyard to get it back. Jeanne laughs so much that she has to sit down.

[long-pause]

The Menagerie no longer exists today. It was abandoned after the French Revolution, and its last animals were sent to Paris, where they started the zoo of the Jardin des Plantes, which still exists. The buildings at Versailles were pulled down. But if you walk to the end of the south arm of the Grand Canal today, you can still find the place where it stood, and imagine the flamingos, standing in their pond, in the summer sun.

[pause]

In the evening, when the visitors have gone, the Menagerie is very peaceful. The birds settle down for the night. The flamingos tuck their heads under their wings. The gazelles lie down in the straw of their little houses. The keepers walk around one last time, checking the gates. And the sun sets over the canal, painting the eight-sided pavilion gold and pink.

[long-pause]

{img:appartement-evening-state-rooms-candles-courtiers-games-music-1685}
Three evenings a week, from six o'clock until ten, there is a special kind of party at Versailles. It is called an appartement. The king opens his grand state rooms to the whole court. And everyone who is properly dressed can come in, and enjoy themselves. There is music, dancing, games, food and drink, all free, all paid for by the king.

[long-pause]

The state rooms are a long row of grand rooms, one after another, each named after a planet or a god. The Room of Venus, the Room of Diana, the Room of Mars, the Room of Mercury, the Room of Apollo. Their ceilings are painted with gods and goddesses. Their walls are covered with coloured marble and red and green velvet. And on appartement evenings, they are lit by hundreds of candles, in chandeliers and silver candlesticks.

[pause]

Each room has its own purpose for the evening. In one room, there are tables with refreshments. Fruit, sweets, jams, cakes, and drinks, both hot and cold. Wine, lemonade, sorbets, and the new and fashionable drinks of coffee, tea and hot chocolate. Footmen stand behind the tables, serving the guests. In another room, there is music, with the king's musicians playing. In another, there is dancing.

[pause]

{img:billiard-table-17th-century-king-playing-cue-courtiers-watching}
In the Room of Diana, there is a billiard table. The king loves billiards. He is very good at it. In the past, he often played on appartement evenings, with his courtiers watching. The courtiers clapped politely whenever he made a good shot, and tried not to win too often. Billiards in those days was played with a curved stick, more like a little hockey stick than a modern cue.

[pause]

In other rooms, there are tables for card games and dice games. The courtiers play for money, often very large sums. Some courtiers lose fortunes in a single evening. Others win. The king does not mind them gambling, as long as they do it at court, where he can see them. The favourite games have strange names. Lansquenet, hoca, reversi and bassette. People crowd around the tables, watching the cards fall.

[long-pause]

{img:ladies-gentlemen-conversation-velvet-room-chandelier-painted-ceiling}
The appartement evenings are less formal than other events at court. People can walk from room to room, talk to whoever they like, eat and drink and play. The king himself walks through the rooms, chatting with people, without too much ceremony. Nobody has to stand up when he passes, if they are playing cards. It is the most relaxed time of the day at Versailles.

[pause]

For the courtiers, these evenings are very important. It is a chance to be seen, and to talk to people who might help them. A young man might meet a powerful minister. A mother might find a good husband for her daughter. A poor nobleman might catch the king's eye. And everyone gossips, about who was wearing what, who was talking to whom, and who was not invited to the king's last supper.

[pause]

{img:footman-serving-hot-chocolate-silver-pot-porcelain-cups-candlelight}
Hot chocolate is the most fashionable drink at court. It comes from the Americas, by way of Spain. It is made by melting chocolate with water or milk, sugar and spices, like vanilla and cinnamon, and whisking it until it foams. It is served in little porcelain cups. The ladies of the court love it. Some of them drink it in bed in the morning. It is very expensive, and only the rich can afford it.

[long-pause]

Marguerite, Jeanne's mother, once helped in the kitchens on an appartement evening, when they needed extra hands to wash the cups. She brought home a little bit of chocolate, wrapped in paper, which the chief cook gave her. The family melted it in a pot with milk, the way she had seen the cooks do. They each had a small cup. It was thick, sweet and dark, and tasted of spices. Pierre said it tasted like the king's own breakfast. And for one evening, in their little house in the town, the gardener's family felt like courtiers too.

[long-pause]

{img:ordinary-visitors-arriving-palace-gates-carriages-crowds-summer-morning}
Versailles is not a closed palace. In fact, almost anyone can visit it. The king wants his subjects to see his palace and his gardens, and to admire them. So every day, people come from Paris and from all over France, and even from other countries, to look around. The palace is like a great public museum, while the king is still living in it.

[long-pause]

There are only a few rules. Visitors must be properly dressed. A man must wear a hat and carry a sword, which is the sign of a gentleman. But at the gates of the palace, there are little shops where you can rent a hat and a sword for the day, if you do not have your own. Beggars, monks and people who have recently had smallpox are not allowed in. Everyone else can come.

[pause]

The visitors can walk through the gardens, and through many of the rooms of the palace. They can watch the king walk to Mass, through the Hall of Mirrors. They can watch the king eat his supper, at the grand couvert. They can see the fountains play, if they are lucky. Some visitors come on special excursions, organised from Paris, in big coaches. They come in the morning and go back in the evening, tired and amazed.

[pause]

{img:shop-at-gate-renting-hats-swords-visitors-1685-street-humour}
The visitors are a mixture of all kinds of people. Rich merchants from Paris, with their wives and daughters. Country gentlemen, who have come to the capital for business. Students and artists. Foreign travellers, writing down everything they see in their notebooks. And sometimes, ordinary working people, on a holiday, who have walked all the way from Paris, to see the famous palace with their own eyes.

[pause]

There are also many people who come to ask the king for something. They wait in the corridors, or along the king's path, holding a written request. A widow asking for a pension. A soldier asking for a reward. A man asking for justice in a dispute. The king has a special table, in the guard room, where people can leave their requests. And every Monday, his officials read them, and some of them are answered.

[long-pause]

{img:stag-hunt-forest-riders-hounds-horns-autumn-light-distance}
Some visitors also come to see the king go hunting. Hunting is one of the king's greatest pleasures. Several times a week, he rides out into the great forests around Versailles, with his courtiers, his huntsmen and his packs of hounds. The huntsmen blow their long curved horns. The dogs bark. The horses gallop. The people in the town stand at their doors to watch the hunt ride past. We will leave the hunt there, riding away into the forest, the sound of the horns growing fainter among the trees.

[pause]

Jeanne likes the visitors. They are often lost in the gardens, and she helps them find their way. They ask her questions. Where is the Latona Fountain? When do the fountains play? Has she seen the king? Jeanne answers politely, and shows them the way. Sometimes they give her a small coin. Once, an English lady gave her a ribbon. And once, a little boy from Lyon gave her a piece of his gingerbread.

[pause]

{img:girl-giving-directions-visitors-gravel-path-garden-pointing-fountain}
There is even talk of visitors from much further away. People say that the king of Siam, which is now called Thailand, in Asia, will soon send ambassadors to the Sun King, on a long voyage across the oceans. They will bring wonderful gifts, of gold, silk and porcelain. And indeed, a year later, in 1686, they will walk through the Hall of Mirrors, and all of Paris will talk about them. Jeanne can hardly wait to see them.

[long-pause]

At the end of each day, the visitors leave the palace, and go back to the town, or to Paris. The gates are closed. The gardens become quiet. And the courtiers have Versailles to themselves again, until the next morning, when the first coaches of visitors arrive once more, and the whole show begins again.

[long-pause]

{img:versailles-summer-night-festival-illuminations-lanterns-gardens-crowds}
At the end of the summer, there is a great festival at Versailles. The king wants to celebrate. And when the Sun King celebrates, the whole of Versailles turns into a fairy tale. For weeks, the workers have been preparing. Carpenters have built stages and pavilions in the gardens. Painters have painted decorations. The fontainiers have checked every pipe. And the fireworks makers have been busy in their workshops, preparing their rockets.

[long-pause]

The festival begins in the late afternoon. The whole court gathers in the gardens, in their finest clothes. The ladies in silk gowns of every colour, glittering with jewels. The gentlemen in coats of gold and silver embroidery, with feathers in their hats. There are thousands of people. The king walks at the front, with his family, guiding his guests through the gardens.

[pause]

All the fountains are playing. Every single one. The water leaps and sparkles in the evening sun. In the groves, musicians are playing. In one grove, there is a little play, with actors in costumes. In another, there is a ballet. In another, tables are laid with food. Fruit, sweets, cakes, cold meats, sorbets and wine, arranged in tall pyramids on silver dishes. The guests walk from grove to grove, eating, listening, watching and talking.

[pause]

{img:banquet-tables-in-garden-grove-pyramids-of-fruit-candles-evening}
As the sun sets, the gardens are lit up. Thousands of lanterns and candles are lit, along the paths, around the fountains, on the terraces and along the edges of the canal. The palace windows glow with light. The whole garden shimmers in the darkness, like a sky full of stars brought down to earth. The light reflects in the fountains and the pools, so that everything seems to glow twice.

[pause]

Then comes the most wonderful moment. The fireworks. From the far end of the Grand Canal, the first rocket shoots up into the night sky, with a whistle and a bang, and bursts into a shower of golden stars. Then another, and another. Red, white, gold and silver. Fountains of fire. Wheels of light, spinning on the water. Rockets bursting high above the trees. The crowd gasps and claps and cheers.

[long-pause]

{img:fireworks-over-grand-canal-golden-bursts-reflections-palace-night}
The fireworks are reflected in the long water of the canal, so that there seem to be twice as many. The smoke drifts over the gardens, smelling of gunpowder. Boats with lanterns float on the canal. Music plays. And at the end, there is a grand finale, with hundreds of rockets at once, lighting up the whole sky, until it seems as bright as day. And then, slowly, darkness returns, and the crowd sighs with pleasure.

[pause]

The workers of Versailles also watch the festival. They are not invited, of course. But they stand at the edges of the gardens, behind the hedges and on the hillsides, and watch from a distance. Jacques and Marguerite stand together on a little hill near the canal, with their children. They have worked hard to make this night possible. Jacques picked the fruit. Marguerite washed the tablecloths. Pierre ran with his whistle all evening, making the fountains play.

[pause]

{img:family-watching-fireworks-from-hillside-distance-children-faces-lit}
And Jeanne helped to carry the flowers for the decorations. Now she stands beside her mother, watching the fireworks burst over the canal. Each one makes her gasp. The colours light up her face. Pierre, out of breath, comes running up the hill to join them, just in time for the finale. He says that he ran more than ten kilometres tonight, and that the king smiled at the Latona Fountain. Everyone laughs.

[long-pause]

Late at night, the festival is over. The courtiers go back to the palace. The lanterns are put out, one by one. The fountains are turned off. The gardens become dark and quiet. The smell of gunpowder slowly fades. And the gardener's family walks home through the sleeping town, tired and happy. Jeanne holds her father's hand. She does not say much. But she knows that she has seen something she will never forget.

[long-pause]

{img:versailles-gardens-early-autumn-golden-leaves-gardeners-raking-quiet}
And then, slowly, the summer comes to an end. The days get shorter. The evenings get cooler. The leaves of the trees in the groves begin to turn yellow and orange. The fruit in the kitchen garden is picked. The last peaches, the first apples, the great pears of autumn. And the gardeners begin to prepare the gardens for the winter.

[long-pause]

In September, the court often leaves Versailles for a few weeks, to go to the king's other palace, at Fontainebleau, where the king hunts in the great forest. Then Versailles becomes very quiet. The corridors are empty. The fountains are silent. The gardens belong to the gardeners again. It is the best time of the year, Jacques says, when the gardeners can work in peace, without having to hide behind the hedges every time someone important walks by.

[pause]

The orange trees are carried back into the Orangery, one by one. The flower pots of the Trianon are taken back to the greenhouses. The boats on the canal are pulled up onto the bank, and covered. The fontainiers check all the pipes and empty some of them, so they do not burst in the frost. Pierre helps them. He has learned so much this summer. The chief fontainier says that he has a gift for water.

[pause]

{img:boy-apprentice-fontainier-studying-pipe-plans-old-master-workshop}
At the end of the summer, the chief fontainier offers Pierre a proper apprenticeship. He will learn everything about the fountains, the pipes, the reservoirs and the machines. One day, perhaps, he will be a master fontainier himself. Pierre is so happy that he cannot speak. His mother cries, and his father shakes his hand, man to man. And Jeanne hugs him, and says that she always knew he would be important.

[pause]

Jeanne also has news. La Quintinie, the great gardener, has noticed her. He has seen how carefully she picks the fruit, how gently she carries the baskets, and how much she knows about the plants. He tells her father that she has a good eye and good hands. And he asks if she would like to learn how to graft fruit trees. Jeanne says yes before her father can even answer.

[long-pause]

{img:girl-learning-to-graft-fruit-tree-old-gardener-knife-careful-hands}
So in the autumn, Jeanne learns how to graft. It is a kind of magic. You take a little branch from a good tree, and join it to the trunk of another tree, binding them together with string and wax. If you do it well, the two grow together, and the new tree gives the same fruit as the good tree. La Quintinie shows her how to cut, how to fit the pieces together, and how to bind them. Her first graft fails. Her second one works.

[pause]

In the spring, there will be a little pear tree in the kitchen garden of the king, grafted by a gardener's daughter of thirteen. In a few years, it will give pears. And perhaps, one day, the king himself will eat one of them, without ever knowing who made the tree. Jeanne likes that thought very much.

[pause]

{img:family-walking-home-autumn-evening-town-palace-lights-distance}
On a cool autumn evening, the family walks home together through the town. Behind them, the palace stands on its hill, with a few windows glowing. The air smells of fallen leaves and wood smoke. Marguerite has her arm through her husband's. Pierre is explaining something about pipes, as usual. And Jeanne walks a little behind, looking up at the first stars, thinking about pears, and fountains, and the summer that has passed.

[long-pause]

Years later, Jeanne will marry a young gardener, and live in the town of Versailles all her life. She will become one of the best fruit growers in the king's kitchen garden. And she will tell her children and grandchildren about the summer of 1685. About the figs and the king, the gondola on the canal, the night among the flowers of the Trianon, and the fireworks over the water. And they will listen, and wish they had been there.

[long-pause]

{img:versailles-1700s-palace-completed-chapel-roof-later-gardens}
The summer of 1685 was a golden time at Versailles. But the autumn of that year brought a sad decision. In October, the king took away the rights of the Protestants in France, who had been allowed to practise their faith for almost ninety years. Many of them left France, and went to live in other countries, like the Netherlands, England and Germany. They took their skills and their crafts with them. It was a great loss for France, and a hard time for them.

[long-pause]

The king lived for thirty more years. He built more and more at Versailles. A beautiful new chapel, finished in 1710. The Grand Trianon, of pink marble. New fountains and new groves. But his later years were difficult. There were long, costly wars. There were terrible winters, and famines, when many people in France went hungry. And one by one, he lost his son and his grandson, and his great-grandson, who all passed away before him.

[pause]

Louis the Fourteenth passed away at Versailles on the first of September 1715, at the age of seventy-six. He had been king for seventy-two years, longer than any other king in the history of Europe. On his deathbed, he told his little great-grandson, who would be the next king, not to copy his love of buildings and of war. Try to live in peace with your neighbours, he said.

[pause]

{img:marie-antoinette-era-versailles-1780s-hamlet-pond-cottages-later}
His great-grandson became Louis the Fifteenth. He was only five years old. He lived at Versailles too, and so did his grandson, Louis the Sixteenth, with his queen, Marie Antoinette. They added new rooms, a beautiful opera house, and the little Petit Trianon, with its gardens and its pretend village. But France was changing. Many people were poor and angry. And they began to see Versailles as a symbol of everything that was wrong.

[pause]

In October 1789, at the beginning of the French Revolution, a great crowd of people marched from Paris to Versailles. Most of them were women, angry about the price of bread. The next day, the king and his family were taken to Paris. They never came back to Versailles. The palace was left empty. Much of its furniture was sold. For years, nobody knew what to do with it.

[long-pause]

{img:versailles-museum-1830s-galleries-paintings-visitors-later}
Then, in the eighteen thirties, a later king, Louis-Philippe, decided to save Versailles. He turned it into a museum, dedicated to all the glories of France. He opened it to the public in 1837, with long galleries full of paintings of French history. And so Versailles became what it still is today. A museum, visited by people from all over the world.

[pause]

The Hall of Mirrors continued to play a role in history. In 1871, after a war, the German Empire was proclaimed there, to the great sorrow of the French. And in 1919, at the end of the First World War, the peace treaty was signed in the Hall of Mirrors. It is called the Treaty of Versailles. The same hall where the Doge of Genoa once bowed before the Sun King.

[pause]

{img:restorers-working-hall-of-mirrors-scaffolding-gilding-mirrors-later}
In the twentieth century, Versailles was slowly and carefully restored. Furniture that had been sold was found and bought back. Paintings were cleaned. Gold leaf was renewed. The fountains were repaired. In the early two thousands, the Hall of Mirrors itself was restored, with its paintings cleaned and its mirrors repaired. Today, it shines almost as brightly as it did in 1685.

[long-pause]

The kitchen garden of the king, the Potager du Roi, still exists too. It is now a school for gardeners. Students learn to grow fruit and vegetables there, using the methods of La Quintinie. They still train the pear trees flat against the walls, in espalier. And in the autumn, they still pick the pears. Perhaps some of the old trees there are descendants of trees grafted long ago, by gardeners like Jacques, and his daughter, Jeanne.

[pause]

The silver furniture of the Hall of Mirrors did not survive for long. Just a few years after our story, the king needed money for his wars. So he had all the silver furniture melted down and turned into coins. The silver tables, the silver benches, the silver vases, all gone into the melting pot. The courtiers were shocked. But the king said that the kingdom needed it more than his palace did.

[long-pause]

{img:versailles-today-golden-gates-tourists-courtyard-summer-sunny}
Today, Versailles is one of the most visited places in the world. Millions of people come every year, from every country. They walk through the golden gates, across the great courtyard, and into the palace. They walk through the state rooms, with their painted ceilings and their marble walls. And they walk, slowly, through the Hall of Mirrors, looking up at the ceiling, and at their own reflections, among the chandeliers.

[long-pause]

The king's bedroom can still be visited, with its golden bed and its red and gold curtains. The queen's bedroom too, with its flowered silk. The chapel, the opera house, and many smaller rooms, where the courtiers once lived and gossiped. Guides tell the stories of the Sun King, of his rising and his going to bed, of the scratching on doors and the hats and swords for rent at the gate.

[pause]

The gardens are still there, almost exactly as Le Nôtre designed them. The long avenues, the clipped hedges, the parterres, the statues and the groves. Many of the trees have been replanted, after great storms. In 1999, a terrible storm blew down thousands of trees in the park. But they were replanted, in the same places, following the old plans. In a hundred years, they will be as tall as they were in the time of Louis.

[pause]

{img:musical-fountains-show-versailles-today-crowds-water-jets-music}
And the fountains still play. On summer weekends, there are special fountain shows, with music. All the great fountains of the gardens play together, while the music of Lully and other composers of the time is played through hidden loudspeakers in the groves. Visitors walk from fountain to fountain, just as the courtiers did. The Latona Fountain, with its golden frogs. The Apollo Fountain, with the sun god rising from the water.

[pause]

On summer nights, there are also night fountain shows, with lights and fireworks. The groves are lit up with coloured lights. The fountains sparkle in the darkness. And at the end of the evening, fireworks burst over the Grand Canal, just as they did in 1685, reflected in the long dark water.

[long-pause]

{img:rowing-boats-grand-canal-today-families-summer-afternoon-trees}
On the Grand Canal, visitors can rent small rowing boats in summer. Families row slowly up and down the water, under the summer sky, between the long rows of trees. Children trail their hands in the water. Swans glide past. And at the end of the canal, there is still a little restaurant, near the place where the gondoliers of Little Venice once lived.

[pause]

The Orangery still shelters its orange trees, more than a thousand of them, in their green boxes. In summer, they stand in neat rows around the parterre, as they always have. In winter, they go back into the great vaulted hall. Some of the trees are very old. And when they are in flower, the scent still drifts up to the terrace, as it did when the Sun King walked there.

[pause]

{img:trianon-today-pink-marble-colonnade-gardens-visitors-quiet}
The Grand Trianon, of pink marble, still stands where the Porcelain Trianon once stood. Its gardens are still full of flowers. Nearby, visitors can see the Petit Trianon and the little village of Marie Antoinette, with its thatched cottages, its mill and its pond, where ducks swim among the reeds.

[long-pause]

And in the town of Versailles, below the palace, people still live and work, as they have for more than three hundred years. The old streets are still there, with their houses and their markets. Every morning, the bakers bake their bread, and the gardeners go to work in the great park. And every evening, when the last visitors have left, the palace stands quiet on its hill, in the setting sun, just as it did in the summer of 1685.

[long-pause]

{img:versailles-1685-summer-evening-sunset-over-grand-canal-palace-glowing}
So we leave Versailles tonight, in the summer of 1685, as the evening falls. The sun is setting at the far end of the Grand Canal, exactly where the king wanted it to set. Its last golden light shines all the way down the long water, over the gardens, and onto the windows of the palace, which glow like gold. The whole palace seems to be on fire with light.

[long-pause]

In the gardens, the fountains are silent now. The courtiers have gone inside, for supper and cards and music. The gravel paths are empty. Only the statues remain, standing quietly among the clipped trees, white and still in the fading light. The swallows are swooping over the water. And from somewhere in the groves, a nightingale is beginning to sing.

[pause]

{img:palace-windows-glowing-candles-dusk-terrace-orange-trees-scent}
In the palace, the candles are being lit, one by one, in the chandeliers and the silver candlesticks. Through the tall windows, you can see the glow of the candlelight, and the shapes of people moving inside. The soft sound of violins drifts out through an open window. Somewhere, people are laughing. And on the terrace below, the orange trees are in flower, and their sweet scent hangs in the warm evening air.

[long-pause]
