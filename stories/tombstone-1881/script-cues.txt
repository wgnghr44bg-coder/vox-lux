{img:01-desert-hills-dawn-prospector}

Imagine stepping down from a dusty stagecoach on a bright autumn morning in 1881. Your legs are stiff from the long, bumpy ride. Around you, wooden sidewalks creak under boots, a mule team rattles past, and somewhere far off, a mine engine thumps like a slow, steady heart. This is Tombstone, a silver town on a high desert mesa in Arizona.

[pause]

Most of us know Tombstone from one famous afternoon and thirty seconds of gunfire. But the people who lived there knew a very different town. A town of miners and cooks, newspaper men and schoolteachers, of oysters in the desert and roses in the courtyards. So what was life really like in Tombstone, on all the ordinary days? Stay with me, and we'll find out together.

[pause]

Should these slow evenings in old Arizona help you unwind, you're welcome to subscribe. There's no hurry, and no obligation at all.

[pause]

Somewhere a kettle is cooling, or a night shift is ending, and you have found your way here. If you like, leave a line below with your town and the hour on your clock.

[pause]

So dim the room a little. Sink into the pillow, and breathe out long and slow. Nothing here has to be remembered. The dust and the sunlight of 1881 will do the rest.

[long-pause]

The richest silver town in Arizona was found by one stubborn man with a mule.

[pause]

In 1877, a young prospector named Ed Schieffelin was wandering through the dry hills of southern Arizona. He was tall and thin, with a long beard and clothes that were mostly patches. He had been looking for ore since he was a boy, in Oregon, in Nevada, in Utah, and he had never found very much. But he had a habit that served him well. He picked up rocks everywhere he went, broke them open with his hammer, and looked closely at what was inside. The hills east of the San Pedro River were dangerous country at the time, and few people went there. Schieffelin went anyway. He walked for weeks with his mule, sleeping under the stars and living on beans and coffee. Most people thought he was wasting his time. Yet he kept going, one ridge after another, with the patience of someone who simply enjoyed the looking. It would turn out to be one of the luckiest walks in the history of the American West.

[long-pause]

{img:02-camp-huachuca-soldiers-tents}

Soldiers told him that the only thing he would find out there was his own tombstone.

[pause]

While he was prospecting, Ed Schieffelin often stopped at Camp Huachuca, a small army post in the mountains to the south. It was little more than a group of tents and rough huts beside a stream, with tall oak and pine trees on the slopes above. The soldiers there watched the thin prospector come and go, and they thought he was foolish. The country to the east was wild, and they warned him not to go alone. According to the story Schieffelin told for the rest of his life, one of them said that all he would ever find out there was his tombstone. Schieffelin did not forget the words. Months later, when he finally found what he was looking for, he gave his first claim that very name. It is a small piece of dry frontier humour, and it gave the town its name. A joke made by a bored soldier at a lonely camp is now on maps all over the world.

[long-pause]

{img:03-prospector-campfire-night}

The first piece of rich silver ore was studied by the light of a small campfire.

[pause]

Late in the summer of 1877, Schieffelin found a vein of rock in the hills that looked different from anything he had seen before. He broke off pieces and studied them carefully. Some of the ore was so rich that the silver could be seen, and it was soft enough to press a coin into. He had no money and no tools to mine it himself, so he filed his claim and went looking for help. He found his brother Al, who was working in a mine in another part of Arizona, and an assayer named Richard Gird. Gird tested the samples, and the results were astonishing. One sample was worth thousands of dollars a ton. The three men became partners on the spot. It is said that Gird told Ed that he was a lucky cuss, and that became the name of one of their next mines. For a while, the men kept the news very quiet. They knew that once the word got out, the hills would never be lonely again.

[long-pause]

{img:04-first-claims-tents-hills}

Within a year, the empty hills were covered with tents, piles of stones and men with shovels.

[pause]

The partners could not keep their secret for long. By 1878, news of the silver had spread across the territory and beyond. Prospectors arrived on foot, on horseback and in wagons. They set up canvas tents and little huts made of brush. Each man marked his claim with a pile of stones and a paper notice tucked inside. The claims were given cheerful and hopeful names, like the Tough Nut, the Contention, the Grand Central and the Lucky Cuss. Most of the new arrivals found nothing of value. But a few claims turned out to be very rich, and that was enough to keep everyone else digging. Life in the early camp was simple and hard. Water had to be carried in, food was expensive, and the nights could be cold. Yet in the evenings, the camp was full of small fires and quiet talk about tomorrow's luck. Every great mining town started like this, as a scattering of tents and hope.

[long-pause]

{img:05-mesa-sunset-mountains-distant}

The hills were full of tents and shovels now. All those people needed somewhere to live.

[pause]

The town was built on a high mesa called Goose Flats, almost a mile above the sea.

[pause]

In 1879, the miners needed a proper town, and they chose a wide, flat stretch of high ground near the mines. It was known as Goose Flats, and it stood more than four thousand five hundred feet above sea level. From the edge of the mesa, you could see for many miles in every direction. To the east rose the Dragoon Mountains, and to the south and west, the Huachucas and the Whetstones. In the evenings, the mountains turned pink and then purple as the sun went down. The height of the mesa made the town cooler than the desert below, and the air was dry and clear. Yet there was no river and no spring on Goose Flats. Water would always be a problem. The town was laid out in a neat grid of streets, with numbers going one way and names going the other. Within a few months, the empty grass of the mesa had become a busy little town.

[long-pause]

{img:06-tombstone-street-morning-1881}

In just two years, Tombstone grew from a few tents into one of the largest towns in the Southwest.

[pause]

By 1881, Tombstone had several thousand people, and some estimates say many more. It had grown faster than almost any town in the territory. There were hotels, banks, churches, a school, newspapers, restaurants, stores of every kind and a great many saloons. Stagecoaches arrived and left every day, and long trains of wagons brought in lumber, food and machinery. In the early morning, the main streets were quiet. Shopkeepers swept the boardwalks in front of their doors, and the smell of coffee and frying bacon drifted out of the kitchens. Then the town woke up, and the streets filled with miners, merchants, ranchers and families. People came from all over the world. There were Irish and Cornish miners, Mexican families, Chinese cooks and laundry owners, German brewers and businessmen from New York and San Francisco. Tombstone was rough at the edges, but it was also lively, curious and surprisingly modern for its time.

[long-pause]

{img:07-adobe-and-wooden-buildings}

Many of the houses in Tombstone were built of mud bricks, the way houses had been built in the desert for centuries.

[pause]

Lumber was expensive in southern Arizona, because there were few large trees nearby. It had to be cut in the mountains and hauled in by wagon. So many people in Tombstone built their homes from adobe instead. Adobe bricks are made from mud mixed with straw, shaped in wooden moulds and left to dry in the sun. Mexican builders in the town knew the craft well. The thick adobe walls kept houses cool on hot afternoons and warm on cold nights. Along the main streets, many shops had wooden fronts that rose higher than the roof, to make the buildings look grander than they were. These are called false fronts, and they became a familiar sight in frontier towns. Behind many of them stood simple adobe rooms. Walking along a Tombstone side street, you could see both styles side by side, the old desert way and the new American way of building, standing comfortably together in the sun.

[long-pause]

{img:08-water-wagon-barrels}

In early Tombstone, drinking water was delivered by wagon and sold by the barrel.

[pause]

Goose Flats had no river and no spring. In the first years, every drop of water used in the town had to be brought in from wells and springs some distance away. Water wagons with big wooden barrels rolled slowly through the streets, and the drivers sold water to houses, hotels and restaurants. People were careful with it. Dishwater was saved for the garden. Baths were small and not very frequent. People sometimes joked that water was harder to find than whiskey. In 1881, a company finally began to build a long pipeline from the Huachuca Mountains, more than twenty miles away, to bring fresh water to the town. It was a great improvement, and the townspeople were very proud of it. Still, for many years, the sight of the water wagon remained part of everyday life. Today, we turn on a tap without thinking. In Tombstone, every bucket of clean water was a small luxury.

[long-pause]

{img:09-miners-walking-to-shift-dawn}

Above ground, the town was growing fast. But the real reason for all of it lay deep beneath the streets.

[pause]

A Tombstone miner earned about four dollars a day, which was good money at the time.

[pause]

The silver mines worked day and night, and the miners worked in shifts of about ten hours. At dawn, groups of men walked up the paths from the town to the mines, carrying tin lunch pails and wearing heavy boots and felt hats. Four dollars a day was a high wage for working men in the 1880s, higher than in many factories back east. In 1880, the mine owners tried to lower the pay, and the miners went on strike. It was one of the first miners' strikes in Arizona. Yet the work was hard and dangerous. The tunnels were hot, dusty and dark, and accidents could happen without warning. Many miners were young men who had come to Tombstone alone, hoping to save enough money to buy land or start a business of their own. Some of them managed it. Others spent their wages as quickly as they earned them. Either way, the whole town depended on them.

[long-pause]

{img:10-wells-fargo-office-stagecoach}

The fastest way to reach Tombstone was a bumpy ride by stagecoach from the railway.

[pause]

In 1880, the Southern Pacific Railroad reached the town of Benson, about twenty-five miles to the north. From there, travellers continued by stagecoach. The coaches were tall, heavy wagons pulled by teams of four or six horses. They were painted in bright colours, and they swayed on thick leather straps instead of springs. The ride took several hours, and passengers arrived covered in dust. Wells Fargo had an office in Tombstone, and the stagecoaches carried its strongboxes, full of mail, money and silver bullion. That made the coaches a tempting target for robbers. Guards called shotgun messengers sat next to the driver to protect the box. Most journeys passed without trouble. Passengers chatted, dozed, or looked out at the golden grass and distant mountains. Still, everyone in the coach knew the stories, and nobody was sorry when the first buildings of Tombstone came into view.

[long-pause]

{img:11-hoisting-works-headframe}

Above every big mine stood a tall wooden tower with a great wheel at the top.

[pause]

The towers over the mine shafts were called headframes. A thick steel cable ran over the big wheel at the top and down into the shaft below. At the other end, a steam engine in a nearby building wound the cable in and out. It raised and lowered the cages that carried the miners down to work, and it brought the ore up to the surface in heavy buckets. The engine houses had tall chimneys, and on a still morning, plumes of smoke and steam rose above the hills around the town. The engines ran day and night. People living nearby grew used to the steady sound of the machinery, the hiss of steam and the clang of the signal bells. For the miners, the signal bells were very important. A certain number of rings told the engineer to raise the cage, lower it, or stop. In a deep mine, a man's life could depend on someone listening carefully.

[long-pause]

{img:12-miners-candles-underground}

Deep underground, the miners worked by the light of ordinary candles.

[pause]

In the 1880s, there was no electric light in the mines of Tombstone. Each miner carried candles, and he pushed them into iron holders with a sharp spike, which he could stick into a crack in the rock or into a wooden beam. The small flames gave just enough light to work by. The miners drilled holes into the rock by hand, using long steel drills and heavy hammers. One man held and turned the drill, while another swung the hammer. It took great trust and steady rhythm. When enough holes had been drilled, they were filled with explosives, and the miners moved to a safe distance before the blast. Afterward, the broken ore was shovelled into carts and pushed to the shaft. A candle also served as a simple warning. If the flame grew weak or went out, it could mean the air was bad. So the miners watched their small lights carefully, all through the long shift.

[long-pause]

{img:13-miners-lunch-break-candlelight}

Many miners carried a pastry for lunch that had travelled all the way from Cornwall.

[pause]

Some of the most experienced miners in Tombstone came from Cornwall, in the southwest of England. Cornwall had been mining tin and copper for centuries, and Cornish miners were known everywhere for their skill. People in the American West called them Cousin Jacks. They brought their own food with them, and one of their favourites was the pasty. A pasty is a folded pastry filled with meat, potato and onion. It was easy to carry, it stayed warm for a long time, and it could be eaten with dirty hands by holding the thick crust along the edge. It is said that some wives marked a corner with initials, so that each man could find his own. At midday, the men sat together in a wide part of the tunnel, eating by candlelight and talking quietly. A small taste of home, deep inside an Arizona hill, made the long shift a little easier.

[long-pause]

{img:14-ore-wagons-desert-road}

The silver ore was carried to the mills by long teams of mules, pulling enormous wagons.

[pause]

There was not enough water on Goose Flats to process the ore, so it had to be taken to mills beside the San Pedro River, about ten miles away. Huge freight wagons, sometimes two or three linked together, were pulled by teams of a dozen mules or more. The driver often rode on one of the mules near the back, and he steered the whole long team with a single line and his voice. The mule skinners, as they were called, were famous for their patience and also for their colourful language. The wagons rolled along the dusty road at walking pace, day after day. Mules were preferred over horses for this work, because they were tougher, more sure-footed and less likely to panic. On a hot afternoon, you could see the dust of the ore wagons rising slowly over the grassland, long before you heard the creak of the wheels and the jingle of the harness.

[long-pause]

{img:15-stamp-mill-river-cottonwoods}

Along the San Pedro River, giant machines pounded the silver rock into powder, day and night.

[pause]

The mills that processed Tombstone's ore stood in little towns along the river, with names like Charleston and Contention. Inside each mill were rows of heavy iron hammers called stamps. They rose and fell, one after another, crushing the rock into fine sand. Water from the river carried the crushed ore through tanks and pans, where the silver was separated from the rest. The sound of the stamps was tremendous, a steady pounding that could be heard far across the valley. People who lived near the mills said they grew so used to it that they woke up when it stopped. The river itself was a pleasant place, lined with tall cottonwood trees whose leaves turned gold in autumn. The finished silver was cast into heavy bars called bullion and sent away by stagecoach. It is strange to think of it now, but much of the quiet river valley once shook with noise.

[long-pause]

{img:16-allen-street-afternoon-boardwalk}

When the shift whistle blew and the miners climbed back into the daylight, the town came alive.

[pause]

Allen Street was the heart of Tombstone, and it was lined with covered wooden sidewalks.

[pause]

The main street of the town was called Allen Street. Along both sides ran wooden boardwalks, raised a little above the dust, with roofs on slender posts to shade people from the sun. Under these awnings, you could walk from shop to shop without stepping into the street. There were general stores and hardware stores, a bank, a stationery shop, a jeweller, a tobacconist, barbers, bakeries and restaurants. Shop windows displayed hats, boots, fabrics and tinned food from far away. In the afternoon, the boardwalks were crowded with people strolling and chatting. Wagons and riders passed slowly along the street, and children ran between the posts. When it rained, the street turned to mud, and the boardwalks became even more important. In summer, the dust rose with every passing wagon. Yet for the people of Tombstone, Allen Street was a proud place. It was where everyone met, and where the town showed its best face.

[long-pause]

{img:17-restaurant-white-tablecloths-lamps}

In the middle of the desert, Tombstone's restaurants served oysters, French food and fine wine.

[pause]

Silver made many people in Tombstone rich, at least for a while, and they liked to spend their money well. The town had restaurants that would not have looked out of place in San Francisco. There were tables with white cloths, silver cutlery and oil lamps, and menus with French names. Oysters were shipped on ice by train and then by wagon, and they were a great luxury in a town so far from the sea. Diners could order lobster, quail, fresh fruit and imported wine. Some restaurants employed cooks from France or China. Miners came in their best suits on special occasions, and ladies came in silk dresses and fine hats. Of course, most people ate much more simply, at boarding houses and lunch counters. Yet the fancy restaurants were part of the town's pride. They showed that Tombstone was not just a rough mining camp, but a place where people wanted to live well.

[long-pause]

{img:18-saloon-interior-lamplight-piano}

The grandest saloons in Tombstone had polished bars, tall mirrors and soft carpets.

[pause]

Tombstone had a great many saloons, and the finest of them were remarkably elegant. The Oriental Saloon, on the corner of Allen and Fifth Street, was said to be one of the most luxurious in the West. It had a long bar of polished wood, big mirrors, chandeliers and carpets. In the evenings, a piano played, and sometimes a small orchestra. Men gathered to drink, talk business, read newspapers and play cards. Saloons were also places where deals were made, claims were bought and sold, and news was passed along. Yet they could be noisy and sometimes rough, especially late at night. Many respectable townspeople never set foot inside. For the miners, though, a warm bright saloon after a long shift underground was a welcome sight. The light from the tall windows spilled out across the boardwalk, and the sound of the piano drifted into the street.

[long-pause]

{img:19-schieffelin-hall-evening-crowd}

Tombstone had a large theatre built of adobe, and it is still standing today.

[pause]

In 1881, Al Schieffelin, the brother of the prospector who had found the silver, built a large hall on the corner of Fremont and Fourth Street. Schieffelin Hall was a two-storey building made of adobe, and it was said to be one of the largest adobe buildings in the American Southwest. Inside, there was a stage and seats for several hundred people. Travelling theatre companies performed plays there. There were concerts, lectures, dances and town meetings. On a performance evening, ladies arrived in silk dresses and men in their best coats, and the windows of the hall glowed warmly against the darkening sky. For people living far from any big city, these evenings were very special. They brought a little of the wider world to the high desert. Schieffelin Hall survived fires and floods and the long decline of the town. You can still visit it today.

[long-pause]

{img:20-hitching-rail-horses-dusk}

Every street in town had wooden hitching rails, where horses waited patiently for their riders.

[pause]

In the 1880s, almost everyone who came into Tombstone from the surrounding country arrived on horseback or by wagon. In front of the shops, saloons and offices stood long wooden rails, and riders tied their horses there while they went about their business. The horses waited quietly, swishing their tails at the flies and dozing in the evening light. Some towns had water troughs beside the rails, so that the animals could drink. Livery stables offered a better place to leave a horse for longer, with hay, water and shelter for the night. As dusk fell, the shopkeepers lit their oil lamps, and squares of yellow light appeared on the boardwalks. The horses stood in the soft glow, their breath steaming a little in the cooler air. It was an ordinary sight at the time, so ordinary that hardly anyone wrote about it. But for a while, it was the sound and smell of every evening in town.

[long-pause]

{img:21-nellie-cashman-restaurant-kitchen}

Among the crowds on those busy streets were a few people that everyone in town seemed to know.

[pause]

One of the most respected people in Tombstone was a small Irish woman who ran a restaurant.

[pause]

Nellie Cashman was born in Ireland around 1845, and she came to America as a girl. She had already lived in mining camps in Nevada, British Columbia and other parts of Arizona before she arrived in Tombstone in 1880. There she ran a restaurant and boarding house called the Russ House, on the corner of Fifth and Toughnut Street. She was slim, dark-haired and full of energy, and she worked from early morning until late at night. Her food was known as good and honest, and her prices were fair. But people remembered Nellie most for her kindness. She cared for sick miners, raised money for the poor and helped anyone who was down on their luck. Some people in the town called her the angel of Tombstone. She never married, and she never seemed to slow down. In a town known for rough men and quarrels, her name was spoken with real warmth.

[long-pause]

{img:22-boarding-house-dining-table-miners}

It is said that Nellie Cashman never let a hungry miner leave her table, even if he could not pay.

[pause]

Many miners lived in boarding houses, where they rented a simple room and ate their meals at long shared tables. At the Russ House, the tables were covered with oilcloth, and the meals came in large bowls and platters. There was stew, beef, beans, bread, potatoes and, on good days, pie. A story is told that a diner once complained loudly about Nellie's food. Another customer quietly told him to eat it and be grateful, and the complaint was not repeated. Nellie was known to feed men who had no money, and she would let them pay later, when their luck changed. Sometimes they never paid at all, and she did not seem to mind. It was not a very good way to run a business. Yet it made her one of the most loved people in town. In a hard place far from home, a warm meal and a kind word could mean a great deal.

[long-pause]

{img:23-small-adobe-church-evening}

Nellie Cashman helped raise the money to build Tombstone's first Catholic church.

[pause]

Nellie Cashman was a devout Catholic, and in the early days, Tombstone had no Catholic church. Mass was held wherever there was room. Nellie decided to change that. She went from door to door and from saloon to saloon, asking for donations. It is said that very few people, even the gamblers, could say no to her. The money she gathered helped to build a small adobe church, the Sacred Heart, which opened in the early 1880s. She also raised funds for a hospital, for the families of injured miners and for many other causes. Her gift was simple. She asked directly and kindly, and she always put the money to good use. On Sunday mornings, families in their best clothes walked along the dusty street toward the little church. For a mining town that was often described as wild, it is good to remember that it was also full of people building ordinary, gentle things.

[long-pause]

{img:24-schoolhouse-children-teacher}

Tombstone's children learned their lessons on slates, in a simple wooden schoolhouse.

[pause]

As more families came to Tombstone, the town needed a school. The first public school opened in 1880, and soon there were dozens of children of all ages in a single building. They sat at rows of wooden desks and wrote on small slates with slate pencils, which could be wiped clean and used again. Paper was expensive, so the slates saved money. The children learned reading, writing, arithmetic, geography and history. They recited poems, practised their handwriting, and learned the names of the states and their capitals. Some of them had lived in several mining towns already, and some had travelled thousands of miles by wagon. In winter, an iron stove kept the room warm, and the older boys were often given the job of bringing in wood. Children in Tombstone also helped at home, carried water and looked after younger brothers and sisters. School was a small, calm island in a busy and noisy town.

[long-pause]

{img:25-nellie-evening-porch-lamp}

Nellie Cashman did not stay in Tombstone forever. She followed the gold rushes all the way to the Arctic.

[pause]

In 1881, Nellie's sister died, leaving five young children. Nellie took them in and raised them as her own, while still running her business. When Tombstone's mines began to decline a few years later, she moved on, as she had done so often before. She opened restaurants and stores in other mining towns, and in the 1890s, when gold was found in the Klondike in northern Canada, she went north. She was already over fifty. She crossed the snowy mountains on foot, ran a restaurant in Dawson City, and later prospected for gold herself in Alaska, travelling by dog sled. She was still working in the far north when she was nearly eighty. Nellie Cashman died in 1925 in Victoria, British Columbia. She is remembered today in Arizona, in Canada and in Alaska. Few people in the Old West travelled so far, and fewer still left so many grateful people behind them.

[long-pause]

{img:26-parsons-writing-diary-lamp}

Nellie fed the town. Another newcomer quietly wrote it all down.

[pause]

Much of what we know about daily life in Tombstone comes from one man's private diary.

[pause]

George Whitwell Parsons was a young man from the east who came to Tombstone in 1880. He had worked in a bank, and he hoped to make his fortune in mining. He was not famous, and he never became rich. But every evening, he sat down at a small table, lit his lamp and wrote in his diary. He wrote about the weather, his work, his friends, the church, the prices of things, and the many small events of the day. He wrote when he was cheerful and when he was tired or worried. Over several years, his diary grew into thousands of pages. Long after his death, it was published, and historians found it a treasure. Because of George Parsons, we know what a dusty afternoon in Tombstone felt like, what people ate, and what they talked about. Most diaries are never read by anyone else. His became a quiet window into a vanished town.

[long-pause]

{img:27-parsons-walking-hills-claim}

Like many people in Tombstone, George Parsons spent years hoping one of his claims would be rich.

[pause]

Parsons and his partners bought shares in small mining claims in the hills around the town. They walked out to them over the rocky ground, dug test holes, hauled up buckets of rock with a simple hand winch, and sent samples to be tested. Most of the time, the results were disappointing. In his diary, Parsons often wrote about his hopes for a claim, and then, a few weeks later, about his disappointment. Yet he rarely gave up. He enjoyed the walks in the hills, the clear air and the views across the valleys. His story was very common. For every famous mine in Tombstone, there were dozens of small claims that never paid at all. The people who made money were often not the miners but the storekeepers, the freighters and the bankers. Still, the hope of a lucky strike kept the hills busy, and it gave men like Parsons a reason to keep walking.

[long-pause]

{img:28-church-sunday-congregation}

The oldest Protestant church still standing in Arizona was built in Tombstone, with help from George Parsons.

[pause]

George Parsons was a religious man, and he was one of the people who helped to found an Episcopal church in Tombstone. A young minister named Endicott Peabody arrived from the east in 1882. He was energetic, friendly and good at sports, and the miners liked him. He even boxed with some of them. Peabody helped to raise money for a church building, and Parsons recorded the work in his diary. St. Paul's Church was built of adobe and stone, and it opened in 1882. It still stands today and is still used for services. It is the oldest Protestant church building in Arizona. Endicott Peabody later returned to Massachusetts and founded a famous school called Groton. Many years later, one of his students was a boy named Franklin Roosevelt. It is a small, surprising thread connecting a desert mining town with the history of the twentieth century.

[long-pause]

{img:29-hilltop-view-valley-afternoon}

On quiet afternoons, Parsons liked to climb the hills and look out over the valleys.

[pause]

In his diary, George Parsons often described the view from the hills above the town. To the west lay the wide valley of the San Pedro River, with its line of green cottonwoods. Beyond it rose the Whetstone Mountains, and to the south the tall Huachucas. On clear days, the air was so dry that mountains fifty miles away looked close enough to walk to. Parsons enjoyed these moments of quiet. After the noise of the town and the worries about money, it was restful to sit on a rock and watch the shadows of clouds drift across the grassland. Many people who lived in Tombstone wrote about the beauty of the country around it. The sunsets were remarkable, and the stars at night were brighter than anything they had seen in the cities back east. It is easy to forget, when we think about the Old West, that people also simply sat and looked at the view.

[long-pause]

{img:30-boarding-house-room-night-window}

At night, the small rooms of Tombstone's boarding houses were simple, plain and often shared.

[pause]

Most single men in Tombstone lived in rented rooms. A room might have a narrow bed, a washstand with a jug and basin, a chair, a few hooks for clothes and a small window. Sometimes two or three men shared one room, and some miners even shared a bed, one sleeping during the day and the other at night, as their shifts changed. The walls were thin, and the sounds of the town drifted in after dark. There was the distant rhythm of the mine engines, a dog barking, footsteps on the boardwalk and, from time to time, a piano from a saloon down the street. Yet many men slept deeply after a long day underground. In winter, the rooms could be cold, and people piled on blankets and coats. In summer, they opened the windows and hoped for a breeze. It was a simple life, and for many of the young men, it was only meant to be temporary.

[long-pause]

{img:31-newspaper-office-printing-press}

Parsons kept his diary for himself. But Tombstone also had people who recorded its days for everyone to read and see.

[pause]

Tombstone's most famous newspaper got its name from a simple joke: every tombstone needs an epitaph.

[pause]

In 1880, a man named John Clum arrived in Tombstone with a printing press. He had once been an agent on an Apache reservation, and he was full of energy and opinions. He started a newspaper and called it the Tombstone Epitaph, because, as he said, every tombstone should have an epitaph. The first issue appeared on the first of May, 1880. Producing a newspaper was slow work. Each letter was a small piece of metal, picked by hand from a wooden case and set in a line, one by one. The pages were printed on a heavy iron press, one sheet at a time. The paper reported on mining news, local events, advertisements, arrivals and departures, and quarrels in the town. There was soon a rival paper, the Nugget, and the two often disagreed sharply. The Epitaph is still published today, which makes it one of the oldest newspapers in Arizona.

[long-pause]

{img:32-man-reading-newspaper-boardwalk}

In Tombstone, the morning newspaper was how most people found out what had happened overnight.

[pause]

There was no radio and no telephone in the town in 1881. A telegraph line connected Tombstone with the outside world, and news from far away arrived in short messages that the newspapers printed the next day. In the mornings, boys walked along the boardwalks with bundles of papers, calling out the headlines. Men stood in the sun outside the shops and read the pages slowly. They read about the price of silver in New York, about elections and about events in Europe. They also read about local things, such as new mines, a wedding, a lost horse or a dance at Schieffelin Hall. People argued about what they read, and the newspapers often took sides in the town's disputes. One paper supported one group of officials, and the other supported their rivals. Yet both papers were read eagerly. In a town so far from anywhere, the newspaper was a daily connection to the rest of the world.

[long-pause]

{img:33-fly-photo-studio-camera}

A photographer named C. S. Fly took many of the pictures we still have of early Tombstone.

[pause]

Camillus Sydney Fly came to Tombstone in 1879 with his wife, Mollie. He opened a photograph studio on Fremont Street. The studio had a large window in the roof, called a skylight, because photographers at the time needed lots of natural light. His camera was a big wooden box on a tripod, with a black cloth over the back. Fly photographed townspeople, miners, families and buildings, and he also travelled into the countryside to take pictures. Later, in 1886, he became famous for photographs taken in the Sierra Madre mountains of Mexico, during the last campaign against the Apache leader Geronimo. Those pictures are some of the only photographs ever taken of Apache people still free in the mountains. Many of the images we see of old Tombstone today come from his studio. Without him, the town would be much harder to imagine. His camera quietly recorded a world that was changing very fast.

[long-pause]

{img:34-portrait-sitting-family-studio}

Having your photograph taken in the 1880s meant sitting perfectly still and trying not to blink.

[pause]

By 1881, photography had become faster than in the early days, but it still took a moment or two for the image to form. So people sat very still. Studios often had metal stands hidden behind the chair, to support the back of the head and keep it from moving. That is one reason why people in old photographs often look so serious. Smiling for several seconds was difficult, and many people believed that a portrait should look dignified. Families dressed in their very best clothes for the occasion. Mothers held babies on their laps, and fathers stood behind them with one hand on the chair. The photographs were printed on small cards and sent to relatives far away, back east or across the ocean. For a family who had travelled thousands of miles to a mining town, a photograph was a way of saying, we are here, and we are well. Many of those cards still survive in family albums.

[long-pause]

{img:35-fly-boarding-house-porch-mollie}

Next to the photograph studio, Mollie Fly ran a boarding house that played a part in Tombstone's most famous day.

[pause]

Mollie Fly was a capable and practical woman. While her husband ran the studio, she ran a boarding house in the long, low building on Fremont Street. It had a covered porch along the front and simple rooms for lodgers. She also helped with the photography business, and when her husband was away on long trips, she kept the studio running herself. Doc Holliday rented a room in the Fly boarding house in 1881. Behind the building, and beside it, lay a narrow empty lot, the kind of space found between many buildings in frontier towns. On the afternoon of the twenty-sixth of October, 1881, that small lot would become the scene of the most famous gunfight in the history of the West. It was not, in fact, inside the O.K. Corral. For Mollie, though, most days were simply full of cooking, cleaning and keeping accounts, like the life of almost everyone else in town.

[long-pause]

{img:36-earp-wagons-arriving-1879}

Some of the people in those photographs would one day become far more famous than they ever expected.

[pause]

The Earp brothers came to Tombstone in December 1879, hoping to make money, not to become legends.

[pause]

The Earps were a large family from the Midwest. Several of the brothers had travelled widely, working as farmers, teamsters, gamblers and lawmen. In late 1879, Virgil, Wyatt and James Earp arrived in Tombstone with their wives, in wagons, after a long journey from Dodge City in Kansas. A younger brother, Morgan, joined them later, and so did their friend Doc Holliday. Like almost everyone else, the Earps came to Tombstone because of the silver. They hoped to buy mining claims, run a stagecoach line or invest in a saloon. Wyatt had worked as a lawman in Kansas, but he did not plan to do so again. They settled into small houses near the edge of town. In the first months, they were simply one more family trying to make a living. It is worth remembering that none of them knew what the next two years would bring. To their neighbours, they were ordinary people.

[long-pause]

{img:37-virgil-allie-earp-home}

Virgil Earp became the town marshal of Tombstone, and his wife Allie was known for her sharp wit.

[pause]

Virgil was the oldest of the brothers in Tombstone. He was tall, broad-shouldered and calm, with a big moustache. He had fought in the Civil War as a young man. Shortly before arriving in the town, he had been appointed a deputy United States marshal, and in 1881 he became the town marshal of Tombstone, in charge of keeping the peace within the town limits. Much of the work was ordinary. He collected fees, enforced the town's rules, dealt with drunken quarrels, and chased stray animals off the streets. His wife, Allie, was small, lively and very talkative. Many years later, she told stories about her life in Tombstone, full of humour and strong opinions. Virgil and Allie had a long and affectionate marriage. They stayed together for the rest of his life. In their small house, a pot of coffee was usually on the stove, and friends and family came and went all day.

[long-pause]

{img:38-oriental-saloon-faro-table}

In Tombstone, Wyatt Earp spent many evenings working at a card table called faro.

[pause]

Faro was one of the most popular card games in the American West. It was played against the house, with a dealer drawing cards from a special box. Gamblers placed bets on a board painted with the thirteen cards of one suit. The game was simple to learn and fast to play, and it was found in nearly every saloon. Wyatt Earp had a share in the gambling at the Oriental Saloon, and he often worked at the faro table himself. It was considered a respectable enough job at the time, and many well-known men in the West were gamblers. Wyatt was tall and lean, with a long moustache and a calm, steady manner. He rarely drank. He also worked at times as a shotgun messenger for Wells Fargo and as a deputy sheriff. He was ambitious and hoped to be elected county sheriff one day. At the faro table, though, his job was simply to keep the game honest and steady.

[long-pause]

{img:39-earp-houses-fremont-evening}

The Earp families lived close together, in small houses near the corner of Fremont and First Street.

[pause]

The houses of the Earp families stood at the quieter western end of town, away from the busy saloons of Allen Street. They were small wooden and adobe houses, with porches, little yards and a few chickens. The wives of the brothers were close neighbours and often spent time together, sewing, cooking and talking on the porch in the evening. Allie was married to Virgil, Louisa to Morgan, and Mattie lived with Wyatt. Life for the women was much like life for other families in Tombstone. They hauled water, cooked on wood stoves, washed clothes by hand and made do with whatever was available. They worried about money and about their husbands. In the evenings, the street was usually quiet, with lamplight in the windows and the faint sound of the town in the distance. It was a very ordinary corner of the town, and for a long time nothing remarkable happened there at all.

[long-pause]

{img:40-fremont-first-street-night-quiet}

On most nights, Tombstone's quieter streets were silent except for the wind and the mine engines.

[pause]

Away from Allen Street, the town grew quiet after dark. Families went to bed early, because most people rose with the sun. Oil lamps glowed in kitchen windows while the dishes were washed and the children were put to bed. Then, one by one, the lights went out. The night air on the mesa was cool and clear. Overhead, the stars were very bright, and the Milky Way stretched across the sky. On some nights, coyotes called from the hills. The steady thumping of the steam engines at the mines carried softly across the town, a sound so constant that people stopped noticing it. Now and then, a horse passed slowly along the street, or a late walker's footsteps sounded on the boardwalk. We tend to imagine the Old West as loud and dramatic. Yet for the people who lived in Tombstone, most nights were simply calm, dark and very still.

[long-pause]

{img:41-doc-holliday-card-table}

Doc Holliday was a trained dentist who became one of the most famous gamblers in the West.

[pause]

John Henry Holliday was born in Georgia in 1851, into a well-to-do family. He was well educated and polite, and he spoke in the soft accent of the American South. In 1872, he graduated from a dental college in Philadelphia, and he began to practise as a dentist. But soon afterward, he learned that he had tuberculosis, a serious disease of the lungs that was very common at the time. Doctors told him that a dry climate might help, so he moved west. Patients did not like a dentist who coughed, and Holliday found that he could earn more money at the card table. He became a professional gambler, travelling from town to town. He was slim and pale, and he dressed neatly in grey suits. He was also known for his quick temper and his loyalty to his friends. His closest friend was Wyatt Earp, whom he had met in Texas years before.

[long-pause]

{img:42-dentist-office-1870s-chair}

In the 1870s, a visit to the dentist was a very different experience from today.

[pause]

When Doc Holliday trained as a dentist, the profession was still quite new in America. Dental offices had a padded wooden chair, a cabinet of brass and steel instruments and a window for light, because good lamps were rare. Drills were turned by hand or by a foot pedal. The most common treatment was pulling a painful tooth. Fillings were made of gold, which was strong and lasted well, or of other metals. Some dentists used ether or a gas called nitrous oxide, sometimes known as laughing gas, to dull the pain, but many patients simply held on tight. Holliday practised dentistry for a few years, first in Atlanta and then in Dallas, and he seems to have been a careful dentist. Yet his illness made the work difficult. As his cough worsened, he left the profession behind. People still called him Doc for the rest of his life, a reminder of the gentle career he had once planned.

[long-pause]

{img:43-doc-kate-hotel-balcony}

Doc Holliday's companion for many years was a strong-willed woman from Hungary named Kate.

[pause]

Mary Katharine Horony was born in Hungary in 1850, and she came to America with her family as a child. Her father had been a doctor. She was well educated and spoke several languages. As a young woman, she made her own way in the frontier towns, and she became known as Kate Elder. She met Doc Holliday in Texas, and the two of them were together on and off for many years. Their relationship was stormy. They quarrelled loudly, separated and then came back together again. Kate was independent and outspoken, and she did not like Doc's friend Wyatt Earp very much. In 1881, she spent some time with Doc in Tombstone. Many years later, as an old woman, she wrote down her memories of those days. Like many old memories, they are not always reliable. Yet they give us a glimpse of the private life of a man who is otherwise remembered only as a legend.

[long-pause]

{img:44-hotel-parlour-piano-evening}

It is said that Doc Holliday played the piano, a reminder of his genteel upbringing in Georgia.

[pause]

Holliday grew up in a comfortable home in the South, where children of good families learned music, manners and classical languages. According to family stories, he was a capable pianist as a young man. In the hotels and parlours of the frontier, upright pianos were common, and it is easy to imagine him sitting down at one in the evening and playing something quiet. Whether he did so often, we do not really know. Much of what is said about Doc Holliday has grown larger in the retelling, and historians are careful about the details. What does seem clear is that he was a complicated man. He could be charming, witty and generous, and he could also be cold and quick to anger. He was clever, well read and very ill. His life was shaped by his disease, which he knew would shorten his life. Perhaps that is why he seemed so careless of danger.

[long-pause]

{img:45-boarding-room-stove-night}

Tuberculosis was one of the most common causes of death in the nineteenth century.

[pause]

Doc Holliday's illness was not unusual. In the 1800s, tuberculosis, often called consumption, killed huge numbers of people in America and Europe. It spread through the air, in crowded homes and towns. There was no cure. Doctors advised rest, fresh air and dry, sunny places, which is one reason why many people with weak lungs travelled west to the deserts and mountains. Some of them recovered, at least for a while. Others grew slowly weaker. On cold nights, a person with tuberculosis might sit up wrapped in a blanket, coughing, beside a small iron stove, unable to sleep. Holliday lived with the disease for about fifteen years. It made him thin and pale and tired, and it gave his life a sense of borrowed time. Only in the twentieth century did scientists develop medicines that could cure the disease. Today, in many countries, it is rare. In Doc Holliday's time, it was simply part of life.

[long-pause]

{img:46-san-pedro-valley-ranch-cattle}

Beyond the edge of town, the land opened out into wide, quiet valleys. And not everyone who lived there saw things the way the town did.

[pause]

Around Tombstone lay wide grassy valleys, where small ranches raised cattle for the mining towns.

[pause]

The mining towns needed food, and the valleys around Tombstone were good cattle country. The San Pedro Valley to the west and the Sulphur Springs Valley to the east had tall grass, scattered mesquite trees and a few precious streams and springs. Ranchers built small houses of adobe, with brush-covered porches for shade, and wooden corrals for their horses. Their cattle grazed across miles of open range, because there were few fences. Twice a year, the ranchers rounded up the cattle, branded the young calves and chose animals to sell. Beef was in great demand in Tombstone, and the army posts and the mines bought large amounts of it. Life on a ranch was lonely and hard, with long days in the saddle and little company. Yet many ranchers loved the open country, the wide sky and the silence. In the evenings, a lamp in a ranch house window could be seen for miles across the dark grassland.

[long-pause]

{img:47-cowboy-campfire-coffee-night}

In Tombstone in 1881, the word cowboy did not always mean someone who simply worked with cattle.

[pause]

Most of the young men working on the ranches around Tombstone were ordinary cowhands. They herded cattle, mended fences, broke horses and slept under the stars on long drives. Around their campfires at night, they drank strong coffee, ate beans and bacon, and told stories. But in southern Arizona at that time, the word cowboys was also used for a loose group of men who stole cattle, especially across the border in Mexico, and sold them on the American side. Some of them also robbed stagecoaches. They were not an organised gang, but a network of friends, relatives and acquaintances. Many ranchers in the valleys knew them, bought cattle from them or were related to them. This made the situation complicated. To some people in Tombstone, the cowboys were dangerous outlaws. To others, they were simply neighbours. The tension between the cowboys and the town lawmen grew steadily during 1881.

[long-pause]

{img:48-mclaury-brothers-ranch-porch}

Tom and Frank McLaury were ranchers from Iowa, who raised cattle in the valleys near Tombstone.

[pause]

The McLaury brothers came from a respectable family in Iowa. Their older brother was a lawyer. Tom and Frank moved to Arizona in the late 1870s and started a ranch, first in the Babocomari area west of Tombstone, and later in the Sulphur Springs Valley to the east. Frank was known as the bolder and more outspoken of the two, while Tom was quieter. The McLaurys were friendly with the Clanton family, who also had a ranch, and with some of the men known as cowboys. They were suspected of buying stolen cattle and horses, but they were never convicted of any crime. In the valleys, they were respected ranchers with many friends. In town, their friendships made them distrusted by the Earps. During 1881, there were several quarrels between them. It is a reminder that the conflict in Tombstone was not simply between good men and bad men. It was between neighbours who did not trust each other.

[long-pause]

{img:49-cowboys-riding-into-town-dusk}

In Tombstone, it was against the law to carry a gun inside the town.

[pause]

Many people are surprised to learn this, but Tombstone had strict rules about weapons. A town ordinance said that visitors arriving in town had to leave their guns at a hotel, a livery stable or another business, and collect them again when they left. Similar rules existed in many frontier towns, such as Dodge City in Kansas. The idea was simple. Cowboys coming in from the ranches after weeks on the range might drink too much, and arguments with loaded guns could quickly end in tragedy. So the town marshal and his deputies were expected to disarm anyone who broke the rule. Most riders accepted it without fuss. They rode in at dusk, tied their horses, handed over their pistols and went to find a meal and a drink. Yet the rule also caused resentment, especially among men who already disliked the lawmen. In October 1881, this simple town ordinance would play an important part in what happened.

[long-pause]

{img:50-sheriff-behan-office}

Tombstone had two different lawmen in charge, and they did not get along.

[pause]

In 1881, the town of Tombstone had its own police, led by the town marshal, Virgil Earp. But Tombstone was also the main town of the new Cochise County, created in February of that year. The county had its own sheriff, a friendly and talkative politician named John Behan. Behan was popular with many people, including the ranchers in the valleys and some of the cowboys. Wyatt Earp had hoped to become county sheriff himself, and he believed that Behan had broken a promise to him. The two men also competed for the affection of the same woman, a young actress named Josephine Marcus, who later became Wyatt's wife. So there were political and personal rivalries between the town lawmen and the county lawmen. The two newspapers took different sides. This division made it harder to keep the peace. In a town full of quarrels, even the people meant to settle them were part of the problem.

[long-pause]

{img:51-valley-sunset-windmill-quiet}

Far from the quarrels in town, the evenings in the valleys were wide, golden and very quiet.

[pause]

On a ranch in the San Pedro or Sulphur Springs Valley, the day ended slowly. As the sun went down, the light turned the grass to gold and the distant mountains to deep blue and purple. Cattle drifted toward the water holes in long, unhurried lines. Doves called from the mesquite trees. A rancher might stand by the corral for a few minutes, watching the colours fade, before going in for supper. The air cooled quickly after sunset, and soon the first stars appeared. In the dark, coyotes began to sing, and an owl hooted from a cottonwood by the stream. For many people in the region, this was the real Arizona. It was not the saloons, the mines or the arguments in town, but this quiet country of grass, sky and silence. It had been here long before the silver was found, and it would still be here long after the mines closed.

[long-pause]

{img:52-october-morning-town-cool}

By October 1881, the summer heat had gone, and the mornings in Tombstone were crisp and cool.

[pause]

Autumn on the high mesa brought clear blue skies, cool nights and golden grass on the hills. In the mornings, smoke from hundreds of chimneys hung low over the roofs, because people had started lighting their stoves again. In the kitchens, women were preserving fruit for the winter, peaches and plums brought in by wagon from the river farms. The stores had put out warm coats, wool blankets and heavy boots. At the school, the children were practising songs for a concert before Christmas. On the surface, it was a pleasant season in a thriving town. The mines were producing well, new buildings were going up and the population was still growing. Yet underneath, there was a feeling of unease. All through the year, there had been robberies, missing cattle and bitter arguments. People talked in low voices on the boardwalks. Many sensed that the quarrels were not going to fade away on their own.

[long-pause]

{img:53-posse-riders-desert-road}

In 1881, stagecoach robberies near Tombstone turned old suspicions into open anger.

[pause]

In March 1881, masked men tried to rob a stagecoach on the road near Contention, and the driver and a passenger were killed. The Earps and Sheriff Behan rode out together in a posse to find the robbers. They caught one man, but he soon escaped, and the others were never brought to justice. Rumours spread in every direction about who had been involved and who had helped whom. In September, another stagecoach was robbed, on the road to the mining town of Bisbee. This time, the Earps and their deputies tracked the robbers and arrested two men, both friends of the cowboys. The cowboys were furious, and in the saloons some of them made angry threats. Meanwhile, the newspapers argued, and the sheriff and the marshal grew further apart. Each new event added to the mistrust. By the middle of October, the town was full of quarrels, and many of them were about these robberies on the dusty desert roads.

[long-pause]

{img:54-virgil-marshal-walking-boardwalk}

Most of a town marshal's work in Tombstone was ordinary, patient and rather dull.

[pause]

When people think of a frontier marshal, they usually picture gunfights. In reality, Virgil Earp's job as town marshal was mostly routine. He walked the streets, checked that the shops were secure at night, and dealt with drunken men and noisy quarrels. He enforced town rules about animals, rubbish, fires and weapons. He collected license fees and helped with lost property. He made arrests for small offences and brought people before the local judge. He had a few deputies, and at times he asked his brothers to help him. Much of the work required patience and a calm manner, and Virgil was known for both. Yet he also knew that the situation in Tombstone was growing more dangerous. Threats had been made against him and his brothers. In the autumn of 1881, he walked the boardwalks as usual, but with a careful eye on the street and on the men who rode in from the valleys.

[long-pause]

{img:55-tombstone-street-dusk-lamps-october}

On autumn evenings, the lamps of Allen Street came on earlier and earlier.

[pause]

As October went on, the days grew shorter, and the evenings in Tombstone grew colder. Shopkeepers lit their lamps in the late afternoon, and soon the whole street glowed. People walked home wrapped in coats and shawls, carrying parcels and baskets. A horse and buggy rolled slowly along the dusty street. In the restaurants, diners lingered over hot soup and coffee. In the saloons, the stoves were lit, and men gathered around them, talking about mining shares, the weather and the latest news. Children were called in from playing. Smoke rose from the chimneys and drifted across the darkening sky. It was the season when frontier towns seemed at their most cosy. The warm windows, the smell of wood smoke and the cool clear air made the town feel almost peaceful. Most of its people went about their evening routines without any idea of how soon their town would become famous.

[long-pause]

{img:56-full-moon-over-dragoon-mountains}

East of Tombstone rise the Dragoon Mountains, once the stronghold of the Apache leader Cochise.

[pause]

The rocky Dragoon Mountains can be seen from almost everywhere in Tombstone. Their granite cliffs and boulders glow orange at sunset and silver under the moon. For many years, these mountains were the homeland of the Chiricahua Apache, and their great leader, Cochise, used a hidden valley in the Dragoons as his stronghold. It was almost impossible for outsiders to find. In 1872, Cochise made peace with the United States, and he died two years later. He was buried somewhere in the mountains, in a secret place that has never been revealed. The county in which Tombstone stands was named after him. By 1881, the Apache people had been forced onto reservations, though some groups still left them and returned to the mountains. On a quiet night, as the full moon rose over the Dragoons, the people of Tombstone could look out at a landscape with a much longer history than their own young town.

[long-pause]

{img:57-wagon-arriving-town-evening-wind}

Through the long summer, old quarrels simmered quietly. Then, in late October, they came to the surface.

[pause]

On the twenty-fifth of October, 1881, two ranchers drove a wagon into Tombstone on ordinary business.

[pause]

Ike Clanton and Tom McLaury came into town that day by wagon. They had cattle business to attend to, and they planned to buy supplies and spend the night. Ike was the son of a rancher, known for his loud voice and his habit of drinking too much and talking too freely. He was friendly with the cowboys, and he had quarrelled with the Earps about a secret arrangement that had gone wrong. He feared that people would find out about it. Tom McLaury was quieter. The afternoon was grey and windy, and dust blew along the streets. The two men left their wagon and horses at a livery stable, as visitors usually did. Neither of them came to Tombstone that day intending to fight. Yet Ike, in particular, was nervous and angry, and he began drinking as the evening went on. Small events, piling up one after another, were about to lead somewhere nobody had planned.

[long-pause]

{img:58-alhambra-lunch-counter-night}

The quarrel that led to the famous gunfight began late at night, at a lunch counter.

[pause]

Late on the night of October twenty-fifth, Ike Clanton was eating at the lunch counter of the Alhambra Saloon on Allen Street. It was a small, warm room with a long wooden counter and a cook frying eggs and steak on an iron stove. Doc Holliday came in. Doc had heard that Ike had been making threats against him and the Earps, and he confronted Ike angrily. The two men exchanged harsh words. Morgan Earp, who was nearby, and then Virgil, stepped in and moved them apart. Virgil, as town marshal, told them both to stop. Ike left, but he was furious, and he kept talking about what he would do the next day. It was a small, ugly argument of the kind that happened in saloons all over the West. On most nights, it would have been forgotten by morning. This time, it was not. The quarrel at the Alhambra became the first link in a short chain of events.

[long-pause]

{img:59-poker-game-late-night-lamp}

That same night, Ike Clanton sat down to play poker with the very men he was quarrelling with.

[pause]

Strange as it seems, after the argument at the Alhambra, Ike Clanton joined a poker game that went on through the night. At the table were Tom McLaury, Sheriff John Behan, Town Marshal Virgil Earp and a few other men. They played under a hanging oil lamp in a nearly empty saloon, while the town slept. In the frontier West, such things were not unusual. Men who disliked each other might still sit at the same card table. The game lasted until early morning. When it ended, Virgil went home to sleep. Ike, who had had no sleep at all and had been drinking for many hours, wandered out into the cold grey dawn. He was tired, angry and still talking about trouble. It was the morning of Wednesday, the twenty-sixth of October, 1881. Later, many people would wonder what might have happened if the game had ended earlier, or if Ike had simply gone to bed.

[long-pause]

{img:60-grey-dawn-allen-street-empty}

The twenty-sixth of October, 1881, began as a cold, grey and windy day.

[pause]

People who lived in Tombstone remembered that the weather that day was unusually cold for October. Heavy clouds hung over the mountains, and a sharp wind blew dust along the streets. Some remembered a few flakes of snow in the air. In the early morning, Allen Street was almost empty. The boardwalks were deserted, the shop doors were still closed and only a few lamps burned in the windows of restaurants getting ready for breakfast. Shopkeepers arrived one by one, unlocked their doors and lit their stoves. Miners walked up to the mines for the morning shift, with their collars turned up against the wind. For most people in Tombstone, it was just another working Wednesday. They had coffee, ate breakfast, opened their stores and got on with their day. Only a handful of people knew that Ike Clanton was walking around town, tired and angry, telling anyone who would listen that there was going to be trouble.

[long-pause]

{img:61-virgil-sleeping-morning-room}

While trouble was brewing in the street, the town marshal was asleep after a long night.

[pause]

Virgil Earp had played cards until dawn, and in the morning he went home to bed. His wife, Allie, let him sleep. During the morning, people came to the house to warn the Earps that Ike Clanton was walking around with a rifle and a pistol, saying that he was looking for them. At first, nobody wanted to wake Virgil. Eventually, someone did. Virgil got up, dressed and went out into the cold streets. As town marshal, he could not ignore a man carrying weapons in town and making threats. It was against the town's rules, and it was dangerous. He found his brothers and told them what he had heard. In many accounts of that day, this moment feels very ordinary. A tired man is woken up because there is a problem at work, puts on his coat and goes to deal with it. Nobody in the little house that morning could have imagined how the day would end.

[long-pause]

{img:62-fourth-street-cold-midday}

Around midday, Virgil and Morgan Earp found Ike Clanton on Fourth Street and took his rifle away.

[pause]

Shortly after noon, Virgil and his brother Morgan came across Ike Clanton near the corner of Fourth Street. Ike was carrying a rifle and a revolver, which was against the town ordinance. Virgil came up behind him and grabbed the rifle. When Ike reached for his revolver, Virgil struck him on the head with his own pistol. Ike fell, and the marshal took his weapons. It was a rough arrest, but it was quick, and nobody was seriously hurt. Ike was taken to the small courtroom of the local justice of the peace, still dazed and angry, with a cut on his head. A small crowd gathered to watch. The weapons were left at a nearby hotel, as was usual. For a short time, it may have seemed that the problem had been dealt with. Ike had been disarmed and would pay a fine. Yet the arrest made him even angrier, and it made his friends angrier too.

[long-pause]

{img:63-small-courtroom-justice-desk}

In a small courtroom, Ike Clanton was fined twenty-five dollars for carrying weapons in town.

[pause]

Tombstone's local court was a plain room with a wooden floor, a few benches and a desk for the judge, a justice of the peace named Albert Wallace. When Ike was brought in, the judge was not there yet, and the men waited. Ike sat on a bench, holding his head and complaining loudly. Wyatt and Morgan Earp were there too, and harsh words were exchanged. Witnesses later said that Ike threatened the Earps, and that Wyatt answered him angrily. When the judge arrived, the case was simple. Ike had broken the town ordinance by carrying weapons, and he was fined twenty-five dollars and court costs. He paid the fine. His rifle and pistol were held at a hotel, to be returned when he left town. It was a small, ordinary case, the kind a frontier court handled every week. Yet by now, emotions were running high on both sides, and they were spreading through the town.

[long-pause]

{img:64-wyatt-leaving-courtroom-street}

Just after leaving the courtroom, Wyatt Earp met Tom McLaury on the street, and they quarrelled.

[pause]

As Wyatt walked out of the courtroom, he came face to face with Tom McLaury on the street outside. The two men exchanged words. Accounts differ about exactly what was said. What is clear is that Wyatt struck Tom with his pistol, and Tom fell to the ground. Then Wyatt walked away. Tom got up and went off, bruised and humiliated. Later, it was said that Tom had not been carrying a gun at that moment, and that he had left his pistol at a shop. This short encounter added one more injury, and one more grudge, to an already tense day. By early afternoon, both of the young ranchers who had driven into town the day before had been hit on the head by an Earp. Their friends were furious. The Earps, for their part, believed they were facing armed men who had threatened to kill them. Each side felt it was in the right, and each side felt it was in danger.

[long-pause]

{img:65-two-riders-arriving-grand-hotel}

In the early afternoon, two more young men rode into town to join their brothers.

[pause]

Frank McLaury and Billy Clanton arrived in Tombstone on horseback around the middle of the day. Frank was Tom's brother, and Billy was Ike's younger brother, only nineteen years old. They stopped at the Grand Hotel on Allen Street, one of the finest hotels in town, and there they heard what had happened to Ike and Tom. They were angry and worried. Visitors arriving in town were supposed to leave their guns at a hotel or a stable, but Frank and Billy were still armed. They led their horses through the streets toward the livery stables, where they met Ike and Tom. Later, it was said that they were planning to leave town. Others believed they were preparing for a fight. We will never know for sure what was in their minds. What is certain is that four young men, two of them hurt and two of them armed, were now standing together near the corrals.

[long-pause]

{img:66-ok-corral-stable-yard}

The O.K. Corral was a livery stable, a place where horses were fed, stabled and rented.

[pause]

Every frontier town had several livery stables. They were large yards with wooden stalls and high fences, where travellers could leave their horses, and where horses and buggies could be hired. The O.K. Corral was one of these, with an entrance on Allen Street and a back entrance near Fremont Street. Inside, horses stood quietly in their stalls, eating hay. Stable hands mucked out the stalls, carried water and harnessed buggies. It was an ordinary, working place, smelling of hay, leather and horses. On the afternoon of October twenty-sixth, the Clantons and McLaurys passed through it, and they were seen talking together near the back. Witnesses heard them talking angrily about the Earps. Today, the O.K. Corral is one of the most famous names in American history. Yet the gunfight did not really happen inside it. It happened a short distance away. The name stuck simply because the corral was the best-known landmark nearby.

[long-pause]

{img:67-earps-gathering-hafford-corner}

At about half past two, the Earp brothers and Doc Holliday gathered on a street corner and talked about what to do.

[pause]

The Earps met near Hafford's saloon, on the corner of Allen and Fourth Street. Virgil, Wyatt and Morgan were there, and Doc Holliday joined them, in a long grey overcoat, carrying a walking stick. They received several reports that the Clantons and McLaurys were armed and gathered near the corral. Virgil, as marshal, decided that he had to disarm them. He asked Sheriff Behan to help, but Behan said he would go first and try to persuade the men to give up their guns himself. Virgil handed Doc a short shotgun, and he took Doc's walking stick in exchange, perhaps so that the shotgun could be hidden under Doc's coat. Witnesses said the men seemed serious and quiet. A cold wind was blowing, and people on the boardwalks were watching them. It is easy, looking back, to see where this was leading. At the time, though, nobody standing on that corner knew how it would end.

[long-pause]

{img:68-four-men-walking-fremont-street}

Shortly before three o'clock, four men walked down Fremont Street, and the town watched them go.

[pause]

Virgil, Wyatt and Morgan Earp, with Doc Holliday beside them, set off along Fremont Street toward the place where the cowboys were standing. They walked side by side down the middle of the wide, dusty street. It was a short walk of only a few blocks. Sheriff Behan met them on the way and told them that he had disarmed the men, or that he was in the process of doing so. Accounts differ about exactly what he said. The Earps kept walking. Townspeople stood in doorways and on the boardwalks, watching. Some went inside and closed their doors. The wind blew dust across the street, and the sky was grey and low. This walk has been described in books, paintings and films many times since, often as a slow and solemn march. In reality, it took only a couple of minutes. Many of the people watching thought it would end with an arrest and nothing more.

[long-pause]

{img:69-distant-view-street-smoke-haze}

What happened next has been told and retold for more than a hundred years. In real life, it was over very quickly.

[pause]

The famous gunfight lasted only about thirty seconds.

[pause]

The Earps and Doc Holliday found the Clantons, the McLaurys and another young man, Billy Claiborne, in the narrow lot beside Fly's boarding house. The two groups stood only a few steps apart. Virgil called on them to throw up their hands, and he said he wanted their guns. Then, in a moment of confusion that witnesses never agreed on, shooting began. About thirty shots were fired in around thirty seconds. Ike Clanton and Billy Claiborne ran away. When it ended, Tom and Frank McLaury and Billy Clanton were dying. Virgil and Morgan Earp were wounded, and Doc Holliday was grazed. Wyatt was unhurt. A thin haze of gun smoke drifted along the street in the wind. It is hard to imagine how quickly it was all over. A quarrel that had grown for months, and through a whole night and morning, ended in less time than it takes to read these few sentences.

[long-pause]

{img:70-quiet-lot-picket-fence-afternoon}

The fight did not take place inside the O.K. Corral, but in a narrow empty lot on Fremont Street.

[pause]

The lot was a small gap between two buildings, Fly's boarding house and a house belonging to a man named Harwood. It was narrow, dusty and a little overgrown with weeds, with a fence at the back. Such empty spaces were common in frontier towns, where buildings went up quickly and not every plot was filled. After the shooting, a frightened horse stood alone in the lot, its reins hanging loose. The townspeople who came running found a scene of confusion and grief. In later years, as the story became famous, it was always called the Gunfight at the O.K. Corral. Newspapers and books used the name because the corral was a well-known place nearby, and the name sounded good. The actual spot was half a block away. Today, visitors to Tombstone can walk into that narrow lot and stand where the men stood. It is a very small, very ordinary space for such a famous event.

[long-pause]

{img:71-townspeople-gathering-street}

Within minutes, the street was full of townspeople, and the mine whistles blew in alarm.

[pause]

As soon as the shooting stopped, people came out of their houses and shops. Some ran toward the lot, and others stood in small groups, talking in shocked voices. The whistles at the mines sounded, as they did in an emergency, and miners came down from the hills, some of them armed, fearing more trouble. A group of citizens who had formed a kind of volunteer town guard gathered in the street. Doctors hurried to help the wounded. The three dying men were carried into a nearby house, where they died soon afterward. The wounded Earp brothers were helped away. Sheriff Behan told Wyatt that he was under arrest, and Wyatt refused, saying that he would answer for what he had done in a proper court. The wind was still blowing, and the afternoon was growing colder. Shop doors were closed early that day. Many families stayed indoors, uncertain of what might happen next.

[long-pause]

{img:72-carriage-carrying-wounded-home}

That evening, Virgil Earp was carried home in a carriage, wounded in the leg.

[pause]

Virgil had been shot through the calf, and Morgan had been hit in the shoulder. Neither wound was fatal, but both were painful and serious. The brothers were taken home in a carriage, through the darkening streets, past small groups of townspeople who watched them go in silence. At the house, their wives were waiting. Allie Earp later remembered that she had heard the shots from home and run out into the street without her hat, terrified, until she learned that Virgil was alive. A doctor came to treat the wounds by lamplight. The house was full of people coming and going, friends bringing news and neighbours bringing food. Outside, the wind was still blowing. Some friends of the Earps stood guard near the house through the night, in case of further trouble. For the families of the Earps and the families of the dead men alike, it was the longest night of their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:73-house-window-lamp-evening-wind}

For the families on both sides, the night after the gunfight was long and sorrowful.

[pause]

In a small house near the edge of town, a lamp burned late in the window, while a wife sat beside a wounded husband. Elsewhere in Tombstone, the bodies of Tom McLaury, Frank McLaury and Billy Clanton lay in the undertaker's rooms. Their friends and relatives gathered in sorrow and anger. Billy Clanton had been only nineteen. The McLaury brothers had family far away in the east, who would receive the news by telegraph. In the boarding houses, saloons and kitchens of the town, people talked late into the night. Some believed the lawmen had done their duty. Others believed they had gone looking for a fight. The two newspapers would soon print very different versions of what happened. Outside, the cold wind moved through the empty streets. It is worth remembering, in all the books and films that followed, that these were real families and real losses, in a small town on a cold October night.

[long-pause]

{img:74-morning-after-quiet-town}

The next morning, Tombstone woke up to sunshine, and to a story that would never quite go away.

[pause]

On the twenty-seventh of October, the wind had dropped and the sky was clear. A pale sun rose over the mountains, and the town was very still. People stood in small groups on the boardwalks, talking quietly. The newspapers printed their first accounts of the gunfight. The Epitaph largely supported the Earps, and the Nugget was more sympathetic to the cowboys. In the shops and kitchens, everyone had an opinion. Some people were relieved, feeling that the lawmen had dealt with dangerous men. Others were shocked and angry, feeling that three men had been killed who might have been arrested peacefully. The mines went back to work, and the stores opened as usual. Life in a busy town does not stop for long. Yet something had changed. Tombstone was now known far beyond Arizona, and the events of that cold afternoon would be argued about for the rest of the century and long after.

[long-pause]

{img:75-funeral-procession-brass-band}

The funeral of the three young men was one of the largest Tombstone had ever seen.

[pause]

The funeral took place on the twenty-eighth of October. The bodies of Tom and Frank McLaury and Billy Clanton had been placed in a shop window on Allen Street, under a sign that described them as murdered. It was a sign of how strongly some people in town felt. The procession was long and slow. A brass band led the way, playing solemn music, followed by black horse-drawn hearses and hundreds of people on foot, on horseback and in carriages. Some accounts say that two thousand people took part or watched. The procession made its way to the cemetery on the hill outside town, where the three young men were buried side by side. Many townspeople who joined the procession had not known the men personally. They came out of respect, or sympathy, or a feeling that something tragic had happened to their town. It was a quiet, sad day, and the dust of the procession hung long in the still air.

[long-pause]

{img:76-winter-tombstone-frost-roofs}

After a month-long hearing, a judge decided that the Earps and Doc Holliday would not be tried for murder.

[pause]

Soon after the gunfight, Ike Clanton accused the Earps and Doc Holliday of murder. A judge named Wells Spicer held a long hearing to decide whether there was enough evidence for a trial. It lasted through most of November 1881. Many witnesses were called, and their stories often contradicted each other. In the end, Spicer decided that the lawmen had acted within their duties, though perhaps unwisely, and he did not send the case to trial. Many people were relieved, and many others were angry. Then winter arrived on the mesa. There was frost on the roofs and snow on the distant mountains. Yet the conflict did not end with the hearing. In the following months, there were further attacks, and both Virgil and Morgan Earp were shot. The violence that followed is a darker chapter of the story. For the townspeople, though, that winter brought mostly cold mornings, smoky chimneys and the hope that things would settle down.

[long-pause]

{img:77-train-station-family-leaving}

In 1882, the Earp families left Tombstone and never lived there again.

[pause]

By the spring of 1882, the Earp brothers had suffered greatly. Virgil had been badly wounded in an ambush in December, and his left arm was crippled for the rest of his life. Morgan had been killed in March. The family decided to leave. Virgil and Allie, with other members of the family, travelled to the railway and took a train to California, where the Earp parents lived. They carried Morgan's body home with them to be buried. Wyatt and Doc Holliday left Arizona a few weeks later. In just over two years, the Earps had come to Tombstone hoping for silver and prosperity, and they left with grief and injuries. The town they left behind continued without them. New people arrived, the mines kept working and the stores stayed open. For a family on a small railway platform in the desert, with their trunks and bags around them, it was simply time to go somewhere quieter.

[long-pause]

{img:78-spring-desert-flowers-town-distance}

In good years, the hills around Tombstone burst into colour with spring wildflowers.

[pause]

After a wet winter, the desert around Tombstone could change completely in spring. The brown hills turned green, and then they filled with flowers. There were golden poppies, orange globemallow, purple lupines and many tiny white and yellow blossoms whose names few people knew. The ocotillo plants, which look like bundles of tall grey sticks for most of the year, grew small green leaves and bright red flowers at their tips. Bees and hummingbirds came to visit them. People in town went out on Sunday afternoons to walk among the flowers or to have picnics on the slopes. Children brought back armfuls of blossoms. These springs did not happen every year, because they depended on the rain. That made them even more precious. For people living in a dusty mining town, a hillside covered in flowers was a reminder of how gentle and beautiful the desert could be, after all the hard times.

[long-pause]

{img:79-mine-pumps-steam-engine-house}

Slowly, life in Tombstone went back to its ordinary rhythm. But the town itself was about to change.

[pause]

Tombstone's mines were eventually defeated not by outlaws, but by water.

[pause]

In 1881, as the miners dug deeper, they struck water about five hundred feet below the surface. At first, it seemed like good news for a dry town. But the water flooded the lower tunnels, and the richest silver lay below it. The mining companies built huge steam pumps, some of the largest in the country, in tall brick engine houses on the hills. The pumps ran day and night, lifting water out of the mines. For a few years, they worked. Then, in 1886, a fire destroyed one of the great pumping plants. Around the same time, the price of silver began to fall. Pumping became too expensive, and the lower levels of the mines slowly filled with water. Many miners left to find work elsewhere. The population of Tombstone shrank year by year. It is a quiet irony that a town once so short of water was finally brought low by having far too much of it underground.

[long-pause]

{img:80-red-brick-courthouse-1882}

In 1882, Tombstone built a grand red brick courthouse, which still stands today.

[pause]

As the seat of Cochise County, Tombstone needed a proper courthouse. In 1882, a handsome two-storey building of red brick was completed on Toughnut Street. It had tall windows, a small tower and offices for the sheriff, the county recorder, the treasurer and the courts. It was a sign of the town's confidence and importance. For the next half century, the courthouse was the centre of county government. Trials were held there, records were kept there, and land and mining claims were registered there. In 1929, the county seat was moved to the town of Bisbee, which had grown larger, and the courthouse was left empty for many years. Later, it was restored and turned into a museum. Today, it is one of the best places to learn about the history of Tombstone. Its rooms are filled with objects from everyday life in the town, and its old courtroom has been carefully preserved.

[long-pause]

{img:81-ed-schieffelin-cabin-oregon-woods}

Ed Schieffelin became rich from his silver, but he never stopped prospecting.

[pause]

After finding the silver at Tombstone, Ed Schieffelin and his partners sold their shares and became wealthy men. Ed married, travelled and lived comfortably for a while. Yet he never seemed quite at home in towns or cities. He kept going back into the wilderness, looking for new veins of ore, in Alaska, in Nevada and in the forests of Oregon. He seemed to love the searching more than the finding. In May 1897, he was found dead in a small cabin in the woods of Oregon, where he had been working on a claim. He was forty-nine years old. On the table were pieces of ore, and a note in his diary suggested he believed he had found something rich again. He had once asked to be buried near Tombstone, dressed as a prospector, with his pick and canteen. His family respected his wish, and he was brought back to the hills where his great discovery had begun.

[long-pause]

{img:82-colton-california-orange-groves}

Virgil and Allie Earp settled in California, where Virgil later became a town marshal once again.

[pause]

After leaving Tombstone, Virgil and Allie lived for some years in Colton, a small town in southern California surrounded by orange groves. Despite his injured arm, Virgil worked as a detective and later became the first city marshal of Colton. He and Allie lived quietly for a time, among neighbours, gardens and the scent of orange blossom in the spring. Later, they moved on to other places, following work and opportunity, as they always had. They spent time in mining towns in Arizona and Nevada. Virgil died in 1905 in Goldfield, Nevada, of pneumonia. Allie lived for many more years, until 1947, and in her old age, she shared her memories of the frontier with a writer. Her stories were lively, funny and sometimes sharp. After so much drama in Tombstone, Virgil and Allie's long, loyal marriage is perhaps one of the gentlest parts of the whole story.

[long-pause]

{img:83-glenwood-springs-hotel-mountains}

Doc Holliday died in a hotel in the Colorado mountains, at the age of thirty-six.

[pause]

After leaving Arizona, Doc Holliday spent most of his remaining years in Colorado. He gambled in Denver, Leadville and other towns, and his health grew steadily worse. In 1887, he travelled to Glenwood Springs, a small town in the Rocky Mountains, famous for its hot springs. Many people believed that the mineral waters and the mountain air could help sick people. But for Doc, it was too late. He spent his last weeks in bed at the Hotel Glenwood, very weak. He died there on the eighth of November, 1887. He was only thirty-six years old. According to a well-known story, his last words were, this is funny, perhaps because he had always expected to die with his boots on, and instead he died quietly in bed. Whether he really said it, no one can be sure. He was buried in the town cemetery, on a hill overlooking the river and the mountains.

[long-pause]

{img:84-wyatt-josephine-older-desert-camp}

Wyatt Earp lived until 1929, long enough to see his own story turned into Hollywood legend.

[pause]

After Tombstone, Wyatt Earp travelled widely, from Colorado and Idaho to San Francisco, Alaska and Nevada. He ran saloons, raced horses, invested in mines and worked in many different jobs. He lived with Josephine Marcus, the young woman he had met in Tombstone, for nearly fifty years. In their later years, they spent the winters in a small cottage in the California desert, near a mining claim, and the summers in Los Angeles. In Los Angeles, Wyatt met some of the early film makers and actors of Hollywood, who were fascinated by his stories of the Old West. He hoped to see an accurate film made about his life, but it did not happen while he was alive. He died in January 1929, at the age of eighty. Within a few years, books and films had made him one of the most famous figures of the American West, often in ways that had little to do with what really happened.

[long-pause]

{img:85-tombstone-today-allen-street-boardwalk}

The people of those years are long gone now. Yet the town they knew is still there, on its high desert mesa.

[pause]

Tombstone still exists today, and it calls itself the town too tough to die.

[pause]

When the mines closed, many boom towns of the West were abandoned and became ghost towns. Tombstone shrank to a small fraction of its former size, but it never disappeared. A few hundred people stayed on, ranching, trading and looking after the old buildings. In the twentieth century, as interest in the Old West grew, visitors began to arrive. The town became a national historic landmark. Today, Allen Street has been closed to cars, and its wooden boardwalks and old buildings have been restored. Stagecoaches carry visitors up and down the street, and people in nineteenth-century costume tell stories of the town's past. Some of the saloons and shops are in the same buildings that stood there in 1881. Each year, many thousands of visitors come. The town's nickname, the town too tough to die, is a little proud and a little playful. Yet it is also true. Through fires, floods and the loss of its mines, Tombstone has survived.

[long-pause]

{img:86-courthouse-museum-today}

In the old courthouse museum, the everyday objects of Tombstone's past are kept behind glass.

[pause]

Inside the red brick courthouse, now a state historic park, visitors can walk through rooms filled with objects from the town's early years. There are miners' candle holders with their sharp spikes, tin lunch pails, picks and drills. There are ladies' fans and gloves, a child's slate, kitchen tools, medicine bottles and old maps. There are photographs taken by C. S. Fly and others, showing the streets, the mines and the faces of the people who lived there. The old courtroom upstairs has been preserved, with its benches and judge's desk. These quiet objects tell a different story from the famous gunfight. They are the things of ordinary life, the cooking, the working, the schooling and the waiting. They remind us that most of the people of Tombstone never fired a gun in anger. They simply lived there, worked hard and hoped for a better future, as people everywhere always have.

[long-pause]

{img:87-boothill-graveyard-evening-light}

Tombstone's old cemetery on the hill is known as Boothill, and many of its graves are marked only with wooden boards.

[pause]

Just outside town, on a rocky slope, lies the old cemetery that served Tombstone in its early years. It was later nicknamed Boothill, a name used in several frontier towns for cemeteries where people were said to have died with their boots on. In reality, most of the people buried there died of illness, accidents and old age, like people everywhere. There are miners, children, travellers, Chinese and Mexican residents and people whose names were never recorded. Tom and Frank McLaury and Billy Clanton are buried there, side by side. Many graves are marked with simple wooden boards and piles of stones, and some boards carry short, dry epitaphs. In the evening, the low golden light falls across the graves, the cactus and the mesquite, and the hills beyond. It is a quiet and rather moving place. It is the one part of the old town where all its people, from every side of every quarrel, now rest together.

[long-pause]

{img:88-schieffelin-monument-hills-sunset}

Ed Schieffelin is buried in the hills west of Tombstone, under a monument shaped like a prospector's claim marker.

[pause]

Ed Schieffelin had asked to be buried near the place where he found his silver, and his wish was granted. His grave lies a few miles west of town, on a small rise in the open grassland. Over it stands a tall monument made of rough piled stones, the same way prospectors marked their claims, only much larger. From the monument, you can see the hills where he walked alone with his mule in 1877, and the mountains all around. Few people come out there, and it is usually very quiet, with only the wind in the grass and the call of a hawk overhead. At sunset, the stones glow warm and golden. It is a fitting place for a man who loved the open country more than the towns his discoveries created. He found the silver, but it seems that what he truly loved was the looking itself, the next ridge, the next valley and the next piece of rock in his hand.

[long-pause]

{img:89-rose-tree-courtyard-white-blossoms}

Tombstone is home to what is often called the largest rose bush in the world.

[pause]

In 1885, a young woman named Mary Gee arrived in Tombstone from Scotland as a bride. She brought with her a small rose cutting from home, a Lady Banks rose. She planted it in the courtyard of her house, and it began to grow. And grow. Over the years, the rose spread across a wooden frame built to support it, until it covered thousands of square feet like a great green roof. Every spring, it is covered with countless tiny white blossoms, and the courtyard beneath fills with dappled shade and a soft, sweet scent. The rose tree, as it is called, still grows in Tombstone today, more than a hundred and thirty years after it was planted. Visitors sit beneath it in the shade. Of all the stories of Tombstone, this may be the gentlest. A small cutting carried across an ocean, by a young woman far from home, quietly outlived the boom, the mines and nearly everyone who knew it.

[long-pause]

{img:90-tombstone-night-stars-desert-slot}

On a clear night, the sky above Tombstone is still full of stars, just as it was in 1881.

[pause]

The town is small and quiet after dark now. The visitors have gone back to their hotels, the shops on Allen Street are closed, and the stagecoaches are back in their stables. A few warm lamps glow along the old boardwalks. Around the town, the dark hills roll away toward the mountains, and the desert is very still. Because there are few big cities nearby, the night sky in southern Arizona is unusually clear and dark. The stars are bright and countless, and on moonless nights, the Milky Way spreads across the sky like a pale river. It is the same sky that Ed Schieffelin looked up at from his campfire, the same sky that George Parsons described in his diary and that the miners saw on their way home from the night shift. The town has changed in many ways. Yet the stars, the silence and the cool night wind on the mesa are just as they always were.

[long-pause]

And so our quiet visit to Tombstone comes to an end.

[pause]

The lamps along Allen Street are dimming, one by one. The last horse at the hitching rail lowers its head. Out on the mesa, the wind moves softly through the grass, and the old mine engines have long since fallen silent. Above the rooftops, the stars are bright and close, just as they were in 1881.

[pause]

If sleep is still on its way, another gentle story from the frontier days is ready for you on the screen. A subscription brings the next stagecoach ride to your door, but only if you want one.

[pause]

The desert night is wide and quiet around you now. Let it hold you as you drift.

[pause]

Sleep well, and good night.
