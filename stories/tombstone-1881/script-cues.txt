{img:desert-hills-dawn-prospector}
Tonight we are going about a hundred and forty-five years back, to a young silver town on a high, dry mesa in the southern corner of Arizona.

[long-pause]

Long before there is a town here, there is only the desert, and a man walking across it with a mule.

[pause]

The land is wide and brown and quiet. Low hills roll away in every direction, covered with grass, mesquite and the tall, thin stems of the ocotillo. Far off, blue mountains stand along the edge of the sky.

[pause]

If you enjoy slow stories like this one, you are warmly invited to subscribe. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

The man with the mule is called Ed Schieffelin, and the year is 1877.

[pause]

He is about thirty years old, tall and very thin, with long dark hair and a thick, untidy beard. His clothes are patched in so many places that it is hard to tell what colour they were when they were new. He has been looking for silver and gold for most of his life, in Oregon, in Nevada, in California and in Utah, and so far he has found almost nothing.

[pause]

He does not seem to mind very much. He likes the work. He likes walking through empty country, turning over stones, and sleeping under the stars at night beside a small fire.

[long-pause]

{img:camp-huachuca-soldiers-tents}
In the spring of that year, he comes to a small army camp at the foot of the Huachuca Mountains.

[pause]

Camp Huachuca is a cluster of tents and rough huts beside a stream that runs down out of a green canyon. The soldiers are there to watch the country, and from time to time a few of them ride out to scout the land to the east, toward the valley of the San Pedro River.

[pause]

Schieffelin rides along with them now and then. And while the soldiers are watching for trouble, he is watching the ground.

[pause]

On the far side of the river, he has noticed a stretch of low, broken hills that no one seems to have looked at very carefully. The rocks there have an interesting colour. He wants to go and see.

[long-pause]

The soldiers think he is foolish.

[pause]

The hills on the other side of the river are lonely and dry, and the country is not safe for a man on his own. When Schieffelin tells them what he plans to do, one of them shakes his head and says that the only thing he will find out there is his own tombstone.

[pause]

Schieffelin remembers the words. He does not argue. He simply loads his mule, fills his canteens, and goes.

[long-pause]

{img:prospector-campfire-night}
For weeks he wanders through the hills, working alone.

[pause]

During the day he climbs the slopes and follows the dry washes, chipping at the rocks with his little hammer and carrying the pieces back to camp in a canvas bag. In the evening, when the heat goes out of the land, he lights a fire of mesquite wood, boils a pot of coffee, and turns his samples over in his hands in the light of the flames.

[pause]

At night it is very still. There are coyotes somewhere far away, and the stars are so thick and bright above the desert that they seem close enough to touch.

[long-pause]

One day, on a ridge above a long, sloping plain, he finds something different.

[pause]

It is a piece of rock with dark, heavy streaks in it, and when he looks closer, he can see that the streaks are rich with silver.

[pause]

He searches the ground nearby and finds more. Then more again.

[pause]

He does not have the money to work a mine, and he does not have anyone to help him. So he does the only thing a prospector can do. He marks out a claim, writes down its boundaries, and gives it a name.

[pause]

He calls it the Tombstone.

[long-pause]

With a sack of samples on his mule and almost no money left, Schieffelin travels west to the little mining town of Signal, where his brother Al is working.

[pause]

There, he meets a quiet, careful man named Richard Gird, an engineer and assayer, who knows how to test rock and tell exactly how much silver is hidden inside it.

[pause]

Gird takes the samples into his workshop. He crushes them, weighs them, and heats them in his small furnace. The work takes some time.

[pause]

When he finally comes out, he looks at Schieffelin with an expression of great surprise. The ore is very rich indeed. Some of it, he says, is worth thousands of dollars a ton.

[long-pause]

The three men shake hands and agree to become partners.

[pause]

Early in 1878, they travel back together to the hills beyond the San Pedro. They camp in a small hollow, with a little shelter of brush and canvas, and spend their days searching the slopes.

[pause]

The work is slow and patient. But this time, Schieffelin does not have to wait for long.

[pause]

He finds another rich vein, so rich that, when he shows it to Gird, Gird tells him that he is a lucky cuss. And so that becomes the name of the second mine: the Lucky Cuss.

[pause]

Not long after, they find a third, set in such hard rock that it is almost impossible to dig. They call it the Tough Nut.

[long-pause]

{img:first-claims-tents-hills}
News travels fast in the West, even across empty country.

[pause]

By the end of 1878, other prospectors have heard that something has been found in the hills east of the San Pedro, and they begin to arrive. First a few, then dozens, then hundreds.

[pause]

They come on horseback and on foot, in wagons and on the backs of mules. They pitch tents beside the claims, build little huts of brush and stone, and start to dig.

[pause]

Soon there are new mines everywhere: the Contention, the Grand Central, the Goodenough, the Toughnut, and many more. Mills are built beside the river to crush the ore, and long lines of wagons begin to roll back and forth between the hills and the mills, carrying rock one way and supplies the other.

[long-pause]

A camp grows up first in one place and then in another.

[pause]

Then, in the spring of 1879, a group of men lay out a proper town site on a broad, flat mesa between the mines, a place the prospectors have been calling Goose Flats.

[pause]

They measure out streets in a neat grid, mark out lots with stakes and string, and sell them to anyone who wants to build.

[pause]

There is really only one name the new town could have. They name it after Ed Schieffelin's first claim.

[pause]

They call it Tombstone.

[long-pause]

{img:mesa-sunset-mountains-distant}
From the edge of the mesa, the view is very wide.

[pause]

To the east are the Dragoon Mountains, rocky and grey, turning pink in the evening light. To the west, beyond the valley of the San Pedro, are the long blue line of the Whetstones and the high, dark Huachucas where the army camp still stands.

[pause]

The air is clean and dry, and the sky above it is enormous. On a clear evening, the clouds over the mountains glow orange and gold, and the shadows of the hills stretch for miles across the grass.

[long-pause]

Ed Schieffelin does not stay long in the town that bears the name of his mine.

[pause]

He is a man who likes empty places, not crowded ones. Within a few years, he and his brother sell their shares and become wealthy, and Ed goes off travelling again, still looking for the next hill, the next streak of silver in a piece of rock.

[pause]

But the town he started keeps on growing.

[pause]

And by the autumn of 1881, where there was once only grass and stones and a lonely man with a mule, there is a busy little city of several thousand people, with hotels and churches, newspapers and theatres, and lamps in the windows every evening, shining out across the dark desert.

[long-pause]

{img:tombstone-street-morning-1881}
It is early on a morning in the autumn of 1881, and the town of Tombstone is waking up.

[pause]

The sun has just come over the Dragoon Mountains, and its first light falls along the wide, dusty streets, catching the tops of the wooden false fronts and the pale walls of the adobe houses.

[pause]

The air is cool now, after the long summer. There is a little dew on the boardwalks, and the smell of wood smoke drifts from hundreds of kitchen stoves.

[long-pause]

The town is laid out like a sheet of squared paper.

[pause]

The long streets run from east to west, and they have plain, practical names. Toughnut Street lies along the southern edge, closest to the mines. Then comes Allen Street, the busiest of all, then Fremont Street, with the post office and many of the houses. Further north are Safford and Bruce and Winnemuca.

[pause]

The cross streets are simply numbered, First Street, Second Street, Third Street, and so on, up to Seventh and beyond.

[pause]

From one end of the town to the other is only a short walk. But on that short walk, in the autumn of 1881, a person can pass almost every kind of business that a small city could need.

[long-pause]

{img:adobe-and-wooden-buildings}
The buildings are a mixture of old and new, rough and grand.

[pause]

There are still a few tents at the edges of the town, and little cabins built of rough boards with canvas roofs. But most of the buildings along the main streets are now solid and permanent.

[pause]

Some are made of wood, painted white or grey or a pale green, with tall square fronts that make them look bigger than they really are. Many others are built of adobe, thick bricks of sun-dried mud, with walls so deep that the rooms inside stay cool even on the hottest days of summer.

[pause]

Along Allen Street, the wooden awnings stretch out over the boardwalks, held up by slender posts, so that people can walk from one end of the street to the other in the shade.

[long-pause]

There are about five or six thousand people living in Tombstone now, perhaps even more, and new ones are arriving every week.

[pause]

They come by stagecoach, mostly. The railway has reached the little town of Benson, about twenty-five miles to the north, and from there the stages rattle down the road past the mills at Contention, with four or six horses in front and the passengers packed inside, while the mail sacks and luggage are tied on top.

[pause]

When the stage pulls into town, there is usually a little crowd waiting to see who has come. Miners looking for work. Merchants with crates of goods. Young clerks from the East, hoping to make their fortune. Wives and children coming to join husbands who arrived the year before.

[long-pause]

{img:water-wagon-barrels}
Water is one of the town's great difficulties.

[pause]

There is no river on the mesa, and the wells that have been dug give only a little. For the first years, most of the water has been brought in by wagon from springs and wells nearby, in big wooden barrels, and sold from door to door.

[pause]

In the mornings, the water carts move slowly along the streets, and the women come out with buckets and jugs to buy what they need for the day. A bath is a luxury. A garden is a great extravagance.

[pause]

But things are changing. Pipes are being laid from springs in the hills, and before long there will be running water in many of the houses, which everyone agrees will be a wonderful thing.

[long-pause]

The town has had one great fright already this year.

[pause]

In June, on a hot afternoon, a fire started in a saloon on Allen Street, when a barrel of whiskey was being measured and a spark from a cigar fell into it. The flames spread quickly through the dry wooden buildings, and within a few hours, several blocks of the busiest part of town were gone.

[pause]

No one was lost in the fire, and the people of Tombstone did not waste much time on regrets.

[pause]

Before the ashes were even cold, they were clearing the lots and ordering new timber. Carpenters came in from every direction. All through the summer, the sound of saws and hammers rang along the street from morning until evening, and by the autumn, most of the burned blocks had been built again, some of them grander than before.

[long-pause]

{img:miners-walking-to-shift-dawn}
Up on the hills to the south, the mine whistles blow.

[pause]

The sound carries clearly across the whole town, long and low, calling the morning shift to work. From all over Tombstone, men begin to walk toward the mines, in twos and threes, with their lunch pails in their hands and their candles tucked into their hats.

[pause]

They walk up Toughnut Street and out across the rocky slopes, past the hoisting works with their tall wooden frames and the dumps of grey rock, and one by one, they disappear underground.

[long-pause]

By the middle of the morning, the town is full of noise and colour.

[pause]

Wagons roll along Allen Street, piled high with lumber and barrels and crates. Horses stand tied at the hitching rails, swishing their tails and dozing in the sun. Shopkeepers call out to one another across the street.

[pause]

There are people here from all over the world. American farmers from Missouri and Kansas, miners from Cornwall and Ireland and Wales, merchants from Germany and New York, families from Sonora in Mexico, cooks and launderers from China. On a single short walk, a person might hear five or six different languages spoken on the boardwalk.

[long-pause]

{img:wells-fargo-office-stagecoach}
In front of the Wells Fargo office, a stagecoach is being loaded for the return journey to Benson.

[pause]

The office is a busy place. It is where the silver from the mills is sent out, packed in heavy wooden boxes, and where money and parcels and letters arrive from the outside world.

[pause]

A man with a shotgun climbs up beside the driver. His job is to sit there all the way to the railway, watching the road, and to keep the box under the seat safe until it reaches the train.

[pause]

The driver gathers the reins, calls to the horses, and the coach rolls slowly out of town, through the dust and the morning sunshine, toward the long road north.

[long-pause]

The town has grown so quickly that it still feels a little surprised at itself.

[pause]

Only three years ago, there was nothing on this mesa at all. Now there are banks and doctors and lawyers, schools and churches, a theatre, two daily newspapers, and an ice cream parlour.

[pause]

There is even talk of a grand new courthouse, because Tombstone has become the seat of a brand new county, called Cochise County, after the great Apache leader who once lived in the mountains to the east.

[pause]

And above it all, every day, the mines go on working, and the silver keeps coming up out of the ground.

[long-pause]

{img:hoisting-works-headframe}
The silver mines are the reason the town exists, and almost everything in Tombstone depends on them.

[pause]

On the hills just south of the streets, the hoisting works stand against the sky. Each one is a tall wooden frame, built over the mouth of a shaft, with a great wheel at the top and a steam engine in a shed beside it.

[pause]

When the engine runs, a steel cable winds around a drum, and a small iron cage rises slowly up out of the shaft, carrying men or buckets of rock. When it is lowered again, it disappears into the darkness below, down and down, hundreds of feet into the earth.

[long-pause]

The biggest mines have names that everyone in town knows by heart.

[pause]

The Contention. The Grand Central. The Toughnut. The Lucky Cuss. The Goodenough. Each has its own hoisting works, its own offices and blacksmith shops and storerooms, and its own crowd of men going in and out at every change of shift.

[pause]

The mines work day and night. When the morning shift goes down, the night shift comes up, blinking in the sunlight, their clothes grey with dust, and walks slowly home to sleep.

[long-pause]

{img:miners-candles-underground}
Underground, it is quiet and warm and very dark.

[pause]

The only light comes from the miners' candles, which they carry in little iron holders with a sharp point, so that they can stick them into a crack in the rock or into a wooden post, wherever they are working.

[pause]

The tunnels run out from the main shaft in all directions, following the veins of silver ore. Some are wide enough for a mule to walk through, pulling a small ore car on rails. Others are so narrow and low that a man must crawl, or work on his knees.

[pause]

The air smells of candle smoke, damp rock and old timber.

[long-pause]

Most of the work is done by hand.

[pause]

The miners work in pairs. One man holds a long steel drill against the rock, turning it a little after every blow, while the other swings a heavy hammer, striking the end of the drill again and again in a slow, steady rhythm. It can take an hour or more to drill a single hole.

[pause]

When enough holes are drilled, they are filled with blasting powder, and the men move back along the tunnel to a safe place, until the rock has been loosened. Then they come back with their shovels and load the broken ore into cars, to be wheeled to the shaft and sent up to the daylight.

[long-pause]

{img:miners-lunch-break-candlelight}
At midday, the men stop to eat.

[pause]

They sit down together in a wide part of the tunnel, with their backs against the rock and their candles flickering beside them, and open their lunch pails.

[pause]

The Cornish miners often bring pasties, the folded pastries of their homeland, filled with meat and potato and onion and wrapped in a cloth to keep them warm. Some say that their wives used to mark each pasty with the miner's initials, so that every man got his own.

[pause]

They talk quietly about the work, about the town, about home. Then the shift boss calls, and they pick up their tools again.

[long-pause]

The miners come from many places.

[pause]

There are Cornishmen, from the old tin mines at the far western tip of England, who are some of the most skilled miners in the world. There are Irishmen and Welshmen and Germans, Americans from the mines of Nevada and California, and Mexicans who have worked in the silver mines of Sonora since they were boys.

[pause]

A miner in Tombstone earns about four dollars a day, which is a good wage for the time. Many of them send part of it home every month, to wives and parents far away, and save the rest in the hope of one day buying a farm, or a little business of their own.

[long-pause]

{img:ore-wagons-desert-road}
When the silver ore comes up out of the ground, it is not yet silver.

[pause]

It is only rock, grey and brown and heavy, with a little of the precious metal hidden inside it. To get the silver out, it must be crushed and treated, and for that it needs water, a great deal of water.

[pause]

There is not enough water on the mesa. So the ore is loaded into big wagons and hauled down to the San Pedro River, about nine or ten miles to the west.

[pause]

All day long, the ore wagons roll along the dusty road between the hills and the river, pulled by long teams of mules, twelve or sixteen or even twenty in a line, with the driver riding on one of the animals near the back and calling out to them by name.

[long-pause]

Along the river, little mill towns have grown up, with names like Charleston, Contention and Millville.

[pause]

{img:stamp-mill-river-cottonwoods}
At Charleston, the stamp mill stands beside the water among tall cottonwood trees. Inside, rows of heavy iron stamps rise and fall, rise and fall, crushing the ore into fine powder. The noise is tremendous. It goes on day and night without stopping, and the people who live nearby say that after a while they cannot sleep without it.

[pause]

The crushed rock is mixed with water and quicksilver and salt and heated in great iron pans, until the silver is drawn out of it. Then it is melted and poured into heavy bars, which are carried back up to Tombstone and sent off on the stagecoach to the railway.

[long-pause]

But the miners have begun to notice something new.

[pause]

The deeper the shafts go, the damper the rock becomes. In some of the mines, the men have found water seeping into the lowest tunnels, at about five hundred feet below the surface.

[pause]

At first, it seems like good news. Water is so scarce on the mesa that some people hope it might be used in the town. But the mine owners are worried. If the water rises, they will need great pumps to keep the tunnels dry, and pumps are very expensive.

[pause]

For now, though, the work goes on as before. The candles burn. The hammers ring. And every evening, when the whistle blows, the men come up into the cool desert air, with the sky turning purple over the mountains, and walk home down the hill toward the lights of the town.

[long-pause]

{img:allen-street-afternoon-boardwalk}
In the afternoon, the busiest place in Tombstone is Allen Street.

[pause]

The boardwalks along both sides are crowded with people, walking slowly in the shade of the awnings, stopping to look into the shop windows, and greeting their friends. The street between them is wide and unpaved, and every passing wagon lifts a soft cloud of dust that drifts slowly away on the breeze.

[long-pause]

There are shops here for almost everything.

[pause]

There is a hardware store with picks and shovels and coils of rope hanging outside the door. There is a drugstore with tall glass jars of coloured water in the window. There are dry goods stores with bolts of calico and gingham and wool stacked on the shelves, and hat shops, and shoe shops, and a jeweller with a little glass case of watches.

[pause]

There are tobacconists and stationers, a bookshop, a gunsmith, a saddle maker, and a bakery where the smell of fresh bread drifts out across the boardwalk all afternoon.

[long-pause]

Inside one of the dry goods stores, two women are choosing fabric for new dresses.

[pause]

The clerk unrolls one bolt after another across the long wooden counter: a dark blue wool for the winter, a soft grey silk for church, a pretty patterned cotton for a little girl's Sunday dress.

[pause]

The women hold the cloth up to the light from the window, rub it between their fingers, and discuss it quietly. Many of the things in this store have travelled a very long way, by ship and by train and finally by wagon across the desert, and they are not cheap. A new dress is a serious decision.

[long-pause]

Tombstone is a young town, but it likes good food very much.

[pause]

There are restaurants all along the street, and some of them are surprisingly fine. One is called the Can Can. Another, the Maison Dorée, has a French chef and serves dinners of several courses on white tablecloths.

[pause]

{img:restaurant-white-tablecloths-lamps}
A miner who has just been paid can sit down in the evening to a meal of roast beef or mutton, fresh fish, quail, or even oysters, which are brought in packed in ice, all the way from the coast. Afterwards there is pie, or cake, or a dish of canned peaches, and a cup of strong coffee.

[pause]

The prices are high, because everything must be hauled across the desert. But people in Tombstone have money in their pockets, and they like to spend it.

[long-pause]

On hot afternoons, there is nothing so popular as the ice cream parlour.

[pause]

Ice is a great luxury in the desert. It is made in an ice plant, or brought in blocks wrapped in sawdust and straw, and kept in a cool cellar. To eat a dish of ice cream on a warm day, sitting at a little marble table, is one of the small pleasures of life in Tombstone.

[pause]

The young people especially love it. On a Saturday afternoon, the parlour is full of young men in their best collars and young women in their best hats, eating slowly and laughing and pretending not to look at one another.

[long-pause]

{img:saloon-interior-lamplight-piano}
There are also a great many saloons on Allen Street, more than anywhere else in town.

[pause]

Some are small and plain, with a few tables, a long bar and a sawdust floor. Others are grand. The Oriental Saloon, on the corner of Allen and Fifth Street, is said to be the finest between San Francisco and New Orleans. Its bar is long and gleaming, its mirrors are tall, and its walls are lined with dark polished wood. In the evenings, there is music from a piano, and sometimes from a small orchestra of violins.

[pause]

The saloons are not only places to drink. They are where men meet to talk business, to read the newspapers, to sell a mine or buy a horse, to hear the news from back East. Many of the miners live in small rooms or crowded boarding houses, and the saloon is the nearest thing they have to a parlour.

[long-pause]

In many of the saloons, there are tables for cards.

[pause]

The most popular game is faro, a slow, quiet game in which the players bet on cards drawn one by one from a little box by the dealer. The dealers are respected men in town, smartly dressed, very calm, and very good with numbers.

[pause]

People play poker, too, late into the night, at round tables under hanging lamps, with the smoke from their cigars drifting up toward the ceiling.

[long-pause]

{img:schieffelin-hall-evening-crowd}
For a different kind of evening, there is the theatre.

[pause]

On Fremont Street stands Schieffelin Hall, built by Al Schieffelin, the brother of the man who found the first silver. It is a large building of adobe, with a stage, a gallery, and seats for hundreds of people, and it is the pride of the town.

[pause]

Travelling companies come all the way to Tombstone to perform there. There are plays and concerts, lectures and dances, and in the evenings the hall is lit with lamps and full of people in their best clothes, the women in silk and lace and the men in dark suits.

[long-pause]

{img:hitching-rail-horses-dusk}
As the afternoon turns to evening, the light along Allen Street changes.

[pause]

The sun goes down behind the Whetstone Mountains, and the sky over the town fades from gold to rose to a deep, soft blue. One by one, the lamps are lit in the windows of the stores and restaurants and saloons, and the boardwalks shine with little squares of yellow light.

[pause]

The horses at the hitching rails stand quietly with their heads down. Somewhere a piano begins to play. The cooks in the restaurants are busy now, and the smell of frying steak and fresh coffee drifts out into the cooling air.

[long-pause]

At the end of the street, where the town gives way to the open desert, the dark comes down quickly and completely.

[pause]

There are no lights out there at all, only the long, low shapes of the hills, and the mountains beyond them, black against the last of the evening sky.

[pause]

And above them, as the night deepens, the stars come out, more and more of them, until the whole sky over Tombstone is thick with them, from one edge of the desert to the other.

[long-pause]

{img:nellie-cashman-restaurant-kitchen}
Among the many people who have come to Tombstone, one of the best loved is a small, dark-haired Irishwoman named Nellie Cashman.

[pause]

She is in her thirties in 1881, slim and quick, with bright eyes and a soft Irish voice. She is not married, and she does not seem to want to be. She has too much to do.

[long-pause]

Nellie was born in County Cork, in the south of Ireland, and came to America as a girl with her mother and her sister, during the hard years after the great famine.

[pause]

They lived for a while in Boston, where Nellie worked as a bellhop in a hotel, carrying luggage up and down the stairs. Then the family moved west, to San Francisco. From there, Nellie began to follow the mining camps.

[pause]

She ran boarding houses and restaurants in the silver towns of Nevada. She went north to the gold fields of British Columbia, high in the mountains of Canada. And when she heard that silver had been found in southern Arizona, she packed her things once again and came to Tombstone.

[long-pause]

{img:boarding-house-dining-table-miners}
In Tombstone, Nellie runs a restaurant and a boarding house, and she is very good at it.

[pause]

Her dining room is plain and clean, with long tables covered in oilcloth and benches on either side. The food is simple, but there is plenty of it: stews and roasts, fresh bread, beans and potatoes, pies made from dried apples and canned peaches.

[pause]

The miners love her cooking, and they love her too. She treats them with kindness and good humour, she listens to their troubles, and she is famous for never letting anyone go hungry.

[long-pause]

But Nellie Cashman is known in Tombstone for much more than her cooking.

[pause]

When a miner is hurt underground, or falls ill with a fever, she is often the first person to visit him. She brings soup and clean linen, sits by his bed, writes letters home for him if he cannot write himself, and makes sure he has what he needs.

[pause]

When a man is out of work and out of money, she finds him a meal and a bed, and often a job as well. When a family has troubles, she goes from door to door, asking the merchants and the saloon keepers and the mine owners for a little help, and very few people ever say no to her.

[pause]

The miners begin to call her the Angel of Tombstone.

[long-pause]

She is a devout Catholic, and the town has no proper Catholic church.

[pause]

So Nellie decides to raise the money to build one. She walks up and down Allen Street, into the stores and the hotels and the saloons, and asks everyone she meets to give whatever they can.

[pause]

The gamblers at the card tables give, and the bartenders give, and the miners give part of their wages. Even people who never go to church at all put a few dollars into her hands, because it is Nellie asking.

[pause]

{img:small-adobe-church-evening}
Before long, there is a little church in Tombstone, built of adobe, with a small wooden cross above the door. On Sunday mornings, the bell rings out across the town, and families in their best clothes walk slowly along the dusty street to Mass.

[long-pause]

Nellie also helps to raise money for a hospital, and for the town's school.

[pause]

Tombstone is full of children now. There are the children of the miners and the merchants, the children of the Mexican families on the south side of town, and the children of the ranchers, who come in from the valleys on Saturdays.

[pause]

{img:schoolhouse-children-teacher}
The schoolhouse is a plain building with rows of wooden desks, a blackboard, a stove in the corner, and a map of the United States hanging on the wall. The teacher writes the lessons in chalk, and the children copy them carefully onto their slates.

[pause]

They learn their letters and their sums, the names of the states and the rivers, and recite poems aloud, all together, in a slow, sing-song chorus. In the afternoons, they run home through the dusty streets, past the shops and the saloons, swinging their books by a strap.

[long-pause]

Like everyone in Tombstone, Nellie has her own hopes and plans.

[pause]

She likes to buy shares in mining claims, and she keeps a careful eye on the news from new strikes in other places. She has the restless heart of a prospector, and every so often she goes off on an expedition into the mountains, or across the border into Mexico, to see what can be found.

[pause]

But she always comes back to Tombstone, to her restaurant and her boarding house, and to the miners who have become, in a way, her family.

[long-pause]

{img:nellie-evening-porch-lamp}
In the evenings, after the last dinners have been served and the dishes are washed, Nellie sometimes sits for a while on the porch of her boarding house.

[pause]

The street is quieter at this hour. A few lamps glow in the windows across the way, and there is music drifting from a saloon further along. The air is cool, and smells faintly of wood smoke and desert sage.

[pause]

She sits with her hands folded in her lap, watching the people pass, nodding to the ones she knows, which is almost everyone. Then, when it grows late, she goes inside, turns down the lamp, and goes up to bed, because tomorrow there will be breakfast for forty men, and it begins before dawn.

[long-pause]

{img:parsons-writing-diary-lamp}
Every evening, in a small room in Tombstone, a young man sits down at a table by the lamp and opens a leather-bound notebook.

[pause]

His name is George Parsons. He is about thirty years old, slim and serious, with a neat moustache and a careful, sloping handwriting. And almost every night of his life in Tombstone, he writes down what happened that day.

[long-pause]

Parsons came to Arizona from San Francisco, where he had worked as a clerk in a bank.

[pause]

The work was safe and steady, but it bored him. He wanted something more. So in the winter of 1880, when he heard about the silver in Tombstone, he left his job, travelled south and east by train and by stage, and arrived in the new town with a little money and a great deal of hope.

[pause]

He has been here ever since.

[long-pause]

{img:parsons-walking-hills-claim}
Like most people in Tombstone, Parsons is trying to make his fortune in the mines.

[pause]

He owns small shares in several claims, out in the hills around the town. Some days he walks out with a partner to work on them, digging and blasting and looking for signs of good ore. Other days he takes whatever work he can find in town, keeping accounts for a store, or helping a friend with his business.

[pause]

The fortune has not come yet. But he is patient, and he is cheerful, and he believes that it will.

[long-pause]

His diary is full of the small details of ordinary days.

[pause]

He writes about the weather, which is often very hot, or very windy, or full of sudden thunderstorms in the summer afternoons. He writes about what he ate for dinner, and who he met on Allen Street, and how much he paid for a new pair of boots.

[pause]

He writes about letters from home, which he reads again and again, and about the long evenings when he feels a little lonely, so far from his family.

[long-pause]

He writes about the town, too, and all its changes.

[pause]

The new buildings going up, one after another. The arrival of the first piano in the house of a friend. A dance in Schieffelin Hall, where he stayed until two in the morning. The great fire in June, when he helped to carry goods out of a burning store, and watched the flames go roaring down Allen Street.

[pause]

{img:church-sunday-congregation}
He writes about church, which is important to him. He goes every Sunday, and sometimes twice. He sings in the choir, and he helps to collect money for a new church building, and he is pleased whenever he sees a new face in the pews.

[long-pause]

Parsons likes to walk.

[pause]

On Sunday afternoons, when the work of the week is done, he often walks out of town into the hills, sometimes alone and sometimes with a friend.

[pause]

He climbs to the top of a rocky ridge and sits there for an hour or two, looking out over the wide valley to the mountains. He watches the shadows of the clouds moving slowly across the grass, and the little dust trails of wagons on the distant roads.

[pause]

{img:hilltop-view-valley-afternoon}
In the spring, after the rains, the desert is covered with wildflowers, yellow and orange and purple, and the air is full of their scent. In the summer, great white thunderclouds build up over the Huachucas every afternoon, and the lightning flickers in the distance. In the autumn, the grass turns gold, and the evenings are cool and clear.

[pause]

He writes about all of it. He loves this country, even when it is hard.

[long-pause]

Parsons knows a great many people in Tombstone.

[pause]

He knows Nellie Cashman, and eats at her restaurant. He knows John Clum, the editor of one of the newspapers, who has become a good friend. He knows the miners and the merchants, the bankers and the lawyers.

[pause]

And he knows the Earp brothers, too, the tall, quiet men who have become some of the best-known people in town. He sees them often on Allen Street, and he thinks well of them.

[long-pause]

{img:boarding-house-room-night-window}
At night, when he has finished writing, Parsons blows out the lamp and lies down on his narrow bed.

[pause]

Through the window, he can hear the sounds of the town settling into the night. A piano somewhere along Allen Street. The creak of a wagon going home late. A dog barking in a yard. And beneath it all, faint and steady from the hills to the south, the low, distant thud of the engines at the mines, working on through the dark.

[pause]

Then he sleeps, and in the morning he gets up and begins again.

[long-pause]

Many years later, George Parsons' diary will become one of the most valuable records of life in Tombstone in these years.

[pause]

Because of it, we know what the weather was like on particular days, and what people talked about on the boardwalks, and how much a dinner cost, and what it felt like to be young and hopeful and far from home in a silver town in the desert.

[pause]

And because of it, we also know how the people of Tombstone saw the events of a certain cold afternoon in October 1881, which Parsons, like everyone else in town, would never forget.

[long-pause]

{img:newspaper-office-printing-press}
On Fremont Street, in a narrow wooden building, the printing press is running late into the evening.

[pause]

The room smells of ink and oil and paper. A young man stands at a tall wooden case full of tiny metal letters, picking them out one at a time and setting them into lines, very quickly, without even looking. Another man feeds sheets of paper into the press, pulls the lever, and lifts each finished page carefully away to dry.

[pause]

This is the office of the Tombstone Epitaph.

[long-pause]

The newspaper was started in the spring of 1880 by a man named John Clum.

[pause]

Clum is not yet thirty, a lively, energetic man with a round face, a quick smile and a great deal of confidence. Before he came to Tombstone, he spent several years as the government's agent at the San Carlos reservation, far to the north, where he lived and worked among the Apache people.

[pause]

When he heard about the new silver town, he bought an old printing press in Tucson, loaded it onto a wagon, and brought it here, across the desert.

[long-pause]

When people asked him what he would call his newspaper, he had a ready answer.

[pause]

Every tombstone, he said, ought to have its epitaph.

[pause]

The name made people laugh, and it stuck. The first issue appeared on the first of May, 1880, and it has been coming out ever since.

[long-pause]

{img:man-reading-newspaper-boardwalk}
In the mornings, the boys sell the Epitaph on the corners of Allen Street, and men stand on the boardwalks in the sunshine, reading it.

[pause]

It is full of everything that happens in Tombstone. News from the mines: which shaft has reached what depth, which vein looks rich, which company has paid a good dividend. News from the town: new buildings, new shops, new arrivals on the stage. Notices of dances and concerts and church suppers. Advertisements for hats and boots and medicines and mining tools.

[pause]

And there is news from the outside world, too, sent by telegraph along the wire that now runs to Tombstone, from Washington and New York and San Francisco and London.

[long-pause]

The Epitaph is not the only newspaper in town.

[pause]

There is also the Nugget, which is older, and which has a different way of seeing things. The two papers often disagree, about politics, about the town government, and about who should be in charge of keeping the peace in the new county.

[pause]

The editors write sharp remarks about each other in their columns, and the people of Tombstone read both papers with great interest, and argue about them in the saloons.

[long-pause]

A little way along Fremont Street from the newspaper office is another building that everyone in town knows.

[pause]

It is a long, low house with a gallery on the front, and a sign that says it is the photograph gallery of C. S. Fly.

[pause]

{img:fly-photo-studio-camera}
Camillus Fly is a quiet man with a neat beard, who came to Tombstone from San Francisco with his wife Mollie at the very beginning of the town.

[pause]

Inside the gallery, there is a studio with a large window in the roof to let in the light, a painted backdrop of trees and columns, a carved chair, and a big wooden camera on three legs, with a black cloth draped over the back of it.

[long-pause]

People come to Fly's studio to have their portraits made.

[pause]

A miner in his best suit, with his hair carefully combed and a watch chain across his waistcoat. A young couple who have just been married, the woman sitting in the chair and the man standing beside her with his hand on her shoulder. A mother with a baby in a long white christening gown.

[pause]

{img:portrait-sitting-family-studio}
Fly asks them to sit very still. He disappears under the black cloth, focuses the lens, slides a glass plate into the back of the camera, and takes the cap off the lens for a few seconds.

[pause]

Then he goes into his darkroom to develop the plate, and a few days later, the family comes back to collect a small, beautiful picture in a card frame, which they will keep on the mantelpiece, or send home to their relatives in the East.

[long-pause]

Fly also likes to photograph the town itself.

[pause]

He carries his camera out to the hills, to the mines, to the streets, and takes pictures of Tombstone as it grows. The hoisting works against the sky. The long view down Allen Street. A group of miners at the mouth of a shaft. A stagecoach standing ready to leave.

[pause]

Many of the photographs that we have of Tombstone in these years were taken by Camillus Fly.

[long-pause]

{img:fly-boarding-house-porch-mollie}
Next to the studio, Mollie Fly runs a boarding house.

[pause]

It is a respectable place, with clean rooms, simple furniture and good meals, and it is popular with single men who have come to town on business. Mollie is a capable, kind woman, who keeps the house in good order and the lodgers well fed.

[pause]

In the autumn of 1881, one of her lodgers is a slim, pale, very polite gentleman from Georgia, who rents a room at the back. He coughs a great deal, and he keeps unusual hours. He is a dentist by training, though he does not do much dentistry any more.

[pause]

His name is John Henry Holliday. But everyone in Tombstone calls him Doc.

[long-pause]

On the western side of the boarding house, between it and the next building along Fremont Street, there is a narrow empty lot, perhaps fifteen or twenty feet wide.

[pause]

There is nothing much in it. A little dust and a few weeds. A fence at the back, and beyond that, the rear yard of a livery stable, where horses are kept and wagons are hired.

[pause]

The stable faces onto Allen Street, on the other side of the block. It has a name that, in the autumn of 1881, means nothing at all to anyone outside Tombstone.

[pause]

It is called the O.K. Corral.

[long-pause]

{img:earp-wagons-arriving-1879}
At the very end of 1879, a little group of wagons came slowly down the road into Tombstone, from the north.

[pause]

In them were three brothers and their wives, with everything they owned. They had travelled a long way, from Dodge City in Kansas, across New Mexico, and on through the high country of Arizona, and they had come to the new silver town to make a fresh start.

[pause]

They were the Earp brothers.

[long-pause]

The Earps were a large family, with roots in Illinois and Iowa, and they had spent most of their lives moving west.

[pause]

Their father, Nicholas Earp, was a restless man who had farmed and kept stores and served as a local lawman in several different towns. In the spring of 1864, when the boys were young, he had led a wagon train all the way across the plains and mountains to California. The children had grown up on the move, used to new places and hard work.

[pause]

There were five brothers in all, and four of them would spend time in Tombstone.

[long-pause]

The oldest was James.

[pause]

James had been badly wounded in the shoulder as a young soldier in the Civil War, and he could not use his left arm very well. He was a quiet, easy-going man, who worked as a bartender and ran a small saloon. He was married to Bessie, and they had two children with them.

[pause]

James stayed out of most of the town's quarrels. He preferred to stand behind his bar, polishing glasses and listening to the talk.

[long-pause]

Next came Virgil.

[pause]

Virgil Earp was tall and broad-shouldered, with a big moustache and a calm, steady manner. He had been a soldier, a farmer, a stagecoach driver and a lawman, and he was good with people. When there was trouble, he usually tried to talk first.

[pause]

{img:virgil-allie-earp-home}
He was married to Allie, a small, lively Irish-American woman with a sharp tongue and a warm laugh. They had no children, but they were very fond of each other. Allie loved to tell stories, and in later years, when she was a very old woman, she would tell many stories about the days in Tombstone.

[pause]

Virgil had come to Arizona already holding a commission as a deputy United States marshal, which meant that he could help to enforce the federal law in the territory. In the summer of 1881, he was also made the town marshal of Tombstone, in charge of keeping the peace in the streets.

[long-pause]

Then there was Wyatt.

[pause]

Wyatt Earp was in his early thirties in 1881, tall and lean, with fair hair, a long moustache and pale blue eyes. He was a serious man, who did not drink much and did not talk much, and who seemed very calm.

[pause]

He had worked as a buffalo hunter, a stagecoach guard and a police officer in the cattle towns of Kansas, in Wichita and in Dodge City, where the cowboys came up from Texas with their great herds of cattle every summer. He knew how to keep order in a rough town, and he had a reputation for being steady and fearless.

[long-pause]

{img:oriental-saloon-faro-table}
In Tombstone, Wyatt had done several kinds of work.

[pause]

For a time, he rode beside the drivers on the Wells Fargo stagecoaches, guarding the silver on the road to Benson. For a while, he was a deputy sheriff. And he had a share in the gambling concession at the Oriental Saloon, where he sometimes sat at the faro table in a dark suit, watching the game.

[pause]

Like everyone else in town, he also bought shares in mining claims and water rights and town lots, hoping one of them would make his fortune.

[pause]

He lived with a woman called Mattie, who had been with him since Dodge City. She was quiet and shy, and she did not go out much in the town.

[long-pause]

The youngest of the Tombstone brothers was Morgan.

[pause]

Morgan Earp was about thirty, the handsomest and the most cheerful of them, with a quick smile and a love of jokes. He looked a great deal like Wyatt, and the two were very close. He had come to Tombstone in the summer of 1880, a few months after the others, and he worked at various jobs, as a guard for Wells Fargo, and as a helper to his brother Virgil when an extra man was needed.

[pause]

His wife, Louisa, lived for a while in Tombstone too. She was gentle and pretty and often unwell, and Morgan was devoted to her.

[long-pause]

{img:earp-houses-fremont-evening}
The Earp families lived close together, in small houses near the corner of Fremont and First Street, at the western edge of the town.

[pause]

The houses were simple, built of wood and adobe, with a few rooms each, a kitchen stove, and a little yard at the back where the women hung the washing. Virgil and Allie lived on one corner, and Wyatt and Mattie lived just across the street.

[pause]

The wives were often in and out of each other's houses. They cooked together, sewed together, and kept each other company while their husbands were at work. It is said that, when money was short in the early months, Allie and Mattie took in sewing, stitching heavy canvas for tents and wagon covers, sitting together on the porch in the afternoons with the cloth spread across their laps.

[long-pause]

In the town, the Earps were well known and, by many people, well liked.

[pause]

The merchants and the mine owners and the bankers, the people who wanted Tombstone to grow into a respectable city, saw them as men who could keep order. John Clum, the editor of the Epitaph and, since the beginning of 1881, the mayor of the town, was their friend, and so was George Parsons.

[pause]

But not everyone felt the same way.

[pause]

Out in the valleys beyond the town, among the ranchers and the cattlemen, there were people who did not like the Earps at all, and who did not like the way they did their work.

[pause]

Over the year 1881, the feeling between them had grown slowly worse.

[long-pause]

{img:fremont-first-street-night-quiet}
For now, though, on an ordinary autumn evening, the little houses at the corner of Fremont and First are peaceful.

[pause]

There is a light in Allie's kitchen, where she is washing the supper dishes. Across the street, Mattie is sitting by the lamp with her sewing. Somewhere in the town, a dog is barking, and a wagon is rolling home along the street.

[pause]

The night is cool and clear, and the stars are very bright above the roofs.

[long-pause]

{img:doc-holliday-card-table}
Of all the people in Tombstone in 1881, perhaps the most unusual is Doc Holliday.

[pause]

He is about thirty years old, slender and fair-haired, with a neat moustache, pale blue eyes and a thin, tired face. He dresses very well, in a grey suit and a clean white shirt, with a watch chain across his waistcoat. He speaks softly, with the slow, gentle accent of the American South, and he has the manners of a gentleman.

[pause]

He is also very ill.

[long-pause]

John Henry Holliday was born in Georgia, in a small town south of Atlanta, in 1851.

[pause]

His family was respectable and fairly well off. His father was a lawyer and a planter, and his mother was a kind, gentle woman who loved music and books, and who taught her son to play the piano and to speak properly and to be polite to everyone.

[pause]

As a boy, he lived in a large white house with a garden full of magnolia trees. He went to a good school, where he learned Latin and Greek and French. He was clever and quick, and he made friends easily.

[pause]

But when he was fifteen, his mother died of tuberculosis, the slow lung disease that people in those days called consumption. It was the great sorrow of his life.

[long-pause]

When he grew up, Holliday decided to become a dentist.

[pause]

He went north to Philadelphia, to study at a college of dental surgery, and at the age of twenty he received his degree. He went back to Georgia, and then to the city of Atlanta, where he began to work.

[pause]

He was good at it. He was patient and careful, and he had fine, steady hands.

[pause]

But not long after he began, he started to cough. And the doctors told him what he had already feared. He had the same disease that had taken his mother.

[long-pause]

In those days, there was no cure for tuberculosis.

[pause]

The doctors told him that he might have only a few months to live, but that a warm, dry climate might help. So, at the age of twenty-two, he left his family and his home and travelled west to Texas, to the city of Dallas, where he opened a dental office.

[pause]

{img:dentist-office-1870s-chair}
For a while, he worked as a dentist there. But his cough made it difficult. Patients did not like to sit in his chair while he was coughing.

[pause]

Slowly, he turned to another way of making a living. He was very good with numbers, and very calm, and he began to play cards for money.

[long-pause]

For the next several years, Doc Holliday moved from town to town across the West.

[pause]

He lived in Texas, in Colorado, in Kansas, in New Mexico. He dealt faro and played poker in saloons in Denver and Dodge City and Las Vegas. He was quick-tempered when he had been drinking, and he got into trouble more than once.

[pause]

In Fort Griffin, in Texas, he met a man who would become his closest friend. That man was Wyatt Earp.

[long-pause]

The two of them were very different. Wyatt was quiet and serious, and he did not drink. Doc was witty and restless and often unwell, and he drank a good deal of whiskey, partly, perhaps, to quiet his cough.

[pause]

But they trusted each other completely. Some say that once, in Dodge City, when Wyatt was in danger, Doc stepped in to help him, and that Wyatt never forgot it.

[pause]

When the Earps went to Tombstone, Doc soon followed them. He arrived in the autumn of 1880.

[long-pause]

{img:doc-kate-hotel-balcony}
Doc is not alone.

[pause]

For several years, he has shared his life, on and off, with a woman called Kate. She was born in Hungary, the daughter of a doctor, and she came to America as a girl. She is well educated, speaks several languages, and has a strong will and a fiery temper.

[pause]

She and Doc quarrel often and make up often. Sometimes she lives with him, and sometimes she leaves for a while and goes to another town, and then she comes back. They seem to care for each other a great deal, even when they are arguing.

[long-pause]

In Tombstone, Doc spends most of his time at the card tables.

[pause]

He plays faro and poker in the saloons on Allen Street, often late into the night, sitting very straight in his chair, with a glass of whiskey at his elbow and his cards held close. He is a very good player. He rarely seems excited, whether he is winning or losing.

[pause]

When he is not playing, he can sometimes be found reading in a quiet corner, or playing the piano in a hotel parlour, softly, the old songs his mother taught him.

[pause]

{img:hotel-parlour-piano-evening}
People in the town do not quite know what to make of him. Some find him charming, with his good manners and his clever jokes. Others are a little afraid of him, because of his temper and his reputation.

[pause]

But the Earps are his friends, and that is all that matters to Doc.

[long-pause]

In the autumn of 1881, Doc is renting a room at the back of Mollie Fly's boarding house, on Fremont Street.

[pause]

It is a small, plain room, with a bed and a chair, a washstand with a jug and a basin, and a trunk for his clothes. On cold nights, he lights a small fire in the stove and sits beside it, wrapped in a blanket, coughing softly.

[pause]

{img:boarding-room-stove-night}
Outside his window, Fremont Street is quiet. Next door is the empty lot, and beyond it, the back of the O.K. Corral, where the horses shift and stamp in their stalls in the dark.

[pause]

Doc Holliday sleeps badly, as he always does. And in the mornings, he often does not get up until long after the miners have gone to work and the shops on Allen Street have opened their doors.

[long-pause]

{img:san-pedro-valley-ranch-cattle}
Outside the town, beyond the last houses and the mine dumps, the country opens out into long, wide valleys of grass.

[pause]

To the west is the valley of the San Pedro River. To the east, beyond the Dragoon Mountains, is the Sulphur Springs Valley, flat and pale and enormous. Here and there, across these valleys, there are cattle ranches, a long way apart from one another.

[long-pause]

A ranch in southern Arizona in 1881 is a simple place.

[pause]

There is a small house of adobe, with a flat roof and a single door, and perhaps a porch of mesquite poles covered with brush to give a little shade. Behind it, there is a corral of wooden rails, a well or a spring, a shed for the saddles and tools, and a few trees, if the ranchers have been lucky.

[pause]

All around, as far as the eye can see, the cattle wander over the open range, eating the grass, looking for water, lying down in the shade of the mesquite through the heat of the day.

[long-pause]

The cowboys are mostly young men, some of them barely more than boys.

[pause]

They wear wide-brimmed hats against the sun, leather chaps over their trousers to protect their legs from the thorny brush, and boots with high heels that fit well in the stirrup. They spend almost all their waking hours on horseback, and they are wonderfully skilled riders.

[pause]

{img:cowboy-campfire-coffee-night}
When they are out on the range, they sleep under the stars, with their saddles for pillows and their horses tethered nearby. They cook their meals over a small fire, beef and beans and biscuits, and drink coffee so strong that it is said a horseshoe would float in it.

[pause]

On quiet nights, someone might sing, an old song from Texas or Missouri, softly, while the cattle settle down to sleep in the dark.

[long-pause]

Among the ranching families of the San Pedro Valley, there are two that people in Tombstone know well.

[pause]

The first is the Clanton family.

[pause]

Old Newman Clanton, the father, came to Arizona some years ago with his sons, and settled on a ranch beside the San Pedro River. He was a rough, weather-beaten old man with a long grey beard, who had spent his life wandering from one frontier to another. In the summer of 1881, while he was driving cattle through a canyon near the Mexican border, he lost his life, and now the ranch belongs to his sons.

[pause]

They are Ike, Phin and Billy.

[long-pause]

Ike Clanton is in his early thirties, a big, talkative man with a dark beard and a loud voice. He likes to drink, and when he drinks, he likes to boast.

[pause]

Billy, the youngest, is only nineteen, a slim, cheerful boy with a fresh face, who is good with horses and loves to ride.

[pause]

The Clantons raise cattle, and they buy and sell them, too. Some of the cattle they sell, it is often said in Tombstone, have come across the border from Mexico without anyone paying for them. But people say many things, and not all of them are true.

[long-pause]

The other family is the McLaurys.

[pause]

There are two brothers here, Frank and Tom. They came to Arizona from Iowa, where their father was a judge, and they have a ranch in the Sulphur Springs Valley, east of the mountains.

[pause]

{img:mclaury-brothers-ranch-porch}
Frank is the elder, in his early thirties, a lean, proud man with a quick temper. Tom is a few years younger, quieter and more easy-going, and he often comes into Tombstone to do business with the butchers.

[pause]

The McLaurys and the Clantons are friends and neighbours, in the way that people are neighbours in country where the nearest house may be twenty miles away. They work together, ride together, and stand up for one another.

[long-pause]

{img:cowboys-riding-into-town-dusk}
There are some men out in the valleys who live outside the law.

[pause]

People in Tombstone call them the cowboys, though most real cowboys are honest working men who have nothing to do with them. These are men who steal cattle across the border, and sometimes rob the stagecoaches on the roads, and who do not much care for marshals or sheriffs or judges.

[pause]

Not all of them are known by name. But some are: a big, cheerful, dangerous man called Curly Bill, and a moody, educated man called Johnny Ringo, who is said to read books in Latin.

[pause]

The ranchers of the San Pedro Valley know these men. Some are their friends. And the Earps, for their part, do not trust any of them.

[long-pause]

There is one more person who matters a great deal in this story.

[pause]

His name is Johnny Behan.

[pause]

{img:sheriff-behan-office}
Behan is a pleasant, friendly man in his late thirties, with a round face and a ready smile. He has been a politician and a lawman in other parts of Arizona, and he is good at making friends with everyone, the miners and the merchants and the ranchers alike.

[pause]

When the new county of Cochise was created at the beginning of 1881, Behan was appointed its first sheriff. It is his job to keep the peace in the whole county, outside the town as well as in it.

[long-pause]

Wyatt Earp had hoped to become sheriff himself. He did not get the job, and he and Behan have never been friends.

[pause]

The ranchers and the cowboys of the valleys like Behan. They think of him as their sheriff, who understands them. The Earps, on the other hand, are close to the townspeople, the merchants and the mine owners, and to John Clum and the Epitaph.

[pause]

So, over the course of 1881, the town and the valleys have slowly divided into two sides. On one side, the Earps. On the other, the Clantons, the McLaurys, and the sheriff, who stands somewhere in between.

[long-pause]

{img:valley-sunset-windmill-quiet}
Out on the ranches, as the sun goes down, none of this seems very important.

[pause]

The cattle drift slowly toward the water. The last light turns the grass to gold, and then to grey. A coyote calls from a ridge, and another answers it, far away.

[pause]

In the adobe house beside the river, someone lights a lamp, and the smell of cooking drifts out through the open door, into the cool blue evening.

[long-pause]

{img:october-morning-town-cool}
By October, the long, hot summer in Tombstone is over.

[pause]

The thunderstorms that rolled over the mountains every afternoon in July and August have gone. The days are still warm and bright, but the mornings are crisp now, and the nights are cold enough that people light their stoves again in the evenings, and pull an extra blanket over the bed.

[pause]

The grass on the hills has turned a pale gold. In the canyons of the mountains, the leaves of the cottonwoods and the sycamores are turning yellow.

[long-pause]

It is a pleasant time of year in the town.

[pause]

In the kitchens, the women are putting up preserves for the winter, peaches and plums and quinces, brought in by wagon from the farms along the rivers. In the stores, the merchants are setting out warm coats and wool blankets and heavy boots, and the first barrels of apples have arrived from the north.

[pause]

At the school, the children are learning about the harvest and the coming of the pilgrims, and practising songs for a little concert before Christmas.

[pause]

In the evenings, the lamps come on earlier and earlier, and the smoke from the chimneys hangs low over the roofs in the still, cold air.

[long-pause]

But under the quiet surface of the town, there is a feeling of unease.

[pause]

All through the year, there has been trouble on the roads around Tombstone. Cattle have gone missing from the ranches. Stagecoaches have been stopped by masked men, and the strongboxes taken.

[pause]

In March, on a dark evening on the road near Contention, there was an attempt to rob the stage that went badly wrong, and two men on the coach lost their lives. The Earps and Sheriff Behan rode out together in a posse to search for the robbers, but they never caught all of them, and since then there has been a great deal of bitterness and suspicion on all sides.

[long-pause]

{img:posse-riders-desert-road}
In September, another stage was robbed, this time on the road to the little mining town of Bisbee, to the south.

[pause]

This time, the Earps tracked the robbers and arrested two men, both friends of the cowboys. The cowboys were angry about it, and in the saloons, some of them said loudly that the Earps had better be careful.

[pause]

There was trouble with the Apaches, too. At the end of September, a group of Apache families left the reservation to the north, and for a few weeks, the ranchers in the valleys were nervous. Volunteers rode out from Tombstone to watch the roads, and then rode home again, dusty and tired, without having seen anything at all.

[long-pause]

So by the middle of October, there are many quarrels in the air.

[pause]

There are quarrels about the robberies, and about who knew what, and who said what to whom. There are quarrels about the sheriff, and about the town marshal, and about the newspapers. In the saloons and on the boardwalks, men talk in low voices, and look over their shoulders, and stop talking when someone walks past.

[pause]

George Parsons writes in his diary that he does not like the feeling in the town.

[long-pause]

{img:virgil-marshal-walking-boardwalk}
Virgil Earp, as town marshal, does what he can.

[pause]

He walks the streets every day, in his dark coat with his badge pinned to it, nodding to the shopkeepers and stopping to talk with people on the corners. He tries to keep the peace without fuss. He has a rule, as the town has a rule, that no one may carry a gun within the town limits. Visitors are supposed to leave their weapons at a hotel or a stable when they arrive.

[pause]

Most people obey the rule. The cowboys, mostly, do not like it.

[long-pause]

Wyatt and Morgan help their brother when they are needed, and Doc Holliday, who is loyal to the Earps above all things, is never far away.

[pause]

On the other side, Ike Clanton has been coming into town more often. He has quarrelled with Wyatt over some private business, a secret plan they once discussed together that came to nothing, and Ike is afraid that Wyatt has told other people about it.

[pause]

Ike does not trust Wyatt, and he does not trust Doc. When he drinks, which is often, he says so, to anyone who will listen.

[long-pause]

{img:tombstone-street-dusk-lamps-october}
Still, for most people in Tombstone, life goes on just as it always has.

[pause]

The miners go down into the shafts each morning and come up again each night. The stages come and go. The children go to school, the shops open and close, and on Sunday the church bells ring.

[pause]

The Epitaph prints the news from the mines, and the advertisements for winter coats, and a notice that a travelling theatre company will be coming soon to Schieffelin Hall.

[pause]

Nellie Cashman serves breakfast to forty men at her long tables. Camillus Fly takes portraits in his studio, and Mollie keeps her boarding house clean and quiet. In his room at the back, Doc Holliday sleeps late, and coughs.

[long-pause]

{img:full-moon-over-dragoon-mountains}
In the evenings, the moon rises over the Dragoon Mountains, large and pale, and lights up the whole wide valley in silver.

[pause]

On some of those nights, it is so bright that a person could almost read a newspaper by it. The town lies quiet beneath it, its roofs and chimneys and false fronts throwing long blue shadows across the dusty streets.

[pause]

Somewhere a horse whinnies. A door closes. A lamp goes out in a window on Fremont Street.

[long-pause]

The weather changes toward the end of the month.

[pause]

A cold wind begins to blow down from the north, raw and gusty, rattling the signs on Allen Street and sending little whirls of dust and leaves along the boardwalks. The sky turns grey. People go about wrapped in their coats, holding their hats, with their collars turned up against the cold.

[pause]

Some say they can feel snow in the air.

[pause]

And on Tuesday, the twenty-fifth of October 1881, in the late afternoon, a light wagon comes rattling up the road into Tombstone from the valley, with two men sitting in it.

[pause]

They are Ike Clanton and Tom McLaury.

[long-pause]

{img:wagon-arriving-town-evening-wind}
Ike Clanton and Tom McLaury have come to Tombstone on business.

[pause]

Tom has some beef to sell to the butchers in town, and some money to collect. Ike has come along for the company, and for the saloons.

[pause]

They leave their wagon and their team at a stable, as the rules require, and walk out into the evening along Allen Street, with their coat collars turned up against the wind.

[long-pause]

The town is busy, as it always is in the evening.

[pause]

The lamps are lit in the windows, and the restaurants are full. Men are standing in little groups on the boardwalks, talking and smoking. From the saloons comes the clink of glasses and the soft murmur of voices and the music of a piano.

[pause]

Ike and Tom go from one saloon to another. They see friends, and have a drink or two, and talk about the cattle business and the price of beef.

[long-pause]

{img:alhambra-lunch-counter-night}
Late that night, a little after midnight, Ike goes into the lunch room at the back of the Alhambra Saloon, to get something to eat.

[pause]

It is a small, warm room, with a long counter and a row of stools, and a cook frying eggs and steak over a stove at the back. At this hour, it is full of men who have been out late, ordering coffee and food before they go to bed.

[pause]

Ike sits down at the counter. A few minutes later, Doc Holliday comes in.

[long-pause]

Doc has been drinking, and he does not like Ike Clanton at all.

[pause]

He has heard the things that Ike has been saying about him and about the Earps. He walks up to Ike at the counter and tells him so, loudly, in front of everyone in the room.

[pause]

Ike says something back. The cook stops frying. The men at the counter put down their forks and watch.

[pause]

Morgan Earp is there too, sitting at the end of the counter. He gets up and stands nearby, but he does not interfere.

[long-pause]

The quarrel moves out of the lunch room and into the street.

[pause]

Doc and Ike stand on the boardwalk in the cold wind, under the light of a lamp, still arguing. Wyatt Earp arrives. Then Virgil comes along, in his role as town marshal, and tells them all to stop it and go home, or he will arrest every one of them.

[pause]

Doc goes off, still angry, toward his room at Fly's. Ike goes off in the other direction, muttering. The few people who stopped to watch drift away again into the night.

[pause]

And for a little while, it seems that the trouble is over.

[long-pause]

But nobody in this story is ready to go to bed.

[pause]

Not long afterwards, a little group of men sits down to a game of poker at a table in the Occidental Saloon, further along Allen Street.

[pause]

{img:poker-game-late-night-lamp}
Around the table, under a hanging lamp, are Ike Clanton and Tom McLaury, Sheriff Johnny Behan, Virgil Earp, and another man or two. It seems a strange group of players, after everything that has happened. But in Tombstone in 1881, men who disagree about almost everything will still sit down together to play cards.

[pause]

They play all through the night.

[pause]

The cards are dealt and gathered and dealt again. The chips click softly on the green cloth. The lamp burns lower. Outside, the wind blows along the empty street, and the town grows quiet around them.

[long-pause]

Virgil Earp keeps his revolver on his lap under the table during the game. Ike notices it, and it worries him.

[pause]

Around dawn, the game comes to an end, and the players stand up, stiff and tired, and push their chairs back from the table.

[pause]

Virgil goes home to bed. Behan goes home to bed. Tom McLaury goes off to find his room.

[pause]

But Ike Clanton does not go to bed at all.

[long-pause]

{img:grey-dawn-allen-street-empty}
It is now the morning of Wednesday, the twenty-sixth of October.

[pause]

The dawn comes up grey and cold over the Dragoon Mountains. There are heavy clouds across the sky, and the wind is still blowing, sharp and raw, and some people later remember little flakes of snow drifting down in the gusts.

[pause]

In the town, the ordinary morning begins. The bakers are at their ovens. The milk wagons come in. The mine whistles blow, and the morning shift walks up the hill to the shafts, with their coats buttoned tight against the wind.

[long-pause]

Ike Clanton walks the streets, tired and cold and angry, still full of the night's whiskey and the night's quarrel.

[pause]

He tells a bartender that the Earps and Doc Holliday will have to fight him when they show themselves on the street. He tells another man the same thing. He goes from one saloon to another, saying it to anyone who will listen.

[pause]

Some people take him seriously. Others shake their heads. Ike has talked like this before, and nothing ever came of it.

[long-pause]

{img:virgil-sleeping-morning-room}
At the little house on the corner of Fremont and First, Virgil Earp is asleep, after his long night at the card table.

[pause]

Allie has kept the house quiet for him all morning. She moves softly around the kitchen, making coffee, so as not to wake him.

[pause]

But late in the morning, there is a knock at the door. One of the town policemen has come to tell Virgil that Ike Clanton is out on the street, carrying a rifle and a revolver, and looking for the Earps.

[pause]

Virgil gets up, gets dressed, puts on his coat, and goes out into the cold.

[long-pause]

{img:fourth-street-cold-midday}
It is a little after noon, and the wind is still blowing hard along the streets of Tombstone.

[pause]

Virgil Earp walks up Fourth Street with his brother Morgan beside him. They are looking for Ike Clanton, and before long, they find him, standing near the corner, with a rifle in his hands and a revolver tucked into his belt.

[long-pause]

Virgil comes up quietly behind him and takes hold of the rifle.

[pause]

There is a brief struggle on the boardwalk. Virgil is the stronger man, and in a moment it is over. He takes the rifle and the revolver away from Ike, and then he arrests him for carrying weapons in the town, and walks him off down the street to the court.

[pause]

A few people on the boardwalk stop and stare. A woman pulls her child closer to her skirts. Then they go on with their business.

[long-pause]

{img:small-courtroom-justice-desk}
The courtroom is a small, plain room with a wooden floor, a few benches, and a desk at the front where the justice of the peace sits.

[pause]

The judge is not there at first, and so they must wait for him. Ike sits on a bench with his hat in his hands, red-faced and angry. Morgan stands by the door. Wyatt arrives, and stands near his brother.

[pause]

The room is cold. Outside, the wind rattles the windows.

[long-pause]

While they wait, Ike and the Earps argue.

[pause]

Ike says that if he had seen Virgil coming, things would have gone differently. Wyatt tells him that if he wants a fight, he can have one, anywhere he likes. Ike says he will see them later.

[pause]

Then the judge arrives, and listens to Virgil, and fines Ike twenty-five dollars for carrying weapons inside the town limits. Ike pays the fine. His rifle and revolver are taken away and left at a hotel, where he can collect them when he leaves town.

[long-pause]

{img:wyatt-leaving-courtroom-street}
Wyatt walks out of the courtroom, and on the boardwalk outside, he meets Tom McLaury.

[pause]

Tom has heard about Ike's arrest and has come to see what is happening. He and Wyatt exchange a few sharp words. Wyatt is angry now, and tired of all of it. There is a brief scuffle on the boardwalk, and Wyatt strikes Tom, who falls into the street.

[pause]

Then Wyatt walks away, toward a tobacconist's shop, to buy a cigar.

[pause]

Tom picks himself up, and brushes off his coat, and goes off slowly down the street.

[long-pause]

Up to this moment, the trouble has been between Ike and Tom on one side, and the Earps and Doc on the other, and the whole town has known about it.

[pause]

But at around half past one in the afternoon, two more men ride into Tombstone from the east.

[pause]

{img:two-riders-arriving-grand-hotel}
They are Frank McLaury, Tom's older brother, and young Billy Clanton, Ike's brother. They have come in from the ranches, perhaps to help with the cattle business, or perhaps because they have heard there is trouble and want to bring their brothers home.

[pause]

They stop at the Grand Hotel on Allen Street and go inside. And there, someone tells them what has happened. Ike has been arrested. Tom has been knocked down in the street.

[long-pause]

Frank and Billy leave the hotel and go to find their brothers.

[pause]

They meet Tom and Ike, and the four of them walk to a gun shop on Fourth Street. Frank's horse wanders up onto the boardwalk outside the shop, and Wyatt, who is passing, takes it by the reins and leads it back into the street.

[pause]

Inside the shop, the young men buy cartridges for their revolvers. Wyatt watches them through the window. Then he walks on.

[long-pause]

{img:ok-corral-stable-yard}
A little later, the four men walk to the O.K. Corral, on Allen Street, where Billy and Frank have left their horses.

[pause]

They stand for a while in the yard among the horses, talking. Billy Claiborne, a young friend of theirs, joins them. People passing on the street notice them there and hear some of what they are saying, and what they hear makes them uneasy.

[pause]

Then the little group walks out of the back of the corral, through to Fremont Street, and stops in the narrow empty lot beside Fly's boarding house.

[long-pause]

Throughout the early afternoon, people keep coming to Virgil Earp to tell him what they have seen.

[pause]

A man stops him on the corner. Another comes into the saloon where he is standing. The cowboys are gathering, they say. They are armed. They are saying things about the Earps.

[pause]

Virgil listens quietly. He does not want a fight. He knows that the cowboys might simply be getting ready to leave town, and that if he leaves them alone, they may ride away, and all of this will blow over.

[pause]

But the whole town is watching now. Along Allen Street and Fremont Street, people are standing in the doorways and at the windows, and talking in low voices on the corners. Some of the leading men of the town have formed a citizens' committee, and they tell Virgil that they will help him if he needs it.

[pause]

Virgil thanks them, and says he will handle it himself.

[long-pause]

At about half past two, Sheriff Johnny Behan is having a shave at a barber's shop.

[pause]

He hears the news, gets up from the chair with the towel still around his neck, and hurries off to find the cowboys. He finds them in the lot on Fremont Street, and he talks to them there for some minutes, trying to persuade them to give up their guns.

[pause]

They do not agree. Some of them say that they are just leaving town, and some of them say that they will give up their guns only if the Earps give up theirs.

[long-pause]

{img:earps-gathering-hafford-corner}
Meanwhile, Virgil Earp is standing on the corner of Fourth and Allen Street, outside a saloon, with his brothers Wyatt and Morgan.

[pause]

Doc Holliday has joined them. He is wearing a long grey overcoat against the cold, and he has his walking stick in his hand.

[pause]

They stand together on the corner, in the wind, waiting, while the people of Tombstone watch them from the windows.

[pause]

Then Virgil makes his decision. He will go down to Fremont Street, and he will take the cowboys' guns away from them, as the law of the town requires.

[pause]

He hands Doc a short shotgun, and Doc slips it under his long coat. And Virgil takes Doc's walking stick, and holds it in his right hand.

[long-pause]

{img:four-men-walking-fremont-street}
It is a few minutes before three o'clock in the afternoon.

[pause]

The four men turn the corner from Fourth Street and begin to walk west along Fremont Street, side by side, at an ordinary, unhurried pace.

[pause]

Virgil is on the right, nearest the buildings, with the walking stick in his hand. Wyatt is beside him. Morgan is next, and Doc Holliday is a step or two behind, on the outside, close to the middle of the street, with his long grey coat flapping in the wind.

[long-pause]

The street is very quiet now.

[pause]

The wind blows little clouds of dust along it, and somewhere a sign creaks on its hinges. The people who were standing on the corners a few minutes ago have stepped back into the doorways. Faces watch from the windows. A man on a wagon pulls up his horses and waits.

[pause]

The four men walk past the post office, and past the front of a butcher's shop, and on toward the corner where Fly's gallery stands.

[long-pause]

Sheriff Behan sees them coming, and hurries up the street to meet them.

[pause]

He stops in front of Virgil and speaks to him, quickly and urgently. He tells him not to go down there. Some people later remember that he also said that he had disarmed the cowboys, or that he was going to.

[pause]

Virgil hears this, and believes, perhaps, that the cowboys are no longer armed. He moves the walking stick into his right hand, and leaves his own revolver in his belt, on the left side, where it will not look like a threat.

[pause]

Then the four men walk on.

[long-pause]

They come to the edge of the narrow lot beside Fly's boarding house.

[pause]

Inside, a few yards away, the cowboys are standing in a loose line: Ike Clanton, Billy Clanton, Tom and Frank McLaury, and young Billy Claiborne. A horse stands beside them, with its reins hanging loose.

[pause]

For a moment, the two groups simply look at each other across the little empty space, in the cold grey light, with the wind blowing between them.

[long-pause]

Virgil raises the walking stick in the air and speaks.

[pause]

He tells them to throw up their hands. He tells them that he has come to take their guns.

[pause]

What happened in the next few seconds has been argued about ever since. People who were there, or who watched from the windows nearby, told different stories, and the stories have never quite agreed. Someone moved. Someone reached. Virgil called out that he did not mean that.

[pause]

{img:distant-view-street-smoke-haze}
And then, suddenly, there was the sound of shots.

[long-pause]

Up and down Fremont Street, the people of Tombstone heard it.

[pause]

To some of them, it sounded at first like firecrackers, a quick, sharp crackling, many little sounds very close together. To others, it sounded like a door being slammed again and again in the wind.

[pause]

In the houses along the street, people stepped back from the windows. On Allen Street, a block away, men stopped in the middle of the boardwalk and turned their heads to listen. In Fly's boarding house, Mollie Fly was at home, and so was her husband, and they kept away from the windows.

[pause]

The sound went on for a short time, and then it stopped.

[long-pause]

It had lasted only about thirty seconds.

[pause]

When it was over, there was a little drifting haze of grey smoke over the empty lot and the street in front of it, slowly blowing away on the wind. There was the sound of a horse running off down the street. And for a moment, there was almost complete silence.

[pause]

{img:quiet-lot-picket-fence-afternoon}
Three of the young men from the ranches had lost their lives: Billy Clanton, who was only nineteen, and Tom and Frank McLaury.

[pause]

Ike Clanton was not among them. When the shooting began, he had run from the lot, through the door of Fly's boarding house, and out the other side, and away down the street. Young Billy Claiborne had run too. Neither of them was hurt.

[long-pause]

Of the four men who had walked down Fremont Street, three had been wounded.

[pause]

Virgil had been hit in the leg, and Morgan across the shoulders. Doc Holliday had a graze on his hip, where a bullet had struck the holster of his revolver.

[pause]

Wyatt Earp was standing in the street, completely unhurt.

[long-pause]

{img:townspeople-gathering-street}
Almost at once, people began to come out of the buildings.

[pause]

They came slowly at first, and then all at once, from every door along the street. Men ran from Allen Street and from the stores on Fourth Street. Up on the hills, someone set the mine whistles blowing, as a signal to the men of the citizens' committee, and the long, low sound came rolling down over the town.

[pause]

Within a few minutes, there was a crowd on Fremont Street, standing in silence around the little lot.

[long-pause]

Camillus Fly came out of his gallery and stood on the boardwalk, looking at what had happened next to his house.

[pause]

Doctors came, with their black bags, and knelt in the dust. Some of the townspeople fetched blankets, and some fetched water. The young men from the ranches were carried gently into a small house nearby, and Virgil and Morgan were helped into a carriage to be taken home.

[pause]

{img:carriage-carrying-wounded-home}
Sheriff Behan came up to Wyatt and told him that he would have to arrest him.

[pause]

Wyatt looked at him for a long moment, and said that he would not be arrested today. And Behan, after a pause, let him go.

[long-pause]

In the little house at the corner of Fremont and First, Allie Earp had heard the shots and the whistles.

[pause]

She ran out into the street without her hat or her shawl, and met the carriage as it came up the road. Virgil was sitting in it, pale and in pain, but alive. He smiled at her and told her he was all right.

[pause]

Some of the men carried him inside and laid him on the bed, and a doctor came to look at his leg. Morgan was brought in too, and laid on another bed in the same house, so that the family could look after both of them together.

[long-pause]

{img:house-window-lamp-evening-wind}
As the afternoon faded into evening, the town grew strangely quiet.

[pause]

The saloons on Allen Street were half empty. People stayed home, and talked in low voices around their kitchen tables. Armed men from the citizens' committee walked the streets in pairs, and some of them stood guard all night outside the Earp houses.

[pause]

In the window of Virgil's house, a lamp burned until morning. Allie sat beside the bed, holding her husband's hand, listening to the wind.

[pause]

And in his room that night, George Parsons opened his diary and wrote a few short lines: about the cold wind, about the great excitement in the town, and about his worry that there were hard times ahead.

[long-pause]

{img:morning-after-quiet-town}
The next morning, the wind had dropped, and the sun came up pale and cold over the Dragoon Mountains.

[pause]

The town of Tombstone woke slowly. The bakers lit their ovens as usual, and the milk wagons came in from the dairies, and the mine whistles blew for the morning shift. But on the boardwalks, people stood in little groups, talking quietly, and there was only one subject that anyone wanted to talk about.

[long-pause]

The Epitaph and the Nugget both printed long accounts of what had happened.

[pause]

They told the story very differently. The Epitaph, John Clum's paper, said that the marshal and his brothers had been doing their duty. The Nugget was not so sure. People bought both papers and read them closely, standing on the corners in the cold, and then argued about them in the saloons for the rest of the day.

[pause]

It was the beginning of an argument that would go on for more than a hundred years.

[long-pause]

{img:funeral-procession-brass-band}
A day or two later, there was a funeral for the three young men from the ranches.

[pause]

It was one of the largest the town had ever seen. A brass band led the way, playing slowly, and behind it came the hearses, drawn by horses with black plumes, and then a long line of mourners on foot and in carriages. Several hundred people walked in the procession, and many more lined the streets in silence to watch it pass.

[pause]

The procession moved slowly along Allen Street and out of the town, to the little cemetery on a rocky slope to the west, where the three were laid to rest side by side, under the wide grey sky.

[long-pause]

Many of the townspeople had liked young Billy Clanton, with his fresh face and his love of horses, and Tom McLaury, who came in so often to sell beef to the butchers. Their friends from the valleys were angry and full of grief.

[pause]

Ike Clanton went to the courts and accused the Earps and Doc Holliday of murder.

[pause]

So, at the end of October, a hearing began before a justice of the peace named Wells Spicer, to decide whether there should be a trial.

[pause]

It went on for a whole month. Day after day, the little courtroom was crowded with people. Witness after witness stood up and told what they had seen or heard on that cold afternoon on Fremont Street, and the stories did not agree. Wyatt Earp read out a long, careful statement of his own.

[long-pause]

At the end of November, Judge Spicer gave his decision.

[pause]

He said that he could not be sure exactly what had happened in those few seconds in the lot beside Fly's. But he believed that the marshal had been doing his duty, and that he had asked his brothers and Doc Holliday to help him. There would be no trial.

[pause]

The Earps and Doc were free.

[pause]

But the bitterness in the town and in the valleys did not go away. It only grew deeper.

[long-pause]

{img:winter-tombstone-frost-roofs}
Winter came to Tombstone.

[pause]

The days were short and bright, the nights were very cold, and in the mornings there was frost on the roofs and on the boardwalks. Sometimes a little snow fell on the mountains, and lay white on the peaks of the Dragoons and the Huachucas.

[pause]

At Christmas, there were parties and dances and church services, as there were every year. Just after Christmas, a new variety theatre opened on Allen Street, a long, narrow building with little boxes hanging along its walls, like birdcages, where people could sit and watch the show. They called it the Bird Cage Theatre.

[long-pause]

But that winter was not a peaceful one for the Earp family.

[pause]

Just after Christmas, Virgil was walking along Allen Street late one night when he was ambushed in the dark and badly hurt. He lived, but he never had the full use of his left arm again.

[pause]

And in March, on a rainy night, Morgan was playing billiards in a saloon on Allen Street, laughing with his friends, when someone fired through the glass of a back door, from the dark alley outside. Morgan, the youngest and most cheerful of the brothers, did not survive the night.

[long-pause]

{img:train-station-family-leaving}
After that, the Earp family decided that it was time to leave Tombstone.

[pause]

Morgan was sent home to the family in California, to be buried. Virgil and Allie went with him, on the train, with James and the other wives. Wyatt and Doc and a few friends stayed behind for a little while, and rode out into the hills after the men they believed had attacked the brothers.

[pause]

Then, in the spring of 1882, they too left Arizona, riding north and east into New Mexico, and on to Colorado.

[pause]

None of the Earp brothers ever lived in Tombstone again.

[long-pause]

{img:spring-desert-flowers-town-distance}
In Tombstone, the spring came, as it always did.

[pause]

The desert turned green after the winter rains, and then bright with flowers, yellow and orange and purple, all across the hills. The mines went on working. The stagecoaches came and went. New people arrived every week, who had never met the Earps or the Clantons, and who knew about them only from the stories they heard in the saloons.

[pause]

Nellie Cashman went on serving breakfasts at her long tables. Camillus Fly went on taking portraits. George Parsons went on writing in his diary every night.

[pause]

And slowly, the events of that cold afternoon in October began to turn from news into memory, and from memory into legend.

[long-pause]

{img:mine-pumps-steam-engine-house}
In the years after 1881, Tombstone went on growing for a while.

[pause]

The mines dug deeper and deeper, and the water that had first appeared in the lowest tunnels kept rising. The mining companies built enormous steam pumps, the largest in the whole territory, housed in tall brick buildings on the hill, and for some years they kept the tunnels dry, pumping thousands of gallons of water out of the ground every minute, day and night.

[long-pause]

A grand new courthouse was built for Cochise County, a handsome building of red brick with tall windows and a little tower on top. Churches and schools were finished. The streets were lined with shade trees. For a few years, Tombstone was one of the largest towns in the whole Southwest.

[pause]

{img:red-brick-courthouse-1882}
But there were hard times, too. Another great fire swept through the business district in 1882. The price of silver began to fall. And in 1886, the pumping works at the Grand Central mine caught fire and burned to the ground.

[pause]

Without the pumps, the water rose quickly in the tunnels, and many of the mines could no longer be worked.

[long-pause]

Slowly, people began to leave.

[pause]

The miners went off to other camps, in Arizona and Nevada and Colorado and Mexico. The merchants packed up their goods and followed them. Houses stood empty, and stores closed, and the grass began to grow in some of the side streets.

[pause]

The town did not disappear entirely, as so many mining towns did. A few hundred people stayed on, and then a few hundred more came back, and the town held on, quietly, year after year.

[pause]

People began to call it the town too tough to die.

[long-pause]

{img:ed-schieffelin-cabin-oregon-woods}
Ed Schieffelin, the man who started it all, never stopped prospecting.

[pause]

He became a rich man, and he married, and for a time he lived in a fine house in California. But he could not stay still for very long. He travelled to Alaska, and paddled up the great Yukon River looking for gold, and wandered through the mountains of Nevada and Oregon.

[pause]

In 1897, he was found in his little cabin in the forests of Oregon, where he had passed away quietly, alone, with a sack of promising rock samples beside him. He was about fifty.

[pause]

He had asked in his will to be buried near Tombstone, in the hills where he had made his first discovery, dressed in his old prospecting clothes, with his pick and his canteen beside him. And that is exactly what was done.

[long-pause]

Nellie Cashman stayed in Tombstone for several more years.

[pause]

When her sister passed away, Nellie took in her five young nephews and nieces and raised them as her own children. Then, when the mines began to close, she followed the next rush, and the next. She went to Mexico, to Montana, and finally north to the Klondike and to Alaska, where she ran stores and prospected for gold in the snow, far above the Arctic Circle, until she was nearly eighty years old.

[pause]

She was still kind, still cheerful, still helping anyone who needed it. People in the north loved her just as much as the people of Tombstone had.

[long-pause]

John Clum left Tombstone in 1882 and worked for many years for the post office, travelling all over the country, and even to Alaska. George Parsons moved to Los Angeles, where he became a well-known businessman, and he went on writing his diary almost to the end of his long life.

[pause]

Camillus Fly stayed on in Tombstone and became famous for his photographs. In 1886, he travelled into the mountains of Mexico and made a remarkable series of pictures of the Apache leader Geronimo and his people, at a meeting with the army, which are still among the most important photographs of the American West.

[long-pause]

{img:colton-california-orange-groves}
Virgil and Allie Earp went to live in the little town of Colton, in southern California, among the orange groves. Virgil, despite his injured arm, became the town's first marshal. Later, he and Allie travelled together from one mining camp to another, and they stayed together, happily, until Virgil passed away in Nevada in 1905.

[pause]

Allie lived on for many more years, until 1947. In her old age, she liked to sit and tell stories about the old days in Tombstone, and she always spoke of Virgil with great affection.

[long-pause]

Doc Holliday went to Colorado, where he lived quietly for a few more years in the mountain towns, playing cards and growing gradually weaker.

[pause]

{img:glenwood-springs-hotel-mountains}
In the end, he went to Glenwood Springs, a little town high in the Rocky Mountains, with hot springs that people hoped would help his lungs. He passed away there in a hotel room, in November 1887, at the age of thirty-six. He had lived for many years longer than the doctors in Atlanta had expected.

[pause]

Kate lived on until 1940, and spent her last years in a home for old Arizona pioneers, in the town of Prescott.

[long-pause]

Wyatt Earp lived the longest of them all.

[pause]

He travelled for many years, to Idaho and San Francisco, and north to Alaska during the great gold rush, where he ran a saloon in the town of Nome. He was accompanied all that time by Josephine Marcus, a lively young woman he had first met in Tombstone, who became his wife and stayed with him for nearly fifty years.

[pause]

{img:wyatt-josephine-older-desert-camp}
In his later years, they lived quietly in Los Angeles, and spent the winters at a little mining claim in the California desert, where Wyatt liked to sit in the sun outside their small cottage and look at the mountains.

[pause]

He made friends with some of the first cowboy actors in Hollywood, who came to hear his stories. When he passed away in January 1929, at the age of eighty, some of those actors carried his coffin. So did John Clum, his old friend from Tombstone.

[long-pause]

By then, the story of that October afternoon had already begun to grow.

[pause]

Books were written about it. Then films, and later, television programmes, many of them, each a little different from the last. Wyatt Earp became one of the most famous lawmen in the world, and the little stable on Allen Street gave its name to the whole event, even though it had really happened in a narrow empty lot on the other side of the block.

[pause]

People around the world began to call it the Gunfight at the O.K. Corral.

[long-pause]

{img:tombstone-today-allen-street-boardwalk}
Tombstone is still there today, on its high, dry mesa in the corner of Arizona.

[pause]

It is a small town now, home to about thirteen hundred people. But the old streets are still laid out just as they were in 1881, in their neat grid, with Toughnut Street to the south and Allen Street and Fremont Street running through the middle.

[long-pause]

Along Allen Street, the wooden boardwalks and the awnings on their slender posts are still there.

[pause]

Many of the old buildings have survived, or have been carefully rebuilt, and visitors walk along the street just as the miners and the merchants once did, looking into the windows and stopping to read the old painted signs. A stagecoach pulled by a team of horses rattles slowly up and down the street, carrying passengers. The Bird Cage Theatre still stands, its little boxes still hanging along the walls.

[pause]

Schieffelin Hall is still on Fremont Street, the great adobe walls as solid as ever. And the Tombstone Epitaph is still being printed, more than a hundred and forty years after John Clum brought his old press across the desert.

[long-pause]

{img:courthouse-museum-today}
The red brick courthouse, built the year after the gunfight, is now a museum.

[pause]

Inside, in the old rooms with their tall windows, there are photographs by Camillus Fly, and maps of the mines, and old documents and letters. There are miners' candleholders and lunch pails, ladies' fans and hats, a faro box, and a dentist's tools from the 1880s.

[pause]

Visitors walk slowly from room to room, reading the names and the dates, and looking into the faces of the people in the old photographs, who look calmly back at them across the years.

[long-pause]

At the place on Fremont Street where the gunfight happened, there is now a little museum, with a reconstruction of Fly's photograph gallery beside it.

[pause]

In the narrow lot, there are life-size figures of the men who stood there on that cold afternoon in 1881, placed where the witnesses said they stood. Visitors come from all over the world to stand among them for a moment, quietly, and look at how small and ordinary the place really is: a few yards of dusty ground between two plain buildings, in a little town in the desert.

[long-pause]

On the western edge of the town, on a rocky slope beside the highway, is the old cemetery.

[pause]

People call it Boothill now. The graves are marked with simple wooden boards and piles of stones, scattered among the cactus and the mesquite. Some of the boards bear names, and some bear only a few words, or nothing at all.

[pause]

{img:boothill-graveyard-evening-light}
Billy Clanton and Tom and Frank McLaury are still there, side by side, where they were laid to rest after the great funeral in October 1881. Visitors walk along the narrow paths between the graves, and sometimes leave a few flowers or a small stone.

[pause]

In the evening, when the sun goes down behind the Whetstones, the light falls golden and low across the hillside, and the shadows of the old grave markers stretch out long over the stony ground.

[long-pause]

A few miles west of the town, out in the empty hills, there is a tall stone monument, built of rough rocks piled up in the shape of the markers that prospectors used to put at the corners of their claims.

[pause]

This is the grave of Ed Schieffelin.

[pause]

{img:schieffelin-monument-hills-sunset}
It stands quite alone, on a little rise in the grassland, with the wind blowing around it and the wide sky above. From there, a person can look out over the very hills where, in 1877, a lonely man with a mule found a streak of silver in a piece of rock, and gave it a name that he had been told in jest.

[pause]

It is very quiet there. Only the wind, and the grass, and sometimes a hawk circling high overhead.

[long-pause]

Back in the town, on Toughnut Street, there is a small museum in an old adobe boarding house, and behind it, in the courtyard, grows a rose bush.

[pause]

It was planted in 1885 by a young bride from Scotland, who had brought the cutting with her all the way from home. Over the years, it has grown and grown, until now it is said to be one of the largest rose bushes in the world, spreading out over a wooden frame above the whole courtyard, like a great green roof.

[pause]

{img:rose-tree-courtyard-white-blossoms}
In the spring, usually in April, it covers itself with millions of tiny white flowers, and people come from far away just to sit beneath it in the soft, scented shade.

[long-pause]

So much has changed since 1881.

[pause]

The silver mines are long closed. The miners and the merchants, the cowboys and the lawmen, the children who sat at their slates in the little schoolhouse, have all gone. The stagecoaches no longer bring the mail, and nobody sells water from barrels in the streets.

[pause]

But the land around the town is very much as it was. The grass still turns gold in the autumn. The Dragoon Mountains still glow pink in the evening light. And on clear nights, the stars still come out over the mesa, thick and bright, from one edge of the desert to the other, just as they did when Ed Schieffelin lay beside his little fire and dreamed of silver.

[long-pause]

{img:tombstone-night-stars-desert-slot}
And so we leave Tombstone for tonight, a little town on a high mesa, with its lamps shining softly along Allen Street, and the cool desert wind blowing in from the hills, under a great sky full of stars.
