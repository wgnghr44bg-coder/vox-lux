The next morning, the wind had dropped, and the sun came up pale and cold over the Dragoon Mountains.

The town of Tombstone woke slowly. The bakers lit their ovens as usual, and the milk wagons came in from the dairies, and the mine whistles blew for the morning shift. But on the boardwalks, people stood in little groups, talking quietly, and there was only one subject that anyone wanted to talk about.

--

The Epitaph and the Nugget both printed long accounts of what had happened.

They told the story very differently. The Epitaph, John Clum's paper, said that the marshal and his brothers had been doing their duty. The Nugget was not so sure. People bought both papers and read them closely, standing on the corners in the cold, and then argued about them in the saloons for the rest of the day.

It was the beginning of an argument that would go on for more than a hundred years.

--

{img:funeral-procession-brass-band}
A day or two later, there was a funeral for the three young men from the ranches.

It was one of the largest the town had ever seen. A brass band led the way, playing slowly, and behind it came the hearses, drawn by horses with black plumes, and then a long line of mourners on foot and in carriages. Several hundred people walked in the procession, and many more lined the streets in silence to watch it pass.

The procession moved slowly along Allen Street and out of the town, to the little cemetery on a rocky slope to the west, where the three were laid to rest side by side, under the wide grey sky.

--

Many of the townspeople had liked young Billy Clanton, with his fresh face and his love of horses, and Tom McLaury, who came in so often to sell beef to the butchers. Their friends from the valleys were angry and full of grief.

Ike Clanton went to the courts and accused the Earps and Doc Holliday of murder.

So, at the end of October, a hearing began before a justice of the peace named Wells Spicer, to decide whether there should be a trial.

It went on for a whole month. Day after day, the little courtroom was crowded with people. Witness after witness stood up and told what they had seen or heard on that cold afternoon on Fremont Street, and the stories did not agree. Wyatt Earp read out a long, careful statement of his own.

--

At the end of November, Judge Spicer gave his decision.

He said that he could not be sure exactly what had happened in those few seconds in the lot beside Fly's. But he believed that the marshal had been doing his duty, and that he had asked his brothers and Doc Holliday to help him. There would be no trial.

The Earps and Doc were free.

But the bitterness in the town and in the valleys did not go away. It only grew deeper.

--

{img:winter-tombstone-frost-roofs}
Winter came to Tombstone.

The days were short and bright, the nights were very cold, and in the mornings there was frost on the roofs and on the boardwalks. Sometimes a little snow fell on the mountains, and lay white on the peaks of the Dragoons and the Huachucas.

At Christmas, there were parties and dances and church services, as there were every year. Just after Christmas, a new variety theatre opened on Allen Street, a long, narrow building with little boxes hanging along its walls, like birdcages, where people could sit and watch the show. They called it the Bird Cage Theatre.

--

But that winter was not a peaceful one for the Earp family.

Just after Christmas, Virgil was walking along Allen Street late one night when he was ambushed in the dark and badly hurt. He lived, but he never had the full use of his left arm again.

And in March, on a rainy night, Morgan was playing billiards in a saloon on Allen Street, laughing with his friends, when someone fired through the glass of a back door, from the dark alley outside. Morgan, the youngest and most cheerful of the brothers, did not survive the night.

--

{img:train-station-family-leaving}
After that, the Earp family decided that it was time to leave Tombstone.

Morgan was sent home to the family in California, to be buried. Virgil and Allie went with him, on the train, with James and the other wives. Wyatt and Doc and a few friends stayed behind for a little while, and rode out into the hills after the men they believed had attacked the brothers.

Then, in the spring of 1882, they too left Arizona, riding north and east into New Mexico, and on to Colorado.

None of the Earp brothers ever lived in Tombstone again.

--

{img:spring-desert-flowers-town-distance}
In Tombstone, the spring came, as it always did.

The desert turned green after the winter rains, and then bright with flowers, yellow and orange and purple, all across the hills. The mines went on working. The stagecoaches came and went. New people arrived every week, who had never met the Earps or the Clantons, and who knew about them only from the stories they heard in the saloons.

Nellie Cashman went on serving breakfasts at her long tables. Camillus Fly went on taking portraits. George Parsons went on writing in his diary every night.

And slowly, the events of that cold afternoon in October began to turn from news into memory, and from memory into legend.
