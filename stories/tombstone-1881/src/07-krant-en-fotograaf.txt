On Fremont Street, in a narrow wooden building, the printing press is running late into the evening.

The room smells of ink and oil and paper. A young man stands at a tall wooden case full of tiny metal letters, picking them out one at a time and setting them into lines, very quickly, without even looking. Another man feeds sheets of paper into the press, pulls the lever, and lifts each finished page carefully away to dry.

This is the office of the Tombstone Epitaph.

--

The newspaper was started in the spring of 1880 by a man named John Clum.

Clum is not yet thirty, a lively, energetic man with a round face, a quick smile and a great deal of confidence. Before he came to Tombstone, he spent several years as the government's agent at the San Carlos reservation, far to the north, where he lived and worked among the Apache people.

When he heard about the new silver town, he bought an old printing press in Tucson, loaded it onto a wagon, and brought it here, across the desert.

--

When people asked him what he would call his newspaper, he had a ready answer.

Every tombstone, he said, ought to have its epitaph.

The name made people laugh, and it stuck. The first issue appeared on the first of May, 1880, and it has been coming out ever since.

--

{img:man-reading-newspaper-boardwalk}
In the mornings, the boys sell the Epitaph on the corners of Allen Street, and men stand on the boardwalks in the sunshine, reading it.

It is full of everything that happens in Tombstone. News from the mines: which shaft has reached what depth, which vein looks rich, which company has paid a good dividend. News from the town: new buildings, new shops, new arrivals on the stage. Notices of dances and concerts and church suppers. Advertisements for hats and boots and medicines and mining tools.

And there is news from the outside world, too, sent by telegraph along the wire that now runs to Tombstone, from Washington and New York and San Francisco and London.

--

The Epitaph is not the only newspaper in town.

There is also the Nugget, which is older, and which has a different way of seeing things. The two papers often disagree, about politics, about the town government, and about who should be in charge of keeping the peace in the new county.

The editors write sharp remarks about each other in their columns, and the people of Tombstone read both papers with great interest, and argue about them in the saloons.

--

A little way along Fremont Street from the newspaper office is another building that everyone in town knows.

It is a long, low house with a gallery on the front, and a sign that says it is the photograph gallery of C. S. Fly.

{img:fly-photo-studio-camera}
Camillus Fly is a quiet man with a neat beard, who came to Tombstone from San Francisco with his wife Mollie at the very beginning of the town.

Inside the gallery, there is a studio with a large window in the roof to let in the light, a painted backdrop of trees and columns, a carved chair, and a big wooden camera on three legs, with a black cloth draped over the back of it.

--

People come to Fly's studio to have their portraits made.

A miner in his best suit, with his hair carefully combed and a watch chain across his waistcoat. A young couple who have just been married, the woman sitting in the chair and the man standing beside her with his hand on her shoulder. A mother with a baby in a long white christening gown.

{img:portrait-sitting-family-studio}
Fly asks them to sit very still. He disappears under the black cloth, focuses the lens, slides a glass plate into the back of the camera, and takes the cap off the lens for a few seconds.

Then he goes into his darkroom to develop the plate, and a few days later, the family comes back to collect a small, beautiful picture in a card frame, which they will keep on the mantelpiece, or send home to their relatives in the East.

--

Fly also likes to photograph the town itself.

He carries his camera out to the hills, to the mines, to the streets, and takes pictures of Tombstone as it grows. The hoisting works against the sky. The long view down Allen Street. A group of miners at the mouth of a shaft. A stagecoach standing ready to leave.

Many of the photographs that we have of Tombstone in these years were taken by Camillus Fly.

--

{img:fly-boarding-house-porch-mollie}
Next to the studio, Mollie Fly runs a boarding house.

It is a respectable place, with clean rooms, simple furniture and good meals, and it is popular with single men who have come to town on business. Mollie is a capable, kind woman, who keeps the house in good order and the lodgers well fed.

In the autumn of 1881, one of her lodgers is a slim, pale, very polite gentleman from Georgia, who rents a room at the back. He coughs a great deal, and he keeps unusual hours. He is a dentist by training, though he does not do much dentistry any more.

His name is John Henry Holliday. But everyone in Tombstone calls him Doc.

--

On the western side of the boarding house, between it and the next building along Fremont Street, there is a narrow empty lot, perhaps fifteen or twenty feet wide.

There is nothing much in it. A little dust and a few weeds. A fence at the back, and beyond that, the rear yard of a livery stable, where horses are kept and wagons are hired.

The stable faces onto Allen Street, on the other side of the block. It has a name that, in the autumn of 1881, means nothing at all to anyone outside Tombstone.

It is called the O.K. Corral.
