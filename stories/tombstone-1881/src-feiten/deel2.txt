BRIDGE: Parsons kept his diary for himself. But Tombstone also had people who recorded its days for everyone to read and see.
===
IMG: newspaper-office-printing-press | a frontier newspaper office with a hand printing press
HEAD: Tombstone's most famous newspaper got its name from a simple joke: every tombstone needs an epitaph.
BODY: In 1880, a man named John Clum arrived in Tombstone with a printing press. He had once been an agent on an Apache reservation, and he was full of energy and opinions. He started a newspaper and called it the Tombstone Epitaph, because, as he said, every tombstone should have an epitaph. The first issue appeared on the first of May, 1880. Producing a newspaper was slow work. Each letter was a small piece of metal, picked by hand from a wooden case and set in a line, one by one. The pages were printed on a heavy iron press, one sheet at a time. The paper reported on mining news, local events, advertisements, arrivals and departures, and quarrels in the town. There was soon a rival paper, the Nugget, and the two often disagreed sharply. The Epitaph is still published today, which makes it one of the oldest newspapers in Arizona.
===
IMG: man-reading-newspaper-boardwalk | men reading newspapers on a boardwalk in the morning sun
HEAD: In Tombstone, the morning newspaper was how most people found out what had happened overnight.
BODY: There was no radio and no telephone in the town in 1881. A telegraph line connected Tombstone with the outside world, and news from far away arrived in short messages that the newspapers printed the next day. In the mornings, boys walked along the boardwalks with bundles of papers, calling out the headlines. Men stood in the sun outside the shops and read the pages slowly. They read about the price of silver in New York, about elections and about events in Europe. They also read about local things, such as new mines, a wedding, a lost horse or a dance at Schieffelin Hall. People argued about what they read, and the newspapers often took sides in the town's disputes. One paper supported one group of officials, and the other supported their rivals. Yet both papers were read eagerly. In a town so far from anywhere, the newspaper was a daily connection to the rest of the world.
===
IMG: fly-photo-studio-camera | a photograph studio with a big wooden camera on a tripod
HEAD: A photographer named C. S. Fly took many of the pictures we still have of early Tombstone.
BODY: Camillus Sydney Fly came to Tombstone in 1879 with his wife, Mollie. He opened a photograph studio on Fremont Street. The studio had a large window in the roof, called a skylight, because photographers at the time needed lots of natural light. His camera was a big wooden box on a tripod, with a black cloth over the back. Fly photographed townspeople, miners, families and buildings, and he also travelled into the countryside to take pictures. Later, in 1886, he became famous for photographs taken in the Sierra Madre mountains of Mexico, during the last campaign against the Apache leader Geronimo. Those pictures are some of the only photographs ever taken of Apache people still free in the mountains. Many of the images we see of old Tombstone today come from his studio. Without him, the town would be much harder to imagine. His camera quietly recorded a world that was changing very fast.
===
IMG: portrait-sitting-family-studio | a family sitting very still for a portrait in a studio
HEAD: Having your photograph taken in the 1880s meant sitting perfectly still and trying not to blink.
BODY: By 1881, photography had become faster than in the early days, but it still took a moment or two for the image to form. So people sat very still. Studios often had metal stands hidden behind the chair, to support the back of the head and keep it from moving. That is one reason why people in old photographs often look so serious. Smiling for several seconds was difficult, and many people believed that a portrait should look dignified. Families dressed in their very best clothes for the occasion. Mothers held babies on their laps, and fathers stood behind them with one hand on the chair. The photographs were printed on small cards and sent to relatives far away, back east or across the ocean. For a family who had travelled thousands of miles to a mining town, a photograph was a way of saying, we are here, and we are well. Many of those cards still survive in family albums.
===
IMG: fly-boarding-house-porch-mollie | a woman sweeping the porch of a long wooden boarding house
HEAD: Next to the photograph studio, Mollie Fly ran a boarding house that played a part in Tombstone's most famous day.
BODY: Mollie Fly was a capable and practical woman. While her husband ran the studio, she ran a boarding house in the long, low building on Fremont Street. It had a covered porch along the front and simple rooms for lodgers. She also helped with the photography business, and when her husband was away on long trips, she kept the studio running herself. Doc Holliday rented a room in the Fly boarding house in 1881. Behind the building, and beside it, lay a narrow empty lot, the kind of space found between many buildings in frontier towns. On the afternoon of the twenty-sixth of October, 1881, that small lot would become the scene of the most famous gunfight in the history of the West. It was not, in fact, inside the O.K. Corral. For Mollie, though, most days were simply full of cooking, cleaning and keeping accounts, like the life of almost everyone else in town.

TOPIC: The Earp brothers and Doc Holliday
BRIDGE: Some of the people in those photographs would one day become far more famous than they ever expected.
===
IMG: earp-wagons-arriving-1879 | covered wagons arriving into a young frontier town in winter
HEAD: The Earp brothers came to Tombstone in December 1879, hoping to make money, not to become legends.
BODY: The Earps were a large family from the Midwest. Several of the brothers had travelled widely, working as farmers, teamsters, gamblers and lawmen. In late 1879, Virgil, Wyatt and James Earp arrived in Tombstone with their wives, in wagons, after a long journey from Dodge City in Kansas. A younger brother, Morgan, joined them later, and so did their friend Doc Holliday. Like almost everyone else, the Earps came to Tombstone because of the silver. They hoped to buy mining claims, run a stagecoach line or invest in a saloon. Wyatt had worked as a lawman in Kansas, but he did not plan to do so again. They settled into small houses near the edge of town. In the first months, they were simply one more family trying to make a living. It is worth remembering that none of them knew what the next two years would bring. To their neighbours, they were ordinary people.
===
IMG: virgil-allie-earp-home | a man and a lively woman in a small frontier kitchen
HEAD: Virgil Earp became the town marshal of Tombstone, and his wife Allie was known for her sharp wit.
BODY: Virgil was the oldest of the brothers in Tombstone. He was tall, broad-shouldered and calm, with a big moustache. He had fought in the Civil War as a young man. Shortly before arriving in the town, he had been appointed a deputy United States marshal, and in 1881 he became the town marshal of Tombstone, in charge of keeping the peace within the town limits. Much of the work was ordinary. He collected fees, enforced the town's rules, dealt with drunken quarrels, and chased stray animals off the streets. His wife, Allie, was small, lively and very talkative. Many years later, she told stories about her life in Tombstone, full of humour and strong opinions. Virgil and Allie had a long and affectionate marriage. They stayed together for the rest of his life. In their small house, a pot of coffee was usually on the stove, and friends and family came and went all day.
===
IMG: oriental-saloon-faro-table | a calm man dealing at a faro table in an elegant saloon
HEAD: In Tombstone, Wyatt Earp spent many evenings working at a card table called faro.
BODY: Faro was one of the most popular card games in the American West. It was played against the house, with a dealer drawing cards from a special box. Gamblers placed bets on a board painted with the thirteen cards of one suit. The game was simple to learn and fast to play, and it was found in nearly every saloon. Wyatt Earp had a share in the gambling at the Oriental Saloon, and he often worked at the faro table himself. It was considered a respectable enough job at the time, and many well-known men in the West were gamblers. Wyatt was tall and lean, with a long moustache and a calm, steady manner. He rarely drank. He also worked at times as a shotgun messenger for Wells Fargo and as a deputy sheriff. He was ambitious and hoped to be elected county sheriff one day. At the faro table, though, his job was simply to keep the game honest and steady.
===
IMG: earp-houses-fremont-evening | small houses at the edge of town with women talking on a porch
HEAD: The Earp families lived close together, in small houses near the corner of Fremont and First Street.
BODY: The houses of the Earp families stood at the quieter western end of town, away from the busy saloons of Allen Street. They were small wooden and adobe houses, with porches, little yards and a few chickens. The wives of the brothers were close neighbours and often spent time together, sewing, cooking and talking on the porch in the evening. Allie was married to Virgil, Louisa to Morgan, and Mattie lived with Wyatt. Life for the women was much like life for other families in Tombstone. They hauled water, cooked on wood stoves, washed clothes by hand and made do with whatever was available. They worried about money and about their husbands. In the evenings, the street was usually quiet, with lamplight in the windows and the faint sound of the town in the distance. It was a very ordinary corner of the town, and for a long time nothing remarkable happened there at all.
===
IMG: fremont-first-street-night-quiet | a quiet street corner at night with lamplit windows
HEAD: On most nights, Tombstone's quieter streets were silent except for the wind and the mine engines.
BODY: Away from Allen Street, the town grew quiet after dark. Families went to bed early, because most people rose with the sun. Oil lamps glowed in kitchen windows while the dishes were washed and the children were put to bed. Then, one by one, the lights went out. The night air on the mesa was cool and clear. Overhead, the stars were very bright, and the Milky Way stretched across the sky. On some nights, coyotes called from the hills. The steady thumping of the steam engines at the mines carried softly across the town, a sound so constant that people stopped noticing it. Now and then, a horse passed slowly along the street, or a late walker's footsteps sounded on the boardwalk. We tend to imagine the Old West as loud and dramatic. Yet for the people who lived in Tombstone, most nights were simply calm, dark and very still.
===
IMG: doc-holliday-card-table | a slender pale man in a grey suit at a poker table
HEAD: Doc Holliday was a trained dentist who became one of the most famous gamblers in the West.
BODY: John Henry Holliday was born in Georgia in 1851, into a well-to-do family. He was well educated and polite, and he spoke in the soft accent of the American South. In 1872, he graduated from a dental college in Philadelphia, and he began to practise as a dentist. But soon afterward, he learned that he had tuberculosis, a serious disease of the lungs that was very common at the time. Doctors told him that a dry climate might help, so he moved west. Patients did not like a dentist who coughed, and Holliday found that he could earn more money at the card table. He became a professional gambler, travelling from town to town. He was slim and pale, and he dressed neatly in grey suits. He was also known for his quick temper and his loyalty to his friends. His closest friend was Wyatt Earp, whom he had met in Texas years before.
===
IMG: dentist-office-1870s-chair | a tidy dentist office with a padded chair and brass instruments
HEAD: In the 1870s, a visit to the dentist was a very different experience from today.
BODY: When Doc Holliday trained as a dentist, the profession was still quite new in America. Dental offices had a padded wooden chair, a cabinet of brass and steel instruments and a window for light, because good lamps were rare. Drills were turned by hand or by a foot pedal. The most common treatment was pulling a painful tooth. Fillings were made of gold, which was strong and lasted well, or of other metals. Some dentists used ether or a gas called nitrous oxide, sometimes known as laughing gas, to dull the pain, but many patients simply held on tight. Holliday practised dentistry for a few years, first in Atlanta and then in Dallas, and he seems to have been a careful dentist. Yet his illness made the work difficult. As his cough worsened, he left the profession behind. People still called him Doc for the rest of his life, a reminder of the gentle career he had once planned.
===
IMG: doc-kate-hotel-balcony | a gentleman and a dark-haired woman on a hotel balcony
HEAD: Doc Holliday's companion for many years was a strong-willed woman from Hungary named Kate.
BODY: Mary Katharine Horony was born in Hungary in 1850, and she came to America with her family as a child. Her father had been a doctor. She was well educated and spoke several languages. As a young woman, she made her own way in the frontier towns, and she became known as Kate Elder. She met Doc Holliday in Texas, and the two of them were together on and off for many years. Their relationship was stormy. They quarrelled loudly, separated and then came back together again. Kate was independent and outspoken, and she did not like Doc's friend Wyatt Earp very much. In 1881, she spent some time with Doc in Tombstone. Many years later, as an old woman, she wrote down her memories of those days. Like many old memories, they are not always reliable. Yet they give us a glimpse of the private life of a man who is otherwise remembered only as a legend.
===
IMG: hotel-parlour-piano-evening | a pale gentleman playing an upright piano in a hotel parlour
HEAD: It is said that Doc Holliday played the piano, a reminder of his genteel upbringing in Georgia.
BODY: Holliday grew up in a comfortable home in the South, where children of good families learned music, manners and classical languages. According to family stories, he was a capable pianist as a young man. In the hotels and parlours of the frontier, upright pianos were common, and it is easy to imagine him sitting down at one in the evening and playing something quiet. Whether he did so often, we do not really know. Much of what is said about Doc Holliday has grown larger in the retelling, and historians are careful about the details. What does seem clear is that he was a complicated man. He could be charming, witty and generous, and he could also be cold and quick to anger. He was clever, well read and very ill. His life was shaped by his disease, which he knew would shorten his life. Perhaps that is why he seemed so careless of danger.
===
IMG: boarding-room-stove-night | a man wrapped in a blanket beside a small iron stove at night
HEAD: Tuberculosis was one of the most common causes of death in the nineteenth century.
BODY: Doc Holliday's illness was not unusual. In the 1800s, tuberculosis, often called consumption, killed huge numbers of people in America and Europe. It spread through the air, in crowded homes and towns. There was no cure. Doctors advised rest, fresh air and dry, sunny places, which is one reason why many people with weak lungs travelled west to the deserts and mountains. Some of them recovered, at least for a while. Others grew slowly weaker. On cold nights, a person with tuberculosis might sit up wrapped in a blanket, coughing, beside a small iron stove, unable to sleep. Holliday lived with the disease for about fifteen years. It made him thin and pale and tired, and it gave his life a sense of borrowed time. Only in the twentieth century did scientists develop medicines that could cure the disease. Today, in many countries, it is rare. In Doc Holliday's time, it was simply part of life.

TOPIC: Ranches, cowboys and lawmen
BRIDGE: Beyond the edge of town, the land opened out into wide, quiet valleys. And not everyone who lived there saw things the way the town did.
===
IMG: san-pedro-valley-ranch-cattle | an adobe ranch house and corral in a wide grassy river valley
HEAD: Around Tombstone lay wide grassy valleys, where small ranches raised cattle for the mining towns.
BODY: The mining towns needed food, and the valleys around Tombstone were good cattle country. The San Pedro Valley to the west and the Sulphur Springs Valley to the east had tall grass, scattered mesquite trees and a few precious streams and springs. Ranchers built small houses of adobe, with brush-covered porches for shade, and wooden corrals for their horses. Their cattle grazed across miles of open range, because there were few fences. Twice a year, the ranchers rounded up the cattle, branded the young calves and chose animals to sell. Beef was in great demand in Tombstone, and the army posts and the mines bought large amounts of it. Life on a ranch was lonely and hard, with long days in the saddle and little company. Yet many ranchers loved the open country, the wide sky and the silence. In the evenings, a lamp in a ranch house window could be seen for miles across the dark grassland.
===
IMG: cowboy-campfire-coffee-night | young cowboys sitting around a campfire on the open range
HEAD: In Tombstone in 1881, the word cowboy did not always mean someone who simply worked with cattle.
BODY: Most of the young men working on the ranches around Tombstone were ordinary cowhands. They herded cattle, mended fences, broke horses and slept under the stars on long drives. Around their campfires at night, they drank strong coffee, ate beans and bacon, and told stories. But in southern Arizona at that time, the word cowboys was also used for a loose group of men who stole cattle, especially across the border in Mexico, and sold them on the American side. Some of them also robbed stagecoaches. They were not an organised gang, but a network of friends, relatives and acquaintances. Many ranchers in the valleys knew them, bought cattle from them or were related to them. This made the situation complicated. To some people in Tombstone, the cowboys were dangerous outlaws. To others, they were simply neighbours. The tension between the cowboys and the town lawmen grew steadily during 1881.
===
IMG: mclaury-brothers-ranch-porch | two ranchers standing on the porch of an adobe ranch house
HEAD: Tom and Frank McLaury were ranchers from Iowa, who raised cattle in the valleys near Tombstone.
BODY: The McLaury brothers came from a respectable family in Iowa. Their older brother was a lawyer. Tom and Frank moved to Arizona in the late 1870s and started a ranch, first in the Babocomari area west of Tombstone, and later in the Sulphur Springs Valley to the east. Frank was known as the bolder and more outspoken of the two, while Tom was quieter. The McLaurys were friendly with the Clanton family, who also had a ranch, and with some of the men known as cowboys. They were suspected of buying stolen cattle and horses, but they were never convicted of any crime. In the valleys, they were respected ranchers with many friends. In town, their friendships made them distrusted by the Earps. During 1881, there were several quarrels between them. It is a reminder that the conflict in Tombstone was not simply between good men and bad men. It was between neighbours who did not trust each other.
===
IMG: cowboys-riding-into-town-dusk | a few cowboys riding into a mining town at dusk
HEAD: In Tombstone, it was against the law to carry a gun inside the town.
BODY: Many people are surprised to learn this, but Tombstone had strict rules about weapons. A town ordinance said that visitors arriving in town had to leave their guns at a hotel, a livery stable or another business, and collect them again when they left. Similar rules existed in many frontier towns, such as Dodge City in Kansas. The idea was simple. Cowboys coming in from the ranches after weeks on the range might drink too much, and arguments with loaded guns could quickly end in tragedy. So the town marshal and his deputies were expected to disarm anyone who broke the rule. Most riders accepted it without fuss. They rode in at dusk, tied their horses, handed over their pistols and went to find a meal and a drink. Yet the rule also caused resentment, especially among men who already disliked the lawmen. In October 1881, this simple town ordinance would play an important part in what happened.
===
IMG: sheriff-behan-office | a friendly man with a sheriff star at a desk in a small office
HEAD: Tombstone had two different lawmen in charge, and they did not get along.
BODY: In 1881, the town of Tombstone had its own police, led by the town marshal, Virgil Earp. But Tombstone was also the main town of the new Cochise County, created in February of that year. The county had its own sheriff, a friendly and talkative politician named John Behan. Behan was popular with many people, including the ranchers in the valleys and some of the cowboys. Wyatt Earp had hoped to become county sheriff himself, and he believed that Behan had broken a promise to him. The two men also competed for the affection of the same woman, a young actress named Josephine Marcus, who later became Wyatt's wife. So there were political and personal rivalries between the town lawmen and the county lawmen. The two newspapers took different sides. This division made it harder to keep the peace. In a town full of quarrels, even the people meant to settle them were part of the problem.
===
IMG: valley-sunset-windmill-quiet | a wide grassy valley at sunset with cattle and a lit ranch house
HEAD: Far from the quarrels in town, the evenings in the valleys were wide, golden and very quiet.
BODY: On a ranch in the San Pedro or Sulphur Springs Valley, the day ended slowly. As the sun went down, the light turned the grass to gold and the distant mountains to deep blue and purple. Cattle drifted toward the water holes in long, unhurried lines. Doves called from the mesquite trees. A rancher might stand by the corral for a few minutes, watching the colours fade, before going in for supper. The air cooled quickly after sunset, and soon the first stars appeared. In the dark, coyotes began to sing, and an owl hooted from a cottonwood by the stream. For many people in the region, this was the real Arizona. It was not the saloons, the mines or the arguments in town, but this quiet country of grass, sky and silence. It had been here long before the silver was found, and it would still be here long after the mines closed.
===
IMG: october-morning-town-cool | a mining town on a crisp autumn morning with chimney smoke
HEAD: By October 1881, the summer heat had gone, and the mornings in Tombstone were crisp and cool.
BODY: Autumn on the high mesa brought clear blue skies, cool nights and golden grass on the hills. In the mornings, smoke from hundreds of chimneys hung low over the roofs, because people had started lighting their stoves again. In the kitchens, women were preserving fruit for the winter, peaches and plums brought in by wagon from the river farms. The stores had put out warm coats, wool blankets and heavy boots. At the school, the children were practising songs for a concert before Christmas. On the surface, it was a pleasant season in a thriving town. The mines were producing well, new buildings were going up and the population was still growing. Yet underneath, there was a feeling of unease. All through the year, there had been robberies, missing cattle and bitter arguments. People talked in low voices on the boardwalks. Many sensed that the quarrels were not going to fade away on their own.
===
IMG: posse-riders-desert-road | riders in long coats on horseback on a desert road
HEAD: In 1881, stagecoach robberies near Tombstone turned old suspicions into open anger.
BODY: In March 1881, masked men tried to rob a stagecoach on the road near Contention, and the driver and a passenger were killed. The Earps and Sheriff Behan rode out together in a posse to find the robbers. They caught one man, but he soon escaped, and the others were never brought to justice. Rumours spread in every direction about who had been involved and who had helped whom. In September, another stagecoach was robbed, on the road to the mining town of Bisbee. This time, the Earps and their deputies tracked the robbers and arrested two men, both friends of the cowboys. The cowboys were furious, and in the saloons some of them made angry threats. Meanwhile, the newspapers argued, and the sheriff and the marshal grew further apart. Each new event added to the mistrust. By the middle of October, the town was full of quarrels, and many of them were about these robberies on the dusty desert roads.
===
IMG: virgil-marshal-walking-boardwalk | a tall marshal with a small badge walking along a boardwalk
HEAD: Most of a town marshal's work in Tombstone was ordinary, patient and rather dull.
BODY: When people think of a frontier marshal, they usually picture gunfights. In reality, Virgil Earp's job as town marshal was mostly routine. He walked the streets, checked that the shops were secure at night, and dealt with drunken men and noisy quarrels. He enforced town rules about animals, rubbish, fires and weapons. He collected license fees and helped with lost property. He made arrests for small offences and brought people before the local judge. He had a few deputies, and at times he asked his brothers to help him. Much of the work required patience and a calm manner, and Virgil was known for both. Yet he also knew that the situation in Tombstone was growing more dangerous. Threats had been made against him and his brothers. In the autumn of 1881, he walked the boardwalks as usual, but with a careful eye on the street and on the men who rode in from the valleys.
===
IMG: tombstone-street-dusk-lamps-october | a frontier main street at dusk in October with glowing lamps
HEAD: On autumn evenings, the lamps of Allen Street came on earlier and earlier.
BODY: As October went on, the days grew shorter, and the evenings in Tombstone grew colder. Shopkeepers lit their lamps in the late afternoon, and soon the whole street glowed. People walked home wrapped in coats and shawls, carrying parcels and baskets. A horse and buggy rolled slowly along the dusty street. In the restaurants, diners lingered over hot soup and coffee. In the saloons, the stoves were lit, and men gathered around them, talking about mining shares, the weather and the latest news. Children were called in from playing. Smoke rose from the chimneys and drifted across the darkening sky. It was the season when frontier towns seemed at their most cosy. The warm windows, the smell of wood smoke and the cool clear air made the town feel almost peaceful. Most of its people went about their evening routines without any idea of how soon their town would become famous.
===
IMG: full-moon-over-dragoon-mountains | a pale full moon rising over rocky desert mountains
HEAD: East of Tombstone rise the Dragoon Mountains, once the stronghold of the Apache leader Cochise.
BODY: The rocky Dragoon Mountains can be seen from almost everywhere in Tombstone. Their granite cliffs and boulders glow orange at sunset and silver under the moon. For many years, these mountains were the homeland of the Chiricahua Apache, and their great leader, Cochise, used a hidden valley in the Dragoons as his stronghold. It was almost impossible for outsiders to find. In 1872, Cochise made peace with the United States, and he died two years later. He was buried somewhere in the mountains, in a secret place that has never been revealed. The county in which Tombstone stands was named after him. By 1881, the Apache people had been forced onto reservations, though some groups still left them and returned to the mountains. On a quiet night, as the full moon rose over the Dragoons, the people of Tombstone could look out at a landscape with a much longer history than their own young town.

TOPIC: Two days in October
BRIDGE: Through the long summer, old quarrels simmered quietly. Then, in late October, they came to the surface.
===
IMG: wagon-arriving-town-evening-wind | a ranch wagon with two men arriving in town on a windy afternoon
HEAD: On the twenty-fifth of October, 1881, two ranchers drove a wagon into Tombstone on ordinary business.
BODY: Ike Clanton and Tom McLaury came into town that day by wagon. They had cattle business to attend to, and they planned to buy supplies and spend the night. Ike was the son of a rancher, known for his loud voice and his habit of drinking too much and talking too freely. He was friendly with the cowboys, and he had quarrelled with the Earps about a secret arrangement that had gone wrong. He feared that people would find out about it. Tom McLaury was quieter. The afternoon was grey and windy, and dust blew along the streets. The two men left their wagon and horses at a livery stable, as visitors usually did. Neither of them came to Tombstone that day intending to fight. Yet Ike, in particular, was nervous and angry, and he began drinking as the evening went on. Small events, piling up one after another, were about to lead somewhere nobody had planned.
===
IMG: alhambra-lunch-counter-night | a small saloon lunch room at night with a cook at the stove
HEAD: The quarrel that led to the famous gunfight began late at night, at a lunch counter.
BODY: Late on the night of October twenty-fifth, Ike Clanton was eating at the lunch counter of the Alhambra Saloon on Allen Street. It was a small, warm room with a long wooden counter and a cook frying eggs and steak on an iron stove. Doc Holliday came in. Doc had heard that Ike had been making threats against him and the Earps, and he confronted Ike angrily. The two men exchanged harsh words. Morgan Earp, who was nearby, and then Virgil, stepped in and moved them apart. Virgil, as town marshal, told them both to stop. Ike left, but he was furious, and he kept talking about what he would do the next day. It was a small, ugly argument of the kind that happened in saloons all over the West. On most nights, it would have been forgotten by morning. This time, it was not. The quarrel at the Alhambra became the first link in a short chain of events.
===
IMG: poker-game-late-night-lamp | men playing poker under a hanging oil lamp late at night
HEAD: That same night, Ike Clanton sat down to play poker with the very men he was quarrelling with.
BODY: Strange as it seems, after the argument at the Alhambra, Ike Clanton joined a poker game that went on through the night. At the table were Tom McLaury, Sheriff John Behan, Town Marshal Virgil Earp and a few other men. They played under a hanging oil lamp in a nearly empty saloon, while the town slept. In the frontier West, such things were not unusual. Men who disliked each other might still sit at the same card table. The game lasted until early morning. When it ended, Virgil went home to sleep. Ike, who had had no sleep at all and had been drinking for many hours, wandered out into the cold grey dawn. He was tired, angry and still talking about trouble. It was the morning of Wednesday, the twenty-sixth of October, 1881. Later, many people would wonder what might have happened if the game had ended earlier, or if Ike had simply gone to bed.
