HEAD: Tombstone's mines were eventually defeated not by outlaws, but by water.
BODY: In 1881, as the miners dug deeper, they struck water about five hundred feet below the surface. You might think outlaws or fire would end the silver boom. But it was something much quieter. At first, it seemed like good news for a dry town. But the water flooded the lower tunnels, and the richest silver lay below it. The mining companies built huge steam pumps, some of the largest in the country, in tall brick engine houses on the hills. The pumps ran day and night, lifting water out of the mines. For a few years, they worked. Then, in 1886, a fire destroyed one of the great pumping plants. Around the same time, the price of silver began to fall. Pumping became too expensive, and the lower levels of the mines slowly filled with water. Many miners left to find work elsewhere. The population of Tombstone shrank year by year. It's a quiet irony that a town once so short of water was finally brought low by having far too much of it underground.

From the monument, you can see the hills where he walked alone with his mule in 1877, and the mountains all around. Few people come out there, and it's usually very quiet, with only the wind in the grass and the call of a hawk overhead. At sunset, the stones glow warm and golden. It's a fitting place for a man who loved the open country more than the towns his discoveries created. I find that a very fitting place for him to rest. He found the silver, but it seems that what he truly loved was the looking itself, the next ridge, the next valley and the next piece of rock in his hand.

HEAD: Tombstone is home to what is often called the largest rose bush in the world.
BODY: In 1885, a young woman named Mary Gee arrived in Tombstone from Scotland as a bride. You'd stand in its shade, under a ceiling of white flowers. She brought with her a small rose cutting from home, a Lady Banks rose. She planted it in the courtyard of her house, and it began to grow. And grow. Over the years, the rose spread across a wooden frame built to support it, until it covered thousands of square feet like a great green roof. Every spring, it's covered with countless tiny white blossoms, and the courtyard beneath fills with dappled shade and a soft, sweet scent. The rose tree, as it's called, still grows in Tombstone today, more than a hundred and thirty years after it was planted. Visitors sit beneath it in the shade. Of all the stories of Tombstone, this may be the gentlest. A small cutting carried across an ocean, by a young woman far from home, quietly outlived the boom, the mines and nearly everyone who knew it.
