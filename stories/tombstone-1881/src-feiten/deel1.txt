TOPIC: A streak of silver in the hills
===
IMG: desert-hills-dawn-prospector | a lone prospector with a pack mule at dawn in the Arizona hills
HEAD: The richest silver town in Arizona was found by one stubborn man with a mule.
BODY: In 1877, a young prospector named Ed Schieffelin was wandering through the dry hills of southern Arizona. He was tall and thin, with a long beard and clothes that were mostly patches. He had been looking for ore since he was a boy, in Oregon, in Nevada, in Utah, and he had never found very much. But he had a habit that served him well. He picked up rocks everywhere he went, broke them open with his hammer, and looked closely at what was inside. The hills east of the San Pedro River were dangerous country at the time, and few people went there. Schieffelin went anyway. He walked for weeks with his mule, sleeping under the stars and living on beans and coffee. Most people thought he was wasting his time. Yet he kept going, one ridge after another, with the patience of someone who simply enjoyed the looking. It would turn out to be one of the luckiest walks in the history of the American West.
===
IMG: camp-huachuca-soldiers-tents | a small army camp of tents at the foot of wooded mountains
HEAD: Soldiers told him that the only thing he would find out there was his own tombstone.
BODY: While he was prospecting, Ed Schieffelin often stopped at Camp Huachuca, a small army post in the mountains to the south. It was little more than a group of tents and rough huts beside a stream, with tall oak and pine trees on the slopes above. The soldiers there watched the thin prospector come and go, and they thought he was foolish. The country to the east was wild, and they warned him not to go alone. According to the story Schieffelin told for the rest of his life, one of them said that all he would ever find out there was his tombstone. Schieffelin did not forget the words. Months later, when he finally found what he was looking for, he gave his first claim that very name. It is a small piece of dry frontier humour, and it gave the town its name. A joke made by a bored soldier at a lonely camp is now on maps all over the world.
===
IMG: prospector-campfire-night | a prospector at a campfire at night studying a rock
HEAD: The first piece of rich silver ore was studied by the light of a small campfire.
BODY: Late in the summer of 1877, Schieffelin found a vein of rock in the hills that looked different from anything he had seen before. He broke off pieces and studied them carefully. Some of the ore was so rich that the silver could be seen, and it was soft enough to press a coin into. He had no money and no tools to mine it himself, so he filed his claim and went looking for help. He found his brother Al, who was working in a mine in another part of Arizona, and an assayer named Richard Gird. Gird tested the samples, and the results were astonishing. One sample was worth thousands of dollars a ton. The three men became partners on the spot. It is said that Gird told Ed that he was a lucky cuss, and that became the name of one of their next mines. For a while, the men kept the news very quiet. They knew that once the word got out, the hills would never be lonely again.
===
IMG: first-claims-tents-hills | an early mining camp of tents among rocky hills
HEAD: Within a year, the empty hills were covered with tents, piles of stones and men with shovels.
BODY: The partners could not keep their secret for long. By 1878, news of the silver had spread across the territory and beyond. Prospectors arrived on foot, on horseback and in wagons. They set up canvas tents and little huts made of brush. Each man marked his claim with a pile of stones and a paper notice tucked inside. The claims were given cheerful and hopeful names, like the Tough Nut, the Contention, the Grand Central and the Lucky Cuss. Most of the new arrivals found nothing of value. But a few claims turned out to be very rich, and that was enough to keep everyone else digging. Life in the early camp was simple and hard. Water had to be carried in, food was expensive, and the nights could be cold. Yet in the evenings, the camp was full of small fires and quiet talk about tomorrow's luck. Every great mining town started like this, as a scattering of tents and hope.
===
TOPIC: A town on Goose Flats
BRIDGE: The hills were full of tents and shovels now. All those people needed somewhere to live.
===
IMG: mesa-sunset-mountains-distant | a high desert mesa at sunset with mountains glowing in the distance
HEAD: The town was built on a high mesa called Goose Flats, almost a mile above the sea.
BODY: In 1879, the miners needed a proper town, and they chose a wide, flat stretch of high ground near the mines. It was known as Goose Flats, and it stood more than four thousand five hundred feet above sea level. From the edge of the mesa, you could see for many miles in every direction. To the east rose the Dragoon Mountains, and to the south and west, the Huachucas and the Whetstones. In the evenings, the mountains turned pink and then purple as the sun went down. The height of the mesa made the town cooler than the desert below, and the air was dry and clear. Yet there was no river and no spring on Goose Flats. Water would always be a problem. The town was laid out in a neat grid of streets, with numbers going one way and names going the other. Within a few months, the empty grass of the mesa had become a busy little town.
===
IMG: tombstone-street-morning-1881 | a wide dusty main street of a silver town in morning light
HEAD: In just two years, Tombstone grew from a few tents into one of the largest towns in the Southwest.
BODY: By 1881, Tombstone had several thousand people, and some estimates say many more. It had grown faster than almost any town in the territory. There were hotels, banks, churches, a school, newspapers, restaurants, stores of every kind and a great many saloons. Stagecoaches arrived and left every day, and long trains of wagons brought in lumber, food and machinery. In the early morning, the main streets were quiet. Shopkeepers swept the boardwalks in front of their doors, and the smell of coffee and frying bacon drifted out of the kitchens. Then the town woke up, and the streets filled with miners, merchants, ranchers and families. People came from all over the world. There were Irish and Cornish miners, Mexican families, Chinese cooks and laundry owners, German brewers and businessmen from New York and San Francisco. Tombstone was rough at the edges, but it was also lively, curious and surprisingly modern for its time.
===
IMG: adobe-and-wooden-buildings | a side street with adobe houses next to wooden shops
HEAD: Many of the houses in Tombstone were built of mud bricks, the way houses had been built in the desert for centuries.
BODY: Lumber was expensive in southern Arizona, because there were few large trees nearby. It had to be cut in the mountains and hauled in by wagon. So many people in Tombstone built their homes from adobe instead. Adobe bricks are made from mud mixed with straw, shaped in wooden moulds and left to dry in the sun. Mexican builders in the town knew the craft well. The thick adobe walls kept houses cool on hot afternoons and warm on cold nights. Along the main streets, many shops had wooden fronts that rose higher than the roof, to make the buildings look grander than they were. These are called false fronts, and they became a familiar sight in frontier towns. Behind many of them stood simple adobe rooms. Walking along a Tombstone side street, you could see both styles side by side, the old desert way and the new American way of building, standing comfortably together in the sun.
===
IMG: water-wagon-barrels | a horse-drawn water wagon with wooden barrels in a town street
HEAD: In early Tombstone, drinking water was delivered by wagon and sold by the barrel.
BODY: Goose Flats had no river and no spring. In the first years, every drop of water used in the town had to be brought in from wells and springs some distance away. Water wagons with big wooden barrels rolled slowly through the streets, and the drivers sold water to houses, hotels and restaurants. People were careful with it. Dishwater was saved for the garden. Baths were small and not very frequent. People sometimes joked that water was harder to find than whiskey. In 1881, a company finally began to build a long pipeline from the Huachuca Mountains, more than twenty miles away, to bring fresh water to the town. It was a great improvement, and the townspeople were very proud of it. Still, for many years, the sight of the water wagon remained part of everyday life. Today, we turn on a tap without thinking. In Tombstone, every bucket of clean water was a small luxury.
===
TOPIC: Down in the silver mines
BRIDGE: Above ground, the town was growing fast. But the real reason for all of it lay deep beneath the streets.
===
IMG: miners-walking-to-shift-dawn | miners with lunch pails walking up a hillside at dawn
HEAD: A Tombstone miner earned about four dollars a day, which was good money at the time.
BODY: The silver mines worked day and night, and the miners worked in shifts of about ten hours. At dawn, groups of men walked up the paths from the town to the mines, carrying tin lunch pails and wearing heavy boots and felt hats. Four dollars a day was a high wage for working men in the 1880s, higher than in many factories back east. In 1880, the mine owners tried to lower the pay, and the miners went on strike. It was one of the first miners' strikes in Arizona. Yet the work was hard and dangerous. The tunnels were hot, dusty and dark, and accidents could happen without warning. Many miners were young men who had come to Tombstone alone, hoping to save enough money to buy land or start a business of their own. Some of them managed it. Others spent their wages as quickly as they earned them. Either way, the whole town depended on them.
===
IMG: wells-fargo-office-stagecoach | a stagecoach with six horses in front of an express office
HEAD: The fastest way to reach Tombstone was a bumpy ride by stagecoach from the railway.
BODY: In 1880, the Southern Pacific Railroad reached the town of Benson, about twenty-five miles to the north. From there, travellers continued by stagecoach. The coaches were tall, heavy wagons pulled by teams of four or six horses. They were painted in bright colours, and they swayed on thick leather straps instead of springs. The ride took several hours, and passengers arrived covered in dust. Wells Fargo had an office in Tombstone, and the stagecoaches carried its strongboxes, full of mail, money and silver bullion. That made the coaches a tempting target for robbers. Guards called shotgun messengers sat next to the driver to protect the box. Most journeys passed without trouble. Passengers chatted, dozed, or looked out at the golden grass and distant mountains. Still, everyone in the coach knew the stories, and nobody was sorry when the first buildings of Tombstone came into view.
===
IMG: hoisting-works-headframe | a tall wooden mine headframe with a large wheel on a hill
HEAD: Above every big mine stood a tall wooden tower with a great wheel at the top.
BODY: The towers over the mine shafts were called headframes. A thick steel cable ran over the big wheel at the top and down into the shaft below. At the other end, a steam engine in a nearby building wound the cable in and out. It raised and lowered the cages that carried the miners down to work, and it brought the ore up to the surface in heavy buckets. The engine houses had tall chimneys, and on a still morning, plumes of smoke and steam rose above the hills around the town. The engines ran day and night. People living nearby grew used to the steady sound of the machinery, the hiss of steam and the clang of the signal bells. For the miners, the signal bells were very important. A certain number of rings told the engineer to raise the cage, lower it, or stop. In a deep mine, a man's life could depend on someone listening carefully.
===
IMG: miners-candles-underground | miners working by candlelight in a narrow tunnel
HEAD: Deep underground, the miners worked by the light of ordinary candles.
BODY: In the 1880s, there was no electric light in the mines of Tombstone. Each miner carried candles, and he pushed them into iron holders with a sharp spike, which he could stick into a crack in the rock or into a wooden beam. The small flames gave just enough light to work by. The miners drilled holes into the rock by hand, using long steel drills and heavy hammers. One man held and turned the drill, while another swung the hammer. It took great trust and steady rhythm. When enough holes had been drilled, they were filled with explosives, and the miners moved to a safe distance before the blast. Afterward, the broken ore was shovelled into carts and pushed to the shaft. A candle also served as a simple warning. If the flame grew weak or went out, it could mean the air was bad. So the miners watched their small lights carefully, all through the long shift.
===
IMG: miners-lunch-break-candlelight | miners eating lunch from tin pails in a tunnel
HEAD: Many miners carried a pastry for lunch that had travelled all the way from Cornwall.
BODY: Some of the most experienced miners in Tombstone came from Cornwall, in the southwest of England. Cornwall had been mining tin and copper for centuries, and Cornish miners were known everywhere for their skill. People in the American West called them Cousin Jacks. They brought their own food with them, and one of their favourites was the pasty. A pasty is a folded pastry filled with meat, potato and onion. It was easy to carry, it stayed warm for a long time, and it could be eaten with dirty hands by holding the thick crust along the edge. It is said that some wives marked a corner with initials, so that each man could find his own. At midday, the men sat together in a wide part of the tunnel, eating by candlelight and talking quietly. A small taste of home, deep inside an Arizona hill, made the long shift a little easier.
===
IMG: ore-wagons-desert-road | a long team of mules pulling ore wagons along a desert road
HEAD: The silver ore was carried to the mills by long teams of mules, pulling enormous wagons.
BODY: There was not enough water on Goose Flats to process the ore, so it had to be taken to mills beside the San Pedro River, about ten miles away. Huge freight wagons, sometimes two or three linked together, were pulled by teams of a dozen mules or more. The driver often rode on one of the mules near the back, and he steered the whole long team with a single line and his voice. The mule skinners, as they were called, were famous for their patience and also for their colourful language. The wagons rolled along the dusty road at walking pace, day after day. Mules were preferred over horses for this work, because they were tougher, more sure-footed and less likely to panic. On a hot afternoon, you could see the dust of the ore wagons rising slowly over the grassland, long before you heard the creak of the wheels and the jingle of the harness.
===
IMG: stamp-mill-river-cottonwoods | a large wooden mill beside a river lined with cottonwood trees
HEAD: Along the San Pedro River, giant machines pounded the silver rock into powder, day and night.
BODY: The mills that processed Tombstone's ore stood in little towns along the river, with names like Charleston and Contention. Inside each mill were rows of heavy iron hammers called stamps. They rose and fell, one after another, crushing the rock into fine sand. Water from the river carried the crushed ore through tanks and pans, where the silver was separated from the rest. The sound of the stamps was tremendous, a steady pounding that could be heard far across the valley. People who lived near the mills said they grew so used to it that they woke up when it stopped. The river itself was a pleasant place, lined with tall cottonwood trees whose leaves turned gold in autumn. The finished silver was cast into heavy bars called bullion and sent away by stagecoach. It is strange to think of it now, but much of the quiet river valley once shook with noise.

TOPIC: Evenings on Allen Street
BRIDGE: When the shift whistle blew and the miners climbed back into the daylight, the town came alive.
===
IMG: allen-street-afternoon-boardwalk | a busy main street with people on a shaded wooden boardwalk
HEAD: Allen Street was the heart of Tombstone, and it was lined with covered wooden sidewalks.
BODY: The main street of the town was called Allen Street. Along both sides ran wooden boardwalks, raised a little above the dust, with roofs on slender posts to shade people from the sun. Under these awnings, you could walk from shop to shop without stepping into the street. There were general stores and hardware stores, a bank, a stationery shop, a jeweller, a tobacconist, barbers, bakeries and restaurants. Shop windows displayed hats, boots, fabrics and tinned food from far away. In the afternoon, the boardwalks were crowded with people strolling and chatting. Wagons and riders passed slowly along the street, and children ran between the posts. When it rained, the street turned to mud, and the boardwalks became even more important. In summer, the dust rose with every passing wagon. Yet for the people of Tombstone, Allen Street was a proud place. It was where everyone met, and where the town showed its best face.
===
IMG: restaurant-white-tablecloths-lamps | an elegant restaurant with white tablecloths and oil lamps
HEAD: In the middle of the desert, Tombstone's restaurants served oysters, French food and fine wine.
BODY: Silver made many people in Tombstone rich, at least for a while, and they liked to spend their money well. The town had restaurants that would not have looked out of place in San Francisco. There were tables with white cloths, silver cutlery and oil lamps, and menus with French names. Oysters were shipped on ice by train and then by wagon, and they were a great luxury in a town so far from the sea. Diners could order lobster, quail, fresh fruit and imported wine. Some restaurants employed cooks from France or China. Miners came in their best suits on special occasions, and ladies came in silk dresses and fine hats. Of course, most people ate much more simply, at boarding houses and lunch counters. Yet the fancy restaurants were part of the town's pride. They showed that Tombstone was not just a rough mining camp, but a place where people wanted to live well.
===
IMG: saloon-interior-lamplight-piano | a grand saloon with a long wooden bar, mirrors and a piano
HEAD: The grandest saloons in Tombstone had polished bars, tall mirrors and soft carpets.
BODY: Tombstone had a great many saloons, and the finest of them were remarkably elegant. The Oriental Saloon, on the corner of Allen and Fifth Street, was said to be one of the most luxurious in the West. It had a long bar of polished wood, big mirrors, chandeliers and carpets. In the evenings, a piano played, and sometimes a small orchestra. Men gathered to drink, talk business, read newspapers and play cards. Saloons were also places where deals were made, claims were bought and sold, and news was passed along. Yet they could be noisy and sometimes rough, especially late at night. Many respectable townspeople never set foot inside. For the miners, though, a warm bright saloon after a long shift underground was a welcome sight. The light from the tall windows spilled out across the boardwalk, and the sound of the piano drifted into the street.
===
IMG: schieffelin-hall-evening-crowd | a large adobe theatre hall at dusk with glowing windows
HEAD: Tombstone had a large theatre built of adobe, and it is still standing today.
BODY: In 1881, Al Schieffelin, the brother of the prospector who had found the silver, built a large hall on the corner of Fremont and Fourth Street. Schieffelin Hall was a two-storey building made of adobe, and it was said to be one of the largest adobe buildings in the American Southwest. Inside, there was a stage and seats for several hundred people. Travelling theatre companies performed plays there. There were concerts, lectures, dances and town meetings. On a performance evening, ladies arrived in silk dresses and men in their best coats, and the windows of the hall glowed warmly against the darkening sky. For people living far from any big city, these evenings were very special. They brought a little of the wider world to the high desert. Schieffelin Hall survived fires and floods and the long decline of the town. You can still visit it today.
===
IMG: hitching-rail-horses-dusk | horses at a hitching rail in front of lamplit shop windows at dusk
HEAD: Every street in town had wooden hitching rails, where horses waited patiently for their riders.
BODY: In the 1880s, almost everyone who came into Tombstone from the surrounding country arrived on horseback or by wagon. In front of the shops, saloons and offices stood long wooden rails, and riders tied their horses there while they went about their business. The horses waited quietly, swishing their tails at the flies and dozing in the evening light. Some towns had water troughs beside the rails, so that the animals could drink. Livery stables offered a better place to leave a horse for longer, with hay, water and shelter for the night. As dusk fell, the shopkeepers lit their oil lamps, and squares of yellow light appeared on the boardwalks. The horses stood in the soft glow, their breath steaming a little in the cooler air. It was an ordinary sight at the time, so ordinary that hardly anyone wrote about it. But for a while, it was the sound and smell of every evening in town.

TOPIC: Nellie Cashman
BRIDGE: Among the crowds on those busy streets were a few people that everyone in town seemed to know.
===
IMG: nellie-cashman-restaurant-kitchen | a small Irish woman cooking in a busy restaurant kitchen
HEAD: One of the most respected people in Tombstone was a small Irish woman who ran a restaurant.
BODY: Nellie Cashman was born in Ireland around 1845, and she came to America as a girl. She had already lived in mining camps in Nevada, British Columbia and other parts of Arizona before she arrived in Tombstone in 1880. There she ran a restaurant and boarding house called the Russ House, on the corner of Fifth and Toughnut Street. She was slim, dark-haired and full of energy, and she worked from early morning until late at night. Her food was known as good and honest, and her prices were fair. But people remembered Nellie most for her kindness. She cared for sick miners, raised money for the poor and helped anyone who was down on their luck. Some people in the town called her the angel of Tombstone. She never married, and she never seemed to slow down. In a town known for rough men and quarrels, her name was spoken with real warmth.
===
IMG: boarding-house-dining-table-miners | a boarding house dining room with long tables and miners eating
HEAD: It is said that Nellie Cashman never let a hungry miner leave her table, even if he could not pay.
BODY: Many miners lived in boarding houses, where they rented a simple room and ate their meals at long shared tables. At the Russ House, the tables were covered with oilcloth, and the meals came in large bowls and platters. There was stew, beef, beans, bread, potatoes and, on good days, pie. A story is told that a diner once complained loudly about Nellie's food. Another customer quietly told him to eat it and be grateful, and the complaint was not repeated. Nellie was known to feed men who had no money, and she would let them pay later, when their luck changed. Sometimes they never paid at all, and she did not seem to mind. It was not a very good way to run a business. Yet it made her one of the most loved people in town. In a hard place far from home, a warm meal and a kind word could mean a great deal.
===
IMG: small-adobe-church-evening | a small adobe church with a wooden cross at evening
HEAD: Nellie Cashman helped raise the money to build Tombstone's first Catholic church.
BODY: Nellie Cashman was a devout Catholic, and in the early days, Tombstone had no Catholic church. Mass was held wherever there was room. Nellie decided to change that. She went from door to door and from saloon to saloon, asking for donations. It is said that very few people, even the gamblers, could say no to her. The money she gathered helped to build a small adobe church, the Sacred Heart, which opened in the early 1880s. She also raised funds for a hospital, for the families of injured miners and for many other causes. Her gift was simple. She asked directly and kindly, and she always put the money to good use. On Sunday mornings, families in their best clothes walked along the dusty street toward the little church. For a mining town that was often described as wild, it is good to remember that it was also full of people building ordinary, gentle things.
===
IMG: schoolhouse-children-teacher | children writing on slates in a plain frontier schoolhouse
HEAD: Tombstone's children learned their lessons on slates, in a simple wooden schoolhouse.
BODY: As more families came to Tombstone, the town needed a school. The first public school opened in 1880, and soon there were dozens of children of all ages in a single building. They sat at rows of wooden desks and wrote on small slates with slate pencils, which could be wiped clean and used again. Paper was expensive, so the slates saved money. The children learned reading, writing, arithmetic, geography and history. They recited poems, practised their handwriting, and learned the names of the states and their capitals. Some of them had lived in several mining towns already, and some had travelled thousands of miles by wagon. In winter, an iron stove kept the room warm, and the older boys were often given the job of bringing in wood. Children in Tombstone also helped at home, carried water and looked after younger brothers and sisters. School was a small, calm island in a busy and noisy town.
===
IMG: nellie-evening-porch-lamp | a woman sitting quietly on a boarding house porch in the evening
HEAD: Nellie Cashman did not stay in Tombstone forever. She followed the gold rushes all the way to the Arctic.
BODY: In 1881, Nellie's sister died, leaving five young children. Nellie took them in and raised them as her own, while still running her business. When Tombstone's mines began to decline a few years later, she moved on, as she had done so often before. She opened restaurants and stores in other mining towns, and in the 1890s, when gold was found in the Klondike in northern Canada, she went north. She was already over fifty. She crossed the snowy mountains on foot, ran a restaurant in Dawson City, and later prospected for gold herself in Alaska, travelling by dog sled. She was still working in the far north when she was nearly eighty. Nellie Cashman died in 1925 in Victoria, British Columbia. She is remembered today in Arizona, in Canada and in Alaska. Few people in the Old West travelled so far, and fewer still left so many grateful people behind them.

TOPIC: George Parsons and his diary
BRIDGE: Nellie fed the town. Another newcomer quietly wrote it all down.
===
IMG: parsons-writing-diary-lamp | a young man writing in a diary by lamplight
HEAD: Much of what we know about daily life in Tombstone comes from one man's private diary.
BODY: George Whitwell Parsons was a young man from the east who came to Tombstone in 1880. He had worked in a bank, and he hoped to make his fortune in mining. He was not famous, and he never became rich. But every evening, he sat down at a small table, lit his lamp and wrote in his diary. He wrote about the weather, his work, his friends, the church, the prices of things, and the many small events of the day. He wrote when he was cheerful and when he was tired or worried. Over several years, his diary grew into thousands of pages. Long after his death, it was published, and historians found it a treasure. Because of George Parsons, we know what a dusty afternoon in Tombstone felt like, what people ate, and what they talked about. Most diaries are never read by anyone else. His became a quiet window into a vanished town.
===
IMG: parsons-walking-hills-claim | a young man and a partner walking toward a small mining claim
HEAD: Like many people in Tombstone, George Parsons spent years hoping one of his claims would be rich.
BODY: Parsons and his partners bought shares in small mining claims in the hills around the town. They walked out to them over the rocky ground, dug test holes, hauled up buckets of rock with a simple hand winch, and sent samples to be tested. Most of the time, the results were disappointing. In his diary, Parsons often wrote about his hopes for a claim, and then, a few weeks later, about his disappointment. Yet he rarely gave up. He enjoyed the walks in the hills, the clear air and the views across the valleys. His story was very common. For every famous mine in Tombstone, there were dozens of small claims that never paid at all. The people who made money were often not the miners but the storekeepers, the freighters and the bankers. Still, the hope of a lucky strike kept the hills busy, and it gave men like Parsons a reason to keep walking.
===
IMG: church-sunday-congregation | a congregation singing in a simple wooden frontier church
HEAD: The oldest Protestant church still standing in Arizona was built in Tombstone, with help from George Parsons.
BODY: George Parsons was a religious man, and he was one of the people who helped to found an Episcopal church in Tombstone. A young minister named Endicott Peabody arrived from the east in 1882. He was energetic, friendly and good at sports, and the miners liked him. He even boxed with some of them. Peabody helped to raise money for a church building, and Parsons recorded the work in his diary. St. Paul's Church was built of adobe and stone, and it opened in 1882. It still stands today and is still used for services. It is the oldest Protestant church building in Arizona. Endicott Peabody later returned to Massachusetts and founded a famous school called Groton. Many years later, one of his students was a boy named Franklin Roosevelt. It is a small, surprising thread connecting a desert mining town with the history of the twentieth century.
===
IMG: hilltop-view-valley-afternoon | a young man sitting on a ridge looking over a wide golden valley
HEAD: On quiet afternoons, Parsons liked to climb the hills and look out over the valleys.
BODY: In his diary, George Parsons often described the view from the hills above the town. To the west lay the wide valley of the San Pedro River, with its line of green cottonwoods. Beyond it rose the Whetstone Mountains, and to the south the tall Huachucas. On clear days, the air was so dry that mountains fifty miles away looked close enough to walk to. Parsons enjoyed these moments of quiet. After the noise of the town and the worries about money, it was restful to sit on a rock and watch the shadows of clouds drift across the grassland. Many people who lived in Tombstone wrote about the beauty of the country around it. The sunsets were remarkable, and the stars at night were brighter than anything they had seen in the cities back east. It is easy to forget, when we think about the Old West, that people also simply sat and looked at the view.
===
IMG: boarding-house-room-night-window | a simple boarding house room at night with moonlight through the window
HEAD: At night, the small rooms of Tombstone's boarding houses were simple, plain and often shared.
BODY: Most single men in Tombstone lived in rented rooms. A room might have a narrow bed, a washstand with a jug and basin, a chair, a few hooks for clothes and a small window. Sometimes two or three men shared one room, and some miners even shared a bed, one sleeping during the day and the other at night, as their shifts changed. The walls were thin, and the sounds of the town drifted in after dark. There was the distant rhythm of the mine engines, a dog barking, footsteps on the boardwalk and, from time to time, a piano from a saloon down the street. Yet many men slept deeply after a long day underground. In winter, the rooms could be cold, and people piled on blankets and coats. In summer, they opened the windows and hoped for a breeze. It was a simple life, and for many of the young men, it was only meant to be temporary.
