{img:pacific-ocean-vast-blue-horizon-double-hulled-voyaging-canoe-small-sails}
Tonight, we travel back about a thousand years, to around the year 1000 AD. We go to the greatest ocean on Earth. The Pacific. It covers almost a third of the whole planet. It is bigger than all the land of the world put together. And scattered across it, like stars in the night sky, there are thousands of small islands. Green, and mountainous, or low and flat, surrounded by coral reefs, and white sand, and turquoise lagoons.

[pause]

On these islands live the Polynesians. They are the greatest sailors and navigators the ancient world has ever known. Long before the Europeans dared to sail out of sight of land, the Polynesians were crossing thousands of kilometres of open ocean, in great double canoes. Without compasses, without maps, without any instruments at all. They found their way by the stars, the sun, the waves, the wind, the birds, and the clouds.

[long-pause]

Over many centuries, they discovered and settled almost every island in the Pacific. A great triangle of ocean, larger than Europe and Asia together. From New Zealand in the south-west, to Easter Island in the south-east, to Hawaii in the north. Hawaii is the most remote group of islands in the whole world. More than three thousand kilometres from the nearest other islands. And the Polynesians found it.

[pause]

{img:raiatea-island-green-mountains-lagoon-village-canoes-on-beach-morning}
In this story, we will follow a group of Polynesian voyagers, as they prepare for one of these great journeys. They live on the island of Raiatea, in the Society Islands, in the middle of the Pacific. And they are going to sail north, across the equator, into the unknown, to find a land that only a few have seen. A land of great mountains, rising out of the sea, far to the north. A land that will one day be called Hawaii.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:old-navigator-grey-hair-sitting-beach-looking-at-stars-granddaughter-beside}
We will meet the people of the voyage. The old navigator, Ru, who knows the stars better than anyone alive. His granddaughter, Hina, who is thirteen, and wants to be a navigator too. Her father, Tane, the canoe builder, and her mother, Mahana, who knows all about the plants and the food. Her little brother Tavi, who is eight, and their dog, Popo. And the chief, Tama, who will lead the new settlement, and the other families who will sail with them.

[pause]

We will see how they build their great canoe, and how they choose the plants and the animals to take with them. We will sail with them, day after day, night after night, across the open ocean. We will learn how they read the stars, and the waves, and the birds. And we will be with them when, at last, after many weeks at sea, they see the great mountains of a new land, rising out of the clouds.

[pause]

{img:double-canoe-under-sail-sunset-ocean-golden-light-crew-silhouettes}
It is a story of courage, and skill, and patience. And of a deep love for the ocean, which the Polynesians did not see as a barrier, or an enemy. For them, the ocean was a road, and a home, and a living thing, connecting all the islands together, like a great family. They called it by many names. Some called it the great sea of Kiwa. And they knew it as well as we know the streets of our own town.

[pause]

So let us go to Raiatea, a thousand years ago. Let us walk along the white beach, under the coconut palms, and see the great canoe being built, on the sand. And let us meet the people who are about to sail into the unknown.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-lapita-pottery-shards-stamped-patterns-beach-sand-pacific}
The story of the Polynesians begins long before the year 1000. More than three thousand years before, in fact. Their ancestors came from the islands of South-East Asia, and before that, perhaps, from the island of Taiwan. They were farmers, and fishermen, and sailors. They spoke languages that were related to each other, and they had a special kind of pottery, decorated with beautiful stamped patterns.

[pause]

Around three thousand five hundred years ago, these people began to sail east, out into the Pacific. They reached the islands near New Guinea, and then went further, and further. They reached Fiji, and Tonga, and Samoa, about three thousand years ago. Archaeologists call them the Lapita people, after a place where their pottery was first found. They were the first people ever to live on these islands.

[long-pause]

{img:samoa-tonga-ancient-village-thatched-houses-canoes-coconut-palms}
In Tonga and Samoa, the Lapita people stayed for a long time. More than a thousand years. Slowly, over the centuries, they changed. Their language changed, and their customs, and their way of life. They stopped making pottery. And they became the Polynesians. A people with their own language, their own gods, their own stories, and their own great skill. The skill of voyaging.

[pause]

Then, many centuries later, they began to sail again. Scholars still discuss exactly when. Perhaps two thousand years ago, perhaps a good deal later. East, into the great unknown ocean, beyond Samoa. They found the Cook Islands, and the Society Islands, where our story takes place, and the Marquesas, and the Tuamotus. Many of these islands are very far apart. Hundreds, or even thousands, of kilometres of open sea. But the Polynesians found them all.

[pause]

{img:voyaging-canoe-departing-island-reef-pass-crowd-on-shore-waving}
How did they find them? Partly by skill, and partly by courage, and partly by patience. They sailed into the wind, against the usual direction of the trade winds. This seems strange. But it was very clever. If they did not find land, they could turn around, and sail home again, quickly, with the wind behind them. So they could explore, and come back safely, to tell what they had found.

[pause]

When they found a new island, they went back home, and told their families. Then a group of people would prepare a great voyage, with canoes full of plants, and animals, and food, and tools. And they would sail to the new island, to settle there, and build a new home. Generation after generation, island by island, they spread across the ocean.

[long-pause]

Around the year 1000, the Polynesians were settling the islands of the central Pacific. The Society Islands, especially Raiatea, and Tahiti, had become a great centre. From there, the voyagers set out, in every direction. To the far east, to the tiny island of Rapa Nui, which we call Easter Island. To the south-west, to the great islands of New Zealand, which they would reach a little later. And to the north, to Hawaii.

[pause]

{img:carved-wooden-figure-ancestor-polynesian-marae-stone-platform-raiatea}
The Polynesians remember their history in stories, and chants, and genealogies. They do not write. But they remember. Their chants tell of the great voyagers of the past, who found the islands. Of their canoes, and their names, and their journeys. Of the stars they followed, and the winds they sailed. Each family knows its ancestors, going back many generations, to the ones who first arrived. And some of those chants are still remembered, and sung, today.

[pause]

Ru, the old navigator, knows many of these chants. He learned them from his own grandfather, when he was a boy. And now, he is teaching them to Hina. In the evenings, they sit together on the beach, and he sings them to her, slowly, line by line. And she sings them back to him, until she knows them by heart.

[long-pause]

{img:raiatea-coast-lagoon-turquoise-coral-reef-waves-breaking-green-peaks}
Raiatea is a beautiful island. It rises out of the sea, green and steep, with tall mountains in the middle, covered in thick forest, and waterfalls, and mist. Around the island, there is a wide, calm lagoon, of clear turquoise water. And around the lagoon, there is a coral reef, where the great waves of the ocean break, in long white lines of foam, with a deep, steady roar, day and night.

[pause]

On the shore, under the coconut palms, there are villages. Houses with walls of woven leaves, and thatched roofs, made of pandanus leaves, or palm fronds. Some are small, for families. Others are big, for meetings, and ceremonies, and for the chiefs. The houses have no walls on some sides, so that the cool sea breeze can blow through. The floors are covered with soft woven mats.

[long-pause]

{img:polynesian-village-thatched-houses-palms-women-weaving-mats-children}
Around the villages, there are gardens, and plantations. The Polynesians are skilled farmers. They grow taro, in wet fields, flooded with water from the streams. Taro is a plant with big green leaves, shaped like hearts, and a starchy root, which is cooked, and pounded into a paste. They grow breadfruit, on tall trees, with big green fruits, as big as a man's head. And bananas, and yams, and sugarcane, and coconuts, everywhere.

[pause]

They keep pigs, and dogs, and chickens. And they fish. In the lagoon, they fish with nets, and spears, and traps made of woven cane, and hooks made of shell and bone. Outside the reef, in the open ocean, they fish from canoes, for tuna, and bonito, and mahi-mahi, and sometimes, sharks. The ocean gives them food, every day. They thank it, and the gods of the sea, for its gifts.

[pause]

{img:fishermen-outrigger-canoe-lagoon-throwing-net-morning-calm-water}
Raiatea is a sacred island. In the old stories, it is called Havaiki, the homeland, the place where the gods first made the world, and where the Polynesian people began. On the eastern shore, there is a great sacred place, called a marae. A wide open courtyard, paved with stones, with tall upright slabs of coral and stone, and a raised stone platform at one end. Here, the priests make offerings to the gods, and the great chiefs are honoured.

[pause]

The marae is the centre of the island's life. Here, the people gather for festivals, and ceremonies. Here, the chiefs meet, from Raiatea, and from the other islands around. And here, the voyagers come, before they set out on a great journey, to ask the gods for a safe passage, and a good landing. The canoes leave from the beach in front of the marae, through a gap in the reef, which the people call the sacred pass.

[long-pause]

Life on Raiatea is good, in many ways. The climate is warm all year round. There is plenty of food, from the land, and the sea. There is time for music, and dancing, and games, and storytelling. The people are strong, and healthy, and handsome. Later, when the first Europeans came to these islands, they thought it was a paradise.

[pause]

{img:polynesian-dance-evening-firelight-drums-dancers-beach-palms}
But there are also problems. The island is not very big. And over many generations, the number of people has grown. Now, there is not quite enough land, for everyone. The chiefs sometimes quarrel, over land, and power. And younger sons, and their families, have no land of their own. So some people decide to leave. To sail away, and find a new land, where they can make a new beginning.

[pause]

This is one of the reasons why the Polynesians are always voyaging. Not only for adventure, although they love adventure. But because they need new land. And because, in their hearts, they are explorers. They want to know what lies beyond the horizon. And they are not afraid to go and find out.

[long-pause]

{img:polynesian-family-outside-thatched-house-father-mother-girl-boy-dog-grandfather}
Hina is thirteen years old. She lives with her family in a house near the beach, on the eastern side of Raiatea. She is tall, and strong, and a very good swimmer. She can dive to the bottom of the lagoon, and stay under the water for a long time, looking at the fish, and the coral. She is quiet, and thoughtful, and she notices everything. The shape of the clouds, the colour of the sea, the flight of the birds.

[pause]

Her grandfather, Ru, is the greatest navigator on the island. He is about sixty years old, with long grey hair, tied back, and a face lined by the sun and the wind. His body is covered with beautiful tattoos, in dark blue patterns, which show his rank, and his family, and his achievements. He has made many long voyages, to other islands, far away. And he has been to the land in the north, once, when he was a young man.

[long-pause]

{img:grandfather-teaching-girl-star-positions-with-stones-in-sand-beach-night}
Ru has chosen Hina to be his student. This is unusual. Most navigators teach their sons, or their grandsons. But Ru says that Hina has the gift. She notices things. She remembers things. She feels the ocean, the way a navigator must. So, for the last two years, he has been teaching her. The names of the stars, and where they rise and set. The patterns of the waves. The habits of the birds. It is a lifetime of learning. And she has only just begun.

[pause]

Hina's father, Tane, is a canoe builder. He is a big, strong, gentle man, with clever hands. He has built many canoes, small ones for fishing, and big ones for voyaging. He knows which trees to choose, and how to shape the hulls, and how to tie them together, so that they are strong, and flexible, and fast. Now, he is building the greatest canoe of his life, for the voyage to the north.

[pause]

{img:polynesian-mother-preparing-plant-cuttings-baskets-taro-breadfruit-shoots}
Hina's mother, Mahana, knows everything about plants. She knows which plants to grow, and when, and where, and how. She knows how to keep them alive, on a long journey, at sea. She knows how to prepare food that will last for weeks, without going bad. And she knows how to make medicines, from leaves, and roots, and bark. On the voyage, she will be in charge of the plants, and the food. It is a great responsibility.

[pause]

Tavi is eight years old. He is cheerful, and noisy, and always hungry. He loves the dog, Popo, a small brown dog, with a curly tail, who follows him everywhere. Tavi is very excited about the voyage. He has never been further than the next island. He asks his grandfather a hundred questions a day. Will there be monsters? Will there be other people? Will there be coconuts? Ru answers all his questions, patiently, with a smile.

[long-pause]

The family has decided to go on the voyage together. It was not an easy decision. They will leave their home, and their friends, and their relatives, and the graves of their ancestors. They may never come back. But Tane is a younger son, and has no land of his own. And Ru says that the land in the north is big, and empty, and beautiful. With great mountains, and rivers, and forests, and room for everyone. Room for a new beginning.

[pause]

{img:family-sitting-together-on-mat-evening-lamp-talking-decision-gentle}
The chief, Tama, will lead the voyage. He is a young chief, about thirty years old, the son of a great chief of Raiatea. He is brave, and wise, and respected. He has chosen the people who will go with him. About twenty men, women, and children, from several families. They are the best and the strongest. Fishermen, farmers, builders, weavers, and a priest. And Ru, the navigator, who will find the way.

[pause]

In the evenings, the families who are going meet together, on the beach, by the fire. They talk about the voyage, and make plans. What to take, and what to leave. How much food, how much water. Which plants, which animals. And they talk about the new land, and dream about it. What will it be like? Will it be as beautiful as Ru says? Will they be happy there? Nobody knows. But they are ready to find out.

[long-pause]

{img:canoe-builders-shaping-large-log-hull-stone-adzes-beach-shade-palms}
On the beach, near the village, under a great shelter of palm leaves, the canoe is being built. It is a double canoe. Two long hulls, side by side, joined together by strong wooden beams, with a platform between them. It is about twenty metres long, as long as four cars, end to end. It will carry about twenty people, and all their plants, and animals, and food, and water. It is the most important thing on the island.

[pause]

Tane is in charge of the building. He works with a team of skilled men, and their apprentices. They have been working on it for more than a year. First, they had to find the right trees. They went up into the forests in the mountains, with the priest, to choose them. The trees had to be tall, and straight, and strong, and without any rot. When they found them, the priest said prayers, and made offerings, to the god of the forest, to ask his permission to cut them down.

[long-pause]

{img:polynesian-craftsmen-stone-adze-blade-basalt-sharpening-tools-workshop}
They cut down the trees, with stone adzes. Sharp blades of hard black basalt stone, tied to wooden handles. The Polynesians have no metal. All their tools are made of stone, bone, shell, and wood. It took many days to cut down each tree. Then they dragged the logs down the mountain, to the beach, with ropes, and rollers, and many strong men, singing as they pulled.

[pause]

On the beach, they began to shape the hulls. They hollowed out the logs, with their adzes, and with fire, burning the wood a little, and then scraping out the charcoal. Slowly, the hulls took shape. Long, and narrow, and smooth, and curved, like the body of a fish. They added planks along the sides, to make them deeper, sewn on with strong cord, through holes drilled in the wood. Then they sealed the joints, with sap from the breadfruit tree, and coconut fibre, so that no water could come in.

[pause]

{img:men-lashing-crossbeams-to-twin-hulls-sennit-cord-close-detail-work}
Now, they are joining the two hulls together. They lay strong wooden beams across them, and tie them down, with cord, made from coconut fibre. This cord is called sennit. It is made by the women and the old men, who twist the fibres together, between their hands, and their thighs, to make long, strong cords. The canoe needs thousands of metres of sennit. Every joint, every beam, every plank is tied with it. There are no nails, no screws, no metal at all.

[pause]

The lashings are not only strong. They are also flexible. When the canoe is at sea, in the big waves, the hulls can move a little, against each other, without breaking. A canoe that was too stiff would crack, in a storm. A canoe tied with sennit bends, and moves, like a living thing, and survives. The canoe builders tie the lashings in beautiful patterns, which are also sacred. Each pattern has a meaning, and a prayer.

[long-pause]

Hina often comes to watch her father at work. She sits on the sand, in the shade, and watches. Sometimes, she helps, twisting sennit, or carrying water, or holding a beam in place. Tane explains everything to her. Why this beam goes here. Why this lashing is tied this way. How the hulls are shaped, to cut through the waves. Hina listens carefully. A navigator must understand her canoe, as well as the stars.

[pause]

{img:nearly-finished-double-canoe-beach-carved-prow-figure-evening-light}
At the front of each hull, the carvers make a beautiful carved figure. A figure of a god, or an ancestor, to protect the canoe, and guide it, on its journey. They carve it from hard wood, with great care, and paint it, with red and black. Then the priest blesses it, with chants, and offerings. The canoe is not just a boat. It is a sacred thing. It has its own spirit, and its own name.

[pause]

The canoe's name is chosen by Ru. He calls it Te Manu o te Moana, the Bird of the Ocean. Because, he says, it will fly across the sea, like a great bird, to the land in the north. And it will carry the people safely, under its wings.

[long-pause]

{img:women-weaving-pandanus-sail-mats-large-triangular-sail-shade-village}
While the men build the hulls, the women make the sails. The sails are made of pandanus leaves. The pandanus is a tree that grows along the beaches, with long, thin, tough leaves, with little spikes along the edges. The women cut the leaves, and dry them in the sun, and strip off the spikes, and soften them. Then they cut them into long, narrow strips, and weave them together, into big mats.

[pause]

The weaving is done in the shade of the houses, by groups of women, sitting on the ground, talking and singing as they work. Their fingers move very fast, in and out, over and under, weaving the strips together, tightly, so that the wind cannot get through. Mahana is one of the best weavers on the island. Hina helps her, and learns. It is slow, patient work. A single sail takes many weeks.

[long-pause]

{img:crab-claw-sail-shape-voyaging-canoe-mast-wind-filled-blue-sky}
When the mats are finished, they are sewn together, to make the sails. The sails of a Polynesian canoe have a special shape. They are tall, and triangular, with the point at the bottom, and a curved top, between two long poles. People today call this shape a crab claw, because it looks like the claw of a crab, open, pointing up at the sky. It is a very good shape for catching the wind. It can sail fast, and it can sail across the wind, and even a little into it.

[pause]

The double canoe has two masts, one on each hull, and two sails. The masts are made of strong, light wood, and they can be taken down, in a storm. The sails are held up by ropes, made of sennit, and they can be turned, to catch the wind from different directions. The crew must know how to handle them, in every kind of weather. It takes strength, and skill, and practice.

[pause]

{img:canoe-sea-trials-lagoon-crew-adjusting-sails-steering-paddle-calm}
The canoe is steered by a great paddle, at the back. A long, heavy wooden paddle, held by a strong man, or two. It is called the steering paddle. By pushing it, or pulling it, the steersman can turn the canoe, left or right. On a long voyage, the steersmen take turns, day and night. They must watch the navigator, and follow his signals. A raised hand, a nod, a word. And keep the canoe on its course.

[pause]

When the canoe is finished, they test it. First in the calm water of the lagoon, then outside the reef, in the open sea. They sail it for days, back and forth, in every kind of wind. They check the lashings, and the hulls, and the sails, and the steering. They find small problems, and fix them. A lashing that is too loose. A sail that is too heavy. A leak, in one of the hulls. By the end, the canoe is ready. Fast, and strong, and safe.

[long-pause]

On the platform between the hulls, there is a small house. A little shelter, with a roof of pandanus leaves, and low walls of woven mats. It is where the people can rest, and sleep, out of the sun, and the rain, and the spray. It is small, and crowded. But it is the only shelter on the whole canoe, for weeks. Under the platform, and in the hulls, there is space for storage. Food, and water, and plants, and tools, and everything else.

[pause]

{img:canoe-deck-platform-small-thatched-shelter-mats-baskets-gourds-lashed}
There is also a fireplace, on the platform. A box, filled with sand, and stones, where a small fire can be lit, very carefully, for cooking. Fire on a wooden canoe is dangerous. So it is only used in calm weather, and always watched. Most of the food on the voyage will be eaten cold, or cooked before they leave.

[pause]

Tavi is allowed to sail on the canoe, during the tests. He sits on the platform, holding on tightly, with Popo in his lap. The canoe leaps over the waves, and the spray flies, and the wind sings in the ropes. Tavi shouts with joy. Popo barks. And Ru, standing at the front, watching the sea, smiles, and says that the Bird of the Ocean is ready to fly.

[long-pause]

{img:star-compass-stones-in-circle-on-sand-navigator-teaching-night-beach}
Every night, on the beach, Ru teaches Hina the stars. He makes a great circle on the sand, with stones, or with pieces of coral. A circle, divided into many parts, like the slices of a pie. Each part is called a house. And each house is named after a star, or a group of stars, that rises from that part of the horizon, in the east, and sets in the same part, on the other side, in the west.

[pause]

This is the star compass. It is in the navigator's head, not in his hands. Every star rises at its own point on the horizon, every night, a little earlier each night. And it sets at its own point. If you know where each star rises and sets, you always know your direction. You know where north is, and south, and east, and west, and every point between, all night long, as the stars move across the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:navigator-and-girl-looking-up-starry-sky-pointing-milky-way-palms}
Ru knows the rising and setting points of more than a hundred and fifty stars. He knows which stars rise in which seasons. He knows which stars pass directly over which islands, at their highest point in the sky. He knows the stars that guide the way to every island he has ever visited. And he knows the star that guides the way to the land in the north. A bright, orange star, which rises high in the north-east. It passes right over the land in the north, every night, at its highest point.

[pause]

Hina has learned the names of about fifty stars, so far. She lies on her back on the sand, beside her grandfather, and looks up at the sky. He points to a star, and she says its name, and where it rises, and where it sets. If she gets it wrong, he tells her, patiently, and she tries again. Night after night, month after month. Slowly, the sky becomes her friend. She knows it, the way she knows the faces of her family.

[pause]

{img:waves-swells-patterns-ocean-surface-sunlight-crossing-directions}
But the stars are only part of it. Ru also teaches her about the waves. Out in the open ocean, there are long, slow waves, called swells. They are made by winds, far, far away. They travel across the ocean, for thousands of kilometres, in the same direction, for days. A good navigator can feel them, through the canoe. He can tell which direction they come from, and use them to keep his course, even when the stars are hidden by clouds.

[pause]

Ru says that the best navigators feel the swells with their bodies, not their eyes. Some of them lie down in the hull of the canoe, and feel the movement, through their back. They can feel several different swells at once, coming from different directions, crossing each other. And they know which is which. Hina tries it, lying in a canoe in the lagoon, with her eyes closed. But the lagoon is too calm. She will have to learn it at sea.

[long-pause]

He teaches her about the sun, too. Where it rises, and where it sets, in each season. How to use it, in the morning and the evening, to find direction. And about the wind. Which winds blow in which seasons, and from which directions. The steady trade winds, from the east. And the changing winds, near the equator, which can be calm, or sudden, or stormy.

[pause]

{img:navigator-memorising-chants-girl-repeating-sitting-canoe-shade-afternoon}
And he teaches her about the signs of land. The birds, which fly out to sea in the morning, to fish, and fly back to land in the evening, to sleep. The clouds, which gather over islands, and stay there, while other clouds move on. The colour of the sky, and the sea, near land. The smell of the land, on the wind. The floating leaves, and branches, and coconuts. All these signs can tell a navigator that land is near, even when he cannot see it.

[pause]

All of this knowledge is held in Ru's memory. Nothing is written down. It is passed on, from navigator to navigator, through chants, and stories, and long years of practice. Ru says that a navigator must hold the whole ocean in his mind. And the canoe, and the people, and the island he has left, and the island he is going to. It is a great weight to carry. But it is also a great honour. And Hina is ready to carry it, one day.

[long-pause]

{img:canoe-plants-breadfruit-shoots-taro-in-baskets-coconuts-banana-suckers}
A voyage of settlement is not like an ordinary voyage. The people are not just going to visit a new land. They are going to live there, for the rest of their lives, and their children after them. So they must take with them everything they will need, to make a new home. And the most important things of all are the plants.

[pause]

Mahana has been preparing the plants for months. She knows that the new land may have very few useful plants of its own. Ru says that the land in the north has forests, and ferns, and flowers. But it has no taro, no breadfruit, no bananas, no coconuts, or very few. So the voyagers must bring them all. Living plants, and cuttings, and roots, and seeds, which must survive many weeks at sea.

[long-pause]

{img:mother-and-daughter-wrapping-plant-roots-moist-moss-leaves-careful-hands}
She chooses the best plants, from the best gardens on the island. Taro, of many different kinds. Some for wet fields, and some for dry. She takes the tops of the taro plants, with a little piece of root, and wraps them in damp moss, and leaves, to keep them alive. Breadfruit is harder. It does not grow from seeds. So she takes young shoots, from the roots of the trees, and plants them in baskets of soil, and covers them, and waters them every day.

[pause]

She takes bananas, and sugarcane, and yams, and the roots of the ti plant, whose leaves are used for wrapping food, and for making skirts, and for blessings. She takes the paper mulberry tree, whose bark is beaten into a soft cloth, called tapa, for clothes and blankets. She takes turmeric, and arrowroot, and the kava plant, whose root makes a sacred drink. And, of course, coconuts. Hundreds of them. Some for eating and drinking, and some for planting.

[pause]

{img:pigs-chickens-dogs-in-woven-cages-on-canoe-platform-tavi-feeding}
The voyagers also take animals. Pigs, in strong woven cages. Chickens, in baskets. And dogs. Polynesian dogs are small, and quiet, and gentle. Some are pets, like Popo. Others are kept for food, which the Polynesians eat on special occasions. The animals must be fed, and watered, and kept calm, on the long voyage. This will be Tavi's job, with the other children. He is very proud of it.

[pause]

There will be other passengers too, who have not been invited. Small Pacific rats, who hide in the baskets, and the food, and the hulls of the canoe. Nobody wants them. But they always come, on every voyage. And in the new land, they will spread, and multiply, and become a nuisance, for ever after.

[long-pause]

Then there is the food for the voyage itself. It must last for many weeks, without going bad, in the heat and the salt spray. Mahana and the other women prepare it carefully. They make a paste of fermented breadfruit, which can last for months, wrapped in leaves, and buried in pits, and then dug up, and packed in baskets. They dry fish, and squid, in the sun, until they are hard. They bake taro, and sweet bananas, and wrap them in leaves.

[pause]

{img:women-preparing-voyage-food-fermented-breadfruit-pits-leaf-parcels-drying-fish}
And there is water. Water is the most precious thing, on a long voyage. Without it, the voyagers will not survive. They carry it in gourds, and in long sections of bamboo, sealed at the ends, and in coconut shells. And they carry hundreds of green coconuts, which are full of sweet, clean coconut water. On the way, they will also catch rainwater, whenever it rains, in the sails, and in mats, and in every container they have.

[pause]

Everything is packed carefully, on the canoe. In the hulls, under the platform, and on the platform itself, under covers, to keep out the sun, and the spray. Heavy things low, light things high. Food where it can be reached easily. Plants where they can be watered. Animals where they can be fed. Tane checks everything, again and again. A badly packed canoe is a dangerous canoe.

[pause]

Mahana goes through her list, in her head, every night, before she sleeps. Taro, breadfruit, banana, coconut, sugarcane, yam, ti, tapa, turmeric, arrowroot, kava. Pigs, chickens, dogs. Fermented breadfruit, dried fish, baked taro. Water, coconuts, gourds. She hopes she has not forgotten anything. Because once they leave, there will be no going back for it.

[long-pause]

{img:marae-ceremony-before-voyage-priest-chanting-offerings-stone-platform-dawn}
The time of departure is chosen carefully. Ru watches the stars, and the winds, and the weather, for many weeks. He waits for the right season, when the winds will be favourable, and the weather calm. He knows that in a certain month, the winds near the equator are usually lighter, and the storms are fewer. And he watches the moon. They will leave just after the new moon, so that the moon will grow, night by night, and give them light, in the middle of the voyage.

[pause]

On the morning of the departure, before dawn, everyone gathers at the marae. The whole island has come. Hundreds of people, from all the villages. They come to say goodbye, and to pray for the voyagers. The priests, in their white tapa robes, stand on the stone platform, and chant the ancient chants, asking the gods for a safe journey. Offerings are made. Food, and pigs, and fine mats, and red feathers, which are very sacred.

[long-pause]

{img:families-saying-goodbye-beach-embracing-tears-garlands-flowers-canoe-waiting}
Then the voyagers go down to the beach, where the canoe is waiting, in the shallow water of the lagoon. There are many farewells. Families embracing, and crying, and laughing. Old people giving their blessings. Friends exchanging gifts. Garlands of flowers are placed around the necks of the voyagers. Hina's best friend, Teura, gives her a necklace of small white shells, which she made herself. Hina promises to wear it always.

[pause]

Ru's old sister, who is too old to come, holds his hands, for a long time. She says she will watch the stars every night, and think of him. He tells her that he will see the same stars, and think of her. Then he kisses her forehead, and turns, and walks into the water, to the canoe. He does not look back. A navigator must always look forward.

[pause]

{img:voyaging-canoe-paddled-through-reef-pass-sunrise-crowd-on-beach-waving}
The voyagers climb aboard. The canoe is very full. Twenty-two people, plants, animals, food, water. They find their places, on the platform, and in the hulls. Tavi sits with Popo, near the animal cages. Mahana checks her plants, one last time. Tane checks the lashings. Chief Tama stands at the front, beside Ru. And the steersmen take their places, at the great steering paddle.

[pause]

Then the conch shell is blown, a long, deep, mournful sound, across the lagoon. The men pick up their paddles, and begin to paddle, in rhythm, singing a paddling chant. The canoe moves slowly away from the beach, across the lagoon, toward the gap in the reef. The people on the beach sing, and wave, and call out their farewells. Their voices follow the canoe, across the water.

[long-pause]

The canoe passes through the gap in the reef, into the open ocean. The waves are bigger here, and the canoe rises and falls, gently. The men stop paddling, and raise the sails. The pandanus sails fill with wind, and the canoe begins to move, faster and faster, through the water. The voyagers look back, at the island, growing smaller behind them. Green mountains, white beach, palm trees, and the tiny figures of their families, still waving.

[pause]

{img:island-fading-behind-canoe-horizon-sails-full-morning-sun-open-ocean}
Hina stands at the back of the canoe, and watches the island, for a long time. Her home. The only place she has ever known. She watches until the beach disappears, and then the palms, and then the hills. Until only the tops of the mountains are visible, blue and faint, on the horizon. And then, at last, they too disappear. And there is nothing around them but the ocean, in every direction.

[pause]

Hina touches her shell necklace, and turns, and looks forward, to the north. Her grandfather is standing at the front of the canoe, watching the sea, and the sky. She goes to stand beside him. He puts his hand on her shoulder. And together, they look toward the horizon, where the new land is waiting.

[long-pause]

{img:canoe-open-ocean-day-two-crew-resting-platform-steady-trade-wind-blue}
The first days at sea are busy, and a little strange. Everyone must get used to life on the canoe. The movement of the waves, up and down, and side to side, all day and all night. The cramped space, with so many people, and animals, and baskets. The sun, the wind, the salt spray. Some people are seasick, at first, and lie on the platform, feeling miserable. Tavi is sick on the first day. But by the third day, he is fine, and hungry again.

[pause]

The canoe sails north-east, at first, into the trade wind. Ru has a plan. He knows that the winds near the equator will push them west. So he sails east first, to make room. Then, when they reach the right place, he will turn north, and let the wind carry them to the land. It is like aiming an arrow, a little to one side, because you know the wind will blow it back.

[long-pause]

{img:steersman-holding-great-steering-paddle-watching-navigator-at-bow-sunlight}
Life on board soon settles into a routine. The crew is divided into watches, like on a ship. Some people work, while others rest. The steersmen take turns at the steering paddle, a few hours at a time. Others handle the sails, adjusting them to the wind. Others bail out the water, which comes in through the joints of the hulls, and over the sides, from the waves. Bailing never stops, day or night.

[pause]

Ru hardly sleeps. He is the navigator, and he must always know where they are. He sits at the front of the canoe, or at the back, watching the sea, and the sky, and the sun, and the stars. He sleeps only for short moments, an hour here, an hour there, sitting up. When he sleeps, Hina watches for him. She keeps the course, by the stars or the swells, and wakes him if anything changes.

[pause]

{img:girl-sitting-at-bow-watching-horizon-wind-in-hair-concentrating-ocean}
Every morning, at sunrise, Ru studies the sky in the east. He watches where the sun rises, and compares it with the stars that rose there, just before dawn. He feels the swells, and notes their direction. He remembers how far they have sailed, how fast, and in which direction. And from all of this, he works out where they are. Not with numbers, or maps. But in his head. He imagines the canoe as standing still, and the islands moving past it, around the edge of the ocean.

[pause]

It is very hard to explain, and very hard to learn. Hina asks him how he does it. He says that it is like walking through your own house, in the dark. You cannot see. But you know where everything is. You know how many steps to the door, and where the table is, and where your bed is. The ocean is his house. He has been walking through it, in the dark, for fifty years.

[long-pause]

The people on the canoe pass the time in many ways. They talk, and tell stories, and sing. They play games, with string, making shapes between their fingers. They weave, and mend the nets, and make sennit. They fish, with long lines, trailing behind the canoe. They sleep, in the little house, or on the platform, under the sky. And they watch the ocean, for hours, in every mood and colour.

[pause]

{img:voyagers-string-figures-games-singing-afternoon-shade-platform-calm-sea}
The ocean is never the same, from one hour to the next. In the morning, it is grey, and silver, and then blue. At midday, it is a deep, brilliant blue, almost purple, so clear that you can see far down into it. In the evening, it turns gold, and pink, and orange, with the sunset. And at night, it is black, with the reflections of the stars, and sometimes, little sparks of green light, glowing in the waves, made by tiny living creatures.

[pause]

Tavi loves the glowing waves. At night, he leans over the edge of the platform, holding on tightly, and trails his hand in the water. Every movement of his hand makes the water sparkle, with little green lights, like fireflies. He shows Popo, who is not impressed. But Hina is. She sits beside her brother, and they make patterns in the glowing water, together, under the stars.

[long-pause]

{img:night-sea-canoe-under-brilliant-stars-milky-way-navigator-silhouette-bow}
The nights at sea are the most beautiful time of the voyage. When the sun goes down, the wind often grows softer, and the sea calmer. The sky darkens, from blue to purple to black. And then the stars come out. Not just a few, as on land, near the fires of the village. But thousands, and thousands, more than anyone could count, from horizon to horizon, all around the canoe.

[pause]

Out here, on the open ocean, there is no land, no light, nothing to block the view. The sky is a great dome, covering everything. The Milky Way stretches across it, like a river of light, or a long cloud of shining dust. The bright stars shine, in their patterns, and the faint ones fill the spaces between. And the sea reflects them all, so that the canoe seems to float in the middle of the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:navigator-pointing-rising-star-horizon-girl-watching-dark-ocean-night}
This is when Ru does his most important work. He sits at the front of the canoe, and watches the horizon, in the east. He waits for the stars to rise. When a star appears, just above the sea, he knows exactly where it is rising, on his star compass. And so he knows in which direction the canoe is heading. He signals to the steersman, with his hand, to steer a little more to the left, or to the right.

[pause]

Each star is useful only for a short time. When it is low, near the horizon, it shows the direction clearly. But as it climbs higher in the sky, it becomes harder to use. So the navigator must use one star, and then the next, and then the next, all night long. Ru calls them a star path. A line of stars, rising one after another, at the same point on the horizon, showing the way, like stepping stones across a river.

[pause]

{img:girl-memorising-star-path-lying-on-platform-counting-stars-quiet}
Hina sits beside him, every night, and learns. He tells her the names of the stars, as they rise. This is the star of the fisherman. This is the star of the canoe prow. This is the bird star. She repeats them, and remembers them, and watches where they rise. And sometimes, when he is tired, he lets her guide the canoe, for an hour or two, while he rests. She is very nervous, the first time. But she does it well.

[pause]

In the middle of the night, the canoe is very quiet. Most of the people are asleep, curled up on the platform, or in the little house, under blankets of tapa. Only the steersman, and the navigator, and a few others are awake. The sails creak softly. The water swishes and gurgles, along the hulls. The ropes hum, in the wind. And now and then, a pig grunts, or a chicken clucks, in its sleep.

[long-pause]

Sometimes, the clouds come, and hide the stars. Then Ru must use the swells instead. He lies down on the platform, on his back, and closes his eyes, and feels the movement of the canoe. The long, slow lift and fall of the swells, coming from the east. And another, smaller swell, from the south. He feels them, with his whole body. And he knows, from the way they move the canoe, whether they are still on course.

[pause]

{img:navigator-lying-on-back-eyes-closed-feeling-swells-cloudy-night-canoe}
Ru also watches the moon. Each night, it grows a little bigger, and rises a little later. It gives light, for the work on the canoe, and makes the sea shine silver. Its path across the sky shows direction too. And the tides of the moon, Ru says, also move the sea, in their own way, which a navigator must understand.

[pause]

Then, before dawn, the sky in the east slowly begins to grow pale. The stars fade, one by one. The last stars to fade are the brightest ones. Ru watches them, until the very last moment, to check his course. Then the sun rises, out of the sea, in a blaze of gold. Another night has passed. And the canoe is a little closer to the land in the north.

[long-pause]

{img:long-ocean-swell-lifting-double-canoe-trade-wind-whitecaps-blue-sky}
After about ten days, the canoe has sailed a long way east and north. They have left the islands of the south far behind. Now, they are in the great empty ocean, where there are no islands at all, for thousands of kilometres. Just the sea, and the sky, and the wind, and the waves. Day after day, night after night, it is the same. And yet, it is never the same.

[pause]

The trade wind blows steadily, from the east, day and night. It is a strong, warm, reliable wind. The canoe sails across it, with its sails full, leaning a little to one side, cutting through the waves. The waves come from the east too, pushed by the wind. They are long, and rolling, and blue, with white tops, which the wind blows into spray. The canoe climbs each wave, and slides down the other side, again and again, thousands of times a day.

[long-pause]

{img:spray-flying-over-canoe-hull-crew-laughing-wet-sunlight-rainbow-mist}
The people on the canoe are wet most of the time. The spray flies over the hulls, and onto the platform. Their skin is sticky with salt. Their hair is stiff with it. Their clothes, made of tapa, and woven leaves, are always damp. But it is warm, so they do not mind too much. And when the sun comes out, they dry quickly. Sometimes, in the spray, there are little rainbows, which come and go, as the canoe moves.

[pause]

Tane is always checking the canoe. He crawls along the hulls, checking the lashings, one by one. He listens to the sounds of the canoe, the creaks, and groans, and squeaks, and knows what each one means. When a lashing comes loose, he ties it again. When a leak appears, he plugs it with coconut fibre, and breadfruit sap. The canoe is like a living thing, he says. It needs looking after, every day.

[pause]

{img:canoe-builder-checking-lashings-along-hull-tools-sennit-coil-careful}
Ru feels the swells, all the time. There are several of them, coming from different directions. The main swell comes from the east, with the trade wind. But there is another, longer, slower swell, coming from the south, from a great storm, far away, near the ice at the bottom of the world. And now, as they sail further north, Ru begins to feel a new swell, coming from the north. It is made by winter storms, in the far north of the ocean.

[pause]

He shows Hina how to feel it. She sits beside him, and closes her eyes, and concentrates. At first, she feels nothing but the main swell, from the east. Then, slowly, she begins to feel the other ones. The long, slow one from the south. And the new one, from the north. They cross each other, under the canoe, and make a pattern, a kind of rhythm. Like music, Ru says. You must learn to hear each instrument, in the music.

[long-pause]

There are some days of rough weather. The wind grows stronger, and the waves bigger, and the sky grey. The canoe climbs the waves, and crashes down, and the spray flies everywhere. The people huddle in the little house, or hold on tightly, on the platform. The sails are made smaller, and the canoe slows down. It is uncomfortable, and a little frightening. But the canoe is strong. It bends, and moves, and rides the waves, like a seabird.

[pause]

{img:calm-evening-after-rough-day-crew-resting-sea-settling-golden-light}
And then, after a day or two, the weather calms again. The sun comes out, and the waves grow smaller, and the people come out of the little house, and stretch, and laugh, and dry their clothes. Tane checks the canoe, and finds that everything is fine. Mahana checks her plants, and finds that they are all still alive. Tavi checks the animals, and finds that Popo has eaten a dried fish he was not supposed to. And Ru checks the stars, that night, and smiles. They are still on course.

[long-pause]

{img:voyagers-sharing-meal-platform-coconut-halves-breadfruit-paste-leaves}
On the canoe, food is carefully shared. Mahana is in charge of it, with the other women. Every morning and every evening, they open the baskets, and give everyone their share. Not too much, and not too little. The food must last for the whole voyage, and there must be some left over, in case the voyage takes longer than expected. Nobody knows exactly how long it will take.

[pause]

The main food is the fermented breadfruit paste. It is sour, and strong smelling, and sticky. Not everyone likes it. Tavi says it smells like old feet. But it is very filling, and it lasts for months. They eat it mixed with coconut cream, which makes it sweeter, and softer. With it, they eat dried fish, or a piece of baked taro, or a sweet banana, while the bananas last. And they drink coconut water, from the green coconuts.

[long-pause]

{img:fishing-line-trolling-behind-canoe-man-pulling-in-tuna-splash-sunlight}
But the voyagers do not live only on what they brought. They also catch fresh food, from the sea. Behind the canoe, they trail long fishing lines, with hooks made of shell, and bone, shaped like little fish, which flash in the water. Tuna, and bonito, and mahi-mahi, chase the hooks, and bite. Then there is a great shout, and the men pull in the line, hand over hand, and a big, shining fish comes flapping onto the platform.

[pause]

Fresh fish is a great treat, after days of dried food. They eat it raw, cut into small pieces, with a little sea water, and coconut. It is delicious. Or, on calm days, they cook it, on the little fire, in its box of sand. The smell of cooking fish fills the canoe, and everyone gathers round. The first piece always goes to the gods, thrown back into the sea, as a thank you.

[pause]

At night, sometimes, flying fish land on the canoe. Flying fish are small, silver fish, with long fins, like wings. When they are chased by bigger fish, they jump out of the water, and glide through the air, for long distances, just above the waves. At night, they are attracted by the light of the fire, or the lamps, and they fly onto the platform, by accident, and flap about. The children catch them, and they are cooked for breakfast.

[pause]

Water is even more carefully shared than food. Each person gets a small amount, every day, from the gourds and the bamboo. They drink it slowly, a sip at a time, making it last. Green coconuts are opened, one by one, and shared. Nobody drinks sea water. Ru says it will make you sick, and mad, and will kill you, in the end. Tavi asks why the fish can drink it. Ru says the fish are different, and the sea is their home, not ours.

[long-pause]

{img:rain-squall-crew-catching-rainwater-in-sails-and-mats-gourds-joy}
When it rains, everyone is happy. A rain squall comes across the sea, a grey curtain of rain, hanging from a dark cloud. As it passes over the canoe, the voyagers spread out the sails, and mats, and leaves, to catch the rain. They fill every gourd, every coconut shell, every container they can find. And they drink as much as they can, and wash the salt off their skin, and their hair. The rain is cool, and fresh, and sweet.

[pause]

Mahana also uses the rain to water her plants. The plants are very precious. If they die, the new settlement will have no food. So she checks them every day, and waters them, with fresh water, and keeps them out of the salt spray, and the hot sun. Some of them have already begun to wilt. She worries about them. But most of them are still green, and alive. She talks to them, softly, sometimes, when she thinks nobody is listening.

[pause]

{img:mother-tending-plants-in-baskets-under-cover-canoe-gentle-hands-shade}
Hina hears her mother talking to the plants, one evening. Mahana is embarrassed, and laughs. But then she explains. She says that the plants are like children. They are leaving their home, as we are, and going to a strange new land. They are frightened, and tired, and thirsty. So she talks to them, and encourages them, and tells them to be brave. Hina thinks about this. And the next day, she talks to the breadfruit too.

[long-pause]

{img:becalmed-canoe-glassy-ocean-limp-sails-hot-sun-voyagers-waiting-patient}
After about two weeks, the canoe comes to the equator. The line around the middle of the world, where the north and the south meet. The Polynesians do not have a word for the equator. But they know this part of the ocean well. It is a strange place, where the winds behave differently. The trade winds from the north and the south meet here, and fight, and cancel each other out. Sometimes there is no wind at all.

[pause]

One morning, the wind dies away. The sails go limp, and hang from the masts. The canoe slows down, and stops. The sea becomes smooth, and flat, like glass, reflecting the sky. The sun beats down, hot and bright, from directly overhead. There is no shade, except in the little house. And there is no sound, except the gentle lapping of the water against the hulls.

[long-pause]

{img:paddlers-moving-canoe-slowly-glassy-sea-heat-shimmer-horizon}
For a whole day, they wait. Nobody can do anything. The steersman has nothing to steer. The navigator has nowhere to navigate. Some of the men take out the paddles, and paddle, slowly, to keep the canoe moving north. But it is hot, hard work, and they cannot do it for long. Most people just lie in the shade, and rest, and wait for the wind to come back.

[pause]

Ru is calm. He has been through calms like this before, many times. He knows that the wind always comes back, in the end. He tells the others not to worry. He sits at the front of the canoe, and watches the sky, and the sea, for signs. He tells stories, to pass the time. Stories of the great voyagers of the past, who crossed this same calm sea, long ago.

[pause]

{img:dark-rain-clouds-towering-over-calm-sea-sunbeams-canoe-small-below}
In the afternoon, great clouds begin to build up, all around the horizon. Huge, towering clouds, white on top, and dark grey underneath. They grow taller, and taller, like mountains in the sky. Then they begin to move. A squall comes across the sea, toward the canoe. First, there is a cool breeze, and the sails flutter. Then a sudden gust of wind, and the canoe leans over. And then the rain. Heavy, warm, tropical rain, pouring down.

[pause]

The squall passes quickly. In half an hour, it is gone, and the sun comes out again, and the sea is calm. But the voyagers have filled all their water containers. And the wind has pushed them a little further north. Then another squall comes, and another. All through the evening, and into the night, the squalls come and go, bringing wind, and rain, and then calm again. It is a strange, changeable kind of weather.

[long-pause]

For three days, the canoe moves slowly north, through the calms and the squalls, from one patch of wind to the next. Ru uses every breath of wind, every squall, to push them a little further. At night, the stars help him. In the day, the sun. When there is no wind, they paddle. When the rain comes, they drink. And slowly, slowly, they make their way across the equator, into the northern half of the world.

[pause]

{img:new-trade-wind-filling-sails-canoe-moving-again-crew-cheering-blue-sea}
On the fourth day, a new wind comes. Steady, and fresh, and cool, from the north-east. It is the northern trade wind. Ru feels it, on his face, early in the morning, and smiles. The sails fill, and the canoe begins to move again, faster, and faster, cutting through the waves. Everyone cheers. Tavi dances on the platform, until his mother tells him to sit down, before he falls into the sea.

[pause]

Ru says that the hardest part of the voyage is over. From here, the wind will carry them north, toward the land. They must be patient, for another week or two. And they must watch carefully, for the signs. Because the land in the north is far away, and small, in the great ocean. And it would be very easy to sail past it, without ever seeing it.

[long-pause]

{img:dolphins-leaping-beside-voyaging-canoe-bow-wave-children-watching-joy}
On the long voyage, the voyagers are never really alone. The ocean is full of life. And many creatures come to visit the canoe, out of curiosity, or for company, or for food. The voyagers watch them, and greet them, and learn from them. For the Polynesians, the creatures of the sea are not just animals. Some of them are guardians, and messengers, and even ancestors.

[pause]

One morning, a group of dolphins appears. Dozens of them, swimming alongside the canoe, leaping out of the water, and diving under the hulls. They play in the bow wave, at the front of the canoe, riding it, like children on a swing. They whistle and click to each other. The children are delighted. Tavi lies on his stomach on the platform, and watches them, for hours. Popo barks at them, and they do not care.

[long-pause]

{img:humpback-whale-surfacing-near-canoe-spout-mist-calm-sea-awe}
Another day, they see whales. Great, dark shapes, in the distance, blowing tall spouts of mist into the air. One of them comes close to the canoe. It is enormous, much bigger than the canoe itself. It swims slowly past, just under the surface, and rolls a little, and looks at them with its small, wise eye. Then it dives, lifting its great tail high into the air, and disappears. Everyone is silent, with wonder.

[pause]

Ru says that the whales are travelling too. Like us, he says, they are going north, to a warm place, to have their babies. Perhaps they are going to the same land. The Hawaiian people, later, believed that the humpback whales came every winter to the waters around their islands, from the cold north. And they still do, today. Ru's guess was not far wrong.

[pause]

{img:sea-turtle-swimming-near-surface-canoe-shadow-clear-blue-water}
There are sea turtles too, swimming slowly along, near the surface, with their heads up, breathing. And sharks, which follow the canoe for a while, circling, curious, and then swim away. The voyagers are not afraid of them. The shark is a guardian, for some families. Tane's family has a shark as its guardian spirit. When he sees one, he greets it, respectfully, by name.

[pause]

And there are the birds. Out here, in the middle of the ocean, there are birds that never come to land, except to nest. Albatrosses, with their huge wings, gliding over the waves, for hours, without flapping. Petrels, and shearwaters, skimming the water. And frigate birds, high in the sky, with their long, thin wings, and forked tails, soaring on the wind, watching for fish.

[long-pause]

Ru watches the birds very carefully. They can tell him things. Some birds can fly very far from land. Others stay closer. When he sees certain birds, he knows that land must be within a certain distance. Not yet, he tells Hina. These birds can fly a thousand kilometres from land. We must wait for the other birds. The ones that stay close. The terns, and the noddies, and the boobies. When we see them, we are near.

[pause]

{img:flock-of-seabirds-diving-on-fish-school-ocean-surface-splash-sunlight}
Once, they come across a great school of fish. The sea boils with them, silver and flashing, as the bigger fish chase them from below, and the birds dive on them from above. The voyagers throw out their lines, and catch more fish than they can eat. That evening, there is a feast, on the platform. Raw fish, and cooked fish, and dried fish for later. Everyone eats until they are full, for the first time in weeks.

[pause]

As the sun goes down that evening, Hina sits at the front of the canoe, beside her grandfather. A single white bird flies past, low over the water, heading north-west. Ru watches it go, and says nothing for a long time. Then he says, that one is a tern. And he smiles.

[long-pause]

{img:north-star-low-on-horizon-night-ocean-canoe-sails-starlight-first-sight}
As the canoe sails further north, something strange and wonderful happens in the night sky. The stars change. The familiar stars of the south, which the voyagers have known all their lives, begin to sink lower, and lower, toward the southern horizon, every night. And new stars appear, in the north, rising up from the sea, stars they have never seen before. Or only seen very rarely, very low, on the edge of the sky.

[pause]

The most important of these new stars is a small, steady star, which appears just above the northern horizon. It does not move. All the other stars wheel around the sky, during the night, rising and setting. But this one stays in the same place, all night long, always in the north. Ru calls it the star that stands still. Today, we call it the North Star, or Polaris.

[long-pause]

Ru shows it to Hina, on the first night that it appears. It is very faint, and very low, just above the waves. She can hardly see it. But Ru says that it will rise a little higher, every night, as they sail north. And the height of the star above the sea will tell them how far north they have come. When it is about as high as the width of his hand, held out at arm's length, he says, then they will be at the right distance north. Then they must look for land.

[pause]

{img:old-navigator-measuring-star-height-with-outstretched-hand-arm-night-sky}
Ru measures the height of the star every night, with his hand. He holds out his arm, straight, toward the star, and puts the edge of his hand on the horizon. Then he sees how high the star is, compared to his fingers. One finger. Two fingers. Three. Each night, it is a little higher. Hina does it too, with her own hand. But her hand is smaller than his, so her measurements are different. Ru laughs, and says that she will have to learn her own hand.

[pause]

At the same time, in the south, the stars they know are sinking. The beautiful cross of four bright stars, which they call by its own name, and which always shows the south, is now very low, on the southern horizon. Soon, it will disappear completely, for a few hours each night. Hina feels a little sad, watching it go. It is like saying goodbye to an old friend, and to home.

[pause]

{img:southern-cross-low-on-southern-horizon-sea-girl-watching-sadly-night}
And there is the orange star. The bright, orange star, rising in the north-east, which passes straight over the land in the north. Every night, Ru watches it, as it climbs up the sky. And he watches where it reaches its highest point. Every night, its highest point is a little closer to the very top of the sky, directly overhead. When it passes right over their heads, at its highest point, Ru says, then they will be on the same line as the land. Then they must turn, and sail along that line.

[pause]

This is the great secret of the navigators. Every island has its own star, which passes right over it. If you know the star of the island, and you can find the place where it passes overhead, then you know how far north or south you are. Then you only need to find out whether the island is to the east, or the west. And for that, you need the other signs. The birds, and the clouds, and the swells.

[long-pause]

{img:orange-star-arcturus-overhead-zenith-canoe-mast-pointing-starry-night}
One night, about three weeks after they left Raiatea, Ru lies on his back on the platform, and looks straight up, at the orange star. It is directly overhead. Right at the top of the sky. He watches it for a long time, to be sure. Then he sits up, and speaks quietly to Chief Tama, and to the steersman. They turn the canoe, a little, to the west. And they sail on, along the line of the star.

[pause]

Ru says that the land is somewhere along this line. To the west, he thinks, perhaps two days, perhaps four, perhaps more. Now, everyone must watch. Watch the birds. Watch the clouds. Watch the sea. Smell the wind. The first one to see a sign of land, he says, will get the first coconut, from the first tree, in the new land.

[pause]

Tavi wants that coconut very much. He spends the whole of the next day at the front of the canoe, staring at the horizon, until his eyes hurt. He sees nothing but the sea. But he does not give up.

[long-pause]

{img:terns-noddies-flying-low-over-sea-evening-heading-same-direction-canoe}
On the second day after they turn west, the signs begin to appear. In the morning, Hina sees a group of birds. Small, dark birds, with white caps on their heads, flying low over the water, fishing. They are noddies. Ru watches them carefully. He says that noddies do not fly far from land, perhaps fifty kilometres, at most. In the evening, they fly back to land, to sleep. So, in the evening, they must watch where the birds go.

[pause]

All day, the voyagers watch the birds, and wait. More birds come. Terns, and noddies, and a big brown booby, which lands on the mast, and sits there, looking at them, for an hour. Then, in the evening, as the sun goes down, the birds stop fishing. They rise up from the sea, and gather together, and fly away, all in the same direction. To the west, and a little north. Ru points. That way, he says. The land is that way.

[long-pause]

{img:stationary-cloud-cap-on-horizon-green-tint-underneath-sea-sunset}
That night, nobody sleeps much. Everyone is excited, and nervous. They sail on, slowly, through the dark, toward the west. Ru does not want to go too fast, in case they come too close to the land, in the dark, and hit the rocks. He feels the swells, and notices that they are changing. The swell from the north is weaker. Something is blocking it. Something big, to the north-west. Land.

[pause]

In the morning, they look out to the west. At first, there is nothing. Just the sea, and a few clouds, on the horizon. But then Ru notices something. One of the clouds is not moving. All the other clouds are drifting slowly across the sky, with the wind. But this one stays in the same place. And it is tall, and thick, and its underside is a strange colour. Not grey, but a little green. Ru says that the cloud is sitting on a mountain. A very high mountain. And the green is the reflection of the forests below.

[pause]

{img:snow-capped-volcano-peak-rising-above-clouds-distant-ocean-morning-light}
The canoe sails on, toward the cloud. All morning, the voyagers stare at it. Then, in the middle of the day, Hina sees something, below the cloud, on the horizon. A dark line. A shape. She looks, and looks, and looks. It does not move. It is not a wave. It is not a cloud. She grabs her grandfather's arm, and points, and cannot speak. Ru looks, for a long time. Then he nods. And he says, quietly, there it is.

[pause]

Then everyone sees it. A great mountain, rising out of the sea, far away. Its top is hidden in the clouds. But then the clouds part, for a moment, and they see something that none of them, except Ru, has ever seen before. The top of the mountain is white. Covered with something white, and shining, in the sun. Tavi asks what it is. Ru says it is snow. Frozen water, that falls from the sky, on the highest mountains. It is cold, he says, colder than anything you can imagine.

[long-pause]

The voyagers cheer, and shout, and cry, and embrace each other. They have been at sea for more than three weeks. They have crossed thousands of kilometres of open ocean. And they have found the land. Chief Tama blows the conch shell, a long, joyful note, across the water. The priest chants a prayer of thanks, to the gods of the sea, and the sky, and the navigators. And Ru sits down, suddenly, on the platform, and closes his eyes. He is very tired.

[pause]

{img:voyagers-celebrating-on-canoe-embracing-conch-shell-mountain-ahead-joy}
Hina sits beside him, and takes his hand. She asks him if he is all right. He opens his eyes, and smiles at her. He says that he is just resting. He says that he was not completely sure, until now. He says that every voyage is a mystery, until the land appears. And he says that she was the first one to see it. So she gets the first coconut.

[pause]

Tavi is disappointed, for a moment. Then he says that he saw the noddies first, yesterday, so he should get a coconut too. Everyone laughs. Chief Tama says that everyone on the canoe will get a coconut, from the new land, when the trees have grown. Even Popo. It will take about six years, Mahana says. Tavi says that he can wait.

[long-pause]

{img:canoe-approaching-black-sand-beach-green-cliffs-waterfalls-new-land}
For the rest of that day, and all the next, the canoe sails toward the land. It grows bigger, and bigger, and more and more amazing. It is not one mountain, but two. Two great mountains, huge and gentle, rising up from the sea, higher than anything the voyagers have ever seen. Their tops are in the clouds, with snow. Their sides are covered with forests, dark green, and ferns, and grass. And along the coast, there are cliffs, and waterfalls, falling straight into the sea.

[pause]

As they come closer, they see more. Streams, and rivers, running down the valleys. Beaches, some of white sand, and some of black sand, made of crushed volcanic rock. Strange trees, that they do not know. Birds, everywhere, of every colour. Red, and yellow, and green, and black. Singing, in the forests. And on one side of the island, they see something very strange. A great black land, with no plants on it at all. And smoke, rising from it.

[long-pause]

{img:distant-volcano-smoke-black-lava-field-coast-glowing-dusk-voyagers-watching}
Ru tells them what it is. It is the place where the ground is still being made. Fire comes up from inside the earth, and pours out, like a red river, and then cools, and becomes black rock. The new land is still being born, he says. It is a young land, and alive. The Polynesians later believed that the goddess of the volcano lived there, in the fire. Her name was Pele.

[pause]

They sail along the coast, looking for a good place to land. Ru is looking for a bay, with a beach, and a stream of fresh water, and flat land for gardens, and a gap in the reef. On the second evening, they find one. A wide, curving bay, with a beach of white sand, and shady trees, and a stream, running down from the mountains, into the sea. Chief Tama says, this is the place.

[pause]

{img:first-footsteps-on-new-beach-chief-wading-ashore-family-following-dawn}
They anchor in the bay, for the night, and wait for the morning. Nobody sleeps. Then, at dawn, they paddle the canoe slowly into the shallow water, and up onto the beach. The priest goes first. He wades ashore, and kneels on the sand, and chants a prayer, to the gods of this new land, asking for their welcome, and their protection. He makes an offering, of a pig, and some coconuts, and red feathers. Then Chief Tama steps ashore. And then everyone else.

[pause]

Hina steps off the canoe, into the warm, shallow water. It feels strange to stand on solid ground again, after so many weeks at sea. Her legs feel wobbly, as if the land is moving, like the waves. She walks up the beach, slowly, and kneels down, and puts her hands in the warm sand. It is soft, and white, and real. She has never been so happy in her life.

[long-pause]

Tavi runs up and down the beach, shouting, with Popo barking beside him, both of them wild with joy. The other children join him. The grown-ups unload the canoe, carrying the baskets, and the plants, and the animals, up the beach, into the shade of the trees. The pigs are let out of their cages, and run around, squealing. The chickens flutter and squawk. Everyone is laughing, and talking, and crying, all at once.

[pause]

{img:mother-planting-first-taro-in-new-soil-stream-bank-family-watching-morning}
Mahana goes straight to the stream, with her plants. She tastes the water. It is cold, and fresh, and sweet. She looks at the soil, along the bank. It is dark, and rich, and moist. She kneels down, and digs a small hole, with her hands, and plants the first taro, in the new land. She pats the soil around it, gently. And she whispers to it, that it is home now, and it can rest, and grow.

[pause]

That evening, the voyagers light a great fire, on the beach, and cook a feast. Fish, from the bay, and a pig, and the last of the taro, and the last green coconuts from the canoe. They eat, and sing, and dance, and give thanks. And when the stars come out, Ru shows Hina the orange star, high above, right at the top of the sky, shining down on the new land. That is the star of this island, he says. Remember it. One day, you will need it, to bring other people here.

[long-pause]

{img:settlers-building-first-thatched-houses-new-island-clearing-mountains-behind}
The first weeks in the new land are very busy. There is so much to do. Houses to build, gardens to plant, fishing grounds to find, and the land to explore. Everyone works, from dawn until dark. But it is happy work. They are building their new home, in a beautiful, rich, empty land. Everything they do is the first time that anyone has ever done it, here.

[pause]

First, they build shelters. Simple houses, of wooden poles, tied together with sennit, and thatched with grass, and leaves. Later, they will build bigger, better houses. But for now, they need somewhere to sleep, out of the rain. And it rains a lot, on this side of the island, in the afternoons. Soft, warm rain, which makes everything grow.

[long-pause]

{img:taro-terraces-being-dug-by-stream-men-women-working-digging-sticks-mud}
Then they make the gardens. Along the stream, they dig flat terraces, and lead water into them, with little channels, to make wet fields for the taro. It is hard work, with wooden digging sticks, and their hands. On the higher, drier land, they clear the forest, and plant bananas, and sugarcane, and yams, and paper mulberry. And along the beach, they plant the coconuts, in long rows. One day, there will be palm trees here, hundreds of them.

[pause]

The plants grow well. The soil is rich, from the old volcanic ash, and the rain is plentiful, and the sun is warm. Within a few weeks, the taro has new leaves. The banana shoots are growing. The sugarcane is sprouting. Mahana is delighted. She says that the land is happy to have them. And she still talks to the plants, every day.

[pause]

{img:native-hawaiian-forest-birds-red-honeycreeper-ohia-lehua-flowers-ferns}
The land is full of wonders. In the forests, there are trees they have never seen, with bright red flowers, like little brushes, which the birds love. There are giant ferns, taller than a man. There are birds of every colour, singing in the trees, which have never seen people before, and are not afraid of them. There are no snakes, no dangerous animals, no mosquitoes. Only birds, and insects, and the plants, and the sea.

[pause]

Ru and Hina explore the coast, in a small canoe, which Tane has made, from a tree in the forest. They find other bays, and beaches, and streams, and good fishing places. They climb a hill, and look out to the north-west. And on the horizon, on a clear day, they see another island. And beyond it, perhaps, another. Ru says that this land is not just one island. It is a chain of islands, stretching away to the north-west. A whole new world.

[long-pause]

As the months pass, the little settlement grows. More houses are built. The gardens spread along the stream. The pigs and the chickens have babies. A baby is born, too, to one of the young couples. The first child of the new land. The whole settlement celebrates, with a feast. The priest gives the baby a name, which means born under the new star.

[pause]

{img:old-navigator-sitting-on-rock-overlooking-ocean-sunset-girl-beside-quiet}
Ru grows older, and slower. The long voyage has tired him. He spends his days sitting on a rock, above the beach, looking out at the sea, to the south. Thinking about Raiatea, and his sister, and his old friends. He knows that he will not see them again. He is too old for another voyage. But he is content. He has done what he came to do. He has brought his family to a new home.

[pause]

And he has passed on his knowledge, to Hina. She knows the stars now, and the swells, and the birds, and the clouds. She knows the way from Raiatea to this land, and the way back. One day, Ru says, she will sail back to Raiatea, to tell them about this land. And she will bring more people here. And she will be a great navigator, like him. Hina is not sure she can be as great as him. But she promises to try.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-hawaiian-village-later-centuries-fishponds-taro-terraces-valley}
In the centuries that followed, more voyagers came to Hawaii, from the islands of the south. For a few hundred years, the canoes sailed back and forth, between Hawaii and Tahiti, and the other islands. The old Hawaiian chants tell of these voyages, and of the great navigators and chiefs who made them. They tell of a land in the south, called Kahiki, from which the ancestors came. Kahiki is the Hawaiian way of saying Tahiti.

[pause]

Then, slowly, for reasons we do not fully understand, the long voyages stopped. Perhaps the people of Hawaii no longer needed to go back. They had everything they needed, in their own islands. Perhaps the knowledge of the long voyages was slowly forgotten. Whatever the reason, for several hundred years, Hawaii lived on its own, separate from the rest of the world. And its people developed their own way of life.

[long-pause]

{img:hawaiian-chiefs-feather-cloaks-yellow-red-ceremony-temple-heiau}
The Hawaiian people grew in number, until there were hundreds of thousands of them, on all the islands. They built great fishponds, along the coasts, where they raised fish. They built terraces of taro, in every valley. They built temples, of stone, called heiau. They had great chiefs, and kings, who wore magnificent cloaks and helmets, made of thousands of tiny red and yellow feathers. They made beautiful tapa cloth, and wooden bowls, and carvings.

[pause]

They had their own music, and dance. The hula, which told stories with movements of the hands, and the body, accompanied by chants, and drums. They had their own sports. Canoe racing, and wrestling, and a sport of riding the great waves on long wooden boards. It was called he'e nalu, wave sliding. Today, we call it surfing. The Hawaiians invented it, or at least made it their own, and it spread to the whole world.

[pause]

{img:ancient-hawaiians-surfing-long-wooden-boards-waves-beach-watching}
In 1778, nearly eight hundred years after our story, a British ship arrived in Hawaii. It was commanded by the famous explorer, Captain James Cook. He was the first European to visit the islands. He was amazed to find people there, so far from anywhere else. And he was even more amazed to find that they spoke a language very like the language of Tahiti, which he had visited before, thousands of kilometres away, to the south.

[pause]

Cook had already met a great Polynesian navigator, called Tupaia, in Tahiti, nine years before. Tupaia had sailed with him for a while, and had drawn a map of the islands he knew. Cook realised that the Polynesians had spread across the whole Pacific, by their own skill. But many Europeans later refused to believe it. They said that the Polynesians must have drifted to the islands by accident, blown by storms, in their canoes. They could not believe that people without compasses could find their way across the ocean.

[long-pause]

After Cook, the world came to Hawaii. Traders, whalers, missionaries, and settlers. They brought new things, and new ideas, and new diseases, which caused great suffering among the Hawaiian people. The islands became a kingdom, under King Kamehameha, and then, in 1898, they were taken over by the United States. In 1959, Hawaii became an American state.

[pause]

{img:hawaiian-elder-teaching-children-old-chants-under-tree-tradition}
Through all these changes, much of the old Polynesian knowledge was lost. The old navigators passed away, and their knowledge passed away with them. The great voyaging canoes rotted, on the beaches, and were not replaced. The chants were forgotten, or only half remembered. By the twentieth century, nobody in Hawaii knew how to navigate by the stars, across the open ocean, as their ancestors had done.

[pause]

But in a few small islands, far away, in Micronesia, to the west, a few old navigators still remembered the old ways. And in Hawaii, a few people began to wonder. Was it really true, that the ancestors had found these islands by accident? Or had they sailed here, on purpose, using their great skill? There was only one way to find out.

[long-pause]

{img:hokulea-voyaging-canoe-red-crab-claw-sails-1976-hawaii-departure}
In the early 1970s, a group of people in Hawaii decided to build a great Polynesian voyaging canoe, just like the ones of the ancestors. And to sail it to Tahiti, without any modern instruments, using only the old ways of navigation. They wanted to show the world that the Polynesians had been great navigators, and not just lucky drifters. And they wanted to give the people of Hawaii pride in their own history.

[pause]

They called the canoe Hōkūle'a. It is the Hawaiian name for the bright orange star, which passes right over Hawaii. The same star that Ru used to find the land. It was a double canoe, about nineteen metres long, with two crab claw sails. It was built with some modern materials, but its shape and its design were based on the old canoes, from drawings, and descriptions, and chants.

[long-pause]

{img:micronesian-master-navigator-weathered-face-watching-sea-from-canoe}
But there was a problem. Nobody in Hawaii knew how to navigate in the old way. So they asked a man from a tiny island in Micronesia, called Satawal. His name was Mau Piailug. He was one of the last traditional navigators in the whole Pacific. He had learned from his grandfather, when he was a small boy, just like Hina. He agreed to guide the canoe, and to teach the Hawaiians what he knew.

[pause]

In May 1976, Hōkūle'a set sail from Hawaii, for Tahiti, with Mau as the navigator. There were no compasses, no maps, no instruments on board. Mau found the way by the stars, the swells, the sun, the wind, and the birds, just as Ru had done, a thousand years before. The voyage took thirty-four days. And it found Tahiti, exactly as Mau had planned.

[pause]

{img:huge-crowd-tahiti-beach-welcoming-voyaging-canoe-arrival-1976-joy}
When Hōkūle'a arrived in Tahiti, more than seventeen thousand people were waiting on the beach, to welcome it. Almost half the population of the island. They cheered, and sang, and cried, and danced. It was one of the greatest days in the history of Polynesia. The voyage proved that the ancestors could have done it, on purpose, with skill, and courage. It filled the people of Hawaii, and of all Polynesia, with pride.

[pause]

After that, a young Hawaiian man called Nainoa Thompson decided to learn the old navigation, from Mau. He studied for years. He studied the stars in a planetarium, and he sailed with Mau, and he spent many nights on the beach, watching the sky, just as Hina did. In 1980, he navigated Hōkūle'a to Tahiti and back himself. He was the first Hawaiian in hundreds of years to do it.

[long-pause]

Since then, Hōkūle'a has sailed hundreds of thousands of kilometres, all over the Pacific, and beyond. To New Zealand, to Rapa Nui, to Japan, and to many other places. From 2014 to 2017, it sailed all the way around the world, visiting more than a hundred and fifty ports, in many countries. Everywhere it went, it carried a message, about caring for the ocean, and the Earth, which is our island home.

[pause]

Today, there are many new voyaging canoes, all over Polynesia. In Hawaii, Tahiti, New Zealand, the Cook Islands, Samoa, and elsewhere. And there are new navigators, young men and women, learning the old ways. Reading the stars, and the swells, and the birds, and the clouds. Many of them are students of Nainoa Thompson, who was a student of Mau Piailug. The knowledge is alive again, after centuries of silence.

[pause]

{img:young-woman-navigator-modern-voyaging-canoe-reading-stars-dusk}
Mau Piailug passed away in 2010, on his little island of Satawal. He was honoured all over the Pacific, as the man who saved the old knowledge, and gave it back to the Polynesian people. And among the new navigators, today, there are many young women. Just like Hina, a thousand years ago, sitting on the beach, beside her grandfather, learning the names of the stars.

[long-pause]

{img:today-hawaii-big-island-mauna-kea-snow-summit-observatories-clouds}
Today, Hawaii is one of the most famous places in the world. More than a million people live there, and many millions of visitors come every year. They come for the beaches, and the mountains, and the warm sea, and the surfing, and the flowers. Many of them do not know the story of how the first people came to these islands, a thousand years ago, across the open ocean.

[pause]

The two great mountains that Ru and Hina saw from the canoe are still there, on the island that is called Hawai'i, or the Big Island. They are called Mauna Kea, the white mountain, and Mauna Loa, the long mountain. Mauna Kea is the tallest mountain in the world, if you measure it from its base, deep under the sea. It is more than ten thousand metres tall, from bottom to top. In the winter, there is still snow on its summit.

[long-pause]

{img:today-kilauea-lava-glow-night-crater-visitors-viewing-area-distance}
On the top of Mauna Kea, today, there are telescopes. Some of the biggest and best telescopes in the world. Astronomers come from all over the world to use them, because the air is so clear and still, high above the clouds. They study the stars, and the galaxies, at the edge of the universe. It is a fitting thing, on a mountain that was first seen by people who navigated by the stars.

[pause]

The volcano that Ru saw smoking is still active too. It is called Kīlauea. It is one of the most active volcanoes in the world. Sometimes, it sends rivers of red lava down to the sea, where they hiss, and steam, and make new land. The island is still growing, as Ru said. Many Hawaiians still honour Pele, the goddess of the volcano, and leave offerings for her, at the edge of the crater.

[pause]

{img:today-taro-fields-loi-green-valley-hawaiian-farmers-working-mountains}
In some valleys, the old taro fields have been restored, and the Hawaiian people grow taro again, as their ancestors did. The taro plants are descended from the ones that the first voyagers brought, a thousand years ago, in their canoes. Taro is still a very important food in Hawaii, and a sacred plant. In the Hawaiian stories, the taro plant is the elder brother of the Hawaiian people.

[pause]

The Hawaiian language, which almost disappeared in the twentieth century, is being taught again, in schools, and spoken again, in homes. There are schools where children learn everything in Hawaiian. The hula is danced again, in its old forms, with the old chants. And the old knowledge of the land, and the sea, and the stars, is being learned again, by young people, who are proud of their ancestors.

[long-pause]

{img:today-hokulea-moored-honolulu-harbour-sunset-crowd-visiting-canoe}
And Hōkūle'a, the voyaging canoe, is still sailing. When it is at home, in Honolulu, people come to see it, and touch it, and talk to the crew. Children come, on school visits, and learn about the stars, and the swells, and the birds. Some of them will become navigators, one day. And the old chants of the voyagers are sung again, on the beaches, as the canoes leave, and when they come home.

[pause]

All across the Pacific, in a great triangle of ocean, the Polynesian people still live, on their islands. In Hawaii, Tahiti, Samoa, Tonga, the Cook Islands, New Zealand, Rapa Nui, and many others. Their languages are still related, so that a Hawaiian and a Tahitian and a Maori can understand some of each other's words. They are one great family, spread across the ocean. And the ocean, for them, is still not a barrier, but a road, connecting them all.

[pause]

If you stand on a beach in Hawaii today, on a clear night, and look up at the sky, you can see the orange star, Hōkūle'a, passing high overhead. The star of Hawaii. The star that guided the first voyagers to these islands, a thousand years ago. And if you look south, low on the horizon, you can see the stars of the southern sky, the stars of home, for the ancestors, far away across the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:new-settlement-beach-evening-fire-houses-canoe-pulled-up-sunset-1000ad}
So we leave the voyagers tonight, in their new land, about a thousand years ago, as the evening falls. The sun is setting over the sea, to the west, in a blaze of orange, and pink, and gold. The great mountains behind the settlement glow in the last light, and then turn purple, and then grey, and then dark. The first stars are coming out, one by one, in the clear sky.

[pause]

On the beach, the great canoe, the Bird of the Ocean, rests on the sand, under a shelter of palm leaves, brought from the forest. Its sails are rolled up, and stored away. Its hulls are dry. Its long voyage is over. Tane has checked every lashing, and every plank. One day, it will sail again, back to Raiatea. But for now, it rests, like a great bird, sleeping, after a long flight.

[long-pause]

{img:family-evening-meal-outside-new-house-fire-children-dog-grandfather}
In front of the new houses, the families are gathered around the fires, cooking their evening meal. Fish, from the bay, and young taro leaves, and a little of the precious breadfruit paste, which is almost finished now. The smell of the cooking drifts along the beach. The children are playing, at the edge of the water, in the last of the light. Tavi and Popo are chasing crabs. And the sound of laughter and singing rises into the evening air.

[pause]

Mahana sits by the stream, looking at her taro, growing in its new field. The leaves are big, and green, and healthy. She is tired, and happy. Tane comes, and sits beside her, and puts his arm around her. They do not say anything. They just sit, and listen to the stream, and watch the stars come out, over the mountains.

[long-pause]

{img:grandfather-and-granddaughter-on-rock-above-sea-night-stars-pointing-up}
Up on his rock, above the beach, Ru sits with Hina, as he does every evening. They look up at the sky, together. The stars are bright, and clear. Ru points, and Hina names them, one after another. The star that stands still, in the north. The orange star, high above them. And far away, low in the south, just above the sea, the stars of home. Ru looks at them for a long time. Then he says goodnight to them, softly. And he says goodnight to Hina too.

[pause]

The fires on the beach burn low, and the families go into their houses, to sleep. The settlement grows quiet. The waves wash gently onto the sand, and pull back, and wash in again, with a soft, steady sound. In the forest, a bird sings a few last notes. The stream murmurs, over its stones. And above the great mountain, the clouds drift slowly, silver in the light of the rising moon.
