The nights at sea are the most beautiful time of the voyage. When the sun goes down, the wind often grows softer, and the sea calmer. The sky darkens, from blue to purple to black. And then the stars come out. Not just a few, as on land, near the fires of the village. But thousands, and thousands, more than anyone could count, from horizon to horizon, all around the canoe.

Out here, on the open ocean, there is no land, no light, nothing to block the view. The sky is a great dome, covering everything. The Milky Way stretches across it, like a river of light, or a long cloud of shining dust. The bright stars shine, in their patterns, and the faint ones fill the spaces between. And the sea reflects them all, so that the canoe seems to float in the middle of the sky.

--

{img:navigator-pointing-rising-star-horizon-girl-watching-dark-ocean-night}
This is when Ru does his most important work. He sits at the front of the canoe, and watches the horizon, in the east. He waits for the stars to rise. When a star appears, just above the sea, he knows exactly where it is rising, on his star compass. And so he knows in which direction the canoe is heading. He signals to the steersman, with his hand, to steer a little more to the left, or to the right.

Each star is useful only for a short time. When it is low, near the horizon, it shows the direction clearly. But as it climbs higher in the sky, it becomes harder to use. So the navigator must use one star, and then the next, and then the next, all night long. Ru calls them a star path. A line of stars, rising one after another, at the same point on the horizon, showing the way, like stepping stones across a river.

{img:girl-memorising-star-path-lying-on-platform-counting-stars-quiet}
Hina sits beside him, every night, and learns. He tells her the names of the stars, as they rise. This is the star of the fisherman. This is the star of the canoe prow. This is the bird star. She repeats them, and remembers them, and watches where they rise. And sometimes, when he is tired, he lets her guide the canoe, for an hour or two, while he rests. She is very nervous, the first time. But she does it well.

In the middle of the night, the canoe is very quiet. Most of the people are asleep, curled up on the platform, or in the little house, under blankets of tapa. Only the steersman, and the navigator, and a few others are awake. The sails creak softly. The water swishes and gurgles, along the hulls. The ropes hum, in the wind. And now and then, a pig grunts, or a chicken clucks, in its sleep.

--

Sometimes, the clouds come, and hide the stars. Then Ru must use the swells instead. He lies down on the platform, on his back, and closes his eyes, and feels the movement of the canoe. The long, slow lift and fall of the swells, coming from the east. And another, smaller swell, from the south. He feels them, with his whole body. And he knows, from the way they move the canoe, whether they are still on course.

{img:navigator-lying-on-back-eyes-closed-feeling-swells-cloudy-night-canoe}
Ru also watches the moon. Each night, it grows a little bigger, and rises a little later. It gives light, for the work on the canoe, and makes the sea shine silver. Its path across the sky shows direction too. And the tides of the moon, Ru says, also move the sea, in their own way, which a navigator must understand.

Then, before dawn, the sky in the east slowly begins to grow pale. The stars fade, one by one. The last stars to fade are the brightest ones. Ru watches them, until the very last moment, to check his course. Then the sun rises, out of the sea, in a blaze of gold. Another night has passed. And the canoe is a little closer to the land in the north.
