In the early 1970s, a group of people in Hawaii decided to build a great Polynesian voyaging canoe, just like the ones of the ancestors. And to sail it to Tahiti, without any modern instruments, using only the old ways of navigation. They wanted to show the world that the Polynesians had been great navigators, and not just lucky drifters. And they wanted to give the people of Hawaii pride in their own history.

They called the canoe Hōkūle'a. It is the Hawaiian name for the bright orange star, which passes right over Hawaii. The same star that Ru used to find the land. It was a double canoe, about nineteen metres long, with two crab claw sails. It was built with some modern materials, but its shape and its design were based on the old canoes, from drawings, and descriptions, and chants.

--

{img:micronesian-master-navigator-weathered-face-watching-sea-from-canoe}
But there was a problem. Nobody in Hawaii knew how to navigate in the old way. So they asked a man from a tiny island in Micronesia, called Satawal. His name was Mau Piailug. He was one of the last traditional navigators in the whole Pacific. He had learned from his grandfather, when he was a small boy, just like Hina. He agreed to guide the canoe, and to teach the Hawaiians what he knew.

In May 1976, Hōkūle'a set sail from Hawaii, for Tahiti, with Mau as the navigator. There were no compasses, no maps, no instruments on board. Mau found the way by the stars, the swells, the sun, the wind, and the birds, just as Ru had done, a thousand years before. The voyage took thirty-four days. And it found Tahiti, exactly as Mau had planned.

{img:huge-crowd-tahiti-beach-welcoming-voyaging-canoe-arrival-1976-joy}
When Hōkūle'a arrived in Tahiti, more than seventeen thousand people were waiting on the beach, to welcome it. Almost half the population of the island. They cheered, and sang, and cried, and danced. It was one of the greatest days in the history of Polynesia. The voyage proved that the ancestors could have done it, on purpose, with skill, and courage. It filled the people of Hawaii, and of all Polynesia, with pride.

After that, a young Hawaiian man called Nainoa Thompson decided to learn the old navigation, from Mau. He studied for years. He studied the stars in a planetarium, and he sailed with Mau, and he spent many nights on the beach, watching the sky, just as Hina did. In 1980, he navigated Hōkūle'a to Tahiti and back himself. He was the first Hawaiian in hundreds of years to do it.

--

Since then, Hōkūle'a has sailed hundreds of thousands of kilometres, all over the Pacific, and beyond. To New Zealand, to Rapa Nui, to Japan, and to many other places. From 2014 to 2017, it sailed all the way around the world, visiting more than a hundred and fifty ports, in many countries. Everywhere it went, it carried a message, about caring for the ocean, and the Earth, which is our island home.

Today, there are many new voyaging canoes, all over Polynesia. In Hawaii, Tahiti, New Zealand, the Cook Islands, Samoa, and elsewhere. And there are new navigators, young men and women, learning the old ways. Reading the stars, and the swells, and the birds, and the clouds. Many of them are students of Nainoa Thompson, who was a student of Mau Piailug. The knowledge is alive again, after centuries of silence.

{img:young-woman-navigator-modern-voyaging-canoe-reading-stars-dusk}
Mau Piailug passed away in 2010, on his little island of Satawal. He was honoured all over the Pacific, as the man who saved the old knowledge, and gave it back to the Polynesian people. And among the new navigators, today, there are many young women. Just like Hina, a thousand years ago, sitting on the beach, beside her grandfather, learning the names of the stars.
