On the beach, near the village, under a great shelter of palm leaves, the canoe is being built. It is a double canoe. Two long hulls, side by side, joined together by strong wooden beams, with a platform between them. It is about twenty metres long, as long as four cars, end to end. It will carry about twenty people, and all their plants, and animals, and food, and water. It is the most important thing on the island.

Tane is in charge of the building. He works with a team of skilled men, and their apprentices. They have been working on it for more than a year. First, they had to find the right trees. They went up into the forests in the mountains, with the priest, to choose them. The trees had to be tall, and straight, and strong, and without any rot. When they found them, the priest said prayers, and made offerings, to the god of the forest, to ask his permission to cut them down.

--

{img:polynesian-craftsmen-stone-adze-blade-basalt-sharpening-tools-workshop}
They cut down the trees, with stone adzes. Sharp blades of hard black basalt stone, tied to wooden handles. The Polynesians have no metal. All their tools are made of stone, bone, shell, and wood. It took many days to cut down each tree. Then they dragged the logs down the mountain, to the beach, with ropes, and rollers, and many strong men, singing as they pulled.

On the beach, they began to shape the hulls. They hollowed out the logs, with their adzes, and with fire, burning the wood a little, and then scraping out the charcoal. Slowly, the hulls took shape. Long, and narrow, and smooth, and curved, like the body of a fish. They added planks along the sides, to make them deeper, sewn on with strong cord, through holes drilled in the wood. Then they sealed the joints, with sap from the breadfruit tree, and coconut fibre, so that no water could come in.

{img:men-lashing-crossbeams-to-twin-hulls-sennit-cord-close-detail-work}
Now, they are joining the two hulls together. They lay strong wooden beams across them, and tie them down, with cord, made from coconut fibre. This cord is called sennit. It is made by the women and the old men, who twist the fibres together, between their hands, and their thighs, to make long, strong cords. The canoe needs thousands of metres of sennit. Every joint, every beam, every plank is tied with it. There are no nails, no screws, no metal at all.

The lashings are not only strong. They are also flexible. When the canoe is at sea, in the big waves, the hulls can move a little, against each other, without breaking. A canoe that was too stiff would crack, in a storm. A canoe tied with sennit bends, and moves, like a living thing, and survives. The canoe builders tie the lashings in beautiful patterns, which are also sacred. Each pattern has a meaning, and a prayer.

--

Hina often comes to watch her father at work. She sits on the sand, in the shade, and watches. Sometimes, she helps, twisting sennit, or carrying water, or holding a beam in place. Tane explains everything to her. Why this beam goes here. Why this lashing is tied this way. How the hulls are shaped, to cut through the waves. Hina listens carefully. A navigator must understand her canoe, as well as the stars.

{img:nearly-finished-double-canoe-beach-carved-prow-figure-evening-light}
At the front of each hull, the carvers make a beautiful carved figure. A figure of a god, or an ancestor, to protect the canoe, and guide it, on its journey. They carve it from hard wood, with great care, and paint it, with red and black. Then the priest blesses it, with chants, and offerings. The canoe is not just a boat. It is a sacred thing. It has its own spirit, and its own name.

The canoe's name is chosen by Ru. He calls it Te Manu o te Moana, the Bird of the Ocean. Because, he says, it will fly across the sea, like a great bird, to the land in the north. And it will carry the people safely, under its wings.
