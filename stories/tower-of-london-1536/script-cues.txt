In 1536, the Tower of London was far more than a prison. Behind its walls lived warders and their families, coin makers, cooks, gunners and even a few lions. And in the spring of that year, a queen came to the Tower.

[long-pause]

In 1536, the Tower of London was a royal palace, a fortress, a prison, a mint and a home to hundreds of people. The Tower of London stands on the north bank of the river Thames, at the eastern edge of the old City of London. In 1536, during the reign of King Henry the Eighth, it was already more than four hundred years old. Historians note that it was many things at once. It was a royal palace, where kings and queens stayed before their coronations. It was a fortress, guarding the river and the city. It was a prison for people accused of crimes against the king. It was also the home of the Royal Mint, where England's coins were made, a store for weapons and armour, and a zoo for wild animals. Even so, it was above all a place where people lived and worked. Soldiers, warders, servants, craftsmen and their families made their homes inside its walls. The Tower was like a small town, with its own streets, chapels and gardens, surrounded by stone.

[long-pause]

{img:02-tower-walls-moat-aerial-tudor}

The Tower was surrounded by two rings of walls and a wide moat filled with water from the Thames. The Tower of London is a concentric castle, which means that it has two rings of defensive walls, one inside the other. Historians note that the inner wall was higher than the outer wall, so that defenders on both could fight at the same time. Along the walls stood more than twenty towers, each with its own name. Outside the outer wall was a wide moat, filled with water from the Thames. Even so, the moat was not always pleasant. It was often dirty and smelly, filled with waste from the Tower and the surrounding streets. It was finally drained in the 1840s and turned into a grassy ditch. In 1536, visitors entered the Tower through a series of gates and bridges on its western side. Boats could also enter from the river through a water gate. The walls and towers had been built over centuries, by many kings, each adding new defences and buildings to the fortress.

Each morning, the gates of the Tower were unlocked, and its small community began another day. Life in the Tower began early each morning. Historians note that the gates were unlocked at dawn by the warders, who had kept them closed through the night. Soldiers changed guard, servants lit fires and began cooking, and workers arrived at the Mint and the workshops. Animals in the royal menagerie were fed, and horses were watered. Even so, the day did not begin the same way for everyone. Prisoners woke in their rooms, wondering what the day would bring. Some were allowed visitors or letters. Others waited for news of their trials. The chapel bells rang for morning prayers. Carts arrived at the gates with food, firewood and supplies. Boats came up the river to unload goods at the wharf. By mid morning, the Tower was busy and noisy. People hurried along its narrow streets between the walls, going about their daily work, much like the inhabitants of any small town.

In 1536, England was ruled by King Henry the Eighth, who was then forty-four years old. Henry the Eighth became king of England in 1509, at the age of seventeen. Historians note that he was tall, strong and athletic in his youth, and he loved hunting, jousting, music and dancing. He was well educated and could speak several languages. By 1536, he was forty-four years old and had ruled for twenty-seven years. Even so, his reign had become troubled. He had broken with the Pope in Rome in order to end his first marriage, to Catherine of Aragon, and marry Anne Boleyn. He had made himself head of the Church in England. He had begun to close the monasteries and take their wealth. Historians note that 1536 was a turning point in his life. In January, he was badly injured in a jousting accident. In the same month, Catherine of Aragon died. In May, Anne Boleyn was executed at the Tower. Henry's actions that year left a deep mark on the Tower's history.

Between the walls of the Tower, there were houses, workshops, gardens and even a parish church. Inside its walls, the Tower of London contained much more than towers. Historians note that there were houses for the officials and workers, workshops, storehouses, stables, kitchens and gardens. There was a parish church, the chapel of Saint Peter ad Vincula, and a Norman chapel inside the White Tower. There were wells for water and ovens for baking bread. Even so, space was limited. Buildings were crowded together along narrow lanes, and every corner was used. Some people kept chickens and pigs. Others grew herbs and vegetables in small gardens. The Tower had its own rules and officials, and it was separate from the City of London around it. The area around the Tower, called the Liberties, was under the control of the Tower's officials. Historians note that the people who lived in the Tower formed a close community. They knew each other well, worked together, married each other and raised their children within the walls.

At night, the gates were locked, and the Tower became a quiet world of its own. When evening came, the gates of the Tower of London were locked. Historians note that the keys were carried by the warders, who walked from gate to gate, making sure that everything was secure. Only those with special permission could enter or leave after dark. Even so, the Tower was not completely silent at night. Guards walked the walls with lanterns, watching for danger. Prisoners lay awake in their rooms, listening to the sounds of the river and the bells of the city churches. In the menagerie, the lions sometimes roared, and the sound echoed through the stone courtyards. In the houses, families gathered around their fires, ate supper and prepared for bed. Candles burned in a few windows. The river flowed quietly past, carrying a few late boats. The great White Tower stood dark against the sky. Locked inside its walls, the small community of the Tower settled down for the night.

Historians know about life in the Tower from royal records, letters, accounts and later surveys. Historians have learned about life in the Tower of London in Tudor times from many sources. Royal accounts record payments to workers, purchases of supplies and repairs to buildings. Letters written by the Tower's officials to the king's ministers describe events and prisoners. Some prisoners also wrote letters and poems, and some carved their names and messages into the walls of their rooms. Even so, there are gaps in the record. Ordinary people, such as servants and labourers, left few written traces. Historians must piece together their lives from small details in the records. Later surveys of the Tower, including detailed plans made in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, show the layout of its buildings. Archaeologists have also excavated parts of the Tower, finding objects such as pottery, coins, tools and animal bones. Together, these sources give a rich picture of the Tower as a living place, full of people, work and stories.

The White Tower was built by William the Conqueror after the Norman conquest of England in 1066. The oldest and most famous part of the Tower of London is the White Tower. Historians note that it was begun by William the Conqueror, the Duke of Normandy, who became king of England after the Battle of Hastings in 1066. He built it to control London and to show the power of the new Norman rulers. Work began around 1078 and was completed about twenty years later. Even so, it was not called the White Tower at first. It got its name in 1240, when King Henry the Third had it whitewashed. The building is about twenty-seven metres tall, with walls up to four and a half metres thick. Its four corner turrets give it a distinctive shape. In 1536, it was more than four hundred and fifty years old. It was used as a storehouse for weapons and armour, a place for royal records and a symbol of royal power, rising high above the river and the city.

[long-pause]

{img:09-caen-stone-blocks-masons-norman}

Some of the stone for the White Tower was brought by ship from Caen, in Normandy. The White Tower was built mainly of Kentish ragstone, a rough grey stone from the county of Kent. Historians note that the corners, windows and doorways were made of a finer, pale stone from Caen, in Normandy, which William the Conqueror had brought across the English Channel by ship. Caen stone was easy to carve and had a beautiful cream colour. Even so, building such a large tower was a huge task. Hundreds of workers were needed, including masons, carpenters, labourers and carters. They worked with simple tools, such as chisels, hammers, ropes and wooden cranes. The work took many years. The building was designed by a monk named Gundulf, who later became Bishop of Rochester. He was an experienced builder who also designed Rochester Castle and cathedral. The White Tower was one of the largest buildings in England at the time, and it impressed and intimidated the people of London.

{img:10-chapel-st-john-norman-arches-candles}

Inside the White Tower is the Chapel of Saint John, one of the oldest churches in London. On the upper floors of the White Tower lies the Chapel of Saint John the Evangelist. Historians note that it was built at the same time as the tower, around 1080, and it is one of the best surviving examples of Norman church architecture in England. It has thick round columns, rounded arches and a stone vaulted ceiling. Its walls are plain and pale, giving it a calm and simple beauty. Even so, in Tudor times the chapel was richly decorated, with painted walls, stained glass and fine cloths on the altar. Kings and queens who stayed at the Tower prayed there. Before their coronations, some kings held special ceremonies in the chapel. In 1536, the chapel was still used for worship. Historians note that it later lost its decorations and was used to store records for many years. Today, it has been restored to its simple Norman form. Visitors can still stand inside and see it much as it was.

In Tudor times, the White Tower was used to store weapons, armour and gunpowder. By the sixteenth century, the White Tower was no longer used as a royal home. Historians note that it had become a great storehouse. Its large rooms were filled with weapons, armour, bows, arrows, pikes and other military equipment. Gunpowder was also stored there, kept in barrels in the basement. The Tower was the main arsenal of the kingdom, supplying the king's armies and navy. Even so, storing gunpowder in the Tower was dangerous. A fire or a spark could cause a terrible explosion. The officials of the Tower were careful to protect the gunpowder from flames. Henry the Eighth was very interested in weapons and armour. He built up a great collection, some of which is still displayed at the Tower today. His own suits of armour show how his body changed over the years, from a slim young athlete to a much larger older man. The armour was made by skilled armourers in royal workshops.

Important royal records were also kept in the Tower, stored in chests and on shelves. For centuries, the Tower of London was used to store the records of the English government. Historians note that these included laws, charters, treaties, court records and accounts. They were written on parchment, a material made from animal skin, and rolled up into scrolls or bound into books. They were kept in chests and on shelves in several of the Tower's buildings, especially the Wakefield Tower and the White Tower. Even so, the records were not always well cared for. Some were damaged by damp, mice and insects. Others were lost or misplaced. Clerks worked to sort and copy them. In the sixteenth century, scholars and lawyers came to the Tower to search the records for information. Historians today owe a great debt to these old archives. Many were later moved to the Public Record Office, now called the National Archives. They provide a detailed picture of English history over many centuries.

From the top of the White Tower, the whole of Tudor London could be seen spread out along the river. The White Tower was one of the tallest buildings in London for centuries. Historians note that from its roof, there was a wide view in every direction. To the west lay the City of London, with its crowded houses, church towers and the great spire of Saint Paul's Cathedral. London Bridge crossed the Thames, lined with houses and shops. To the south, across the river, lay the town of Southwark, with its inns, theatres and gardens. To the east, the river flowed towards the sea, past docks, marshes and villages. Even so, the view was not only beautiful. It was also useful for defence. Watchmen on the roof could see approaching enemies, ships on the river and fires in the city. In 1536, the Thames was busy with ships and boats carrying goods and people. The roof of the White Tower offered a view of a growing, lively city, the heart of Tudor England.

The Tower contained a royal palace, where kings and queens stayed on special occasions. For centuries, the Tower of London was also a royal palace. Historians note that the medieval kings built fine lodgings within its walls, near the river, with great halls, private chambers and gardens. Henry the Third and Edward the First added luxurious rooms, decorated with paintings and stained glass. Even so, by the Tudor period, the kings and queens rarely lived at the Tower. They preferred palaces such as Whitehall, Greenwich and Hampton Court, which were more comfortable and modern. The Tower's palace was used mainly for special occasions. Before their coronation, English monarchs traditionally stayed at the Tower and then rode in procession through the City of London to Westminster Abbey. Henry the Eighth did this before his coronation in 1509. In 1533, the palace was repaired and redecorated for the coronation of Anne Boleyn. Three years later, she would return to the same rooms as a prisoner.

In 1533, Anne Boleyn left the Tower in a grand procession to be crowned queen. On the twenty-ninth of May 1533, Anne Boleyn arrived at the Tower of London by river, in a magnificent procession of decorated barges. Historians note that she was greeted by Henry the Eighth and stayed in the royal lodgings for two nights. On the thirty-first of May, she left the Tower in a grand procession through the City of London to Westminster. She was carried in a litter covered with white and gold cloth, wearing white and with her long dark hair loose. Nobles, officials and ladies rode in front of and behind her. Even so, the crowds were not all enthusiastic. Many people in London still supported Catherine of Aragon, Henry's first wife. Anne was crowned queen in Westminster Abbey on the first of June 1533. She was pregnant, and in September she gave birth to a daughter, the future Queen Elizabeth the First. The coronation was one of the most splendid events of Henry's reign.

The palace at the Tower had a great hall, used for feasts, ceremonies and trials. The royal palace at the Tower of London included a great hall, a large room used for feasts, ceremonies and important meetings. Historians note that it had a high timber roof and tall windows, and its walls were hung with tapestries. In medieval times, kings held feasts there for their nobles and guests. Even so, the great hall was also used for more serious purposes. In May 1536, the trials of Anne Boleyn and her brother, George Boleyn, were held there. A large wooden stand was built so that about two thousand people could watch. The trials were presided over by Anne's uncle, the Duke of Norfolk. The judges found her guilty, and she was sentenced to death. The great hall no longer exists. It fell into ruin and was demolished in the late eighteenth century. Today, only the foundations of some parts of the palace remain, hidden beneath later buildings and lawns.

The royal lodgings had small gardens where flowers and herbs were grown. Like most Tudor palaces, the royal lodgings at the Tower had gardens. Historians note that they were small, walled gardens, with raised beds of flowers and herbs, gravel paths and benches. Roses, lavender, rosemary, violets and other plants were grown there. Herbs were used in cooking, medicine and to sweeten the air in the palace rooms. Even so, the gardens of the Tower were not as grand as those of palaces like Hampton Court. Space inside the walls was limited, and the Tower was crowded with other buildings. Yet the gardens offered a quiet place to walk and rest. For important prisoners, being allowed to walk in a garden was a great privilege. Some prisoners in the Tower were given permission to walk in the gardens or along the walls for exercise. Historians note that gardens were important in Tudor life. They were places of beauty, rest and reflection, even within the walls of a fortress.

The queen's lodgings at the Tower had been newly decorated in 1533, with fine furniture and wall hangings. For Anne Boleyn's coronation in 1533, Henry the Eighth ordered the repair and redecoration of the royal lodgings at the Tower. Historians know from the royal accounts that workmen repaired roofs, windows and floors, and that the rooms were painted and hung with tapestries. New furniture was brought in, including beds, chairs and chests. Even so, the work was done quickly, because the coronation was planned in a short time. Hundreds of workmen worked day and night. The queen's lodgings were a series of rooms, including a great chamber, a dining chamber, a bedchamber and private rooms. They looked out over the river and the gardens. Historians note that in May 1536, when Anne Boleyn was arrested, she was lodged in these same rooms. According to the records, she was treated with some respect, and she had several ladies to attend her. Yet she was now a prisoner in the palace where she had prepared to become queen.

Kings and queens usually arrived at the Tower by royal barge along the river Thames. In Tudor times, the river Thames was the main road of London. Historians note that it was easier and safer to travel by water than through the crowded and muddy streets. Kings and queens had royal barges, large rowing boats decorated with carvings, paint, banners and canopies. They were rowed by teams of oarsmen in royal livery. Even so, the river could also be dangerous. Its currents were strong, especially near London Bridge, where the water rushed through the narrow arches. Royal barges usually landed at the Tower on its river side. There, a special water gate allowed boats to enter the Tower directly from the Thames. This gate was later called Traitors' Gate, because many prisoners accused of treason entered the Tower through it. In 1536, Anne Boleyn was brought to the Tower by barge, arriving on the afternoon of the second of May. Tradition says that she entered by the water gate, though she probably landed at a gate near the Byward Tower.

The Tower was governed by the Constable, a trusted royal official appointed by the king. The most senior official of the Tower of London was the Constable. Historians note that the post had existed since the time of William the Conqueror, and it was given to men whom the king trusted completely. The Constable was responsible for the security of the Tower, its prisoners, its garrison and its buildings. In 1536, the Constable was Sir William Kingston, a soldier and courtier who had served Henry the Eighth for many years. Even so, the Constable did not do all the work himself. He was helped by the Lieutenant of the Tower, who lived in a house within the walls and managed daily affairs. The Lieutenant in 1536 was Sir Edmund Walsingham. Kingston's letters to the king's chief minister, Thomas Cromwell, survive. They describe in detail the behaviour and words of Anne Boleyn during her imprisonment in May 1536. These letters are among the most important sources for her last days.

The Yeoman Warders guarded the gates of the Tower and watched over its prisoners. The guards of the Tower of London were called Yeoman Warders. Historians note that their history is connected with the Yeomen of the Guard, a royal bodyguard created by Henry the Seventh in 1485, after he won the crown at the Battle of Bosworth. Some of these yeomen were posted to the Tower to guard the gates and the prisoners. They wore red uniforms with gold decorations and carried halberds, long poles with an axe blade and a spike. Even so, their work was often routine. They opened and closed the gates, checked visitors, guarded prisoners and kept watch at night. Some escorted prisoners to their trials and executions. The Yeoman Warders are still at the Tower today, wearing uniforms based on Tudor designs. They are popularly known as Beefeaters. The origin of this nickname is uncertain, but it may refer to the generous rations of beef that royal guards once received.

Warders escorted prisoners to their rooms and brought them food, letters and visitors. Guarding prisoners was one of the most important duties of the Yeoman Warders. Historians note that each important prisoner was assigned a warder, who was responsible for watching over him or her. The warder escorted the prisoner to and from their room, brought them meals and allowed visitors only with permission. He also reported on the prisoner's behaviour and conversations to the Lieutenant. Even so, the relationship between warders and prisoners was not always harsh. Many prisoners were nobles or educated people, and they were treated with courtesy. Some warders became friendly with their prisoners, talking with them, bringing them news and doing small favours. Prisoners sometimes paid warders for extra comforts, such as better food, candles or firewood. The warders were paid by the crown, but they often earned extra money from prisoners and visitors. Their position gave them a respected place in the small community of the Tower.

The Lieutenant of the Tower lived in a fine timber-framed house within the walls. The Lieutenant of the Tower lived in lodgings in the southwest corner of the inner ward. Historians note that around 1540, a few years after 1536, a new house was built there in the Tudor style, with a timber frame and a steep roof. Today it is called the Queen's House. It is one of the finest Tudor buildings in the Tower. Even so, it was not only a home. Important prisoners were sometimes held there, under the close watch of the Lieutenant. In later years, famous prisoners such as Guy Fawkes were questioned in the house. The Lieutenant lived there with his family and servants. He was responsible for the daily running of the Tower, including the care of prisoners, the payment of staff and the upkeep of buildings. His house was at the heart of the Tower's life. From its windows, he could watch the comings and goings in the inner ward.

The keys of the Tower were guarded carefully, and the gates were locked every night. The security of the Tower of London depended on its gates and their keys. Historians note that each evening, the gates were locked by the warders, and the keys were taken to the Lieutenant or the Constable for safekeeping. In the morning, they were collected again and the gates were unlocked. Even so, this simple routine was taken very seriously. Losing a key or leaving a gate unlocked could allow a prisoner to escape or an enemy to enter. A ceremony for locking the gates has been held at the Tower for centuries. Today, it is called the Ceremony of the Keys. Every night at about ten o'clock, the Chief Yeoman Warder locks the main gates, escorted by soldiers. He is challenged by a sentry, who asks who comes there. He replies that the keys belong to the king or queen. Historians are not certain how old the ceremony is, but it is one of the oldest military traditions in the world.

Many warders lived inside the Tower with their wives and children. The Yeoman Warders and other workers of the Tower often lived within its walls with their families. Historians note that they had small houses or rooms along the walls and in the towers. Their wives cooked, cleaned and cared for the children. Some wives also worked, as laundresses, cooks or sellers of food and drink. Children grew up inside the Tower, playing in its courtyards and along its walls. Even so, life in the Tower was not always easy. The houses were often small and crowded, and some were damp and cold. Yet the families formed a close community. They knew each other well and helped each other in times of need. Some families lived in the Tower for generations, with sons following their fathers as warders. Today, the Yeoman Warders and their families still live in the Tower. They are one of the few communities in the world that lives inside a medieval fortress.

The community of the Tower of London had its own parish church, the chapel of Saint Peter ad Vincula. Historians note that its name means Saint Peter in chains, referring to the story in the Bible of Saint Peter being freed from prison by an angel. A chapel had stood on the site since before the Norman conquest. The present building was rebuilt in 1520, during the reign of Henry the Eighth, after a fire. Even so, the chapel was not only for the living. It was also the burial place for many people who died in the Tower, including those executed for treason. In 1536, Anne Boleyn was buried there, as were her brother George and later Catherine Howard and Lady Jane Grey. For the families of the warders and workers, the chapel was the centre of religious life. They attended services there, baptised their children and buried their dead. Between the inner and outer walls of the Tower ran a long narrow street, lined with workshops and houses. Between the inner and outer walls of the Tower of London ran a long, narrow passage. Historians note that in Tudor times, it was lined with workshops, storehouses and small houses. Much of the western and northern part of this passage was occupied by the Royal Mint. Historians note that living and working between the walls was not always pleasant. There was little sunlight, and the air could be smoky and stale. Yet it was a lively place, full of people working to keep the Tower and its many functions running.

The Tower's kitchens prepared meals for officials, guards, workers and prisoners. Feeding the people of the Tower of London was a large task. Historians note that the Tower had several kitchens, where cooks prepared meals for officials, guards, workers and prisoners. They baked bread, brewed ale, roasted meat and made pottages, thick soups of vegetables, grains and sometimes meat. Food was brought into the Tower from markets in the city and from farms outside London. Even so, not everyone ate the same food. The Constable, the Lieutenant and important prisoners ate well, with meat, fish, white bread, wine and spices. Ordinary workers and guards ate simpler meals, with brown bread, cheese, pottage and ale. Prisoners' food depended on their rank and wealth. Meals were an important part of daily life in the Tower.

Washerwomen, cooks and servants did the daily work that kept the Tower running. Behind the famous events of the Tower's history were many ordinary people who did the daily work. Historians note that washerwomen washed linen and clothes in wooden tubs, using water from wells and the river. Servants cleaned rooms, carried water and firewood and emptied chamber pots. Cooks and kitchen boys prepared meals. Stable hands cared for horses. Even so, their names were rarely recorded. Historians know about them mainly from payments in the royal accounts, which list wages for laundresses, porters and labourers. Many of these workers were women. They worked long hours for low pay, but their work was essential. Without them, the Tower could not have functioned as a palace, a prison or a workplace. Some workers lived inside the Tower, while others came in each day from the streets nearby. Historians today try to recover the lives of these ordinary people, who were as much a part of the Tower's story as its kings and prisoners.

Children grew up inside the Tower, playing in its courtyards and along its walls. The children of the warders, soldiers and workers grew up inside the Tower of London. Water for drinking, cooking and washing came from wells inside the Tower and from the river. Water was essential to life in the Tower of London. Historians note that there were several wells inside the walls, providing fresh water for drinking, cooking and washing. Servants drew water from the wells with buckets and ropes and carried it to the kitchens, houses and workshops. Water from the river and the moat was used for other purposes, such as washing floors and putting out fires. Even so, the water was not always clean. Wells could become contaminated, and the river was polluted with waste from the city. People in Tudor times often drank ale or beer instead of water, since brewing made it safer. Ale was drunk by adults and children alike, at every meal. The Tower had its own brewhouse and bakehouse. Historians note that providing clean water and food for a large community was a constant challenge. The officials of the Tower had to organise supplies carefully to keep everyone healthy.

Traders came to the gates of the Tower each day to sell food and goods to its inhabitants. The people of the Tower of London depended on traders from the city and the surrounding area. Historians note that each day, sellers came to the gates with bread, fish, meat, vegetables, fruit, eggs and ale. Others brought firewood, candles, cloth and other goods. The area around the Tower, known as the Liberties, was busy with markets, inns and shops. Even so, trading with the Tower had its rules. The officials of the Tower controlled who could enter and sell goods. They also collected fees and taxes. The Tower had special rights over the river nearby. Historians note that boats passing the Tower sometimes had to pay tolls, often in the form of goods such as wine or fish. These customs were part of the Tower's ancient privileges. The traders brought news and gossip from the city as well as goods. For the people inside, they were a link to the bustling world beyond the walls.

[long-pause]

{img:33-tower-bell-tower-ringing-evening}

A bell in the Bell Tower rang each evening to tell prisoners to return to their rooms. The Bell Tower stands at the southwest corner of the inner ward of the Tower of London. Historians note that it was built in the late twelfth century and is one of the oldest towers in the fortress. On its roof is a small timber belfry with a bell. For centuries, the bell rang each evening as a curfew, telling prisoners who were allowed to walk outside that it was time to return to their rooms. It also rang to warn of danger. Even so, the Bell Tower was not only a bell tower. It was also used as a prison for important people. In 1534 and 1535, Sir Thomas More, the former Lord Chancellor of England, was imprisoned there. Bishop John Fisher was also held in the Bell Tower. Later, the young Princess Elizabeth, the future Queen Elizabeth the First, was imprisoned there in 1554. The bell still rings at the Tower each evening, as it has for centuries.

[long-pause]

{img:34-mint-workers-hammering-coins-anvil}

England's coins were made by hand at the Royal Mint, inside the walls of the Tower. From around 1279, the Royal Mint was located inside the Tower of London. Historians note that it stayed there for more than five hundred years, until 1810. In Tudor times, all of England's coins were made by hand. Workers called moneyers placed a blank piece of silver or gold between two iron dies, engraved with the designs for each side of the coin. Then they struck the top die with a heavy hammer, pressing the design into the metal. Even so, the work required great skill. The coins had to be the right weight and purity, and the designs had to be clear. The Mint was strictly controlled, to prevent cheating and forgery. In 1536, the coins showed a portrait of Henry the Eighth on one side and the royal coat of arms on the other. The sound of hammers striking coins rang out from the Mint all day long, echoing between the walls.

[long-pause]

{img:35-mint-furnace-melting-silver-crucible}

Gold and silver were melted in furnaces and poured into moulds before they were made into coins. Before coins could be struck, the metal had to be prepared. Historians note that gold and silver were brought to the Mint as bars, old coins or plate, such as cups and dishes. They were melted in furnaces, purified and mixed with small amounts of other metals to make them harder. The molten metal was poured into moulds to make thin bars. Even so, this work was hot and dangerous. Furnaces burned day and night, filling the workshops with heat, smoke and fumes. Workers wore leather aprons and gloves to protect themselves. The bars were then hammered flat and cut into small round pieces, called blanks. Each blank was weighed and checked. Only then could it be struck into a coin. Historians note that the Mint employed many workers, including melters, refiners, cutters, weighers and moneyers. Each had a special role in the long process of turning precious metal into money.

[long-pause]

{img:36-coins-tudor-silver-groats-table}

Tudor coins included silver pennies, groats and shillings, and gold sovereigns and crowns. The coins of Tudor England came in several values. Historians note that the smallest was the silver penny, followed by the halfgroat, worth two pennies, and the groat, worth four. The testoon, later called the shilling, was worth twelve pennies. Gold coins included the sovereign, the crown and the angel, which showed the archangel Michael defeating a dragon. Even so, most ordinary people rarely saw gold coins. They used silver pennies and groats for daily purchases, such as bread, ale and candles. Some people still used barter, exchanging goods instead of money. In 1526, Henry the Eighth's chief minister, Cardinal Thomas Wolsey, reformed the coinage, changing the value and weight of coins. Later, in the 1540s, Henry would reduce the amount of silver in the coins, to raise money for his wars. This was called the Great Debasement. It caused prices to rise and made the coins less valuable.

[long-pause]

{img:37-mint-official-weighing-coins-balance}

Officials checked the weight and purity of coins carefully, to make sure that no one cheated. The Royal Mint was run by officials who were responsible for the quality of the coins. Historians note that the most important was the Master of the Mint, who managed the production of coins and paid the workers. Other officials, such as the Warden and the Comptroller, checked his work and kept accounts. Coins were tested regularly to make sure that they contained the right amount of gold or silver. Even so, cheating was a constant worry. Some workers might try to steal metal or make coins that were too light. Forgers outside the Mint might try to make fake coins. The punishment for forging the king's coins was very severe. It was considered a form of treason. Each year, a ceremony called the Trial of the Pyx was held to test samples of the coins. A jury of goldsmiths weighed and examined them. This ancient ceremony still takes place today.

[long-pause]

{img:38-mint-street-workers-break-ale}

Mint workers lived and worked together in the narrow street between the Tower's walls. The workers of the Royal Mint formed their own community within the Tower of London. Historians note that many of them lived in houses along the narrow street between the walls, close to their workshops. They worked long hours, from early morning until evening, with breaks for meals. They were paid according to the number of coins they produced. Even so, the work was well paid compared with many other trades. Moneyers were skilled craftsmen and were respected. Some families worked at the Mint for generations, passing their skills from father to son. The workers had their own customs, celebrations and rules. They also had disagreements with the Tower's other officials, especially about space and privileges. Historians note that the Mint and the Tower's garrison did not always get along. The Mint needed room for its workshops, while the soldiers needed room for their defences. The narrow street between the walls was often a crowded and contested place.

[long-pause]

{img:39-mint-machinery-later-milling-museum}

In 1810, the Mint left the Tower, and today it makes coins in Wales. The Royal Mint stayed inside the Tower of London for more than five centuries. Historians note that over time, its methods changed. In the seventeenth century, machines powered by horses or water began to replace hand striking. These machines produced coins that were more regular and harder to forge. Even so, the Mint's workshops in the Tower became too small and crowded. In 1810, the Mint moved to a new building on Tower Hill, just outside the Tower. In the 1960s and 1970s, it moved again, to Llantrisant in Wales, where it still makes coins today for Britain and many other countries. The old street between the Tower's walls, where moneyers once hammered coins by hand, is now part of the Tower's visitor route. Historians and visitors can see where the Mint once stood. The Tower's museum displays some of the tools and coins made there, reminders of centuries of work.

[long-pause]

{img:40-lion-tower-menagerie-entrance-1536}

For six hundred years, the Tower of London was home to a royal collection of wild animals. The Tower of London was home to a royal menagerie, a collection of wild animals, from the thirteenth century until 1835. Historians note that it began in the reign of King John or Henry the Third, when foreign rulers sent animals as gifts to the English king. The animals were kept near the entrance of the Tower, in a building called the Lion Tower, which stood beside the western gate. Even so, the menagerie was not a zoo in the modern sense. It was a royal collection, meant to show the king's power and wealth. Few people were allowed to see the animals at first. Later, the public could visit for a fee. In 1536, the menagerie held lions and other big cats. The animals were kept in cages and dens built into the walls of the Lion Tower. Their roars could be heard throughout the Tower and in the streets nearby.

[long-pause]

{img:41-keeper-feeding-lions-meat-cage}

A keeper was paid to look after the lions and other animals, feeding them meat every day. The animals of the royal menagerie were cared for by a keeper. Historians note that this was an official post, paid for by the crown. The keeper fed the animals, cleaned their cages and cared for them when they were ill. Lions and other big cats were fed large amounts of meat each day. Even so, the keeper's knowledge of the animals was limited. In Tudor times, little was known about how to care for wild animals from distant lands. The animals were kept in small, dark cages, very different from their natural homes. Many fell ill and died young. Yet some lived for many years and became famous. The keeper and his family usually lived near the Lion Tower. The job was often passed down through families. Historians note that the keepers sometimes earned extra money by showing the animals to visitors. Being the keeper of the king's lions was an unusual and sometimes dangerous job.

[long-pause]

{img:42-polar-bear-swimming-thames-chain-medieval}

In the thirteenth century, a white bear in the Tower was allowed to swim and fish in the Thames. One of the most famous animals in the history of the Tower's menagerie was a white bear, probably a polar bear. Historians note that it was sent to King Henry the Third in 1252 by King Haakon of Norway. The bear was kept at the Tower, and the king ordered that it should be given a long chain, so that it could swim and catch fish in the river Thames. The Sheriffs of London were ordered to pay for its food and keep. Even so, this was long before the Tudor period. The bear lived nearly three hundred years before 1536. Yet stories about it were still told in Tudor London. Historians note that the menagerie also held an elephant, a gift from the king of France in 1255. It was kept in a special house built for it, but it died after only two years. These unusual animals made the Tower famous across Europe.

[long-pause]

{img:43-visitors-watching-lion-cage-tudor}

People in Tudor London were fascinated by the lions, which they rarely had a chance to see. For most people in Tudor England, lions were creatures of stories, sermons and heraldry, not real animals. Historians note that the lion was a symbol of the English monarchy, appearing on the royal coat of arms. People knew lions from the Bible, from fables and from paintings. Seeing a real lion was a rare and exciting experience. Even so, visiting the menagerie was not easy for ordinary people in 1536. Visits were usually by permission, and important guests were given special tours. In later centuries, the menagerie became a popular attraction. In the eighteenth century, visitors could pay three halfpence to see the animals, or bring a cat or dog to feed to the lions instead. Historians note that people believed that the lions' health was linked to the health of the king. If a lion died, some feared that the king might die too. The lions were treated as royal symbols as well as animals.

[long-pause]

{img:44-leopard-cage-straw-tower-exotic-animals}

Over the centuries, the menagerie held leopards, bears, monkeys, an elephant and even an ostrich. The royal menagerie at the Tower of London held many kinds of animals over the centuries. Historians note that, in addition to lions, there were leopards, bears, wolves, monkeys, birds and even an ostrich. Some were gifts from foreign rulers, while others were bought by merchants and sailors from distant lands. Even so, the menagerie was often small. In some periods, it held only a few animals. In others, it was larger and more varied. In Tudor times, it probably held a handful of lions and other big cats, along with some smaller animals. The animals' living conditions were poor by modern standards. They were kept in cramped, dark cages, and many died young. In 1835, the menagerie was closed, and the remaining animals were moved to the new London Zoo in Regent's Park. Today, sculptures of animals made of wire stand around the Tower, reminding visitors of the creatures that once lived there.

[long-pause]

{img:45-lion-skull-museum-display-archaeology}

In 1937, the skulls of two lions from the Tower's menagerie were found in the old moat. In 1937, workers digging in the moat of the Tower of London found the skulls of two lions. Historians and scientists later studied them. Using radiocarbon dating, a method of measuring the age of ancient remains, they found that the skulls dated from between the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries. They were among the oldest remains of lions ever found in Britain. Even so, the skulls revealed more than their age. Scientists found that the lions were related to North African Barbary lions, a type of lion that is now extinct in the wild. Their bones also showed signs of poor health, caused by their life in captivity. Historians note that these discoveries give a rare glimpse into the history of the menagerie. They show that real lions lived at the Tower in the Middle Ages, much as the records describe. The skulls are now kept at the Natural History Museum in London.

[long-pause]

{img:46-gunners-cannons-tower-wharf-river}

The guns of the Tower stood along its wharf, facing the river Thames. Along the river side of the Tower of London ran a long stone wharf. Historians note that it was used to load and unload goods brought by boat, including weapons, gunpowder and supplies. Cannons stood along the wharf, facing the river. They were used to defend the Tower and to fire salutes on special occasions, such as royal births, coronations and victories. Even so, the cannons were rarely fired in anger. The Tower was never seriously attacked from the river in Tudor times. The gunners who cared for the cannons were skilled soldiers, trained to load, aim and fire. They lived in the Tower and were part of its garrison. Historians note that the tradition of firing salutes from the Tower continues today. On royal birthdays and other special occasions, guns are fired from Tower Wharf. The sound echoes across the river, as it has done for hundreds of years.

[long-pause]

{img:47-armourers-workshop-hammering-steel}

Armourers and craftsmen made and repaired weapons and armour for the king's armies. The Tower of London was the main store of weapons and armour for the English crown. Historians note that craftsmen worked there to make, repair and maintain equipment. Armourers hammered and shaped steel plates into helmets, breastplates and other pieces of armour. Bowyers made longbows, and fletchers made arrows. Gunsmiths cared for firearms and cannons. Even so, the most famous armour of Henry the Eighth's reign was made at Greenwich, where the king had set up royal workshops with armourers from Germany and the Low Countries. Finished armour was brought to the Tower for storage. The craftsmen at the Tower also prepared weapons for war. In 1536, England was not at war with a foreign enemy, but there was trouble at home. In October, a great rebellion broke out in the north of England, called the Pilgrimage of Grace. Weapons and soldiers were sent from the Tower to help put it down.

[long-pause]

{img:48-thames-watermen-boats-ferrying-passengers}

The river Thames was London's busiest road, crowded with boats, barges and ships. In Tudor times, the river Thames was the main road of London. Historians note that thousands of boats travelled on it every day. Watermen rowed small boats called wherries, carrying passengers across and along the river. Barges carried goods such as coal, timber, grain and stone. Large sailing ships brought cargoes from Europe and beyond, anchoring below London Bridge. Even so, travelling on the river was not always safe. The currents were strong, and accidents were common. Passing under London Bridge was especially dangerous, since the water rushed through the narrow arches like a waterfall. Many passengers preferred to get out before the bridge and walk around. The watermen were a large and proud group, with their own company and customs. They called out to passengers along the banks, shouting for customers. Historians note that the river was the lifeblood of London, linking the city to the rest of England and the world.

[long-pause]

{img:49-traitors-gate-water-arch-boat}

Prisoners accused of treason were often brought into the Tower by boat, through a water gate. On the river side of the Tower of London stands Saint Thomas's Tower, built by Edward the First in the 1270s. Historians note that beneath it is a great water gate, which allowed boats to enter the Tower directly from the Thames. In Tudor times, it was often used to bring prisoners into the Tower, especially those accused of treason, the crime of betraying the king. The gate became known as Traitors' Gate. Even so, not every prisoner entered this way. Some came by road, through the main gates on the western side. Historians are not sure that Anne Boleyn entered by Traitors' Gate, as is often said. She may have landed at another gate near the Byward Tower. Yet the water gate became a powerful symbol of the Tower's role as a prison. Today, the river has been walled off, and the gate no longer opens onto the water. Visitors can see its great arch and heavy timbers.

[long-pause]

{img:50-london-bridge-houses-heads-spikes}

London Bridge, crowded with houses and shops, was the only bridge across the Thames in London. In 1536, London Bridge was the only bridge across the river Thames in London. Historians note that it had been built of stone in the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries and had nineteen arches. It was lined with houses and shops, some of them several storeys high. A chapel stood in the middle of the bridge, and gatehouses stood at each end. Even so, the bridge also had a grim purpose. On the gatehouse at its southern end, the heads of people executed for treason were displayed on spikes, as a warning to others. In July 1535, the heads of Sir Thomas More and Bishop John Fisher were placed there after their executions on Tower Hill. More's daughter, Margaret Roper, later recovered her father's head. The bridge was a busy and lively place, crowded with people, carts and animals. It remained the only river crossing in London until the eighteenth century.

[long-pause]

{img:51-sunset-thames-tower-silhouette-boats}

In the evening, the boats on the river grew fewer, and the Tower stood quietly by the water. As evening fell over the Thames, the river grew quieter. Historians imagine the scene: the last boats heading for home, the watermen tying up their wherries, the barges anchoring for the night. The sun set behind the city, and the sky turned orange and pink. The Tower of London stood dark against the glowing sky, its towers and turrets outlined against the light. Even so, life on the river never completely stopped. Some boats travelled at night, carrying goods or passengers by lantern light. Fishermen set their nets. The tide rose and fell, washing against the Tower's wharf. On the walls, guards began their night watch. In the menagerie, the lions stirred in their cages. Inside the Tower, families sat down to supper, and prisoners prepared for another night in their rooms. Historians note that the river was always present in the life of the Tower, flowing past day and night, through every season.

[long-pause]

{img:52-prisoner-room-tower-furniture-books}

Most prisoners in the Tower were nobles, officials or churchmen, and many were held in furnished rooms. The Tower of London was not an ordinary prison. Historians note that it was used mainly for important prisoners, such as nobles, officials, churchmen and people accused of crimes against the king. Ordinary criminals were usually sent to other prisons in London, such as Newgate. Prisoners in the Tower were often held in rooms in the towers, rather than in dungeons. Some rooms were quite comfortable, with beds, tables, chairs and fireplaces. Wealthy prisoners could bring their own furniture, clothes and books, and pay for better food. Even so, imprisonment in the Tower was frightening. Prisoners did not know how long they would be held or what would happen to them. Many were held for months or years, waiting for trial. Some were released, while others were executed. Historians note that the uncertainty was often the hardest part. The Tower's walls kept the prisoners safely inside, but they could not keep out fear.

[long-pause]

{img:53-carved-names-stone-wall-prison-graffiti}

Many prisoners carved their names, prayers and symbols into the stone walls of their rooms. To pass the long hours of imprisonment, many prisoners in the Tower of London carved inscriptions into the walls of their rooms. Historians note that the walls of the Beauchamp Tower are especially rich in these carvings. They include names, dates, prayers, coats of arms, religious symbols and short poems. Some are simple scratches, while others are carefully designed and beautifully carved. Even so, most of the carvings in the Beauchamp Tower date from after 1536, from the reigns of Edward the Sixth, Mary the First and Elizabeth the First. Some were made by members of the Dudley family, who were imprisoned there in the 1550s. Historians value these carvings as a direct link to the prisoners. They show their faith, their fears and their hopes. Some carvings ask God for help, while others express sorrow or courage. Visitors to the Tower today can still see them, preserved behind glass, centuries after they were made.

[long-pause]

{img:54-thomas-more-prison-writing-desk}

Sir Thomas More, the former Lord Chancellor, spent over a year as a prisoner in the Tower. Sir Thomas More was a lawyer, scholar and writer, famous across Europe for his book Utopia, published in 1516. Historians note that he served Henry the Eighth as Lord Chancellor, the highest official in the kingdom, from 1529 to 1532. However, he disagreed with Henry's break with the Pope and his marriage to Anne Boleyn. In April 1534, he refused to swear an oath accepting the new order of succession and Henry's position as head of the Church. He was imprisoned in the Bell Tower. Even so, he spent his time in prison writing and praying. He wrote A Dialogue of Comfort against Tribulation, a book about finding strength in suffering. His daughter Margaret visited him and brought him news. In July 1535, he was tried, found guilty of treason and executed on Tower Hill. According to tradition, he said that he died the king's good servant, but God's first. He was later made a saint.

[long-pause]

{img:55-bishop-fisher-elderly-prisoner-cold-room}

Bishop John Fisher, an elderly churchman, was also imprisoned in the Tower and executed in 1535. John Fisher was the Bishop of Rochester and a respected scholar. Historians note that he had been a supporter and adviser of Henry the Eighth's grandmother, Lady Margaret Beaufort, and had helped found colleges at Cambridge. Like Thomas More, he opposed Henry's divorce from Catherine of Aragon and his break with Rome. In 1534, he refused to take the oath of succession and was sent to the Tower. Even so, his imprisonment was harsh. He was elderly and in poor health, and he suffered from the cold. In a letter, he asked for warmer clothing and better food. In May 1535, the Pope made him a cardinal, hoping to protect him. Henry was furious. He is said to have declared that he would send Fisher's head to Rome for the cardinal's hat. Fisher was executed on Tower Hill on the twenty-second of June 1535. He, too, was later made a saint.

[long-pause]

{img:56-prisoner-walking-on-walls-with-warder}

Some prisoners were allowed to walk on the walls or in the gardens for exercise. Prisoners in the Tower of London were not always kept locked in their rooms. Historians note that some were allowed to walk on the walls, in the gardens or in the courtyards for exercise and fresh air. They were always accompanied by a warder. This privilege depended on the prisoner's rank, behaviour and the seriousness of the charges. Even so, the walks were carefully controlled. Prisoners were not allowed to speak to other prisoners or to people outside. The walk along the walls between the Beauchamp Tower and the Bell Tower became known as the Prisoners' Walk. From the walls, prisoners could see the river, the boats and the city beyond. For people who spent most of their time indoors, these walks were precious moments. Historians note that some prisoners later wrote about the beauty of the river and the sky, seen from the walls of their prison. These views reminded them of the freedom they had lost.

[long-pause]

{img:57-family-visiting-prisoner-room-tower}

Family members were sometimes allowed to visit prisoners and bring them food, clothes and news. Prisoners in the Tower of London were sometimes allowed visits from their families. Historians note that these visits required permission from the king's council or the Constable. Visitors were searched and watched by the warders, and their conversations were often listened to. Even so, the visits were important to the prisoners. Family members brought food, clothes, books, money and news from the outside world. They also brought comfort and love. Thomas More's daughter, Margaret Roper, visited him several times and tried to persuade him to take the oath, but he refused. His wife, Alice, also visited him. Their conversations were later written down by More's family and friends. Historians note that letters were another way for prisoners to stay in touch with their families. Letters were read by the officials before being sent. Some prisoners wrote secret messages, using tricks such as writing in lemon juice, which became visible only when heated.

[long-pause]

{img:58-candle-prison-window-night-quiet}

For many prisoners, the quiet nights in the Tower were the longest and loneliest hours. At night, the Tower of London grew quiet. Historians imagine the prisoners in their rooms, lit by a single candle, listening to the sounds of the night. The bells of city churches rang the hours. The river lapped against the wharf. The footsteps of the guards echoed on the walls. Even so, many prisoners found the nights especially hard. Alone with their thoughts, they worried about their families, their futures and their fates. Some prayed, some read, and some wrote letters or poems. Many prisoners turned to their faith for comfort. Thomas More wrote that prison could be a place of spiritual growth, where a person could think about God and eternity without the distractions of the world. Historians note that the Tower was a place of great suffering, but also of great courage. The prisoners who passed through it faced their fears in different ways. Their stories are an important part of the Tower's history.

[long-pause]

{img:59-anne-boleyn-portrait-tudor-gown-1536}

In May 1536, Queen Anne Boleyn was arrested and brought to the Tower of London. Anne Boleyn had been queen of England for only three years when she fell from power. Historians note that she was intelligent, well educated and strong-willed. She had grown up partly at the courts of the Netherlands and France, and she was admired for her elegance and wit. Henry the Eighth had broken with Rome in order to marry her. Even so, the marriage did not bring Henry the son he wanted. Anne gave birth to a daughter, Elizabeth, in 1533, but she later lost several babies. In January 1536, she had a miscarriage. By then, Henry had become interested in one of her ladies-in-waiting, Jane Seymour. Anne also had powerful enemies at court, including, by the spring of 1536, the king's chief minister, Thomas Cromwell. On the second of May 1536, she was arrested at Greenwich Palace and accused of adultery and treason. That afternoon, she was taken by barge to the Tower.

[long-pause]

{img:60-barge-arriving-tower-afternoon-guards}

When she arrived at the Tower, Anne asked whether she would be put in a dungeon. Anne Boleyn arrived at the Tower of London on the afternoon of the second of May 1536. Historians know about her arrival from the letters of Sir William Kingston, the Constable of the Tower, who received her. According to Kingston, she asked him whether she would go into a dungeon. He replied that she would stay in the lodgings she had used before her coronation. She is said to have answered that it was too good for her, and then knelt down and wept, and then laughed. Even so, historians must be careful with these details. Kingston was reporting to Thomas Cromwell, and his letters were written to serve the king's purposes. Yet they give a vivid and moving picture of the queen's distress. Over the following days, Kingston and his wife, along with several ladies chosen by the king, attended Anne. They reported her words and actions back to Cromwell, and their reports were used against her.

[long-pause]

{img:61-five-men-accused-court-tudor}

Five men, including the queen's own brother, were also arrested and sent to the Tower. Along with Anne Boleyn, five men were arrested and imprisoned in the Tower of London in May 1536. Historians note that they were accused of adultery with the queen and of plotting against the king. They were Anne's brother, George Boleyn, Lord Rochford, and four courtiers: Henry Norris, Francis Weston, William Brereton and a young musician named Mark Smeaton. Smeaton, the only one who was not a gentleman, confessed, probably after being threatened or tortured. The others denied the charges. Even so, all five were found guilty. Historians today believe that the charges were almost certainly false. Many think that Cromwell built the case against them to remove Anne and her supporters. The poet Thomas Wyatt and another courtier, Richard Page, were also arrested but later released. On the seventeenth of May 1536, the five men were executed on Tower Hill. Their deaths shocked the court and the people of London.

[long-pause]

{img:62-trial-great-hall-crowd-wooden-stands}

Anne Boleyn was tried in the great hall of the Tower, watched by about two thousand people. On the fifteenth of May 1536, Anne Boleyn was tried in the great hall of the Tower of London. Historians note that a large wooden stand had been built for spectators, and about two thousand people came to watch. The trial was presided over by Anne's uncle, Thomas Howard, Duke of Norfolk, as Lord High Steward. A jury of peers, nobles of the realm, heard the case. Even so, the outcome was never really in doubt. Anne defended herself calmly and clearly, denying all the charges. Witnesses praised her composure and dignity. Yet the peers found her guilty. Norfolk pronounced the sentence: she was to be burned or beheaded, at the king's pleasure. Historians note that Henry chose beheading. Anne's brother George was tried immediately afterwards and also found guilty. Some spectators believed that the evidence was weak. One of the peers, Henry Percy, who had once hoped to marry Anne, collapsed and had to be helped from the hall.

[long-pause]

{img:63-swordsman-from-calais-arrives-ship}

Henry ordered a skilled swordsman to be brought from Calais to carry out the execution. In England, people of high rank who were executed for treason were usually beheaded with an axe. Historians note that Henry the Eighth decided that Anne Boleyn should be beheaded with a sword instead. He sent for an expert swordsman from Calais, a town on the French coast that belonged to England at that time. Beheading with a sword was common in France, and it was thought to be quicker and more merciful than an axe. Even so, the reason for Henry's choice is not certain. Some historians think he wished to spare Anne unnecessary suffering. Others believe the arrangement had been made even before her trial. The swordsman's name is not known. Anne's execution was delayed by a day, partly because the swordsman had not yet arrived. According to Kingston, Anne said she had heard that the executioner was very good, and that she had a little neck. She then put her hands around it and laughed.

[long-pause]

{img:64-early-morning-tower-green-scaffold-crowd}

On the morning of the nineteenth of May 1536, Anne Boleyn was executed inside the walls of the Tower. Anne Boleyn was executed on the morning of Friday, the nineteenth of May 1536. Historians note that the execution took place inside the walls of the Tower of London, rather than on Tower Hill, where most executions were held in public. Only a small crowd of officials, nobles and citizens was allowed to watch. Anne walked to the scaffold wearing a dark grey gown and a cape trimmed with fur, accompanied by her ladies. Even so, she remained calm. She gave a short speech, in which she did not admit guilt but praised the king and asked the people to pray for him. Then she knelt, and the swordsman ended her life with a single stroke. Her ladies wrapped her body and head in a white cloth. Historians note that no proper coffin had been prepared. She was placed in an old chest for arrows and buried in the chapel of Saint Peter ad Vincula, close to her brother George.

[long-pause]

{img:65-jane-seymour-portrait-tudor-1536}

Eleven days after Anne's death, Henry the Eighth married Jane Seymour. On the twentieth of May 1536, the day after Anne Boleyn's execution, Henry the Eighth was formally betrothed to Jane Seymour. Historians note that they were married on the thirtieth of May at Whitehall Palace. Jane was the daughter of a Wiltshire gentleman and had served as a lady-in-waiting to both Catherine of Aragon and Anne Boleyn. She was described as quiet, gentle and modest, very different from Anne. Even so, her marriage to Henry was short. In October 1537, she gave birth to the son Henry had longed for, the future King Edward the Sixth. But she fell ill after the birth and died twelve days later. Henry was deeply grieved. Historians note that Jane was the only one of his wives to receive a queen's funeral, and Henry later asked to be buried beside her. The spring of 1536 had changed the life of the court completely, and the Tower had been at its centre.

[long-pause]

{img:66-tower-green-grass-chapel-quiet}

Only a few people were executed privately inside the Tower, on or near the area called Tower Green. Tower Green is an open area of grass inside the inner ward of the Tower of London, near the chapel of Saint Peter ad Vincula. Historians note that it was used for private executions, held away from the eyes of the public. Only a small number of people were executed there, usually people of high rank whose public execution might cause sympathy or unrest. They included Anne Boleyn in 1536, Margaret Pole, Countess of Salisbury, in 1541, Catherine Howard in 1542, and Lady Jane Grey in 1554. Even so, historians are not certain exactly where on or near the green these executions took place. Some evidence suggests that Anne Boleyn's scaffold stood near the White Tower, on the parade ground. Today, a memorial stands on Tower Green, a glass cushion created by the artist Brian Catling in 2006, remembering those who died there. It is a quiet and reflective place, visited by many people each year.

[long-pause]

{img:67-tower-hill-scaffold-crowd-public}

Most executions took place in public on Tower Hill, just outside the walls of the Tower. Tower Hill is an area of high ground just outside the northwestern walls of the Tower of London. Historians note that from the fourteenth century until the eighteenth, it was the main place for public executions of people who had been held in the Tower. A scaffold stood there, and crowds gathered to watch. Thomas More, John Fisher, the five men accused with Anne Boleyn and many others were executed there. Even so, executions on Tower Hill were not common events. They were usually reserved for people of high rank. Ordinary criminals were hanged at Tyburn, on the western edge of London. Historians note that public executions were meant to show the power of the king and to warn others against treason. Crowds came out of curiosity, sympathy or a wish to see justice done. Today, a memorial in the gardens of Trinity Square marks the site of the old scaffold, listing the names of many who died there.

[long-pause]

{img:68-poet-thomas-wyatt-window-bell-tower}

The poet Thomas Wyatt, held in the Tower in 1536, may have watched the executions from his window. Sir Thomas Wyatt was a courtier, diplomat and poet, one of the first to write sonnets in English. Historians note that he was a friend of Anne Boleyn and had known her since childhood. In May 1536, he was arrested and sent to the Tower, accused of being one of her lovers. Unlike the others, he was released after a few weeks, probably thanks to his friendship with Thomas Cromwell and his father's service to the king. Even so, the experience marked him deeply. According to tradition, he watched the executions of the five men on Tower Hill, and possibly that of Anne, from a window in the Bell Tower. He later wrote a poem about the fall of the men, with a Latin refrain meaning that thunder rolls around the throne. In it, he described the dangers of life at court. Historians note that the poem is one of the most moving reflections on the events of 1536. Wyatt died in 1542.

[long-pause]

{img:69-arrow-chest-chapel-burial-candles}

Anne Boleyn and others who died in the Tower were buried in the chapel of Saint Peter ad Vincula. The chapel of Saint Peter ad Vincula became the burial place for many of those executed at the Tower. Historians note that Anne Boleyn was buried there on the day of her death, in an old arrow chest, beneath the floor near the altar. Her brother George and the other men executed with them were buried in the chapel or its churchyard. Later, Catherine Howard, Lady Jane Grey, Thomas Cromwell and others were buried there too. Even so, the burials were often hurried and simple, and the graves were not well marked. In the nineteenth century, during repairs to the chapel, remains were found beneath the floor. They were examined and reburied, and their names were marked with memorial stones. Historians note that the identification of some remains, including those thought to be Anne's, is uncertain. The chapel is still used for worship today. Its quiet interior is a place where visitors can remember the people buried there.

[long-pause]

{img:70-memorial-tower-green-glass-sculpture}

Today, a memorial on Tower Green remembers those who were executed within the Tower's walls. In 2006, a memorial was unveiled on Tower Green, inside the Tower of London. Historians note that it was designed by the artist Brian Catling. It is a round glass sculpture shaped like a cushion, resting on a dark stone base. Around it are engraved the names of those who were executed inside the Tower's walls, including Anne Boleyn, Catherine Howard and Lady Jane Grey. A short poem is engraved around the edge. Even so, the memorial is not a grave or a monument to great deeds. It is a quiet place for reflection, inviting visitors to think of those who died. The glass cushion suggests the place where a head might rest. Historians note that the memorial reflects changing attitudes towards the Tower's history. Instead of celebrating royal power, it remembers the human cost of the political struggles of the past. Many visitors pause there, reading the names and thinking about the lives behind them.

[long-pause]

{img:71-tower-hill-garden-memorial-trees}

Tower Hill, once a place of public executions, is now a quiet garden in the heart of London. Today, Tower Hill is very different from the grim place it was in Tudor times. Historians note that the site of the old scaffold is now part of Trinity Square Gardens, a quiet green space with trees, flowers and benches. A small memorial marks the spot where the scaffold once stood, listing the names of more than a hundred people who were executed there. Even so, the area is busy and lively. People walk through the gardens on their way to work, sit on benches to eat lunch and visit the nearby memorials to sailors lost at sea in the two world wars. Tourists pass by on their way to the Tower. Historians note that the contrast between the peaceful gardens and their violent history is striking. The trees grow tall, and birds sing in their branches. Few people who sit there today stop to think about the crowds who once gathered on the same ground.

[long-pause]

{img:72-tudor-london-cheapside-market-street}

In 1536, London was a growing city of about fifty thousand people, crowded inside its old Roman walls. In 1536, London had about fifty thousand inhabitants. Historians note that it was growing quickly and would reach about two hundred thousand by 1600. Most people lived inside the old city walls, which had first been built by the Romans. The streets were narrow, crowded and noisy, lined with timber-framed houses, shops and churches. Even so, London was also a city of wealth and trade. Cheapside, its main market street, was famous for its goldsmiths' shops, which glittered with silver and gold. Merchants traded wool, cloth, wine, spices and many other goods. The city was governed by the Lord Mayor and the aldermen, elected by the guilds. Historians note that there were more than a hundred parish churches inside the walls, each with its own bells. Above them all rose the great spire of Saint Paul's Cathedral. The Tower stood at the eastern edge of the city, guarding the river and watching over its people.

[long-pause]

{img:73-old-st-pauls-cathedral-spire-1536}

Old Saint Paul's Cathedral, with its enormous spire, was the largest building in Tudor London. Before the Great Fire of London in 1666, the city was dominated by Old Saint Paul's Cathedral. Historians note that it was one of the largest churches in Europe, with a spire that rose to about one hundred and fifty metres. It was built of stone in the Gothic style, mostly in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. Even so, the cathedral was not only a place of worship. Its long central aisle, called Paul's Walk, was used as a meeting place where people gathered to gossip, do business and look for work. Booksellers set up stalls in the churchyard, which became the centre of the London book trade. Preachers gave sermons at an outdoor pulpit called Paul's Cross, attracting large crowds. Historians note that the spire was struck by lightning and destroyed in 1561 and never rebuilt. The whole cathedral was destroyed in the Great Fire. The present Saint Paul's, with its famous dome, was designed by Christopher Wren.

[long-pause]

{img:74-all-hallows-church-near-tower-tudor}

Near the Tower stood the ancient church of All Hallows, one of the oldest churches in London. Just west of the Tower of London stands the church of All Hallows by the Tower. Historians note that it was founded in the seventh century, around the year 675, by the Abbey of Barking, making it one of the oldest churches in London. Parts of an Anglo-Saxon arch still survive inside it. In Tudor times, it was the parish church for many of the people who lived near the Tower. Even so, it had a special connection with the Tower's history. The bodies of some people executed on Tower Hill were brought to All Hallows for temporary burial. In the seventeenth century, the diarist Samuel Pepys climbed its tower to watch the Great Fire of London. The church was badly damaged by bombing in the Second World War but was rebuilt. Historians note that it contains a small museum with Roman remains found beneath the church. It is a quiet link to London's long history, right beside the Tower.

[long-pause]

{img:75-dissolution-monastery-empty-cloister-1536}

In 1536, Henry the Eighth began to close the monasteries of England and take their wealth. In 1536, the English Parliament passed a law to close the smaller monasteries of England, those with an income of less than two hundred pounds a year. Historians note that this was the beginning of the Dissolution of the Monasteries, which by 1540 had closed all the religious houses in England and Wales. The monks, nuns and friars were sent away, and the monasteries' lands, buildings and treasures were taken by the crown. Even so, the dissolution was not only about religion. It brought great wealth to the king, who needed money for his court and his wars. Much of the land was sold to nobles and gentry, who became richer and more loyal to the crown. In London, many monasteries and priories were closed, and their buildings were turned into houses, workshops or ruins. Historians note that the dissolution changed the landscape of England and the lives of thousands of people forever.

[long-pause]

{img:76-pilgrimage-of-grace-banners-northern-army}

In the autumn of 1536, a great rebellion called the Pilgrimage of Grace broke out in the north of England. In October 1536, a great rebellion broke out in Lincolnshire and Yorkshire, in the north of England. Historians note that it was called the Pilgrimage of Grace. The rebels were angry about the closing of the monasteries, the changes to religion, new taxes and the power of Thomas Cromwell. They marched under banners showing the five wounds of Christ. At its height, the rebel army had perhaps thirty thousand men. Even so, the rebellion did not lead to war. The Duke of Norfolk, sent by the king with a much smaller army, negotiated with the rebels. He promised that their complaints would be heard and that they would be pardoned. The rebels went home. However, early in 1537, there were new risings, and Henry took his revenge. The leaders, including the lawyer Robert Aske, were arrested. Some were held in the Tower of London before their trials. About two hundred people were executed.

[long-pause]

{img:77-sweating-sickness-tudor-house-doctor}

Disease was a constant danger in Tudor London, including the dreaded sweating sickness. Life in Tudor London was often cut short by disease. Historians note that plague returned to the city again and again, killing thousands of people in bad years. There was also a mysterious illness called the sweating sickness. It struck suddenly, causing fever, sweating, pain and exhaustion, and it could kill within hours. It appeared in England in 1485 and returned several times, with a serious outbreak in 1528. Even so, its cause has never been identified. Some historians and scientists believe it may have been a virus, but no one is certain. The sweating sickness seemed to affect the rich as well as the poor. In 1528, Anne Boleyn herself caught it but survived. Henry the Eighth was terrified of the illness and fled from place to place to avoid it. Historians note that disease was a constant fear in Tudor life. People prayed, used herbs and medicines and left the city when outbreaks struck.

[long-pause]

{img:78-thames-frost-fair-ice-tudor-winter}

In very cold winters, the Thames sometimes froze, and Londoners walked across it on the ice. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, Europe experienced a period of colder weather, sometimes called the Little Ice Age. Historians note that in some especially cold winters, the river Thames froze solid in London. The old London Bridge, with its many narrow arches, slowed the flow of the river, which made it easier for ice to form. When the river froze, Londoners walked, skated and even rode across it. Even so, the great frost fairs, with stalls, games and entertainments on the ice, came later, especially in the seventeenth century. In the winter of 1536 to 1537, the Thames froze, and Henry the Eighth and Jane Seymour are said to have travelled across the ice on horseback from Westminster to Greenwich. Historians note that a frozen river meant hardship for many. Boatmen could not work, and goods could not be moved by water. Yet for others, the ice was a source of wonder and fun.

[long-pause]

{img:79-ravens-on-grass-tower-white-tower}

Ravens have lived around the Tower of London for centuries, and a legend says the kingdom will fall if they leave. Today, the Tower of London is famous for its ravens. Historians note that a legend says that if the ravens ever leave the Tower, the kingdom will fall. According to some stories, King Charles the Second ordered that at least six ravens should always be kept there, after being warned by an astronomer. Even so, historians have found no evidence for this legend before the nineteenth century. In Tudor times, ravens and red kites were common birds in London. They scavenged for food in the streets, eating scraps and waste. They may have gathered around the Tower, especially after executions. Today, the ravens are cared for by a Yeoman Warder called the Ravenmaster. They live in comfortable enclosures and are fed meat, eggs and biscuits soaked in blood. They have names and personalities. Some have lived at the Tower for more than twenty years. They are among the Tower's most popular residents.

[long-pause]

{img:80-ravenmaster-feeding-ravens-morning}

The Ravenmaster looks after the ravens every day, feeding them and getting to know each one. The Ravenmaster is a Yeoman Warder who is responsible for the care of the Tower's ravens. Historians note that the position was created in the twentieth century. Each morning, the Ravenmaster lets the ravens out of their enclosures and feeds them. In the evening, he calls them back for the night. He knows each bird well and can recognise their individual voices and behaviour. Even so, the ravens are not always easy to manage. They are intelligent, curious and sometimes mischievous. They have been known to steal food from visitors, hide shiny objects and imitate sounds. Some have even learned to say a few words. Occasionally, a raven has flown away from the Tower, but most stay close to home. The Ravenmaster makes sure there are always at least six ravens at the Tower, with extra birds in case one is lost. Historians note that the ravens have become a symbol of the Tower and its long history.

[long-pause]

{img:81-ceremony-keys-lantern-night-warder}

Every night, the Chief Yeoman Warder locks the gates of the Tower in the Ceremony of the Keys. The Ceremony of the Keys is one of the oldest traditions at the Tower of London. Historians note that it has been performed every night for many centuries, though its exact age is not known. At about seven minutes to ten each evening, the Chief Yeoman Warder sets out with a lantern and the keys of the Tower. Escorted by soldiers, he locks the main gates. On his way back, he is challenged by a sentry, who calls out to ask who comes there. The Warder replies that the keys belong to the king or queen. The sentry lets him pass, and the guards present arms. Even so, the ceremony is more than a show. It is the real locking of the Tower for the night. It is said to have been interrupted only once, during an air raid in the Second World War, when the escort was delayed by a few minutes. The ceremony ends with the sound of a bugle.

[long-pause]

{img:82-crown-jewels-jewel-house-glass-cases}

The Crown Jewels of England are kept in the Tower of London and can be seen by visitors. The Crown Jewels of the United Kingdom are kept in the Jewel House at the Tower of London. Historians note that royal treasures have been stored in the Tower since the Middle Ages. In 1536, Henry the Eighth's crowns, sceptres and other regalia were kept in a jewel house within the Tower's walls. Even so, most of the medieval and Tudor regalia no longer exist. In 1649, after the execution of King Charles the First, Parliament ordered the royal regalia to be melted down or sold. When the monarchy was restored in 1660, new crowns and regalia were made for King Charles the Second. These form the core of today's collection. They include Saint Edward's Crown, used at coronations, and the Imperial State Crown. Historians note that the collection contains famous jewels, including the Cullinan diamonds. Millions of visitors see the Crown Jewels each year, passing slowly along moving walkways in front of the glittering treasures.

[long-pause]

{img:83-white-tower-visitors-sunny-day}

The Tower of London is now one of the most visited historic sites in the world. Today, the Tower of London is one of the most famous historic sites in the world. Historians note that it was listed as a World Heritage Site in 1988. It is cared for by a charity called Historic Royal Palaces, which also looks after other royal palaces, such as Hampton Court. Each year, about three million people visit the Tower. They walk through its gates, explore its towers and walls, see the Crown Jewels and the armour of Henry the Eighth and hear stories from the Yeoman Warders. Even so, the Tower is still a living place. The Yeoman Warders and their families live there, and soldiers guard the Crown Jewels. Services are held in the chapel. The ravens wander across the lawns. Historians note that the Tower has been in use for nearly a thousand years. It has been a palace, a fortress, a prison, a mint, a zoo and an armoury. Today, it is a place of memory and learning.

[long-pause]

{img:84-armour-henry-viii-museum-display}

Visitors can still see suits of armour made for Henry the Eighth in the White Tower. Inside the White Tower, visitors can see the Royal Armouries' collection of arms and armour. Historians note that it is one of the oldest museums in the world, as royal armour has been displayed at the Tower since at least the seventeenth century. Among its treasures are several suits of armour made for Henry the Eighth. One, made when he was young, shows a tall, athletic man. Another, made near the end of his life, shows a much larger man, with a wide waist. Even so, the armour is not only about the king. It shows the skill of the armourers who made it, shaping and decorating steel with great care. Some suits are engraved with fine patterns and gilded with gold. Historians note that the armour helps visitors imagine the people of the past, their size, their strength and their love of display. Standing before it, it is easy to picture the king who ruled England in 1536.

[long-pause]

{img:85-yeoman-warder-telling-story-visitors}

Today's Yeoman Warders are former soldiers who guide visitors and tell the Tower's stories. Today, the Yeoman Warders of the Tower of London are all former members of the armed forces, with at least twenty-two years of service. Historians note that they are chosen for their good conduct and experience. They live in the Tower with their families and wear uniforms based on Tudor designs. On ordinary days, they wear dark blue and red. On special occasions, they wear the scarlet and gold state dress. Even so, their main role today is to guide and inform visitors. They lead tours of the Tower, telling stories of its history, its prisoners and its legends. They are famous for their humour and knowledge. In 2007, Moira Cameron became the first woman to be appointed a Yeoman Warder. Historians note that the Warders are a living link to the Tower's Tudor past. Their uniforms, ceremonies and traditions connect today's visitors with the warders who guarded the gates in 1536.

[long-pause]

{img:86-tower-london-night-lights-river-calm}

Nearly a thousand years after it was built, the Tower of London still stands by the Thames. Nearly a thousand years after William the Conqueror began building the White Tower, the Tower of London still stands on the bank of the Thames. Historians note that it has survived wars, fires, revolutions and bombings. It has seen kings and queens crowned and dethroned, prisoners arrive and depart, and the city around it grow from a small medieval town into one of the largest cities in the world. Even so, much has stayed the same. The White Tower still rises above the walls. The ravens still walk on the grass. The gates are still locked each night with the same words. The river still flows past, as it did in 1536. On quiet evenings, when the visitors have gone, the Tower returns to its small community of warders and their families. Lights glow in the windows of their houses, and the old stones settle into darkness, holding their many stories safely within the walls.
