TOPIC: The Constable and the Yeoman Warders
===
IMG: constable-tower-portrait-tudor-office | a Tudor nobleman in a dark velvet gown with a fur collar and a gold chain of office seated at a desk with letters and keys in a stone-walled office of the Tower of London, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: The Tower was governed by the Constable, a trusted royal official appointed by the king.
BODY: The most senior official of the Tower of London was the Constable. Historians note that the post had existed since the time of William the Conqueror, and it was given to men whom the king trusted completely. The Constable was responsible for the security of the Tower, its prisoners, its garrison and its buildings. In 1536, the Constable was Sir William Kingston, a soldier and courtier who had served Henry the Eighth for many years. Even so, the Constable did not do all the work himself. He was helped by the Lieutenant of the Tower, who lived in a house within the walls and managed daily affairs. The Lieutenant in 1536 was Sir Edmund Walsingham. Kingston's letters to the king's chief minister, Thomas Cromwell, survive. They describe in detail the behaviour and words of Anne Boleyn during her imprisonment in May 1536. These letters are among the most important sources for her last days.
===
IMG: yeoman-warders-tudor-uniform-gate | Tudor Yeoman Warders in red tunics with gold trim, flat black hats and white ruffs standing guard with halberds at a stone gate of the Tower of London, warm morning light
HEAD: The Yeoman Warders guarded the gates of the Tower and watched over its prisoners.
BODY: The guards of the Tower of London were called Yeoman Warders. Historians note that their history is connected with the Yeomen of the Guard, a royal bodyguard created by Henry the Seventh in 1485, after he won the crown at the Battle of Bosworth. Some of these yeomen were posted to the Tower to guard the gates and the prisoners. They wore red uniforms with gold decorations and carried halberds, long poles with an axe blade and a spike. Even so, their work was often routine. They opened and closed the gates, checked visitors, guarded prisoners and kept watch at night. Some escorted prisoners to their trials and executions. The Yeoman Warders are still at the Tower today, wearing uniforms based on Tudor designs. They are popularly known as Beefeaters. The origin of this nickname is uncertain, but it may refer to the generous rations of beef that royal guards once received.
===
IMG: warder-escorting-prisoner-stone-stairs | a Tudor Yeoman Warder in a red tunic escorting a well-dressed prisoner in a dark doublet down a narrow stone staircase inside a tower, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft light from a narrow window
HEAD: Warders escorted prisoners to their rooms and brought them food, letters and visitors.
BODY: Guarding prisoners was one of the most important duties of the Yeoman Warders. Historians note that each important prisoner was assigned a warder, who was responsible for watching over him or her. The warder escorted the prisoner to and from their room, brought them meals and allowed visitors only with permission. He also reported on the prisoner's behaviour and conversations to the Lieutenant. Even so, the relationship between warders and prisoners was not always harsh. Many prisoners were nobles or educated people, and they were treated with courtesy. Some warders became friendly with their prisoners, talking with them, bringing them news and doing small favours. Prisoners sometimes paid warders for extra comforts, such as better food, candles or firewood. The warders were paid by the crown, but they often earned extra money from prisoners and visitors. Their position gave them a respected place in the small community of the Tower.
===
IMG: lieutenants-lodgings-timber-house-tower | a large Tudor timber-framed house with a steep roof and leaded windows built against the stone walls inside the Tower of London, a small garden in front, warm afternoon light
HEAD: The Lieutenant of the Tower lived in a fine timber-framed house within the walls.
BODY: The Lieutenant of the Tower lived in lodgings in the southwest corner of the inner ward. Historians note that around 1540, a few years after 1536, a new house was built there in the Tudor style, with a timber frame and a steep roof. Today it is called the Queen's House. It is one of the finest Tudor buildings in the Tower. Even so, it was not only a home. Important prisoners were sometimes held there, under the close watch of the Lieutenant. In later years, famous prisoners such as Guy Fawkes were questioned in the house. The Lieutenant lived there with his family and servants. He was responsible for the daily running of the Tower, including the care of prisoners, the payment of staff and the upkeep of buildings. His house was at the heart of the Tower's life. From its windows, he could watch the comings and goings in the inner ward.
===
IMG: gate-keeper-key-ring-gatehouse | a Tudor gatekeeper in a dark coat holding a large iron ring of keys beside a heavy oak door in a stone gatehouse of the Tower of London, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft lantern light
HEAD: The keys of the Tower were guarded carefully, and the gates were locked every night.
BODY: The security of the Tower of London depended on its gates and their keys. Historians note that each evening, the gates were locked by the warders, and the keys were taken to the Lieutenant or the Constable for safekeeping. In the morning, they were collected again and the gates were unlocked. Even so, this simple routine was taken very seriously. Losing a key or leaving a gate unlocked could allow a prisoner to escape or an enemy to enter. A ceremony for locking the gates has been held at the Tower for centuries. Today, it is called the Ceremony of the Keys. Every night at about ten o'clock, the Chief Yeoman Warder locks the main gates, escorted by soldiers. He is challenged by a sentry, who asks who comes there. He replies that the keys belong to the king or queen. Historians are not certain how old the ceremony is, but it is one of the oldest military traditions in the world.
===
IMG: warders-families-children-cottage-door | a Tudor Yeoman Warder in a red tunic standing at the door of a small stone cottage inside the Tower walls with his wife in a long dress and two children, a cat on the step, warm evening light
HEAD: Many warders lived inside the Tower with their wives and children.
BODY: The Yeoman Warders and other workers of the Tower often lived within its walls with their families. Historians note that they had small houses or rooms along the walls and in the towers. Their wives cooked, cleaned and cared for the children. Some wives also worked, as laundresses, cooks or sellers of food and drink. Children grew up inside the Tower, playing in its courtyards and along its walls. Even so, life in the Tower was not always easy. The houses were often small and crowded, and some were damp and cold. Yet the families formed a close community. They knew each other well and helped each other in times of need. Some families lived in the Tower for generations, with sons following their fathers as warders. Today, the Yeoman Warders and their families still live in the Tower. They are one of the few communities in the world that lives inside a medieval fortress.
===
IMG: chapel-st-peter-ad-vincula-service | a small Tudor parish chapel with a timber roof and plain stone walls, a priest leading a service for warders, soldiers and their families seated on wooden benches, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a timber ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: The people of the Tower worshipped in their own parish church, the chapel of Saint Peter ad Vincula.
BODY: The community of the Tower of London had its own parish church, the chapel of Saint Peter ad Vincula. Historians note that its name means Saint Peter in chains, referring to the story in the Bible of Saint Peter being freed from prison by an angel. A chapel had stood on the site since before the Norman conquest. The present building was rebuilt in 1520, during the reign of Henry the Eighth, after a fire. Even so, the chapel was not only for the living. It was also the burial place for many people who died in the Tower, including those executed for treason. In 1536, Anne Boleyn was buried there, as were her brother George and later Catherine Howard and Lady Jane Grey. For the families of the warders and workers, the chapel was the centre of religious life. They attended services there, baptised their children and buried their dead. It is still used for worship today.

TOPIC: A small town within the walls
===
IMG: tower-lane-between-walls-workers | a narrow cobbled lane between the high stone inner and outer walls of the Tower of London in 1536, workers carrying sacks, a horse and cart and small timber buildings against the walls, soft daylight
HEAD: Between the inner and outer walls of the Tower ran a long narrow street, lined with workshops and houses.
BODY: Between the inner and outer walls of the Tower of London ran a long, narrow passage. Historians note that in Tudor times, it was lined with workshops, storehouses and small houses. Much of the western and northern part of this passage was occupied by the Royal Mint. It was later called Mint Street. Even so, the passage was crowded and busy. Workers hurried along it, carrying materials and tools. Carts brought supplies, and horses waited to be loaded. The noise of hammering from the Mint echoed off the walls. In some places, the passage was so narrow that two carts could barely pass. Smoke from furnaces and fires drifted in the air. Historians note that living and working between the walls was not always pleasant. There was little sunlight, and the air could be smoky and stale. Yet it was a lively place, full of people working to keep the Tower and its many functions running.
===
IMG: tower-kitchen-cooks-bread-ovens | Tudor cooks in aprons baking bread and roasting meat in a large stone kitchen with an open fireplace and brick ovens inside the Tower of London, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: The Tower's kitchens prepared meals for officials, guards, workers and prisoners.
BODY: Feeding the people of the Tower of London was a large task. Historians note that the Tower had several kitchens, where cooks prepared meals for officials, guards, workers and prisoners. They baked bread, brewed ale, roasted meat and made pottages, thick soups of vegetables, grains and sometimes meat. Food was brought into the Tower from markets in the city and from farms outside London. Even so, not everyone ate the same food. The Constable, the Lieutenant and important prisoners ate well, with meat, fish, white bread, wine and spices. Ordinary workers and guards ate simpler meals, with brown bread, cheese, pottage and ale. Prisoners' food depended on their rank and wealth. The crown paid a fixed sum for each prisoner's food each week, and wealthy prisoners could pay for extra. Historians note that Thomas More's family sent him food and supplies during his imprisonment. Meals were an important part of daily life in the Tower.
===
IMG: washerwomen-laundry-courtyard-tower | Tudor washerwomen in long dresses and white caps washing linen in wooden tubs and hanging it on lines in a courtyard inside the Tower of London, stone walls around them, soft sunlight
HEAD: Washerwomen, cooks and servants did the daily work that kept the Tower running.
BODY: Behind the famous events of the Tower's history were many ordinary people who did the daily work. Historians note that washerwomen washed linen and clothes in wooden tubs, using water from wells and the river. Servants cleaned rooms, carried water and firewood and emptied chamber pots. Cooks and kitchen boys prepared meals. Stable hands cared for horses. Even so, their names were rarely recorded. Historians know about them mainly from payments in the royal accounts, which list wages for laundresses, porters and labourers. Many of these workers were women. They worked long hours for low pay, but their work was essential. Without them, the Tower could not have functioned as a palace, a prison or a workplace. Some workers lived inside the Tower, while others came in each day from the streets nearby. Historians today try to recover the lives of these ordinary people, who were as much a part of the Tower's story as its kings and prisoners.
===
IMG: tower-children-playing-courtyard-walls | Tudor children in simple clothes playing with a ball and a small dog on the grass of a courtyard inside the Tower of London, stone walls and towers around them, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Children grew up inside the Tower, playing in its courtyards and along its walls.
BODY: The children of the warders, soldiers and workers grew up inside the Tower of London. Historians note that they played games in the courtyards, ran along the walls and explored the narrow passages. They knew every corner of the fortress. Some helped their parents with work, carrying water, feeding animals or running errands. Even so, the Tower was not always a safe place for children. There were deep wells, high walls and dangerous animals in the menagerie. Parents had to keep a close watch on them. Children in Tudor England usually began working at a young age. Boys might become apprentices to craftsmen at the Mint or in the workshops. Girls might help their mothers or go into service. Some children went to school, where they learned to read and write. Historians note that growing up in the Tower must have been an unusual experience. The children lived alongside famous prisoners, royal guards and lions.
===
IMG: tower-well-water-buckets-servant | a Tudor servant drawing water from a stone well with a wooden bucket and rope in a courtyard of the Tower of London, other buckets nearby, stone walls behind, soft morning light
HEAD: Water for drinking, cooking and washing came from wells inside the Tower and from the river.
BODY: Water was essential to life in the Tower of London. Historians note that there were several wells inside the walls, providing fresh water for drinking, cooking and washing. Servants drew water from the wells with buckets and ropes and carried it to the kitchens, houses and workshops. Water from the river and the moat was used for other purposes, such as washing floors and putting out fires. Even so, the water was not always clean. Wells could become contaminated, and the river was polluted with waste from the city. People in Tudor times often drank ale or beer instead of water, since brewing made it safer. Ale was drunk by adults and children alike, at every meal. The Tower had its own brewhouse and bakehouse. Historians note that providing clean water and food for a large community was a constant challenge. The officials of the Tower had to organise supplies carefully to keep everyone healthy.
===
IMG: tower-market-traders-gate-outside | Tudor traders selling bread, fish, vegetables and ale from stalls and baskets outside the gates of the Tower of London, warders and townspeople buying goods, the river nearby, warm morning light
HEAD: Traders came to the gates of the Tower each day to sell food and goods to its inhabitants.
BODY: The people of the Tower of London depended on traders from the city and the surrounding area. Historians note that each day, sellers came to the gates with bread, fish, meat, vegetables, fruit, eggs and ale. Others brought firewood, candles, cloth and other goods. The area around the Tower, known as the Liberties, was busy with markets, inns and shops. Even so, trading with the Tower had its rules. The officials of the Tower controlled who could enter and sell goods. They also collected fees and taxes. The Tower had special rights over the river nearby. Historians note that boats passing the Tower sometimes had to pay tolls, often in the form of goods such as wine or fish. These customs were part of the Tower's ancient privileges. The traders brought news and gossip from the city as well as goods. For the people inside, they were a link to the bustling world beyond the walls.
===
IMG: tower-bell-tower-ringing-evening | a small bell hanging in a timber belfry on top of a round stone tower of the Tower of London at sunset, a Tudor warder pulling the rope below, the sky glowing orange
HEAD: A bell in the Bell Tower rang each evening to tell prisoners to return to their rooms.
BODY: The Bell Tower stands at the southwest corner of the inner ward of the Tower of London. Historians note that it was built in the late twelfth century and is one of the oldest towers in the fortress. On its roof is a small timber belfry with a bell. For centuries, the bell rang each evening as a curfew, telling prisoners who were allowed to walk outside that it was time to return to their rooms. It also rang to warn of danger. Even so, the Bell Tower was not only a bell tower. It was also used as a prison for important people. In 1534 and 1535, Sir Thomas More, the former Lord Chancellor of England, was imprisoned there. Bishop John Fisher was also held in the Bell Tower. Later, the young Princess Elizabeth, the future Queen Elizabeth the First, was imprisoned there in 1554. The bell still rings at the Tower each evening, as it has for centuries.

TOPIC: The Royal Mint
===
IMG: mint-workers-hammering-coins-anvil | Tudor mint workers in leather aprons striking silver coins by hand with hammers and dies on wooden blocks in a smoky workshop inside the Tower of London, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: England's coins were made by hand at the Royal Mint, inside the walls of the Tower.
BODY: From around 1279, the Royal Mint was located inside the Tower of London. Historians note that it stayed there for more than five hundred years, until 1810. In Tudor times, all of England's coins were made by hand. Workers called moneyers placed a blank piece of silver or gold between two iron dies, engraved with the designs for each side of the coin. Then they struck the top die with a heavy hammer, pressing the design into the metal. Even so, the work required great skill. The coins had to be the right weight and purity, and the designs had to be clear. The Mint was strictly controlled, to prevent cheating and forgery. In 1536, the coins showed a portrait of Henry the Eighth on one side and the royal coat of arms on the other. The sound of hammers striking coins rang out from the Mint all day long, echoing between the walls.
===
IMG: mint-furnace-melting-silver-crucible | a Tudor mint worker pouring molten silver from a glowing crucible into moulds beside a brick furnace in a dark workshop, sparks and smoke rising, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm orange firelight
HEAD: Gold and silver were melted in furnaces and poured into moulds before they were made into coins.
BODY: Before coins could be struck, the metal had to be prepared. Historians note that gold and silver were brought to the Mint as bars, old coins or plate, such as cups and dishes. They were melted in furnaces, purified and mixed with small amounts of other metals to make them harder. The molten metal was poured into moulds to make thin bars. Even so, this work was hot and dangerous. Furnaces burned day and night, filling the workshops with heat, smoke and fumes. Workers wore leather aprons and gloves to protect themselves. The bars were then hammered flat and cut into small round pieces, called blanks. Each blank was weighed and checked. Only then could it be struck into a coin. Historians note that the Mint employed many workers, including melters, refiners, cutters, weighers and moneyers. Each had a special role in the long process of turning precious metal into money.
===
IMG: coins-tudor-silver-groats-table | a small pile of hand-struck Tudor silver coins showing a king's bearded face, scattered on a wooden table beside a leather purse and a small brass balance, soft window light
HEAD: Tudor coins included silver pennies, groats and shillings, and gold sovereigns and crowns.
BODY: The coins of Tudor England came in several values. Historians note that the smallest was the silver penny, followed by the halfgroat, worth two pennies, and the groat, worth four. The testoon, later called the shilling, was worth twelve pennies. Gold coins included the sovereign, the crown and the angel, which showed the archangel Michael defeating a dragon. Even so, most ordinary people rarely saw gold coins. They used silver pennies and groats for daily purchases, such as bread, ale and candles. Some people still used barter, exchanging goods instead of money. In 1526, Henry the Eighth's chief minister, Cardinal Thomas Wolsey, reformed the coinage, changing the value and weight of coins. Later, in the 1540s, Henry would reduce the amount of silver in the coins, to raise money for his wars. This was called the Great Debasement. It caused prices to rise and made the coins less valuable.
===
IMG: mint-official-weighing-coins-balance | a Tudor mint official in a dark gown carefully weighing coins on a brass balance at a desk, a clerk writing in a ledger beside him, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: Officials checked the weight and purity of coins carefully, to make sure that no one cheated.
BODY: The Royal Mint was run by officials who were responsible for the quality of the coins. Historians note that the most important was the Master of the Mint, who managed the production of coins and paid the workers. Other officials, such as the Warden and the Comptroller, checked his work and kept accounts. Coins were tested regularly to make sure that they contained the right amount of gold or silver. Even so, cheating was a constant worry. Some workers might try to steal metal or make coins that were too light. Forgers outside the Mint might try to make fake coins. The punishment for forging the king's coins was very severe. It was considered a form of treason. Each year, a ceremony called the Trial of the Pyx was held to test samples of the coins. A jury of goldsmiths weighed and examined them. This ancient ceremony still takes place today.
===
IMG: mint-street-workers-break-ale | Tudor mint workers in leather aprons taking a break, sitting on a bench outside their workshop in a narrow lane between high stone walls, drinking ale from wooden cups, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Mint workers lived and worked together in the narrow street between the Tower's walls.
BODY: The workers of the Royal Mint formed their own community within the Tower of London. Historians note that many of them lived in houses along the narrow street between the walls, close to their workshops. They worked long hours, from early morning until evening, with breaks for meals. They were paid according to the number of coins they produced. Even so, the work was well paid compared with many other trades. Moneyers were skilled craftsmen and were respected. Some families worked at the Mint for generations, passing their skills from father to son. The workers had their own customs, celebrations and rules. They also had disagreements with the Tower's other officials, especially about space and privileges. Historians note that the Mint and the Tower's garrison did not always get along. The Mint needed room for its workshops, while the soldiers needed room for their defences. The narrow street between the walls was often a crowded and contested place.
===
IMG: mint-machinery-later-milling-museum | an old hand-operated coin press and engraved iron dies displayed on a wooden table in a quiet museum room with stone walls, soft warm light, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: In 1810, the Mint left the Tower, and today it makes coins in Wales.
BODY: The Royal Mint stayed inside the Tower of London for more than five centuries. Historians note that over time, its methods changed. In the seventeenth century, machines powered by horses or water began to replace hand striking. These machines produced coins that were more regular and harder to forge. Even so, the Mint's workshops in the Tower became too small and crowded. In 1810, the Mint moved to a new building on Tower Hill, just outside the Tower. In the 1960s and 1970s, it moved again, to Llantrisant in Wales, where it still makes coins today for Britain and many other countries. The old street between the Tower's walls, where moneyers once hammered coins by hand, is now part of the Tower's visitor route. Historians and visitors can see where the Mint once stood. The Tower's museum displays some of the tools and coins made there, reminders of centuries of work.

TOPIC: The lions of the Tower
===
IMG: lion-tower-menagerie-entrance-1536 | a round stone tower near the entrance of the Tower of London in 1536 with barred cages built into its walls, a lion visible behind the bars, a few Tudor visitors watching, soft daylight
HEAD: For six hundred years, the Tower of London was home to a royal collection of wild animals.
BODY: The Tower of London was home to a royal menagerie, a collection of wild animals, from the thirteenth century until 1835. Historians note that it began in the reign of King John or Henry the Third, when foreign rulers sent animals as gifts to the English king. The animals were kept near the entrance of the Tower, in a building called the Lion Tower, which stood beside the western gate. Even so, the menagerie was not a zoo in the modern sense. It was a royal collection, meant to show the king's power and wealth. Few people were allowed to see the animals at first. Later, the public could visit for a fee. In 1536, the menagerie held lions and other big cats. The animals were kept in cages and dens built into the walls of the Lion Tower. Their roars could be heard throughout the Tower and in the streets nearby.
===
IMG: keeper-feeding-lions-meat-cage | a Tudor animal keeper in a leather jerkin feeding raw meat through iron bars to a lion and a lioness in a stone cage, straw on the floor, soft daylight
HEAD: A keeper was paid to look after the lions and other animals, feeding them meat every day.
BODY: The animals of the royal menagerie were cared for by a keeper. Historians note that this was an official post, paid for by the crown. The keeper fed the animals, cleaned their cages and cared for them when they were ill. Lions and other big cats were fed large amounts of meat each day. Even so, the keeper's knowledge of the animals was limited. In Tudor times, little was known about how to care for wild animals from distant lands. The animals were kept in small, dark cages, very different from their natural homes. Many fell ill and died young. Yet some lived for many years and became famous. The keeper and his family usually lived near the Lion Tower. The job was often passed down through families. Historians note that the keepers sometimes earned extra money by showing the animals to visitors. Being the keeper of the king's lions was an unusual and sometimes dangerous job.
===
IMG: polar-bear-swimming-thames-chain-medieval | a white bear on a long chain swimming and catching fish in the river Thames beside the walls of a medieval castle, a keeper holding the chain from the bank, soft morning light
HEAD: In the thirteenth century, a white bear in the Tower was allowed to swim and fish in the Thames.
BODY: One of the most famous animals in the history of the Tower's menagerie was a white bear, probably a polar bear. Historians note that it was sent to King Henry the Third in 1252 by King Haakon of Norway. The bear was kept at the Tower, and the king ordered that it should be given a long chain, so that it could swim and catch fish in the river Thames. The Sheriffs of London were ordered to pay for its food and keep. Even so, this was long before the Tudor period. The bear lived nearly three hundred years before 1536. Yet stories about it were still told in Tudor London. Historians note that the menagerie also held an elephant, a gift from the king of France in 1255. It was kept in a special house built for it, but it died after only two years. These unusual animals made the Tower famous across Europe.
===
IMG: visitors-watching-lion-cage-tudor | Tudor visitors in hats and cloaks, including children, gazing at a large lion behind iron bars in a stone enclosure, a keeper standing nearby, soft daylight
HEAD: People in Tudor London were fascinated by the lions, which they rarely had a chance to see.
BODY: For most people in Tudor England, lions were creatures of stories, sermons and heraldry, not real animals. Historians note that the lion was a symbol of the English monarchy, appearing on the royal coat of arms. People knew lions from the Bible, from fables and from paintings. Seeing a real lion was a rare and exciting experience. Even so, visiting the menagerie was not easy for ordinary people in 1536. Visits were usually by permission, and important guests were given special tours. In later centuries, the menagerie became a popular attraction. In the eighteenth century, visitors could pay three halfpence to see the animals, or bring a cat or dog to feed to the lions instead. Historians note that people believed that the lions' health was linked to the health of the king. If a lion died, some feared that the king might die too. The lions were treated as royal symbols as well as animals.
===
IMG: leopard-cage-straw-tower-exotic-animals | a spotted leopard resting on straw in a stone and iron cage inside an old castle tower, a shaft of light from a high window, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: Over the centuries, the menagerie held leopards, bears, monkeys, an elephant and even an ostrich.
BODY: The royal menagerie at the Tower of London held many kinds of animals over the centuries. Historians note that, in addition to lions, there were leopards, bears, wolves, monkeys, birds and even an ostrich. Some were gifts from foreign rulers, while others were bought by merchants and sailors from distant lands. Even so, the menagerie was often small. In some periods, it held only a few animals. In others, it was larger and more varied. In Tudor times, it probably held a handful of lions and other big cats, along with some smaller animals. The animals' living conditions were poor by modern standards. They were kept in cramped, dark cages, and many died young. In 1835, the menagerie was closed, and the remaining animals were moved to the new London Zoo in Regent's Park. Today, sculptures of animals made of wire stand around the Tower, reminding visitors of the creatures that once lived there.
===
IMG: lion-skull-museum-display-archaeology | an old lion skull displayed under glass in a museum case with a small brass label, soft warm light in a quiet museum gallery with stone walls, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: In 1937, the skulls of two lions from the Tower's menagerie were found in the old moat.
BODY: In 1937, workers digging in the moat of the Tower of London found the skulls of two lions. Historians and scientists later studied them. Using radiocarbon dating, a method of measuring the age of ancient remains, they found that the skulls dated from between the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries. They were among the oldest remains of lions ever found in Britain. Even so, the skulls revealed more than their age. Scientists found that the lions were related to North African Barbary lions, a type of lion that is now extinct in the wild. Their bones also showed signs of poor health, caused by their life in captivity. Historians note that these discoveries give a rare glimpse into the history of the menagerie. They show that real lions lived at the Tower in the Middle Ages, much as the records describe. The skulls are now kept at the Natural History Museum in London.

TOPIC: Guns, armour and the river
===
IMG: gunners-cannons-tower-wharf-river | Tudor gunners in leather jerkins loading bronze cannons lined up on a stone wharf beside the river Thames outside the Tower of London, barges on the water, soft daylight
HEAD: The guns of the Tower stood along its wharf, facing the river Thames.
BODY: Along the river side of the Tower of London ran a long stone wharf. Historians note that it was used to load and unload goods brought by boat, including weapons, gunpowder and supplies. Cannons stood along the wharf, facing the river. They were used to defend the Tower and to fire salutes on special occasions, such as royal births, coronations and victories. Even so, the cannons were rarely fired in anger. The Tower was never seriously attacked from the river in Tudor times. The gunners who cared for the cannons were skilled soldiers, trained to load, aim and fire. They lived in the Tower and were part of its garrison. Historians note that the tradition of firing salutes from the Tower continues today. On royal birthdays and other special occasions, guns are fired from Tower Wharf. The sound echoes across the river, as it has done for hundreds of years.
===
IMG: armourers-workshop-hammering-steel | Tudor armourers in leather aprons hammering and shaping steel breastplates and helmets on anvils in a workshop full of tools and finished armour, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: Armourers and craftsmen made and repaired weapons and armour for the king's armies.
BODY: The Tower of London was the main store of weapons and armour for the English crown. Historians note that craftsmen worked there to make, repair and maintain equipment. Armourers hammered and shaped steel plates into helmets, breastplates and other pieces of armour. Bowyers made longbows, and fletchers made arrows. Gunsmiths cared for firearms and cannons. Even so, the most famous armour of Henry the Eighth's reign was made at Greenwich, where the king had set up royal workshops with armourers from Germany and the Low Countries. Finished armour was brought to the Tower for storage. The craftsmen at the Tower also prepared weapons for war. In 1536, England was not at war with a foreign enemy, but there was trouble at home. In October, a great rebellion broke out in the north of England, called the Pilgrimage of Grace. Weapons and soldiers were sent from the Tower to help put it down.

HEAD: The river Thames was London's busiest road, crowded with boats, barges and ships.
BODY: In Tudor times, the river Thames was the main road of London. Historians note that thousands of boats travelled on it every day. Watermen rowed small boats called wherries, carrying passengers across and along the river. Barges carried goods such as coal, timber, grain and stone. Large sailing ships brought cargoes from Europe and beyond, anchoring below London Bridge. Even so, travelling on the river was not always safe. The currents were strong, and accidents were common. Passing under London Bridge was especially dangerous, since the water rushed through the narrow arches like a waterfall. Many passengers preferred to get out before the bridge and walk around. The watermen were a large and proud group, with their own company and customs. They called out to passengers along the banks, shouting for customers. Historians note that the river was the lifeblood of London, linking the city to the rest of England and the world.

IMG: traitors-gate-water-arch-boat | a great stone arch over a water gate in the river wall of the Tower of London with a heavy wooden portcullis, a small boat with a prisoner and guards approaching on dark water, soft evening light
HEAD: Prisoners accused of treason were often brought into the Tower by boat, through a water gate.
BODY: On the river side of the Tower of London stands Saint Thomas's Tower, built by Edward the First in the 1270s. Historians note that beneath it is a great water gate, which allowed boats to enter the Tower directly from the Thames. In Tudor times, it was often used to bring prisoners into the Tower, especially those accused of treason, the crime of betraying the king. The gate became known as Traitors' Gate. Even so, not every prisoner entered this way. Some came by road, through the main gates on the western side. Historians are not sure that Anne Boleyn entered by Traitors' Gate, as is often said. She may have landed at another gate near the Byward Tower. Yet the water gate became a powerful symbol of the Tower's role as a prison. Today, the river has been walled off, and the gate no longer opens onto the water. Visitors can see its great arch and heavy timbers.

IMG: london-bridge-houses-heads-spikes | the old London Bridge in 1536 crossing the river Thames on many stone arches, crowded with tall timber houses and shops, a gatehouse at its southern end, boats passing beneath, soft afternoon light
HEAD: London Bridge, crowded with houses and shops, was the only bridge across the Thames in London.
BODY: In 1536, London Bridge was the only bridge across the river Thames in London. Historians note that it had been built of stone in the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries and had nineteen arches. It was lined with houses and shops, some of them several storeys high. A chapel stood in the middle of the bridge, and gatehouses stood at each end. Even so, the bridge also had a grim purpose. On the gatehouse at its southern end, the heads of people executed for treason were displayed on spikes, as a warning to others. In July 1535, the heads of Sir Thomas More and Bishop John Fisher were placed there after their executions on Tower Hill. More's daughter, Margaret Roper, later recovered her father's head. The bridge was a busy and lively place, crowded with people, carts and animals. It remained the only river crossing in London until the eighteenth century.
