TOPIC: Life inside the Tower of London
===
IMG: tower-of-london-thames-1536-evening | the Tower of London in 1536 seen from the river Thames, the great pale stone White Tower rising above high curtain walls and round towers, wooden boats and barges on the water, soft golden evening light
HEAD: In 1536, the Tower of London was a royal palace, a fortress, a prison, a mint and a home to hundreds of people.
BODY: The Tower of London stands on the north bank of the river Thames, at the eastern edge of the old City of London. In 1536, during the reign of King Henry the Eighth, it was already more than four hundred years old. Historians note that it was many things at once. It was a royal palace, where kings and queens stayed before their coronations. It was a fortress, guarding the river and the city. It was a prison for people accused of crimes against the king. It was also the home of the Royal Mint, where England's coins were made, a store for weapons and armour, and a zoo for wild animals. Even so, it was above all a place where people lived and worked. Soldiers, warders, servants, craftsmen and their families made their homes inside its walls. The Tower was like a small town, with its own streets, chapels and gardens, surrounded by stone.
===
IMG: tower-walls-moat-aerial-tudor | the Tower of London around 1536 with its two rings of stone walls, many round and square towers, a wide water-filled moat and the great White Tower in the centre, the river Thames beside it, soft morning light
HEAD: The Tower was surrounded by two rings of walls and a wide moat filled with water from the Thames.
BODY: The Tower of London is a concentric castle, which means that it has two rings of defensive walls, one inside the other. Historians note that the inner wall was higher than the outer wall, so that defenders on both could fight at the same time. Along the walls stood more than twenty towers, each with its own name. Outside the outer wall was a wide moat, filled with water from the Thames. Even so, the moat was not always pleasant. It was often dirty and smelly, filled with waste from the Tower and the surrounding streets. It was finally drained in the 1840s and turned into a grassy ditch. In 1536, visitors entered the Tower through a series of gates and bridges on its western side. Boats could also enter from the river through a water gate. The walls and towers had been built over centuries, by many kings, each adding new defences and buildings to the fortress.
===
IMG: tudor-warders-walking-inner-ward-morning | Tudor guards in red and gold tunics walking across the grassy inner courtyard of the Tower of London in the early morning, stone buildings and the White Tower behind, soft morning light
HEAD: Each morning, the gates of the Tower were unlocked, and its small community began another day.
BODY: Life in the Tower began early each morning. Historians note that the gates were unlocked at dawn by the warders, who had kept them closed through the night. Soldiers changed guard, servants lit fires and began cooking, and workers arrived at the Mint and the workshops. Animals in the royal menagerie were fed, and horses were watered. Even so, the day did not begin the same way for everyone. Prisoners woke in their rooms, wondering what the day would bring. Some were allowed visitors or letters. Others waited for news of their trials. The chapel bells rang for morning prayers. Carts arrived at the gates with food, firewood and supplies. Boats came up the river to unload goods at the wharf. By mid morning, the Tower was busy and noisy. People hurried along its narrow streets between the walls, going about their daily work, much like the inhabitants of any small town.
===
IMG: henry-viii-portrait-1536-palace | King Henry VIII of England in 1536, a broad, bearded man in a jewelled doublet with puffed sleeves, a feathered cap and a heavy gold chain, standing in a richly decorated palace room, warm light
HEAD: In 1536, England was ruled by King Henry the Eighth, who was then forty-four years old.
BODY: Henry the Eighth became king of England in 1509, at the age of seventeen. Historians note that he was tall, strong and athletic in his youth, and he loved hunting, jousting, music and dancing. He was well educated and could speak several languages. By 1536, he was forty-four years old and had ruled for twenty-seven years. Even so, his reign had become troubled. He had broken with the Pope in Rome in order to end his first marriage, to Catherine of Aragon, and marry Anne Boleyn. He had made himself head of the Church in England. He had begun to close the monasteries and take their wealth. Historians note that 1536 was a turning point in his life. In January, he was badly injured in a jousting accident. In the same month, Catherine of Aragon died. In May, Anne Boleyn was executed at the Tower. Henry's actions that year left a deep mark on the Tower's history.
===
IMG: tower-stone-buildings-courtyard-chickens | the cobbled inner courtyard of the Tower of London in 1536 with timber-framed and stone houses, a well, chickens and a cart, servants carrying baskets, the White Tower rising behind, warm daylight
HEAD: Between the walls of the Tower, there were houses, workshops, gardens and even a parish church.
BODY: Inside its walls, the Tower of London contained much more than towers. Historians note that there were houses for the officials and workers, workshops, storehouses, stables, kitchens and gardens. There was a parish church, the chapel of Saint Peter ad Vincula, and a Norman chapel inside the White Tower. There were wells for water and ovens for baking bread. Even so, space was limited. Buildings were crowded together along narrow lanes, and every corner was used. Some people kept chickens and pigs. Others grew herbs and vegetables in small gardens. The Tower had its own rules and officials, and it was separate from the City of London around it. The area around the Tower, called the Liberties, was under the control of the Tower's officials. Historians note that the people who lived in the Tower formed a close community. They knew each other well, worked together, married each other and raised their children within the walls.
===
IMG: tower-evening-lamps-gates-closing | Tudor guards with lanterns closing the heavy wooden gates of a stone gatehouse at the Tower of London in the evening, the sky deep blue and the first stars appearing
HEAD: At night, the gates were locked, and the Tower became a quiet world of its own.
BODY: When evening came, the gates of the Tower of London were locked. Historians note that the keys were carried by the warders, who walked from gate to gate, making sure that everything was secure. Only those with special permission could enter or leave after dark. Even so, the Tower was not completely silent at night. Guards walked the walls with lanterns, watching for danger. Prisoners lay awake in their rooms, listening to the sounds of the river and the bells of the city churches. In the menagerie, the lions sometimes roared, and the sound echoed through the stone courtyards. In the houses, families gathered around their fires, ate supper and prepared for bed. Candles burned in a few windows. The river flowed quietly past, carrying a few late boats. The great White Tower stood dark against the sky. Locked inside its walls, the small community of the Tower settled down for the night.
===
IMG: tudor-map-london-tower-river-scroll | an old hand-drawn map of London around 1540 spread on a wooden table, showing the river Thames, London Bridge and the Tower, beside a candle and a quill, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: Historians know about life in the Tower from royal records, letters, accounts and later surveys.
BODY: Historians have learned about life in the Tower of London in Tudor times from many sources. Royal accounts record payments to workers, purchases of supplies and repairs to buildings. Letters written by the Tower's officials to the king's ministers describe events and prisoners. Some prisoners also wrote letters and poems, and some carved their names and messages into the walls of their rooms. Even so, there are gaps in the record. Ordinary people, such as servants and labourers, left few written traces. Historians must piece together their lives from small details in the records. Later surveys of the Tower, including detailed plans made in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, show the layout of its buildings. Archaeologists have also excavated parts of the Tower, finding objects such as pottery, coins, tools and animal bones. Together, these sources give a rich picture of the Tower as a living place, full of people, work and stories.

TOPIC: The White Tower
===
IMG: white-tower-norman-keep-pale-stone | the great square Norman keep of the White Tower with four corner turrets, built of pale stone, rising above the courtyard of the Tower of London in 1536, people and a cart below, bright morning light
HEAD: The White Tower was built by William the Conqueror after the Norman conquest of England in 1066.
BODY: The oldest and most famous part of the Tower of London is the White Tower. Historians note that it was begun by William the Conqueror, the Duke of Normandy, who became king of England after the Battle of Hastings in 1066. He built it to control London and to show the power of the new Norman rulers. Work began around 1078 and was completed about twenty years later. Even so, it was not called the White Tower at first. It got its name in 1240, when King Henry the Third had it whitewashed. The building is about twenty-seven metres tall, with walls up to four and a half metres thick. Its four corner turrets give it a distinctive shape. In 1536, it was more than four hundred and fifty years old. It was used as a storehouse for weapons and armour, a place for royal records and a symbol of royal power, rising high above the river and the city.
===
IMG: caen-stone-blocks-masons-norman | Norman stonemasons around 1080 cutting and lifting pale cream stone blocks with wooden cranes beside the rising walls of a great square keep, a river and boats behind, soft daylight
HEAD: Some of the stone for the White Tower was brought by ship from Caen, in Normandy.
BODY: The White Tower was built mainly of Kentish ragstone, a rough grey stone from the county of Kent. Historians note that the corners, windows and doorways were made of a finer, pale stone from Caen, in Normandy, which William the Conqueror had brought across the English Channel by ship. Caen stone was easy to carve and had a beautiful cream colour. Even so, building such a large tower was a huge task. Hundreds of workers were needed, including masons, carpenters, labourers and carters. They worked with simple tools, such as chisels, hammers, ropes and wooden cranes. The work took many years. The building was designed by a monk named Gundulf, who later became Bishop of Rochester. He was an experienced builder who also designed Rochester Castle and cathedral. The White Tower was one of the largest buildings in England at the time, and it impressed and intimidated the people of London.
===
IMG: chapel-st-john-norman-arches-candles | a simple Norman chapel inside a stone keep with thick round columns, rounded arches and a barrel-vaulted ceiling, candles burning on a small altar, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a vaulted ceiling, soft candlelight
HEAD: Inside the White Tower is the Chapel of Saint John, one of the oldest churches in London.
BODY: On the upper floors of the White Tower lies the Chapel of Saint John the Evangelist. Historians note that it was built at the same time as the tower, around 1080, and it is one of the best surviving examples of Norman church architecture in England. It has thick round columns, rounded arches and a stone vaulted ceiling. Its walls are plain and pale, giving it a calm and simple beauty. Even so, in Tudor times the chapel was richly decorated, with painted walls, stained glass and fine cloths on the altar. Kings and queens who stayed at the Tower prayed there. Before their coronations, some kings held special ceremonies in the chapel. In 1536, the chapel was still used for worship. Historians note that it later lost its decorations and was used to store records for many years. Today, it has been restored to its simple Norman form. Visitors can still stand inside and see it much as it was.
===
IMG: armour-weapons-store-white-tower-interior | rows of steel armour, helmets, pikes and swords stored on wooden racks inside a great stone hall with thick walls and small windows, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft daylight
HEAD: In Tudor times, the White Tower was used to store weapons, armour and gunpowder.
BODY: By the sixteenth century, the White Tower was no longer used as a royal home. Historians note that it had become a great storehouse. Its large rooms were filled with weapons, armour, bows, arrows, pikes and other military equipment. Gunpowder was also stored there, kept in barrels in the basement. The Tower was the main arsenal of the kingdom, supplying the king's armies and navy. Even so, storing gunpowder in the Tower was dangerous. A fire or a spark could cause a terrible explosion. The officials of the Tower were careful to protect the gunpowder from flames. Henry the Eighth was very interested in weapons and armour. He built up a great collection, some of which is still displayed at the Tower today. His own suits of armour show how his body changed over the years, from a slim young athlete to a much larger older man. The armour was made by skilled armourers in royal workshops.
===
IMG: records-chests-scrolls-stone-room | wooden chests and shelves filled with rolled parchment scrolls and old leather-bound books in a stone room inside a castle tower, a clerk reading by a small window, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft daylight
HEAD: Important royal records were also kept in the Tower, stored in chests and on shelves.
BODY: For centuries, the Tower of London was used to store the records of the English government. Historians note that these included laws, charters, treaties, court records and accounts. They were written on parchment, a material made from animal skin, and rolled up into scrolls or bound into books. They were kept in chests and on shelves in several of the Tower's buildings, especially the Wakefield Tower and the White Tower. Even so, the records were not always well cared for. Some were damaged by damp, mice and insects. Others were lost or misplaced. Clerks worked to sort and copy them. In the sixteenth century, scholars and lawyers came to the Tower to search the records for information. Historians today owe a great debt to these old archives. Many were later moved to the Public Record Office, now called the National Archives. They provide a detailed picture of English history over many centuries.
===
IMG: white-tower-roof-view-thames-ships | a view from the top of the White Tower in 1536 looking out over the walls of the Tower of London to the river Thames with sailing ships, barges and London Bridge in the distance, soft afternoon light
HEAD: From the top of the White Tower, the whole of Tudor London could be seen spread out along the river.
BODY: The White Tower was one of the tallest buildings in London for centuries. Historians note that from its roof, there was a wide view in every direction. To the west lay the City of London, with its crowded houses, church towers and the great spire of Saint Paul's Cathedral. London Bridge crossed the Thames, lined with houses and shops. To the south, across the river, lay the town of Southwark, with its inns, theatres and gardens. To the east, the river flowed towards the sea, past docks, marshes and villages. Even so, the view was not only beautiful. It was also useful for defence. Watchmen on the roof could see approaching enemies, ships on the river and fires in the city. In 1536, the Thames was busy with ships and boats carrying goods and people. The roof of the White Tower offered a view of a growing, lively city, the heart of Tudor England.

TOPIC: A royal palace
===
IMG: royal-palace-lodgings-tower-tudor | the royal palace buildings inside the Tower of London in 1533 with half-timbered upper floors and tall stone walls, a garden with clipped hedges and gravel paths, courtiers walking, warm afternoon light
HEAD: The Tower contained a royal palace, where kings and queens stayed on special occasions.
BODY: For centuries, the Tower of London was also a royal palace. Historians note that the medieval kings built fine lodgings within its walls, near the river, with great halls, private chambers and gardens. Henry the Third and Edward the First added luxurious rooms, decorated with paintings and stained glass. Even so, by the Tudor period, the kings and queens rarely lived at the Tower. They preferred palaces such as Whitehall, Greenwich and Hampton Court, which were more comfortable and modern. The Tower's palace was used mainly for special occasions. Before their coronation, English monarchs traditionally stayed at the Tower and then rode in procession through the City of London to Westminster Abbey. Henry the Eighth did this before his coronation in 1509. In 1533, the palace was repaired and redecorated for the coronation of Anne Boleyn. Three years later, she would return to the same rooms as a prisoner.
===
IMG: coronation-procession-anne-boleyn-1533 | a grand Tudor coronation procession leaving the Tower of London in 1533, a queen in white and gold seated in a litter carried by horses, nobles on horseback in rich clothes, crowds lining the street, bright summer light
HEAD: In 1533, Anne Boleyn left the Tower in a grand procession to be crowned queen.
BODY: On the twenty-ninth of May 1533, Anne Boleyn arrived at the Tower of London by river, in a magnificent procession of decorated barges. Historians note that she was greeted by Henry the Eighth and stayed in the royal lodgings for two nights. On the thirty-first of May, she left the Tower in a grand procession through the City of London to Westminster. She was carried in a litter covered with white and gold cloth, wearing white and with her long dark hair loose. Nobles, officials and ladies rode in front of and behind her. Even so, the crowds were not all enthusiastic. Many people in London still supported Catherine of Aragon, Henry's first wife. Anne was crowned queen in Westminster Abbey on the first of June 1533. She was pregnant, and in September she gave birth to a daughter, the future Queen Elizabeth the First. The coronation was one of the most splendid events of Henry's reign.
===
IMG: great-hall-tower-tudor-tapestries | a great medieval hall with a high timber roof, tapestries on the stone walls and long tables, Tudor courtiers and officials standing in groups, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a high timber ceiling, soft light from tall windows
HEAD: The palace at the Tower had a great hall, used for feasts, ceremonies and trials.
BODY: The royal palace at the Tower of London included a great hall, a large room used for feasts, ceremonies and important meetings. Historians note that it had a high timber roof and tall windows, and its walls were hung with tapestries. In medieval times, kings held feasts there for their nobles and guests. Even so, the great hall was also used for more serious purposes. In May 1536, the trials of Anne Boleyn and her brother, George Boleyn, were held there. A large wooden stand was built so that about two thousand people could watch. The trials were presided over by Anne's uncle, the Duke of Norfolk. The judges found her guilty, and she was sentenced to death. The great hall no longer exists. It fell into ruin and was demolished in the late eighteenth century. Today, only the foundations of some parts of the palace remain, hidden beneath later buildings and lawns.
===
IMG: tudor-garden-tower-roses-herbs | a small walled Tudor garden inside the Tower of London with raised beds of roses, herbs and flowers, gravel paths and a wooden bench, stone walls and towers behind, soft summer light
HEAD: The royal lodgings had small gardens where flowers and herbs were grown.
BODY: Like most Tudor palaces, the royal lodgings at the Tower had gardens. Historians note that they were small, walled gardens, with raised beds of flowers and herbs, gravel paths and benches. Roses, lavender, rosemary, violets and other plants were grown there. Herbs were used in cooking, medicine and to sweeten the air in the palace rooms. Even so, the gardens of the Tower were not as grand as those of palaces like Hampton Court. Space inside the walls was limited, and the Tower was crowded with other buildings. Yet the gardens offered a quiet place to walk and rest. For important prisoners, being allowed to walk in a garden was a great privilege. Some prisoners in the Tower were given permission to walk in the gardens or along the walls for exercise. Historians note that gardens were important in Tudor life. They were places of beauty, rest and reflection, even within the walls of a fortress.
===
IMG: queens-chamber-bed-curtains-window-river | a richly furnished Tudor bedchamber with a large curtained bed, tapestries, a carved chest and a window overlooking a river, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft daylight
HEAD: The queen's lodgings at the Tower had been newly decorated in 1533, with fine furniture and wall hangings.
BODY: For Anne Boleyn's coronation in 1533, Henry the Eighth ordered the repair and redecoration of the royal lodgings at the Tower. Historians know from the royal accounts that workmen repaired roofs, windows and floors, and that the rooms were painted and hung with tapestries. New furniture was brought in, including beds, chairs and chests. Even so, the work was done quickly, because the coronation was planned in a short time. Hundreds of workmen worked day and night. The queen's lodgings were a series of rooms, including a great chamber, a dining chamber, a bedchamber and private rooms. They looked out over the river and the gardens. Historians note that in May 1536, when Anne Boleyn was arrested, she was lodged in these same rooms. According to the records, she was treated with some respect, and she had several ladies to attend her. Yet she was now a prisoner in the palace where she had prepared to become queen.
===
IMG: royal-barge-thames-tudor-oarsmen | a richly decorated Tudor royal barge with a canopy and banners being rowed by oarsmen in livery along the river Thames, other boats and the walls of the Tower of London beside it, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Kings and queens usually arrived at the Tower by royal barge along the river Thames.
BODY: In Tudor times, the river Thames was the main road of London. Historians note that it was easier and safer to travel by water than through the crowded and muddy streets. Kings and queens had royal barges, large rowing boats decorated with carvings, paint, banners and canopies. They were rowed by teams of oarsmen in royal livery. Even so, the river could also be dangerous. Its currents were strong, especially near London Bridge, where the water rushed through the narrow arches. Royal barges usually landed at the Tower on its river side. There, a special water gate allowed boats to enter the Tower directly from the Thames. This gate was later called Traitors' Gate, because many prisoners accused of treason entered the Tower through it. In 1536, Anne Boleyn was brought to the Tower by barge, arriving on the afternoon of the second of May. Tradition says that she entered by the water gate, though she probably landed at a gate near the Byward Tower.
