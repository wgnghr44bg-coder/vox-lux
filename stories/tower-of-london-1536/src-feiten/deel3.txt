TOPIC: Prisoners of the Tower
===
IMG: prisoner-room-tower-furniture-books | a furnished Tudor prison room in a stone tower with a bed, a table with books and papers, a wooden chair and a small fire, a prisoner in a dark gown reading by a narrow window, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft daylight
HEAD: Most prisoners in the Tower were nobles, officials or churchmen, and many were held in furnished rooms.
BODY: The Tower of London was not an ordinary prison. Historians note that it was used mainly for important prisoners, such as nobles, officials, churchmen and people accused of crimes against the king. Ordinary criminals were usually sent to other prisons in London, such as Newgate. Prisoners in the Tower were often held in rooms in the towers, rather than in dungeons. Some rooms were quite comfortable, with beds, tables, chairs and fireplaces. Wealthy prisoners could bring their own furniture, clothes and books, and pay for better food. Even so, imprisonment in the Tower was frightening. Prisoners did not know how long they would be held or what would happen to them. Many were held for months or years, waiting for trial. Some were released, while others were executed. Historians note that the uncertainty was often the hardest part. The Tower's walls kept the prisoners safely inside, but they could not keep out fear.
===
IMG: carved-names-stone-wall-prison-graffiti | close view of names, dates, coats of arms and small symbols carefully carved into the pale stone wall of an old prison room, soft light falling across the carvings
HEAD: Many prisoners carved their names, prayers and symbols into the stone walls of their rooms.
BODY: To pass the long hours of imprisonment, many prisoners in the Tower of London carved inscriptions into the walls of their rooms. Historians note that the walls of the Beauchamp Tower are especially rich in these carvings. They include names, dates, prayers, coats of arms, religious symbols and short poems. Some are simple scratches, while others are carefully designed and beautifully carved. Even so, most of the carvings in the Beauchamp Tower date from after 1536, from the reigns of Edward the Sixth, Mary the First and Elizabeth the First. Some were made by members of the Dudley family, who were imprisoned there in the 1550s. Historians value these carvings as a direct link to the prisoners. They show their faith, their fears and their hopes. Some carvings ask God for help, while others express sorrow or courage. Visitors to the Tower today can still see them, preserved behind glass, centuries after they were made.
===
IMG: thomas-more-prison-writing-desk | a grey-haired Tudor scholar in a plain dark gown writing at a small table by a narrow barred window in a stone prison room, papers and a prayer book beside him, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft daylight
HEAD: Sir Thomas More, the former Lord Chancellor, spent over a year as a prisoner in the Tower.
BODY: Sir Thomas More was a lawyer, scholar and writer, famous across Europe for his book Utopia, published in 1516. Historians note that he served Henry the Eighth as Lord Chancellor, the highest official in the kingdom, from 1529 to 1532. However, he disagreed with Henry's break with the Pope and his marriage to Anne Boleyn. In April 1534, he refused to swear an oath accepting the new order of succession and Henry's position as head of the Church. He was imprisoned in the Bell Tower. Even so, he spent his time in prison writing and praying. He wrote A Dialogue of Comfort against Tribulation, a book about finding strength in suffering. His daughter Margaret visited him and brought him news. In July 1535, he was tried, found guilty of treason and executed on Tower Hill. According to tradition, he said that he died the king's good servant, but God's first. He was later made a saint.
===
IMG: bishop-fisher-elderly-prisoner-cold-room | a frail elderly bishop with white hair wrapped in a worn cloak sitting on a wooden stool in a cold, bare stone prison room, a small window letting in pale light, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: Bishop John Fisher, an elderly churchman, was also imprisoned in the Tower and executed in 1535.
BODY: John Fisher was the Bishop of Rochester and a respected scholar. Historians note that he had been a supporter and adviser of Henry the Eighth's grandmother, Lady Margaret Beaufort, and had helped found colleges at Cambridge. Like Thomas More, he opposed Henry's divorce from Catherine of Aragon and his break with Rome. In 1534, he refused to take the oath of succession and was sent to the Tower. Even so, his imprisonment was harsh. He was elderly and in poor health, and he suffered from the cold. In a letter, he asked for warmer clothing and better food. In May 1535, the Pope made him a cardinal, hoping to protect him. Henry was furious. He is said to have declared that he would send Fisher's head to Rome for the cardinal's hat. Fisher was executed on Tower Hill on the twenty-second of June 1535. He, too, was later made a saint.
===
IMG: prisoner-walking-on-walls-with-warder | a Tudor prisoner in a black gown walking slowly along the top of the stone walls of the Tower of London beside a Yeoman Warder in red, the river and boats below, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Some prisoners were allowed to walk on the walls or in the gardens for exercise.
BODY: Prisoners in the Tower of London were not always kept locked in their rooms. Historians note that some were allowed to walk on the walls, in the gardens or in the courtyards for exercise and fresh air. They were always accompanied by a warder. This privilege depended on the prisoner's rank, behaviour and the seriousness of the charges. Even so, the walks were carefully controlled. Prisoners were not allowed to speak to other prisoners or to people outside. The walk along the walls between the Beauchamp Tower and the Bell Tower became known as the Prisoners' Walk. From the walls, prisoners could see the river, the boats and the city beyond. For people who spent most of their time indoors, these walks were precious moments. Historians note that some prisoners later wrote about the beauty of the river and the sky, seen from the walls of their prison. These views reminded them of the freedom they had lost.
===
IMG: family-visiting-prisoner-room-tower | a Tudor woman in a dark gown and white hood visiting a prisoner in a stone room of the Tower, the two sitting together and talking quietly, a warder standing by the door, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: Family members were sometimes allowed to visit prisoners and bring them food, clothes and news.
BODY: Prisoners in the Tower of London were sometimes allowed visits from their families. Historians note that these visits required permission from the king's council or the Constable. Visitors were searched and watched by the warders, and their conversations were often listened to. Even so, the visits were important to the prisoners. Family members brought food, clothes, books, money and news from the outside world. They also brought comfort and love. Thomas More's daughter, Margaret Roper, visited him several times and tried to persuade him to take the oath, but he refused. His wife, Alice, also visited him. Their conversations were later written down by More's family and friends. Historians note that letters were another way for prisoners to stay in touch with their families. Letters were read by the officials before being sent. Some prisoners wrote secret messages, using tricks such as writing in lemon juice, which became visible only when heated.
===
IMG: candle-prison-window-night-quiet | a single candle burning on the stone windowsill of a small prison room in a castle tower at night, the dark river and a few distant lights beyond the narrow window, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: For many prisoners, the quiet nights in the Tower were the longest and loneliest hours.
BODY: At night, the Tower of London grew quiet. Historians imagine the prisoners in their rooms, lit by a single candle, listening to the sounds of the night. The bells of city churches rang the hours. The river lapped against the wharf. The footsteps of the guards echoed on the walls. Even so, many prisoners found the nights especially hard. Alone with their thoughts, they worried about their families, their futures and their fates. Some prayed, some read, and some wrote letters or poems. Many prisoners turned to their faith for comfort. Thomas More wrote that prison could be a place of spiritual growth, where a person could think about God and eternity without the distractions of the world. Historians note that the Tower was a place of great suffering, but also of great courage. The prisoners who passed through it faced their fears in different ways. Their stories are an important part of the Tower's history.

TOPIC: Spring 1536
===
IMG: anne-boleyn-portrait-tudor-gown-1536 | a Tudor queen with dark hair under a French hood edged with pearls, wearing a dark velvet gown and a pearl necklace with a small golden pendant, standing in a palace room, soft window light
HEAD: In May 1536, Queen Anne Boleyn was arrested and brought to the Tower of London.
BODY: Anne Boleyn had been queen of England for only three years when she fell from power. Historians note that she was intelligent, well educated and strong-willed. She had grown up partly at the courts of the Netherlands and France, and she was admired for her elegance and wit. Henry the Eighth had broken with Rome in order to marry her. Even so, the marriage did not bring Henry the son he wanted. Anne gave birth to a daughter, Elizabeth, in 1533, but she later lost several babies. In January 1536, she had a miscarriage. By then, Henry had become interested in one of her ladies-in-waiting, Jane Seymour. Anne also had powerful enemies at court, including, by the spring of 1536, the king's chief minister, Thomas Cromwell. On the second of May 1536, she was arrested at Greenwich Palace and accused of adultery and treason. That afternoon, she was taken by barge to the Tower.
===
IMG: barge-arriving-tower-afternoon-guards | a Tudor barge with guards and a woman in a dark gown arriving at the river gate of the Tower of London in the afternoon, officials in dark gowns waiting on the steps, soft grey light
HEAD: When she arrived at the Tower, Anne asked whether she would be put in a dungeon.
BODY: Anne Boleyn arrived at the Tower of London on the afternoon of the second of May 1536. Historians know about her arrival from the letters of Sir William Kingston, the Constable of the Tower, who received her. According to Kingston, she asked him whether she would go into a dungeon. He replied that she would stay in the lodgings she had used before her coronation. She is said to have answered that it was too good for her, and then knelt down and wept, and then laughed. Even so, historians must be careful with these details. Kingston was reporting to Thomas Cromwell, and his letters were written to serve the king's purposes. Yet they give a vivid and moving picture of the queen's distress. Over the following days, Kingston and his wife, along with several ladies chosen by the king, attended Anne. They reported her words and actions back to Cromwell, and their reports were used against her.
===
IMG: five-men-accused-court-tudor | five Tudor gentlemen in rich doublets and dark cloaks standing together in a stone hall, guarded by warders with halberds, their faces serious, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: Five men, including the queen's own brother, were also arrested and sent to the Tower.
BODY: Along with Anne Boleyn, five men were arrested and imprisoned in the Tower of London in May 1536. Historians note that they were accused of adultery with the queen and of plotting against the king. They were Anne's brother, George Boleyn, Lord Rochford, and four courtiers: Henry Norris, Francis Weston, William Brereton and a young musician named Mark Smeaton. Smeaton, the only one who was not a gentleman, confessed, probably after being threatened or tortured. The others denied the charges. Even so, all five were found guilty. Historians today believe that the charges were almost certainly false. Many think that Cromwell built the case against them to remove Anne and her supporters. The poet Thomas Wyatt and another courtier, Richard Page, were also arrested but later released. On the seventeenth of May 1536, the five men were executed on Tower Hill. Their deaths shocked the court and the people of London.
===
IMG: trial-great-hall-crowd-wooden-stands | a crowded great hall with a high timber roof and wooden stands filled with Tudor nobles and spectators, a woman in a dark gown standing before a raised bench of judges, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a high timber ceiling, soft light
HEAD: Anne Boleyn was tried in the great hall of the Tower, watched by about two thousand people.
BODY: On the fifteenth of May 1536, Anne Boleyn was tried in the great hall of the Tower of London. Historians note that a large wooden stand had been built for spectators, and about two thousand people came to watch. The trial was presided over by Anne's uncle, Thomas Howard, Duke of Norfolk, as Lord High Steward. A jury of peers, nobles of the realm, heard the case. Even so, the outcome was never really in doubt. Anne defended herself calmly and clearly, denying all the charges. Witnesses praised her composure and dignity. Yet the peers found her guilty. Norfolk pronounced the sentence: she was to be burned or beheaded, at the king's pleasure. Historians note that Henry chose beheading. Anne's brother George was tried immediately afterwards and also found guilty. Some spectators believed that the evidence was weak. One of the peers, Henry Percy, who had once hoped to marry Anne, collapsed and had to be helped from the hall.
===
IMG: swordsman-from-calais-arrives-ship | a tall man in a plain dark cloak carrying a long wrapped sword stepping off a small sailing ship onto a wooden quay, gulls overhead, grey morning light
HEAD: Henry ordered a skilled swordsman to be brought from Calais to carry out the execution.
BODY: In England, people of high rank who were executed for treason were usually beheaded with an axe. Historians note that Henry the Eighth decided that Anne Boleyn should be beheaded with a sword instead. He sent for an expert swordsman from Calais, a town on the French coast that belonged to England at that time. Beheading with a sword was common in France, and it was thought to be quicker and more merciful than an axe. Even so, the reason for Henry's choice is not certain. Some historians think he wished to spare Anne unnecessary suffering. Others believe the arrangement had been made even before her trial. The swordsman's name is not known. Anne's execution was delayed by a day, partly because the swordsman had not yet arrived. According to Kingston, Anne said she had heard that the executioner was very good, and that she had a little neck. She then put her hands around it and laughed.
===
IMG: early-morning-tower-green-scaffold-crowd | a small low wooden scaffold draped in black cloth on the grass inside the walls of the Tower of London early on a May morning, a small crowd of Tudor officials and nobles gathered around it, soft grey dawn light
HEAD: On the morning of the nineteenth of May 1536, Anne Boleyn was executed inside the walls of the Tower.
BODY: Anne Boleyn was executed on the morning of Friday, the nineteenth of May 1536. Historians note that the execution took place inside the walls of the Tower of London, rather than on Tower Hill, where most executions were held in public. Only a small crowd of officials, nobles and citizens was allowed to watch. Anne walked to the scaffold wearing a dark grey gown and a cape trimmed with fur, accompanied by her ladies. Even so, she remained calm. She gave a short speech, in which she did not admit guilt but praised the king and asked the people to pray for him. Then she knelt, and the swordsman ended her life with a single stroke. Her ladies wrapped her body and head in a white cloth. Historians note that no proper coffin had been prepared. She was placed in an old chest for arrows and buried in the chapel of Saint Peter ad Vincula, close to her brother George.
===
IMG: jane-seymour-portrait-tudor-1536 | a pale, calm young Tudor woman in a rich gown with a gable hood and pearls, standing in a palace room with tapestries, soft window light
HEAD: Eleven days after Anne's death, Henry the Eighth married Jane Seymour.
BODY: On the twentieth of May 1536, the day after Anne Boleyn's execution, Henry the Eighth was formally betrothed to Jane Seymour. Historians note that they were married on the thirtieth of May at Whitehall Palace. Jane was the daughter of a Wiltshire gentleman and had served as a lady-in-waiting to both Catherine of Aragon and Anne Boleyn. She was described as quiet, gentle and modest, very different from Anne. Even so, her marriage to Henry was short. In October 1537, she gave birth to the son Henry had longed for, the future King Edward the Sixth. But she fell ill after the birth and died twelve days later. Henry was deeply grieved. Historians note that Jane was the only one of his wives to receive a queen's funeral, and Henry later asked to be buried beside her. The spring of 1536 had changed the life of the court completely, and the Tower had been at its centre.

TOPIC: Tower Green and Tower Hill
===
IMG: tower-green-grass-chapel-quiet | a quiet green lawn inside the Tower of London with the small Tudor chapel of Saint Peter ad Vincula behind it, old stone buildings around and tall trees, soft morning light
HEAD: Only a few people were executed privately inside the Tower, on or near the area called Tower Green.
BODY: Tower Green is an open area of grass inside the inner ward of the Tower of London, near the chapel of Saint Peter ad Vincula. Historians note that it was used for private executions, held away from the eyes of the public. Only a small number of people were executed there, usually people of high rank whose public execution might cause sympathy or unrest. They included Anne Boleyn in 1536, Margaret Pole, Countess of Salisbury, in 1541, Catherine Howard in 1542, and Lady Jane Grey in 1554. Even so, historians are not certain exactly where on or near the green these executions took place. Some evidence suggests that Anne Boleyn's scaffold stood near the White Tower, on the parade ground. Today, a memorial stands on Tower Green, a glass cushion created by the artist Brian Catling in 2006, remembering those who died there. It is a quiet and reflective place, visited by many people each year.
===
IMG: tower-hill-scaffold-crowd-public | a crowd of Tudor Londoners gathered on an open hill outside the walls of the Tower of London around a high wooden scaffold, soldiers with halberds keeping order, the Tower behind, grey morning light
HEAD: Most executions took place in public on Tower Hill, just outside the walls of the Tower.
BODY: Tower Hill is an area of high ground just outside the northwestern walls of the Tower of London. Historians note that from the fourteenth century until the eighteenth, it was the main place for public executions of people who had been held in the Tower. A scaffold stood there, and crowds gathered to watch. Thomas More, John Fisher, the five men accused with Anne Boleyn and many others were executed there. Even so, executions on Tower Hill were not common events. They were usually reserved for people of high rank. Ordinary criminals were hanged at Tyburn, on the western edge of London. Historians note that public executions were meant to show the power of the king and to warn others against treason. Crowds came out of curiosity, sympathy or a wish to see justice done. Today, a memorial in the gardens of Trinity Square marks the site of the old scaffold, listing the names of many who died there.
===
IMG: poet-thomas-wyatt-window-bell-tower | a young Tudor poet with a beard in a dark doublet looking out sadly through the narrow window of a stone tower room, a sheet of paper and a quill on the sill, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft grey light
HEAD: The poet Thomas Wyatt, held in the Tower in 1536, may have watched the executions from his window.
BODY: Sir Thomas Wyatt was a courtier, diplomat and poet, one of the first to write sonnets in English. Historians note that he was a friend of Anne Boleyn and had known her since childhood. In May 1536, he was arrested and sent to the Tower, accused of being one of her lovers. Unlike the others, he was released after a few weeks, probably thanks to his friendship with Thomas Cromwell and his father's service to the king. Even so, the experience marked him deeply. According to tradition, he watched the executions of the five men on Tower Hill, and possibly that of Anne, from a window in the Bell Tower. He later wrote a poem about the fall of the men, with a Latin refrain meaning that thunder rolls around the throne. In it, he described the dangers of life at court. Historians note that the poem is one of the most moving reflections on the events of 1536. Wyatt died in 1542.
===
IMG: arrow-chest-chapel-burial-candles | a plain old wooden chest placed before a simple altar in a small dim Tudor chapel, candles burning and a priest standing quietly nearby, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a timber ceiling, soft candlelight
HEAD: Anne Boleyn and others who died in the Tower were buried in the chapel of Saint Peter ad Vincula.
BODY: The chapel of Saint Peter ad Vincula became the burial place for many of those executed at the Tower. Historians note that Anne Boleyn was buried there on the day of her death, in an old arrow chest, beneath the floor near the altar. Her brother George and the other men executed with them were buried in the chapel or its churchyard. Later, Catherine Howard, Lady Jane Grey, Thomas Cromwell and others were buried there too. Even so, the burials were often hurried and simple, and the graves were not well marked. In the nineteenth century, during repairs to the chapel, remains were found beneath the floor. They were examined and reburied, and their names were marked with memorial stones. Historians note that the identification of some remains, including those thought to be Anne's, is uncertain. The chapel is still used for worship today. Its quiet interior is a place where visitors can remember the people buried there.
===
IMG: memorial-tower-green-glass-sculpture | a simple round glass memorial shaped like a cushion resting on a stone base on a quiet green lawn inside an old castle, old stone buildings and a small chapel behind, soft overcast light
HEAD: Today, a memorial on Tower Green remembers those who were executed within the Tower's walls.
BODY: In 2006, a memorial was unveiled on Tower Green, inside the Tower of London. Historians note that it was designed by the artist Brian Catling. It is a round glass sculpture shaped like a cushion, resting on a dark stone base. Around it are engraved the names of those who were executed inside the Tower's walls, including Anne Boleyn, Catherine Howard and Lady Jane Grey. A short poem is engraved around the edge. Even so, the memorial is not a grave or a monument to great deeds. It is a quiet place for reflection, inviting visitors to think of those who died. The glass cushion suggests the place where a head might rest. Historians note that the memorial reflects changing attitudes towards the Tower's history. Instead of celebrating royal power, it remembers the human cost of the political struggles of the past. Many visitors pause there, reading the names and thinking about the lives behind them.
===
IMG: tower-hill-garden-memorial-trees | a quiet garden square with trees, benches and a low stone memorial in the middle, the walls of the Tower of London visible beyond, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Tower Hill, once a place of public executions, is now a quiet garden in the heart of London.
BODY: Today, Tower Hill is very different from the grim place it was in Tudor times. Historians note that the site of the old scaffold is now part of Trinity Square Gardens, a quiet green space with trees, flowers and benches. A small memorial marks the spot where the scaffold once stood, listing the names of more than a hundred people who were executed there. Even so, the area is busy and lively. People walk through the gardens on their way to work, sit on benches to eat lunch and visit the nearby memorials to sailors lost at sea in the two world wars. Tourists pass by on their way to the Tower. Historians note that the contrast between the peaceful gardens and their violent history is striking. The trees grow tall, and birds sing in their branches. Few people who sit there today stop to think about the crowds who once gathered on the same ground.

TOPIC: London around the Tower
===
IMG: tudor-london-cheapside-market-street | the broad street of Cheapside in Tudor London around 1536 with tall timber-framed houses, goldsmiths' shops, market stalls, a stone cross and crowds of townspeople, horses and carts, warm morning light
HEAD: In 1536, London was a growing city of about fifty thousand people, crowded inside its old Roman walls.
BODY: In 1536, London had about fifty thousand inhabitants. Historians note that it was growing quickly and would reach about two hundred thousand by 1600. Most people lived inside the old city walls, which had first been built by the Romans. The streets were narrow, crowded and noisy, lined with timber-framed houses, shops and churches. Even so, London was also a city of wealth and trade. Cheapside, its main market street, was famous for its goldsmiths' shops, which glittered with silver and gold. Merchants traded wool, cloth, wine, spices and many other goods. The city was governed by the Lord Mayor and the aldermen, elected by the guilds. Historians note that there were more than a hundred parish churches inside the walls, each with its own bells. Above them all rose the great spire of Saint Paul's Cathedral. The Tower stood at the eastern edge of the city, guarding the river and watching over its people.
===
IMG: old-st-pauls-cathedral-spire-1536 | the enormous medieval Saint Paul's Cathedral in London in 1536 with its very tall wooden spire rising above the rooftops, a busy churchyard with booksellers' stalls and townspeople, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Old Saint Paul's Cathedral, with its enormous spire, was the largest building in Tudor London.
BODY: Before the Great Fire of London in 1666, the city was dominated by Old Saint Paul's Cathedral. Historians note that it was one of the largest churches in Europe, with a spire that rose to about one hundred and fifty metres. It was built of stone in the Gothic style, mostly in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. Even so, the cathedral was not only a place of worship. Its long central aisle, called Paul's Walk, was used as a meeting place where people gathered to gossip, do business and look for work. Booksellers set up stalls in the churchyard, which became the centre of the London book trade. Preachers gave sermons at an outdoor pulpit called Paul's Cross, attracting large crowds. Historians note that the spire was struck by lightning and destroyed in 1561 and never rebuilt. The whole cathedral was destroyed in the Great Fire. The present Saint Paul's, with its famous dome, was designed by Christopher Wren.
===
IMG: all-hallows-church-near-tower-tudor | an old stone parish church with a square tower near the walls of the Tower of London in 1536, Tudor townspeople walking past, a few trees and timber-framed houses around it, soft light
HEAD: Near the Tower stood the ancient church of All Hallows, one of the oldest churches in London.
BODY: Just west of the Tower of London stands the church of All Hallows by the Tower. Historians note that it was founded in the seventh century, around the year 675, by the Abbey of Barking, making it one of the oldest churches in London. Parts of an Anglo-Saxon arch still survive inside it. In Tudor times, it was the parish church for many of the people who lived near the Tower. Even so, it had a special connection with the Tower's history. The bodies of some people executed on Tower Hill were brought to All Hallows for temporary burial. In the seventeenth century, the diarist Samuel Pepys climbed its tower to watch the Great Fire of London. The church was badly damaged by bombing in the Second World War but was rebuilt. Historians note that it contains a small museum with Roman remains found beneath the church. It is a quiet link to London's long history, right beside the Tower.
===
IMG: dissolution-monastery-empty-cloister-1536 | an empty monastery cloister with Gothic arches and an overgrown garden in 1536, a few royal officials in dark gowns making lists, a lone monk in a black habit walking away, soft grey light
HEAD: In 1536, Henry the Eighth began to close the monasteries of England and take their wealth.
BODY: In 1536, the English Parliament passed a law to close the smaller monasteries of England, those with an income of less than two hundred pounds a year. Historians note that this was the beginning of the Dissolution of the Monasteries, which by 1540 had closed all the religious houses in England and Wales. The monks, nuns and friars were sent away, and the monasteries' lands, buildings and treasures were taken by the crown. Even so, the dissolution was not only about religion. It brought great wealth to the king, who needed money for his court and his wars. Much of the land was sold to nobles and gentry, who became richer and more loyal to the crown. In London, many monasteries and priories were closed, and their buildings were turned into houses, workshops or ruins. Historians note that the dissolution changed the landscape of England and the lives of thousands of people forever.
===
IMG: pilgrimage-of-grace-banners-northern-army | a large crowd of northern English men in rough clothes and armour marching with religious banners showing the five wounds of Christ across a green moorland in October 1536, cloudy sky
HEAD: In the autumn of 1536, a great rebellion called the Pilgrimage of Grace broke out in the north of England.
BODY: In October 1536, a great rebellion broke out in Lincolnshire and Yorkshire, in the north of England. Historians note that it was called the Pilgrimage of Grace. The rebels were angry about the closing of the monasteries, the changes to religion, new taxes and the power of Thomas Cromwell. They marched under banners showing the five wounds of Christ. At its height, the rebel army had perhaps thirty thousand men. Even so, the rebellion did not lead to war. The Duke of Norfolk, sent by the king with a much smaller army, negotiated with the rebels. He promised that their complaints would be heard and that they would be pardoned. The rebels went home. However, early in 1537, there were new risings, and Henry took his revenge. The leaders, including the lawyer Robert Aske, were arrested. Some were held in the Tower of London before their trials. About two hundred people were executed.
===
IMG: sweating-sickness-tudor-house-doctor | a Tudor doctor in a long dark gown visiting a sick man lying in a bed with curtains in a timber-framed room, a worried woman beside the bed holding a cloth, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft candlelight
HEAD: Disease was a constant danger in Tudor London, including the dreaded sweating sickness.
BODY: Life in Tudor London was often cut short by disease. Historians note that plague returned to the city again and again, killing thousands of people in bad years. There was also a mysterious illness called the sweating sickness. It struck suddenly, causing fever, sweating, pain and exhaustion, and it could kill within hours. It appeared in England in 1485 and returned several times, with a serious outbreak in 1528. Even so, its cause has never been identified. Some historians and scientists believe it may have been a virus, but no one is certain. The sweating sickness seemed to affect the rich as well as the poor. In 1528, Anne Boleyn herself caught it but survived. Henry the Eighth was terrified of the illness and fled from place to place to avoid it. Historians note that disease was a constant fear in Tudor life. People prayed, used herbs and medicines and left the city when outbreaks struck.
===
IMG: thames-frost-fair-ice-tudor-winter | the frozen river Thames in winter with Tudor Londoners walking, skating and setting up stalls on the ice, the walls of the Tower and London Bridge in the background, cold pale light
HEAD: In very cold winters, the Thames sometimes froze, and Londoners walked across it on the ice.
BODY: In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, Europe experienced a period of colder weather, sometimes called the Little Ice Age. Historians note that in some especially cold winters, the river Thames froze solid in London. The old London Bridge, with its many narrow arches, slowed the flow of the river, which made it easier for ice to form. When the river froze, Londoners walked, skated and even rode across it. Even so, the great frost fairs, with stalls, games and entertainments on the ice, came later, especially in the seventeenth century. In the winter of 1536 to 1537, the Thames froze, and Henry the Eighth and Jane Seymour are said to have travelled across the ice on horseback from Westminster to Greenwich. Historians note that a frozen river meant hardship for many. Boatmen could not work, and goods could not be moved by water. Yet for others, the ice was a source of wonder and fun.

TOPIC: Ravens, keys and the Tower today

HEAD: Ravens have lived around the Tower of London for centuries, and a legend says the kingdom will fall if they leave.
BODY: Today, the Tower of London is famous for its ravens. Historians note that a legend says that if the ravens ever leave the Tower, the kingdom will fall. According to some stories, King Charles the Second ordered that at least six ravens should always be kept there, after being warned by an astronomer. Even so, historians have found no evidence for this legend before the nineteenth century. In Tudor times, ravens and red kites were common birds in London. They scavenged for food in the streets, eating scraps and waste. They may have gathered around the Tower, especially after executions. Today, the ravens are cared for by a Yeoman Warder called the Ravenmaster. They live in comfortable enclosures and are fed meat, eggs and biscuits soaked in blood. They have names and personalities. Some have lived at the Tower for more than twenty years. They are among the Tower's most popular residents.

IMG: ravenmaster-feeding-ravens-morning | a Yeoman Warder in a dark blue and red Tudor-style uniform feeding a group of black ravens on the grass in the early morning inside an old castle, stone walls behind, soft light
HEAD: The Ravenmaster looks after the ravens every day, feeding them and getting to know each one.
BODY: The Ravenmaster is a Yeoman Warder who is responsible for the care of the Tower's ravens. Historians note that the position was created in the twentieth century. Each morning, the Ravenmaster lets the ravens out of their enclosures and feeds them. In the evening, he calls them back for the night. He knows each bird well and can recognise their individual voices and behaviour. Even so, the ravens are not always easy to manage. They are intelligent, curious and sometimes mischievous. They have been known to steal food from visitors, hide shiny objects and imitate sounds. Some have even learned to say a few words. Occasionally, a raven has flown away from the Tower, but most stay close to home. The Ravenmaster makes sure there are always at least six ravens at the Tower, with extra birds in case one is lost. Historians note that the ravens have become a symbol of the Tower and its long history.

HEAD: Every night, the Chief Yeoman Warder locks the gates of the Tower in the Ceremony of the Keys.
BODY: The Ceremony of the Keys is one of the oldest traditions at the Tower of London. Historians note that it has been performed every night for many centuries, though its exact age is not known. At about seven minutes to ten each evening, the Chief Yeoman Warder sets out with a lantern and the keys of the Tower. Escorted by soldiers, he locks the main gates. On his way back, he is challenged by a sentry, who calls out to ask who comes there. The Warder replies that the keys belong to the king or queen. The sentry lets him pass, and the guards present arms. Even so, the ceremony is more than a show. It is the real locking of the Tower for the night. It is said to have been interrupted only once, during an air raid in the Second World War, when the escort was delayed by a few minutes. The ceremony ends with the sound of a bugle.

IMG: crown-jewels-jewel-house-glass-cases | jewelled royal crowns, a golden orb and sceptres displayed in glass cases in a dark museum room with soft spotlights, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: The Crown Jewels of England are kept in the Tower of London and can be seen by visitors.
BODY: The Crown Jewels of the United Kingdom are kept in the Jewel House at the Tower of London. Historians note that royal treasures have been stored in the Tower since the Middle Ages. In 1536, Henry the Eighth's crowns, sceptres and other regalia were kept in a jewel house within the Tower's walls. Even so, most of the medieval and Tudor regalia no longer exist. In 1649, after the execution of King Charles the First, Parliament ordered the royal regalia to be melted down or sold. When the monarchy was restored in 1660, new crowns and regalia were made for King Charles the Second. These form the core of today's collection. They include Saint Edward's Crown, used at coronations, and the Imperial State Crown. Historians note that the collection contains famous jewels, including the Cullinan diamonds. Millions of visitors see the Crown Jewels each year, passing slowly along moving walkways in front of the glittering treasures.

HEAD: The Tower of London is now one of the most visited historic sites in the world.
BODY: Today, the Tower of London is one of the most famous historic sites in the world. Historians note that it was listed as a World Heritage Site in 1988. It is cared for by a charity called Historic Royal Palaces, which also looks after other royal palaces, such as Hampton Court. Each year, about three million people visit the Tower. They walk through its gates, explore its towers and walls, see the Crown Jewels and the armour of Henry the Eighth and hear stories from the Yeoman Warders. Even so, the Tower is still a living place. The Yeoman Warders and their families live there, and soldiers guard the Crown Jewels. Services are held in the chapel. The ravens wander across the lawns. Historians note that the Tower has been in use for nearly a thousand years. It has been a palace, a fortress, a prison, a mint, a zoo and an armoury. Today, it is a place of memory and learning.

HEAD: Visitors can still see suits of armour made for Henry the Eighth in the White Tower.
BODY: Inside the White Tower, visitors can see the Royal Armouries' collection of arms and armour. Historians note that it is one of the oldest museums in the world, as royal armour has been displayed at the Tower since at least the seventeenth century. Among its treasures are several suits of armour made for Henry the Eighth. One, made when he was young, shows a tall, athletic man. Another, made near the end of his life, shows a much larger man, with a wide waist. Even so, the armour is not only about the king. It shows the skill of the armourers who made it, shaping and decorating steel with great care. Some suits are engraved with fine patterns and gilded with gold. Historians note that the armour helps visitors imagine the people of the past, their size, their strength and their love of display. Standing before it, it is easy to picture the king who ruled England in 1536.

IMG: yeoman-warder-telling-story-visitors | a Yeoman Warder in a dark blue and red Tudor-style uniform and flat bonnet standing on a stone path inside an old castle, gesturing as he tells a story, the White Tower behind him, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Today's Yeoman Warders are former soldiers who guide visitors and tell the Tower's stories.
BODY: Today, the Yeoman Warders of the Tower of London are all former members of the armed forces, with at least twenty-two years of service. Historians note that they are chosen for their good conduct and experience. They live in the Tower with their families and wear uniforms based on Tudor designs. On ordinary days, they wear dark blue and red. On special occasions, they wear the scarlet and gold state dress. Even so, their main role today is to guide and inform visitors. They lead tours of the Tower, telling stories of its history, its prisoners and its legends. They are famous for their humour and knowledge. In 2007, Moira Cameron became the first woman to be appointed a Yeoman Warder. Historians note that the Warders are a living link to the Tower's Tudor past. Their uniforms, ceremonies and traditions connect today's visitors with the warders who guarded the gates in 1536.

HEAD: Nearly a thousand years after it was built, the Tower of London still stands by the Thames.
BODY: Nearly a thousand years after William the Conqueror began building the White Tower, the Tower of London still stands on the bank of the Thames. Historians note that it has survived wars, fires, revolutions and bombings. It has seen kings and queens crowned and dethroned, prisoners arrive and depart, and the city around it grow from a small medieval town into one of the largest cities in the world. Even so, much has stayed the same. The White Tower still rises above the walls. The ravens still walk on the grass. The gates are still locked each night with the same words. The river still flows past, as it did in 1536. On quiet evenings, when the visitors have gone, the Tower returns to its small community of warders and their families. Lights glow in the windows of their houses, and the old stones settle into darkness, holding their many stories safely within the walls.
