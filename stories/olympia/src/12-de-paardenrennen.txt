The third day begins with the horse races. They are held in a place called the hippodrome, south of the stadium. It is a huge, long, open field, much bigger than the stadium. Around it, there are grassy banks, where the crowds sit and watch. At each end of the track, there is a post, and the horses must race around the posts again and again.

--

The horse races are the most exciting events of the Games. And they are also the most expensive. Only very rich people can afford to keep racing horses and chariots. Kings, nobles and wealthy families from all over the Greek world send their best horses to Olympia. But they do not drive the chariots themselves. They pay skilled drivers to do it for them. And yet, when a chariot wins, it is the owner who is crowned as the winner, not the driver.

Because of this, a woman could win at Olympia too. Not as a driver, but as the owner of the horses. Some years after our story, a Spartan princess called Kyniska will do exactly that. Her chariot will win the race, twice. She will be the first woman to become an Olympic champion. She will set up a statue at Olympia, with proud words carved on the base.

{img:starting-gate-hippodrome-bronze-eagle-dolphin-chariots-horses-waiting}
The start of the chariot race is a wonderful sight. There is a clever starting gate, shaped like the front of a ship. Each chariot waits in its own stall, with a rope across the front. On top of the gate, there is a bronze eagle. When the race begins, the eagle rises into the air, and a bronze dolphin drops down. Then the ropes are pulled away, one after another, so that the chariots on the outside start first. It is a fair start for everyone.

The most important race is the four-horse chariot race. Each chariot is a small, light cart on two wheels, pulled by four horses side by side. The driver stands in the cart, holding the reins in his hands. He wears a long white robe, which flutters in the wind. Sometimes there are more than forty chariots in a single race.

--

{img:four-horse-chariots-racing-hippodrome-dust-cloud-drivers-white-robes}
The trumpet sounds, and the chariots burst out of the gate. The ground shakes with the thunder of hooves. A cloud of dust rises into the air. The crowd jumps to its feet, shouting. The chariots race down the long track, toward the far post. They must race around it twelve times. That is about fourteen kilometres. It is a long, hard race.

The most dangerous moments are at the turning posts. All the chariots try to turn as close to the post as possible, to save time. Sometimes they crash into each other, and the drivers fall out. It is a race where drivers need to be brave and very skilful. Lykos holds his breath at every turn. But on this day, the drivers are careful, and the horses are well trained.

{img:chariot-turning-around-post-close-horses-leaning-skilful-driver}
Halfway through the race, a chariot from Sicily is in the lead. It belongs to a rich family from Syracuse. Its horses are beautiful, all four of them black, with white feet. But slowly, a chariot from Athens comes closer. The two chariots race side by side, lap after lap. The crowd is screaming. And on the last turn, the Athenian chariot cuts in close to the post, and wins by the length of a horse.

--

After the chariot race, there is also a race for horses with riders. The riders are boys, very light, and they ride without saddles or stirrups. They hold on to the horses' manes, and bend low over their necks. Their horses race once around the track, as fast as the wind. It is over very quickly. Then the crowd goes back to the stadium, hot and dusty and very happy. It has been a wonderful morning.

Lykos goes to look at the horses afterwards, in the shade of the trees where they are resting. They are the most beautiful animals he has ever seen. Tall and slender, with shining coats and proud heads. Grooms are brushing them and giving them water. One of the black horses from Syracuse puts its soft nose into Lykos's hand. He strokes it gently, and thinks of the old donkey at home, who would be very surprised to see such a cousin.
