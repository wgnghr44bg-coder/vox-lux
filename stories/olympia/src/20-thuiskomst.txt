The journey home takes five days. Lykos walks with his father and Philon, back through the hills and valleys of the Peloponnese. He carries his olive wreath carefully, wrapped in a soft cloth, so that the leaves will not break. Every evening, when they stop to rest, he takes it out and looks at it.

--

The news of his victory has travelled faster than they have. In every village they pass through, people come out to see them. They have heard that a shepherd boy from Arcadia has won at Olympia. They bring them food and water, and invite them into their houses. Children run beside them, asking Lykos to show them the wreath. Old men shake his hand. Lykos is shy, but he shows them all.

On the fifth day, in the late afternoon, they climb the last steep path toward the village. Lykos can see the stone houses on the green slope. He can hear the goat bells. He can smell the wood smoke from the chimneys. His heart is beating faster than it did before the race.

{img:village-people-running-out-to-greet-champion-mountain-village-evening}
Then someone in the village sees them coming, and shouts. And suddenly, everyone is running out of the houses. Men, women, children, old people. They are cheering and waving branches and flowers. In front of them all runs a girl of eleven, with her hair flying behind her. It is Theano. She throws her arms around her brother so hard that they almost fall over.

And then Lykos sees his mother, standing at the gate of their house, just where he left her. She does not run. She just stands there, with her hands pressed against her mouth, and tears running down her cheeks. Lykos walks to her slowly, and puts the olive wreath into her hands. She holds it, and then she holds him, for a long, long time.

--

{img:mother-holding-olive-wreath-embracing-son-gate-stone-house-sunset}
In the old days, when an Olympic champion came home to a big city, sometimes the people broke down a part of the city wall, to let him in. They said that a city with such a champion did not need walls to protect it. Lykos's village has no walls. But the people make him a feast, in the little square under the big plane tree. Everyone brings food. And the old men tell the story of the race again and again, each time a little more exciting.

Theano wants to hear everything. The journey, the month in Elis, the great statue of Zeus, the tent camp, the chariots. And most of all, the races of the girls in honour of Hera. Lykos tells her about the little painted pictures in the Temple of Hera, and the girls' starting line in the stadium. Theano listens with shining eyes. Then she says, very seriously, that she wants to start training tomorrow.

{img:olive-wreath-hanging-on-wall-simple-stone-house-hearth-glow}
The olive wreath is hung on the wall of the house, above the fire. Slowly, its leaves dry and turn grey-green. But it stays there for many years, through many winters. Whenever visitors come, they look at it, and ask about it. And Lykos's grandmother always tells the story, very proudly, from the beginning to the end.

--

And Lykos goes back to the goats. Every morning, before the sun rises, he takes them up into the mountains, as he always did. He runs after them over the rocks and through the bushes. But now, on many mornings, Theano runs beside him. And when she is fifteen, she runs at Olympia, in the race of the girls, in honour of Hera. Her brother walks with her all the way, and stands on the grassy bank, and cheers for her louder than anyone.
