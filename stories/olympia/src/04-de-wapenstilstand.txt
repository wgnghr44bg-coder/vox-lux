In the spring of the Olympic year, three men set out from the town of Elis. Elis is the city that looks after the sanctuary of Olympia and organizes the Games. The three men are heralds. They wear white robes and wreaths of olive leaves on their heads. They carry staffs as a sign of their office. And they have a very important task.

--

The heralds travel to every Greek city, near and far. Some of them sail across the sea, to the Greek cities of Italy, Sicily and Asia. In each city, they go to the market square and announce the Games. They tell the people the date of the festival. And they announce the sacred truce.

The sacred truce is one of the most remarkable things about the Olympic Games. It is a promise of peace. During the time of the Games, nobody may attack the land of Elis, and nobody may harm the people travelling to Olympia or back home again. Even if two cities are at war, their athletes and visitors must be allowed to travel safely. The truce lasted for about a month, and later for even longer, so that people from far away could get home.

{img:herald-announcing-in-agora-crowd-listening-market-stalls-columns}
When the heralds come to a city, people crowd around to hear them. The herald stands on a step and raises his staff. In a loud voice, he calls out the words of the truce. Let all Greeks hear. The festival of Zeus at Olympia is coming. Let there be peace. Let all who wish to go, go in safety. The people cheer. Everyone knows what it means. The Games are coming again.

Was the truce always kept? Mostly, yes. The Greeks took it very seriously, because it was a promise made to Zeus. Breaking it meant angering the king of the gods. Once, a city sent soldiers into the land of Elis during the truce. It was punished with a heavy fine, and its people were not allowed to take part in the Games until the fine was paid. Most cities did not want to risk that.

--

The heralds also come to the small towns of Arcadia. In the market of the nearest town, Lykos and his father hear them announce the festival. The Games will be held in the summer, at the second full moon after the longest day of the year. That is in the middle of the hot summer. And all athletes who wish to compete must come to Elis one month before the Games, to train under the eyes of the judges.

Lykos feels a shiver of excitement run through him. It is really happening. In a few weeks, he will leave his village and travel to Elis. He has never been further than the market town. Now he will see the great sanctuary of Zeus, and the athletes from all over the world.

{img:mother-packing-bundle-cloak-sandals-bread-boy-leaving-home-greek}
The weeks before he leaves are busy. His mother weaves him a new cloak of soft wool. His father buys him a little flask for oil, made of clay, and a curved bronze scraper. Athletes use it to scrape the oil and dust from their skin after training. His grandmother gives him a small charm, a little clay figure of Zeus, to keep him safe. And Theano gives him a ribbon, woven by herself, to tie around his arm.

--

On the morning he leaves, the whole village comes out to see him off. The women give him bread and cheese and figs for the road. The men slap him on the back and wish him luck. The children run beside him to the edge of the village. His father will travel with him, and so will old Philon, on a donkey. His mother hugs him tightly and tells him to come home safe, whether he wins or not.

{img:father-son-old-man-donkey-leaving-village-sunrise-mountain-path}
They set off down the mountain path, in the cool of the early morning. At the bend in the road, Lykos turns around for one last look. His mother is still standing at the gate, with Theano beside her. They wave. Lykos waves back. And then the path turns, and the village is gone behind the trees.
