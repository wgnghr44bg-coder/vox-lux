How did the Olympic Games begin? Nobody knows for sure. Even the ancient Greeks themselves did not really know. But they told many beautiful stories about it.

One story says that the hero Heracles started the Games. Heracles was the strongest man in the world, the son of Zeus. After he had finished one of his great labours, he came to Olympia. He wanted to honour his father. So he marked out a running track. He measured it with his own feet, six hundred steps. And that, the story says, is why the stadion is exactly six hundred feet long. Because those were the feet of Heracles.

--

Another story is about a young prince called Pelops. He wanted to marry a princess. But her father, the king, said that any young man who wanted to marry her must first beat him in a chariot race. Pelops won the race, and married the princess. He became king of the whole area. And the southern part of Greece is still named after him today. It is called the Peloponnese, which means the island of Pelops.

{img:pelops-chariot-race-legend-horses-dust-princess-watching-ancient-style}
The truth is probably simpler. For hundreds of years, people had come to this valley to pray to Zeus. They held festivals with music and sacrifices. And as part of the festival, young men held races, to honour the god. Slowly, these races became more and more important. Until, in the year 776 before Christ, someone began to write down the names of the winners.

--

For the first fifty years or so, the single short race was the only event. Then, slowly, more contests were added. A race of two lengths of the track, there and back. A long race, of about four kilometres. Then wrestling, and the pentathlon, which had five different events. Then boxing, and the chariot race. Then races for boys. Each new event was added at a particular Games, and the Greeks wrote down the year.

{img:early-olympia-simple-altar-zeus-pilgrims-praying-smoke-rising-trees}
The Greeks also began to count the years by the Games. The four years from one Games to the next were called an Olympiad. A Greek might say that something happened in the third year of the fiftieth Olympiad. In a world where every city had its own calendar, the Olympic Games gave all the Greeks one way of counting time together.

In those days, Greece was not one country. It was made up of hundreds of small, independent cities, each with its own laws and its own little army. Athens, Sparta, Corinth, Thebes and many more. They often quarrelled with each other, and sometimes they went to war. But they all spoke Greek. They all believed in the same gods. And they all came to Olympia.

--

Greeks also lived far away from Greece itself. They had built cities on the coasts of Italy, Sicily, France, Spain, North Africa and the Black Sea. They had sailed out, many years before, to find new land. But they still felt Greek. And every four years, they sent their best athletes home, across the sea, to compete at Olympia. A runner from Sicily might race against a runner from Sparta, and a wrestler from Africa against a wrestler from Athens.

So the Games became much more than a sports festival. They became a meeting place for the whole Greek world. A place where people from far away could see each other, talk, trade, make friendships and make peace. And at the centre of it all, there was always Zeus, the father of the gods, to whom the Games were dedicated.

{img:olympia-growing-over-centuries-new-temple-being-built-workers-stone}
Over the centuries, the little sanctuary grew. Around 600 before Christ, a stone temple for the goddess Hera was built. Little treasure houses were added, where cities kept their gifts to the gods. A real stadium was built, with grassy banks for the spectators. And then, about a hundred years before our story, the greatest building of all was begun. A huge temple for Zeus himself, with a statue inside that would become one of the wonders of the world.

--

And so we come to the time of our story, about the year 432 before Christ. The Games have been held every four years for more than three hundred years. They are now the most famous festival in the world. And in the mountains of Arcadia, a boy called Lykos is dreaming of running there.
