The next morning, Lykos goes with Philon to see the sanctuary. It is called the Altis, which means the grove. It is surrounded by a low wall. Inside the wall, everything is sacred to the gods. There are tall plane trees and olive trees, giving cool shade. And between the trees, there are temples, altars and hundreds of statues.

--

The oldest temple is the Temple of Hera, the wife of Zeus. It stands near the foot of the Hill of Kronos. It is long and low, with thick columns. Long ago, its columns were made of wood. When a wooden column became old and rotten, it was replaced with a stone one. So now the columns are all slightly different from each other, some thick and some thin. It looks a little old-fashioned, but very peaceful.

In front of the Temple of Hera, there is a large altar. And nearby is the most sacred place of all, the great altar of Zeus. It does not look like an altar at all. It is a huge mound of ash, taller than a man. For hundreds of years, the ashes of the sacrifices have been piled up here, mixed with water from the river, and pressed together. So the altar grows a little higher every year.

{img:temple-of-hera-olympia-old-columns-different-sizes-afternoon-shade}
Along the bottom of the Hill of Kronos, there is a row of small buildings, like little temples. They are the treasuries. Each one was built by a different city, to keep its gifts to Zeus safe. There is a treasury of Sikyon, of Syracuse, of Megara, of Gela and of many others. Inside, there are golden cups, bronze shields, statues and other precious things. Each city wants its treasury to be the most beautiful.

And everywhere, there are statues. Statues of gods and heroes. Statues of horses and bulls. And statues of Olympic champions. A winner at Olympia was allowed to set up a statue of himself in the sanctuary. Over the years, there have come to be hundreds of them, in bronze and stone. Lykos walks among them, looking up at the faces of the old champions. He reads their names, carved into the stone bases.

--

{img:bronze-statues-olympic-champions-stone-bases-boy-looking-up-sunlight}
Near the entrance to the stadium, there is a row of bronze statues of Zeus. Philon explains what they are. They were paid for with the fines of athletes who cheated. Each one has a little poem carved on its base, saying who cheated and how. It is a warning to every athlete who walks into the stadium. At Olympia, you must win with the speed of your feet and the strength of your body, not with money or tricks.

Lykos looks at the statues and feels a little afraid. He would never cheat. But he understands how seriously the Games are taken here. This is not just a race. It is a gift to Zeus. And Zeus is watching.

{img:stadium-entrance-vaulted-tunnel-olympia-grassy-banks-track-beyond}
Then Philon takes him to see the stadium itself. It lies just outside the sanctuary, to the east. To get there, you walk through a long tunnel with a stone roof. When Lykos comes out at the other end, the stadium opens in front of him. A long, flat track of hard, pale earth, almost two hundred metres long. On both sides, there are grassy banks, sloping up. There are no seats. The spectators sit or stand on the grass. Up to forty thousand people can fit here.

--

At each end of the track, there is a line of stone slabs, set into the ground. These are the starting lines. Each slab has two grooves cut into it, for the toes of the runners. Lykos kneels down and touches the grooves with his fingers. Thousands of runners have stood here before him. In a few days, he will stand here too. His heart beats faster at the thought.

Philon smiles at him. He tells Lykos to walk the track, slowly, from one end to the other. To feel the earth under his feet. To look at the hill and the sky. So that on the day of the race, it will feel like a familiar place. Lykos walks the whole length of the stadium, barefoot, in the morning sun. And when he reaches the other end, he turns around, and looks back, and he imagines the crowds cheering.
