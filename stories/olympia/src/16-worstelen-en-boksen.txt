In the afternoon of the fourth day, there are the combat contests. Wrestling, boxing and the pankration, which is a mix of wrestling and boxing. These were some of the most popular events at Olympia. But they could also be rough, so we will watch them from a gentle distance, sitting high up on the grassy bank.

--

Wrestling was the favourite sport of many Greeks. Almost every Greek town had a wrestling school, called a palaestra. Boys learned to wrestle from a young age. The goal was to throw your opponent to the ground three times. The wrestlers grab each other by the arms and shoulders, and push and pull and twist. It looks a little like a slow dance, until one of them suddenly throws the other into the sand.

The great wrestlers were famous all over Greece. People told many stories about Milo of Kroton, who had won six times at Olympia, more than a hundred years before. They said he trained by carrying a little calf every day. As the calf grew bigger, so did his strength. Until, one day, he could carry a fully grown bull. Nobody knows if this story is true. But it is a good story, and Greek children loved it.

{img:wrestling-school-palaestra-boys-learning-holds-teacher-courtyard}
Boxing at Olympia was different from boxing today. There were no gloves. Instead, the boxers wound long strips of soft leather around their hands and wrists. There were no rounds and no breaks. The boxers fought until one of them gave up, by raising one finger in the air. There were also no weight classes. So a big man might box against a smaller man.

The pankration was the roughest contest of all. Almost everything was allowed, except biting and poking someone's eyes. It was a contest of great strength and courage. The athletes trained for years. And the crowd watched with great excitement. But we will let them have their contest, and look instead at the sky over the stadium, where the swallows are swooping in the warm air.

--

{img:swallows-swooping-over-stadium-evening-sky-hill-of-kronos-pines}
The judges watch the combat contests very closely. They stand near the athletes, with their long sticks. If an athlete breaks a rule, the judge taps him with the stick, and the contest stops for a moment. The judges are very strict. They want the contests to be fair and honourable. And they want to make sure that nobody is seriously hurt.

Sometimes, an athlete is so famous and so strong that nobody wants to compete against him. Then he is declared the winner without fighting at all. The Greeks had a special word for this. It meant winning without dust. Because the winner never had to lie down in the dusty sand. The crowds were a little disappointed when this happened. But the athlete got his olive wreath all the same.

{img:victor-without-dust-athlete-standing-alone-sand-pit-crowd-applause}
Lykos does not enjoy the combat contests very much. He is a runner, not a fighter. He prefers the clean speed of the races. Dion feels the same. So in the middle of the afternoon, they leave the stadium and walk down to the river. They lie in the shade of a plane tree and talk. The sounds of the crowd come to them from far away, like the sound of distant waves.

--

Dion tells Lykos about his home in Sicily. About the big city of Syracuse, with its harbour full of ships. About the great volcano, Mount Etna, which sometimes smokes and rumbles. About the lemon trees and the blue sea. He says that Lykos must come and visit him one day. Lykos laughs. He has never even seen the sea, except on the road to Olympia. But he promises that he will try.

In the evening, when the contests are over, the boys walk back to the camp. The last of the sun is shining on the roof of the Temple of Zeus. Tomorrow is the last day of the Games. The day of the olive wreaths.
