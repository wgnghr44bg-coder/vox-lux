The Olympic Games continued for a very long time after Lykos. Every four years, the heralds went out to announce the sacred truce. Every four years, the athletes came to Elis, and the crowds came to the valley. The Games went on through good times and bad times, through wars and peace, for more than eight hundred years after our story.

--

In the centuries that followed, Greece changed a great deal. First, the kings of Macedonia, in the north, became the masters of Greece. The most famous of them was Alexander the Great. Then, about two hundred years before Christ, the Romans came. They conquered Greece and made it part of their great empire. But the Romans admired Greek culture very much. And they did not stop the Olympic Games. They joined in.

The Romans built new buildings at Olympia. Bath houses with warm water, guest houses for visitors, and a beautiful fountain with statues. One rich man built an aqueduct, to bring fresh water to the sanctuary. At last, the visitors no longer had to queue for water in the summer heat. Athletes now came from all over the Roman Empire, from Egypt, Syria and Spain. The Games became bigger than ever.

{img:fountain-nymphaeum-olympia-water-flowing-statues-visitors-drinking}
Not every Roman behaved well at Olympia. The emperor Nero came to the Games in the year 67 after Christ. He wanted to win everything. He even had the Games moved by two years, so that he could compete. He entered the chariot race with ten horses. He fell out of his chariot and did not finish the race. But the judges gave him the olive wreath anyway, because he was the emperor. After he died, his name was removed from the list of winners.

Slowly, the world changed again. Christianity spread through the Roman Empire. The old gods, Zeus and Hera and the others, were slowly forgotten. And the Games, which were a festival in honour of Zeus, began to seem out of place. The last Games that we know of were held around the year 393 after Christ, or perhaps a little later. After more than a thousand years, the Olympic Games came quietly to an end.

--

{img:olympia-ruins-fallen-columns-earthquake-grass-growing-quiet-centuries}
The sanctuary was slowly abandoned. The great statue of Zeus was taken away to the city of Constantinople, far to the east. There, many years later, it was destroyed in a fire. At Olympia, the temples fell into ruin. Then, in the sixth century, great earthquakes shook the valley. The huge columns of the Temple of Zeus fell down. They still lie on the ground today, exactly where they fell, like giant stacks of coins that have toppled over.

After that, the rivers did their work. The river Alpheios and the little river Kladeos flooded again and again. Each flood left a layer of sand and mud behind. Slowly, over hundreds of years, the whole sanctuary was buried. In some places, the ground rose by five or even seven metres. The temples, the statues, the stadium, everything disappeared under the earth.

{img:buried-valley-olympia-fields-sheep-grazing-river-no-ruins-visible}
For more than a thousand years, Olympia was forgotten. Shepherds grazed their sheep on the green fields where the stadium had been. Farmers ploughed the earth above the temples. Nobody remembered exactly where the famous sanctuary had been. Only the old books remembered it, in libraries far away. The stories of the Games, of the great statue and of the olive wreaths, lived on only on paper.

--

But the stories were never really forgotten. People in Europe kept reading the old Greek books. They read about the Games, and the champions, and the sacred truce. And they began to wonder. Where was Olympia? What did it look like? Could it ever be found again? In the seventeen hundreds, travellers began to come to the valley, looking for the lost sanctuary. And slowly, the ground began to give up its secrets.

Some of the things that were buried at Olympia were very fragile. Painted clay tiles, small bronze figures, the iron tools of the workers. Under the earth, protected from wind and rain, many of them survived for a very long time. In a way, the rivers that buried Olympia also kept it safe. They wrapped it in a thick, soft blanket of earth, and let it sleep, until the day when someone would come to wake it.
