On the fifth and last day of the Games, there is the most beautiful ceremony of all. It is the crowning of the winners. Early in the morning, a boy walks to an old wild olive tree, which grows behind the Temple of Zeus. It is called the tree of the beautiful wreaths. The boy must be a child whose mother and father are both still alive. In his hand, he carries a small golden sickle.

--

Carefully, the boy cuts branches from the tree. He cuts one branch for every winner of the Games. The olive branches are then bent into the shape of a ring, and tied, to make wreaths. Then the wreaths are laid out on a beautiful table, made of gold and ivory, in the Temple of Zeus. There they wait, in the cool darkness, at the feet of the great statue.

The olive wreath is the only prize at Olympia. It has no value in money. In a few days, its leaves will dry and turn brown. But to the Greeks, it is the most precious prize in the world. Because it comes from the sacred tree of Zeus. And because it means that the gods have chosen you, out of all the Greeks, as the best.

{img:winners-procession-to-temple-of-zeus-ribbons-palm-branches-crowd}
In the morning, the winners walk in a procession to the Temple of Zeus. They are dressed in fine clothes, with ribbons tied around their heads and arms. Their families walk beside them, singing. The crowd lines the way, cheering and throwing flowers and leaves. Lykos walks with the other winners. He wears a new white tunic, which his father bought for him in the market. Around his arm, he still wears Theano's ribbon.

In front of the temple, the judges are waiting. The herald calls out the names of the winners, one by one. With each name, he calls out the name of the winner's father and his city. Then the winner steps forward. And the chief judge places the olive wreath on his head.

--

{img:judge-placing-olive-wreath-on-boys-head-temple-steps-sunlight}
When the herald calls out the name of Lykos, son of Kleon, from Arcadia, Lykos steps forward. His legs are shaking more than they did before the race. The judge smiles at him, and places the wreath on his head. It is light and cool, and smells of leaves. The crowd cheers. Somewhere in the crowd, Lykos hears his father shouting his name. And old Philon, leaning on his stick, cheering in his cracked voice.

Lykos looks out over the crowd, and the temples, and the green valley. He will remember this moment for the rest of his life. When he is an old man, he will close his eyes and see it again. The bright sunlight, the white columns, the faces of the crowd, and the smell of the olive leaves.

{img:winners-standing-together-olive-wreaths-temple-columns-proud-calm}
After the crowning, the winners go into the temple, to thank Zeus. They make an offering at the feet of the great golden statue. Lykos takes the little clay figure of Zeus from his grandmother out of his bundle. He holds it up, so that it can see the great Zeus too. And he says thank you, very quietly, in his heart.

--

In the afternoon, the winners also have the right to set up a statue of themselves at Olympia. But statues are expensive. A bronze statue costs more than a shepherd earns in many years. So Lykos will not have a statue. But it does not matter. His name has been written in the list of winners, kept by the judges of Elis. It will stay there, with the names of all the other champions, back to Koroibos the cook.
