In 1766, an English traveller found the ruins of a large temple in the valley, half buried in the earth. It was the Temple of Zeus. Then, about a hundred years later, in 1875, German archaeologists began to dig at Olympia. They dug for six years. And slowly, the whole sanctuary came back into the light.

--

They found the Temple of Zeus, with its fallen columns. They found the Temple of Hera, the treasuries and the altars. Later, in the nineteen fifties, archaeologists also found the workshop of Phidias, the sculptor who made the great statue of Zeus. In it, they found clay moulds he used to shape the golden robe. And they found a little cup with his name scratched on the bottom. It says, I belong to Phidias. It was probably his own drinking cup.

Today, Olympia is one of the most visited ancient sites in Greece. Visitors can walk through the sanctuary, among the ruins and the pine trees. They can see the fallen columns of the Temple of Zeus, still lying where the earthquake left them. One column has been put back up again, to show how tall they were. It stands alone, high above the ruins.

{img:single-restored-column-temple-of-zeus-olympia-today-blue-sky}
And visitors can walk through the old stone tunnel into the stadium. The stadium looks almost exactly as it did in the time of Lykos. The long flat track, the grassy banks on each side, and the stone starting lines at each end, with their grooves for the toes of the runners. Many visitors cannot resist. They take off their shoes, stand at the starting line, and run the length of the track, laughing.

In the museum at Olympia, there are many treasures. There are the great carved scenes from the Temple of Zeus, with the labours of Heracles. There is a beautiful statue of the god Hermes, carrying a little baby. There are bronze helmets, offered to Zeus by soldiers. And there is a row of the stone bases of the statues of Zeus, paid for by athletes who cheated. Their names are still there, carved in the stone, after all these years.

--

{img:olympic-flame-lighting-ceremony-temple-of-hera-priestesses-mirror-today}
At the end of the eighteen hundreds, a Frenchman called Pierre de Coubertin had a dream. He wanted to bring the Olympic Games back to life. Not as a festival for Zeus, but as a festival of peace and friendship between all the countries of the world. In 1896, the first modern Olympic Games were held in Athens. Athletes came from fourteen countries. And the most popular winner was a Greek water carrier called Spyridon Louis, who won the marathon.

Since then, the Olympic Games have been held every four years, in different cities all over the world. They are now the biggest sporting festival on Earth. Women compete in almost every sport, just as the girls of the Heraia once ran in the stadium of Olympia. And winners receive gold, silver and bronze medals. But at the Games of Athens in 2004, the winners also received something else. A wreath of olive leaves.

{img:torch-relay-runner-carrying-olympic-flame-olympia-road-today}
And there is one more tradition, which connects the modern Games with ancient Olympia. A few months before each Games, a ceremony is held in front of the old Temple of Hera. Women dressed as ancient priestesses use a curved mirror to catch the rays of the sun. The sunlight lights a torch. And that flame is carried by runners, from hand to hand, all the way to the city where the Games will be held. It is the Olympic flame.

--

So every time the Olympic Games begin, somewhere in the world, the fire in the great stadium was lit by the sun of Olympia. It was carried from the quiet valley by the river Alpheios, past the Hill of Kronos, and the fallen columns, and the old stadium where Koroibos ran, almost three thousand years ago.
