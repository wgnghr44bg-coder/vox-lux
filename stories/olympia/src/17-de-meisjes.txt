There is something we have not yet talked about. Where are the women and girls of Olympia? During the Games, married women were not allowed to watch the contests. This was an old rule, and it was strictly kept. But unmarried girls were allowed to watch. And the girls also had their own festival at Olympia, with their own races.

--

This festival was called the Heraia, in honour of Hera, the wife of Zeus and the queen of the gods. It was held every four years, at a different time from the Olympic Games. It was organised by a group of sixteen women from Elis. Every four years, they wove a beautiful new robe for the statue of Hera in her temple. And they held races for girls, in the Olympic stadium.

The girls ran in three groups, according to their age. The youngest girls ran first, then the older girls, and then the young women. They wore a short tunic, which came down to just above the knee, with one shoulder bare. They wore their hair loose, flowing behind them as they ran. Their track was a little shorter than the track of the men, about five-sixths of the length.

{img:sixteen-women-weaving-robe-for-hera-loom-sunlit-room-elis}
The winners of the girls' races received prizes too. They were given an olive wreath, just like the men. They also received a share of the meat from a cow sacrificed to Hera. And they were allowed to put up a little painted picture of themselves in the Temple of Hera, with their names written beneath. Today, there is a small bronze statue in a museum, of a girl running, with one shoulder bare. She may be one of these young runners.

Lykos learns about the Heraia from old Philon, during the Games. He has promised Theano that he will find out everything. So he goes to the Temple of Hera, and looks at the little pictures of the girl winners, hanging on the walls and columns. Some of them are old and faded. Some are new and bright. He reads their names. Then he goes to look at the girls' starting line in the stadium.

--

{img:temple-of-hera-interior-small-painted-portraits-girl-winners-columns}
There is also a famous story about the old rule against married women. It happened some years after our story. There was a woman from the island of Rhodes, called Kallipateira. Her husband had died, and she trained her son to be a boxer. When her son went to Olympia, she wanted so much to see him that she dressed up as a trainer, a man. Her son won. And in her joy, she jumped over the fence to hug him, and everyone saw that she was a woman.

According to the rule, she should have been punished. But the judges forgave her, because her father, her brothers and her son had all been Olympic champions. Instead, they made a new rule. From then on, the trainers had to come into the stadium without clothes, just like the athletes. It is a story that people at Olympia told and retold for hundreds of years, always with a smile.

{img:girl-running-alone-mountain-meadow-flowers-hair-flying-free}
Many people think it was unfair that married women could not watch the Games. And they are right. But the girls of Greece did run. And in Sparta especially, girls trained in running and wrestling, just like the boys. The Spartans thought that strong girls would become strong mothers. And a Spartan princess, Kyniska, would one day become an Olympic champion herself, with her chariot.

--

Lykos thinks of Theano, running beside him in the green field by the stream, laughing, with her hair flying. He thinks that one day, perhaps, she will run here, in the races of Hera. He decides that he will help her train, the way Philon helped him. And when the day comes, he will walk with her all the way to Olympia, and stand on the grassy bank, and cheer for her louder than anyone.
