{img:olympia-valley-dawn-ancient-greece-river-alpheios-pine-trees-temples-mist}
Tonight we travel back almost three thousand years, to ancient Greece. We go to a quiet green valley in the south of Greece, where two rivers meet. It is called Olympia. And in this valley, something began that still exists today, all over the world. The Olympic Games.

[long-pause]

It is early morning, and a soft mist lies over the valley. The river Alpheios flows slowly between the trees, wide and shallow and silver. On the hills around the valley, there are pine trees and wild olive trees. Birds are singing in the bushes. A low hill rises above the valley, covered with pines. It is called the Hill of Kronos, after an old god.

[pause]

At the foot of the hill, there is a sacred grove. It is a quiet place, with old trees, a few altars and a simple wooden temple. People come here to pray to Zeus, the king of the Greek gods. And on this morning, a crowd has gathered at the edge of the grove, beside a long, flat strip of earth. Young men are standing in a row, barefoot, waiting. They are about to run a race.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:first-olympic-footrace-776-bc-young-men-running-barefoot-simple-track-grove}
According to the old Greek stories, this is the year 776 before Christ. It is the first Olympic Games that the Greeks wrote down. There is only one event. A single footrace, from one end of the track to the other. It is about two hundred metres long. The Greeks call this distance a stadion. That is where our word stadium comes from.

[pause]

A man raises his hand. The runners lean forward. And then they are off, running as fast as they can, their feet thudding on the hard earth. The crowd shouts and cheers. In a few seconds, it is over. The winner is a young man called Koroibos. He comes from the town of Elis, close by. And the old stories say that he was a cook. He is the first Olympic champion whose name we know.

[pause]

Koroibos does not win a gold medal. There are no medals at Olympia. There is no money either. His prize is a branch from a wild olive tree, which grows in the sacred grove. That is all. But to the Greeks, it is the greatest honour in the world. His name will be remembered for almost three thousand years. And tonight, we remember it too.

[pause]

From this small beginning, the Games will slowly grow. More races will be added, and wrestling, and boxing, and chariot races. More and more people will come, from all over the Greek world. The simple grove will be filled with beautiful temples and statues. And every four years, for more than a thousand years, the Games will be held here, in this quiet valley.

[long-pause]

{img:greek-boy-runner-arcadia-hills-goats-olive-trees-morning-light}
Tonight, we will visit Olympia twice. First, we will look at those early beginnings. And then, we will travel forward about three hundred years, to the time when the Games were at their greatest. We will follow a boy of sixteen, called Lykos. He is a shepherd's son from the mountains of Arcadia, in the middle of Greece. And he is a very fast runner.

[pause]

We will travel with Lykos to Olympia. We will see the great temple of Zeus, and the tents of the crowds, and the athletes training in the sun. We will watch the races, and the chariots thundering around the track. We will see the great sacrifice under the full moon, and the crowning of the winners with olive wreaths. And we will meet the girls of Olympia, who held their own races, in honour of the goddess Hera.

[pause]

{img:ancient-olympia-sanctuary-temples-statues-crowds-summer-day-distant}
Some of the contests at Olympia were rough, and we will only look at those from a distance. But most of all, the Games were a great festival, full of music, prayers, friends and feasting. For five days, people from all over Greece came together in peace, in this green valley by the river. So let us begin, on a summer morning, long ago, in the valley of Olympia, where the first runners are waiting at the line.

[long-pause]

{img:heracles-legend-pacing-out-stadium-ancient-greek-vase-painting-style}
How did the Olympic Games begin? Nobody knows for sure. Even the ancient Greeks themselves did not really know. But they told many beautiful stories about it.

[pause]

One story says that the hero Heracles started the Games. Heracles was the strongest man in the world, the son of Zeus. After he had finished one of his great labours, he came to Olympia. He wanted to honour his father. So he marked out a running track. He measured it with his own feet, six hundred steps. And that, the story says, is why the stadion is exactly six hundred feet long. Because those were the feet of Heracles.

[long-pause]

Another story is about a young prince called Pelops. He wanted to marry a princess. But her father, the king, said that any young man who wanted to marry her must first beat him in a chariot race. Pelops won the race, and married the princess. He became king of the whole area. And the southern part of Greece is still named after him today. It is called the Peloponnese, which means the island of Pelops.

[pause]

{img:pelops-chariot-race-legend-horses-dust-princess-watching-ancient-style}
The truth is probably simpler. For hundreds of years, people had come to this valley to pray to Zeus. They held festivals with music and sacrifices. And as part of the festival, young men held races, to honour the god. Slowly, these races became more and more important. Until, in the year 776 before Christ, someone began to write down the names of the winners.

[long-pause]

For the first fifty years or so, the single short race was the only event. Then, slowly, more contests were added. A race of two lengths of the track, there and back. A long race, of about four kilometres. Then wrestling, and the pentathlon, which had five different events. Then boxing, and the chariot race. Then races for boys. Each new event was added at a particular Games, and the Greeks wrote down the year.

[pause]

{img:early-olympia-simple-altar-zeus-pilgrims-praying-smoke-rising-trees}
The Greeks also began to count the years by the Games. The four years from one Games to the next were called an Olympiad. A Greek might say that something happened in the third year of the fiftieth Olympiad. In a world where every city had its own calendar, the Olympic Games gave all the Greeks one way of counting time together.

[pause]

In those days, Greece was not one country. It was made up of hundreds of small, independent cities, each with its own laws and its own little army. Athens, Sparta, Corinth, Thebes and many more. They often quarrelled with each other, and sometimes they went to war. But they all spoke Greek. They all believed in the same gods. And they all came to Olympia.

[long-pause]

Greeks also lived far away from Greece itself. They had built cities on the coasts of Italy, Sicily, France, Spain, North Africa and the Black Sea. They had sailed out, many years before, to find new land. But they still felt Greek. And every four years, they sent their best athletes home, across the sea, to compete at Olympia. A runner from Sicily might race against a runner from Sparta, and a wrestler from Africa against a wrestler from Athens.

[pause]

So the Games became much more than a sports festival. They became a meeting place for the whole Greek world. A place where people from far away could see each other, talk, trade, make friendships and make peace. And at the centre of it all, there was always Zeus, the father of the gods, to whom the Games were dedicated.

[pause]

{img:olympia-growing-over-centuries-new-temple-being-built-workers-stone}
Over the centuries, the little sanctuary grew. Around 600 before Christ, a stone temple for the goddess Hera was built. Little treasure houses were added, where cities kept their gifts to the gods. A real stadium was built, with grassy banks for the spectators. And then, about a hundred years before our story, the greatest building of all was begun. A huge temple for Zeus himself, with a statue inside that would become one of the wonders of the world.

[long-pause]

And so we come to the time of our story, about the year 432 before Christ. The Games have been held every four years for more than three hundred years. They are now the most famous festival in the world. And in the mountains of Arcadia, a boy called Lykos is dreaming of running there.

[long-pause]

{img:arcadia-mountain-village-stone-houses-goats-shepherd-boy-ancient-greece}
High in the mountains of Arcadia, in the middle of the Peloponnese, there is a small village of stone houses. It lies on a green slope, with oak trees and chestnut trees all around. Below the village, a stream runs down through the valley. Above it, the bare mountains rise up into the sky. Sheep and goats graze on the hillsides. And the bells around their necks make a soft clinking sound, all day long.

[long-pause]

This is where Lykos lives. He is sixteen years old. His father is a shepherd, and so was his grandfather. Their house is small and simple, built of rough stone, with a roof of clay tiles. There is one main room, with a fire in the middle, and a little courtyard where the chickens scratch in the dust. Lykos lives there with his father, his mother, his grandmother and his little sister, Theano.

[pause]

Every morning, before the sun rises, Lykos takes the goats up into the mountains. He follows them over the rocks and through the bushes, all day long. When a goat wanders off, he runs after it. When a kid is in danger, he runs to help. He has been running in these mountains since he was a little boy. His legs are thin but very strong. And he is the fastest runner in the whole valley.

[pause]

{img:boy-running-mountain-path-goats-scattering-wind-ancient-greece-sunlight}
The other boys of the village race each other on feast days, on a flat field by the stream. Lykos always wins. He wins so easily that the older men begin to talk about him. At the spring festival, he even beats the grown men. And one old man, called Philon, comes to talk to his father.

[pause]

Philon was once a runner himself. Many years ago, as a young man, he ran at Olympia. He did not win. But he has never forgotten it. Now he is old and grey, and he walks with a stick. He sits by the fire in the house of Lykos, and he says that the boy should go to Olympia. To run in the race for boys. He says that he has never seen a boy run like that.

[long-pause]

{img:old-trainer-and-father-talking-by-hearth-fire-stone-house-evening}
At first, the father of Lykos says no. Olympia is far away. It costs money to travel there, and to stay for many weeks. And who will look after the goats? But Philon talks to him for a long time. He says that he will train the boy himself, for nothing. He says that if Lykos wins, the whole village will be proud. And he says that Zeus himself has given the boy those fast legs, so it would be wrong not to use them.

[pause]

In the end, the father agrees. Lykos can hardly believe it. That night, he lies awake on his straw mattress, looking at the dark beams of the roof. He thinks about Olympia. He has heard so many stories about it. The great temple. The crowds. The famous runners. And the olive wreath, the prize that every athlete dreams of.

[pause]

{img:old-man-coaching-boy-runner-dusty-field-dawn-stick-pointing}
So the training begins. Every morning, before he takes the goats out, Lykos goes down to the flat field by the stream. Philon is already waiting for him, leaning on his stick. He makes Lykos run short races, again and again, as fast as he can. He makes him run up the steep hillside, until his legs burn. He teaches him how to start quickly, and how to lean forward at the end.

[pause]

Philon also tells him what to eat. Lots of bread and porridge, cheese and figs, and a little meat when there is some. Not too much wine. And plenty of sleep. He teaches Lykos to rub his body with olive oil before he runs, to keep his muscles supple. And he tells him stories, in the evenings, about the famous runners of the past.

[long-pause]

Little Theano is eleven years old. She often comes down to the field to watch her brother train. Sometimes she runs beside him, laughing, her hair flying. She is fast too, almost as fast as Lykos. But she knows that girls cannot run in the Olympic Games. Philon tells her that there is another festival at Olympia, for girls, in honour of the goddess Hera. Theano's eyes go wide. From that day on, she trains too.

[pause]

The training lasts for many months, through the autumn, the winter and the spring. The days get shorter and then longer again. Snow falls on the mountains, and melts. The almond trees blossom. And slowly, Lykos becomes even faster. He is ready. Now he only has to wait for the heralds to come.

[long-pause]

{img:olympic-heralds-truce-bearers-white-robes-olive-wreaths-arriving-greek-town}
In the spring of the Olympic year, three men set out from the town of Elis. Elis is the city that looks after the sanctuary of Olympia and organizes the Games. The three men are heralds. They wear white robes and wreaths of olive leaves on their heads. They carry staffs as a sign of their office. And they have a very important task.

[long-pause]

The heralds travel to every Greek city, near and far. Some of them sail across the sea, to the Greek cities of Italy, Sicily and Asia. In each city, they go to the market square and announce the Games. They tell the people the date of the festival. And they announce the sacred truce.

[pause]

The sacred truce is one of the most remarkable things about the Olympic Games. It is a promise of peace. During the time of the Games, nobody may attack the land of Elis, and nobody may harm the people travelling to Olympia or back home again. Even if two cities are at war, their athletes and visitors must be allowed to travel safely. The truce lasted for about a month, and later for even longer, so that people from far away could get home.

[pause]

{img:herald-announcing-in-agora-crowd-listening-market-stalls-columns}
When the heralds come to a city, people crowd around to hear them. The herald stands on a step and raises his staff. In a loud voice, he calls out the words of the truce. Let all Greeks hear. The festival of Zeus at Olympia is coming. Let there be peace. Let all who wish to go, go in safety. The people cheer. Everyone knows what it means. The Games are coming again.

[pause]

Was the truce always kept? Mostly, yes. The Greeks took it very seriously, because it was a promise made to Zeus. Breaking it meant angering the king of the gods. Once, a city sent soldiers into the land of Elis during the truce. It was punished with a heavy fine, and its people were not allowed to take part in the Games until the fine was paid. Most cities did not want to risk that.

[long-pause]

The heralds also come to the small towns of Arcadia. In the market of the nearest town, Lykos and his father hear them announce the festival. The Games will be held in the summer, at the second full moon after the longest day of the year. That is in the middle of the hot summer. And all athletes who wish to compete must come to Elis one month before the Games, to train under the eyes of the judges.

[pause]

Lykos feels a shiver of excitement run through him. It is really happening. In a few weeks, he will leave his village and travel to Elis. He has never been further than the market town. Now he will see the great sanctuary of Zeus, and the athletes from all over the world.

[pause]

{img:mother-packing-bundle-cloak-sandals-bread-boy-leaving-home-greek}
The weeks before he leaves are busy. His mother weaves him a new cloak of soft wool. His father buys him a little flask for oil, made of clay, and a curved bronze scraper. Athletes use it to scrape the oil and dust from their skin after training. His grandmother gives him a small charm, a little clay figure of Zeus, to keep him safe. And Theano gives him a ribbon, woven by herself, to tie around his arm.

[long-pause]

On the morning he leaves, the whole village comes out to see him off. The women give him bread and cheese and figs for the road. The men slap him on the back and wish him luck. The children run beside him to the edge of the village. His father will travel with him, and so will old Philon, on a donkey. His mother hugs him tightly and tells him to come home safe, whether he wins or not.

[pause]

{img:father-son-old-man-donkey-leaving-village-sunrise-mountain-path}
They set off down the mountain path, in the cool of the early morning. At the bend in the road, Lykos turns around for one last look. His mother is still standing at the gate, with Theano beside her. They wave. Lykos waves back. And then the path turns, and the village is gone behind the trees.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-elis-town-gymnasium-colonnade-athletes-training-dust-afternoon}
After four days of walking, Lykos, his father and old Philon arrive in Elis. It is a busy town, on a wide plain near the sea, about fifty kilometres north of Olympia. In this month before the Games, Elis is full of athletes. They have come from every part of the Greek world. Lykos has never seen so many strong young men in one place.

[long-pause]

All the athletes must stay in Elis for a whole month, and train together. This is one of the strict rules of the Games. During this month, they are watched by the judges. The judges are ten men from Elis, chosen for the job. They are called the Hellanodikai, which means the judges of the Greeks. They wear purple robes, and they carry long sticks. Everyone respects them, even the most famous athletes.

[pause]

The judges watch every athlete carefully. They want to see that each one is good enough to compete at Olympia. They also check that the boys really are boys, and not young men pretending to be younger. They look at their size, their faces and their voices. Some boys who look too old are sent to compete with the men instead. Lykos is a little nervous when the judges look at him. But they nod, and write his name on the list for the boys' race.

[pause]

{img:hellanodikai-judges-purple-robes-sticks-watching-young-runners-elis}
Elis has several training grounds. There is a big gymnasium, which is an open courtyard surrounded by covered walkways with columns. There is a running track, and a place for wrestling, with soft sand on the ground. Every day, from early morning, the athletes train there. The air is full of dust and shouting and the smell of olive oil.

[pause]

Greek athletes train and compete naked. This was normal for them. They saw the healthy, strong human body as something beautiful, a gift from the gods. Before training, each athlete rubs his whole body with olive oil. Then some of them sprinkle a fine dust over the oil, to protect the skin from the sun. Afterwards, they scrape it all off with their bronze scrapers, and wash in cold water.

[long-pause]

{img:athletes-oiling-skin-clay-flasks-bronze-strigil-courtyard-shade}
Lykos trains every day with the other boys. There are runners from Sparta, from Corinth, from Athens, and from the faraway Greek cities of Sicily and Italy. Some of them are friendly. Some of them are proud, and do not talk to a shepherd boy from the mountains. One boy from Sicily, called Dion, becomes his friend. Dion is the son of a rich family, and has travelled across the sea in a big ship. He has never seen a goat up close.

[pause]

The training is hard. The judges are strict. They watch the athletes run and wrestle, and they shout at anyone who is lazy. Sometimes they use their sticks, to tap an athlete who breaks a rule. Philon watches Lykos from the side of the track, and gives him advice every evening. Start faster. Keep your head still. Do not look at the other runners. Just run.

[pause]

In the evenings, the athletes eat together and talk. They tell stories about famous champions. There is Milo of Kroton, a wrestler who won at Olympia six times. People said he once carried a young bull on his shoulders around the whole stadium. And there are runners who won the short race and the long race at the same Games. The boys listen with shining eyes. They only dream of winning one wreath.

[long-pause]

Lykos misses home. He misses his mother and his sister, and the quiet of the mountains. Elis is noisy and crowded. At night, he lies on his mat and listens to the sounds of the town. Dogs barking, people laughing, carts rolling past. He holds the little clay figure of Zeus in his hand. And he thinks of the green valley at home, and the goat bells, and the stream.

[pause]

{img:boy-lying-awake-night-holding-small-clay-figure-oil-lamp-elis}
But the month passes quickly. The athletes grow stronger and faster. And at last, at the end of the month, the judges announce the final list of who may compete. Lykos is on it. The next morning, all the athletes, the judges and many others will set out together on the road to Olympia. The Games are about to begin.

[long-pause]

{img:procession-elis-to-olympia-judges-athletes-horses-road-summer-morning}
Early in the morning, a great procession leaves Elis. At the front walk the judges, in their purple robes. Behind them come the athletes, and their trainers, fathers and brothers. Then come the horses and chariots, for the horse races. And then a long line of people on foot, on donkeys and in carts. It is a holy procession, as well as a journey.

[long-pause]

The road to Olympia runs south along the coast, for about fifty kilometres. It takes two days. The summer sun is hot, and the road is dusty. On one side, there is the sparkling sea. On the other side, there are olive groves, vineyards and fields of golden wheat. Cicadas sing loudly in the trees. Lykos walks beside his father and Dion, carrying his bundle on his back.

[pause]

On the way, the procession stops at a sacred spring. There, the judges make a sacrifice of a pig, and the athletes wash themselves in the clear water. It is a way of making themselves pure before they enter the holy place of Zeus. Lykos splashes the cold water over his face and arms. It feels wonderful after the long, hot walk.

[pause]

{img:sacred-spring-athletes-washing-cool-water-plane-trees-shade}
The road is crowded. Thousands of people are travelling to Olympia. There are rich men in fine chariots, with servants walking beside them. There are farmers on donkeys, and poor people on foot, carrying food in baskets. There are merchants with carts full of goods to sell. There are families, and groups of friends, singing as they walk.

[pause]

There are also official delegations, sent by the cities. Each important city sends a group of its leading citizens, to make sacrifices to Zeus and to show the wealth of their city. Some of them have beautiful horses, and golden cups, and animals for the sacrifice. They want everyone to see how rich and powerful their city is.

[long-pause]

{img:crowds-travelling-road-donkeys-carts-families-olive-groves-dust}
People come from much further away too. Ships from Italy and Sicily have landed at the nearby coast, full of visitors. Some have travelled for weeks across the sea. They have come from Marseille in France, from Cyrene in Africa, and from the Greek cities on the shores of the Black Sea. They speak Greek with many different accents. Lykos can hardly understand some of them.

[pause]

Thanks to the sacred truce, the roads are safe. Even people whose cities are enemies walk side by side on the road to Olympia. A man from Athens shares his water with a man from Corinth. A Spartan helps a family from Thebes to fix the wheel of their cart. For these few weeks, everyone is simply a Greek, travelling to the festival of Zeus.

[pause]

{img:first-view-olympia-valley-temples-roofs-glinting-sun-river-pine-hill}
On the second day, in the afternoon, the procession comes over a low hill. And there, below them, is the valley of Olympia. Lykos stops and stares. He has heard about it all his life, but he never imagined it would look like this. The river Alpheios shining in the sun. The pine-covered Hill of Kronos. And at its foot, the sanctuary, with its temples, statues and altars, gleaming white and gold among the green trees.

[long-pause]

All around the sanctuary, the valley is already filling up with people. There are tents and huts everywhere, and the smoke of a thousand cooking fires. The sound of the crowd rises up from the valley, like the sound of the sea. Old Philon puts his hand on Lykos's shoulder. There it is, he says quietly. Olympia. And the old man's eyes are wet with tears.

[pause]

That evening, they find a place for their little tent near the river, among the other people from Arcadia. Some of them are from villages close to their own. They greet each other like old friends, and share their bread and olives. An old woman gives Lykos a handful of dried figs, and tells him to eat them on the morning of the race. They bring luck, she says. Lykos thanks her, and puts them carefully in his bundle.

[long-pause]

{img:altis-sacred-grove-olympia-temples-treasuries-statues-plane-trees-morning}
The next morning, Lykos goes with Philon to see the sanctuary. It is called the Altis, which means the grove. It is surrounded by a low wall. Inside the wall, everything is sacred to the gods. There are tall plane trees and olive trees, giving cool shade. And between the trees, there are temples, altars and hundreds of statues.

[long-pause]

The oldest temple is the Temple of Hera, the wife of Zeus. It stands near the foot of the Hill of Kronos. It is long and low, with thick columns. Long ago, its columns were made of wood. When a wooden column became old and rotten, it was replaced with a stone one. So now the columns are all slightly different from each other, some thick and some thin. It looks a little old-fashioned, but very peaceful.

[pause]

In front of the Temple of Hera, there is a large altar. And nearby is the most sacred place of all, the great altar of Zeus. It does not look like an altar at all. It is a huge mound of ash, taller than a man. For hundreds of years, the ashes of the sacrifices have been piled up here, mixed with water from the river, and pressed together. So the altar grows a little higher every year.

[pause]

{img:temple-of-hera-olympia-old-columns-different-sizes-afternoon-shade}
Along the bottom of the Hill of Kronos, there is a row of small buildings, like little temples. They are the treasuries. Each one was built by a different city, to keep its gifts to Zeus safe. There is a treasury of Sikyon, of Syracuse, of Megara, of Gela and of many others. Inside, there are golden cups, bronze shields, statues and other precious things. Each city wants its treasury to be the most beautiful.

[pause]

And everywhere, there are statues. Statues of gods and heroes. Statues of horses and bulls. And statues of Olympic champions. A winner at Olympia was allowed to set up a statue of himself in the sanctuary. Over the years, there have come to be hundreds of them, in bronze and stone. Lykos walks among them, looking up at the faces of the old champions. He reads their names, carved into the stone bases.

[long-pause]

{img:bronze-statues-olympic-champions-stone-bases-boy-looking-up-sunlight}
Near the entrance to the stadium, there is a row of bronze statues of Zeus. Philon explains what they are. They were paid for with the fines of athletes who cheated. Each one has a little poem carved on its base, saying who cheated and how. It is a warning to every athlete who walks into the stadium. At Olympia, you must win with the speed of your feet and the strength of your body, not with money or tricks.

[pause]

Lykos looks at the statues and feels a little afraid. He would never cheat. But he understands how seriously the Games are taken here. This is not just a race. It is a gift to Zeus. And Zeus is watching.

[pause]

{img:stadium-entrance-vaulted-tunnel-olympia-grassy-banks-track-beyond}
Then Philon takes him to see the stadium itself. It lies just outside the sanctuary, to the east. To get there, you walk through a long tunnel with a stone roof. When Lykos comes out at the other end, the stadium opens in front of him. A long, flat track of hard, pale earth, almost two hundred metres long. On both sides, there are grassy banks, sloping up. There are no seats. The spectators sit or stand on the grass. Up to forty thousand people can fit here.

[long-pause]

At each end of the track, there is a line of stone slabs, set into the ground. These are the starting lines. Each slab has two grooves cut into it, for the toes of the runners. Lykos kneels down and touches the grooves with his fingers. Thousands of runners have stood here before him. In a few days, he will stand here too. His heart beats faster at the thought.

[pause]

Philon smiles at him. He tells Lykos to walk the track, slowly, from one end to the other. To feel the earth under his feet. To look at the hill and the sky. So that on the day of the race, it will feel like a familiar place. Lykos walks the whole length of the stadium, barefoot, in the morning sun. And when he reaches the other end, he turns around, and looks back, and he imagines the crowds cheering.

[long-pause]

{img:temple-of-zeus-olympia-huge-doric-columns-golden-ornaments-blue-sky}
In the middle of the sanctuary stands the greatest building of all. The Temple of Zeus. It is enormous, much bigger than any building Lykos has ever seen. It stands on a high platform of stone, with steps all around. Thirty-four huge columns hold up the roof. Each column is more than ten metres tall, and so thick that three men holding hands could hardly reach around it.

[long-pause]

The temple is made of local stone, covered with a smooth layer of white plaster, so that it looks like marble. The roof is made of real marble tiles. At the corners of the roof, there are golden statues of the goddess Victory, and golden bowls, shining in the sun. At the front and back of the temple, in the triangle under the roof, there are great carved scenes. One shows the chariot race of Pelops. The other shows a battle between people and wild centaurs, half-man and half-horse.

[pause]

All along the top of the walls, there are twelve carved panels. They show the twelve great labours of Heracles. Heracles fighting the lion. Heracles cleaning the stables. Heracles holding up the sky. The carvings are painted in bright colours, red, blue and gold. Lykos walks slowly around the temple, looking up at the pictures, one after another, like the pages of a storybook.

[pause]

{img:temple-zeus-carved-metope-heracles-labours-painted-colours-close}
Inside the temple, it is dark and cool. As Lykos's eyes get used to the dim light, he sees it. At the far end of the hall, there is a giant statue of Zeus, sitting on a throne. It is so big that his head almost touches the roof. People say that if Zeus stood up, he would break through the ceiling.

[pause]

The statue is made by the most famous sculptor in Greece, a man from Athens called Phidias. He has only just finished it. The body of Zeus is made of ivory, pale and smooth like skin. His robe is made of gold, decorated with little flowers and animals. In his right hand, he holds a small statue of Victory. In his left hand, he holds a tall staff with an eagle on top. On his head, there is a wreath of golden olive leaves.

[long-pause]

{img:great-statue-zeus-gold-ivory-throne-dim-temple-light-awe-small-people}
The throne of Zeus is made of gold, ivory, ebony and precious stones. It is decorated with carvings of gods, heroes and animals. In front of the statue, on the floor, there is a shallow pool of olive oil. The oil keeps the ivory from cracking in the dry air. And it also reflects the light from the doorway, so that the golden statue seems to glow in the darkness.

[pause]

Lykos stands very still and looks up at the face of Zeus. It is calm and kind, and very wise. Not angry, not frightening. Just quietly powerful, like a good king or a loving father. Around him, other visitors are standing in silence too. Some of them are praying. Some of them have tears in their eyes. Nobody speaks.

[pause]

{img:pilgrims-standing-silent-before-zeus-statue-oil-pool-reflection-glow}
This statue will become famous all over the ancient world. It will be called one of the Seven Wonders of the World. People will travel for months to see it. A Greek writer will say that a man who has seen it can never be completely unhappy again. And it will stand in this temple for more than eight hundred years.

[long-pause]

Lykos takes out the little clay figure of Zeus his grandmother gave him. It is rough and simple, made by a village potter. He holds it in his hand and looks from the little Zeus to the great Zeus. Then he says a quiet prayer. He does not pray to win. Philon told him not to do that. He prays to run well, and to make his family proud, and to come home safe.

[pause]

When he comes out of the temple, the bright sunlight dazzles him. He blinks. The sanctuary is busy and noisy again, full of people and voices. But inside him, there is a feeling of calm, which stays with him for the rest of the day.

[long-pause]

{img:tent-city-olympia-thousands-tents-cooking-fires-crowds-river-evening}
Outside the walls of the sanctuary, the whole valley has become a huge camp. Tens of thousands of people have come to the Games. And there are almost no buildings for them to stay in. So they sleep in tents, in huts made of branches, or simply under the open sky, wrapped in their cloaks.

[long-pause]

The rich visitors have big, colourful tents, with carpets and cushions inside. The official delegations from the cities have the best places, close to the sanctuary. Some of them bring their own cooks and servants, and give big dinners every night. The poorer visitors sleep wherever they can find a space. Along the river, under the trees, on the hillsides. Lykos, his father and Philon have a small tent of goatskin, near the river, among the people from Arcadia.

[pause]

The camp is crowded and noisy. And in the middle of the summer, it is very hot. The sun beats down all day long. There is not enough shade, and not enough water. There are flies everywhere. People queue at the springs and wells with their jars. Some people even complain about it. One Greek writer later joked that a master once threatened his lazy slave, saying he would take him to Olympia as a punishment.

[pause]

{img:market-stalls-olympia-camp-merchants-selling-food-wine-souvenirs}
But most people do not mind the heat and the crowds. They are here for the Games, and for the festival. And the camp is full of life. Everywhere, there are merchants selling things. Bread, cheese, olives, figs, grilled meat and wine. Little clay figures of Zeus and of athletes, as souvenirs. Pots and cups, jewellery, sandals and hats against the sun. The merchants shout out their prices, and people bargain with them.

[pause]

There are also entertainers. Jugglers throw balls and knives into the air. Acrobats do somersaults. Musicians play the flute and the lyre. Fortune tellers sit on little stools, offering to tell people's futures. And there are men who sell fresh water, carried from the river in big jars on their backs. On hot afternoons, they sell a great deal of it.

[long-pause]

{img:poet-reciting-crowd-listening-shade-tree-olympia-scroll}
In the shade of the trees, there are speakers and poets. Olympia is the best place in the world to become famous, because people from every Greek city are here. So writers and thinkers come to read their works aloud to the crowds. Painters show their pictures. Teachers give speeches about wisdom and politics. An old story says that the great historian Herodotus once read his history book aloud here, to a big crowd, and they loved it so much that his fame spread all over Greece.

[pause]

Lykos walks through the camp with Dion, his friend from Sicily. They look at everything, with wide eyes. Dion has a little money, and buys them each a honey cake. They sit on the riverbank and eat them, with their feet in the cool water. They talk about their homes, and about the race, and about what they will do afterwards.

[pause]

{img:two-boys-sitting-riverbank-feet-in-water-eating-honey-cakes-evening}
In the evening, the camp is lit up by thousands of little fires. The smell of cooking fills the air. People sit together in groups, eating and talking and singing. From the tents of the rich, there comes the sound of music and laughter. Someone is playing a sad, sweet melody on a flute, somewhere near the river. And above the valley, the moon is rising. It is almost full.

[long-pause]

Lykos lies in the little goatskin tent, between his father and Philon. He is tired, but he cannot sleep. Outside, the camp is slowly getting quieter. The fires burn down. The voices fade. A dog barks far away. An owl calls from the Hill of Kronos. Lykos thinks about the race, in two days' time. His heart beats a little faster. Then he hears his father breathing slowly beside him, and old Philon snoring softly. And at last, he too falls asleep.

[long-pause]

{img:bouleuterion-olympia-statue-zeus-horkios-thunderbolts-athletes-oath-morning}
On the first morning of the Games, all the athletes gather in a building near the sanctuary, called the council house. Inside, there is a statue of Zeus with a thunderbolt in each hand. He is called Zeus of Oaths. Of all the statues of Zeus at Olympia, he is the one most likely to frighten anyone who might be tempted to cheat.

[long-pause]

In front of the statue, a boar is sacrificed. Then the athletes swear an oath, one after another. So do their fathers and brothers and trainers. They swear that they have trained for ten months, as the rules demand. They swear that they will not cheat in any way. They swear that they will compete fairly, and accept the decisions of the judges. Lykos's voice shakes a little when it is his turn. But he says the words clearly.

[pause]

The judges also swear an oath. They promise to judge fairly, and not to accept any bribes. And they promise to keep secret what they know about the athletes, such as their true ages. Then the oath is finished. The Games have officially begun.

[pause]

{img:herald-contest-stadium-olympia-man-calling-loudly-crowd-grassy-bank}
On the first day, there are also two special contests. They are not for athletes, but for heralds and trumpeters. The heralds compete to see who has the loudest and clearest voice. The trumpeters compete to see who can blow the trumpet best. The winners will have important jobs during the Games. The winning herald will announce the names of the athletes and the winners. The winning trumpeter will give the signal for the start of the races.

[pause]

The crowd enjoys these contests very much. One after another, the heralds stand up on a little platform and shout out a few lines. Some of them have voices like thunder. Some of them squeak a little at the end, and the crowd laughs kindly. The winner is a big man from Megara, whose voice can be heard at the other end of the stadium. He bows, and everyone applauds.

[long-pause]

{img:people-praying-altars-olympia-offerings-flowers-honey-cakes-smoke}
For the rest of the first day, people visit the sanctuary, pray and make offerings. The athletes go to the altars of the gods and heroes, to ask for help. Some of them sacrifice animals. Others offer cakes, wine or honey. Some ask the priests to look into the future for them. Old Philon takes Lykos to a little altar of Hermes, the god of speed, and they offer a small honey cake together.

[pause]

There are many priests and helpers in the sanctuary. They look after the altars and temples, and help people with their sacrifices. There are even special priests, whose families have been seers at Olympia for many generations. They read signs in the sacrifices, to tell what the gods want. Athletes and generals from all over Greece come to ask their advice.

[pause]

{img:sunset-hill-of-kronos-pine-trees-golden-light-sanctuary-quiet}
In the late afternoon, Lykos goes back to the tent to rest. Tomorrow is the day of the boys' contests. Philon tells him to eat a good supper and go to sleep early. He eats bread and cheese and a little grilled meat. Then he lies down in the tent and closes his eyes.

[long-pause]

His father sits outside the tent for a long time, looking at the evening sky. He is a simple shepherd. He never imagined that his son would one day run at Olympia. He thinks about his wife and his daughter, at home in the mountains. And he thinks that, whatever happens tomorrow, he is the proudest father in all of Greece.

[long-pause]

{img:stadium-olympia-morning-crowds-grassy-banks-boys-waiting-start-line}
On the morning of the second day, Lykos wakes up before sunrise. For a moment, he does not remember where he is. Then he hears the sounds of the camp, and he remembers. Today is the day of the boys' race. His stomach is full of butterflies. He can hardly eat his breakfast. Philon makes him eat a little bread with honey anyway.

[long-pause]

The boys' contests are held in the stadium. There is the boys' race, boys' wrestling and boys' boxing. Early in the morning, the crowd begins to fill the grassy banks. The people bring cushions to sit on, and hats against the sun, and jars of water. Soon the banks are completely full. Thousands and thousands of people, talking, laughing, eating and waiting.

[pause]

On one side of the stadium, there is a little stone platform. This is where the judges sit, in their purple robes. On the other side, there is an altar. And beside the altar sits a woman, dressed in white. She is the priestess of the goddess Demeter. She is the only married woman who is allowed to watch the Games. All the others must stay away.

[pause]

Lykos stands at the end of the track with the other boys. There are about twenty of them. They are oiled and ready. The judges have divided them into groups, by drawing lots from a silver jar. Each group will run a race. And the winners of each group will run again, in a final race. Lykos is in the third group. Dion is in the first.

[pause]

Lykos watches Dion run. The trumpet sounds. The boys run. Dion runs well, but he is beaten by a tall boy from Sparta. He comes back with his head down. Lykos puts his hand on his shoulder. Dion smiles bravely and says, now you must win for both of us.

[long-pause]

{img:boys-crouched-stone-starting-line-toes-in-grooves-tense-moment}
Then it is Lykos's turn. He walks to the starting line, with five other boys. He puts his toes into the grooves in the stone, as Philon taught him. He leans forward, with his arms stretched out in front of him. He looks down the long track. It seems very long, and very bright in the morning sun. The crowd goes quiet.

[pause]

Lykos breathes in slowly. He thinks of the mountains, and the goats, and the green field by the stream. He thinks of his mother, and Theano. He can feel his heart beating in his chest. The herald calls out the names of the runners. Lykos, son of Kleon, from Arcadia. Lykos does not hear it. He is listening only for the trumpet.

[pause]

{img:boy-runners-sprinting-stadium-dust-flying-crowd-standing-cheering}
The trumpet sounds. And Lykos runs. He runs the way he has always run, in the mountains, after the goats. Light and fast, his feet barely touching the ground. He does not look at the other boys. He just runs. The crowd roars. The end of the track comes closer and closer. And Lykos crosses the line first, two steps ahead of everyone else.

[pause]

He can hardly believe it. He has won his group. But it is not over yet. There is still the final. Philon gives him water and tells him to rest, and breathe slowly. Lykos sits down in the shade, his heart pounding. He looks at the other winners. One of them is the tall Spartan boy, who beat Dion.

[long-pause]

The final race comes in the afternoon. Six boys stand at the starting line. The crowd is louder now. The judges lean forward on their platform. The trumpet sounds. And the boys are off. This time, the Spartan boy is fast, very fast. Halfway down the track, he is ahead. Lykos runs harder than ever before. His legs burn. His chest burns. Slowly, slowly, he catches up. And at the very end, he leans forward, as Philon taught him, and crosses the line, half a step in front.

[pause]

{img:boy-winner-palm-branch-red-ribbons-judges-crowd-joy-afternoon}
For a moment, there is silence. Then the herald stands up, and calls out in his great voice. The winner of the boys' race is Lykos, son of Kleon, from Arcadia! The crowd cheers. A judge gives Lykos a palm branch, the sign of a winner. People throw flowers and ribbons. His father runs down onto the track and lifts him up in his arms. Old Philon is crying openly. And Dion is jumping up and down on the bank, shouting his name. The olive wreath will be given on the last day. But today, Lykos is a champion of Olympia.

[long-pause]

{img:hippodrome-olympia-wide-track-crowds-banks-chariots-lining-up-morning}
The third day begins with the horse races. They are held in a place called the hippodrome, south of the stadium. It is a huge, long, open field, much bigger than the stadium. Around it, there are grassy banks, where the crowds sit and watch. At each end of the track, there is a post, and the horses must race around the posts again and again.

[long-pause]

The horse races are the most exciting events of the Games. And they are also the most expensive. Only very rich people can afford to keep racing horses and chariots. Kings, nobles and wealthy families from all over the Greek world send their best horses to Olympia. But they do not drive the chariots themselves. They pay skilled drivers to do it for them. And yet, when a chariot wins, it is the owner who is crowned as the winner, not the driver.

[pause]

Because of this, a woman could win at Olympia too. Not as a driver, but as the owner of the horses. Some years after our story, a Spartan princess called Kyniska will do exactly that. Her chariot will win the race, twice. She will be the first woman to become an Olympic champion. She will set up a statue at Olympia, with proud words carved on the base.

[pause]

{img:starting-gate-hippodrome-bronze-eagle-dolphin-chariots-horses-waiting}
The start of the chariot race is a wonderful sight. There is a clever starting gate, shaped like the front of a ship. Each chariot waits in its own stall, with a rope across the front. On top of the gate, there is a bronze eagle. When the race begins, the eagle rises into the air, and a bronze dolphin drops down. Then the ropes are pulled away, one after another, so that the chariots on the outside start first. It is a fair start for everyone.

[pause]

The most important race is the four-horse chariot race. Each chariot is a small, light cart on two wheels, pulled by four horses side by side. The driver stands in the cart, holding the reins in his hands. He wears a long white robe, which flutters in the wind. Sometimes there are more than forty chariots in a single race.

[long-pause]

{img:four-horse-chariots-racing-hippodrome-dust-cloud-drivers-white-robes}
The trumpet sounds, and the chariots burst out of the gate. The ground shakes with the thunder of hooves. A cloud of dust rises into the air. The crowd jumps to its feet, shouting. The chariots race down the long track, toward the far post. They must race around it twelve times. That is about fourteen kilometres. It is a long, hard race.

[pause]

The most dangerous moments are at the turning posts. All the chariots try to turn as close to the post as possible, to save time. Sometimes they crash into each other, and the drivers fall out. It is a race where drivers need to be brave and very skilful. Lykos holds his breath at every turn. But on this day, the drivers are careful, and the horses are well trained.

[pause]

{img:chariot-turning-around-post-close-horses-leaning-skilful-driver}
Halfway through the race, a chariot from Sicily is in the lead. It belongs to a rich family from Syracuse. Its horses are beautiful, all four of them black, with white feet. But slowly, a chariot from Athens comes closer. The two chariots race side by side, lap after lap. The crowd is screaming. And on the last turn, the Athenian chariot cuts in close to the post, and wins by the length of a horse.

[long-pause]

After the chariot race, there is also a race for horses with riders. The riders are boys, very light, and they ride without saddles or stirrups. They hold on to the horses' manes, and bend low over their necks. Their horses race once around the track, as fast as the wind. It is over very quickly. Then the crowd goes back to the stadium, hot and dusty and very happy. It has been a wonderful morning.

[pause]

Lykos goes to look at the horses afterwards, in the shade of the trees where they are resting. They are the most beautiful animals he has ever seen. Tall and slender, with shining coats and proud heads. Grooms are brushing them and giving them water. One of the black horses from Syracuse puts its soft nose into Lykos's hand. He strokes it gently, and thinks of the old donkey at home, who would be very surprised to see such a cousin.

[long-pause]

{img:pentathlon-stadium-afternoon-athletes-discus-javelin-jump-crowd}
In the afternoon of the same day, the crowds go back to the stadium, for the pentathlon. Penta means five in Greek. So the pentathlon is a contest of five events. Running, jumping, throwing the discus, throwing the javelin and wrestling. The athletes who take part must be good at everything. They are not the fastest runners or the strongest wrestlers. But they are the best all-round athletes.

[long-pause]

The Greeks loved the pentathlon athletes. They said their bodies were the most beautiful of all, because they were strong and fast and graceful at the same time. Sculptors liked to use them as models for their statues. One famous statue, called the Discus Thrower, shows a pentathlon athlete at the moment just before he throws.

[pause]

The first event is the discus. The discus is a flat, round plate of bronze or stone. It is quite heavy. The athlete stands at a line, holding the discus in one hand. He swings it back, twists his whole body, and then spins around and lets it fly. The discus sails through the air and lands with a thud in the dust. A judge puts a little peg in the ground where it landed. Each athlete throws five times, and his best throw counts.

[pause]

{img:discus-thrower-athlete-twisting-body-bronze-disc-sunlight-stadium}
The second event is the long jump. But it is different from the long jump today. The athletes hold a weight in each hand. The weights are made of stone or metal, shaped to fit the hand. When the athlete jumps, he swings the weights forward, to carry him further. Just before he lands, he swings them back again. Some people think the athletes made several small jumps in a row. Nobody is quite sure today.

[pause]

To help the athletes jump in rhythm, a man plays the flute while they jump. It must have looked rather lovely. The soft music of the flute, the athlete running and swinging his weights, and then rising up into the air like a bird. When he lands, he must leave clear footprints in the soft earth. If he falls over, the jump does not count.

[long-pause]

{img:long-jumper-holding-stone-weights-mid-air-flute-player-beside}
The third event is the javelin. The javelin is a long, light spear, made of wood. Around the middle of it, there is a little loop of leather. The athlete puts two fingers in the loop. When he throws, the loop makes the javelin spin through the air. So it flies further and straighter. The athletes throw at a target, or for distance, and the crowd cheers each good throw.

[pause]

The fourth event is a running race, the length of the stadium. And the fifth and last event is wrestling. But often, the wrestling is not needed. Because if one athlete has already won three of the first four events, he is the winner of the whole pentathlon. Then the contest is over.

[pause]

On this afternoon, the pentathlon is won by a young man from the island of Aegina. He is the best at the discus and the javelin, and he is very fast. He wins three events, so he does not have to wrestle. The crowd cheers, and the herald calls out his name. His family and friends rush onto the track, throwing ribbons and leaves over him.

[long-pause]

Lykos watches the pentathlon with great interest. He has never seen a discus or a javelin before. In the mountains, there were only stones, which the boys threw at targets. He decides that, when he gets home, he will try it. He will find a flat stone for a discus, and a straight branch for a javelin. And he will teach Theano too.

[pause]

{img:boy-watching-from-grassy-bank-chin-on-hands-dreaming-afternoon}
By now, the news of his victory has spread through the camp. People recognise him as he walks by. They point at him and smile. Look, there is the shepherd boy who won the boys' race. A man from his own region, Arcadia, comes to shake his hand. He says that all of Arcadia is proud of him. Lykos blushes and does not know what to say. He is still just a shepherd's son from a small village in the mountains. But now, he is also something more.

[long-pause]

{img:full-moon-rising-over-olympia-sanctuary-procession-torches-evening}
On the evening of the third day, the full moon rises over the valley of Olympia. It is huge and golden, coming up slowly over the hills in the east. Its light shines on the river and the temples, and on the white robes of the people walking in a great procession. This is the most sacred moment of the whole festival.

[long-pause]

The procession begins at a building where the judges and the priests have gathered. At the front walk the priests and the judges in their robes. Then come the official delegations from all the Greek cities, carrying golden cups, bowls and other gifts. Then come the athletes, the winners walking first. Lykos walks among them, carrying his palm branch. And then come a hundred oxen, white and brown, with ribbons and garlands of flowers on their horns.

[pause]

The procession winds slowly through the sanctuary. Musicians play flutes and lyres. Choirs of boys sing hymns to Zeus. The air is full of the smell of incense and flowers. Thousands of people stand along the way, watching in silence. In the moonlight, everything looks silver and mysterious.

[pause]

{img:procession-oxen-garlands-flowers-priests-white-robes-moonlight-temple}
The procession comes to the great altar of Zeus, the tall mound of ash. Here, the sacrifice takes place. A hundred oxen are offered to Zeus. This is called a hecatomb, which means a hundred. It is the biggest sacrifice of the whole Greek year. We will not watch it closely. For the Greeks, it was a holy act of thanks, and the most important moment of the Games.

[pause]

Only the thigh bones of the animals are burned on the altar, wrapped in fat. The smoke rises up into the night sky, toward the gods. The Greeks believe that Zeus enjoys the smell of the smoke. The rest of the meat is cooked over huge fires. And it is shared with the people at a great feast. For many poor people in the crowd, this is one of the few times in the year that they eat meat.

[long-pause]

{img:great-ash-altar-zeus-smoke-rising-night-sky-moon-crowd-silhouettes}
The fires burn all night. All around the sanctuary, people sit together and eat, and drink wine, and sing. The camp is full of light and music. People who do not know each other share their food and their wine. People from cities that are enemies sit side by side. On this night, under the full moon, everyone is one big family, the family of the Greeks.

[pause]

Lykos sits with his father, Philon and Dion by a fire near the river. They eat roasted meat with bread, and figs and grapes. His father drinks a little more wine than usual, and starts to sing an old shepherd's song from Arcadia. Philon joins in, in his cracked old voice. Dion does not know the words, but he claps along. And Lykos laughs until his stomach hurts.

[pause]

{img:friends-sitting-by-campfire-river-moonlight-singing-feast-ancient-greek}
Later in the night, when the singing has faded, Lykos walks a little way along the river by himself. The moon is high now, small and white. Its light makes a silver path across the water. The Hill of Kronos is a dark shape against the stars. Far behind him, the fires of the camp are glowing, and he can hear the soft sound of a flute.

[long-pause]

He thinks about everything that has happened. The long walk from the mountains, the month in Elis, the race, the crowds, the great statue of Zeus. It all seems like a dream. He wishes his mother and Theano could be here, to see it all. But soon he will be home, and he will tell them everything. He stays by the river for a long time, listening to the water. Then he goes back to the tent, and sleeps more deeply than he has slept in weeks.

[pause]

Before dawn, the camp is completely still. The fires have burned down to grey ashes. The moon is sinking behind the hills in the west. In the sanctuary, a priest walks quietly to the great altar of Zeus, and looks at the last thin trail of smoke rising into the pale sky. Then the first birds begin to sing in the plane trees. And a new day begins in the valley of Olympia.

[long-pause]

{img:mens-stadion-race-olympia-crowd-packed-banks-runners-start-line-morning}
On the fourth day, there are the running races for men. And the most important of them is the stadion, the short race of one length of the track. It is the oldest event of the Games, the race that Koroibos won so long ago. The winner of the stadion is the most famous athlete of the whole festival. In fact, the whole four years until the next Games will be named after him.

[long-pause]

So the stadium is fuller than ever. People have come early in the morning, to find a good place on the banks. They sit packed together, shoulder to shoulder, in the hot sun. There is no space left on the grass. Some people have climbed the Hill of Kronos, to watch from between the pine trees. The noise of the crowd rises up into the sky.

[pause]

Lykos sits on the bank with Dion and his father. He is a winner now, so people make room for him. He watches the runners walk onto the track. They are grown men, tall and strong, with long legs. The most famous of them is a runner from Corinth, who won the race four years ago. Everyone expects him to win again. But there is also a young runner from the island of Rhodes, who is said to be very fast.

[pause]

{img:grown-men-runners-oiled-crouching-start-stadium-crowd-hushed}
First, there are the heats, just as in the boys' race. Then, in the late morning, comes the final. Six men stand at the starting line. The herald calls out their names, one by one. The crowd cheers for each of them. Then there is silence. Forty thousand people, completely silent. Lykos can hear a bird singing in a tree, somewhere far away. He can hear his own heart.

[pause]

The trumpet sounds. The runners shoot forward. It is unbelievably fast. In just a few seconds, they are halfway down the track. The runner from Corinth is in front. But the young man from Rhodes is right beside him. Their feet hammer the earth. The crowd is on its feet, roaring. Lykos is shouting too, though he does not know who he is shouting for.

[long-pause]

{img:two-runners-neck-and-neck-finish-line-stadium-dust-crowd-roaring}
At the very end, the young man from Rhodes leans forward and crosses the line first. It is so close that the crowd is not sure. Everyone looks at the judges. The judges talk together for a moment. Then the herald stands up and calls out the name of the man from Rhodes. A great roar goes up from the crowd. The runner from Corinth smiles, and shakes the winner's hand. It was a fair and wonderful race.

[pause]

After the stadion, there are the longer races. First the diaulos, which is two lengths of the stadium, there and back. The runners race to the far end, turn around a post, and race back again. Then the dolichos, the long race. It is about twenty lengths of the stadium, almost four kilometres. The runners must be patient, and save their strength for the end.

[pause]

The long race is very different from the short one. The runners go round and round, at a steady pace. For a long time, nothing much seems to happen. The crowd talks and eats and drinks. Then, in the last laps, the runners begin to speed up. One of them breaks away from the others. The crowd notices and starts to shout. And at the end, a thin, wiry runner from Crete wins, far ahead of everyone else.

[long-pause]

The last running race of the Games is the strangest of all. It is the race in armour. The runners wear bronze helmets on their heads, and carry heavy round shields on their arms. They run two lengths of the stadium, there and back, clanking loudly. It is a race that reminds everyone of war. But here, at Olympia, it is only a race, and the crowd laughs and cheers as the runners clatter past.

[pause]

{img:race-in-armour-helmets-round-shields-runners-clanking-crowd-laughing}
It is the end of the running. Lykos has watched every race, from beginning to end. He has seen the fastest men in the world. And he has learned something important. Even the great champions are nervous before the start. Even they feel their hearts pounding. Old Philon nods when Lykos tells him this. Yes, he says. That is the secret. Everyone is afraid. The champions are just the ones who run anyway.

[long-pause]

{img:wrestling-pit-sand-olympia-two-wrestlers-gripping-judges-watching-afternoon}
In the afternoon of the fourth day, there are the combat contests. Wrestling, boxing and the pankration, which is a mix of wrestling and boxing. These were some of the most popular events at Olympia. But they could also be rough, so we will watch them from a gentle distance, sitting high up on the grassy bank.

[long-pause]

Wrestling was the favourite sport of many Greeks. Almost every Greek town had a wrestling school, called a palaestra. Boys learned to wrestle from a young age. The goal was to throw your opponent to the ground three times. The wrestlers grab each other by the arms and shoulders, and push and pull and twist. It looks a little like a slow dance, until one of them suddenly throws the other into the sand.

[pause]

The great wrestlers were famous all over Greece. People told many stories about Milo of Kroton, who had won six times at Olympia, more than a hundred years before. They said he trained by carrying a little calf every day. As the calf grew bigger, so did his strength. Until, one day, he could carry a fully grown bull. Nobody knows if this story is true. But it is a good story, and Greek children loved it.

[pause]

{img:wrestling-school-palaestra-boys-learning-holds-teacher-courtyard}
Boxing at Olympia was different from boxing today. There were no gloves. Instead, the boxers wound long strips of soft leather around their hands and wrists. There were no rounds and no breaks. The boxers fought until one of them gave up, by raising one finger in the air. There were also no weight classes. So a big man might box against a smaller man.

[pause]

The pankration was the roughest contest of all. Almost everything was allowed, except biting and poking someone's eyes. It was a contest of great strength and courage. The athletes trained for years. And the crowd watched with great excitement. But we will let them have their contest, and look instead at the sky over the stadium, where the swallows are swooping in the warm air.

[long-pause]

{img:swallows-swooping-over-stadium-evening-sky-hill-of-kronos-pines}
The judges watch the combat contests very closely. They stand near the athletes, with their long sticks. If an athlete breaks a rule, the judge taps him with the stick, and the contest stops for a moment. The judges are very strict. They want the contests to be fair and honourable. And they want to make sure that nobody is seriously hurt.

[pause]

Sometimes, an athlete is so famous and so strong that nobody wants to compete against him. Then he is declared the winner without fighting at all. The Greeks had a special word for this. It meant winning without dust. Because the winner never had to lie down in the dusty sand. The crowds were a little disappointed when this happened. But the athlete got his olive wreath all the same.

[pause]

{img:victor-without-dust-athlete-standing-alone-sand-pit-crowd-applause}
Lykos does not enjoy the combat contests very much. He is a runner, not a fighter. He prefers the clean speed of the races. Dion feels the same. So in the middle of the afternoon, they leave the stadium and walk down to the river. They lie in the shade of a plane tree and talk. The sounds of the crowd come to them from far away, like the sound of distant waves.

[long-pause]

Dion tells Lykos about his home in Sicily. About the big city of Syracuse, with its harbour full of ships. About the great volcano, Mount Etna, which sometimes smokes and rumbles. About the lemon trees and the blue sea. He says that Lykos must come and visit him one day. Lykos laughs. He has never even seen the sea, except on the road to Olympia. But he promises that he will try.

[pause]

In the evening, when the contests are over, the boys walk back to the camp. The last of the sun is shining on the roof of the Temple of Zeus. Tomorrow is the last day of the Games. The day of the olive wreaths.

[long-pause]

{img:heraia-girls-race-olympia-short-tunics-one-shoulder-bare-stadium-ancient}
There is something we have not yet talked about. Where are the women and girls of Olympia? During the Games, married women were not allowed to watch the contests. This was an old rule, and it was strictly kept. But unmarried girls were allowed to watch. And the girls also had their own festival at Olympia, with their own races.

[long-pause]

This festival was called the Heraia, in honour of Hera, the wife of Zeus and the queen of the gods. It was held every four years, at a different time from the Olympic Games. It was organised by a group of sixteen women from Elis. Every four years, they wove a beautiful new robe for the statue of Hera in her temple. And they held races for girls, in the Olympic stadium.

[pause]

The girls ran in three groups, according to their age. The youngest girls ran first, then the older girls, and then the young women. They wore a short tunic, which came down to just above the knee, with one shoulder bare. They wore their hair loose, flowing behind them as they ran. Their track was a little shorter than the track of the men, about five-sixths of the length.

[pause]

{img:sixteen-women-weaving-robe-for-hera-loom-sunlit-room-elis}
The winners of the girls' races received prizes too. They were given an olive wreath, just like the men. They also received a share of the meat from a cow sacrificed to Hera. And they were allowed to put up a little painted picture of themselves in the Temple of Hera, with their names written beneath. Today, there is a small bronze statue in a museum, of a girl running, with one shoulder bare. She may be one of these young runners.

[pause]

Lykos learns about the Heraia from old Philon, during the Games. He has promised Theano that he will find out everything. So he goes to the Temple of Hera, and looks at the little pictures of the girl winners, hanging on the walls and columns. Some of them are old and faded. Some are new and bright. He reads their names. Then he goes to look at the girls' starting line in the stadium.

[long-pause]

{img:temple-of-hera-interior-small-painted-portraits-girl-winners-columns}
There is also a famous story about the old rule against married women. It happened some years after our story. There was a woman from the island of Rhodes, called Kallipateira. Her husband had died, and she trained her son to be a boxer. When her son went to Olympia, she wanted so much to see him that she dressed up as a trainer, a man. Her son won. And in her joy, she jumped over the fence to hug him, and everyone saw that she was a woman.

[pause]

According to the rule, she should have been punished. But the judges forgave her, because her father, her brothers and her son had all been Olympic champions. Instead, they made a new rule. From then on, the trainers had to come into the stadium without clothes, just like the athletes. It is a story that people at Olympia told and retold for hundreds of years, always with a smile.

[pause]

{img:girl-running-alone-mountain-meadow-flowers-hair-flying-free}
Many people think it was unfair that married women could not watch the Games. And they are right. But the girls of Greece did run. And in Sparta especially, girls trained in running and wrestling, just like the boys. The Spartans thought that strong girls would become strong mothers. And a Spartan princess, Kyniska, would one day become an Olympic champion herself, with her chariot.

[long-pause]

Lykos thinks of Theano, running beside him in the green field by the stream, laughing, with her hair flying. He thinks that one day, perhaps, she will run here, in the races of Hera. He decides that he will help her train, the way Philon helped him. And when the day comes, he will walk with her all the way to Olympia, and stand on the grassy bank, and cheer for her louder than anyone.

[long-pause]

{img:sacred-olive-tree-boy-golden-sickle-cutting-branches-morning-temple}
On the fifth and last day of the Games, there is the most beautiful ceremony of all. It is the crowning of the winners. Early in the morning, a boy walks to an old wild olive tree, which grows behind the Temple of Zeus. It is called the tree of the beautiful wreaths. The boy must be a child whose mother and father are both still alive. In his hand, he carries a small golden sickle.

[long-pause]

Carefully, the boy cuts branches from the tree. He cuts one branch for every winner of the Games. The olive branches are then bent into the shape of a ring, and tied, to make wreaths. Then the wreaths are laid out on a beautiful table, made of gold and ivory, in the Temple of Zeus. There they wait, in the cool darkness, at the feet of the great statue.

[pause]

The olive wreath is the only prize at Olympia. It has no value in money. In a few days, its leaves will dry and turn brown. But to the Greeks, it is the most precious prize in the world. Because it comes from the sacred tree of Zeus. And because it means that the gods have chosen you, out of all the Greeks, as the best.

[pause]

{img:winners-procession-to-temple-of-zeus-ribbons-palm-branches-crowd}
In the morning, the winners walk in a procession to the Temple of Zeus. They are dressed in fine clothes, with ribbons tied around their heads and arms. Their families walk beside them, singing. The crowd lines the way, cheering and throwing flowers and leaves. Lykos walks with the other winners. He wears a new white tunic, which his father bought for him in the market. Around his arm, he still wears Theano's ribbon.

[pause]

In front of the temple, the judges are waiting. The herald calls out the names of the winners, one by one. With each name, he calls out the name of the winner's father and his city. Then the winner steps forward. And the chief judge places the olive wreath on his head.

[long-pause]

{img:judge-placing-olive-wreath-on-boys-head-temple-steps-sunlight}
When the herald calls out the name of Lykos, son of Kleon, from Arcadia, Lykos steps forward. His legs are shaking more than they did before the race. The judge smiles at him, and places the wreath on his head. It is light and cool, and smells of leaves. The crowd cheers. Somewhere in the crowd, Lykos hears his father shouting his name. And old Philon, leaning on his stick, cheering in his cracked voice.

[pause]

Lykos looks out over the crowd, and the temples, and the green valley. He will remember this moment for the rest of his life. When he is an old man, he will close his eyes and see it again. The bright sunlight, the white columns, the faces of the crowd, and the smell of the olive leaves.

[pause]

{img:winners-standing-together-olive-wreaths-temple-columns-proud-calm}
After the crowning, the winners go into the temple, to thank Zeus. They make an offering at the feet of the great golden statue. Lykos takes the little clay figure of Zeus from his grandmother out of his bundle. He holds it up, so that it can see the great Zeus too. And he says thank you, very quietly, in his heart.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, the winners also have the right to set up a statue of themselves at Olympia. But statues are expensive. A bronze statue costs more than a shepherd earns in many years. So Lykos will not have a statue. But it does not matter. His name has been written in the list of winners, kept by the judges of Elis. It will stay there, with the names of all the other champions, back to Koroibos the cook.

[long-pause]

{img:victory-banquet-prytaneion-olympia-couches-tables-lamps-evening-winners}
In the evening of the last day, the winners are invited to a great banquet. It is held in a building called the prytaneion, near the sanctuary. Inside, there is a sacred fire, which burns day and night and is never allowed to go out. Around it, the winners lie on couches, as the Greeks liked to do at dinner, and eat a wonderful meal.

[long-pause]

Lykos has never been to a banquet before. He has never lain on a couch to eat. He feels very awkward at first, and does not know where to put his elbows. The man next to him, a famous wrestler, laughs and shows him how. Servants bring in plates of food. Roasted meat, fresh fish, bread, cheese, olives, honey cakes, grapes and figs. There is wine, mixed with water, as the Greeks always drink it.

[pause]

During the banquet, there is music and singing. Poets sing songs in honour of the winners. Some rich winners have paid famous poets to write special songs about their victory, called victory odes. The greatest of these poets was Pindar, from Thebes. He wrote beautiful, difficult poems, full of gods and heroes and shining light. People sang his odes at the victory feasts, with music and dancing.

[pause]

{img:chorus-singing-victory-ode-lyre-player-torchlight-courtyard}
One of Pindar's odes begins with words that the Greeks loved. Best of all things is water. But if you want to praise the games, look no further than the sun, and no further than Olympia. The singers at the banquet sing these words, and everybody raises their cup. Lykos does not understand all of the poem. But he understands the feeling of it. He feels it himself, in his heart.

[pause]

Outside, in the camp, there are also many parties. Each city celebrates its own winners. The people from Athens are celebrating the chariot race. The people from Rhodes are celebrating their runner. And the people from Arcadia have gathered by the river, around a big fire, to celebrate Lykos. When he comes out of the banquet, late in the evening, they cheer and lift him onto their shoulders.

[long-pause]

{img:arcadians-celebrating-riverbank-bonfire-dancing-boy-on-shoulders}
The Arcadians dance the old dances of the mountains, in a long line, holding hands. Someone plays the shepherd's pipes, the simple pipes made of reeds that Arcadian shepherds have played for thousands of years. Lykos's father dances too, with a big smile on his face. Old Philon claps the rhythm with his stick. And Dion, who is not from Arcadia at all, joins the line and dances with them, laughing.

[pause]

The party goes on late into the night. But at last, people begin to drift away to their tents. The fire burns down to glowing embers. Lykos sits beside it with his father, Philon and Dion. Nobody says very much. They are tired and happy, and a little sad, because tomorrow the Games are over, and everyone will go home.

[pause]

{img:four-figures-by-glowing-embers-night-river-stars-quiet-farewell}
Dion will sail back to Sicily, across the sea. Lykos will walk back to the mountains. They know they may never see each other again. Sicily is very far away. Dion takes a little bronze ring from his finger, and gives it to Lykos. To remember me, he says. Lykos has nothing to give back. So he gives Dion one of the leaves from his olive wreath. Dion holds it carefully, as if it were made of gold.

[long-pause]

The next morning, the valley of Olympia begins to empty. All over the camp, tents are taken down. Carts are loaded. Donkeys are packed. Long lines of people walk away along the roads, north and south and east, toward the coast and the mountains. By the evening, the valley will be quiet again. The merchants will be gone. The fires will be cold. And the sanctuary will be peaceful once more, with only the priests and the birds and the old trees, waiting for the next Games, four years from now.

[long-pause]

{img:boy-with-olive-wreath-walking-home-mountain-road-father-old-man-donkey}
The journey home takes five days. Lykos walks with his father and Philon, back through the hills and valleys of the Peloponnese. He carries his olive wreath carefully, wrapped in a soft cloth, so that the leaves will not break. Every evening, when they stop to rest, he takes it out and looks at it.

[long-pause]

The news of his victory has travelled faster than they have. In every village they pass through, people come out to see them. They have heard that a shepherd boy from Arcadia has won at Olympia. They bring them food and water, and invite them into their houses. Children run beside them, asking Lykos to show them the wreath. Old men shake his hand. Lykos is shy, but he shows them all.

[pause]

On the fifth day, in the late afternoon, they climb the last steep path toward the village. Lykos can see the stone houses on the green slope. He can hear the goat bells. He can smell the wood smoke from the chimneys. His heart is beating faster than it did before the race.

[pause]

{img:village-people-running-out-to-greet-champion-mountain-village-evening}
Then someone in the village sees them coming, and shouts. And suddenly, everyone is running out of the houses. Men, women, children, old people. They are cheering and waving branches and flowers. In front of them all runs a girl of eleven, with her hair flying behind her. It is Theano. She throws her arms around her brother so hard that they almost fall over.

[pause]

And then Lykos sees his mother, standing at the gate of their house, just where he left her. She does not run. She just stands there, with her hands pressed against her mouth, and tears running down her cheeks. Lykos walks to her slowly, and puts the olive wreath into her hands. She holds it, and then she holds him, for a long, long time.

[long-pause]

{img:mother-holding-olive-wreath-embracing-son-gate-stone-house-sunset}
In the old days, when an Olympic champion came home to a big city, sometimes the people broke down a part of the city wall, to let him in. They said that a city with such a champion did not need walls to protect it. Lykos's village has no walls. But the people make him a feast, in the little square under the big plane tree. Everyone brings food. And the old men tell the story of the race again and again, each time a little more exciting.

[pause]

Theano wants to hear everything. The journey, the month in Elis, the great statue of Zeus, the tent camp, the chariots. And most of all, the races of the girls in honour of Hera. Lykos tells her about the little painted pictures in the Temple of Hera, and the girls' starting line in the stadium. Theano listens with shining eyes. Then she says, very seriously, that she wants to start training tomorrow.

[pause]

{img:olive-wreath-hanging-on-wall-simple-stone-house-hearth-glow}
The olive wreath is hung on the wall of the house, above the fire. Slowly, its leaves dry and turn grey-green. But it stays there for many years, through many winters. Whenever visitors come, they look at it, and ask about it. And Lykos's grandmother always tells the story, very proudly, from the beginning to the end.

[long-pause]

And Lykos goes back to the goats. Every morning, before the sun rises, he takes them up into the mountains, as he always did. He runs after them over the rocks and through the bushes. But now, on many mornings, Theano runs beside him. And when she is fifteen, she runs at Olympia, in the race of the girls, in honour of Hera. Her brother walks with her all the way, and stands on the grassy bank, and cheers for her louder than anyone.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-era-olympia-new-buildings-bath-houses-aqueduct-fountain-later}
The Olympic Games continued for a very long time after Lykos. Every four years, the heralds went out to announce the sacred truce. Every four years, the athletes came to Elis, and the crowds came to the valley. The Games went on through good times and bad times, through wars and peace, for more than eight hundred years after our story.

[long-pause]

In the centuries that followed, Greece changed a great deal. First, the kings of Macedonia, in the north, became the masters of Greece. The most famous of them was Alexander the Great. Then, about two hundred years before Christ, the Romans came. They conquered Greece and made it part of their great empire. But the Romans admired Greek culture very much. And they did not stop the Olympic Games. They joined in.

[pause]

The Romans built new buildings at Olympia. Bath houses with warm water, guest houses for visitors, and a beautiful fountain with statues. One rich man built an aqueduct, to bring fresh water to the sanctuary. At last, the visitors no longer had to queue for water in the summer heat. Athletes now came from all over the Roman Empire, from Egypt, Syria and Spain. The Games became bigger than ever.

[pause]

{img:fountain-nymphaeum-olympia-water-flowing-statues-visitors-drinking}
Not every Roman behaved well at Olympia. The emperor Nero came to the Games in the year 67 after Christ. He wanted to win everything. He even had the Games moved by two years, so that he could compete. He entered the chariot race with ten horses. He fell out of his chariot and did not finish the race. But the judges gave him the olive wreath anyway, because he was the emperor. After he died, his name was removed from the list of winners.

[pause]

Slowly, the world changed again. Christianity spread through the Roman Empire. The old gods, Zeus and Hera and the others, were slowly forgotten. And the Games, which were a festival in honour of Zeus, began to seem out of place. The last Games that we know of were held around the year 393 after Christ, or perhaps a little later. After more than a thousand years, the Olympic Games came quietly to an end.

[long-pause]

{img:olympia-ruins-fallen-columns-earthquake-grass-growing-quiet-centuries}
The sanctuary was slowly abandoned. The great statue of Zeus was taken away to the city of Constantinople, far to the east. There, many years later, it was destroyed in a fire. At Olympia, the temples fell into ruin. Then, in the sixth century, great earthquakes shook the valley. The huge columns of the Temple of Zeus fell down. They still lie on the ground today, exactly where they fell, like giant stacks of coins that have toppled over.

[pause]

After that, the rivers did their work. The river Alpheios and the little river Kladeos flooded again and again. Each flood left a layer of sand and mud behind. Slowly, over hundreds of years, the whole sanctuary was buried. In some places, the ground rose by five or even seven metres. The temples, the statues, the stadium, everything disappeared under the earth.

[pause]

{img:buried-valley-olympia-fields-sheep-grazing-river-no-ruins-visible}
For more than a thousand years, Olympia was forgotten. Shepherds grazed their sheep on the green fields where the stadium had been. Farmers ploughed the earth above the temples. Nobody remembered exactly where the famous sanctuary had been. Only the old books remembered it, in libraries far away. The stories of the Games, of the great statue and of the olive wreaths, lived on only on paper.

[long-pause]

But the stories were never really forgotten. People in Europe kept reading the old Greek books. They read about the Games, and the champions, and the sacred truce. And they began to wonder. Where was Olympia? What did it look like? Could it ever be found again? In the seventeen hundreds, travellers began to come to the valley, looking for the lost sanctuary. And slowly, the ground began to give up its secrets.

[pause]

Some of the things that were buried at Olympia were very fragile. Painted clay tiles, small bronze figures, the iron tools of the workers. Under the earth, protected from wind and rain, many of them survived for a very long time. In a way, the rivers that buried Olympia also kept it safe. They wrapped it in a thick, soft blanket of earth, and let it sleep, until the day when someone would come to wake it.

[long-pause]

{img:olympia-archaeological-site-today-ruins-columns-pine-trees-visitors-sunny}
In 1766, an English traveller found the ruins of a large temple in the valley, half buried in the earth. It was the Temple of Zeus. Then, about a hundred years later, in 1875, German archaeologists began to dig at Olympia. They dug for six years. And slowly, the whole sanctuary came back into the light.

[long-pause]

They found the Temple of Zeus, with its fallen columns. They found the Temple of Hera, the treasuries and the altars. Later, in the nineteen fifties, archaeologists also found the workshop of Phidias, the sculptor who made the great statue of Zeus. In it, they found clay moulds he used to shape the golden robe. And they found a little cup with his name scratched on the bottom. It says, I belong to Phidias. It was probably his own drinking cup.

[pause]

Today, Olympia is one of the most visited ancient sites in Greece. Visitors can walk through the sanctuary, among the ruins and the pine trees. They can see the fallen columns of the Temple of Zeus, still lying where the earthquake left them. One column has been put back up again, to show how tall they were. It stands alone, high above the ruins.

[pause]

{img:single-restored-column-temple-of-zeus-olympia-today-blue-sky}
And visitors can walk through the old stone tunnel into the stadium. The stadium looks almost exactly as it did in the time of Lykos. The long flat track, the grassy banks on each side, and the stone starting lines at each end, with their grooves for the toes of the runners. Many visitors cannot resist. They take off their shoes, stand at the starting line, and run the length of the track, laughing.

[pause]

In the museum at Olympia, there are many treasures. There are the great carved scenes from the Temple of Zeus, with the labours of Heracles. There is a beautiful statue of the god Hermes, carrying a little baby. There are bronze helmets, offered to Zeus by soldiers. And there is a row of the stone bases of the statues of Zeus, paid for by athletes who cheated. Their names are still there, carved in the stone, after all these years.

[long-pause]

{img:olympic-flame-lighting-ceremony-temple-of-hera-priestesses-mirror-today}
At the end of the eighteen hundreds, a Frenchman called Pierre de Coubertin had a dream. He wanted to bring the Olympic Games back to life. Not as a festival for Zeus, but as a festival of peace and friendship between all the countries of the world. In 1896, the first modern Olympic Games were held in Athens. Athletes came from fourteen countries. And the most popular winner was a Greek water carrier called Spyridon Louis, who won the marathon.

[pause]

Since then, the Olympic Games have been held every four years, in different cities all over the world. They are now the biggest sporting festival on Earth. Women compete in almost every sport, just as the girls of the Heraia once ran in the stadium of Olympia. And winners receive gold, silver and bronze medals. But at the Games of Athens in 2004, the winners also received something else. A wreath of olive leaves.

[pause]

{img:torch-relay-runner-carrying-olympic-flame-olympia-road-today}
And there is one more tradition, which connects the modern Games with ancient Olympia. A few months before each Games, a ceremony is held in front of the old Temple of Hera. Women dressed as ancient priestesses use a curved mirror to catch the rays of the sun. The sunlight lights a torch. And that flame is carried by runners, from hand to hand, all the way to the city where the Games will be held. It is the Olympic flame.

[long-pause]

So every time the Olympic Games begin, somewhere in the world, the fire in the great stadium was lit by the sun of Olympia. It was carried from the quiet valley by the river Alpheios, past the Hill of Kronos, and the fallen columns, and the old stadium where Koroibos ran, almost three thousand years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:olympia-valley-evening-sunset-river-alpheios-pine-hill-temples-golden}
So we leave Olympia tonight, in the time of the Games, as the evening falls. The sun is going down behind the hills in the west. Its last golden light is shining on the roof of the Temple of Zeus, and on the old columns of the Temple of Hera. The shadows of the plane trees are growing long across the sanctuary. And the valley is quiet now.

[long-pause]

The crowds have gone home. The tents are gone. The merchants and the jugglers and the poets have all left. Only the priests remain, walking slowly between the altars, lighting the evening lamps. In the prytaneion, the sacred fire is still burning, as it always does. A small flame in the dusk, that is never allowed to go out.

[pause]

{img:empty-stadium-olympia-dusk-grassy-banks-starting-line-first-stars}
The stadium lies empty in the twilight. The grassy banks are quiet. The long track is smooth and pale. At each end, the stone starting lines are waiting, with their little grooves for the toes of the runners. In four years, the crowds will come back, and the runners will stand here again. But for now, there is only the sound of the crickets, and the wind in the pine trees on the Hill of Kronos.

[long-pause]

The river Alpheios flows slowly through the valley, wide and shallow and silver in the evening light. It has flowed here for thousands of years. It flowed past Koroibos the cook, and past Lykos the shepherd boy, and past all the runners and wrestlers and chariot drivers who ever came to this valley. And it flows on, quietly, toward the sea.

[pause]

{img:mountain-village-arcadia-night-lamp-glow-window-olive-wreath-wall}
