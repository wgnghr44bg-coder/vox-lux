Tonight, we travel back almost two thousand years, to the year 100 AD. We go to a hidden city, deep in the mountains of the desert, in the land that is now called Jordan. A city of rose-red stone, carved into the cliffs. A city of temples, tombs, gardens and fountains, in the middle of the dry, rocky wilderness. Its name is Petra.

[pause]

Petra lies in a wide valley, surrounded on all sides by tall mountains of sandstone. The stone is soft, and beautifully coloured. Red, and pink, and orange, and purple, and yellow, and white, in wavy stripes, like silk, or the inside of a shell. When the sun shines on it, in the morning and the evening, the whole valley glows, like fire. That is why, much later, a poet would call it the rose-red city.

[long-pause]

In the year 100 AD, Petra is the capital of a rich and powerful kingdom. The kingdom of the Nabataeans. They are an Arab people, who were once nomads, wandering the desert with their camels and their tents. Now, they are merchants, and builders, and farmers. And they control the great trade routes of the desert, along which precious goods travel, from Arabia to the cities of the Mediterranean Sea.

[pause]

{img:camel-caravan-arriving-petra-desert-mountains-incense-bundles-dust}
Through Petra pass the caravans. Long lines of camels, loaded with frankincense and myrrh, from the far south of Arabia. With spices, and silk, and pearls, and precious stones, from India, and beyond. Petra is where the caravans rest, and trade, and pay their taxes. And the Nabataeans have become very rich, from this trade. Rich enough to carve a whole city from the living rock.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

In this story, we will follow one family through a season in Petra. The family of Maliku, a merchant, who trades in frankincense. His wife Hagaru, who runs their household. Their daughter Kamilat, who is twelve, and their son Obodat, who is nine. And Maliku's father, old Wahballahi, who once led the caravans across the desert himself, and now sits in the shade, and tells stories.

And we will climb the mountain, to the great carved monument at the top, and watch the sun set over the desert.

{img:petra-high-view-sunset-desert-mountains-purple-sky-ancient-city}
Petra was a city of wonders. Its people were clever, and practical, and peaceful. They welcomed travellers from every land. They worshipped their own gods, in their own way. And they made one of the most beautiful cities the world has ever seen. Then, for many centuries, it was forgotten, by almost everyone, except the desert people who lived nearby.

[pause]

So let us go to Petra, in the year 100 AD. Let us walk through the cool, shadowy Siq, and come out into the sunlight, and see the city for the first time, as travellers did, all those years ago.

[pause]

Petra is a city of contrasts. Outside, there is the hot, dry, empty desert, where the sun beats down on the rocks, and almost nothing grows. Inside, among the mountains, there are gardens, and fountains, and shady courtyards, and the sound of running water. Travellers who arrive after weeks in the desert can hardly believe their eyes. It is like coming home, to a place you have only dreamed of.

[long-pause]

{img:nabataean-nomads-black-tents-camels-desert-night-fire-ancient}
Long ago, perhaps six hundred years before our story, the Nabataeans were wandering herders, in the deserts of northern Arabia. They lived in tents of black goat hair. They moved from place to place, with their camels, their sheep and their goats, looking for water and grass. They knew the desert better than anyone. Every well, every spring, every hidden pool of rainwater, among the rocks.

[pause]

An old Greek writer described them, about four hundred years before our story. He said that they lived in the open air, and did not build houses, or plant crops, or drink wine. They had a law against it, he said, so that they would always stay free, and could never be conquered. They kept their water in secret cisterns, dug into the rock, and hidden, so that no enemy could find them.

[long-pause]

{img:hidden-rock-cistern-desert-nabataean-water-store-cool-shadow}
But the Nabataeans were clever. They saw that the desert was not empty. Across it, every year, came the caravans, carrying frankincense and myrrh, from the south of Arabia, to the cities of Egypt, Syria, and Greece. The caravans needed guides, and water, and protection. And the Nabataeans could give them all three. So they began to work with the caravans, and then to trade themselves.

[pause]

Over time, they grew rich. They stopped wandering, and began to settle down. They built a city, in the valley of Petra, which was a perfect place. It was hidden among the mountains, easy to defend, and there was a spring of fresh water nearby. They began to farm the land around, and to plant vines, and olives, and wheat. And they began to carve their tombs and temples, into the cliffs.

[pause]

{img:nabataean-king-coin-silver-profile-crown-ancient-script}
They had kings. The first known king of the Nabataeans was called Aretas, more than two hundred and fifty years before our story. After him came many others. Kings called Obodas, and Rabbel, and Malichus, and Aretas again. Some of them were great warriors, and fought against the Greeks, and the Jews, and the Romans. But mostly, the Nabataeans preferred trade to war. They made treaties, and alliances, and paid tribute when they had to.

[pause]

The Nabataeans spoke a form of Arabic, in their daily lives. But they wrote in Aramaic, the common language of the Middle East in those days. They had their own way of writing it, with flowing, joined-up letters. Over many centuries, the Nabataean script slowly changed, and became the Arabic script, which is used by hundreds of millions of people today.

[long-pause]

{img:nabataean-inscription-carved-stone-flowing-letters-sandstone-wall}
By the year 100 AD, the Nabataean kingdom is large. It stretches from Damascus, in the north, to the Red Sea, in the south, and deep into Arabia. It includes the land of the Negev desert, and the coast near Gaza. There are other Nabataean cities too. Bostra, in the north, and Hegra, far to the south, in Arabia, with its own great carved tombs. But Petra is the capital, the heart of the kingdom, and the home of the king.

[pause]

The king in the year 100 AD is Rabbel the Second. He has ruled for about thirty years. He is a peaceful king, and the people love him. They call him the one who brought life and deliverance to his people. His kingdom lives in peace with its great neighbour, Rome, which rules almost all the lands around the Mediterranean Sea. But Rome is very powerful, and very close. And the Nabataeans know that they must be careful.

[pause]

The people of Petra are proud of their history. They still remember their nomad ancestors, and the old ways of the desert. Many families still keep camels, and black tents, and go out into the desert in the spring, when there is grass for the animals. And the old virtues of the desert are still respected in the city. Hospitality, and loyalty, and honesty, and courage, and generosity to strangers.

[long-pause]

{img:nabataean-merchant-robes-headcloth-counting-incense-sacks-storeroom}
Maliku is forty years old. He is a merchant, like his father before him. He buys frankincense and myrrh from the caravans that come up from the south, and sells it to the traders who take it on, to Gaza, and Damascus, and Egypt. He has a storeroom, near the market, full of sacks of incense, and jars of oil, and bales of cloth. He is honest, and careful, and well respected in the city.

[pause]

Maliku is a calm, patient man, with a short black beard, and kind brown eyes. He wears a long linen robe, and a cloak of fine wool, and a cloth on his head, against the sun. He speaks Arabic at home, and Aramaic in the market, and a little Greek, for the foreign traders. He can read and write, and keeps careful records of all his buying and selling, on tablets and scrolls.

[long-pause]

{img:nabataean-woman-mother-household-courtyard-grinding-grain-jewellery}
His wife Hagaru is thirty-four. She runs the household, and the servants, and the family's money, when Maliku is away. Among the Nabataeans, women are respected, and have more freedom than in many other lands. They can own property, and run businesses, and even have their own tombs carved, in their own names. Hagaru owns a vineyard, outside the city, which she inherited from her mother. She is very proud of it.

[pause]

Hagaru is lively and quick, with a loud laugh, and many friends. She wears a long, colourful dress, and a shawl over her hair, and bracelets of silver on her arms. She is a wonderful cook, and her house is always full of guests. Merchants from far away, relatives, neighbours, and friends. Nabataean hospitality is famous. No guest is ever turned away.

[pause]

{img:girl-and-boy-nabataean-children-playing-rock-steps-red-cliffs-sunny}
Kamilat is twelve. She is clever, and curious, and loves to learn. Her father has taught her to read and write, and she helps him in the storeroom, with the records. She wants to be a merchant herself, one day, like her father. She loves the market, and the caravans, and the foreign traders, with their strange clothes and languages. She has already learned a few words of Greek, and a few of Latin.

[pause]

Obodat is nine. He is named after one of the old kings of the Nabataeans, who was later worshipped as a god. He is lively, and noisy, and always climbing. He climbs the rocks, and the cliffs, and the walls, and his mother is always worried that he will fall. He loves camels, and wants to be a caravan leader, like his grandfather, when he grows up.

[long-pause]

And there is old Wahballahi, Maliku's father. He is seventy years old. When he was young, he led the caravans himself, across the great desert, from the south of Arabia to Petra. He knows every road, every well, every star. Now, he is too old to travel. He sits in the shade of the courtyard, on a cushion, with a cup of wine, and tells stories to his grandchildren. About sandstorms, and robbers, and lost wells, and the great markets of the south.

[pause]

{img:old-man-telling-stories-shade-courtyard-grandchildren-listening-wine-cup}
The family lives in a house on the hillside, above the main street of Petra, with a view across the valley. It is a comfortable house, built of stone, around a courtyard, with a little garden, and a fig tree, and a vine, which gives shade in the summer. It has many rooms, for the family, the servants, and the guests. And there is a cistern beneath the courtyard, full of cool water, from the winter rains.

[pause]

In this story, it is the end of summer, and the beginning of autumn. The worst of the heat is over. The grapes are ripening in Hagaru's vineyard. And a great caravan is expected soon, from the south, carrying the year's new frankincense. Maliku is busy, getting ready for it. And the whole city is waiting.

[long-pause]

{img:the-siq-narrow-gorge-towering-red-walls-sliver-of-sky-travellers-walking}
To enter Petra from the east, where the road comes down from the high desert plateau, the caravans and travellers come through a narrow gorge, called the Siq. It is one of the most remarkable entrances to any city in the world. A long, winding crack in the mountain, more than a kilometre long, with walls of rock on both sides, rising up almost straight, as high as a tall building, or higher.

[pause]

In places, the Siq is very narrow. Only three or four metres wide. You could almost touch both walls at once, with your arms stretched out. Above, the walls lean together, and you can see only a thin strip of blue sky, far above. The light down below is soft, and dim, and rosy, reflected from the red walls. It is cool, and quiet, and the footsteps echo.

[long-pause]

{img:siq-floor-paved-stone-water-channel-carved-along-wall-cool-light}
The floor of the Siq is paved with stones, smooth and even, so that the camels and the carts can walk easily. Along both walls, there are channels, carved into the rock, about the height of a man's knee. One of them carries water, from the spring outside the city, all the way into Petra. The other has clay pipes, laid in it, carrying more water. You can hear the water trickling, as you walk.

[pause]

The walls of the Siq are carved, here and there, with little niches, and shrines, and images of the gods. The travellers stop at them, to say a prayer, and give thanks for a safe journey. There are carved blocks of stone, standing in the niches, which represent the gods. The Nabataeans do not usually make statues of their gods, with faces and bodies. They prefer these simple stone blocks, which they call betyls. A betyl is the house of a god.

[pause]

{img:life-size-carved-camels-caravan-men-relief-siq-wall-worn-ancient}
Halfway along the Siq, there is a great carving on the wall. Life-size camels, with men leading them, walking toward the city. It is a carving of a caravan, made in honour of the merchants, and the trade that made Petra rich. Obodat loves this carving. Whenever the family walks through the Siq, he stops and stares at it. He pats the stone camels, and says that one day, he will lead a caravan into Petra, just like that.

[pause]

The Siq is not only a road. It is also a sacred way. On festival days, there are processions through it, with music, and singing, and offerings, to the temples of the city. And at the entrance to the Siq, there is a great arch, built across the top, with carved decorations. It marks the beginning of the sacred city. Everyone passes beneath it, as they come in.

[long-pause]

When the rains come in the winter, there is a danger. The water rushes down from the mountains, into the valley outside the Siq, and could flood through the gorge, carrying away everything in its path. But the Nabataeans have thought of this. They have built a dam, at the entrance, to turn the water away, into a tunnel, through the mountain, and around the city. So the Siq stays safe, and dry.

[pause]

{img:siq-opening-first-glimpse-treasury-facade-glowing-light-through-gap}
And then, at the end of the Siq, comes the most wonderful moment. The gorge turns a corner, and suddenly, through a narrow gap between the dark walls, you see a slice of brilliant, glowing pink. A great carved building, shining in the sunlight, like a vision. You walk on, and the gap widens, and there it is, in front of you, filling the whole view. The most famous building in Petra. The great tomb that later travellers would call the Treasury.

[pause]

Every traveller who comes to Petra remembers this moment, for the rest of their life. Even Kamilat and Obodat, who have seen it many times, still feel a little thrill, every time they come out of the Siq, and see it again.

[long-pause]

{img:al-khazneh-treasury-facade-petra-columns-carved-sculpture-morning-sun}
The Treasury stands in a small open square, at the end of the Siq, carved directly into the face of the cliff. It is about forty metres high, as tall as a twelve storey building. It is not built, stone by stone. It is carved, from the solid rock, from the top down. Every column, every statue, every decoration, was once part of the mountain.

[pause]

The front of the Treasury is like the front of a Greek temple, or a palace. At the bottom, there are six tall columns, with beautiful carved tops, and a triangular roof above them. On top of that, there is a second level, with more columns, and a round building in the middle, like a little temple, with a cone shaped roof, and an urn on top. All around, there are carved figures. Gods and goddesses, and eagles, and animals, and flowers.

[long-pause]

The Treasury is not really a treasury at all. Long after our story, the local people believed that the stone urn at the top was full of gold, hidden there by an ancient king, or a pharaoh. They even shot at it, hoping to break it open, and make the treasure fall out. You can still see the marks of the bullets today. But there was never any treasure in it. The urn is solid stone.

[pause]

{img:treasury-interior-plain-carved-chamber-doorway-light-falling-in-stone}
In fact, the Treasury is a tomb. It was probably made for one of the great Nabataean kings, perhaps King Aretas the Fourth, who reigned until about seventy years before our story. Inside, behind the great doorway, there is a large, plain room, carved from the rock, with smaller rooms on either side. The walls are smooth, and bare, and the colours of the rock, in waves of red, and pink, and white, are very beautiful.

[pause]

The Treasury is the most famous tomb in Petra. But there are hundreds of others. All around the valley, in every cliff, there are tombs carved into the rock. Some are small and simple, just a doorway in the rock, with a plain front. Others are big and grand, with columns, and carved decorations. Some have little steps carved on the top, which the Nabataeans copied from the buildings of Assyria, far to the east.

[pause]

{img:royal-tombs-petra-cliff-row-of-carved-facades-evening-glow}
On the eastern side of the valley, there is a great wall of rock, with a row of the biggest and grandest tombs. They are called the Royal Tombs. Some of them may have been made for kings and queens, and others for rich and important families. In the evening, when the sun is setting in the west, the light shines straight on them, and they glow, red and gold and purple, as if they were on fire.

[long-pause]

The Nabataeans care a great deal about their tombs. They believe that the dead must be honoured, and remembered. Each family has its own tomb, where its members are laid to rest, generation after generation. In front of many tombs, there are carved rooms, called triclinia, with stone benches on three sides. Here, the family gathers on special days, for a feast, in honour of the people who have passed away. They eat, and drink, and remember them, together.

[pause]

Maliku's family has its own tomb, a small one, carved into the hillside above their house. Maliku's grandfather paid for it to be made, many years ago. It has a plain front, with a doorway, and a row of steps carved on top. Inside, there are niches in the walls. Maliku's mother is buried there, and his grandparents. One day, Wahballahi will be buried there too. He says he is in no hurry.

[pause]

{img:family-feast-triclinium-stone-benches-lamps-remembrance-quiet-evening}
Once a year, on the anniversary of his mother's death, the family goes to the tomb, and has a feast in the little carved room in front of it. They bring bread, and dates, and wine, and lamps. They sit on the stone benches, and eat, and talk about her. Wahballahi tells stories about when they were young. The children listen, and learn about the grandmother they never knew. It is a gentle, loving custom. And in the lamplight, among the carved rock, it feels as if she is there with them.

[long-pause]

{img:nabataean-water-channel-carved-hillside-petra-terracotta-pipes-cistern}
Petra lies in the desert. It rains there only a few times a year, in the winter. The summers are long, and hot, and completely dry. And yet, in the year 100 AD, Petra is a green city, with gardens, and fountains, and pools, and trees, and about twenty or thirty thousand people. How is this possible? The answer is water. The Nabataeans are masters of water.

[pause]

They learned it in the desert, long ago, when they were nomads. They knew how to find water, and how to save it, and how to hide it. And when they built Petra, they used all that knowledge, and more. They built one of the cleverest water systems of the ancient world. Channels, pipes, dams, cisterns, and pools, all working together, to catch every drop of water, and bring it to the city.

[long-pause]

{img:spring-ain-musa-water-flowing-rocks-green-plants-desert-valley}
The most important source of water is a spring, outside the city, in the valley to the east. It is called the Spring of Moses, because people say that Moses struck the rock there, with his staff, and water came out. It gives fresh, clear water, all year round. The Nabataeans bring this water into the city, along the channels in the Siq, and through pipes of baked clay, fitted together, mile after mile.

[pause]

The pipes are made very carefully. Each one is about thirty centimetres long, and they fit together, end to end, sealed with mortar. The channels slope very gently, all the way, so that the water flows steadily, but not too fast. There are little basins along the way, where the water slows down, and the sand and dirt settle to the bottom, so that the water arriving in the city is clean.

[pause]

{img:rock-cut-cistern-steps-down-dark-water-cool-petra-ancient}
But the spring is not enough, for a whole city. So the Nabataeans also catch the rainwater. When it rains in the winter, the water rushes down the bare rock of the mountains, in streams and waterfalls. The Nabataeans have carved channels all over the mountainsides, to catch this water, and lead it into cisterns. Hundreds of them, all over the city. Big tanks, carved into the rock, or built under the houses, where the water stays cool, and clean, all through the summer.

[pause]

Maliku's house has its own cistern, under the courtyard. In the winter, the rain falls on the roof, and runs down into it, through a pipe. By the spring, it is full. And all through the summer, the family draws water from it, with a bucket on a rope. It is cool and sweet. Hagaru is very careful with it. Nothing is ever wasted. The water for washing is used again, for the garden, and the plants.

[long-pause]

There are dams, too. In the valleys around the city, the Nabataeans have built walls of stone, across the dry riverbeds, to slow the floods, and hold back the water, so that it soaks into the ground. Behind the dams, the soil stays moist, and the farmers can grow crops. Wheat, and barley, and grapes, and olives, and figs. The desert around Petra is not as empty as it looks. There are fields, and terraces, and orchards, wherever there is a little water.

[pause]

{img:terraced-fields-desert-valley-stone-walls-green-crops-nabataean-farm}
The water engineers of Petra are respected men. They inspect the channels, and the pipes, and the cisterns, and repair them when they break. They clear the sand and the stones, after the winter floods. They know exactly where every drop of water goes. Without them, the city would die. And everyone in Petra knows it. Water is life, in the desert. And the Nabataeans never forget it.

[pause]

In the centre of the city, there is a great pool, with a little island in the middle, and a pavilion on the island. Around it, there is a garden, full of trees and flowers. In a city in the desert, where water is so precious, this pool is a sign of great wealth and power. It says to every visitor, look how rich we are. We have so much water, we can use it just for beauty. The children love to sit by the pool, in the evening, and watch the reflections of the lamps on the water.

[long-pause]

{img:nabataean-stone-house-courtyard-fig-tree-vine-shade-petra-hillside}
Maliku's house stands on a hillside, among other houses, on the north side of the main street. It is built of blocks of sandstone, cut from the nearby cliffs, and plastered, inside and out. From the street, it does not look like much. Just a plain wall, with a wooden door. But inside, it is cool, and spacious, and beautiful.

[pause]

Through the door, there is a short passage, and then the courtyard. It is open to the sky, and paved with stone. In the middle, there is the opening of the cistern, with a stone lid. In one corner, the fig tree grows, and over the walls, a grapevine climbs, on a wooden frame, making a cool green roof, against the sun. There are pots of herbs, and flowers, along the walls. And there are always a few birds, drinking from a little stone basin.

[long-pause]

{img:nabataean-reception-room-painted-plaster-walls-colourful-patterns-cushions}
Around the courtyard, there are rooms. The biggest and best is the reception room, where guests are entertained. Its walls are covered with painted plaster, in bright colours. Red, and yellow, and blue, and green. There are painted patterns, like marble, and painted vines, and birds, and flowers. There are cushions and carpets on the floor, and low tables, and oil lamps hanging from the ceiling. It is a beautiful room, and Hagaru is very proud of it.

[pause]

There is a kitchen, with an oven, and a hearth, and shelves of pots and jars. There are storerooms, full of grain, and oil, and wine, and dates. There are bedrooms, for the family, and rooms for the servants. And upstairs, there is a flat roof, where the family sleeps on hot summer nights, under the stars, on mats and cushions. Obodat loves sleeping on the roof. He lies on his back, and counts the stars, until he falls asleep.

[pause]

{img:family-sleeping-flat-roof-summer-night-stars-petra-cliffs-silhouette}
The family's day begins early, at dawn, before the heat. Hagaru gets up first, and wakes the servants. One of them lights the fire in the kitchen, and another draws water from the cistern. Hagaru grinds the wheat into flour, on a stone quern, and makes the dough for the day's bread. It is baked in the oven, in flat, round loaves. The smell of fresh bread fills the house.

[pause]

For breakfast, the family eats bread, with olive oil, and cheese, and dates, and figs from the tree in the courtyard. They drink water, or milk, or a little wine mixed with water. Then Maliku goes to his storeroom, near the market. Kamilat goes with him, some days, to help with the records. Obodat goes to his teacher, a Greek man, who teaches a group of merchants' sons to read and write and count. And Wahballahi takes his cushion into the shade of the courtyard.

[long-pause]

In the middle of the day, when the heat is greatest, everything in Petra slows down. The shops close, the streets empty, and people rest in the coolest rooms of their houses. Maliku comes home, and the family eats the main meal of the day. Bread, and lentils, or beans, or a stew of lamb or goat, with vegetables, and herbs. And fruit. Melons, and grapes, and pomegranates, in their season. Then they rest, until the afternoon.

[pause]

{img:nabataean-meal-low-table-bread-dates-grapes-pomegranate-painted-bowls}
The bowls and plates they eat from are very special. They are made in Petra, and they are famous. They are very thin, as thin as an eggshell, and very light. They are painted with beautiful patterns, in dark red, or black, on a pink background. Leaves, and flowers, and palm branches, and little fruits. Every family in Petra has them, and they are sent all over the Nabataean kingdom. Hagaru has a whole set of them, which she keeps for guests.

[pause]

In the evening, when it is cooler, the family sits in the courtyard, under the vine. Neighbours and friends come to visit. They drink wine, and eat nuts, and dates, and talk about the day. The children play in the corner. The lamps are lit, and their light flickers on the painted walls. And above, through the leaves of the vine, the first stars come out, over the dark mountains.

[long-pause]

{img:frankincense-tree-boswellia-southern-arabia-harvester-cutting-bark-resin}
To understand why Petra is so rich, we must understand frankincense. It is a resin, a kind of sticky sap, that comes from a small, twisted tree, which grows in the far south of Arabia, in the land that is now Yemen and Oman. The harvesters cut the bark of the tree, with a special knife. A milky sap oozes out, and slowly hardens in the air, into little white or golden lumps, like tears.

[pause]

When frankincense is burned, it gives off a beautiful, sweet, rich smoke. The smell is like nothing else in the world. In the ancient world, frankincense was used in every temple, of every god, from Egypt to Rome to India. The smoke rose up to heaven, carrying the prayers of the people to the gods. It was burned at funerals, and weddings, and festivals, and in the houses of the rich, to make them smell sweet.

[long-pause]

{img:frankincense-resin-tears-golden-pieces-bowl-smoke-rising-incense-burner}
And there was myrrh, too, another resin, from another small tree, from the same lands. Myrrh is darker, and red-brown, and more bitter. It was used in medicines, and perfumes, and for preparing the dead for burial. In Egypt, it was used to make the mummies. Both frankincense and myrrh were very precious. Sometimes, they were worth almost as much as gold.

[pause]

The trees grow only in a few places, in the far south of Arabia, and across the sea in Somalia. Nowhere else. So everyone, everywhere, who wanted frankincense and myrrh, had to get it from there. And it had to travel a very long way. More than two thousand kilometres, across the deserts of Arabia, to reach the cities of the Mediterranean. The journey took about two months, by camel.

[pause]

{img:incense-route-map-style-arabia-caravan-path-oases-desert-ancient}
The route the caravans took is called the Incense Road. It runs north, along the western side of Arabia, from oasis to oasis, from well to well, through the desert. There are about sixty or seventy stops along the way, each a day's journey apart. At each stop, there is water, and food, and shelter, and the caravans must pay a tax, to the people who control that place. At the northern end of the road, the last great stop, before the Mediterranean, is Petra.

[pause]

In Petra, the caravans rest, and trade. The merchants of Petra buy the incense, and sell it on, to merchants from Gaza, and Egypt, and Syria. From Gaza, it goes by ship, to Rome, and Greece, and all the cities of the Mediterranean. Every time it changes hands, the price goes up. By the time it reaches Rome, it is very expensive. And everyone along the way has made a profit, including the Nabataeans.

[long-pause]

Maliku's storeroom smells wonderful. It is full of sacks of frankincense, of different qualities. The best is pale, almost white, and very pure. The worst is dark, and full of bits of bark. Maliku knows exactly how to judge it. He takes a piece, and looks at it in the light, and smells it, and breaks it, and sometimes burns a little piece, to test the smoke. Kamilat is learning to do it too. She has a very good nose, her father says.

[pause]

{img:merchant-and-daughter-testing-incense-storeroom-sacks-scales-lamplight}
In the Roman world, the demand for incense is enormous. The Romans burn it in their temples, and their homes, and at the funerals of rich people. When the wife of the emperor Nero died, about thirty-five years before our story, people said that he burned more incense at her funeral than Arabia could produce in a whole year. That was probably an exaggeration. But it shows how much incense the Romans used.

[pause]

The Nabataeans also trade in other things. Spices, like pepper, and cinnamon, and ginger, which come by sea from India, to the ports of Arabia, and then by caravan to Petra. Silk, from China, very rare and expensive. Pearls, from the Persian Gulf. Precious stones, and ivory, and ebony, and perfumes. And from the Dead Sea, nearby, the Nabataeans collect bitumen, a black, sticky substance, which the Egyptians use for making mummies. Petra is a great crossroads of the ancient world.

[long-pause]

{img:great-camel-caravan-desert-plain-dust-cloud-approaching-mountains-evening}
One morning, early in the autumn, a boy comes running down from the hills, shouting. The caravan is coming. The great caravan from the south, which the whole city has been waiting for. It has been seen from the watchtowers, on the edge of the desert, a day's journey away. It will arrive tomorrow. And suddenly, all of Petra is busy.

[pause]

The merchants hurry to their storerooms, to make room for the new goods. The innkeepers prepare their rooms and their courtyards, for the caravan men. The bakers bake extra bread. The water sellers fill their skins and jars. The tax collectors sharpen their pens, and check their scales. And the children run up to the high places, to watch for the first sight of the camels, on the horizon.

[long-pause]

The next day, in the afternoon, the caravan arrives. First, there is a cloud of dust, far away, on the desert plain. Then, slowly, the line of camels appears, coming closer, and closer. There are hundreds of them. Perhaps a thousand. They walk in a long, long line, one behind the other, each tied to the one in front, swaying gently, with their heavy loads. It takes more than an hour for them all to pass.

[pause]

{img:camel-train-entering-city-gate-loaded-sacks-drivers-robes-crowds-watching}
The camels are tall, and patient, and strong. Each one carries a load of about a hundred and fifty kilos, in sacks and bundles, tied on both sides of a wooden saddle. They can walk for days without water, across the hottest deserts. Without camels, the incense trade would be impossible. The camel drivers walk beside them, or ride on top, in long robes, with cloths wrapped around their heads and faces, against the sun and the sand.

[pause]

The leader of the caravan is a tall, thin man, with a grey beard, and a face burned dark by the sun. He comes from the south of Arabia, from the land of the incense. He has made this journey many times. He knows Wahballahi well. They led caravans together, when they were young men. Now, he comes to Maliku's house, to greet his old friend, and to do business.

[pause]

{img:two-old-friends-embracing-courtyard-caravan-leader-and-grandfather-joy}
Wahballahi is so happy to see him. The two old men embrace, and laugh, and slap each other on the back. They sit together, in the courtyard, under the vine, and drink wine, and talk for hours. About the old days, and the long journeys, and the friends they had, and the adventures. About the time they were caught in a great sandstorm, for three days. And the time they found a well that had dried up, and nearly died of thirst. And the time they were attacked by robbers, and chased them away.

[pause]

The children sit at their feet, and listen, wide-eyed. Obodat asks a hundred questions. How far is it to the land of the incense? How many days? What is it like? Are there lions? Are there dragons? The caravan leader laughs, and tells him. No dragons. But there are lions, and leopards, and hyenas, and snakes, and scorpions. And there are cities, in the south, with great temples, and palaces, and towers, many storeys high, built of mud brick.

[long-pause]

Over the next few days, the business is done. The caravan leader shows Maliku his frankincense, and his myrrh, and his other goods. They discuss the quality, and the price, for a long time, politely, over many cups of wine. It is not quick. There is no hurry, in the desert. At last, they agree, and shake hands. Maliku buys a large amount of the best frankincense, enough for the whole year.

[pause]

{img:caravanserai-courtyard-evening-camels-resting-drivers-fire-cooking-stars}
The caravan men stay in Petra for several weeks. They rest, and eat, and drink, and visit the temples, and the market, and the baths. The camels rest too, in the big yards outside the city, and eat, and drink, and grow strong again. Then the caravan loads up again, with new goods, bought in Petra. Cloth, and metal tools, and glass, and pottery, and wine, and olive oil. And it sets off south, on the long journey home.

[pause]

On the day it leaves, the whole family goes to see it off. Wahballahi and his old friend embrace again, for a long time. They both know that they may never see each other again. They are old men now. The caravan leader climbs onto his camel, and raises his hand. And the long line of camels moves off, slowly, into the desert, swaying, and disappears into the dust and the haze.

[long-pause]

{img:petra-colonnaded-street-100ad-shops-columns-crowds-merchants-awnings}
The heart of Petra is the main street. It runs along the bottom of the valley, beside the dry riverbed, from the end of the Siq toward the great temple at the far end of the city. On both sides, there are rows of tall columns, holding up covered walkways, which give shade from the sun. And behind the columns, there are shops, and workshops, and offices, and taverns.

[pause]

The street is always busy, but especially in the morning, and in the late afternoon. It is full of people from many lands. Nabataeans, in their long robes and headcloths. Greeks, in their short tunics. Romans, with their togas and cloaks. Jews, from Judaea, and Egyptians, and Syrians, and Arabs from the far south, and even, sometimes, a merchant from India, with his turban and his gold earrings. A dozen languages are spoken.

[long-pause]

{img:market-stalls-spices-baskets-fruit-cloth-pottery-colourful-ancient-petra}
There are shops selling everything. Fruit and vegetables, from the farms around the city. Grapes, and figs, and dates, and melons, and pomegranates. Bread, and cakes made with honey, and roasted nuts. Meat, and cheese, and olives, and oil. Spices, in great open baskets, in every colour. Red, and yellow, and brown, and green. The smell is wonderful. Cinnamon, and pepper, and cumin, and saffron, and cardamom.

[pause]

There are shops selling cloth. Fine linen from Egypt, and wool from Syria, and cotton from India, and very rarely, a piece of shining silk from China, which costs a fortune. There are shops selling jewellery. Silver bracelets, and earrings, and necklaces of glass beads, and gold rings, with precious stones. There are shops selling glass bottles, and perfumes, and oils, and the beautiful painted pottery of Petra.

[pause]

There are money changers too, at their little tables, with their scales and their boxes of coins. Coins from everywhere. Nabataean coins, with the heads of the king and queen on them. Roman coins, with the head of the emperor. Greek coins, Egyptian coins, Jewish coins. The money changers weigh them, and test them, and change them, for a small fee. Kamilat loves to look at all the different coins, with their different faces and pictures.

[pause]

Kamilat often goes to the market with her mother. They buy food for the house, and cloth for new clothes, and sometimes a little present. Hagaru bargains hard. She knows the price of everything, and she does not let anyone cheat her. She argues, and laughs, and walks away, and comes back, and in the end, she always gets a good price. Kamilat watches her, and learns. She thinks her mother would make a very good merchant.

[long-pause]

{img:mother-and-daughter-bargaining-jewellery-stall-petra-street-laughing}
On the main street, there are also the public baths. A big building, with warm rooms, and hot rooms, and cold pools, like the baths of Rome. The men go there in the afternoon, to wash, and relax, and talk, and do business. The women go at other times. It is one of the pleasures of city life. And it shows, once again, how much water the Nabataeans have, in their desert city.

[pause]

In the evening, when the shops close, the street becomes quieter. But there are still taverns open, where people eat and drink, and talk, and listen to music. Lamps are lit, along the colonnades, and they cast long shadows on the paving stones. The caravan men sit in the taverns, telling stories of their journeys. And the people of Petra stroll along the street, in the cool of the evening, greeting their friends, and enjoying the night air.

[pause]

{img:petra-street-evening-oil-lamps-colonnade-shadows-strolling-people}
Maliku and his family sometimes walk along the main street in the evening, after their supper. They walk slowly, and greet their neighbours, and stop to talk. The children run ahead, and look in the shops, and buy a little cake, or some roasted nuts. And then they walk home, up the hill, under the stars, with the sound of music and laughter fading behind them.

[long-pause]

{img:qasr-al-bint-temple-petra-great-stone-temple-steps-altar-courtyard}
The Nabataeans are a religious people. They have many gods, and they worship them in many ways. Their most important god is called Dushara. His name means the one from the Shara, which is the name of the mountains around Petra. He is the god of the mountains, and of the king, and of the whole Nabataean people. He is a god of the sun, perhaps, and of the sky.

[pause]

The most important goddess is called al-Uzza. Her name means the mighty one. She is a goddess of the morning star, and of love, and of power. The Greeks and Romans in Petra say that she is like their own goddess Aphrodite, or Venus. And there is Allat, another great goddess, and Manat, the goddess of fate, and many others, gods of the city, and of the desert, and of the caravans, and of the family.

[long-pause]

At the far end of the main street, there is the greatest temple in Petra. It is a huge square building, of stone, standing on a high platform, with wide steps leading up to it. Inside, it is tall, and dim, and holy. The people call it the Temple of Dushara. Later, the local people would call it the Palace of the Pharaoh's Daughter, because of an old story. In front of it, there is a big paved courtyard, with a great altar, where offerings are made.

[pause]

{img:betyl-stone-block-niche-carved-eyes-nose-simple-god-image-nabataean}
As we have seen, the Nabataeans do not usually make statues of their gods. Instead, they worship simple blocks of stone, called betyls. Sometimes, the betyls have eyes, and a nose, carved on them, very simply. But mostly, they are just plain, square or tall blocks of stone, standing in a niche, or on a platform. The Nabataeans believe that the god lives in the stone. You can see them everywhere in Petra. In the temples, in the Siq, on the mountainsides, beside the roads.

[pause]

Above the city, on the tops of the mountains, there are high places. Flat areas, carved out of the rock, at the very top of the mountains, close to the sky. There are altars there, and carved basins, and steps. The most famous is called the High Place of Sacrifice. It is reached by a long, steep stairway, carved into the mountain. At festivals, the priests climb up there, with the people, to make offerings to the gods. Incense is burned, and prayers are said.

[pause]

{img:high-place-mountaintop-petra-carved-altar-view-over-valley-dawn}
Hagaru takes the children up to the high place sometimes, early in the morning, before the heat. It is a long climb, up hundreds of steps, cut into the rock. They stop to rest, many times, and look at the view. At the top, there is a wonderful view over the whole city, and the mountains, and the desert beyond. Hagaru burns a little frankincense, from her husband's storeroom, on the altar, and says a prayer for her family. The smoke rises slowly, straight up into the still morning air.

[long-pause]

At home, too, the family has its own little shrine. A niche in the wall of the courtyard, with a small betyl in it, and a lamp, and a little incense burner. Every morning, Hagaru lights the lamp, and burns a little incense, and says a prayer. For her husband, on his journeys. For her children, to grow up healthy and happy. For old Wahballahi, to stay well. And for rain, in the winter, to fill the cisterns.

[pause]

The Nabataeans also honour their kings, after they have passed away. One of the old kings, Obodas, became a god after his death. People built temples for him, and made offerings to him. Obodat, who is named after him, is very proud of this. He tells his friends that he is named after a god. His sister says that the god would not be very impressed with his manners. Obodat sticks out his tongue at her.

[pause]

{img:family-household-shrine-niche-lamp-incense-mother-praying-courtyard-morning}
Petra welcomes the gods of other peoples too. The Greek and Roman traders bring their own gods, and build little shrines to them. The Egyptian merchants worship Isis, the great mother goddess of Egypt, and there is a shrine to her, carved into the rock, on the edge of the city. Nobody minds. In Petra, there is room for everyone, and for every god. It is a city of travellers, and travellers bring their gods with them.

[long-pause]

{img:petra-theatre-carved-into-mountainside-rows-of-seats-stage-tombs-above}
Not far from the end of the Siq, on the side of the main valley, there is the theatre. It is carved into the side of the mountain, like everything else in Petra. It is shaped like half of a great bowl, with rows and rows of stone seats, rising up the slope, in a curve. At the bottom, there is a round flat space, and a stage, with a tall wall behind it, decorated with columns and niches.

[pause]

The theatre is big. It can hold about four thousand people, perhaps more. It was carved by the Nabataeans, about seventy or eighty years before our story, in the time of King Aretas the Fourth. When they carved it, they cut right through some older tombs, which were in the way. You can still see the doorways of those tombs, high up at the back of the theatre, above the top row of seats, like dark windows.

[long-pause]

{img:theatre-performance-masked-actors-stage-audience-torches-evening-ancient}
The theatre is used for many things. For plays, in the Greek style, with actors wearing masks. For music, and singing, and dancing. For poetry readings, and speeches. And for religious festivals, and ceremonies, in honour of the gods and the king. On festival days, the theatre is full, and the sound of the crowd, and the music, echoes around the valley.

[pause]

One evening, in the autumn, there is a performance in the theatre. A group of travelling actors, from the Greek cities of Syria, has come to Petra, with the caravans. They are going to perform a play, an old Greek comedy, about a clever slave, and a foolish master, and two young lovers. Everyone is going. Maliku has bought seats for the whole family, in the middle rows.

[pause]

{img:family-sitting-stone-seats-theatre-cushions-snacks-waiting-sunset}
They go early, to get a good view. They bring cushions to sit on, because the stone seats are hard. And they bring a basket, with bread, and cheese, and fruit, and a jar of watered wine. The theatre fills up slowly, as the sun goes down. Rich people, poor people, merchants, soldiers, priests, caravan men, women and children. Everyone talks, and laughs, and eats, and waits.

[pause]

Then the music starts. A man plays the pipes, and another plays a drum. The actors come out, in their colourful costumes, and their big masks, with funny faces. The masks have wide open mouths, which help their voices to carry, all the way to the back of the theatre. The actors speak in Greek. Not everyone understands every word. But they understand enough. And the actors make everything very clear, with big gestures, and funny walks, and silly faces.

[long-pause]

The play is very funny. The clever slave tricks his foolish master, again and again. The young lovers sigh, and quarrel, and make up. There is a fat cook, who keeps falling over. And a grumpy old man, who shouts at everyone. The audience laughs, and laughs. Obodat laughs so much that he falls off his cushion. Even Wahballahi, who has seen many plays in his life, chuckles, and wipes his eyes.

[pause]

{img:theatre-at-night-empty-seats-moonlight-carved-tombs-silent-valley}
When the play is over, the audience cheers, and claps, and throws flowers onto the stage. The actors bow, and take off their masks, and smile. Then everyone streams out of the theatre, into the dark, with torches and lamps, and walks home, talking about the play, and laughing about the funniest parts. Obodat walks home doing the funny walk of the fat cook, and his mother tells him to stop.

[pause]

Later that night, the theatre is empty and silent. The moon rises over the mountains, and shines on the rows of stone seats, and the empty stage. And high above, the doorways of the old tombs look down, dark and quiet, as they have for hundreds of years, and will for hundreds more.

[pause]

The theatre is a place where everyone in Petra comes together. Rich and poor, Nabataeans and foreigners, young and old. For a few hours, they forget their differences, and their worries, and their work. They laugh at the same jokes, and cheer at the same moments. And when they go home, they feel, a little more, that they belong to the same city.

[long-pause]

{img:nabataean-king-rabbel-ii-royal-robes-crown-palace-hall-courtiers}
In the year 100 AD, the king of the Nabataeans is Rabbel the Second. He became king when he was just a boy, about thirty years earlier, after his father passed away. At first, his mother ruled for him, as regent, until he was old enough. Now, he is a grown man, in his forties, and a wise and respected king. He is married to Queen Gamilath, and they have children. Their faces appear together on the coins of the kingdom.

[pause]

The Nabataean kings are not like the kings of other lands, the emperors of Rome, or the kings of Persia, with their great palaces, and their thousands of servants, and their cruel punishments. A Greek writer called Strabo described them, about eighty years before our story, from the reports of a friend who had lived in Petra. He said that the king was very modest, and lived simply. He said that the king often served his guests himself, at his own table.

[long-pause]

{img:nabataean-banquet-guests-reclining-singers-musicians-wine-lamps}
Strabo also described the way the Nabataeans liked to feast. He said that they held banquets, for groups of thirteen people at a time, with two singing girls for each banquet, to entertain them. Each guest could drink eleven cups of wine, from golden cups, and no more. They had few slaves, Strabo said, and most of the work was done by their own families. And sometimes, he said, the king had to give an account of his rule to the people, in an assembly. Strabo was surprised by this. In most kingdoms, the king answered to nobody.

[pause]

The king lives in a palace, somewhere in the city. Nobody today knows exactly where. Perhaps on a hilltop, near the centre, with a view over the city. It is probably a grand building, with courtyards, and halls, and gardens, and painted walls, and fine furniture. But not as grand as the palaces of Rome. The Nabataeans spend their money on their temples, and their tombs, more than on their palaces.

[pause]

{img:king-procession-main-street-petra-crowds-cheering-banners-camels-ceremony}
One day, in the autumn, the king passes through the city, in a procession, on his way to the great temple, for a festival. The streets are crowded with people, who have come to see him. Maliku and his family are there, on the steps of a shop, near the temple, with a good view. There is music, and singing, and the smell of incense, burning in great bowls, along the way.

[pause]

The procession comes slowly along the main street. First, the musicians, with drums and pipes and cymbals. Then the priests, in white robes, carrying incense and offerings. Then the soldiers of the king's guard, on camels and horses, with spears and shields, shining in the sun. And then the king himself, riding on a white horse, in a purple robe, with a golden band around his head. He smiles, and raises his hand, to the people. They cheer, and call out blessings.

[long-pause]

Behind the king comes the queen, in a covered carriage, with her ladies. And then the king's family, and his ministers, and his officials. The chief minister of the kingdom is called the brother of the king, even if he is not really his brother. It is a title of honour. He is a powerful man, who runs the government, and the army, and the taxes, for the king.

[pause]

{img:queen-gamilath-carriage-procession-veiled-ladies-jewels-sunlight}
At the temple, the king climbs the steps, and goes inside, with the priests, to make the offerings to Dushara. The crowd waits outside, in the courtyard, and around the temple. When the king comes out again, there is a great cheer. Then the feasting begins. The king gives food, and wine, to the people, and there is music, and dancing, in the streets, until late in the night.

[pause]

Kamilat watches the king very carefully, as he passes by. She thinks he looks kind, and a little tired. She wonders what it is like to be a king. To have so many people looking at you, all the time. To have to make so many decisions. To carry the whole kingdom on your shoulders. She decides that she would rather be a merchant. It seems much more fun.

[long-pause]

{img:nabataean-potters-workshop-wheel-kiln-thin-painted-bowls-drying-shelves}
On the edge of the city, beside the road that leads out to the east, there are the workshops of the potters. Here, the famous Nabataean pottery is made. The thin, delicate bowls and plates and cups, painted with leaves and flowers, that every family in Petra uses. The potters are skilled craftsmen, who have learned their trade from their fathers, and their grandfathers.

[pause]

Kamilat has a friend called Shullay, whose father is a potter. Sometimes, Kamilat goes to visit her, at the workshop. She loves to watch the potters at work. It seems like magic, the way a lump of wet clay becomes a beautiful, thin bowl, in just a few minutes, on the spinning wheel.

[long-pause]

The clay comes from the hills around Petra. It is dug out, and carried to the workshop, in baskets, on donkeys. Then it is mixed with water, in big pits, and left for a long time, until it is smooth, and fine, with no stones or lumps. The potters knead it with their hands and feet, like bread dough, until it is just right. It is hard work, and messy. The apprentices are covered in clay, from head to foot.

[pause]

{img:potter-hands-shaping-thin-bowl-spinning-wheel-wet-clay-close-detail}
Shullay's father sits at his wheel, a round stone disc, which spins on a pivot. His son turns it with his foot, faster and faster. The potter throws a lump of clay onto the middle of the wheel, and wets his hands. Then he presses the clay, and shapes it, and pulls it up, with his fingers, and the clay rises, like a living thing, into a bowl. Thinner and thinner, until it is as thin as an eggshell. It takes great skill. One wrong move, and the whole bowl collapses.

[pause]

When the bowls are made, they are left to dry, on long wooden shelves, in the shade. Then they are painted. This is done by Shullay's mother, and her sisters, and Shullay herself, who is learning. They use thin brushes, made from animal hair, and paint made from crushed red and black earth, mixed with water. They paint the patterns very quickly, and very surely, without any drawing first. Leaves, and vines, and palm branches, and little round fruits, and dots, and lines.

[pause]

{img:women-painting-pottery-fine-brush-leaf-patterns-red-black-workshop-shade}
Kamilat is allowed to try painting one bowl. She takes the brush, very carefully, and dips it in the red paint. She tries to paint a leaf, like Shullay does. But her leaf is wobbly, and too big, and the paint runs. Shullay laughs, kindly, and shows her how to do it. Hold the brush lightly. Move quickly, not slowly. Don't think too much. Kamilat tries again. It is a little better. Shullay's mother says that it is not bad, for a first try.

[long-pause]

When the bowls are painted, and dry, they are fired in the kiln. The kiln is a big round oven, built of stone and clay, with a fire underneath. The bowls are stacked inside, very carefully, and the fire is lit. It burns for a whole day and night, very hot, hotter than any kitchen fire. The potters watch it carefully, and feed it with wood, and brush, and dried dung. When the fire goes out, they wait a day for the kiln to cool. Then they open it, and take out the bowls.

[pause]

{img:kiln-opened-finished-pink-bowls-potters-inspecting-morning-light}
Opening the kiln is always an exciting moment. Some of the bowls may have cracked, in the heat. Some of the colours may have gone wrong. But most of them come out perfect. Pink and smooth, with the red and black patterns shining. The potters hold them up to the light, and tap them, and listen to the ringing sound they make. A good bowl rings like a bell.

[pause]

Kamilat's bowl comes out of the kiln too. It has a wobbly red leaf on it, and a few drops of paint in the wrong place. But it has not cracked. Shullay's father wraps it in a cloth, and gives it to her, to take home. Kamilat gives it to her mother. Hagaru says it is the most beautiful bowl in the house. She keeps it on a shelf, in the kitchen, and uses it for her best olives.

[pause]

Nabataean pottery is still found today, in many places, from Jordan to Arabia to Egypt. Archaeologists find broken pieces of it, everywhere they dig. They call it eggshell ware, because it is so thin. And when they find it, they know that the Nabataeans were there, long ago.

[long-pause]

{img:stonemasons-carving-tomb-facade-top-down-scaffolding-cliff-petra}
All over Petra, the sound of hammers and chisels can be heard. Tap, tap, tap, on the soft sandstone. It is the sound of the stonemasons, carving new tombs, and temples, and houses, into the cliffs. They are the great artists of Petra. Without them, the city would be just another town in the desert. With them, it is one of the wonders of the world.

[pause]

The stonemasons work in a very special way. They do not build their monuments, stone by stone, from the ground up, like other builders. They carve them, from the top down. First, they choose a good place on the cliff, where the stone is solid, and the colours are beautiful. Then they cut steps into the side of the cliff, to climb up. And at the very top, they begin.

[long-pause]

They cut away the rock, little by little, to make a flat surface, like a wall. Then they carve the top of the monument first. The roof, the decorations, the urn, the statues. Then they work their way down, carving the columns, and the doorway, and the details, as they go. They stand on the rock that has not been carved yet, below them. So they do not need any scaffolding. As they work downward, the uncarved rock beneath their feet becomes the next part of the monument.

[pause]

{img:master-mason-showing-apprentice-chisel-technique-carving-capital-detail}
It is very clever. And it means there is no room for mistakes. If a stonemason makes an error, he cannot take it back, or add more stone. He must change the design, or start again somewhere else. So the master masons plan everything very carefully, before they begin. They draw the design on the rock, with lines and measurements. And they check, and check again, as they work.

[pause]

Kamilat and Obodat sometimes go to watch the stonemasons at work, on a new tomb, at the edge of the city. It is being carved for a rich merchant family. The masons have been working on it for more than a year. Now, the top part is finished, with its row of carved steps, and its decorations. And they are working on the columns, halfway down.

[pause]

{img:children-watching-masons-from-rocks-dust-chips-falling-sunlight}
The master mason is an old man, called Aftah. He is very famous in Petra. He has carved many tombs, and some of them are among the most beautiful in the city. He has his name carved on some of them, proudly, in the Nabataean letters. He lets the children watch, as long as they stay out of the way. Sometimes, he lets Obodat hold a chisel, and tap it with a hammer, on a loose piece of stone.

[pause]

The stonemasons use simple tools. Iron chisels, of different sizes, and wooden mallets, and iron picks. For the rough work, they use the picks, to cut away big pieces of rock. For the fine work, they use small, sharp chisels, and tap them gently, again and again. If you look closely at the walls of the tombs in Petra, you can still see the marks of their chisels, in neat diagonal lines, after two thousand years.

[long-pause]

{img:finished-tomb-facade-fresh-carving-masons-resting-proud-evening-light}
When the stonemasons have finished carving a monument, it is often covered with plaster, and painted. In those days, many of the monuments of Petra were not just the natural colour of the rock. They were painted in bright colours, red, and white, and blue, and gold. Today, the paint and the plaster have almost all worn away, after two thousand years of wind and rain and sun. Only the natural colours of the rock are left. Many people think that is even more beautiful.

[pause]

The stonemasons work only in the cooler parts of the day. In the early morning, and in the late afternoon, and evening. In the heat of the middle of the day, they rest, in the shade, and eat, and drink, and sleep. They sing as they work, old songs of the desert, in rhythm with their hammers. And the sound of their singing, and their hammering, echoes around the valley.

[pause]

As the sun goes down, they stop work for the day. They put down their tools, and stretch their tired arms, and look at what they have done. Another few centimetres of column. Another carved leaf. Another step closer to the finished tomb. It is slow work. But they are patient. The tomb will last for thousands of years. A few more months, to make it perfect, is nothing.

[long-pause]

{img:petra-garden-pool-island-pavilion-trees-flowers-colonnade-green}
In the middle of the city, beside the main street, on a raised terrace, there is a wonderful place. A great garden, with a pool. The pool is big, as big as a modern swimming pool, and quite deep. In the middle of it, there is a little island, with a pavilion, a small building with columns, open to the breeze. You can reach it by a little boat, or perhaps by swimming.

[pause]

Around the pool, there is the garden. It is full of trees, and plants, and flowers. Date palms, and fig trees, and pomegranates, and olives, and vines. Roses, and lilies, and herbs, and sweet-smelling bushes. There are paths, and stone benches, and little channels of running water, between the flower beds. In the heat of the day, it is cool, and green, and shady. The air smells of flowers, and wet earth, and water.

[long-pause]

{img:great-temple-petra-tall-columns-elephant-headed-capitals-courtyard}
Next to the garden, there is another great building, called by people today the Great Temple. It is huge, with tall columns, at the front, and a big courtyard, with more columns around it. The tops of some of the columns are carved with the heads of elephants, with their trunks and their big ears. The Nabataeans have never seen an elephant, most of them. But they have heard about them, from the traders who come from India.

[pause]

Nobody today is quite sure what the Great Temple was for. Perhaps it was a temple. Perhaps it was a meeting place, for the city council, or for the king. Inside, there is a small theatre, with seats for a few hundred people. Perhaps it was used for meetings, or for music, or for ceremonies. It is one of the mysteries of Petra.

[pause]

{img:children-sitting-edge-of-pool-feet-in-water-reflections-evening-lamps}
The garden and the pool are open to the people of the city, at certain times. In the evening, when it is cooler, families come to walk there, and sit by the water, and enjoy the fresh air. It is the most beautiful place in Petra. Kamilat and Obodat love it. They sit on the edge of the pool, and put their feet in the cool water, and watch the fish swimming, and the reflections of the trees, and the lamps, in the water.

[pause]

Hagaru and Maliku sit on a bench, nearby, and talk quietly. About the business, and the children, and the future. About Kamilat, who is growing up so fast, and will soon be old enough to be married. About Obodat, who is so lively, and so restless. About Wahballahi, who is getting old, and tired. And about the Romans, and the news from the north, which worries Maliku a little.

[long-pause]

The garden is watered by the water system of the city. The water comes in, through pipes and channels, from the spring, and from the cisterns. It flows into the pool, and from the pool into the garden channels, and then away, down the hill. It is a marvel of engineering. Visitors from other lands are always amazed, to find such a green, cool, beautiful place, in the middle of the desert.

[pause]

{img:pavilion-island-moonlight-still-pool-garden-dark-palms-quiet-night}
At night, the garden is closed, and the gates are locked. The moon rises over the mountains, and shines on the still water of the pool. The pavilion on the island stands silent, in the moonlight. The palms rustle a little, in the night breeze. An owl calls from the cliffs. And the water trickles softly, through the channels, all night long, keeping the garden alive, in the desert.

[pause]

The garden and pool of Petra were forgotten for many centuries. They were buried under sand, and earth, and fallen stones. Then, in the 1990s and 2000s, archaeologists discovered them, and dug them out. At first, they did not know what they were. They thought the big terrace was a market. Then they found the pool, and the island, and the channels, and the remains of plants. And they realised that it had been a great garden.

[long-pause]

{img:ad-deir-monastery-facade-petra-mountaintop-huge-carved-urn-afternoon}
High up in the mountains, to the north-west of the city, there is another great monument. It is even bigger than the Treasury. Later, people would call it the Monastery, because, much later, Christian monks lived nearby, and carved crosses on its walls. But in the year 100 AD, it is a sacred place of the Nabataeans. Perhaps a temple, or a hall for religious feasts, in honour of a king who became a god.

[pause]

To reach it, you must climb. There is a long path, carved into the mountain, with about eight hundred steps. It winds up, and up, through narrow gorges, and along the edges of cliffs, and around great rocks. It is steep, and hard, and long. It takes about an hour, at a steady pace. But it is one of the most beautiful walks in the world.

[long-pause]

One morning, in the autumn, Maliku takes the children up to the Monastery. Hagaru stays at home with Wahballahi, who is too old for the climb. They set off early, at dawn, before the heat. They bring water, and bread, and dates. Obodat runs ahead, up the steps, two at a time, and then has to stop, and sit down, out of breath. Kamilat walks steadily, beside her father, and talks to him about everything.

[pause]

{img:steep-rock-cut-steps-mountain-path-father-children-climbing-petra-gorge}
The path winds up through a narrow, shady gorge, with tall red walls. Then it comes out onto the open mountainside, with wide views over the valley below. There are little shrines along the way, carved into the rock, with betyls and niches. Some people stop at them, to rest, and say a prayer. Maliku and the children stop too. Maliku lights a little incense, and gives thanks for the beautiful morning.

[pause]

As they climb higher, the air becomes cooler, and the views become wider. They can see the whole city of Petra, spread out below them, in the valley. The main street, the temples, the theatre, the houses, the gardens, the tombs. The people, like ants, moving along the streets. And the smoke from the kitchen fires, rising straight up into the still morning air.

[long-pause]

{img:monastery-plateau-wide-view-wadi-araba-desert-hazy-mountains-distance}
At last, they come to the top. And there, in front of them, is the Monastery. It is enormous. About fifty metres wide, and almost as tall. It is carved into the face of the mountain, like the Treasury, but plainer, with fewer decorations. Big columns, and a great doorway, and above, a round building, with a cone-shaped roof, and a huge stone urn on top. The urn alone is as tall as two men.

[pause]

In front of the Monastery, there is a wide, flat area, carved out of the mountain top. It is big enough for hundreds of people to gather, for festivals and ceremonies. The children run across it, and stand in front of the great doorway, and look up. They feel very small. Maliku tells them that the Monastery was carved only a few years ago, and that he remembers watching the masons at work, when he was a young man.

[pause]

{img:father-and-children-sitting-on-rock-edge-view-desert-valley-sunlight-wind}
Then they walk to the edge of the mountain, a little way beyond the Monastery. And there, the view is breathtaking. To the west, the mountains drop away, steeply, thousands of feet, into a great valley, the Wadi Araba. It is a long, wide desert valley, running north and south, as far as the eye can see. And beyond it, far away, in the haze, are the mountains of the Negev. On a very clear day, Maliku says, you can almost see Gaza, and the sea.

[pause]

They sit on a rock, at the edge, and eat their bread and dates, and look at the view, for a long time. The wind blows softly. An eagle circles, high above. Nobody says anything. They do not need to. It is one of those moments that stay in your heart, all your life. Many years later, when she is an old woman, Kamilat will still remember this morning, on the mountain, with her father and her brother.

[long-pause]

{img:desert-camp-black-goat-hair-tents-bedouin-family-camels-wadi-evening}
Outside Petra, the desert stretches away, in every direction. It is not all sand, as many people imagine. Much of it is rock, and gravel, and dry, stony hills, with a few tough bushes, and thorny trees. In some places, there are great red sand dunes. In others, there are tall mountains of sandstone and granite, with strange shapes, carved by the wind. And here and there, there are springs, and wells, and green oases.

[pause]

Some of the Nabataeans still live in the desert, as their ancestors did. They live in black tents, made of goat hair, and move from place to place, with their camels, their sheep and their goats. They are proud people, and free, and they know the desert better than anyone. They are cousins of the people of Petra. They come to the city to trade, and to visit the temples, and then go back to the desert.

[long-pause]

Wahballahi has a cousin, called Zaidu, who lives in the desert, with his family. Every autumn, Maliku takes the family out to visit him, for a few days. It is one of the highlights of the year for the children. This year, Wahballahi comes too, riding on a quiet old camel, wrapped in his cloak. He says that he wants to sleep in the desert one more time, under the stars.

[pause]

{img:family-riding-camels-across-red-sand-desert-mountains-sunlit-journey}
They travel for a day, south-east, across the desert, on camels. The children love riding the camels. They sit high up, on the saddles, swaying from side to side. At first, it feels strange, and a little frightening. But they soon get used to it. Obodat says it is like being on a boat, on the sea. Kamilat asks how he knows, since he has never been on a boat. Obodat says he can imagine.

[pause]

In the evening, they reach Zaidu's camp. It is in a wide valley, among tall red mountains, near a spring. There are four or five black tents, and a flock of goats, and a dozen camels, resting on the sand. Zaidu comes out to meet them, with his wife and his sons, and welcomes them warmly. In the desert, hospitality is sacred. A guest is treated like a king.

[pause]

{img:bedouin-tent-interior-guests-seated-carpets-coffee-fire-hospitality}
That night, there is a feast. Zaidu kills a young goat, and his wife and daughters cook it, over the fire, with herbs, and bread, baked on a hot stone. Everyone sits on carpets, in the big tent, and eats together, from a great dish, with their fingers. They drink milk, and water, and a little wine, which Maliku has brought. And they talk, and laugh, and sing, until late in the night.

[long-pause]

Then they lie down to sleep, outside the tent, on blankets, on the soft sand. The night is cool, after the heat of the day. And the sky is full of stars. Thousands and thousands of them. More stars than the children have ever seen, even from the roof of their house in Petra. The Milky Way stretches across the sky, like a river of light. It is completely silent, except for the soft breathing of the camels.

[pause]

{img:night-sky-milky-way-desert-camp-figures-lying-on-sand-stars-silhouettes}
Wahballahi lies beside the children, and points up at the stars. He tells them their names, the names that the caravan men use, to find their way across the desert at night. This star shows the north. That star rises in the east, just before dawn, in the autumn. These stars, together, make the shape of a camel. When he was young, he says, he led the caravans by the stars, night after night, across the great desert. The stars were his map, and his friends.

[pause]

The children listen, sleepily. Obodat falls asleep first, in the middle of a sentence. Then Kamilat. Wahballahi lies awake for a long time, looking at the stars, and remembering. Then he too closes his eyes, and sleeps, peacefully, under the great sky of the desert, as he did when he was young.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-legionaries-marching-desert-road-standards-eagle-distant-dust}
In the year 100 AD, there is news from the north, which worries Maliku and the other merchants. It is about Rome. The Roman Empire is the greatest power in the world. It rules all the lands around the Mediterranean Sea, from Spain to Syria, from Britain to Egypt. And it is all around the Nabataean kingdom. To the north, in Syria. To the west, in Judaea. And to the south-west, in Egypt.

[pause]

For many years, the Nabataeans have been friends of Rome. They have paid tribute, and sent soldiers to help the Romans in their wars, and traded with them peacefully. The Romans have left them alone, to rule themselves, as a client kingdom. But the Romans are always hungry for more land, and more wealth. And the Nabataean kingdom, with its rich trade, is very tempting.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-emperor-trajan-bust-marble-laurel-wreath-stern-face}
In Rome, a new emperor has come to power, two years ago. His name is Trajan. He is a great soldier, and a strong ruler. He wants to make the empire bigger, and stronger, and richer. Many people say that he has his eye on the Nabataean kingdom. The merchants of Petra talk about it, in the market, and in the taverns, in low voices. What will happen to us, if the Romans come?

[pause]

Maliku is not sure. He thinks that perhaps it will not be so bad. The Romans are good for trade. They build roads, and keep the peace, and the Roman cities are full of rich people, who want frankincense. But he is a Nabataean, and proud of his people, and his king, and his gods. He does not want to be ruled by Rome. Nobody in Petra does.

[pause]

In the end, it happened six years later, in 106 AD. King Rabbel the Second passed away, after a long and peaceful reign. And almost at once, the Roman governor of Syria, sent by Trajan, marched into the Nabataean kingdom, with his soldiers. There was very little fighting. Perhaps the Nabataeans knew that they could not win against Rome. Perhaps they made an agreement. Nobody knows exactly what happened.

[pause]

The Nabataean kingdom became a Roman province, called Arabia Petraea. Its capital was moved to Bostra, in the north. Petra was still an important city, and Trajan gave it the title of metropolis, or mother city. But it was no longer the capital of a kingdom. There was no Nabataean king anymore. A Roman governor ruled instead, with Roman soldiers, and Roman laws, and Roman taxes.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-road-milestone-desert-via-nova-traiana-paved-stones-caravans}
The Romans built a great new road, through the province, from Bostra in the north, to the Red Sea, in the south. It was called the Via Nova Traiana, the new road of Trajan. It passed close to Petra. It was paved, and straight, and had milestones, all the way along. It made travel faster and safer. And for a while, Petra continued to grow, and prosper, under the Romans.

[pause]

The Romans built new things in Petra. They paved the main street, and built a great arch, and new temples, and baths. Some Roman soldiers settled in the city, and married local women. Latin and Greek became more common, and the Nabataean language slowly faded. Over time, the people of Petra became more and more Roman, in their clothes, and their customs, and their names.

[pause]

{img:petra-under-rome-mixed-crowd-roman-soldiers-nabataean-merchants-street}
But in 100 AD, all of that is still in the future. For now, King Rabbel is still on his throne, and the Nabataean kingdom is still free. The caravans still come, and the incense still flows, and the stonemasons still carve. The people of Petra live their lives, and do their business, and worship their gods, as they have always done. And Maliku decides not to worry too much about the future. He says that nobody can know what tomorrow will bring. Better to enjoy today.

[long-pause]

{img:petra-byzantine-church-mosaics-floor-animals-later-centuries}
In the centuries after the Romans came, Petra slowly changed. At first, it continued to prosper. But the trade routes began to change. More and more of the goods from India and Arabia went by sea, up the Red Sea, to Egypt, instead of by caravan, across the desert. And other cities, further north, like Palmyra, in Syria, took more and more of the caravan trade. Petra became less important, and less rich.

[pause]

Then, in the year 363 AD, there was a great earthquake. It shook the whole region, and destroyed many buildings in Petra. Houses collapsed, and temples fell, and the water channels were broken. Many people left the city. Some of the buildings were rebuilt, but many were not. Petra was never quite the same again.

[long-pause]

{img:petra-papyrus-scrolls-charred-church-archive-ancient-documents}
By then, many of the people of Petra had become Christians. They built churches in the city, with beautiful mosaic floors, showing animals, and birds, and plants, and the seasons. Petra had its own bishop. Some of the old tombs were turned into churches, and crosses were carved on their walls. In one of the churches, many years later, archaeologists found a room full of burned scrolls, written in Greek. They were the records of a family, about their land, and their marriages, and their property, from the sixth century.

[pause]

Then there was another earthquake, in the year 551, which damaged the city again. After that, Petra slowly emptied. The people moved away, to other towns, and villages. The great buildings crumbled. Sand and earth and fallen stones filled the streets, and the pool, and the garden. Only a few people stayed, living in the tombs and the caves, with their sheep and goats.

[pause]

{img:crusader-castle-ruins-petra-hilltop-al-wueira-stone-walls}
In the twelfth century, the Crusaders came to the region, knights from Europe, who had come to fight in the Holy Land. They built two small castles in and near Petra, on the tops of hills, to guard the roads. But they did not stay long. When they left, Petra was forgotten again, by the rest of the world. For more than six hundred years, almost no one from outside came there.

[pause]

Only the local Bedouin people knew about Petra. They lived in the area, and grazed their animals among the ruins, and sometimes lived in the old tombs. They told stories about the ancient buildings. They said that the Treasury had been built by a pharaoh, who hid his gold in the urn. They said that the great temple was the palace of the pharaoh's daughter. They kept the secret of Petra, and did not want strangers coming there.

[long-pause]

{img:petra-tombs-moonlight-empty-valley-centuries-forgotten-silent}
For all those centuries, the city stood empty and silent. The tombs, and the temples, and the theatre, carved in the rock, waiting. The wind blew through the Siq, and the sand drifted into the doorways. The winter rains came, and the floods. The sun rose, and set, and the red cliffs glowed, every morning and every evening, with nobody to see them, except the shepherds and their goats.

[pause]

In those long, quiet centuries, the shepherds sometimes sheltered in the old tombs, from the winter rain, or the summer sun. They lit their fires inside, and the smoke blackened the ceilings. They made pens for their goats in the old carved rooms. And at night, they sat in the doorways, and looked out at the empty city, in the moonlight, and told each other stories about the people who had lived there, long, long ago.

[pause]

And yet, the carved monuments survived. Because they were carved from the living rock, and not built of separate stones, they did not fall down, like the buildings of other cities. The earthquakes cracked them, and the wind and the rain wore them away, a little. But they stayed. The Treasury, the Monastery, the Royal Tombs, the theatre, and hundreds of others. Waiting, patiently, for the world to find them again.

[pause]

And in the year 1812, someone did.

[long-pause]

{img:johann-burckhardt-1812-disguised-arab-traveller-robes-beard-guide-goat}
In the year 1812, a young Swiss explorer was travelling through the lands east of the Jordan River. His name was Johann Ludwig Burckhardt. He was twenty-seven years old. He had studied Arabic, and Islam, and the customs of the Arab world, for years, so that he could travel in disguise. He dressed as an Arab, and called himself Sheikh Ibrahim. And he spoke Arabic so well that many people believed he really was an Arab scholar.

[pause]

Burckhardt was on his way to Egypt, and then, he hoped, to Africa, to find the source of the River Niger. As he travelled, he heard the local people talking about an ancient city, hidden in the mountains, in a valley called Wadi Musa. Full of wonderful buildings, carved in the rock. No European had seen it, for hundreds of years. Burckhardt wanted very much to see it.

[long-pause]

But he had a problem. The local people did not like strangers coming to the valley. They were suspicious of foreigners, and afraid that they had come to steal the treasure, which they believed was hidden in the ruins. If they discovered that Burckhardt was a European, he might be in danger. So he had to be very careful.

[pause]

{img:traveller-and-local-guide-walking-through-siq-goat-on-rope-1812}
He thought of a clever plan. He told the people that he had made a vow, to sacrifice a goat at the tomb of Aaron, the brother of Moses, which stood on top of a mountain, beyond the valley. The local people respected this. They agreed to guide him there. And so, on the twenty-second of August, 1812, Burckhardt walked through the Siq, with a local guide, leading a goat.

[pause]

He came out of the Siq, and saw the Treasury, glowing in the sunlight. He must have been amazed. But he could not show it. He could not stop, and stare, or make drawings, or take notes, or his guide would become suspicious. So he walked on, looking around as much as he could, trying to remember everything. He passed the theatre, and the tombs, and the great temple. And then he climbed the mountain, and made his sacrifice, as he had promised.

[pause]

{img:explorer-writing-secret-notes-by-lamplight-tent-journal-1812}
Burckhardt wrote about Petra in his journal, that night, in secret. He described what he had seen, as well as he could. And he wrote that he was almost certain that this was the ancient city of Petra, which was mentioned in the old Greek and Roman books, and which had been lost for so long. He was right. Sadly, Burckhardt never saw Petra again. He passed away five years later, in Cairo, of an illness, at the age of only thirty-two. His journals were published after his death, and the world learned about Petra.

[long-pause]

After Burckhardt, more travellers came. In 1828, a French artist, Léon de Laborde, came, and made drawings of the monuments. In 1839, a Scottish artist, David Roberts, came, and painted beautiful pictures of Petra, which were printed, and sold all over Europe. People were amazed by them. A city of rose-red stone, carved into the cliffs, in the middle of the desert. It seemed like something from a dream, or a fairy tale.

[pause]

{img:david-roberts-style-painting-petra-treasury-travellers-camels-1839-romantic}
In 1845, an English poet called John William Burgon wrote a poem about Petra. He had never been there. He had only seen the pictures, and read the books. But his poem became very famous. It ended with the words, a rose-red city, half as old as time. Since then, Petra has been known as the rose-red city. When Burgon finally visited Petra, many years later, he admitted that the stone was not really rose-red, but more of a brown and pink and orange. But by then, it was too late. The name had stuck.

[pause]

Over the next hundred years, more and more people came to Petra. Explorers, artists, writers, and archaeologists. They studied the monuments, and drew them, and measured them, and dug among the ruins. Slowly, they began to understand the story of Petra, and of the Nabataeans, and their kingdom, and their trade, and their gods. And they are still learning, today.

[long-pause]

{img:today-petra-treasury-tourists-crowd-camels-morning-light-visitors}
Today, Petra is one of the most famous places in the world. In 1985, it was made a World Heritage Site, a place of special importance to all of humanity. In 2007, it was chosen as one of the New Seven Wonders of the World, in a vote by millions of people from all over the globe. And every year, hundreds of thousands of visitors come from every country, to see it.

[pause]

They come through the Siq, just as the caravans did, two thousand years ago. They walk along the same paving stones, past the same water channels, under the same towering red walls. And they come out, at the end, in front of the Treasury, glowing in the sunlight. And they feel the same wonder, and the same amazement, that travellers have always felt, at that moment.

[long-pause]

{img:today-petra-monastery-hikers-resting-steps-view-tea-stall}
Many of the visitors walk all the way through the city, to the theatre, and the Royal Tombs, and the colonnaded street, and the great temple. And many of them climb the eight hundred steps, up to the Monastery, as Maliku and his children did. At the top, there is a little café now, in a cave, where you can drink sweet tea, and rest, and look at the view. The same view, over the Wadi Araba, that Kamilat remembered all her life.

[pause]

The local Bedouin people still live in the area. Until the 1980s, some of them lived in the old tombs and caves of Petra itself. Then the government built them a new village, nearby, and they moved there. Many of them now work in Petra, as guides, and camel drivers, and donkey drivers, and selling souvenirs and tea. They know every corner of the ancient city, and every story about it.

[pause]

{img:today-petra-by-night-treasury-candles-luminaries-siq-silent-crowd}
On some evenings, there is a special event, called Petra by Night. The Siq is lit by hundreds of candles, in paper bags, all along the path. Visitors walk through it in silence, in the dark, by candlelight. At the end, in front of the Treasury, there are more than a thousand candles, glowing on the ground. And someone plays music, on a Bedouin flute, and tells stories. It is very beautiful, and very peaceful.

[pause]

Petra has also appeared in films. The most famous is a film from 1989, in which the hero, an archaeologist with a hat and a whip, rides through the Siq, on a horse, and comes to the Treasury, in search of a sacred treasure. Many people today know Petra from that film. And some of them come, because of it, to see the real thing.

[long-pause]

{img:today-archaeologists-excavating-petra-trench-brushes-sandstone-ruins}
Archaeologists are still working in Petra. Only a small part of the ancient city has been dug up so far. There is still much more, buried under the sand and the earth. New discoveries are made almost every year. In 2016, using satellites and drones, archaeologists found a huge monument, hidden in plain sight, near the city, that nobody had noticed before. Who knows what else is still waiting, under the ground?

[pause]

Petra faces some dangers today. The soft sandstone is being worn away, slowly, by the wind, and the rain, and the salt. And by the millions of feet of visitors, and the hands that touch the walls. Floods are a danger too, as they were in ancient times. So people are working hard to protect Petra, and to repair the old Nabataean dams and channels, so that the city can be preserved, for the future.

[pause]

And in the evening, when the visitors have gone, and the valley is quiet again, the sun sets behind the mountains, and the red cliffs glow, just as they did two thousand years ago. Red, and pink, and gold, and purple. And then the colours fade, and the stars come out, over the silent city.

[long-pause]

{img:petra-100ad-evening-cliffs-glowing-red-city-lamps-lighting-up}
So we leave Petra tonight, in the year 100 AD, as the evening falls. The sun is going down, behind the mountains to the west. And the whole valley is glowing. The cliffs, the tombs, the temples, are all red, and pink, and orange, and gold, in the last light of the day. It is the most beautiful time in Petra. Everyone stops, for a moment, to look.

[pause]

In the market, the shops are closing. The merchants are counting their money, and putting away their goods. The caravan men are gathering in the taverns, for their supper. The stonemasons have put down their tools, and are walking home, covered in dust. And in the temples, the priests are lighting the evening lamps, and burning incense, to the gods.

[long-pause]

{img:courtyard-under-vine-family-evening-meal-lamps-grandfather-children}
In Maliku's house, on the hillside, the family is gathering in the courtyard, under the vine. Hagaru and the servants bring out the evening meal. Bread, and cheese, and olives, in Kamilat's painted bowl, and grapes, and a stew of lentils. And a jar of wine, from Hagaru's own vineyard. They sit together, on cushions, and eat, and talk about the day. The lamps are lit, and their light flickers on the painted walls.

[pause]

Wahballahi tells one more story, about the caravans, and the desert, and the stars. Obodat falls asleep, with his head on his grandfather's knee. Kamilat sits beside her mother, and listens, and looks up at the sky, through the leaves of the vine. She is thinking about the future. About the market, and the caravans, and the faraway lands she wants to see. And about her father, and her mother, and her brother, and her grandfather, and how much she loves them.

[long-pause]

{img:family-on-flat-roof-under-stars-sleeping-mats-petra-cliffs-moon}
Then the family goes up to the roof, to sleep, under the stars. It is still warm, in the early autumn. They lie on their mats and cushions, and look up at the sky. The stars are coming out, one by one, and then in thousands. The moon is rising, over the mountains, and shining on the cliffs, and the tombs, and the city. And far away, in the desert, a jackal calls, and another answers.

[pause]

Down below, the city is going quiet. The last lamps are going out, in the houses. The streets are empty. The water trickles softly through the channels, from the spring, as it does day and night. In the garden, by the pool, the palms rustle in the night breeze. And in the Siq, the great carved camels walk on, in the dark, toward the city, as they will for two thousand years.
