In the year 100 AD, the king of the Nabataeans is Rabbel the Second. He became king when he was just a boy, about thirty years earlier, after his father passed away. At first, his mother ruled for him, as regent, until he was old enough. Now, he is a grown man, in his forties, and a wise and respected king. He is married to Queen Gamilath, and they have children. Their faces appear together on the coins of the kingdom.

The Nabataean kings are not like the kings of other lands, the emperors of Rome, or the kings of Persia, with their great palaces, and their thousands of servants, and their cruel punishments. A Greek writer called Strabo described them, about eighty years before our story, from the reports of a friend who had lived in Petra. He said that the king was very modest, and lived simply. He said that the king often served his guests himself, at his own table.

--

{img:nabataean-banquet-guests-reclining-singers-musicians-wine-lamps}
Strabo also described the way the Nabataeans liked to feast. He said that they held banquets, for groups of thirteen people at a time, with two singing girls for each banquet, to entertain them. Each guest could drink eleven cups of wine, from golden cups, and no more. They had few slaves, Strabo said, and most of the work was done by their own families. And sometimes, he said, the king had to give an account of his rule to the people, in an assembly. Strabo was surprised by this. In most kingdoms, the king answered to nobody.

The king lives in a palace, somewhere in the city. Nobody today knows exactly where. Perhaps on a hilltop, near the centre, with a view over the city. It is probably a grand building, with courtyards, and halls, and gardens, and painted walls, and fine furniture. But not as grand as the palaces of Rome. The Nabataeans spend their money on their temples, and their tombs, more than on their palaces.

{img:king-procession-main-street-petra-crowds-cheering-banners-camels-ceremony}
One day, in the autumn, the king passes through the city, in a procession, on his way to the great temple, for a festival. The streets are crowded with people, who have come to see him. Maliku and his family are there, on the steps of a shop, near the temple, with a good view. There is music, and singing, and the smell of incense, burning in great bowls, along the way.

The procession comes slowly along the main street. First, the musicians, with drums and pipes and cymbals. Then the priests, in white robes, carrying incense and offerings. Then the soldiers of the king's guard, on camels and horses, with spears and shields, shining in the sun. And then the king himself, riding on a white horse, in a purple robe, with a golden band around his head. He smiles, and raises his hand, to the people. They cheer, and call out blessings.

--

Behind the king comes the queen, in a covered carriage, with her ladies. And then the king's family, and his ministers, and his officials. The chief minister of the kingdom is called the brother of the king, even if he is not really his brother. It is a title of honour. He is a powerful man, who runs the government, and the army, and the taxes, for the king.

{img:queen-gamilath-carriage-procession-veiled-ladies-jewels-sunlight}
At the temple, the king climbs the steps, and goes inside, with the priests, to make the offerings to Dushara. The crowd waits outside, in the courtyard, and around the temple. When the king comes out again, there is a great cheer. Then the feasting begins. The king gives food, and wine, to the people, and there is music, and dancing, in the streets, until late in the night.

Kamilat watches the king very carefully, as he passes by. She thinks he looks kind, and a little tired. She wonders what it is like to be a king. To have so many people looking at you, all the time. To have to make so many decisions. To carry the whole kingdom on your shoulders. She decides that she would rather be a merchant. It seems much more fun.
