Petra lies in the desert. It rains there only a few times a year, in the winter. The summers are long, and hot, and completely dry. And yet, in the year 100 AD, Petra is a green city, with gardens, and fountains, and pools, and trees, and about twenty or thirty thousand people. How is this possible? The answer is water. The Nabataeans are masters of water.

They learned it in the desert, long ago, when they were nomads. They knew how to find water, and how to save it, and how to hide it. And when they built Petra, they used all that knowledge, and more. They built one of the cleverest water systems of the ancient world. Channels, pipes, dams, cisterns, and pools, all working together, to catch every drop of water, and bring it to the city.

--

{img:spring-ain-musa-water-flowing-rocks-green-plants-desert-valley}
The most important source of water is a spring, outside the city, in the valley to the east. It is called the Spring of Moses, because people say that Moses struck the rock there, with his staff, and water came out. It gives fresh, clear water, all year round. The Nabataeans bring this water into the city, along the channels in the Siq, and through pipes of baked clay, fitted together, mile after mile.

The pipes are made very carefully. Each one is about thirty centimetres long, and they fit together, end to end, sealed with mortar. The channels slope very gently, all the way, so that the water flows steadily, but not too fast. There are little basins along the way, where the water slows down, and the sand and dirt settle to the bottom, so that the water arriving in the city is clean.

{img:rock-cut-cistern-steps-down-dark-water-cool-petra-ancient}
But the spring is not enough, for a whole city. So the Nabataeans also catch the rainwater. When it rains in the winter, the water rushes down the bare rock of the mountains, in streams and waterfalls. The Nabataeans have carved channels all over the mountainsides, to catch this water, and lead it into cisterns. Hundreds of them, all over the city. Big tanks, carved into the rock, or built under the houses, where the water stays cool, and clean, all through the summer.

Maliku's house has its own cistern, under the courtyard. In the winter, the rain falls on the roof, and runs down into it, through a pipe. By the spring, it is full. And all through the summer, the family draws water from it, with a bucket on a rope. It is cool and sweet. Hagaru is very careful with it. Nothing is ever wasted. The water for washing is used again, for the garden, and the plants.

--

There are dams, too. In the valleys around the city, the Nabataeans have built walls of stone, across the dry riverbeds, to slow the floods, and hold back the water, so that it soaks into the ground. Behind the dams, the soil stays moist, and the farmers can grow crops. Wheat, and barley, and grapes, and olives, and figs. The desert around Petra is not as empty as it looks. There are fields, and terraces, and orchards, wherever there is a little water.

{img:terraced-fields-desert-valley-stone-walls-green-crops-nabataean-farm}
The water engineers of Petra are respected men. They inspect the channels, and the pipes, and the cisterns, and repair them when they break. They clear the sand and the stones, after the winter floods. They know exactly where every drop of water goes. Without them, the city would die. And everyone in Petra knows it. Water is life, in the desert. And the Nabataeans never forget it.

In the centre of the city, there is a great pool, with a little island in the middle, and a pavilion on the island. Around it, there is a garden, full of trees and flowers. In a city in the desert, where water is so precious, this pool is a sign of great wealth and power. It says to every visitor, look how rich we are. We have so much water, we can use it just for beauty. The children love to sit by the pool, in the evening, and watch the reflections of the lamps on the water.
