The Nabataeans are a religious people. They have many gods, and they worship them in many ways. Their most important god is called Dushara. His name means the one from the Shara, which is the name of the mountains around Petra. He is the god of the mountains, and of the king, and of the whole Nabataean people. He is a god of the sun, perhaps, and of the sky.

The most important goddess is called al-Uzza. Her name means the mighty one. She is a goddess of the morning star, and of love, and of power. The Greeks and Romans in Petra say that she is like their own goddess Aphrodite, or Venus. And there is Allat, another great goddess, and Manat, the goddess of fate, and many others, gods of the city, and of the desert, and of the caravans, and of the family.

--

At the far end of the main street, there is the greatest temple in Petra. It is a huge square building, of stone, standing on a high platform, with wide steps leading up to it. Inside, it is tall, and dim, and holy. The people call it the Temple of Dushara. Later, the local people would call it the Palace of the Pharaoh's Daughter, because of an old story. In front of it, there is a big paved courtyard, with a great altar, where offerings are made.

{img:betyl-stone-block-niche-carved-eyes-nose-simple-god-image-nabataean}
As we have seen, the Nabataeans do not usually make statues of their gods. Instead, they worship simple blocks of stone, called betyls. Sometimes, the betyls have eyes, and a nose, carved on them, very simply. But mostly, they are just plain, square or tall blocks of stone, standing in a niche, or on a platform. The Nabataeans believe that the god lives in the stone. You can see them everywhere in Petra. In the temples, in the Siq, on the mountainsides, beside the roads.

Above the city, on the tops of the mountains, there are high places. Flat areas, carved out of the rock, at the very top of the mountains, close to the sky. There are altars there, and carved basins, and steps. The most famous is called the High Place of Sacrifice. It is reached by a long, steep stairway, carved into the mountain. At festivals, the priests climb up there, with the people, to make offerings to the gods. Incense is burned, and prayers are said.

{img:high-place-mountaintop-petra-carved-altar-view-over-valley-dawn}
Hagaru takes the children up to the high place sometimes, early in the morning, before the heat. It is a long climb, up hundreds of steps, cut into the rock. They stop to rest, many times, and look at the view. At the top, there is a wonderful view over the whole city, and the mountains, and the desert beyond. Hagaru burns a little frankincense, from her husband's storeroom, on the altar, and says a prayer for her family. The smoke rises slowly, straight up into the still morning air.

--

At home, too, the family has its own little shrine. A niche in the wall of the courtyard, with a small betyl in it, and a lamp, and a little incense burner. Every morning, Hagaru lights the lamp, and burns a little incense, and says a prayer. For her husband, on his journeys. For her children, to grow up healthy and happy. For old Wahballahi, to stay well. And for rain, in the winter, to fill the cisterns.

The Nabataeans also honour their kings, after they have passed away. One of the old kings, Obodas, became a god after his death. People built temples for him, and made offerings to him. Obodat, who is named after him, is very proud of this. He tells his friends that he is named after a god. His sister says that the god would not be very impressed with his manners. Obodat sticks out his tongue at her.

{img:family-household-shrine-niche-lamp-incense-mother-praying-courtyard-morning}
Petra welcomes the gods of other peoples too. The Greek and Roman traders bring their own gods, and build little shrines to them. The Egyptian merchants worship Isis, the great mother goddess of Egypt, and there is a shrine to her, carved into the rock, on the edge of the city. Nobody minds. In Petra, there is room for everyone, and for every god. It is a city of travellers, and travellers bring their gods with them.
