The heart of Petra is the main street. It runs along the bottom of the valley, beside the dry riverbed, from the end of the Siq toward the great temple at the far end of the city. On both sides, there are rows of tall columns, holding up covered walkways, which give shade from the sun. And behind the columns, there are shops, and workshops, and offices, and taverns.

The street is always busy, but especially in the morning, and in the late afternoon. It is full of people from many lands. Nabataeans, in their long robes and headcloths. Greeks, in their short tunics. Romans, with their togas and cloaks. Jews, from Judaea, and Egyptians, and Syrians, and Arabs from the far south, and even, sometimes, a merchant from India, with his turban and his gold earrings. A dozen languages are spoken.

--

{img:market-stalls-spices-baskets-fruit-cloth-pottery-colourful-ancient-petra}
There are shops selling everything. Fruit and vegetables, from the farms around the city. Grapes, and figs, and dates, and melons, and pomegranates. Bread, and cakes made with honey, and roasted nuts. Meat, and cheese, and olives, and oil. Spices, in great open baskets, in every colour. Red, and yellow, and brown, and green. The smell is wonderful. Cinnamon, and pepper, and cumin, and saffron, and cardamom.

There are shops selling cloth. Fine linen from Egypt, and wool from Syria, and cotton from India, and very rarely, a piece of shining silk from China, which costs a fortune. There are shops selling jewellery. Silver bracelets, and earrings, and necklaces of glass beads, and gold rings, with precious stones. There are shops selling glass bottles, and perfumes, and oils, and the beautiful painted pottery of Petra.

There are money changers too, at their little tables, with their scales and their boxes of coins. Coins from everywhere. Nabataean coins, with the heads of the king and queen on them. Roman coins, with the head of the emperor. Greek coins, Egyptian coins, Jewish coins. The money changers weigh them, and test them, and change them, for a small fee. Kamilat loves to look at all the different coins, with their different faces and pictures.

Kamilat often goes to the market with her mother. They buy food for the house, and cloth for new clothes, and sometimes a little present. Hagaru bargains hard. She knows the price of everything, and she does not let anyone cheat her. She argues, and laughs, and walks away, and comes back, and in the end, she always gets a good price. Kamilat watches her, and learns. She thinks her mother would make a very good merchant.

On the main street, there are also the public baths. A big building, with warm rooms, and hot rooms, and cold pools, like the baths of Rome. The men go there in the afternoon, to wash, and relax, and talk, and do business. The women go at other times. It is one of the pleasures of city life. And it shows, once again, how much water the Nabataeans have, in their desert city.

In the evening, when the shops close, the street becomes quieter. But there are still taverns open, where people eat and drink, and talk, and listen to music. Lamps are lit, along the colonnades, and they cast long shadows on the paving stones. The caravan men sit in the taverns, telling stories of their journeys. And the people of Petra stroll along the street, in the cool of the evening, greeting their friends, and enjoying the night air.

{img:petra-street-evening-oil-lamps-colonnade-shadows-strolling-people}
Maliku and his family sometimes walk along the main street in the evening, after their supper. They walk slowly, and greet their neighbours, and stop to talk. The children run ahead, and look in the shops, and buy a little cake, or some roasted nuts. And then they walk home, up the hill, under the stars, with the sound of music and laughter fading behind them.
