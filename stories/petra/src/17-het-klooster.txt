High up in the mountains, to the north-west of the city, there is another great monument. It is even bigger than the Treasury. Later, people would call it the Monastery, because, much later, Christian monks lived nearby, and carved crosses on its walls. But in the year 100 AD, it is a sacred place of the Nabataeans. Perhaps a temple, or a hall for religious feasts, in honour of a king who became a god.

To reach it, you must climb. There is a long path, carved into the mountain, with about eight hundred steps. It winds up, and up, through narrow gorges, and along the edges of cliffs, and around great rocks. It is steep, and hard, and long. It takes about an hour, at a steady pace. But it is one of the most beautiful walks in the world.

--

One morning, in the autumn, Maliku takes the children up to the Monastery. Hagaru stays at home with Wahballahi, who is too old for the climb. They set off early, at dawn, before the heat. They bring water, and bread, and dates. Obodat runs ahead, up the steps, two at a time, and then has to stop, and sit down, out of breath. Kamilat walks steadily, beside her father, and talks to him about everything.

{img:steep-rock-cut-steps-mountain-path-father-children-climbing-petra-gorge}
The path winds up through a narrow, shady gorge, with tall red walls. Then it comes out onto the open mountainside, with wide views over the valley below. There are little shrines along the way, carved into the rock, with betyls and niches. Some people stop at them, to rest, and say a prayer. Maliku and the children stop too. Maliku lights a little incense, and gives thanks for the beautiful morning.

As they climb higher, the air becomes cooler, and the views become wider. They can see the whole city of Petra, spread out below them, in the valley. The main street, the temples, the theatre, the houses, the gardens, the tombs. The people, like ants, moving along the streets. And the smoke from the kitchen fires, rising straight up into the still morning air.

--

{img:monastery-plateau-wide-view-wadi-araba-desert-hazy-mountains-distance}
At last, they come to the top. And there, in front of them, is the Monastery. It is enormous. About fifty metres wide, and almost as tall. It is carved into the face of the mountain, like the Treasury, but plainer, with fewer decorations. Big columns, and a great doorway, and above, a round building, with a cone-shaped roof, and a huge stone urn on top. The urn alone is as tall as two men.

In front of the Monastery, there is a wide, flat area, carved out of the mountain top. It is big enough for hundreds of people to gather, for festivals and ceremonies. The children run across it, and stand in front of the great doorway, and look up. They feel very small. Maliku tells them that the Monastery was carved only a few years ago, and that he remembers watching the masons at work, when he was a young man.

{img:father-and-children-sitting-on-rock-edge-view-desert-valley-sunlight-wind}
Then they walk to the edge of the mountain, a little way beyond the Monastery. And there, the view is breathtaking. To the west, the mountains drop away, steeply, thousands of feet, into a great valley, the Wadi Araba. It is a long, wide desert valley, running north and south, as far as the eye can see. And beyond it, far away, in the haze, are the mountains of the Negev. On a very clear day, Maliku says, you can almost see Gaza, and the sea.

They sit on a rock, at the edge, and eat their bread and dates, and look at the view, for a long time. The wind blows softly. An eagle circles, high above. Nobody says anything. They do not need to. It is one of those moments that stay in your heart, all your life. Many years later, when she is an old woman, Kamilat will still remember this morning, on the mountain, with her father and her brother.
