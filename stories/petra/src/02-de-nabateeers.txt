Long ago, perhaps six hundred years before our story, the Nabataeans were wandering herders, in the deserts of northern Arabia. They lived in tents of black goat hair. They moved from place to place, with their camels, their sheep and their goats, looking for water and grass. They knew the desert better than anyone. Every well, every spring, every hidden pool of rainwater, among the rocks.

An old Greek writer described them, about four hundred years before our story. He said that they lived in the open air, and did not build houses, or plant crops, or drink wine. They had a law against it, he said, so that they would always stay free, and could never be conquered. They kept their water in secret cisterns, dug into the rock, and hidden, so that no enemy could find them.

--

But the Nabataeans were clever. They saw that the desert was not empty. Across it, every year, came the caravans, carrying frankincense and myrrh, from the south of Arabia, to the cities of Egypt, Syria, and Greece. The caravans needed guides, and water, and protection. And the Nabataeans could give them all three. So they began to work with the caravans, and then to trade themselves.

Over time, they grew rich. They stopped wandering, and began to settle down. They built a city, in the valley of Petra, which was a perfect place. It was hidden among the mountains, easy to defend, and there was a spring of fresh water nearby. They began to farm the land around, and to plant vines, and olives, and wheat. And they began to carve their tombs and temples, into the cliffs.

{img:nabataean-king-coin-silver-profile-crown-ancient-script}
They had kings. The first known king of the Nabataeans was called Aretas, more than two hundred and fifty years before our story. After him came many others. Kings called Obodas, and Rabbel, and Malichus, and Aretas again. Some of them were great warriors, and fought against the Greeks, and the Jews, and the Romans. But mostly, the Nabataeans preferred trade to war. They made treaties, and alliances, and paid tribute when they had to.

The Nabataeans spoke a form of Arabic, in their daily lives. But they wrote in Aramaic, the common language of the Middle East in those days. They had their own way of writing it, with flowing, joined-up letters. Over many centuries, the Nabataean script slowly changed, and became the Arabic script, which is used by hundreds of millions of people today.

By the year 100 AD, the Nabataean kingdom is large. It stretches from Damascus, in the north, to the Red Sea, in the south, and deep into Arabia. It includes the land of the Negev desert, and the coast near Gaza. There are other Nabataean cities too. Bostra, in the north, and Hegra, far to the south, in Arabia, with its own great carved tombs. But Petra is the capital, the heart of the kingdom, and the home of the king.

The king in the year 100 AD is Rabbel the Second. He has ruled for about thirty years. He is a peaceful king, and the people love him. They call him the one who brought life and deliverance to his people. His kingdom lives in peace with its great neighbour, Rome, which rules almost all the lands around the Mediterranean Sea. But Rome is very powerful, and very close. And the Nabataeans know that they must be careful.

The people of Petra are proud of their history. They still remember their nomad ancestors, and the old ways of the desert. Many families still keep camels, and black tents, and go out into the desert in the spring, when there is grass for the animals. And the old virtues of the desert are still respected in the city. Hospitality, and loyalty, and honesty, and courage, and generosity to strangers.
