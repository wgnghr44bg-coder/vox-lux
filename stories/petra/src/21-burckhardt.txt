In the year 1812, a young Swiss explorer was travelling through the lands east of the Jordan River. His name was Johann Ludwig Burckhardt. He was twenty-seven years old. He had studied Arabic, and Islam, and the customs of the Arab world, for years, so that he could travel in disguise. He dressed as an Arab, and called himself Sheikh Ibrahim. And he spoke Arabic so well that many people believed he really was an Arab scholar.

Burckhardt was on his way to Egypt, and then, he hoped, to Africa, to find the source of the River Niger. As he travelled, he heard the local people talking about an ancient city, hidden in the mountains, in a valley called Wadi Musa. Full of wonderful buildings, carved in the rock. No European had seen it, for hundreds of years. Burckhardt wanted very much to see it.

--

But he had a problem. The local people did not like strangers coming to the valley. They were suspicious of foreigners, and afraid that they had come to steal the treasure, which they believed was hidden in the ruins. If they discovered that Burckhardt was a European, he might be in danger. So he had to be very careful.

{img:traveller-and-local-guide-walking-through-siq-goat-on-rope-1812}
He thought of a clever plan. He told the people that he had made a vow, to sacrifice a goat at the tomb of Aaron, the brother of Moses, which stood on top of a mountain, beyond the valley. The local people respected this. They agreed to guide him there. And so, on the twenty-second of August, 1812, Burckhardt walked through the Siq, with a local guide, leading a goat.

He came out of the Siq, and saw the Treasury, glowing in the sunlight. He must have been amazed. But he could not show it. He could not stop, and stare, or make drawings, or take notes, or his guide would become suspicious. So he walked on, looking around as much as he could, trying to remember everything. He passed the theatre, and the tombs, and the great temple. And then he climbed the mountain, and made his sacrifice, as he had promised.

{img:explorer-writing-secret-notes-by-lamplight-tent-journal-1812}
Burckhardt wrote about Petra in his journal, that night, in secret. He described what he had seen, as well as he could. And he wrote that he was almost certain that this was the ancient city of Petra, which was mentioned in the old Greek and Roman books, and which had been lost for so long. He was right. Sadly, Burckhardt never saw Petra again. He passed away five years later, in Cairo, of an illness, at the age of only thirty-two. His journals were published after his death, and the world learned about Petra.

--

After Burckhardt, more travellers came. In 1828, a French artist, Léon de Laborde, came, and made drawings of the monuments. In 1839, a Scottish artist, David Roberts, came, and painted beautiful pictures of Petra, which were printed, and sold all over Europe. People were amazed by them. A city of rose-red stone, carved into the cliffs, in the middle of the desert. It seemed like something from a dream, or a fairy tale.

{img:david-roberts-style-painting-petra-treasury-travellers-camels-1839-romantic}
In 1845, an English poet called John William Burgon wrote a poem about Petra. He had never been there. He had only seen the pictures, and read the books. But his poem became very famous. It ended with the words, a rose-red city, half as old as time. Since then, Petra has been known as the rose-red city. When Burgon finally visited Petra, many years later, he admitted that the stone was not really rose-red, but more of a brown and pink and orange. But by then, it was too late. The name had stuck.

Over the next hundred years, more and more people came to Petra. Explorers, artists, writers, and archaeologists. They studied the monuments, and drew them, and measured them, and dug among the ruins. Slowly, they began to understand the story of Petra, and of the Nabataeans, and their kingdom, and their trade, and their gods. And they are still learning, today.
