{img:st-louis-levee-1850-dawn-steamboats-lined-up-smokestacks-mist-river}
Tonight we travel back about a hundred and seventy-five years, to the year 1850. We go to the middle of America, to the banks of a great, wide, brown river. It is the Mississippi. And on its waters, tall white boats with smoking chimneys are carrying people and goods up and down the river, day and night.

[long-pause]

It is early morning in spring, in the city of St. Louis. A soft mist lies over the river. Along the riverbank, there is a long, sloping stone shore, called the levee. And along the levee, as far as you can see, there are steamboats, lying side by side, their noses pointed at the shore. There are dozens of them. Their tall black chimneys rise up through the mist like a forest of dark trees.

[pause]

The levee is already busy. Men are rolling barrels and carrying sacks. Carts pulled by mules rattle over the stones. Bales of cotton, crates of goods and piles of firewood are stacked everywhere. Bells are ringing on the boats. Steam is hissing. And somewhere, a deep steam whistle sounds, long and low, echoing across the water. It is the sound of a steamboat getting ready to leave.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:white-paddle-steamboat-1850-decks-railings-twin-chimneys-pilothouse}
The steamboats of the Mississippi are wonderful to look at. They are long and wide and low in the water, painted white, with two or three decks stacked on top of each other, like the layers of a wedding cake. They have fancy wooden railings and decorations. On top, there is a little glass house, where the pilot steers. And at the front, two tall chimneys rise high into the air, sometimes with decorations on top, like crowns.

[pause]

In 1850, the steamboat is the queen of the river. There are hardly any railways yet in this part of America, and the roads are very bad. So almost everything travels by river. Cotton, sugar, wheat, corn, pork, whisky, furniture, machines and people. Hundreds of steamboats sail on the Mississippi and its rivers. They connect the farms and towns of the middle of America with the great port of New Orleans, and with the rest of the world.

[pause]

{img:boy-fifteen-1850-standing-levee-looking-at-steamboat-carpet-bag-morning}
Tonight, we will take a journey on one of these steamboats, down the Mississippi, from St. Louis to New Orleans. We will travel with a boy of fifteen, called Ben. He is about to start learning the most admired job on the river. He will become an apprentice pilot, learning to steer the great steamboats along the winding river, by day and by night.

[long-pause]

We will explore the boat, from the grand saloon to the boiler deck. We will meet the passengers and the crew. We will stop at woodyards and little river towns. We will see the river at night, under the stars, and in the thick fog of the morning. And we will see the cotton fields of the South, where enslaved people were forced to work, for we cannot tell the story of the river in 1850 without them. We will tell their part of the story carefully and gently.

[pause]

{img:steamboat-pulling-away-from-levee-paddlewheel-turning-smoke-sunrise}
So let us begin on the levee at St. Louis, on a spring morning in 1850. The mist is lifting from the brown water. The sun is rising over the Illinois shore, on the other side of the river. And on the deck of a steamboat called the Clara Belle, a boy with a carpet bag is climbing the stairs to the top of the boat, to meet the pilot who will teach him the river.

[pause]

Far up the river, in a little town in Missouri called Hannibal, there lives another boy of the same age. He also watches the steamboats every day, and dreams of becoming a pilot. His name is Samuel Clemens. One day, he will become a pilot himself. And later, he will become one of the most famous writers in America, under the name Mark Twain. We will meet him again, later in our story.

[long-pause]

{img:mississippi-river-aerial-feeling-wide-brown-water-forests-both-banks-bends}
The Mississippi is one of the great rivers of the world. It begins far in the north, as a small clear stream, flowing out of a lake in the forests of Minnesota. From there, it flows south, through the whole of America, for almost four thousand kilometres. Along the way, many other big rivers flow into it. And at the end, it flows into the warm sea, the Gulf of Mexico, near New Orleans.

[long-pause]

The name Mississippi comes from the language of the Ojibwe people, who live near its source. It means great river, or father of waters. For thousands of years, before any Europeans came, many Native American peoples lived along its banks. They fished in the river and farmed the rich soil. They travelled on it in canoes. Long ago, near where St. Louis is now, they built a great city, with huge earth mounds. It is called Cahokia today, and its mounds are still there.

[pause]

The river is wide and brown. It carries an enormous amount of mud and sand, washed down from the farmlands and the mountains. That is why it is brown. The sailors call it the Big Muddy. If you fill a glass with Mississippi water and let it stand, after a while there is a thick layer of mud at the bottom. The steamboat passengers drink it anyway, after the mud has settled.

[pause]

{img:river-bend-mississippi-sandbar-willows-cottonwood-trees-quiet-water}
The Mississippi never flows in a straight line. It winds and twists, in great loops and bends, back and forth, through the flat land. Sometimes the river makes a loop so big that it almost meets itself. And then, during a flood, it breaks through the narrow neck of land, and makes a new, shorter channel. The old loop is left behind, as a quiet lake, shaped like a horseshoe.

[pause]

So the river is always changing. Every year, it moves a little. It eats away the land on one side, and builds up new land on the other. Islands appear and disappear. Sandbars grow and shrink. Trees fall into the water from the crumbling banks, and lie hidden under the surface. A town that was on the riverbank one year may be a kilometre from the river a few years later.

[long-pause]

{img:flatboat-keelboat-men-poling-river-early-1800s-cargo-cabin}
Before the steamboats, travelling on the Mississippi was slow and hard. Going downstream was easy. People built big wooden rafts and flatboats, loaded them with goods, and floated down to New Orleans with the current. There, they sold the goods, broke up the boats, sold the wood, and walked home. It took them months. Going upstream was almost impossible. Men had to push their boats against the current with long poles, or pull them along the bank with ropes.

[pause]

Then, in 1811, the first steamboat came down the river. It was called the New Orleans. People along the river had never seen anything like it. A boat that moved by itself, with smoke and fire, without sails or oars, even against the current! Some people thought it was a monster. Others thought it was a miracle. Within a few years, there were dozens of steamboats on the river. And by 1850, there were hundreds.

[pause]

{img:early-steamboat-new-orleans-1811-smoke-people-on-bank-amazed}
The steamboats changed everything. Now a journey from New Orleans to St. Louis, against the current, took only about a week, instead of months. Towns grew up along the river, at every landing. Farmers could send their crops to market. Merchants could bring goods from the cities. Immigrants from Europe could travel into the middle of America, to find new land.

[long-pause]

So the river became the great highway of America. And in 1850, it is at its busiest and most glorious. The steamboats are bigger, faster and more beautiful than ever before. Their captains and pilots are famous. Their races are the talk of the river. And every boy who grows up along the Mississippi dreams of working on a steamboat one day.

[long-pause]

{img:sidewheel-steamboat-clara-belle-1850-white-decks-paddle-boxes-painted-name}
The Clara Belle is a sidewheel steamboat. That means that she has two huge paddle wheels, one on each side of the boat. Each wheel is taller than a house, made of wood and iron, with wide wooden paddles all around it. When the wheels turn, the paddles push against the water, and the boat moves forward. The wheels are covered by big curved wooden boxes. On each box, the name of the boat is painted in large golden letters.

[long-pause]

The Clara Belle is about eighty metres long. She has three decks. At the bottom, close to the water, is the main deck. This is where the engines and boilers are, and where the cargo is stored. It is open on the sides, so that the cargo can be loaded easily. The firemen work here, feeding the furnaces, and the deck passengers sleep here, among the crates and barrels.

[pause]

Above the main deck is the boiler deck. Despite its name, there are no boilers here. It is the deck for the cabin passengers, who have paid more for their tickets. In the middle of the boiler deck, there is a long, beautiful hall, called the grand saloon. On both sides of the saloon, there are rows of small cabins, called staterooms, where the passengers sleep. Each stateroom has its own name, painted on the door, after one of the states of America.

[pause]

{img:grand-saloon-steamboat-chandeliers-carpet-gilded-arches-long-hall}
The grand saloon is the most splendid part of the boat. It is very long, almost the whole length of the boat. The ceiling is decorated with carved wood, painted white and gold. Crystal chandeliers hang down, sparkling in the light. There are thick carpets on the floor, mirrors on the walls, and soft chairs and sofas. At one end, there is a piano. People called these saloons floating palaces.

[pause]

On top of the boiler deck, there is a smaller deck, called the hurricane deck. And on top of that, there is a row of cabins for the officers. It is called the Texas deck. Nobody is quite sure why. Some people say it is because the officers' cabins were the biggest, and Texas was the biggest state. On top of the Texas, at the very top of the boat, sits the pilothouse.

[long-pause]

{img:pilothouse-glass-windows-big-wooden-wheel-view-over-river-bow}
The pilothouse is a small room with windows on all sides. From here, the pilot can see the whole river, in every direction. In the middle, there is a huge wooden steering wheel, taller than a man. Half of it disappears into a slot in the floor. The pilot stands behind the wheel and steers the boat. There is also a bench at the back, a little stove for cold nights, and ropes that ring bells down in the engine room.

[pause]

Down in the engine room, there are two big steam engines. And in front of them, there is a row of boilers, long iron tubes full of water. Under the boilers, there are furnaces, where wood is burned to heat the water and make steam. The steam goes through pipes to the engines, which turn the paddle wheels. Then the used steam escapes through two thin pipes, with a loud puffing sound. Chuff. Chuff. Chuff. That is the heartbeat of the steamboat.

[pause]

{img:engine-room-steamboat-pistons-brass-gauges-engineer-oil-can}
A steamboat burns an enormous amount of wood. The Clara Belle burns about twenty cords of wood every day. That is a pile of wood as big as a small house. So the boat must stop several times a day, to take on more wood from the woodyards along the river.

[long-pause]

The Clara Belle is not the biggest or the fastest boat on the river. But she is a good, steady boat, well looked after. Her captain is proud of her. Her crew is good. And from the shore, as she steams past with her white decks shining and her chimneys smoking, she looks as beautiful as any boat on the whole Mississippi.

[pause]

At the very front of the boat, on the main deck, there are two long wooden poles, standing up like the arms of a giant. They are called spars. If the boat gets stuck on a sandbar, the crew can push the spars down into the river bottom, and use ropes and the steam engine to lift the front of the boat, and walk her forward over the sand. The sailors call this grasshoppering, because the boat looks like a grasshopper, jumping slowly along.

[long-pause]

{img:farmhouse-missouri-river-town-1850-boy-watching-steamboat-from-bluff}
Ben Harlan is fifteen years old. He grew up in a small town on the Missouri side of the Mississippi, a little north of St. Louis. His father runs a small store near the landing, selling flour, nails, coffee, tobacco and everything else that people need. Ben has two older sisters and a younger brother. And for as long as he can remember, he has wanted to work on the steamboats.

[long-pause]

Every day, when he was a little boy, Ben ran down to the landing when a steamboat came in. All the boys of the town did. Someone would shout, steamboat a-coming! And the whole sleepy town would suddenly wake up. Shopkeepers came out of their stores. Dogs barked. Carts rattled down to the river. And the great white boat came gliding in, with its bells ringing and its chimneys smoking.

[pause]

The boys admired everyone on the steamboat. But most of all, they admired the pilot. The pilot was the most important man on the boat, even more important than the captain, while the boat was moving. He stood high up in his glass pilothouse, above everyone else. He was paid more than almost anyone in America, more than many judges and senators. And he knew every bend and every tree and every sandbar of the river, for more than a thousand kilometres.

[pause]

{img:boys-running-down-to-landing-steamboat-arriving-town-excitement}
Ben's father wanted him to work in the store. But Ben could think of nothing but the river. He spent every free moment at the landing. He learned the names of all the boats, and the sounds of their whistles. He talked to the deckhands and the clerks. And one day, he met a pilot called Mr. Elias Grant, who came into the store to buy tobacco.

[pause]

Mr. Grant was a big, quiet man of about fifty, with a grey beard and sharp blue eyes. He had been a pilot for more than twenty years. Ben asked him a thousand questions. Mr. Grant answered them patiently. And after a few visits, he said something that made Ben's heart jump. He was looking for a boy to train. A cub pilot, as they called the apprentices.

[long-pause]

{img:father-and-pilot-talking-in-country-store-shelves-barrels-lamp}
But learning to be a pilot was expensive. The young man had to pay the pilot to teach him. It cost five hundred dollars, which was a fortune. Ben's father did not have that much money. Mr. Grant thought about it, and then he made an offer. Ben could pay him back slowly, later, from his wages, when he became a pilot himself. Ben's father was quiet for a long time. Then he shook Mr. Grant's hand.

[pause]

Ben's mother was worried. The river was dangerous. There were accidents, fires and explosions. But she could see how much her son wanted it. So she packed his carpet bag. Two shirts, a pair of trousers, a warm coat, socks, a Bible, and a little tin box with some money sewn into the lining of his coat. And on the morning he left, she hugged him for a long time, and told him to write.

[pause]

{img:mother-hugging-son-porch-carpet-bag-morning-river-town}
Ben's father took him down to St. Louis on a small boat. They walked along the busy levee together, looking for the Clara Belle. And there she was, lying at the levee with steam rising from her chimneys. Mr. Grant was waiting at the top of the stairs. He shook Ben's hand and said, well, son, are you ready to learn the river?

[long-pause]

Ben said yes. His father put a hand on his shoulder, and then walked back down the stairs to the levee. Ben watched him go. He felt a little lump in his throat. But then the bells began to ring, and the whistle blew. And he climbed up, up, up, all the way to the pilothouse, where the whole river lay spread out before him.

[long-pause]

{img:steamboat-departure-st-louis-crowd-levee-waving-bell-ringing-smoke}
At ten o'clock in the morning, the Clara Belle is ready to leave. The last cargo is loaded. The last passengers hurry up the wooden gangplank, carrying their bags. The clerk checks the tickets. The mate shouts orders to the deckhands. And on the levee, a crowd has gathered to watch the boat leave. Leaving was always a little show on the Mississippi.

[long-pause]

The captain stands on the hurricane deck, in a dark blue coat and a tall hat. His name is Captain Lowell. He is a round, cheerful man with white side whiskers. He pulls a rope, and the great bell on the roof rings out. Ding, ding, ding. Then he signals to the pilothouse. Mr. Grant pulls another rope, and the deep steam whistle sounds, long and loud, over the whole levee. Ben feels it in his chest.

[pause]

Down below, the deckhands pull in the heavy ropes and lift the gangplank. Mr. Grant rings a bell to the engine room. The engines begin to work. Chuff, chuff, chuff. The great paddle wheels begin to turn, slowly at first, churning up the brown water into white foam. And slowly, the Clara Belle backs away from the levee, out into the river.

[pause]

{img:pilot-turning-big-wheel-boy-watching-pilothouse-city-behind}
In the pilothouse, Mr. Grant turns the big wheel. The boat swings around, until her nose points downstream, toward the south. Then he rings the bell again, and the paddle wheels begin to turn forward. The boat picks up speed. The levee slides away behind them, with its crowds and its long row of steamboats. The city of St. Louis, with its church towers and warehouses and smoke, gets smaller and smaller.

[pause]

Ben stands at the window of the pilothouse, watching everything. He has never been this high above the river before. He can see everything. The wide brown water, the green banks, the islands, the other boats. The wind blows through the open windows, cool and fresh. The deck vibrates gently under his feet with the beat of the engines. And he thinks that he has never been so happy in his whole life.

[long-pause]

{img:river-below-st-louis-wide-water-wooded-banks-other-steamboats-passing}
Below St. Louis, the river is wide and busy. They pass other steamboats, coming up from the south, heavily loaded with cargo. Each time they pass a boat, the pilots blow their whistles in greeting. And sometimes the passengers wave to each other across the water. They pass flatboats and rafts, drifting slowly with the current, with families living in little huts on top. They pass small towns and landings, and lonely farmhouses among the trees.

[pause]

A few kilometres below St. Louis, there is a muddy, swirling stretch of water. Ben does not notice anything special about it. But Mr. Grant points it out. That is where the Missouri River came in, a little above the city, he says. The Missouri is even muddier than the Mississippi. Its water is almost the colour of coffee with milk. For many kilometres, the two waters flow side by side, without mixing. One side brown, and the other side a little lighter.

[pause]

{img:two-colours-of-water-flowing-side-by-side-river-sunlight-boat}
Mr. Grant does not talk very much. He stands at the wheel, looking ahead at the river, and steers the boat with small, calm movements. Sometimes he hums a tune to himself. Sometimes he spits tobacco juice out of the window. And sometimes, without turning around, he says something to Ben. That point there, with the big cottonwood tree, is called such and such. Remember it. Ben tries to remember everything. But there is so much.

[long-pause]

At midday, the dinner bell rings, and Mr. Grant's partner comes up to take the wheel. Every steamboat has two pilots. They take turns, four hours on and four hours off, day and night. The other pilot is a younger man called Mr. Hale, thin and cheerful. Mr. Grant and Ben go down to eat. And Ben realises that he is starving. He has been too excited to eat breakfast.

[pause]

After dinner, Ben goes back up to the pilothouse, although it is not his watch. He cannot stay away. Mr. Hale, the other pilot, does not mind. He lets Ben sit on the high bench at the back, and he tells him funny stories about the river, about pilots he has known, and boats he has sailed on. By the end of the afternoon, Ben has laughed so much that his stomach hurts.

[long-pause]

{img:old-pilot-teaching-boy-pilothouse-pointing-at-river-afternoon-light}
Learning to be a pilot is very hard. A pilot must know the whole river, by heart. Every bend, every point, every island, every sandbar, every hidden rock and sunken tree. For more than a thousand kilometres, from St. Louis to New Orleans. And not only going downstream, but also going upstream, when everything looks different. He must know it by day, and he must know it by night, when he can see almost nothing at all.

[long-pause]

On the first day, Mr. Grant starts teaching Ben. He tells him the names of the points and bends and islands as they pass. He tells him where the deep water is, and where the shallow water is. He tells him where to steer close to the shore, and where to keep away. Ben writes everything down in a little notebook. By the evening, he has filled ten pages. And he feels that he does not remember any of it.

[pause]

Mr. Grant laughs when Ben tells him this. He says that every cub pilot feels the same. He says that it takes two or three years to learn the river properly. And that even then, you never stop learning, because the river is always changing. Every time you come down the river, something is different. A new sandbar, a fallen tree, a bank that has washed away.

[pause]

{img:reading-river-surface-ripples-smooth-patch-slanting-line-sunlight}
The most important thing, Mr. Grant says, is to learn to read the water. The surface of the river is like a book. It tells you what is underneath, if you know how to read it. A long, slanting line on the surface means there is a sandbar or a reef below. A smooth, oily patch might mean deep water, or a hidden danger. Little rings and boils on the surface mean that something under the water is disturbing the current.

[pause]

At first, Ben cannot see any of these things. To him, the river just looks like brown water, with some ripples here and there. But Mr. Grant points them out, again and again. That line there. That patch there. See how the water is breaking? Slowly, very slowly, Ben begins to see them too. It is like learning to read a new language.

[long-pause]

{img:pilot-at-wheel-calm-concentrated-river-ahead-cottonwood-points-bends}
Mr. Grant also teaches him how to steer the boat. The big wheel is heavy, and the boat is slow to respond. You must turn the wheel early, before the bend comes. You must think ahead. In a strong current, the boat drifts sideways, and you must correct for it. At first, Ben's steering is very clumsy. The boat swings from side to side. But Mr. Grant stands beside him and talks calmly, and slowly Ben gets better.

[pause]

Later, Mark Twain wrote a famous book about his own years as a cub pilot. He wrote that, at first, the river was full of beauty for him. The colours of the sunset on the water, the shapes of the clouds, the floating logs. But when he became a pilot, he saw everything differently. The beautiful colours were warnings. The pretty floating log meant the river was rising. And he said he had lost something, because he could no longer just see the river as beautiful.

[pause]

{img:sunset-on-mississippi-red-gold-water-floating-log-silhouette-trees}
Ben does not feel that way yet. He still sees the beauty. In the evening, when the sun goes down, the river turns red and gold. The trees on the bank are black against the sky. The water is smooth, with long ripples in the light. And Ben thinks that it is the most beautiful thing he has ever seen. Mr. Grant stands beside him, and looks at it too. He does not say anything about reefs or sandbars. He just looks, for a long time.

[long-pause]

Then the light fades, and the night comes. The lamps are lit below. And Mr. Grant says quietly that now Ben must learn the river all over again, in the dark.

[long-pause]

{img:cabin-passengers-1850-grand-saloon-ladies-crinolines-gentlemen-top-hats-reading}
The Clara Belle carries about two hundred passengers. They are very different from each other. Up on the boiler deck, in the cabins and the grand saloon, there are the cabin passengers. They have paid about twenty-five dollars each for the journey to New Orleans. That is a lot of money in 1850. For that price, they get a stateroom, all their meals, and the comforts of the floating palace.

[long-pause]

The cabin passengers are mostly well-off people. There are cotton planters, going home to their plantations in the South. There are merchants from St. Louis and Cincinnati, travelling on business to New Orleans. There are lawyers and doctors, politicians and preachers. There are families, travelling to visit relatives. And there are a few travellers from Europe, who have come to see the famous Mississippi with their own eyes.

[pause]

The men wear dark coats, waistcoats, and tall hats. Many of them have long beards or side whiskers. The women wear wide dresses down to the floor, with many petticoats underneath, and bonnets with ribbons. The children are dressed like little grown-ups. Everyone looks very elegant, at least on the first day. After a few days on the river, they become a little more relaxed.

[pause]

{img:ladies-cabin-steamboat-sewing-circle-piano-afternoon-light-windows}
There is a special part of the saloon for the ladies, at the back of the boat. It is called the ladies' cabin. It has a soft carpet, comfortable sofas and a piano. Men are only allowed in if they are invited. Here, the ladies sit and sew, read, play the piano and talk. They also look after the children, who run up and down the long saloon, playing games.

[pause]

The men spend their time at the front of the saloon. They sit in the chairs and talk about business, politics and cotton prices. They read newspapers, which they bought in St. Louis. They smoke cigars on the deck outside. Some of them play cards. And there are always a few men on the boat who play cards for money, and who are very good at winning. The other passengers call them riverboat gamblers, and keep a careful eye on their wallets.

[long-pause]

{img:gentlemen-smoking-cigars-railing-boiler-deck-watching-river-shore}
Ben does not have much time for the passengers. But when he is off duty, he likes to walk along the railings and watch them. He makes friends with a small boy called Henry, who is about eight years old. Henry is travelling with his mother to New Orleans, to meet his father, who is a cotton merchant there. Henry is fascinated by everything on the boat. He follows Ben everywhere, asking questions.

[pause]

There is also an old Englishwoman on board, a writer. She is travelling through America, to write a book about it. She sits on the deck every day with a notebook, writing about everything she sees. She asks Ben many questions about the river, and about the steamboats. He answers as well as he can. She thanks him, and says that she will put him in her book. Ben does not know if she is joking.

[pause]

{img:old-english-lady-writing-notebook-deck-chair-bonnet-river-behind}
Many travellers from Europe did write books about their journeys on the Mississippi in those years. Some of them were full of admiration. They loved the size of the river, the beauty of the forests and the speed of the steamboats. Others were not so kind. They complained about the noise, the dirt, the bad manners of some passengers, and the habit of chewing tobacco and spitting. And many of them were shocked and saddened by slavery, which they saw everywhere along the lower river.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, the grand saloon is lit up by lamps and chandeliers. After supper, the tables are cleared away. Someone sits at the piano and plays. Sometimes there is dancing. The ladies and gentlemen dance in the long saloon, while the boat glides on through the darkness, and the paddle wheels splash softly outside. It is a strange and lovely thing. A ballroom, floating down a wild river, in the middle of the night.

[pause]

On Sundays, a preacher on board sometimes holds a short service in the grand saloon. The passengers sit in rows on the chairs, and sing hymns together, while the piano plays. Through the open doors, the deck passengers can hear the singing, and some of them sing along softly, in German or Irish or English. For a little while, the whole boat sings the same songs, as she glides on down the river.

[long-pause]

{img:main-deck-steamboat-deck-passengers-immigrant-families-crates-barrels}
Down below, on the main deck, there is a very different world. Here, among the crates and barrels and bales of cargo, travel the deck passengers. They have paid only a few dollars for the whole journey. For that price, they get a space on the floor of the deck, and nothing else. No cabin, no bed and no meals. They bring their own food and blankets, and sleep wherever they can find a space.

[long-pause]

Many of the deck passengers are immigrants. In 1850, hundreds of thousands of people are coming to America every year, from Europe. Many of them come from Ireland, where there has been a terrible famine. Many come from Germany. They arrive in New Orleans or New York, and then travel up and down the rivers, looking for work or land. On the main deck of the Clara Belle, you can hear Irish, German and many other accents.

[pause]

The deck is crowded and uncomfortable. It is noisy, because the engines and the boilers are right there. It is hot near the furnaces, and cold and damp at the open sides. When it rains, the rain blows in. The deck passengers cook their food on a small stove, and wash in buckets of river water. Babies cry. Men smoke their pipes. Women try to keep their children away from the edge.

[pause]

{img:german-immigrant-family-deck-sitting-on-trunks-mother-child-blanket}
Ben makes friends with a German family, the Webers. The father is a carpenter. The mother has a baby on her lap, and there are three other children. They come from a small village in the south of Germany. They have been travelling for months. Across the ocean, then up the river from New Orleans to St. Louis. Now they are going back down again, because they have heard there is good land and work in Texas.

[pause]

The oldest Weber boy, Karl, is the same age as Ben. He speaks only a little English. But the two boys manage to talk, with hands and smiles and a few words. Karl tells Ben about the mountains and forests of his home. Ben teaches him the names of things on the boat. Paddle wheel. Chimney. Pilot. Karl repeats them carefully, laughing at his own accent.

[long-pause]

{img:firemen-feeding-furnaces-glow-sweat-night-main-deck-steamboat}
The main deck is also where much of the crew works. There are the firemen, who feed wood into the furnaces, day and night. It is hot, hard and exhausting work. There are the deckhands, who load and unload the cargo at every landing, and carry the wood on board. Many of them are Irish immigrants. And on the Clara Belle, as on many steamboats in 1850, some of the deckhands and firemen are enslaved Black men.

[pause]

They do not work for themselves. They have been hired out by their owners, who keep their wages. They work as hard as anyone on the boat, often harder. They are not free to leave. This was one of the great wrongs of America in those years. Ben has grown up in Missouri, a slave state, and he has seen slavery all his life. But on the river, he begins to think about it more than he did before.

[pause]

{img:steward-free-black-man-1850-white-jacket-saloon-dignified}
There are also free Black men and women working on the boat. The chief steward of the Clara Belle is a free Black man called Mr. Jonas Price. He is in charge of all the waiters, cooks and chambermaids. He is a tall, dignified man, very well dressed, who runs the dining room perfectly. He is kind to Ben, and sometimes saves him a piece of cake from the captain's table. He was born free in Ohio, and he has worked on the river for twenty years.

[long-pause]

At night, the main deck is quiet, except for the hiss of the boilers and the beat of the engines. The deck passengers sleep on the floor, wrapped in their blankets. The firemen work on, their faces lit up by the orange glow of the furnaces. And the boat glides on, carrying all these different people, rich and poor, free and not free, down the great river together.

[long-pause]

{img:steamboat-dining-long-table-grand-saloon-white-cloths-silver-dishes-waiters}
On a steamboat, mealtimes are very important. For the cabin passengers, there are three big meals a day. Breakfast, dinner at midday and supper in the evening. And between the meals, there is not much to do. So people look forward to them, and talk about them, and eat a great deal.

[long-pause]

At mealtimes, a long table is set up in the grand saloon. It stretches almost the whole length of the room. It is covered with white tablecloths, and set with silver forks and knives, china plates and glass goblets. When the bell rings, the passengers hurry to the table. The ladies and their families sit at the end near the ladies' cabin. The captain sits at the head of the table, and the most important passengers sit beside him.

[pause]

The food is plentiful. For dinner, there might be roast beef and roast chicken, ham, turkey and fish from the river. There are potatoes, corn, beans, peas and cabbage. There is soft white bread and cornbread, with butter. Then there are puddings, pies and cakes, and fruit. And there is coffee, tea and plenty of iced water. Yes, iced water. The boat carries big blocks of ice in its hold, packed in sawdust, cut from frozen lakes in the north during the winter.

[pause]

{img:waiters-carrying-trays-saloon-steward-directing-dinner-bell}
The meals are served by the waiters, under the command of Mr. Price, the steward. The waiters are Black men, some free and some enslaved, dressed in white jackets. They carry huge trays of food up from the kitchen, and serve the dishes along the table, very quickly and smoothly. Mr. Price stands at the end of the room and watches everything. If a dish is late, he only has to raise his eyebrows.

[pause]

Some travellers from Europe were surprised by how fast the Americans ate. They wrote that the passengers rushed to the table as soon as the bell rang, ate everything in ten minutes without saying a word, and then left. The Europeans, who liked long, slow dinners with conversation, did not know what to think. But perhaps the Americans were just very hungry, after a morning of watching the river.

[long-pause]

{img:galley-kitchen-steamboat-cooks-stoves-pots-busy-steam}
The kitchen of the boat, called the galley, is on the main deck, near the back. It is small, hot and busy. There are several cooks, working at big iron stoves. They bake bread, roast meat, make soup and pies, all day long. The head cook is an older woman called Mrs. Hattie Moore. She is famous on the river for her peach pie. Some passengers say they choose the Clara Belle just for her pie.

[pause]

The officers and the pilots eat at the long table too, after the passengers have finished. Ben eats with Mr. Grant. At first, he is shy at the big table, with all the silver and china. He does not know which fork to use. But Mr. Price shows him quietly, without anyone noticing. And soon Ben is eating as much as anyone. After a long morning in the pilothouse, he is always hungry.

[pause]

{img:deck-passengers-cooking-small-stove-cornbread-bacon-children-eating}
The deck passengers, down below, eat much more simply. They bring their own food on board. Bread, bacon, cornmeal, cheese, dried apples and coffee. They cook it on a small stove that is shared by everyone. At the landings, they buy eggs, milk and fruit from the farmers who come down to the riverbank to sell. The food is plain, but filling.

[long-pause]

Sometimes, when there is food left over from the saloon, Mr. Price sends it down to the main deck, to the deck passengers. Ben carries a basket of bread and cold meat down to the Weber family one evening. Mrs. Weber thanks him, with tears in her eyes. The children eat it all at once. Karl shakes Ben's hand very seriously, and says danke, thank you, in two languages at once.

[long-pause]

{img:woodyard-riverbank-stacks-of-cordwood-cabin-family-steamboat-approaching}
Several times a day, the Clara Belle stops to take on wood. All along the river, there are woodyards. A woodyard is a clearing on the riverbank, with a small log cabin, and big stacks of firewood, cut into pieces about a metre long. The people who live there spend their days cutting trees in the forest, and stacking the wood. Then they sell it to the passing steamboats.

[long-pause]

When the pilot sees a woodyard, he rings the bell, and the boat slows down. She turns toward the shore, and gently noses up against the bank. The deckhands push out the gangplank. Then they run down onto the bank, pick up the wood, and carry it on board, as fast as they can. It is hard work. Each man carries a big armful of wood on his shoulder, up the gangplank, onto the deck, and back again for more.

[pause]

The mate stands on the bank and shouts at the men to hurry. Every minute the boat waits is a minute lost. The clerk counts the wood and pays the woodyard owner. Sometimes the passengers come down onto the bank too, to stretch their legs, pick flowers or buy things from the woodyard family. In about twenty minutes, all the wood is on board. The bell rings. The passengers hurry back up the gangplank. And the boat backs out into the river again.

[pause]

{img:deckhands-carrying-firewood-gangplank-shoulders-riverbank-afternoon}
The woodyard families live very lonely lives. Their cabins are often far from any town, in the middle of the forest or the swamp. Their nearest neighbours may be kilometres away. They see nobody except the people on the passing steamboats. For them, a steamboat stopping is a big event. The children come running down to the bank, to look at the fine ladies and gentlemen on the decks.

[pause]

At one woodyard, there is a little girl of about six, barefoot, in a faded dress, holding a big yellow cat. She stares at the boat with huge eyes. Henry, the little boy from the saloon, waves at her. She waves back, shyly. Henry's mother sends Ben down with a little bag of sweets from the saloon. The girl takes it, and runs to her mother, holding it up with both hands. Ben thinks about her for a long time afterwards.

[long-pause]

{img:little-girl-barefoot-holding-yellow-cat-riverbank-cabin-waving}
Besides wood, the boat also stops at many landings, to take on or put off passengers and cargo. Some landings are big towns, with wharves and warehouses. Others are just a muddy bank, with a single house and a path into the woods. When someone on shore wants the boat to stop, they wave a flag or a handkerchief. At night, they light a fire on the bank.

[pause]

At each landing, the whole crew works fast. Barrels of flour, sacks of corn, crates of machinery, bales of cotton, all must be carried on or off. A farmer comes on board with a cow. A family gets off with all their furniture. A man brings a cage of chickens. Sometimes the cargo is strange. Once, the Clara Belle carried a piano, a coffin and a very angry goat, all on the same day.

[pause]

{img:landing-small-town-riverbank-people-waiting-flag-waving-steamboat}
For the people along the river, the steamboat is everything. It brings their mail and their newspapers. It brings visitors and news from the cities. It takes their crops to market. It carries their sons and daughters away into the wide world, and sometimes brings them home again. When the whistle sounds around the bend, everyone stops what they are doing, and looks up.

[long-pause]

Ben loves the landings. Each one is different. A town with a white church on a hill. A plantation with a big house among old oak trees. A wild riverbank, with a single cabin and smoke rising from the chimney. He writes them all down in his notebook, for the pilot must know every landing on the river. And slowly, the notebook fills up, page after page, with the names of places.

[pause]

Some woodyards are run by free families. Others are run by men who use enslaved workers to cut the wood. And some woodyards in the lonely parts of the river are run by enslaved men themselves, who are allowed to sell some wood for their own small earnings. A few of them, over many years, saved enough money to buy their own freedom, and the freedom of their families. It took them a lifetime of hard work.

[long-pause]

{img:leadsman-bow-steamboat-throwing-weighted-line-into-river-calling-depth}
Sometimes the river is shallow. Especially in summer and autumn, when there is little rain, the water level drops. Then the sandbars come close to the surface, and the boat might run aground. So in difficult places, the pilot needs to know exactly how deep the water is. And for that, there is the leadsman.

[long-pause]

The leadsman stands at the front of the boat, at the edge of the deck. In his hand, he holds a long rope, with a heavy piece of lead tied to the end. Along the rope, there are little marks of cloth and leather, at regular distances. The leadsman swings the lead and throws it forward into the river. When it touches the bottom, he looks at the marks on the rope, to see how deep the water is. Then he calls out the depth, in a long, singing voice.

[pause]

The calls have special names. The depth is measured in fathoms. One fathom is about the height of a man, almost two metres. Mark one means one fathom. Mark twain means two fathoms, about three and a half metres. Mark three means three fathoms. And quarter less twain, or half twain, mean the depths in between. When the water is very deep, the leadsman calls out, no bottom!

[pause]

{img:pilothouse-pilot-listening-calls-relayed-boy-repeating-tense-shallow}
The leadsman's call is repeated by another man on the deck above, and then by another, until it reaches the pilothouse. So the calls go up through the boat, like an echo. M-a-a-a-rk three! Quarter less three! Half twain! M-a-a-a-rk twain! The pilot listens carefully, and steers the boat into the deepest water. When the water is two fathoms, mark twain, it is just deep enough for a steamboat to pass safely.

[pause]

That is where Samuel Clemens, the boy from Hannibal, later found his famous name. When he became a writer, he called himself Mark Twain. Two fathoms. Safe water. It was a sound he had heard thousands of times, as a pilot on the Mississippi. And now that name is known all over the world.

[long-pause]

{img:night-sounding-lantern-bow-leadsman-dark-river-calm-voice}
One afternoon, the Clara Belle comes to a difficult crossing. A crossing is a place where the deep channel of the river crosses from one side to the other. The water there is often shallow. Mr. Grant rings the bell for the leadsmen, and slows the boat down. Two leadsmen take their places at the front, one on each side. And they begin to throw their leads, and call out the depths.

[pause]

Ben stands beside Mr. Grant in the pilothouse, listening. The calls come up, one after another. Mark three. Quarter less three. Half twain. Quarter twain. The water is getting shallower. Ben holds his breath. Mr. Grant moves the wheel a little to the left. Mark twain. Quarter less twain. Ben's heart is beating fast. Mr. Grant's face is calm. He moves the wheel a little more.

[pause]

{img:steamboat-gliding-over-shallow-crossing-sandbar-visible-calm-river}
And then the calls begin to change. Mark twain. Quarter twain. Half twain. Mark three. The water is getting deeper again. They are across. Ben lets out his breath. Mr. Grant turns to him, with a little smile under his beard. There, he says. Now you have heard the river talk. You must always listen to it.

[long-pause]

The next day, Mr. Grant lets Ben take the wheel through an easy crossing, with the leadsmen calling. Ben's hands are sweating. He listens to every call, and moves the wheel very carefully. The water gets shallower, and then deeper again. And the boat glides safely over. Mr. Grant does not say anything. But he nods, once. And for Ben, that nod is worth more than any praise.

[long-pause]

{img:confluence-ohio-mississippi-cairo-illinois-two-rivers-meeting-1850}
On the second day, the Clara Belle comes to Cairo, a small town in Illinois. It lies on a low point of land, between two great rivers. Here, the Ohio River flows into the Mississippi. The Ohio comes from the east, from the mountains of Pennsylvania. Its water is clearer and greener than the brown Mississippi. And for a long way, the two waters flow side by side, without mixing.

[long-pause]

Below Cairo, the Mississippi becomes even bigger and wider. It now carries the water of almost half of North America. The banks are further apart. The current is stronger. And the river becomes even more winding, with great loops and bends, through endless forests and swamps. This is called the lower Mississippi. For many pilots, it is the most difficult part of the whole river.

[pause]

Cairo is a low, muddy town. It floods often, and the people have built high banks of earth around it, to keep the water out. There are warehouses, a hotel, a few stores and a lot of mud. Many travellers in those days did not like Cairo very much. The English writer Charles Dickens visited it a few years before our story, and called it a dismal swamp. But the people of Cairo were proud of their town, which they believed would one day be a great city.

[pause]

{img:muddy-river-town-cairo-levee-warehouses-steamboats-moored-grey-day}
Cairo is also an important place for another reason. The Ohio River is a border. On its northern side are the free states, like Illinois, Indiana and Ohio, where slavery is not allowed. On its southern side are the slave states, like Kentucky. And the Mississippi itself is a border too, between free Illinois and slave Missouri. For enslaved people in the South, the Ohio River was often called the River Jordan, after the river in the Bible that the Israelites crossed to reach the promised land.

[pause]

Many enslaved people tried to escape to freedom across the Ohio. They hid in the forests and swamps, travelled at night, and crossed the river by boat, or over the ice in winter. On the other side, there were people who helped them, hiding them in their homes and helping them travel further north. This network of brave helpers was called the Underground Railroad. It was not a real railroad, but a secret path to freedom.

[long-pause]

{img:lantern-in-window-farmhouse-night-river-bank-quiet-hope}
Later in this very year, 1850, the American government will pass a harsh new law. It will say that people who have escaped from slavery can be caught even in the free states, and sent back. So many escaped people will have to travel all the way to Canada, to be truly safe. It will be a difficult and dangerous time for them. But the helpers of the Underground Railroad will continue their work, quietly, with lanterns in their windows, at night.

[pause]

On the Clara Belle, Mr. Price, the free steward from Ohio, stands at the railing as the boat passes Cairo. He looks at the Ohio River, flowing in from the east, for a long time. Ben comes and stands beside him. Mr. Price does not say much. But he tells Ben that he has a sister who lives on the other side of that river, in Ohio. And that, one day, he hopes, every person in this country will be free.

[pause]

{img:steward-and-boy-standing-at-railing-looking-at-river-thoughtful-afternoon}
Ben thinks about this for a long time. In his town, there were enslaved people too. He grew up with it, and he never questioned it much. Many white people at that time did not. But now, on the river, he sees things he did not see before. And he begins to understand that the world he grew up in is not the way the world should be.

[long-pause]

That evening, the Clara Belle steams on, past Cairo, into the lower river. On the right side, there is Missouri. On the left side, there is Kentucky, and then Tennessee. The forests become thicker, and the air becomes warmer and softer. Further south, long grey Spanish moss will soon hang from the branches. They are going south.

[long-pause]

{img:memphis-1850-bluff-town-cotton-bales-landing-steamboats-afternoon}
On the third day, the Clara Belle comes to Memphis, in the state of Tennessee. Memphis is built on a high bluff, a steep bank above the river, so it is safe from the floods. In 1850, it is a young, fast-growing town, with about eight thousand people. It is becoming one of the most important cotton markets in the South.

[long-pause]

At the landing below the bluff, the riverbank is covered with bales of cotton. Thousands of them, stacked high, waiting to be loaded onto the steamboats. Each bale is a big, heavy bundle of cotton, wrapped in rough cloth and tied with ropes. It weighs about two hundred kilos. Men roll them down the bank and up the gangplanks, onto the boats. Some steamboats are loaded so high with cotton that you can hardly see the boat underneath.

[pause]

The Clara Belle stops at Memphis for several hours. She takes on cotton, and some new passengers, and fresh food for the kitchen. Ben has a few hours off. Mr. Grant tells him to go and see the town. So Ben walks up the steep road from the landing to the top of the bluff, with Karl, the German boy, who has permission from his parents.

[pause]

{img:memphis-main-street-1850-wooden-buildings-wagons-mud-shops-people}
Memphis is a lively, rough town. The main street is wide and muddy, with wooden buildings on both sides. There are stores, hotels, banks and saloons. Wagons pulled by mules and oxen come in from the countryside, loaded with cotton. Farmers in straw hats stand in groups, talking about the weather and the cotton prices. Ladies in bonnets pick their way carefully across the mud, holding up their skirts.

[pause]

On the corner of the main square, there is a market, where farmers sell vegetables, eggs, chickens and fruit. Ben buys some apples and a bag of roasted peanuts, and shares them with Karl. They sit on a wall at the edge of the bluff and look down at the river. From up here, the Mississippi looks enormous. It stretches away to the west, wide and brown, to the flat green forests of Arkansas on the other side.

[long-pause]

{img:two-boys-sitting-on-wall-bluff-overlooking-wide-river-eating-apples}
Karl tells Ben about his family's plans. In Texas, there are other German families, who have started farms and villages. Karl's father wants to build a house, and grow corn and cotton, and keep cattle. Karl is excited. But he is also a little afraid. Texas is very far, and very wild. He has heard stories about the heat, the snakes and the storms. Ben tells him that he will be fine. He does not really know. But it seems like the right thing to say.

[pause]

In the town, they also pass a place that makes them both go quiet. It is a building with bars on the windows, near the square. A sign on the front says that enslaved people are bought and sold here. Memphis has many of these places. The boys do not stop. They do not talk about it. But Karl, who comes from a country without slavery, looks at Ben with a puzzled and troubled face. Ben does not know what to say.

[pause]

Before they go back to the boat, the boys stop at a little bakery and buy a loaf of fresh bread for Karl's mother, with Ben's money. Karl protests, but Ben insists. He says that it is a present for the journey to Texas. Karl carries it carefully all the way down the hill, holding it like a treasure. When his mother sees it, she smiles so warmly that Ben feels his face turn red.

[pause]

{img:steamboat-leaving-memphis-evening-bluff-lights-river-gold}
In the late afternoon, the boys walk back down to the landing. The Clara Belle is ready to leave, loaded with cotton bales all around her main deck. The bell rings, and the whistle sounds. The boat backs out into the river and turns south. The town of Memphis, on its bluff, glows in the evening sun, and then slowly disappears behind a bend.

[long-pause]

That evening, Ben is quiet in the pilothouse. Mr. Grant notices, but he does not ask. After a while, Ben tells him about the building in Memphis. Mr. Grant is silent for a long time. Then he says, slowly, that some things in this country are very wrong. And that one day, they will have to change. He says he hopes he lives to see it. Then he turns back to the river, and they say nothing more that night.

[long-pause]

{img:steamboat-at-night-lit-windows-reflected-dark-river-stars-sparks-chimneys}
The steamboats on the Mississippi do not stop at night. They run all through the darkness, day after day, until they reach the end of their journey. And at night, a steamboat on the river is one of the most beautiful sights in the world.

[long-pause]

From the shore, you can see her coming from far away. First, you hear the sound of the engines, puffing in the darkness. Then, you see the sparks, flying out of the tops of the tall chimneys, like little red stars. And then she comes around the bend. All her windows are lit up, glowing golden in the dark. The grand saloon shines like a palace. The lights are reflected in the black water. And she glides past, slowly and majestically, and disappears into the night.

[pause]

For the pilot, the night is the hardest time. On a dark night, he can see almost nothing. The river is black. The banks are black. The sky is a little less black. And yet he must steer the boat through the winding river, past the sandbars and the sunken trees, at full speed. How does he do it? He uses his memory, and his eyes, and something that is almost like a sixth sense.

[pause]

{img:pilothouse-at-night-dark-glass-pilot-silhouette-wheel-faint-shoreline}
At night, the pilot looks at the shapes of the shore against the sky. The line of the trees, the shape of a hill, a gap in the forest. He knows these shapes by heart. He knows that when he sees a certain tall tree against the sky, he must turn the wheel. He knows that when the shore looks like a certain shape, there is a sandbar ahead. He reads the darkness, just as he reads the water.

[pause]

The pilothouse is kept completely dark at night. No lamp is allowed, because the light would blind the pilot. There is only the faint glow of the stove in the corner. Ben sits on the high bench at the back, very quietly, and watches Mr. Grant at the wheel. The only sounds are the creak of the wheel, the beat of the engines, and the soft splash of the paddle wheels.

[long-pause]

{img:moonlit-river-silver-path-steamboat-gliding-forest-silhouettes}
On moonlit nights, it is easier. The moon shines on the river, making a silver path on the water. The banks are clearly visible, dark against the silver. Mr. Grant says that moonlit nights are the pilot's friends. But he also warns Ben that the moonlight can play tricks. Shadows look like banks. Banks look like shadows. You must never trust your eyes completely.

[pause]

Some nights, Mr. Grant lets Ben take the wheel for a while, on easy stretches of the river. Ben stands there in the darkness, holding the big wheel, with the whole boat in his hands. Two hundred people are sleeping below. The engines are beating. The river is flowing past. It is the most frightening thing Ben has ever done, and the most wonderful.

[pause]

{img:passengers-asleep-staterooms-lamp-dim-quiet-night-steamboat}
Down below, the boat is quiet. The passengers are asleep in their staterooms. In the grand saloon, a single lamp is burning low. On the main deck, the deck passengers are sleeping among the cargo, wrapped in their blankets. Only the firemen are awake, feeding the furnaces, and the watchman, walking slowly along the decks. And somewhere, a baby is crying softly, and a mother is singing it back to sleep.

[long-pause]

Sometimes, in the middle of the night, they pass another steamboat. First, there is a light in the distance. Then the sound of engines. Then the other boat comes past, all lit up, like a floating palace in the dark. The two pilots blow their whistles to each other, to say which side they will pass on. And then the other boat is gone, and the night is dark and quiet again.

[pause]

Towards morning, the sky in the east begins to turn grey, and then pale pink. The stars fade, one by one. Mist rises from the water in soft white curls. Birds begin to sing in the forests along the bank. And the river, which was so black and mysterious in the night, slowly becomes brown and ordinary again. Mr. Grant stretches his arms and yawns. Then Mr. Hale comes up the stairs with two cups of hot coffee, and the night is over.

[long-pause]

{img:two-steamboats-side-by-side-racing-river-smoke-passengers-cheering-morning}
On the fourth morning, something exciting happens. Behind the Clara Belle, around a bend in the river, another steamboat appears. She is a big, new boat, painted white and red, called the Natchez Star. She is coming up fast behind them. And everyone on the Clara Belle knows at once what that means. A race.

[long-pause]

Steamboat races are the great sport of the Mississippi. The captains are proud of their boats, and they hate to be passed by another boat. The passengers love the excitement. People along the river bet money on the races. And a fast boat gets more passengers, because everyone wants to travel on the fastest boat. So whenever two boats meet, going the same way, there is often a race.

[pause]

The Natchez Star comes closer and closer. Her chimneys are pouring out black smoke. Her paddle wheels are churning up the water. Her passengers are crowding along the railings, shouting and waving their hats. On the Clara Belle, the passengers rush to the back of the boat to watch. Everyone is excited. Even little Henry is jumping up and down.

[pause]

{img:captain-ordering-more-steam-firemen-pine-knots-furnaces-glow}
Captain Lowell comes up to the pilothouse. He is red in the face. He says to Mr. Grant, she shall not pass us. Not today. Mr. Grant nods calmly. The captain sends word to the engine room, to give the boat more steam. Down below, the firemen throw more wood into the furnaces, and pieces of pine full of resin, which burn very hot. The engines beat faster. The paddle wheels spin faster. And the Clara Belle begins to pick up speed.

[pause]

For a while, the two boats race side by side. They are so close that the passengers can shout to each other across the water. Some of them are making jokes. Some of them are betting. The smoke from the chimneys mixes in the air above them. The noise of the engines is tremendous. Ben stands in the pilothouse, holding on to the bench, his heart racing as fast as the boat.

[long-pause]

{img:pilot-steering-close-to-shore-shortcut-shallow-water-other-boat-behind}
Mr. Grant knows the river better than the pilot of the Natchez Star. Ahead, there is a long bend. On the outside of the bend, the water is deep, and the other pilot steers that way, the safe way. But Mr. Grant knows of a narrower channel, close to the inside of the bend, where the water is just deep enough at this time of year. It is shorter. He turns the wheel. The leadsmen begin to call. Mark three. Quarter less three. Mark twain.

[pause]

The Clara Belle slips through the narrow channel, close to the trees on the shore. The branches almost touch the railings. The passengers hold their breath. And when the boat comes out at the other end of the bend, she is far ahead of the Natchez Star. A great cheer goes up from the passengers. Captain Lowell throws his hat in the air. And Mr. Grant just smiles quietly, under his grey beard.

[pause]

{img:other-steamboat-falling-behind-whistle-salute-friendly-passengers-waving}
In the end, the Natchez Star gives up. She is too far behind. Her pilot blows a long whistle, as a friendly salute, and Mr. Grant answers with the Clara Belle's whistle. The two boats go on, each at its own speed. The passengers go back to the saloon, still excited, talking about the race. It will be the main subject of conversation for the rest of the journey.

[long-pause]

But racing was also dangerous. When the boilers were pushed too hard, they could become too hot. On a few sad occasions, they burst, and many people were hurt. That is why Mr. Grant never lets the race go too far. He keeps an eye on the engine room, and he stops the race when it is time. Later, Ben asks him why. Mr. Grant says that a good pilot always knows when to stop. Winning a race is nice. But bringing everyone home safely is the only thing that really matters.

[long-pause]

{img:cotton-fields-mississippi-delta-white-bolls-flat-land-big-house-distance}
Below Memphis, the land along the river becomes flat and very fertile. This is the land of cotton. On both sides of the river, there are huge plantations, with fields stretching away as far as the eye can see. In the middle of each plantation, there is a big white house, with tall columns and a wide porch, shaded by old oak trees. From the river, it looks beautiful and peaceful.

[long-pause]

But behind this beauty, there was a great sorrow. On these plantations, the cotton was grown and picked by enslaved people. Men, women and children, who had been taken from their families, bought and sold, and forced to work without pay, for their whole lives. In 1850, there were more than three million enslaved people in America. Most of them lived in the South, and many of them worked in the cotton fields along the Mississippi.

[pause]

We will not look closely at their suffering. But we should remember it, and honour the people who lived through it. Because the wealth of the river, the fine steamboats and the big white houses, was built on their work. Cotton was the most valuable export of America. It went to the factories of England and the North, where it was made into cloth for the whole world. And it was picked by people who were not free.

[pause]

{img:enslaved-families-cabins-evening-singing-together-fire-gentle-distance}
From the steamboat, Ben sees them working in the fields. Long rows of people, bending over the cotton plants, in the hot sun. He sees the small cabins where they live, in rows behind the big house. And in the evenings, as the boat passes close to the shore, he sometimes hears singing. Slow, beautiful songs, rising up from the fields and the cabins, in the dusk.

[pause]

These songs were called spirituals. They were songs of faith and hope, of sorrow and of longing for freedom. Some of them told stories from the Bible, like the story of Moses leading his people out of slavery in Egypt. Some of them, people say, carried secret messages, about the way to freedom. Many of them are still sung today, all over the world. They are among the most beautiful songs ever made.

[long-pause]

{img:people-holding-hands-river-shore-dusk-quiet-strength-hope}
Despite everything, the enslaved people of the South built their own lives and their own communities. They loved their families, raised their children, and cared for each other. They kept their faith, their stories and their music. They held secret prayer meetings in the woods at night. They taught each other to read, although it was forbidden. And many of them never stopped hoping and working for freedom.

[pause]

On the Clara Belle, Mr. Price tells Ben about a man he once knew, who escaped from a plantation near Natchez. He hid on a steamboat, among the cotton bales, and travelled all the way to St. Louis. From there, the helpers of the Underground Railroad took him north to Canada. Years later, Mr. Price received a letter from him. He was free. He had a family and a little farm. And he wrote that every morning, when he woke up, he thanked God.

[pause]

{img:sunrise-over-cotton-fields-mist-quiet-promise-of-new-day}
Ben listens to Mr. Price's story, and does not say anything for a long time. He is learning many things on this journey. Not only how to steer a steamboat. But also how to look at the world, and see what is really there. Mr. Price puts a hand on his shoulder. He says that the river is long, and that it always flows on. And that one day, even this will change.

[long-pause]

It did change, fifteen years later, at the end of a long and terrible war. In 1865, slavery was finally ended in the whole of the United States. Four million people became free. It was the beginning of a new chapter, a long and difficult struggle for equal rights, which would last for more than a hundred years. But on that day in 1865, along the banks of the Mississippi, people sang and prayed and cried with joy.

[long-pause]

{img:natchez-bluff-1850-mansions-trees-landing-below-under-the-hill-steamboat}
On the fifth day, the Clara Belle comes to Natchez, in the state of Mississippi. It is one of the oldest towns on the river. It was founded by the French, more than a hundred and thirty years before. And it is named after the Natchez people, a Native American nation who lived here long before the Europeans came.

[long-pause]

Natchez is really two towns. Up on top of the high bluff, there is the elegant town, with wide, quiet streets, shady trees and beautiful houses. Many of the richest cotton planters in America live here, in grand mansions with white columns, surrounded by gardens of roses and magnolias. Down below, at the foot of the bluff, by the river, there is another town. It is called Natchez Under-the-Hill.

[pause]

Natchez Under-the-Hill is a rough, noisy little place, crowded between the river and the steep bluff. There are taverns, cheap hotels, warehouses and stores. It is where the boatmen, the deckhands and the travellers stay. In the old days, it was famous for its gamblers and its fights. By 1850, it is a little calmer than it used to be. But respectable people from the town on the hill still do not like to go there.

[pause]

{img:natchez-mansion-white-columns-magnolia-trees-garden-afternoon}
The Clara Belle stops at Natchez for the afternoon. Little Henry's mother wants to visit a friend who lives in one of the big houses on the hill. She asks Ben to come along and carry her bags. So Ben and Henry and his mother walk up the long, steep road from the landing to the top of the bluff. It is hot. By the time they reach the top, they are all out of breath.

[pause]

The house of the friend is enormous. It has two floors, with tall white columns along the front, and a wide porch where people sit in the shade. In the garden, there are magnolia trees, with big white flowers that smell sweet and heavy. Inside, the rooms are high and cool, with fine furniture from Europe, crystal chandeliers and portraits on the walls. Ben has never seen such a house.

[long-pause]

{img:magnolia-blossom-close-white-petals-sweet-southern-afternoon-light}
But Ben also sees the people who keep the house running. The cooks in the kitchen, behind the house. The maids carrying trays. The gardeners working in the heat. They are all enslaved. They work silently and carefully, and the people in the house hardly seem to see them. Ben sees them now. After everything he has heard and seen on the river, he cannot stop seeing them.

[pause]

While Henry's mother visits her friend, Ben and Henry walk to the edge of the bluff. There is a little park there, with benches under the trees. From here, there is a wonderful view over the river. It is very wide here, wide and brown and slow, curving away to the south. On the other side, there is Louisiana, flat and green, with forests stretching away to the horizon.

[pause]

{img:view-from-natchez-bluff-wide-river-louisiana-flat-forests-horizon}
Henry asks Ben how far it is to New Orleans. Ben says it is about four hundred and fifty kilometres, about two more days on the boat. Henry says that he cannot wait to see his father. He has not seen him for almost a year. His father has written that he has a surprise for him. Henry thinks it might be a pony. Ben thinks it might be a puppy. They argue about it happily, all the way back down the hill.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the Clara Belle leaves Natchez. As she backs away from the landing, the sun is setting behind the forests of Louisiana. The bluff glows red and gold. The windows of the big houses on top catch the last light. And down below, in Natchez Under-the-Hill, the lamps are being lit in the taverns. Someone is playing a fiddle. The music drifts out over the water, as the boat turns south.

[pause]

Ben will remember Natchez for a long time. The grand houses, the sweet smell of the magnolias, the beautiful view over the river. But also the quiet people in the kitchen, behind the house, whom nobody seemed to see. Many years later, when he is an old man, he will tell his grandchildren about that afternoon. He will tell them that he saw both sides of the town that day, and that he never forgot either of them.

[long-pause]

{img:thick-fog-on-river-dawn-steamboat-faint-shape-white-nothing}
On the morning of the sixth day, Ben wakes up early and goes up to the pilothouse. When he opens the door, he stops. Outside the windows, there is nothing. Only white. A thick fog has come down over the river during the night. It is so thick that he cannot even see the front of the boat.

[long-pause]

Fog is one of the greatest dangers on the river. In fog, the pilot cannot see the banks, or the sandbars, or the floating logs, or other boats. He cannot see anything at all. Mr. Grant has already slowed the boat down, until she is barely moving. He stands at the wheel, peering into the whiteness. His face is very serious.

[pause]

The boat moves forward, very slowly. The leadsmen are at the front, throwing their leads and calling out the depths. Their voices sound strange and muffled in the fog. Mark three. Quarter less three. Every few minutes, Mr. Grant blows the whistle, a long, deep sound, so that any other boats will know they are there. From far away, somewhere in the fog, another whistle answers.

[pause]

{img:leadsmen-in-fog-bow-ropes-dim-figures-calling-depth-white-mist}
Ben stands beside Mr. Grant, staring out into the fog. He cannot see anything. But Mr. Grant seems to know where they are. He listens to the leadsmen. He listens to the sound of the water. He notices little changes in the current. And sometimes, for just a moment, the fog thins a little, and he sees the dark shape of a tree on the shore. That is all he needs.

[pause]

Then, suddenly, the leadsman calls out in a sharp voice. Snag ahead! A snag is a whole tree, fallen into the river, with its roots stuck in the mud at the bottom, and its trunk pointing up, just under the surface. Snags are very dangerous. They can tear a hole in the bottom of a boat. Mr. Grant rings the bell for the engines to stop, and then to go back. The paddle wheels churn the water.

[long-pause]

{img:fallen-tree-snag-branches-rising-from-water-fog-close-danger}
Out of the fog, very close, Ben sees it. A huge dark tree trunk, sticking up out of the water, with broken branches like black fingers. The boat slows down, slower and slower. Ben holds his breath. The snag comes closer. And then the boat stops, and begins to move backwards, just a few metres from the tree. Mr. Grant turns the wheel, and the boat slides slowly past the snag, with plenty of room to spare.

[pause]

Ben lets out his breath. His hands are shaking. Mr. Grant looks at him and says calmly, that is why we go slowly in the fog, son. The river always gives you a little warning. You just have to be listening. Then he says that they will tie up to the bank and wait until the fog lifts. It is too dangerous to go on.

[pause]

{img:steamboat-tied-to-trees-riverbank-fog-lifting-sun-breaking-through}
So the Clara Belle slowly finds the bank, and the deckhands tie her to a big tree with thick ropes. And there she waits, in the white silence. The passengers come out on deck, wrapped in shawls, and look at the fog. It is very quiet. They can hear birds singing in the forest. A fish jumps in the water. Somewhere far away, a cow is mooing.

[long-pause]

After about three hours, the sun begins to break through. The fog turns golden, and then thin, and then it lifts slowly off the water, like a curtain rising in a theatre. And there is the river, wide and calm and sparkling, with the green forest on both sides. The snag is far behind them now. The deckhands untie the ropes. The whistle blows. And the Clara Belle steams on, toward New Orleans.

[long-pause]

{img:new-orleans-levee-1850-hundreds-steamboats-ships-masts-cotton-bales-busy}
On the morning of the seventh day, the Clara Belle comes around a great curve of the river. And there, on the left bank, lies New Orleans. Ben has heard about it all his life. But nothing has prepared him for what he sees. Along the riverbank, for kilometres, there are ships and steamboats. Hundreds of them. Steamboats lying side by side at the levee, and behind them, the tall masts of ocean ships, from every country in the world.

[long-pause]

New Orleans in 1850 is one of the biggest and richest cities in America. More than a hundred thousand people live here. It is the great port of the Mississippi. Everything that comes down the river ends up here. Cotton, sugar, wheat, corn, pork, tobacco and whisky. From here, it is loaded onto ocean ships, and carried to New York, Liverpool, Le Havre and many other ports. And the ships bring back goods from all over the world.

[pause]

The city is built in a great curve of the river. That is why it is called the Crescent City. It lies very low, below the level of the river in some places, protected by high earth banks. The oldest part of the city is called the French Quarter. It was built by the French and the Spanish, long ago, when New Orleans belonged to them. It has narrow streets, old houses with iron balconies, little courtyards with fountains, and a big old church on a square.

[pause]

{img:french-quarter-1850-iron-balconies-narrow-street-flowers-people}
The Clara Belle ties up at the levee. The whole crew is busy unloading the cargo. Ben says goodbye to the passengers. Little Henry runs down the gangplank and into the arms of his father, who is waiting on the levee. And the surprise is not a pony or a puppy. It is a baby sister, born just two months ago. Henry does not know whether to be happy or disappointed. In the end, he decides to be happy.

[pause]

Ben says goodbye to the Weber family too. They will stay in New Orleans for a few days, and then take a smaller boat west, toward Texas. Mrs. Weber kisses Ben on both cheeks. Mr. Weber shakes his hand. And Karl gives Ben a small wooden horse that he carved himself, during the journey. Ben gives Karl his pocket knife. They promise to write. Ben watches the family walk away along the levee, carrying everything they own, into a new life.

[long-pause]

{img:french-market-new-orleans-1850-stalls-fruit-coffee-many-people-languages}
The boat will stay in New Orleans for four days, to unload and load again. So Ben has some time to explore the city. He goes with Mr. Price to the French Market, by the river. It is an enormous market, under long covered halls. Everything is sold here. Fish, crabs and oysters, fruit and vegetables, flowers, coffee and spices, bananas and oranges from the islands of the Caribbean. Ben hears more languages than he has ever heard in his life. French, Spanish, English, German, Italian, and many more.

[pause]

Mr. Price buys them each a cup of strong black coffee, and a plate of fried dough, covered in powdered sugar. Ben has never tasted anything so good. They sit at a little table and watch the people go by. There are rich ladies with parasols, sailors from every country, fishermen, nuns, soldiers, and many free people of colour, who are an important part of the life of New Orleans. It is the most colourful city Ben has ever seen.

[pause]

{img:jackson-square-cathedral-1850-evening-people-strolling-music}
In the evening, they walk to the big square in front of the old cathedral. People are strolling under the trees in the cool evening air. Somewhere, a band is playing. Children are running around. Old men are sitting on benches, talking. The cathedral bells ring for evening prayers. And Ben thinks that the river has brought him a very long way from his father's little store in Missouri.

[long-pause]

But New Orleans had a dark side too. It was the biggest market for enslaved people in the whole country. Thousands of people were brought here every year, and sold. Mr. Price walks Ben quickly past those places, without stopping. He does not need to say anything. Ben understands. That night, in his small cabin on the Texas deck, he lies awake for a long time, thinking about everything he has seen.

[pause]

On the last evening in New Orleans, Ben sits on the hurricane deck of the Clara Belle, looking out over the levee. The city lights are shining. Music drifts across from the French Quarter. The great ocean ships are lying quietly at anchor, their masts black against the stars. Ben thinks about the ocean beyond the river, and all the countries on the other side of it. One day, perhaps, he will see them too.

[long-pause]

{img:steamboat-leaving-new-orleans-heading-upstream-morning-city-behind}
After four days, the Clara Belle is ready to go back up the river. Her main deck is loaded with new cargo. Sugar, coffee, molasses, cloth, iron, and goods from all over the world, for the towns along the river. New passengers have come on board. The bell rings, the whistle sounds, and the boat turns her nose upstream, toward the north.

[long-pause]

Going upstream is very different from going downstream. The boat must fight against the current the whole way. It takes much longer, about twice as long. And for the pilot, it is like learning a new river. Everything looks different from the other direction. The points, the bends, the islands. The pilot must steer differently too. Going upstream, he keeps close to the banks, where the current is weaker, and avoids the strong current in the middle.

[pause]

Mr. Grant teaches Ben all of this, patiently, day after day. Ben's notebook gets fuller and fuller. He now has two notebooks, one for downstream and one for upstream. He studies them in his free time, in his cabin, by the light of a candle. And slowly, the river begins to make sense to him. The names of the points and bends start to connect. He begins to see the river as a whole, like a long, winding map in his head.

[pause]

{img:boy-studying-notebook-candle-small-cabin-night-river-sounds}
On the way back, they pass all the places they passed on the way down. Natchez, on its bluff. The plantations, with their big houses and long fields. Memphis, with its cotton bales. Cairo, at the meeting of the rivers. But now Ben sees them differently. He knows their names. He knows the people who live there. He knows the stories of the river, the beautiful ones and the sad ones.

[pause]

He has changed during this journey. He has grown up a little. He is no longer just a boy from a small town who dreams of steamboats. He has seen the great river from top to bottom. He has met people from many countries and many walks of life. He has seen great beauty and great injustice. And he has begun to understand that the world is bigger, and more complicated, than he ever imagined.

[long-pause]

{img:pilot-and-cub-standing-together-pilothouse-dawn-mutual-respect}
One evening, near the end of the journey, Mr. Grant lets Ben take the wheel for a whole hour, on a long, difficult stretch of river. Ben steers carefully, reading the water, watching the banks, listening to the leadsmen. He makes no mistakes. When the hour is over, Mr. Grant takes the wheel back. He says quietly that Ben will make a good pilot one day. A very good pilot. Ben does not say anything. But inside, he is shining like the sun.

[pause]

After almost two weeks, the Clara Belle comes back to St. Louis. And on the way, she passes the landing of Ben's hometown. Mr. Grant blows the whistle, three times, long and loud. And on the landing, Ben sees a small crowd. His father, his mother, his sisters and his little brother, all waving. Someone has told them the boat was coming. Ben waves back, from the window of the pilothouse, as high as he can reach.

[pause]

{img:family-waving-small-river-landing-steamboat-passing-whistle-steam}
The boat does not stop. There is no time. But that does not matter. His family has seen him, standing in the pilothouse, beside the pilot. And Ben has seen them, standing on the landing, where he once stood as a little boy, watching the steamboats go by and dreaming. His mother is crying, and waving her handkerchief. His father has taken off his hat. And his little brother is jumping up and down, shouting something that Ben cannot hear, but can easily guess.

[long-pause]

Ben became a pilot two and a half years later. He piloted steamboats on the Mississippi for many years. He never forgot Mr. Grant, or Mr. Price, or Karl, or little Henry. And he never forgot that first journey down the great river, when he was fifteen, and the whole world lay before him, wide and brown and shining in the sun.

[long-pause]

{img:railroad-bridge-over-mississippi-1870s-train-steam-river-below-later}
In 1850, the steamboats of the Mississippi were at the height of their glory. The next ten years were the golden age of the steamboat. The boats became bigger, faster and more splendid than ever. There were more than a thousand steamboats on the western rivers. And the young Samuel Clemens, the boy from Hannibal, finally became a pilot himself, in 1859.

[long-pause]

But then, in 1861, the American Civil War began. The Northern states and the Southern states went to war, over slavery and the future of the country. The Mississippi became a battlefield. Many steamboats were taken by the armies, and used to carry soldiers and supplies. Trade on the river stopped almost completely. Samuel Clemens left the river. He went west, to Nevada and California, and began to write. He never worked as a pilot again.

[pause]

In 1865, the war ended. The North had won. And slavery was abolished in the whole country. Four million people were free at last. It was the most important change in the history of America. But the long struggle for true equality was only beginning. It would take another hundred years before Black Americans would gain equal rights by law, after the great civil rights movement of the nineteen fifties and sixties.

[pause]

{img:last-golden-steamboats-1870s-river-fewer-boats-quiet-landing-later}
After the war, the steamboats came back to the river. But the world had changed. More and more railways were being built across America. The trains were faster than the steamboats, and they could go anywhere, not only along the rivers. Great iron bridges were built across the Mississippi, carrying the railways over the water. And slowly, year by year, the trains took away the passengers and the cargo from the steamboats.

[pause]

By the end of the eighteen hundreds, there were only a few steamboats left on the river. The great floating palaces were gone. The levees of St. Louis and New Orleans, once crowded with steamboats, were quiet. The woodyards disappeared. The pilots found other jobs. And the golden age of the Mississippi steamboat became a memory, and then a legend.

[long-pause]

{img:writer-white-suit-white-hair-porch-rocking-chair-river-view-later}
That legend lives on, mostly thanks to one man. Samuel Clemens, as Mark Twain, became the most famous writer in America. In 1883, he wrote a book called Life on the Mississippi, about his years as a cub pilot. It describes the river, the steamboats, the pilots and the passengers, just as they were. And he wrote The Adventures of Huckleberry Finn, about a boy and a man escaping from slavery, who float down the Mississippi on a raft. It is one of the most famous books in American literature.

[pause]

In 1882, Mark Twain went back to the river, as an old man, to see it again. He travelled on a steamboat from St. Louis to New Orleans, and then up to Minnesota. He found that everything had changed. The river had moved. The towns had grown. The old pilots were gone. And the steamboats were few. He wrote about it with great love, and a little sadness, as if he were visiting an old friend who had grown old.

[pause]

{img:modern-towboat-pushing-barges-mississippi-river-later-sunset}
The river kept changing too. People built great levees along its banks, to stop the floods. They dug channels and built locks and dams, to make the river easier for ships. They cut off some of the loops, to make the river shorter and straighter. Today, the Mississippi is still one of the busiest rivers in the world. But now, instead of steamboats, there are towboats, pushing long rows of barges, loaded with grain, coal and oil.

[long-pause]

As for the people in our story, they were not real, but they were like many real people of those days. Pilots and cub pilots, free stewards and enslaved deckhands, immigrant families and cotton merchants. Their lives were all connected by the great river. And their stories, the happy ones and the sad ones, flowed down the river together, like the brown water, toward the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:modern-paddlewheel-steamboat-tourists-new-orleans-riverfront-today}
Today, there are still a few steamboats on the Mississippi. Not to carry cotton or wood, but to carry visitors, who want to know what it was like to travel on the river long ago. In New Orleans, a white paddlewheel steamboat still leaves the levee every day, with a tall red paddle wheel at the back. She takes the passengers on a short trip along the river, past the city, while a band plays jazz on the deck.

[long-pause]

There are also bigger boats, which make long journeys up and down the river, for several days or weeks, just like the old steamboats. They have staterooms and dining rooms, and a grand saloon, with chandeliers and a piano. The passengers sit on the decks and watch the river go by. They stop at the old river towns. And in the evenings, someone plays the steam organ, called a calliope, whose whistling music can be heard for kilometres.

[pause]

The old river towns are still there too. St. Louis is now a big city, with a tall silver arch on the riverbank, called the Gateway Arch. It marks the place where so many travellers once set out into the west. Memphis is famous today for its music, the blues and rock and roll. Natchez still has its beautiful old houses on the bluff, and its little street Under-the-Hill. And Cairo, at the meeting of the rivers, is a quiet little town, much smaller than its people once dreamed.

[pause]

{img:hannibal-missouri-today-mark-twain-boyhood-home-white-fence-river}
In Hannibal, Missouri, you can visit the boyhood home of Mark Twain. It is a small white house, with a white fence in front. Visitors from all over the world come to see it. They walk down to the river, where the young Samuel Clemens once watched the steamboats and dreamed. Every summer, there are festivals, with fence-painting contests, frog-jumping contests and boys and girls dressed up as characters from his books.

[pause]

There are also places where the darker history of the river is remembered. In many towns along the river, there are now museums and memorials about slavery, about the Underground Railroad, and about the long struggle for freedom and equal rights. Visitors can learn about the people who were enslaved along the Mississippi, and about the brave people who helped them escape. Their stories are no longer forgotten.

[long-pause]

{img:river-sunset-today-wide-mississippi-towboat-lights-calm-water}
And the river itself still flows, as it always has. It is still wide and brown and powerful. It still winds through the flat land, past forests and fields and towns. In the spring, it still floods. And it still carries the mud and the water of half a continent, down to the sea.

[pause]

If you stand on the levee at New Orleans in the evening, you can watch the river flow past, wide and slow, in the last light of the day. The towboats go by with their long lines of barges. The ferry crosses to the other side. The lights of the city come on. And sometimes, from far away, you can hear a steamboat whistle, deep and long, echoing across the water. The same sound that Ben heard, and Mark Twain, and so many others, long ago.

[pause]

{img:old-steamboat-whistle-sounding-steam-plume-evening-sky-riverfront}
Some people say that, on quiet nights, they can still feel the old river. The river of the steamboats and the pilots, the woodyards and the landings, the leadsmen calling in the dark. Mark twain. Two fathoms. Safe water.

[long-pause]

It is a good thought to end on. Safe water. Wherever you are tonight, may you be in safe water too. Calm and deep, with the river flowing gently past, and nothing to worry about until the morning.

[long-pause]

{img:mississippi-1850-evening-steamboat-golden-sunset-wide-river-forest-silhouettes}
So we leave the Mississippi tonight, in the year 1850, as the evening falls. The sun is setting over the forests on the western shore. The wide brown river has turned to gold. And on the gold, a white steamboat is gliding slowly downstream, with smoke rising from her tall chimneys into the evening sky.

[long-pause]

On the decks, the passengers are leaning on the railings, watching the sunset. In the grand saloon, the lamps are being lit, and the long table is being set for supper. On the main deck, the deck passengers are cooking their simple meals, and the children are playing among the crates. Down in the engine room, the engines are beating steadily, like a great slow heart.

[pause]

{img:pilothouse-dusk-old-pilot-and-boy-at-wheel-river-turning-blue}
High up in the pilothouse, an old pilot is standing at the wheel, with his hands resting lightly on the spokes. Beside him stands a boy of fifteen, with a notebook in his hand, looking out at the river. The light is fading. The banks are turning dark. And the old pilot is quietly telling the boy the names of the points and the bends, one by one, as they pass.

[long-pause]

The night comes down softly over the river. The first stars appear in the sky. The windows of the boat light up, golden in the dark. Sparks fly up from the chimneys, and drift away over the water like little red fireflies. On the shore, a light is burning in the window of a lonely woodyard cabin. A dog barks. An owl calls from the forest.

[pause]

{img:steamboat-night-lit-windows-reflections-stars-sparks-calm-river}
The paddle wheels turn and turn, splashing softly in the dark water. The boat rocks very gently. In their staterooms, the passengers are going to sleep. On the main deck, the immigrant families are wrapped in their blankets, dreaming of new land. And somewhere along the dark shore, in a little cabin, people are singing quietly, a slow song of hope.

[long-pause]

The river flows on, wide and calm, through the night. It flows past the sleeping towns and the dark forests, past the landings and the woodyards, past the cotton fields and the big white houses. It has flowed this way for thousands of years, long before the steamboats, and it will flow on long after. Slowly, patiently, all the way to the sea.

[pause]

If you enjoyed this journey on a Mississippi steamboat in 1850, you are warmly invited to subscribe, so that another calm story from the past is always close by, whenever you need one.

[pause]

{img:moonlight-over-wide-river-distant-steamboat-lights-peaceful-night}
There should be another quiet story waiting on the screen now, if you are still awake.

[pause]

Sleep well, and good night.
