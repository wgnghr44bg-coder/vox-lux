At ten o'clock in the morning, the Clara Belle is ready to leave. The last cargo is loaded. The last passengers hurry up the wooden gangplank, carrying their bags. The clerk checks the tickets. The mate shouts orders to the deckhands. And on the levee, a crowd has gathered to watch the boat leave. Leaving was always a little show on the Mississippi.

--

The captain stands on the hurricane deck, in a dark blue coat and a tall hat. His name is Captain Lowell. He is a round, cheerful man with white side whiskers. He pulls a rope, and the great bell on the roof rings out. Ding, ding, ding. Then he signals to the pilothouse. Mr. Grant pulls another rope, and the deep steam whistle sounds, long and loud, over the whole levee. Ben feels it in his chest.

Down below, the deckhands pull in the heavy ropes and lift the gangplank. Mr. Grant rings a bell to the engine room. The engines begin to work. Chuff, chuff, chuff. The great paddle wheels begin to turn, slowly at first, churning up the brown water into white foam. And slowly, the Clara Belle backs away from the levee, out into the river.

{img:pilot-turning-big-wheel-boy-watching-pilothouse-city-behind}
In the pilothouse, Mr. Grant turns the big wheel. The boat swings around, until her nose points downstream, toward the south. Then he rings the bell again, and the paddle wheels begin to turn forward. The boat picks up speed. The levee slides away behind them, with its crowds and its long row of steamboats. The city of St. Louis, with its church towers and warehouses and smoke, gets smaller and smaller.

Ben stands at the window of the pilothouse, watching everything. He has never been this high above the river before. He can see everything. The wide brown water, the green banks, the islands, the other boats. The wind blows through the open windows, cool and fresh. The deck vibrates gently under his feet with the beat of the engines. And he thinks that he has never been so happy in his whole life.

--

{img:river-below-st-louis-wide-water-wooded-banks-other-steamboats-passing}
Below St. Louis, the river is wide and busy. They pass other steamboats, coming up from the south, heavily loaded with cargo. Each time they pass a boat, the pilots blow their whistles in greeting. And sometimes the passengers wave to each other across the water. They pass flatboats and rafts, drifting slowly with the current, with families living in little huts on top. They pass small towns and landings, and lonely farmhouses among the trees.

A few kilometres below St. Louis, there is a muddy, swirling stretch of water. Ben does not notice anything special about it. But Mr. Grant points it out. That is where the Missouri River came in, a little above the city, he says. The Missouri is even muddier than the Mississippi. Its water is almost the colour of coffee with milk. For many kilometres, the two waters flow side by side, without mixing. One side brown, and the other side a little lighter.

{img:two-colours-of-water-flowing-side-by-side-river-sunlight-boat}
Mr. Grant does not talk very much. He stands at the wheel, looking ahead at the river, and steers the boat with small, calm movements. Sometimes he hums a tune to himself. Sometimes he spits tobacco juice out of the window. And sometimes, without turning around, he says something to Ben. That point there, with the big cottonwood tree, is called such and such. Remember it. Ben tries to remember everything. But there is so much.

--

At midday, the dinner bell rings, and Mr. Grant's partner comes up to take the wheel. Every steamboat has two pilots. They take turns, four hours on and four hours off, day and night. The other pilot is a younger man called Mr. Hale, thin and cheerful. Mr. Grant and Ben go down to eat. And Ben realises that he is starving. He has been too excited to eat breakfast.

After dinner, Ben goes back up to the pilothouse, although it is not his watch. He cannot stay away. Mr. Hale, the other pilot, does not mind. He lets Ben sit on the high bench at the back, and he tells him funny stories about the river, about pilots he has known, and boats he has sailed on. By the end of the afternoon, Ben has laughed so much that his stomach hurts.
