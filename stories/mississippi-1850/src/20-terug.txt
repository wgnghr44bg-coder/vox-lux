After four days, the Clara Belle is ready to go back up the river. Her main deck is loaded with new cargo. Sugar, coffee, molasses, cloth, iron, and goods from all over the world, for the towns along the river. New passengers have come on board. The bell rings, the whistle sounds, and the boat turns her nose upstream, toward the north.

--

Going upstream is very different from going downstream. The boat must fight against the current the whole way. It takes much longer, about twice as long. And for the pilot, it is like learning a new river. Everything looks different from the other direction. The points, the bends, the islands. The pilot must steer differently too. Going upstream, he keeps close to the banks, where the current is weaker, and avoids the strong current in the middle.

Mr. Grant teaches Ben all of this, patiently, day after day. Ben's notebook gets fuller and fuller. He now has two notebooks, one for downstream and one for upstream. He studies them in his free time, in his cabin, by the light of a candle. And slowly, the river begins to make sense to him. The names of the points and bends start to connect. He begins to see the river as a whole, like a long, winding map in his head.

{img:boy-studying-notebook-candle-small-cabin-night-river-sounds}
On the way back, they pass all the places they passed on the way down. Natchez, on its bluff. The plantations, with their big houses and long fields. Memphis, with its cotton bales. Cairo, at the meeting of the rivers. But now Ben sees them differently. He knows their names. He knows the people who live there. He knows the stories of the river, the beautiful ones and the sad ones.

He has changed during this journey. He has grown up a little. He is no longer just a boy from a small town who dreams of steamboats. He has seen the great river from top to bottom. He has met people from many countries and many walks of life. He has seen great beauty and great injustice. And he has begun to understand that the world is bigger, and more complicated, than he ever imagined.

--

{img:pilot-and-cub-standing-together-pilothouse-dawn-mutual-respect}
One evening, near the end of the journey, Mr. Grant lets Ben take the wheel for a whole hour, on a long, difficult stretch of river. Ben steers carefully, reading the water, watching the banks, listening to the leadsmen. He makes no mistakes. When the hour is over, Mr. Grant takes the wheel back. He says quietly that Ben will make a good pilot one day. A very good pilot. Ben does not say anything. But inside, he is shining like the sun.

After almost two weeks, the Clara Belle comes back to St. Louis. And on the way, she passes the landing of Ben's hometown. Mr. Grant blows the whistle, three times, long and loud. And on the landing, Ben sees a small crowd. His father, his mother, his sisters and his little brother, all waving. Someone has told them the boat was coming. Ben waves back, from the window of the pilothouse, as high as he can reach.

{img:family-waving-small-river-landing-steamboat-passing-whistle-steam}
The boat does not stop. There is no time. But that does not matter. His family has seen him, standing in the pilothouse, beside the pilot. And Ben has seen them, standing on the landing, where he once stood as a little boy, watching the steamboats go by and dreaming. His mother is crying, and waving her handkerchief. His father has taken off his hat. And his little brother is jumping up and down, shouting something that Ben cannot hear, but can easily guess.

--

Ben became a pilot two and a half years later. He piloted steamboats on the Mississippi for many years. He never forgot Mr. Grant, or Mr. Price, or Karl, or little Henry. And he never forgot that first journey down the great river, when he was fifteen, and the whole world lay before him, wide and brown and shining in the sun.
