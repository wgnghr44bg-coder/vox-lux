In 1850, the steamboats of the Mississippi were at the height of their glory. The next ten years were the golden age of the steamboat. The boats became bigger, faster and more splendid than ever. There were more than a thousand steamboats on the western rivers. And the young Samuel Clemens, the boy from Hannibal, finally became a pilot himself, in 1859.

--

But then, in 1861, the American Civil War began. The Northern states and the Southern states went to war, over slavery and the future of the country. The Mississippi became a battlefield. Many steamboats were taken by the armies, and used to carry soldiers and supplies. Trade on the river stopped almost completely. Samuel Clemens left the river. He went west, to Nevada and California, and began to write. He never worked as a pilot again.

In 1865, the war ended. The North had won. And slavery was abolished in the whole country. Four million people were free at last. It was the most important change in the history of America. But the long struggle for true equality was only beginning. It would take another hundred years before Black Americans would gain equal rights by law, after the great civil rights movement of the nineteen fifties and sixties.

{img:last-golden-steamboats-1870s-river-fewer-boats-quiet-landing-later}
After the war, the steamboats came back to the river. But the world had changed. More and more railways were being built across America. The trains were faster than the steamboats, and they could go anywhere, not only along the rivers. Great iron bridges were built across the Mississippi, carrying the railways over the water. And slowly, year by year, the trains took away the passengers and the cargo from the steamboats.

By the end of the eighteen hundreds, there were only a few steamboats left on the river. The great floating palaces were gone. The levees of St. Louis and New Orleans, once crowded with steamboats, were quiet. The woodyards disappeared. The pilots found other jobs. And the golden age of the Mississippi steamboat became a memory, and then a legend.

--

{img:writer-white-suit-white-hair-porch-rocking-chair-river-view-later}
That legend lives on, mostly thanks to one man. Samuel Clemens, as Mark Twain, became the most famous writer in America. In 1883, he wrote a book called Life on the Mississippi, about his years as a cub pilot. It describes the river, the steamboats, the pilots and the passengers, just as they were. And he wrote The Adventures of Huckleberry Finn, about a boy and a man escaping from slavery, who float down the Mississippi on a raft. It is one of the most famous books in American literature.

In 1882, Mark Twain went back to the river, as an old man, to see it again. He travelled on a steamboat from St. Louis to New Orleans, and then up to Minnesota. He found that everything had changed. The river had moved. The towns had grown. The old pilots were gone. And the steamboats were few. He wrote about it with great love, and a little sadness, as if he were visiting an old friend who had grown old.

{img:modern-towboat-pushing-barges-mississippi-river-later-sunset}
The river kept changing too. People built great levees along its banks, to stop the floods. They dug channels and built locks and dams, to make the river easier for ships. They cut off some of the loops, to make the river shorter and straighter. Today, the Mississippi is still one of the busiest rivers in the world. But now, instead of steamboats, there are towboats, pushing long rows of barges, loaded with grain, coal and oil.

--

As for the people in our story, they were not real, but they were like many real people of those days. Pilots and cub pilots, free stewards and enslaved deckhands, immigrant families and cotton merchants. Their lives were all connected by the great river. And their stories, the happy ones and the sad ones, flowed down the river together, like the brown water, toward the sea.
