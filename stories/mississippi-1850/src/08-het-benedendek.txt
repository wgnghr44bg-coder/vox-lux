Down below, on the main deck, there is a very different world. Here, among the crates and barrels and bales of cargo, travel the deck passengers. They have paid only a few dollars for the whole journey. For that price, they get a space on the floor of the deck, and nothing else. No cabin, no bed and no meals. They bring their own food and blankets, and sleep wherever they can find a space.

--

Many of the deck passengers are immigrants. In 1850, hundreds of thousands of people are coming to America every year, from Europe. Many of them come from Ireland, where there has been a terrible famine. Many come from Germany. They arrive in New Orleans or New York, and then travel up and down the rivers, looking for work or land. On the main deck of the Clara Belle, you can hear Irish, German and many other accents.

The deck is crowded and uncomfortable. It is noisy, because the engines and the boilers are right there. It is hot near the furnaces, and cold and damp at the open sides. When it rains, the rain blows in. The deck passengers cook their food on a small stove, and wash in buckets of river water. Babies cry. Men smoke their pipes. Women try to keep their children away from the edge.

{img:german-immigrant-family-deck-sitting-on-trunks-mother-child-blanket}
Ben makes friends with a German family, the Webers. The father is a carpenter. The mother has a baby on her lap, and there are three other children. They come from a small village in the south of Germany. They have been travelling for months. Across the ocean, then up the river from New Orleans to St. Louis. Now they are going back down again, because they have heard there is good land and work in Texas.

The oldest Weber boy, Karl, is the same age as Ben. He speaks only a little English. But the two boys manage to talk, with hands and smiles and a few words. Karl tells Ben about the mountains and forests of his home. Ben teaches him the names of things on the boat. Paddle wheel. Chimney. Pilot. Karl repeats them carefully, laughing at his own accent.

--

{img:firemen-feeding-furnaces-glow-sweat-night-main-deck-steamboat}
The main deck is also where much of the crew works. There are the firemen, who feed wood into the furnaces, day and night. It is hot, hard and exhausting work. There are the deckhands, who load and unload the cargo at every landing, and carry the wood on board. Many of them are Irish immigrants. And on the Clara Belle, as on many steamboats in 1850, some of the deckhands and firemen are enslaved Black men.

They do not work for themselves. They have been hired out by their owners, who keep their wages. They work as hard as anyone on the boat, often harder. They are not free to leave. This was one of the great wrongs of America in those years. Ben has grown up in Missouri, a slave state, and he has seen slavery all his life. But on the river, he begins to think about it more than he did before.

{img:steward-free-black-man-1850-white-jacket-saloon-dignified}
There are also free Black men and women working on the boat. The chief steward of the Clara Belle is a free Black man called Mr. Jonas Price. He is in charge of all the waiters, cooks and chambermaids. He is a tall, dignified man, very well dressed, who runs the dining room perfectly. He is kind to Ben, and sometimes saves him a piece of cake from the captain's table. He was born free in Ohio, and he has worked on the river for twenty years.

--

At night, the main deck is quiet, except for the hiss of the boilers and the beat of the engines. The deck passengers sleep on the floor, wrapped in their blankets. The firemen work on, their faces lit up by the orange glow of the furnaces. And the boat glides on, carrying all these different people, rich and poor, free and not free, down the great river together.
