Tonight we travel back about a hundred and seventy-five years, to the year 1850. We go to the middle of America, to the banks of a great, wide, brown river. It is the Mississippi. And on its waters, tall white boats with smoking chimneys are carrying people and goods up and down the river, day and night.

--

It is early morning in spring, in the city of St. Louis. A soft mist lies over the river. Along the riverbank, there is a long, sloping stone shore, called the levee. And along the levee, as far as you can see, there are steamboats, lying side by side, their noses pointed at the shore. There are dozens of them. Their tall black chimneys rise up through the mist like a forest of dark trees.

The levee is already busy. Men are rolling barrels and carrying sacks. Carts pulled by mules rattle over the stones. Bales of cotton, crates of goods and piles of firewood are stacked everywhere. Bells are ringing on the boats. Steam is hissing. And somewhere, a deep steam whistle sounds, long and low, echoing across the water. It is the sound of a steamboat getting ready to leave.

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

--

{img:white-paddle-steamboat-1850-decks-railings-twin-chimneys-pilothouse}
The steamboats of the Mississippi are wonderful to look at. They are long and wide and low in the water, painted white, with two or three decks stacked on top of each other, like the layers of a wedding cake. They have fancy wooden railings and decorations. On top, there is a little glass house, where the pilot steers. And at the front, two tall chimneys rise high into the air, sometimes with decorations on top, like crowns.

In 1850, the steamboat is the queen of the river. There are hardly any railways yet in this part of America, and the roads are very bad. So almost everything travels by river. Cotton, sugar, wheat, corn, pork, whisky, furniture, machines and people. Hundreds of steamboats sail on the Mississippi and its rivers. They connect the farms and towns of the middle of America with the great port of New Orleans, and with the rest of the world.

{img:boy-fifteen-1850-standing-levee-looking-at-steamboat-carpet-bag-morning}
Tonight, we will take a journey on one of these steamboats, down the Mississippi, from St. Louis to New Orleans. We will travel with a boy of fifteen, called Ben. He is about to start learning the most admired job on the river. He will become an apprentice pilot, learning to steer the great steamboats along the winding river, by day and by night.

--

We will explore the boat, from the grand saloon to the boiler deck. We will meet the passengers and the crew. We will stop at woodyards and little river towns. We will see the river at night, under the stars, and in the thick fog of the morning. And we will see the cotton fields of the South, where enslaved people were forced to work, for we cannot tell the story of the river in 1850 without them. We will tell their part of the story carefully and gently.

{img:steamboat-pulling-away-from-levee-paddlewheel-turning-smoke-sunrise}
So let us begin on the levee at St. Louis, on a spring morning in 1850. The mist is lifting from the brown water. The sun is rising over the Illinois shore, on the other side of the river. And on the deck of a steamboat called the Clara Belle, a boy with a carpet bag is climbing the stairs to the top of the boat, to meet the pilot who will teach him the river.

Far up the river, in a little town in Missouri called Hannibal, there lives another boy of the same age. He also watches the steamboats every day, and dreams of becoming a pilot. His name is Samuel Clemens. One day, he will become a pilot himself. And later, he will become one of the most famous writers in America, under the name Mark Twain. We will meet him again, later in our story.
