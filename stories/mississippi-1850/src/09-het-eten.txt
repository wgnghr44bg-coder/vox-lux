On a steamboat, mealtimes are very important. For the cabin passengers, there are three big meals a day. Breakfast, dinner at midday and supper in the evening. And between the meals, there is not much to do. So people look forward to them, and talk about them, and eat a great deal.

--

At mealtimes, a long table is set up in the grand saloon. It stretches almost the whole length of the room. It is covered with white tablecloths, and set with silver forks and knives, china plates and glass goblets. When the bell rings, the passengers hurry to the table. The ladies and their families sit at the end near the ladies' cabin. The captain sits at the head of the table, and the most important passengers sit beside him.

The food is plentiful. For dinner, there might be roast beef and roast chicken, ham, turkey and fish from the river. There are potatoes, corn, beans, peas and cabbage. There is soft white bread and cornbread, with butter. Then there are puddings, pies and cakes, and fruit. And there is coffee, tea and plenty of iced water. Yes, iced water. The boat carries big blocks of ice in its hold, packed in sawdust, cut from frozen lakes in the north during the winter.

{img:waiters-carrying-trays-saloon-steward-directing-dinner-bell}
The meals are served by the waiters, under the command of Mr. Price, the steward. The waiters are Black men, some free and some enslaved, dressed in white jackets. They carry huge trays of food up from the kitchen, and serve the dishes along the table, very quickly and smoothly. Mr. Price stands at the end of the room and watches everything. If a dish is late, he only has to raise his eyebrows.

Some travellers from Europe were surprised by how fast the Americans ate. They wrote that the passengers rushed to the table as soon as the bell rang, ate everything in ten minutes without saying a word, and then left. The Europeans, who liked long, slow dinners with conversation, did not know what to think. But perhaps the Americans were just very hungry, after a morning of watching the river.

--

{img:galley-kitchen-steamboat-cooks-stoves-pots-busy-steam}
The kitchen of the boat, called the galley, is on the main deck, near the back. It is small, hot and busy. There are several cooks, working at big iron stoves. They bake bread, roast meat, make soup and pies, all day long. The head cook is an older woman called Mrs. Hattie Moore. She is famous on the river for her peach pie. Some passengers say they choose the Clara Belle just for her pie.

The officers and the pilots eat at the long table too, after the passengers have finished. Ben eats with Mr. Grant. At first, he is shy at the big table, with all the silver and china. He does not know which fork to use. But Mr. Price shows him quietly, without anyone noticing. And soon Ben is eating as much as anyone. After a long morning in the pilothouse, he is always hungry.

{img:deck-passengers-cooking-small-stove-cornbread-bacon-children-eating}
The deck passengers, down below, eat much more simply. They bring their own food on board. Bread, bacon, cornmeal, cheese, dried apples and coffee. They cook it on a small stove that is shared by everyone. At the landings, they buy eggs, milk and fruit from the farmers who come down to the riverbank to sell. The food is plain, but filling.

--

Sometimes, when there is food left over from the saloon, Mr. Price sends it down to the main deck, to the deck passengers. Ben carries a basket of bread and cold meat down to the Weber family one evening. Mrs. Weber thanks him, with tears in her eyes. The children eat it all at once. Karl shakes Ben's hand very seriously, and says danke, thank you, in two languages at once.
