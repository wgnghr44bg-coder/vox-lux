Tonight we are going nearly two thousand five hundred years back, to the city of Athens, in ancient Greece, in the year 432 before Christ.

[long-pause]

On a great rock above the city, a new temple has just been finished, of shining white marble, the most beautiful building the Greeks have ever made. Below it, in the streets and the market place, in the theatre and the assembly, the people of Athens are talking, and arguing, and voting, and writing plays, and asking questions that people will still be asking two and a half thousand years later.

[pause]

It is the golden age of Athens. And tonight, we will spend a little time there, in its last peaceful summer.

[pause]

If you enjoy these slow evenings in the distant past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, and come back for another one.

[long-pause]

{img:greek-coast-blue-sea-islands-olive-hills-summer-haze}
Let us imagine Greece, as it is in 432 before Christ.

[pause]

It is a land of mountains and sea. Everywhere, there are steep, rocky mountains, grey and brown and green, covered with pine trees, and scrub, and wild herbs, thyme and sage and oregano, which fill the air with their scent in the summer heat. Between the mountains, there are little plains, and valleys, with fields, and olive groves, and vineyards. And everywhere, the sea is close: a deep, clear, shining blue, scattered with islands.

[long-pause]

The Greeks do not live in one country, with one king. They live in hundreds of little independent cities, each with its own government, and its own laws, and its own gods, and its own army, and its own coins. Each city, with the countryside around it, is called a polis. There are big ones, like Athens, and Sparta, and Corinth, and Thebes. And small ones, no more than a village, on a little island, or in a mountain valley.

[pause]

They often quarrel, and fight each other. But they all speak Greek, and worship the same gods, and know the same stories, the poems of Homer, about the war at Troy. And they all come together, every four years, at Olympia, for the Olympic Games, in honour of Zeus, the king of the gods.

[pause]

Greek cities are scattered all around the Mediterranean, and the Black Sea, wherever Greek sailors and settlers have gone: in southern Italy, and Sicily, and the south of France, and North Africa, and the coast of Turkey. A philosopher, Plato, will later say that the Greeks live around the sea like frogs around a pond.

[long-pause]

And of all the Greek cities, in 432, the richest, and the most powerful, and the most brilliant, is Athens.

[pause]

Athens is the leader of a great alliance of Greek cities, around the Aegean Sea, which pay it tribute, in silver, every year, for protection. It has the greatest navy in the world: three hundred warships. It has silver mines, at Laurion, in the south of its country. It has a great port, the Piraeus, full of ships, and merchants, and goods, from all over the Mediterranean.

[pause]

{img:piraeus-harbour-triremes-merchant-ships-warehouses-sunlight}
And it has something new, something that no other people in the world has ever tried before, in quite this way: democracy. The government of the people.

[long-pause]

In Athens, the ordinary citizens, the farmers, and the shoemakers, and the potters, and the sailors, all meet together, on a hill, every few days, and decide, by voting, all the great questions of the city: war and peace, and laws, and taxes, and buildings, and who will lead them.

[pause]

It is not a perfect democracy. Women cannot vote. Slaves cannot vote. Foreigners cannot vote. Perhaps only one person in five or six in Athens has a vote. But for the time, it is a remarkable thing.

[pause]

{img:pnyx-hill-assembly-citizens-seated-speaker-platform-morning}
And under this democracy, in these years, Athens has become the most extraordinary city in the world: a city of builders, and sculptors, and painters, and poets, and playwrights, and philosophers, and historians, and doctors, and scientists. Their names are still known, all over the world, today: Pericles, and Phidias, and Sophocles, and Euripides, and Socrates, and Herodotus.

[long-pause]

{img:attica-plain-olive-groves-mountains-hymettus-athens-distance}
Athens is not only a city. It is a whole little country, called Attica: a triangle of land, about the size of a small English county, or a Dutch province, jutting out into the sea, in the south east of mainland Greece.

[pause]

Attica has mountains: Mount Parnes, in the north, and Mount Pentelikon, in the north east, where the white marble comes from, and Mount Hymettus, to the east of the city, famous for its honey, and the purple colour it turns at sunset. And between the mountains, there are plains, with olive groves, and fields of barley and wheat, and vineyards, and little villages.

[long-pause]

Perhaps two hundred and fifty thousand, or three hundred thousand people live in Attica, in 432. About half of them live in the city of Athens, and its port, the Piraeus. The rest live in the country, in about a hundred and forty villages, called demes.

[pause]

The city itself lies in the middle of the plain, about seven kilometres from the sea. It is surrounded by a great wall, of stone and mud brick. And from the city to the port of Piraeus, there are two long walls, running side by side, all the way to the sea, with a road between them, so that even if an enemy army comes into Attica, the city can always reach the sea, and its ships, and get food.

[pause]

{img:long-walls-athens-to-piraeus-road-between-plain-sea}
In the middle of the city, there is a great rock, rising steeply out of the plain, with cliffs on every side, and a flat top: the Acropolis, the high city. On its top, there are the temples of the gods, and above all, the temple of Athena, the goddess of the city.

[long-pause]

Around the Acropolis, the city spreads out, on every side: a great jumble of houses, and streets, and temples, and markets, and workshops, and gardens, and fountains, and graveyards, outside the walls.

[pause]

The houses of ordinary people are small, and plain, built of mud bricks, on stone foundations, with roofs of red clay tiles, and whitewashed walls. The streets are narrow, and winding, and dusty in summer, and muddy in winter, and often smelly, because there are no drains, and people throw their rubbish into the street.

[pause]

{img:narrow-athens-street-whitewashed-walls-tiles-donkey-shade}
Even the rich live in quite simple houses. The Athenians believe that private houses should be modest, and that the money of the city should be spent on beautiful public buildings, for everyone: temples, and theatres, and colonnades, and fountains.

[long-pause]

The climate is hot and dry, in summer, with clear blue skies, day after day, and hardly any rain from May to September. The light is very bright, and very clear, and the shadows are sharp. In the afternoon, the cicadas sing in the olive trees, loud and endless.

[pause]

In winter, it is cooler, with rain, and wind, and sometimes, for a few days, snow on the mountains, and even in the city.

[pause]

{img:olive-grove-cicadas-midday-heat-light-shade-stone-wall}
The Athenians love their city, and their land. The great playwright Sophocles, who is about sixty-five in 432, will write a famous song, in one of his plays, about the beauty of Attica: about the white town of Colonus, near Athens, where he was born, where the nightingale sings in the green glades, among the ivy, and the narcissus and the crocus bloom, and the river never runs dry, and the olive trees grow, grey-green, which no enemy has ever been able to destroy.

[long-pause]

{img:greek-house-courtyard-altar-rooms-around-well-tiled-roof}
Let us step inside an ordinary Athenian house, in 432 before Christ.

[pause]

From the street, there is not much to see: a plain wall, of mud brick, whitewashed, with a few small, high windows, and a wooden door. Beside the door, there is the herm, the little stone pillar of the god Hermes, with a few flowers laid in front of it. The house turns its back on the street, and looks inward.

[long-pause]

Through the door, there is a short passage, and then the heart of the house: a courtyard, open to the sky, paved with stones, or beaten earth. In the middle, there is an altar, to Zeus, the protector of the household, and perhaps a well, or a cistern, which collects the rain from the roofs. There may be a vine, growing over a trellis, for shade, or a fig tree, or a few pots of herbs and flowers.

[pause]

All around the courtyard, there are the rooms of the house, opening onto it: the kitchen, with its hearth, and its clay oven. The storerooms, with great jars of oil, and wine, and grain. A workroom, where the weaving is done, on the great upright loom. The bedrooms. And the andron, the men's dining room, near the street door, where the husband entertains his friends.

[pause]

{img:greek-kitchen-hearth-clay-pots-oven-woman-cooking-light}
Upstairs, in many houses, there is a second floor, reached by a wooden ladder, or stairs, with more rooms, where the women and children often live and sleep, away from visitors.

[long-pause]

The rooms are small, and simply furnished. There are wooden couches, which are beds at night, and seats in the day. Wooden chairs and stools. Low tables. Wooden chests, for clothes and blankets. Baskets, for wool. Pottery of every kind: jars for water, and oil, and wine, and cups, and bowls, and plates, and lamps.

[pause]

The walls are plastered, and sometimes painted, in plain colours, red, or white. The floors are of beaten earth, or in better houses, of pebbles, set in mortar, in patterns. There is no glass in the windows, only wooden shutters. In winter, the house is cold, and heated only by little charcoal braziers, carried from room to room.

[pause]

{img:greek-bedroom-wooden-couch-blankets-chest-lamp-shutters}
At night, the house is lit by little clay lamps, filled with olive oil, with a wick, which give a soft, small, flickering light. People go to bed early, and get up with the sun.

[long-pause]

It is a simple house. But it is home: the family, and its slaves, and its animals, a dog, perhaps, and a few hens, and a cat, which the Greeks are just beginning to keep as pets, living together, around the courtyard, under the bright Greek sky.

[long-pause]

{img:pericles-bust-helmet-bearded-calm-statesman-portrait}
The leading man of Athens, in 432 before Christ, is Pericles.

[pause]

He is about sixty-three years old. He is not a king, or a tyrant. He holds no special office, except that every year, the people elect him as one of the ten generals of the city, the strategoi, who lead the army and the navy. He has been elected general almost every year, for fifteen years. And he leads the city by persuading the people, in the assembly, with his speeches.

[long-pause]

Pericles comes from one of the oldest and noblest families of Athens. His mother, Agariste, was the niece of Cleisthenes, the man who founded the democracy of Athens, about seventy-five years before.

[pause]

The story goes that, before Pericles was born, his mother dreamed that she gave birth to a lion. He was a serious, clever child, and was taught by the best teachers in Athens: musicians, and philosophers. One of them, Anaxagoras, a philosopher from Ionia, taught him to think about the nature of the world, and the stars, and not to be afraid of omens, or eclipses, because they had natural causes.

[pause]

{img:philosopher-teaching-young-noble-courtyard-stars-diagram-sand}
Pericles is tall, and handsome, and dignified, with a calm face, and a quiet voice. But his head is unusually long, and high, which the comic poets make fun of, calling him onion head, or squill head. That is why, in his statues, he is always shown wearing a helmet, pushed back on his head, to hide its shape.

[long-pause]

He is famous for his calm. Plutarch tells a story that, once, a man followed him all day, through the market place, shouting insults at him, and Pericles took no notice, and went on with his business. And in the evening, when he went home, the man followed him to his door, still shouting. And Pericles, without a word, told his servant to take a torch, and light the man home, because it was dark.

[pause]

He is famous for his honesty, too. He does not take bribes, or make himself rich from his power. He lives simply, and carefully. He hardly ever goes to parties, or dinners. The only time he went to a wedding feast, people say, he left before the drinking began.

[pause]

And he is famous, above all, as a speaker. The comic poet Eupolis wrote that persuasion sat on his lips, and that he was the only speaker who left his sting in his listeners. They called him the Olympian, because when he spoke, it was like thunder and lightning, like Zeus on Mount Olympus.

[long-pause]

Under Pericles, Athens has become rich, and powerful, and beautiful.

[pause]

He has persuaded the people to spend the money of the alliance, the tribute paid by the allied cities, on rebuilding the temples of the Acropolis, which were burned by the Persians, nearly fifty years before. He has hired the greatest architects, and sculptors, and craftsmen, and paid thousands of workers, to build the Parthenon, and the great gateway of the Acropolis, and other temples, and buildings, all over Athens.

[pause]

{img:workers-carrying-marble-blocks-acropolis-ramp-ox-carts}
Some people in Athens criticize him. They say that he is using the money of the allies, which was meant for defence against Persia, to decorate Athens, like a vain woman, with precious stones and statues and temples. But Pericles says that as long as Athens protects the allies, it may spend the money as it likes. And that the buildings give work, and wages, to thousands of ordinary Athenians.

[long-pause]

Pericles also pays the citizens who serve on juries, and in the council, a small daily wage, so that poor men can take part in the government, and not only the rich. This is one of the most important things he has done for the democracy.

[pause]

And he has a great dream, for Athens: that it will be, as he says, the school of Greece. A city where people are free, and equal before the law, and where they love beauty, and wisdom, and live generously, and openly, and bravely.

[pause]

{img:pericles-speaking-bema-crowd-listening-pnyx-afternoon}
But in 432, there are dark clouds on the horizon. Sparta, the other great power of Greece, is growing afraid of Athens, and angry. And Pericles knows that war may be coming.

[long-pause]

{img:aspasia-elegant-greek-woman-chiton-himation-courtyard-thoughtful}
In the house of Pericles, there lives a remarkable woman. Her name is Aspasia.

[pause]

She is not from Athens. She comes from Miletus, a great Greek city on the coast of what is now Turkey, famous for its philosophers, and its learning. She is about thirty-eight, in 432. She is clever, and well educated, and witty, and beautiful, and she speaks well, better than most men, people say.

[long-pause]

Pericles divorced his first wife, many years before, and has been living with Aspasia for about twelve years. They cannot marry, under the law of Athens, because she is not an Athenian citizen. But they love each other, and live together, as husband and wife. Plutarch says that Pericles kissed her every morning, when he left the house, and every evening, when he came home, which the Athenians found very surprising.

[pause]

They have a son, also called Pericles.

[pause]

{img:greek-house-doorway-morning-man-leaving-woman-farewell}
Aspasia is unusual, in Athens. Respectable Athenian women, in 432, live very quiet lives, mostly at home, and do not take part in public life, or talk with men who are not their relatives. But Aspasia talks with the most famous men of Athens, philosophers, and politicians, and artists, who come to the house of Pericles. Socrates himself, it is said, came to talk with her, and admired her, and said that she had taught him about rhetoric, and about love.

[long-pause]

The comic poets make cruel jokes about her, in their plays, and call her names. Her enemies say that she controls Pericles, and makes him go to war. Some of them even take her to court, accusing her of not respecting the gods. Pericles defends her himself, in court, and weeps, it is said, the only time anyone ever saw him weep, and she is acquitted.

[pause]

We do not know very much about her for certain. None of her own words have survived. But she is remembered, more than two thousand years later, as one of the most remarkable women of the ancient world.

[pause]

What is life like for other women in Athens?

[long-pause]

An Athenian girl, of a citizen family, is brought up at home, by her mother, and the household slaves. She learns to spin, and weave, and cook, and run a household. Some learn to read, a little. She is married young, at fourteen or fifteen, to a husband chosen by her father, often a man of thirty.

[pause]

After marriage, she runs her husband's household: the slaves, and the stores, and the cooking, and the spinning and weaving of the family's clothes, and the care of the children. She spends most of her time at home, in the women's rooms, at the back, or upstairs. When her husband's friends come to dinner, she does not join them.

[pause]

{img:greek-women-fountain-house-water-jars-chatting-morning}
But women do go out. They visit their relatives, and their friends. They go to the fountain houses, to fetch water, and talk. They go to funerals, and weddings. And above all, they take part in the great religious festivals of the city: the processions, and the sacrifices, and the dances, and the women's festivals, like the Thesmophoria, in honour of the goddess Demeter, from which men are excluded.

[long-pause]

And poorer women, of course, must work: in the market, selling bread, and vegetables, and ribbons, and perfume. As nurses, and midwives, and washerwomen, and wool workers. Many of them are widows, or foreigners, or freed slaves.

[pause]

The great playwright Euripides, in one of his plays, Medea, which will be performed the very next year, in 431, gives a woman a famous speech about the hard life of women: that they must buy a husband, with a dowry, and take him as master of their body, and that they would rather stand three times in the battle line than give birth once.

[pause]

{img:woman-sitting-courtyard-evening-oil-lamp-quiet-thought}
The audience, in the theatre of Dionysus, most of them men, will listen in silence. And some of them, perhaps, will think about it, afterwards.

[long-pause]

{img:acropolis-rock-cliffs-propylaea-gateway-parthenon-erechtheion-site}
Let us climb up to the Acropolis, on a summer morning, in the year 432 before Christ.

[pause]

The Acropolis is a great flat-topped rock, rising steeply out of the middle of the city, about a hundred and fifty metres high, with cliffs on every side, except the west. People have lived here, and worshipped here, for thousands of years. In ancient times, it was the fortress of the kings of Athens. Now, it is the home of the gods.

[long-pause]

The only way up is from the west, by a long, zigzag path, a ramp, up the slope, which is used by the great processions.

[pause]

At the top of the ramp, there is the new gateway to the Acropolis, the Propylaea, which is still being built, in 432, of white marble from Mount Pentelikon. It is a great building, like the front of a temple, with tall columns, and a roof of marble, and five doorways, the middle one wide enough for the procession, with its animals for the sacrifice, to pass through.

[pause]

{img:propylaea-marble-columns-steps-workers-scaffolding-sunlight}
To the right of the gateway, on a little bastion of stone, there is a small, delicate temple, which will be built a few years later, to Athena Nike, Athena of Victory.

[long-pause]

And then, as you pass through the gateway, and come out onto the top of the Acropolis, you see it, in front of you, on the highest point of the rock, shining white, and gold, and coloured, in the sunshine: the Parthenon, the temple of Athena Parthenos, Athena the Maiden.

[pause]

But before we go to the Parthenon, let us look around.

[pause]

In front of us, as we come through the gateway, there is a huge bronze statue of Athena, standing, with a spear, and a shield, and a helmet, nine metres tall, made by the great sculptor Phidias, about twenty years before. It is called Athena Promachos, Athena who fights in front. Sailors, coming into Athens from the sea, can see the gleam of the tip of her spear, and the crest of her helmet, from far out at sea, when the sun shines on them.

[long-pause]

To the left, there is an old temple, of Athena Polias, Athena of the City, the oldest and holiest temple on the Acropolis, which was burned by the Persians, and partly repaired. Inside it, there is an ancient wooden statue of Athena, very old, so old that the Athenians believe it fell from heaven. It is dressed in a robe, which is woven new, every four years, by the women of Athens.

[pause]

Near it, there is a sacred olive tree, which, the Athenians believe, Athena herself gave them, long ago, in a contest with Poseidon, the god of the sea, for the city. Poseidon struck the rock with his trident, and a spring of salt water came out. Athena gave them an olive tree. And the people chose the olive tree, and Athena became their goddess.

[pause]

{img:sacred-olive-tree-acropolis-old-temple-marble-sunlight}
When the Persians burned the Acropolis, the story goes, the sacred olive tree was burned too. But the next day, a new green shoot, a cubit long, had grown from the burned stump. And the Athenians knew that their city would live.

[long-pause]

The top of the Acropolis is crowded with things: altars, and statues, and little shrines, and offerings, given to the gods over hundreds of years. Bronze statues, and marble statues, of gods, and heroes, and athletes, and horsemen, and young women, and animals. Little votive tablets, and pots, and inscriptions, carved in stone, recording the laws, and the decrees of the assembly, and the accounts of the treasurers.

[pause]

It is not a quiet, empty place, like the ruins we see today, but a busy, crowded, colourful one, full of priests, and worshippers, and visitors, and workmen, and the smoke of sacrifices, rising from the altars.

[pause]

{img:acropolis-crowded-statues-altars-smoke-priests-visitors-morning}
And from the edge of the rock, you can look out over the whole of Athens, spread out below: the red-tiled roofs, and the streets, and the market place, and the theatre, and the hills around, and the plain, with its olive groves, and the long walls, running down to the sea, and the shining sea itself, with the island of Salamis, and the mountains beyond.

[long-pause]

{img:parthenon-new-white-marble-painted-sculptures-colours-sunlight-432bc}
In 432 before Christ, the Parthenon is finished at last.

[pause]

It has taken fifteen years to build. The building itself was finished six years before, in 438. But the sculptors have been working on it ever since, on the sculptures that fill the triangular gables at each end, the pediments. And now, in 432, the last of them have been put in place.

[long-pause]

The Parthenon is made almost entirely of marble, white marble, from the quarries of Mount Pentelikon, about sixteen kilometres away, brought down the mountain, on wooden sledges, and in ox carts, to the city, and hauled up the ramp, to the top of the Acropolis. More than twenty thousand tons of marble.

[pause]

It is about seventy metres long, and thirty metres wide. All around it, there is a row of tall columns, forty-six of them, each made of drums of marble, placed one on top of the other, and fluted, with shallow grooves, running up and down. On top of the columns, there is the roof, of wood, covered with marble tiles.

[pause]

{img:column-drums-marble-masons-fluting-chisels-workshop-acropolis}
The architects were Ictinus and Callicrates. And they did something very clever. In the Parthenon, there are almost no straight lines. The floor curves up slightly in the middle. The columns lean slightly inward, and swell slightly in the middle. The corner columns are a little thicker. All these little curves are so small that you cannot see them. But they make the building look perfectly straight, and balanced, and alive, instead of stiff and heavy.

[long-pause]

The marble is not plain white, as it looks today.

[pause]

In 432, the sculptures, and parts of the building, are painted, in bright colours: red, and blue, and green, and gold. The figures in the sculptures have coloured clothes, and painted eyes, and lips, and gilded weapons, and jewellery of bronze. The building glows, in the bright Greek sunlight.

[pause]

On the pediments, there are great groups of statues, larger than life, of the gods. At the east end, over the main door, the birth of Athena, who sprang fully grown, and in armour, from the head of her father, Zeus. At the west end, the contest between Athena and Poseidon, for the land of Athens.

[pause]

Around the top of the outside walls, there are ninety-two square carved panels, called metopes, showing battles: gods against giants, Greeks against Amazons, Greeks against Trojans, and the Lapiths, a people of Greek legend, against the centaurs, half man and half horse.

[long-pause]

And inside the colonnade, high up around the top of the walls of the temple itself, there is a long band of carving, a frieze, a hundred and sixty metres long, showing a great procession: the Panathenaic procession, the great festival of Athena, with horsemen, and chariots, and young men, and old men, and young women, and musicians, and priests, and animals for sacrifice, all moving toward the gods, who sit, at the east end, waiting to receive them.

[pause]

It shows the people of Athens themselves, honouring their goddess. It is perhaps the first time, in Greek art, that ordinary people have been shown, on a temple, beside the gods.

[pause]

{img:frieze-horsemen-procession-marble-relief-young-riders-flowing}
The horses, and the young riders, flowing along, with their cloaks blowing, are some of the most beautiful sculptures ever made.

[long-pause]

And inside the temple, in the great dim hall, there stands the statue of Athena Parthenos, made by the great sculptor Phidias, the friend of Pericles, who was in charge of all the sculptures of the Parthenon.

[pause]

The statue is twelve metres tall, almost reaching the roof. It is made of a wooden frame, covered with ivory, for the skin, and gold, for the clothes, and the helmet, and the shield. More than a ton of gold. She stands, in her helmet, with her shield beside her, and a great snake, and in her right hand, a little figure of Victory, with wings, as tall as a man.

[pause]

{img:chryselephantine-athena-statue-dim-temple-hall-gold-ivory-pool}
In front of her, there is a shallow pool of water, which reflects the light from the door, onto the gold and ivory, and keeps the air moist, so that the ivory does not crack.

[pause]

The Athenians come to look at her, and bow, and pray. The gold of her robe can be taken off, in an emergency, and melted down, to pay for war. Pericles says so himself, in the assembly. Athena will help her city, if it needs her.

[long-pause]

{img:athenian-agora-stoa-colonnade-market-stalls-crowds-morning}
Below the Acropolis, to the north west, there is the heart of everyday life in Athens: the Agora.

[pause]

The Agora is a great open square, the market place, and the meeting place, and the centre of government, and business, and gossip, all at once. Every morning, it is crowded with people: shoppers, and sellers, and politicians, and philosophers, and lawyers, and slaves, and foreigners, and idle men, walking, and talking, and buying, and arguing, in the sunshine.

[long-pause]

Around the edges of the square, there are long buildings, called stoas: covered colonnades, with a row of columns along the front, and a wall at the back, and a roof, where people can walk, and talk, in the shade, out of the sun and the rain.

[pause]

The most famous is the Painted Stoa, the Stoa Poikile, whose back wall is covered with great paintings, by famous painters, of the great battles of Athens: the battle of Marathon, against the Persians, and the war against the Amazons, and the fall of Troy. More than a hundred years later, a philosopher, Zeno, will teach in this stoa, and his followers will be called the Stoics, after it.

[pause]

There is the council house, the Bouleuterion, where the council of five hundred meets, every day, to prepare business for the assembly. There is the round building, the Tholos, where fifty members of the council live, eat, and sleep, day and night, so that someone is always on duty, to deal with any emergency. There are law courts. And temples, and altars, and fountain houses, and statues.

[long-pause]

On a little hill, beside the Agora, there is a new temple, of Hephaestus, the god of fire, and of smiths, and craftsmen, which is being built at the same time as the Parthenon. It still stands today, almost complete, the best preserved Greek temple in the world.

[pause]

Around it, there are the workshops of the smiths, and the bronze workers, whose god Hephaestus is. The sound of their hammers rings out across the Agora.

[pause]

{img:temple-hephaestus-hill-agora-smiths-workshops-smoke}
And in the middle of the Agora, there are the market stalls: wooden tables, and booths, and baskets, and awnings, where everything is sold. Each kind of goods has its own place, its own circle, as the Athenians call it.

[long-pause]

There is the fish market, the most popular of all, because the Athenians love fish, and the fish sellers are famous for being rude, and greedy, and for charging high prices, and refusing to bargain.

[pause]

There are the sellers of bread, and of vegetables, garlic, and onions, and cabbages, and lettuces, and beans, and lentils, and of fruit, figs, and grapes, and pomegranates, and apples, and pears. There are sellers of olive oil, and wine, and honey, and cheese, and eggs, and chickens, and little birds. There are sellers of perfumes, and wreaths of flowers, and ribbons, and pots, and pans, and lamps, and shoes, and cloth, and furniture, and books, written on rolls of papyrus.

[pause]

{img:agora-fish-stall-seller-arguing-customer-baskets-octopus}
There is a slave market, too. Because Athens, like all ancient cities, depends on the work of slaves.

[long-pause]

Slaves are everywhere in Athens. Perhaps one person in three, or one in four, in Attica, is a slave. They are people captured in war, or sold by pirates, or born to slave mothers, from all around the Mediterranean and the Black Sea: Thracians, and Scythians, and Syrians, and Carians, and many others.

[pause]

Some work in the houses of the rich, as cooks, and maids, and nurses, and tutors. Some work in workshops, beside their masters, making pots, and shoes, and furniture. Some work in the fields. Some, the most unlucky, work in the silver mines of Laurion, in terrible conditions. And some belong to the city itself, and work as clerks, and policemen: the city's police are Scythian archers, slaves, owned by the state, who keep order in the assembly, and the streets.

[pause]

Life for a slave in Athens is often hard. But many are treated as members of the household, and some are set free, by their masters. And a visitor from another city once complained that in Athens, you cannot tell a slave from a free man, in the street, because they dress the same, and slaves are not afraid to speak their minds.

[long-pause]

And the Agora is where people come to talk.

[pause]

The Athenians love talking, more than anything. They talk about politics, and the latest news, and the decisions of the assembly, and the trials in the courts, and the new plays at the theatre, and the price of fish, and the gods, and the meaning of life. They stand in groups, in the shade of the stoas, or sit on the steps of the temples, and argue, for hours.

[pause]

{img:men-talking-in-groups-stoa-steps-afternoon-shade-gestures}
And among them, there is a short, round, ugly man, with a snub nose, and bulging eyes, and a beard, who walks barefoot, and wears the same old cloak, summer and winter, and asks everyone questions. His name is Socrates.

[long-pause]

{img:athens-dawn-rooftops-acropolis-pink-light-roosters}
Let us spend an ordinary day in Athens, in the summer of 432 before Christ, with an ordinary Athenian family.

[pause]

The father is a citizen, a potter, perhaps, with a little workshop, in the potters' quarter, the Kerameikos, near the north west gate of the city. He has a wife, and three children, and two slaves, who help in the workshop and the house.

[long-pause]

The day begins at dawn. The Athenians get up early, at first light, to make the most of the cool of the morning, before the heat of the day.

[pause]

The father washes his face and hands, in cold water, from a jar. He does not shave: Athenian men wear beards. He puts on his tunic, a chiton, a simple garment of wool, or linen, made from a rectangle of cloth, pinned at the shoulders, and belted at the waist. And over it, if it is cool, a cloak, a himation, a large rectangle of wool, wrapped around the body, and over the left shoulder.

[pause]

{img:greek-man-wrapping-himation-cloak-courtyard-morning-light}
Breakfast is simple: a piece of barley bread, dipped in wine, mixed with water. Perhaps a few figs, or olives. And then he goes to work.

[long-pause]

In the workshop, the potters are already busy.

[pause]

Athens is famous for its pottery, all over the Mediterranean. Athenian pots are made of fine red clay, from the banks of the river, shaped on a potter's wheel, turned by hand, by a slave, or a boy. They are painted with a special black glaze, made from very fine clay. And when they are fired, in the kiln, the clay turns red, and the glaze turns black, shiny, and smooth.

[pause]

In 432, the painters use the red-figure style: they paint the background black, and leave the figures red, the colour of the clay, and draw in the details with fine black lines, with a brush, sometimes made of a single hair. The pictures show the gods, and heroes, and scenes from Homer, and athletes, and warriors, and women at the fountain, and young men at parties, and ordinary life. Many of the pots are signed by the potter and the painter. And they are sold, all over the Greek world, and beyond, to Italy, and the Black Sea, and Egypt.

[long-pause]

The wife, at home, is running the household.

[pause]

She gives orders to the slave woman, about the cleaning, and the cooking, and the water, which must be fetched from the fountain, in a jar, carried on the head. She spins wool, with a spindle, and weaves cloth, on a big upright loom, in the courtyard, or in the women's room: cloth for the family's clothes, and blankets, and cushions.

[pause]

{img:woman-upright-loom-weaving-wool-courtyard-daughter-spinning}
The little children play in the courtyard, with toys: dolls, of clay, with jointed arms and legs, and little carts, and spinning tops, and hoops, and balls, and knucklebones, and pet birds, and dogs, and tortoises. The older son goes to school.

[long-pause]

At midday, the family eats a light meal. And in the heat of the early afternoon, everyone rests, in the shade, and the streets are empty, and quiet, except for the cicadas.

[pause]

In the afternoon, the father finishes his work, and goes to the gymnasium, to exercise, and to bathe, and to talk with his friends. Or to the Agora, to hear the news. Or, if it is a day of the assembly, to the Pnyx, to vote.

[pause]

{img:athens-street-afternoon-quiet-heat-shadows-cat-doorway}
In the evening, as the sun goes down, and the air grows cooler, the family eats its main meal together: barley porridge, or bread, and vegetables, and beans, and lentils, and cheese, and olives, and figs, and perhaps a little fish, on good days. Meat is rare, eaten mostly at festivals, after a sacrifice.

[long-pause]

And then, as darkness falls, the little clay oil lamps are lit, and the family sits together, in the courtyard, under the stars, in the warm summer night. The father tells the children stories, about the gods, and heroes, about Theseus, the hero of Athens, who killed the Minotaur, and about the battle of Marathon, where their grandfather fought.

[pause]

And then they go to bed, on simple wooden beds, with mattresses stuffed with wool, and blankets. Some, in the hottest weather, sleep on the flat roof, under the stars. And Athens is quiet, under the moon, with the great white temple on the Acropolis, glowing faintly, above the sleeping city.

[long-pause]

{img:greek-woman-peplos-chiton-pinned-shoulders-himation-veil-courtyard}
What do the Athenians wear, in 432 before Christ?

[pause]

Their clothes are very simple. Almost every garment is just a large rectangle of cloth, woven at home, on the family loom, of wool, or linen, and draped around the body, and held with pins, or brooches, and a belt. There is very little sewing, and no buttons.

[long-pause]

Men wear a chiton, a tunic, made of two rectangles of cloth, sewn at the sides, and pinned at the shoulders, and belted at the waist. Workmen, and slaves, and soldiers, wear a short chiton, to the knees, sometimes leaving one shoulder bare, to be free to move. Older men, and rich men, wear a long chiton, to the ankles.

[pause]

Over it, a himation, a large cloak, of wool, wrapped around the body, over the left shoulder, and under the right arm. Wearing a himation gracefully, so that it hangs in elegant folds, is a sign of a gentleman. Young horsemen wear a short cloak, called a chlamys, pinned at the right shoulder.

[pause]

Women wear a long chiton, of linen, fine and light, pleated, falling to the feet, or a peplos, of wool, heavier, folded over at the top, and pinned at the shoulders. And over it, a himation, which they can pull over their heads, as a veil, when they go out.

[long-pause]

The clothes are often brightly coloured: saffron yellow, and red, and purple, and green, and blue, with patterned borders, woven in. The finest purple dye, made from sea snails, is very expensive, and worn only by the rich.

[pause]

People go barefoot at home, and often in the street. Or they wear sandals, of leather, or boots, for travelling, or riding. When they travel, in the sun, they wear a wide-brimmed hat of felt, or straw, called a petasos.

[pause]

{img:sandal-maker-workshop-leather-sandals-hanging-street-front}
Men wear their hair short, in 432, and have beards, neatly trimmed. Young men, before their beards grow, are thought the most beautiful. Women wear their hair long, tied up, in elegant styles, with ribbons, and bands, and nets, and scarves. They use make-up: white lead, to make their skin pale, and red, made from plants, for their cheeks and lips.

[long-pause]

Rich women wear jewellery: earrings, and necklaces, and bracelets, and rings, of gold and silver, beautifully made, with tiny figures, and flowers, and animals. And they carry fans, and parasols, and little bottles of perfume, made of glass, or pottery.

[pause]

Everyone smells of olive oil, and perfume. The Athenians rub perfumed oil on their skin, and hair, after bathing, and before parties. There are perfume shops in the Agora, where people meet to gossip.

[pause]

{img:perfume-shop-agora-small-bottles-oils-customers-chatting}
And for festivals, and parties, people wear wreaths of flowers, and leaves: myrtle, and ivy, and violets, and roses. Athens is called violet-crowned Athens, by the poet Pindar, perhaps because of the purple colour of the hills around it at sunset, or because of the violet wreaths its people wore.

[long-pause]

{img:symposium-men-reclining-couches-wine-krater-lamps-andron}
In the evening, in the houses of richer Athenians, there are dinner parties, called symposia.

[pause]

The word symposion means drinking together. It is a party for men only: the host, and his friends, perhaps seven, or nine, or eleven of them. They meet in a special room in the house, the andron, the men's room, with couches along the walls, and a floor of pebble mosaic.

[long-pause]

The guests arrive, in the evening, and take off their sandals, at the door, and a slave washes their feet. They lie down on the couches, two to a couch, leaning on their left elbows, on cushions. Little tables are set in front of them, with the food.

[pause]

First, they eat: bread, and fish, and vegetables, and cheese, and olives, and perhaps a little meat. They eat with their fingers, and wipe their hands on pieces of bread, and throw the bread to the dogs, under the tables.

[pause]

{img:slave-boy-pouring-wine-from-krater-into-kylix-cups-lamps}
Then the tables are taken away, and the floor is swept, and the drinking begins. The guests put on wreaths of flowers, or ivy, or myrtle, on their heads. They pour a little wine on the floor, as an offering to the gods, and sing a hymn.

[long-pause]

The wine is always mixed with water, in a great bowl, called a krater. The Greeks think that only barbarians drink wine without water. The guests choose a leader, the symposiarch, who decides how strong the wine should be, perhaps three parts water to one part wine, and how many bowls they will drink. And slaves fill the cups, from the krater, with a ladle.

[pause]

The cups are wide, shallow bowls, with two handles, called kylikes, often beautifully painted inside, so that as you drink, a picture appears at the bottom: a dolphin, or a satyr, or a young man, or a gorgon's face.

[pause]

And then the guests talk. They talk about politics, and philosophy, and love, and poetry. They tell jokes, and riddles. They take turns to sing songs, with a branch of myrtle, passed from hand to hand, from one singer to the next. They recite poems, from Homer, and other poets.

[long-pause]

There is entertainment too. A girl plays the double pipes, the aulos. There are dancers, and acrobats, and jugglers. And the guests play games.

[pause]

The favourite game is kottabos. Each guest, lying on his couch, holds his cup by one handle, and flicks the last drops of wine at a target: a little bronze disc, balanced on top of a tall stand, or little saucers floating in a bowl of water. If he knocks the disc down, with a clang, or sinks a saucer, he wins. And as he throws, he says the name of the person he loves. If he hits the target, it is a good sign.

[pause]

{img:kottabos-game-wine-drops-flicked-bronze-stand-laughter}
The party can go on late into the night. Sometimes, it becomes noisy, and drunken, and at the end, the guests stagger out into the streets, with torches, singing, and knock on the doors of their friends, and wake up the neighbours.

[long-pause]

But the best symposia are about conversation.

[pause]

The philosopher Plato, who is not yet born in 432, will later write a famous book, called the Symposium, about a dinner party in Athens, a few years later, at which Socrates, and the comic poet Aristophanes, and other famous men, decide not to drink too much, but to take turns to make speeches about love. Each one gives a different speech. And at the end, Socrates gives the most beautiful of all, about how love of beauty can lead the soul upward, from the love of one beautiful person, to the love of beauty itself, and of wisdom, and of the good.

[pause]

{img:dawn-light-symposium-room-guests-asleep-one-man-leaving}
And at the end of the book, as dawn comes, all the guests have fallen asleep on their couches, except Socrates, who gets up, and walks out into the morning, and goes to the Lyceum, to wash, and spends the day as usual, talking with anyone he meets. And in the evening, he goes home to bed.

[long-pause]

{img:greek-schoolroom-boys-wax-tablets-teacher-lyre-scrolls}
At about seven years old, the son of an Athenian citizen goes to school.

[pause]

There are no public schools, paid for by the city. Parents pay small fees to private teachers, who keep little schools, in rooms, or in the open air. Rich families send their sons for many years. Poor families, only for a few, or not at all. Girls, mostly, do not go to school, but learn at home.

[long-pause]

Every morning, the boy walks to school, accompanied by a slave, called a paidagogos, a leader of the boy, an older man, who carries his things, and looks after him, and makes sure he behaves, and sits with him in the lessons. Our word pedagogy, teaching, comes from his name.

[pause]

The boy has three teachers.

[pause]

{img:paidagogos-old-slave-walking-boy-to-school-street-morning}
The grammatistes teaches him to read and write, and to count. The boy writes on a wooden tablet, covered with wax, with a stylus, a pointed stick of bone, or metal, which has a flat end, for rubbing out. He learns his letters, and then words, and then whole passages of poetry, especially Homer, which he learns by heart, long passages, of hundreds of lines. Every educated Athenian knows much of Homer by heart.

[long-pause]

The kitharistes teaches him music. He learns to play the lyre, a stringed instrument, made with the shell of a tortoise, and the horns of a goat, and to sing, and to recite poetry to music. The Greeks believe that music shapes the character, and makes the soul gentle, and harmonious.

[pause]

And the paidotribes teaches him physical exercise, at the palaestra, the wrestling school: wrestling, and running, and jumping, and throwing the discus, and the javelin, and boxing.

[pause]

The boys exercise naked, as Greek athletes always do, their bodies rubbed with olive oil, which they scrape off afterwards, with a curved metal tool, called a strigil. The Greeks believe that a beautiful, healthy, strong body is as important as a good mind.

[long-pause]

When they are older, young men, and grown men, go to the gymnasium.

[pause]

There are three great gymnasia in Athens, outside the city walls, in pleasant places, with trees, and streams, and gardens: the Academy, the Lyceum, and the Cynosarges. They are large open spaces, with running tracks, and wrestling grounds, and baths, and shady walks, under plane trees and olive trees, and colonnades, and altars.

[pause]

{img:academy-gymnasium-plane-trees-olive-grove-running-track-shade}
The men go there in the afternoon, to exercise, and bathe, and talk. And the philosophers go there too, to talk with the young men, and teach them. A few years later, Plato will found his famous school, at the Academy, which will give its name to all the academies of the world. And later still, Aristotle will teach at the Lyceum.

[long-pause]

At eighteen, a young Athenian becomes an ephebe. For two years, he trains as a soldier, and guards the frontiers of Attica, in the forts on the mountains. And then he becomes a full citizen, and may go to the assembly, and vote, and serve in the army, as a hoplite, a heavily armed foot soldier, with a bronze helmet, and a great round shield, and a spear, or, if he is poor, as a rower, in the navy.

[pause]

{img:young-men-oath-temple-ephebes-shields-spears-morning-solemn}
The young men take an oath, in the temple of Aglauros, on the slope of the Acropolis: I will not disgrace these sacred arms, or desert my comrade in the battle line. I will defend the sacred and holy things. I will leave my fatherland not smaller, but greater, and better, than I received it.

[long-pause]

{img:socrates-snub-nose-beard-barefoot-cloak-agora-talking-young-men}
In 432 before Christ, Socrates is about thirty-seven years old.

[pause]

He is the son of a stonemason, Sophroniscus, and a midwife, Phaenarete. He was trained as a stonemason himself, as a young man, and some people said that he carved a statue of the Graces, which stood at the entrance to the Acropolis. But he has given up his trade, and spends his days, every day, talking.

[long-pause]

He is very ugly, by the standards of the Greeks, who love beauty. He has a snub nose, and thick lips, and bulging eyes, and a big belly, and he walks with a strange, rolling walk, like a pelican, his friends say, looking from side to side. He walks barefoot, summer and winter, and wears the same old, worn cloak, always. He is poor, and does not care.

[pause]

But he has something that draws people to him, like a magnet. His friends say that he is like those little statues of Silenus, ugly satyrs, which are sold in the market, and which open up, and inside, there are little golden figures of the gods.

[pause]

Socrates goes to the Agora, and the gymnasia, and the workshops, and the parties, and talks to everyone: politicians, and poets, and craftsmen, and generals, and young men, and old men, rich and poor. And he asks them questions.

[long-pause]

He asks them about things they think they know. What is courage? What is justice? What is beauty? What is a good life? What is friendship? What is virtue, and can it be taught?

[pause]

And when they answer, he asks them more questions, gently, politely, about their answers. And slowly, step by step, he shows them that they do not really know what they thought they knew. That their answers are confused, or contradict each other. And then he says: I do not know either. Let us look for the answer together.

[pause]

{img:philosopher-walking-with-young-men-path-plane-trees-river}
This is called the Socratic method. It is still used today, by teachers, all over the world.

[long-pause]

Socrates says that he knows only one thing: that he knows nothing. And that, perhaps, this makes him a little wiser than other people, who think they know things that they do not.

[pause]

He says that the most important thing in life is not money, or fame, or power, but to take care of your soul, to try to become a good person, and to understand what goodness is. And he says: the unexamined life is not worth living.

[pause]

{img:socrates-standing-still-thinking-dawn-soldiers-camp-watching}
Socrates is also a brave soldier. He has fought for Athens, as a hoplite. And very soon, in 432, he will go to war, at Potidaea, in the north of Greece. There, his friends will tell later, he walked barefoot over the ice in the bitter winter, and once stood still, thinking about a problem, from one dawn to the next, while the other soldiers watched him in wonder. And in the battle, he saved the life of a young man, Alcibiades.

[long-pause]

Socrates never writes anything down. He believes that wisdom is found in conversation, between living people, not in books. Everything we know about him comes from his friends and his students, who wrote about him after his death: above all Plato, and Xenophon.

[pause]

He has a wife, Xanthippe, who, the stories say, is quick-tempered, and scolds him, because he does not earn any money, and is always out, talking. Socrates says, jokingly, that if he can live with her, he can get along with anyone.

[pause]

Many young men of Athens love him, and follow him around, and listen to him, and try to argue with him. Their fathers, sometimes, are less pleased. Because Socrates teaches the young to question everything, even the laws, and the gods, and the customs of their city.

[long-pause]

And that, in the end, will cost him his life. More than thirty years later, in 399, when he is seventy, the Athenians will put him on trial, for not believing in the gods of the city, and for corrupting the young. And the jury will condemn him.

[pause]

He could escape, but he refuses, because he respects the laws of Athens. And he spends his last day talking with his friends, calmly, about the soul, and what happens after death, and then drinks the cup of hemlock, and passes away, peacefully, among his friends.

[pause]

{img:quiet-evening-prison-room-friends-gathered-calm-light}
But in 432, all that is far in the future. He is a man in the prime of life, walking barefoot through the Agora, in the morning sunshine, looking for someone to talk to.

[long-pause]

{img:theatre-of-dionysus-hillside-seats-acropolis-slope-audience}
On the south slope of the Acropolis, there is the theatre of Dionysus, the god of wine, and of the theatre.

[pause]

It is an open-air theatre, built into the hillside. The audience sits on the slope, on wooden benches, rising up the hill, in a great half circle. Below them, there is a round, flat dancing floor, called the orchestra, where the chorus sings and dances. And behind it, a low building, the skene, the stage building, where the actors change their costumes, and which is painted, to look like a palace, or a temple, or a house.

[long-pause]

The theatre can hold perhaps fifteen thousand people, or more: almost all the citizens of Athens.

[pause]

Plays are not performed all year round, as they are today. They are performed at festivals, in honour of Dionysus. The greatest is the City Dionysia, in the spring, in March, when the sea is calm again, and visitors come to Athens from all over the Greek world.

[pause]

{img:procession-statue-dionysus-city-dionysia-spring-crowds-ivy}
For five days, there is a festival: processions, and sacrifices, and choruses of men and boys singing, and then, for three days, from dawn to dusk, plays: three tragedies, and a satyr play, by one playwright, each day, and comedies. It is a competition. At the end, judges choose the best playwright, and the best actor, and they receive a prize, an ivy crown, and great honour.

[long-pause]

The tragedies tell the old stories of the gods and heroes: of the war at Troy, and the house of Agamemnon, and Oedipus, the king of Thebes, and Medea, and Antigone, and many others. They are serious, and sad, and full of great poetry, and deep questions: about fate, and justice, and the gods, and suffering, and how people should live, and what happens when they make terrible mistakes.

[pause]

The three great tragic poets of Athens are alive in these years. Aeschylus, the oldest, passed away twenty-four years before, in 456. Sophocles is about sixty-five, and still writing. In these very years, perhaps, he is writing, or has just written, his most famous play, Oedipus the King. And Euripides, about fifty, is writing plays that question everything, and are sometimes shocking to the Athenians.

[pause]

The actors are all men, even those who play women. There are only three actors, in each play, and each one plays several parts, by changing masks. The masks are made of linen, and wood, and plaster, painted, with large open mouths, so that the voices carry, and so that everyone, even at the back, can see who is who. The actors wear long robes, and boots with thick soles, to make them look taller.

[long-pause]

The chorus, twelve or fifteen men, sing, and dance, in the orchestra, between the scenes, and comment on what is happening, and give advice, and pray, and mourn. The music is played on the aulos, the double pipes.

[pause]

There are no women in the plays. And historians still argue whether women were allowed to come and watch. Perhaps some did.

[pause]

{img:comic-actors-padded-costumes-funny-masks-audience-laughing}
And then there are the comedies: rude, and silly, and wild, and very funny, full of jokes about politics, and the famous men of Athens, Pericles, and Socrates, and the generals, and the poets, who are often sitting in the audience, being laughed at. In a few years, the greatest comic poet, Aristophanes, will begin writing his plays. In one of them, The Clouds, he will show Socrates hanging in a basket in the air, studying the clouds, and the audience will laugh, and Socrates will stand up in the theatre, it is said, so that the audience can see how like his mask he is.

[long-pause]

Going to the theatre is part of the religion of Athens, and part of being a citizen. Poor citizens are given money, by the city, to pay for their seats.

[pause]

The audience brings cushions, and food, and wine, and spends the whole day in the theatre, in the spring sunshine, watching play after play, and shouting, and cheering, and weeping, and laughing, and throwing nuts and figs at the actors, if they do not like them.

[pause]

{img:sunset-theatre-empty-seats-acropolis-above-golden-quiet}
And behind the stage, the audience can see the plain of Attica, and the mountains, and the sea, in the distance. So that the old stories of gods and heroes are played out, against the real landscape of Greece, under the open sky.

[long-pause]

{img:pnyx-assembly-thousands-citizens-hillside-speaker-bema-dawn}
About forty times a year, early in the morning, the citizens of Athens climb up a hill, west of the Acropolis, called the Pnyx, for a meeting of the assembly, the ekklesia.

[pause]

The Pnyx is a great sloping hillside, with a platform of stone at the bottom, called the bema, where the speakers stand. The citizens sit on the slope, on the ground, or on cushions they have brought, facing the speakers. From the top of the hill, they can see the Acropolis, and the Parthenon, shining in the morning sun.

[long-pause]

Any male citizen over eighteen may come, and speak, and vote. About six thousand usually come. The meeting begins at dawn, with a prayer, and a sacrifice of a little pig, and goes on until the business is finished, usually by midday.

[pause]

Scythian archers, the city police, go around the Agora, before the meeting, with a rope dipped in red paint, and herd the citizens toward the Pnyx. Anyone caught with red paint on his clothes, who has not gone to the assembly, has to pay a fine.

[pause]

{img:scythian-archers-red-rope-herding-citizens-agora-morning}
The herald calls out: who wishes to speak? And anyone may stand up, go to the bema, put a wreath of myrtle on his head, as a sign that he is speaking, and address the people. Usually, the speakers are the politicians, the experienced men, like Pericles. But anyone may speak. If a speaker is boring, or foolish, the crowd shouts him down, and laughs, and the archers lead him away.

[long-pause]

The assembly decides everything: whether to go to war, or make peace. Which laws to make. How much tax to raise. Which buildings to build. Who will be sent as ambassadors. What to do with allies who rebel.

[pause]

They vote by raising their hands. The officials look at the hillside, and judge which side has more hands. On some important matters, they vote with pebbles, or little bronze discs, dropped into urns.

[pause]

The business of the assembly is prepared by the council, the boule, of five hundred citizens, fifty from each of the ten tribes of Athens, chosen by lot, for one year. Chosen by lot: not elected, but drawn by chance, like a lottery. The Athenians believe that this is the most democratic way, because it gives every citizen an equal chance. Most officials of the city are chosen by lot too. Only the generals, and a few others, who need special skills, are elected.

[long-pause]

There are law courts too, with juries, huge juries, of two hundred, or five hundred, or even a thousand, or more citizens, chosen by lot, every morning, from those who have volunteered. There are no professional judges, and no lawyers. Each side speaks for itself, for a fixed time, measured by a water clock: a pot with a hole in it, through which the water runs out. Then the jury votes, at once, without discussion, by dropping little bronze ballots into urns.

[pause]

To choose the jurors, the Athenians use a machine, called a kleroterion: a slab of stone, with rows of slots, into which the citizens put their little bronze identity tickets, and a tube, down which black and white balls are dropped, at random, to choose which rows will serve. Some of these machines have been found, by archaeologists, in the Agora.

[pause]

{img:kleroterion-stone-slots-bronze-tickets-allotment-machine-citizens}
And there is ostracism. Once a year, the assembly may decide to hold an ostracism, to send one man into exile, for ten years, if he is becoming too powerful, and might become a tyrant. Each citizen scratches the name of the man he wants to send away, on a broken piece of pottery, called an ostrakon. If more than six thousand votes are cast, the man with the most votes must leave Athens, for ten years. He keeps his property, and can come back afterwards.

[long-pause]

Archaeologists have found thousands of these ostraka, in the Agora, with names scratched on them: Themistocles, and Aristides, and Cimon, and Pericles himself, although he was never ostracized. Some of them, it seems, were written in advance, by the same hand, for voters who could not write.

[pause]

There is a famous story about Aristides, called the Just, because he was so honest. At an ostracism, an illiterate farmer, who did not recognize him, asked him to write a name on his ostrakon for him: Aristides. Aristides asked him what harm Aristides had ever done to him. None, said the farmer. I do not even know him. But I am tired of hearing everybody call him the Just. And Aristides wrote his own name on the potsherd, and gave it back.

[pause]

{img:broken-pottery-sherds-names-scratched-ostraka-museum-light}
The democracy of Athens has many faults. It excludes women, and slaves, and foreigners. Sometimes the assembly makes rash, cruel, or foolish decisions, swayed by clever speakers. But it is the first great experiment in government by ordinary people, and it has inspired people who believe in freedom, all over the world, ever since.

[long-pause]

{img:panathenaic-procession-sacred-way-young-women-baskets-horsemen-dawn}
The greatest festival of Athens is the Panathenaia, the festival of all the Athenians, in honour of Athena, on her birthday, in the middle of summer, in July or August.

[pause]

Every year, there is a small festival. And every four years, there is the Great Panathenaia, which lasts for about a week, with games, and contests, and music, and sacrifices, and a great procession.

[long-pause]

The games are like the Olympic Games, but with even more events. There are races, on foot, and on horseback, and in chariots. There is wrestling, and boxing, and the pankration, a kind of all-in wrestling. There are contests of music: singing to the lyre, and playing the pipes. And contests of reciting Homer, by professional reciters, called rhapsodes, who compete to recite the Iliad and the Odyssey most beautifully.

[pause]

There are boat races, in the harbour. There is a torch race, at night, a relay, from an altar outside the city, to the altar of Athena on the Acropolis, with the runners carrying burning torches, passing them from hand to hand, trying to keep the flame alight.

[pause]

{img:torch-relay-race-night-runners-flames-crowds-cheering}
And there are contests for the tribes: a beauty contest for men, called the euandria, the contest of manliness, and a dance in armour, called the pyrrhic.

[long-pause]

The prizes for the athletes are wonderful: great jars, called Panathenaic amphoras, filled with olive oil, from the sacred olive trees of Athena. Hundreds of jars, each holding about forty litres. A winner of the chariot race might win a hundred and forty jars of oil, a fortune, which he could sell.

[pause]

The jars are painted, on one side, with a picture of Athena, striding forward, with her spear and her shield, and on the other side, with a picture of the event that was won: runners, or wrestlers, or chariots. And with the words: one of the prizes from Athens. Many of these jars still exist, in museums, all over the world.

[pause]

And on the last great day of the festival, the twenty-eighth day of the month, Athena's birthday, there is the great procession.

[long-pause]

At dawn, the procession gathers at the Dipylon Gate, the double gate, in the north west of the city.

[pause]

And then it moves off, along the Panathenaic Way, a broad road, which crosses the Agora, and climbs up to the Acropolis. Everyone is there. Priests, and priestesses. Magistrates. Young women of noble families, carrying baskets on their heads, with the sacred barley, and the knives for the sacrifice. Old men, carrying olive branches. Young men, leading the cattle and the sheep for the sacrifice. Musicians, playing pipes, and lyres. Horsemen, the young knights of Athens, riding their fine horses. Chariots. And the allies of Athens, and the foreigners who live in Athens, carrying offerings.

[pause]

{img:new-peplos-robe-carried-like-sail-ship-on-wheels-procession}
And in the middle of the procession, there is the most important thing of all: a new robe for Athena, the peplos, woven by the women and girls of Athens, during the past nine months, of saffron yellow wool, with pictures woven into it, of the battle of the gods and giants. At the Great Panathenaia, the robe is so large that it is hung, like a sail, from the mast of a ship, on wheels, which is pulled along the road.

[long-pause]

The procession climbs up to the Acropolis, through the gateway, and the robe is given to the priestess of Athena, to dress the ancient wooden statue of the goddess, in her old temple.

[pause]

And then there is the great sacrifice: a hundred cattle, or more, at the great altar of Athena, on the Acropolis. And afterward, the meat is roasted, and shared out among all the people of Athens, in the Kerameikos, in a great feast, the biggest meal of the year, for rich and poor alike.

[pause]

{img:great-feast-athenians-sharing-roasted-meat-bread-evening-joy}
It is this procession, perhaps, that the frieze of the Parthenon shows: the horsemen, and the young women, and the old men, and the cattle, and the gods, waiting to receive them. The people of Athens, honouring their goddess, carved in marble, forever.

[long-pause]

{img:piraeus-port-harbours-ship-sheds-triremes-merchant-ships-sun}
Seven kilometres from the city, along the road between the Long Walls, there is the port of Athens: the Piraeus.

[pause]

It is a busy, noisy, crowded town, built around three natural harbours, on a rocky peninsula. It was laid out, about twenty years before, by a famous town planner, Hippodamus of Miletus, with straight streets, crossing each other at right angles, in a grid, the first planned town of its kind in Greece.

[long-pause]

The great harbour, Kantharos, is the commercial port, full of merchant ships, from all over the Mediterranean, and the Black Sea. They bring grain, wheat from the Black Sea, from Egypt, and from Sicily, because Attica cannot grow enough to feed all its people. And timber, from Macedonia, for building ships. And slaves, and fish, and hides, and wine, and papyrus, and spices, and ivory, and perfumes, and carpets, from Persia, and Egypt, and Phoenicia.

[pause]

Pericles himself says, in a famous speech, that because of the greatness of Athens, the products of the whole earth flow into the city, so that the Athenians enjoy the goods of other countries as freely as their own.

[pause]

{img:merchants-unloading-grain-amphoras-quay-piraeus-porters}
Along the quays, there are warehouses, and a great market hall, where the merchants show samples of their goods. There are bankers, at their tables, changing money, and lending it. There are taverns, and inns, for the sailors. And people from everywhere: Phoenicians, and Egyptians, and Thracians, and Persians, and Greeks from every city, speaking every language.

[long-pause]

The other two harbours, Zea and Mounichia, are the harbours of the navy.

[pause]

Around them, there are hundreds of ship sheds: long, narrow buildings, with sloping floors, running down into the water, with roofs on columns, where the warships are kept, dry, out of the water, when they are not at sea, so that their wooden hulls do not rot, or get eaten by shipworms. There are about three hundred and seventy ship sheds.

[pause]

The warships are called triremes. They are long, slender, fast ships, about thirty-seven metres long, but only five or six metres wide, made of pine and fir. They have a square sail, for cruising. But in battle, they are rowed, by a hundred and seventy rowers, sitting in three rows, one above the other, on each side, pulling long oars. At the front, at the waterline, there is a great bronze ram, to smash into enemy ships. And on each side of the bow, there is a painted eye, so that the ship can see its way.

[long-pause]

The rowers are not slaves. They are free men, mostly poor citizens of Athens, who are paid a wage, a drachma a day. They row to the music of a piper, who plays a rhythm, so that they all pull together. A trireme, rowed well, can go as fast as a running man, and turn very quickly.

[pause]

Because the navy depends on the poor citizens, the rowers, the poor citizens are very important in Athens, and have great power in the assembly. That is one of the reasons why Athens is a democracy. The rich fight as hoplites, or horsemen. But the poor row the ships that make Athens great.

[pause]

{img:rowers-benches-trireme-piper-rhythm-pulling-oars-together}
Every year, in the sailing season, from spring to autumn, the fleet goes out, to patrol the seas, and protect the merchant ships, and collect the tribute from the allies, and show the power of Athens to the world.

[long-pause]

In 1987, more than two thousand four hundred years later, a group of Greek and British scientists and sailors built a full-size copy of an Athenian trireme, called the Olympias, and rowed it, with a crew of volunteers, on the sea, to see how it worked. They found that it could go very fast, and turn very quickly, just as the ancient writers said. It is now kept in a museum, near the Piraeus.

[pause]

{img:evening-piraeus-harbour-ships-moored-lamps-quiet-water}
And in the evening, in the Piraeus, the sailors come ashore, and go to the taverns, and eat fish, and drink wine, and tell stories of the sea, and the islands, and the far-off ports, while the ships rock gently in the harbour, and the sun sets behind the island of Salamis.

[long-pause]

{img:athenian-silver-tetradrachm-owl-athena-coins-hand-close}
In the markets of Athens, and all over the Mediterranean, people pay with the coins of Athens: little thick silver coins, with the head of Athena on one side, wearing her helmet, and on the other side, an owl, the bird of Athena, with big round eyes, and a little sprig of olive, and the letters ATHE, for the Athenians.

[pause]

People call them owls. A big coin, worth four drachmas, is a tetradrachm. A drachma is about a day's wages for a skilled worker. And there are smaller coins, obols, six to a drachma, so small that people carry them in their mouths, in the market, tucked in their cheeks, because they have no pockets.

[long-pause]

The owls of Athens are the most trusted coins in the Greek world, because their silver is always pure, and their weight is always right. They are used everywhere, from Italy to Egypt to Persia. Archaeologists have found them as far away as Afghanistan. The Greeks have a saying, about doing something useless: bringing owls to Athens, like bringing coals to Newcastle.

[pause]

The silver comes from the mines of Laurion, in the south of Attica, near Cape Sounion.

[pause]

{img:laurion-hills-mine-entrances-washing-tables-ore-cisterns}
The mines are deep tunnels, dug into the hills, narrow, and low, and dark, where thousands of slaves work, by the light of little oil lamps, digging out the silver ore, with picks and hammers, and carrying it up to the surface, in baskets. It is terrible, hard, dangerous work. The ore is crushed, and washed, on great sloping tables, with water from cisterns, and then smelted, in furnaces, to get the silver.

[long-pause]

Fifty years before, in 483, the Athenians found a rich new vein of silver at Laurion. And a wise politician, Themistocles, persuaded them not to share the money out among the citizens, but to build a fleet of two hundred warships with it. Three years later, those ships defeated the great fleet of the Persians, at the battle of Salamis, and saved Greece. And they became the beginning of the great navy, and the empire, and the wealth, of Athens.

[pause]

So the silver of Laurion, dug by slaves in the dark, paid for the Parthenon, and the fleet, and much of the glory of Athens.

[pause]

{img:temple-of-poseidon-sounion-cliff-sea-sunset-columns}
At the tip of Attica, near the mines, on a high cliff above the sea, there is a temple of Poseidon, the god of the sea, built just a few years before, of white marble. Sailors, coming home to Athens, see it from far out at sea, shining on its cliff, and know that they are nearly home.

[pause]

It still stands today, at Cape Sounion. And at sunset, people come from Athens to sit on the rocks, beside its columns, and watch the sun go down into the sea, among the islands. On one of its columns, in 1810, the English poet Lord Byron carved his name.

[long-pause]

{img:olympia-sanctuary-temple-zeus-valley-alpheus-river-pines}
Every four years, in the middle of summer, the Greeks from every city, all over the Greek world, travel to a quiet valley, in the west of the Peloponnese, beside the river Alpheus, among the pine trees and the olive groves, to a place called Olympia.

[pause]

Olympia is not a city. It is a sanctuary, a holy place, of Zeus, the king of the gods. And every four years, there are the Olympic Games, in his honour.

[long-pause]

The Games are very old. The Greeks believe that the first Olympic Games were held in 776 before Christ, more than three hundred years before our story. They count their history in Olympiads, periods of four years, between the Games.

[pause]

Before the Games, heralds travel all over Greece, to announce a sacred truce: all wars between Greek cities must stop, so that the athletes and the spectators can travel safely to Olympia, and home again.

[pause]

{img:herald-announcing-sacred-truce-town-square-crowd-listening}
The last Games were in 436. The next will be in 432, in the summer, in our very year. Thousands of people will come: athletes, and their trainers, and their families, and spectators, and merchants, and poets, and philosophers, and ambassadors. They camp in tents, around the sanctuary, in the summer heat, by the river.

[long-pause]

In the middle of the sanctuary, there is a sacred grove, called the Altis, with temples, and altars, and treasuries, little temples built by the different cities to keep their offerings, and hundreds of statues of victorious athletes.

[pause]

And there is the great temple of Zeus, built about twenty years before. And inside it, there will soon be one of the Seven Wonders of the World: a great statue of Zeus, seated on a throne, more than twelve metres high, made of gold and ivory, by Phidias, the same sculptor who made the Athena of the Parthenon. He is working on it, in these years, in a workshop at Olympia. Archaeologists have found his workshop, with his tools, and moulds for the golden drapery, and even a little cup, with his name scratched on the bottom: I belong to Phidias.

[pause]

The statue of Zeus is so large that, the Greeks said, if he stood up, he would go through the roof.

[long-pause]

The Games last five days. There are races, on foot, in the stadium, a long, narrow running track, two hundred metres long, with grassy banks on each side, where the spectators sit, or stand, in the sun. The most famous race is the stadion, one length of the stadium. The winner of this race gives his name to the whole Olympiad.

[pause]

There are longer races, and a race in armour. There is wrestling, and boxing, and the pankration. There is the pentathlon: running, jumping, discus, javelin, and wrestling. And in the hippodrome, there are horse races, and chariot races, with four-horse chariots, thundering around the track.

[pause]

{img:olympia-stadium-runners-starting-line-crowd-banks-sun}
Only free Greek men may compete. Married women may not even watch, except for the priestess of Demeter, who has a special seat. But unmarried girls have their own games, at Olympia, in honour of Hera, the queen of the gods, with races for girls.

[long-pause]

The prize for an Olympic victory is a simple crown of olive leaves, cut with a golden knife, from the sacred olive tree, behind the temple of Zeus.

[pause]

But the glory is enormous. The winner's name is announced to all of Greece. When he comes home, his city gives him a hero's welcome, and sometimes pulls down part of the city wall, so that he can enter by a new way. Statues are made of him. Poets, like Pindar, write songs in his honour. And in Athens, an Olympic winner is given free meals, at the expense of the city, for the rest of his life.

[pause]

{img:olive-wreath-crowning-victor-temple-steps-crowd-cheering}
Today, the ruins of Olympia are still there, in their quiet valley, among the pine trees. And every two years, before the modern Olympic Games, the Olympic flame is lit there, from the rays of the sun, with a curved mirror, by women dressed as priestesses, at the ruins of the temple of Hera, and carried by runners, all over the world.

[long-pause]

{img:greek-household-altar-courtyard-offering-wine-cakes-family}
The Athenians live with their gods every day.

[pause]

There are the twelve great gods of Mount Olympus: Zeus, the king of the gods, with his thunderbolt. Hera, his wife, the queen. Athena, the goddess of wisdom, and crafts, and the city. Apollo, the god of the sun, and music, and prophecy. Artemis, his twin sister, the goddess of the hunt, and the moon. Poseidon, the god of the sea. Aphrodite, the goddess of love. Ares, the god of war. Hermes, the messenger, the god of travellers, and merchants, and thieves. Hephaestus, the smith. Demeter, the goddess of the grain. And Dionysus, the god of wine, and the theatre.

[long-pause]

And there are many more: little gods, and nymphs, and heroes, and spirits, of every spring, and river, and tree, and mountain, and house, and crossroads.

[pause]

The gods of the Greeks are very human. They love, and quarrel, and are jealous, and proud, and kind, and cruel. They have families, and favourites. The Greeks tell endless stories about them, which they learn as children, and which are painted on their pots, and carved on their temples, and told in their plays.

[pause]

{img:herm-pillar-street-corner-doorway-hermes-head-flowers}
In every house, there is a little altar, in the courtyard, to Zeus, the protector of the household. At the door, there is a herm, a stone pillar, with the head of the god Hermes on top, to protect the house. In the kitchen, the hearth is sacred to Hestia, the goddess of the hearth. Before every meal, a little food is offered to the gods. Before every drink, a few drops of wine are poured on the ground.

[long-pause]

The Greeks pray standing, with their arms raised to the sky, for the gods above, or stretched down to the ground, for the gods below. They make vows: if the god helps them, they will give a gift. And the temples are full of these gifts: statues, and little figures, and painted plaques, and models of the parts of the body that the god has healed.

[pause]

The great act of worship is the sacrifice, at the altar, outside the temple, when an animal is offered to the god, and the meat is shared among the people, at a feast. It is a joyful occasion, with music, and garlands, and processions.

[pause]

{img:temple-of-asclepius-healing-dreams-sleepers-colonnade-night}
And there are the oracles, where people go to ask the gods for advice about the future. The most famous is at Delphi, in the mountains, where the priestess of Apollo, the Pythia, sitting on a tripod, in the temple, answers questions, in the name of the god, in strange, mysterious words, which the priests interpret. Cities send ambassadors to Delphi, to ask whether they should go to war, or found a colony. And ordinary people go too, to ask about marriage, and money, and journeys, and illness.

[long-pause]

When the Spartans asked the oracle, in 432, whether they should go to war with Athens, the god answered, Thucydides says, that if they fought with all their might, they would win, and that he himself would help them, whether they asked him or not.

[pause]

Over the door of the temple at Delphi, there are two famous words, carved in the stone, which the philosophers love to talk about: Know thyself. And: Nothing too much.

[long-pause]

{img:herodotus-reading-histories-aloud-crowd-festival-scroll}
Athens, in 432, is a magnet for clever people, from all over the Greek world. They come to Athens, because it is rich, and free, and full of people who love to talk, and listen, and argue.

[pause]

One of them is Herodotus, from Halicarnassus, on the coast of what is now Turkey. He is about fifty-two. He has travelled more than almost anyone in the Greek world: to Egypt, and Babylon, and Persia, and Scythia, north of the Black Sea, and Italy.

[long-pause]

And he has written a great book, about the wars between the Greeks and the Persians, fifty years before, and about all the peoples, and lands, and customs, and wonders, he has seen and heard of on his travels. It is the first great work of history ever written. He calls it his Inquiries, in Greek, his historiai, from which our word history comes.

[pause]

He begins: this is the account of the inquiries of Herodotus of Halicarnassus, so that the deeds of men may not be forgotten with time, and that the great and wonderful things done by Greeks and barbarians may not lose their glory.

[pause]

{img:herodotus-traveller-egypt-pyramids-notes-guide-pointing}
Herodotus has read parts of his book aloud, in Athens, to great audiences, and the Athenians loved it, and gave him a reward. He is a wonderful storyteller, and he writes about everything: the pyramids, and the crocodiles of the Nile, and the hippopotamus, and the customs of the Persians, and the gold-digging ants of India, and the people who eat their dead, and the people who wear the skins of their enemies. Some of his stories are true, and some are not, and he often says: I tell what I was told, but I do not have to believe it.

[long-pause]

And there is a younger man, an Athenian, about thirty, who has heard Herodotus, it is said, and wept, and resolved to write history himself. His name is Thucydides. In a year or two, when the war with Sparta begins, he will begin to write its history, carefully, accurately, without stories of gods or marvels, and his book will be one of the greatest history books ever written.

[pause]

On the island of Cos, across the sea, there is a doctor, also about thirty, called Hippocrates.

[pause]

Hippocrates and his followers are changing medicine. They teach that illnesses have natural causes, not supernatural ones: not the anger of the gods, but bad food, or bad water, or the weather, or the way people live. They teach that doctors must observe their patients carefully, and write down what they see, and learn from experience. And that the body has its own power to heal itself, which the doctor must help, with rest, and good food, and exercise, and fresh air.

[long-pause]

The doctors who follow Hippocrates take an oath, which is still remembered, and in some form still taken by doctors today: that they will help the sick, according to their ability and judgment, and never do harm. That they will keep the secrets of their patients. That they will treat everyone, rich or poor, free or slave.

[pause]

And there are philosophers, who ask questions about the nature of the world.

[pause]

{img:philosopher-anaxagoras-looking-at-sun-moon-sky-students}
Anaxagoras, the teacher of Pericles, taught that the sun is not a god, but a great burning stone, larger than the Peloponnese, and that the moon reflects the light of the sun, and that eclipses happen when the moon passes in front of the sun, or the earth's shadow falls on the moon. For saying this, he was accused of not respecting the gods, and had to leave Athens.

[pause]

And a young man, Democritus, from Abdera, in the north, will soon teach that everything in the world is made of tiny particles, too small to see, which cannot be cut, and which he calls atoms, from the Greek word for uncuttable. Two thousand three hundred years later, scientists will discover that he was, in a way, right.

[long-pause]

There are the Sophists, too: travelling teachers, who come to Athens, and teach rich young men, for money, how to speak well, and persuade people, and win arguments, in the assembly, and the courts. The most famous is Protagoras, who says: man is the measure of all things. And that, about the gods, he cannot know whether they exist or not, because the question is difficult, and life is short.

[pause]

{img:sophist-teacher-young-men-colonnade-gesturing-debate}
And there is Phidias, the sculptor, and Ictinus, the architect, and Polygnotus, the painter, and Damon, the musician, and Meton, the astronomer, who has just, in 432, worked out a calendar, based on the observation that nineteen years of the sun are almost exactly equal to two hundred and thirty-five months of the moon. It is still used, today, to work out the date of Easter, and the Jewish calendar.

[pause]

So many great minds, in one small city, at one time. It has hardly ever happened before, or since.

[long-pause]

{img:attic-farm-stone-house-olive-trees-terraces-goats-hills}
Most Athenians, in 432, do not live in the city. They live in the countryside of Attica, in the villages, and on their farms, and they come into the city only for the assembly, and the festivals, and the markets.

[pause]

The historian Thucydides says that, before the war, most Athenians had always lived in the country, and loved it, and their old houses, and their temples, and their way of life there.

[long-pause]

A typical Athenian farm is small: a few fields, of barley and wheat, on the plain, or on terraces, on the hillsides, built with walls of stone, to stop the soil being washed away. A vineyard. An olive grove. A few fig trees. A vegetable garden. Some sheep, and goats, and pigs, and chickens, and an ox, or a donkey, or a mule, to plough, and to carry loads.

[pause]

The farmhouse is built of stone, and mud brick, with a courtyard, and a tower, sometimes, for safety, and storerooms, and a threshing floor, a round, flat, paved area, outside, where the grain is threshed, by animals walking round and round on it.

[pause]

{img:threshing-floor-mules-circling-grain-farmer-winnowing-summer}
The farmer works hard, all year round, with his family, and perhaps a slave or two. A farmer's year is described by an old Greek poet, Hesiod, who lived three hundred years before, in a poem called Works and Days, which every Greek knows.

[long-pause]

In the autumn, when the cranes fly south, crying, high in the sky, it is time to plough, and sow the barley and the wheat. In the winter, the farmer stays by the fire, and mends his tools, and prunes his vines. In the spring, when the swallows come back, he digs around the vines. In early summer, when the snail climbs up the plants, to escape the heat, it is time to harvest the grain, with sickles, and thresh it, and store it.

[pause]

In the hottest days of summer, when the thistle blooms, and the cicada sings in the trees, the farmer may rest, in the shade of a rock, and drink wine, and eat bread and milk, and goat's meat, with his face turned to the cool west wind.

[pause]

And in September, there is the grape harvest, the vintage. The grapes are picked, and carried in baskets, and trodden by foot, in great vats, to the music of pipes, and the juice is poured into great jars, to become wine.

[long-pause]

And in the late autumn and winter, the olives are harvested: beaten from the trees with long sticks, and gathered from the ground, and taken to the press, where they are crushed, and the oil is squeezed out.

[pause]

Olive oil is the most important product of Attica. It is used for everything: for cooking, and for eating, with bread, and for lamps, to give light, and for washing, and for the skin, after exercise, and for medicines, and perfumes. And it is sold, all over the Mediterranean, in painted jars.

[pause]

{img:olive-harvest-sticks-nets-family-trees-winter-light}
The olive trees grow slowly. A new tree takes many years to give fruit, and lives for hundreds of years. So the olive groves are precious, and passed down from father to son, for generations.

[long-pause]

In the country, there are festivals too: the Rural Dionysia, in the winter, in the villages, with processions, and songs, and dances, and plays, and a game in which young men try to stand on a greasy, inflated wineskin, and everyone laughs when they fall off.

[pause]

And there are the old shrines of the country: of the nymphs, in caves, by springs. Of Pan, the god of shepherds, with his pipes. Of Artemis, the goddess of wild things, and hunting. Of Demeter, the goddess of the grain, and her daughter, Persephone, at Eleusis, where, every autumn, thousands of people walk in procession from Athens, along the Sacred Way, to be initiated into the Mysteries, the great secret ceremony, which promises a happy life after death. Nobody ever revealed what happened there. The secret has been kept, for two and a half thousand years.

[pause]

{img:shrine-of-nymphs-cave-spring-offerings-shepherd-goats}
The countryside of Attica is beautiful, and peaceful, in 432. But very soon, the farmers will have to leave it.

[long-pause]

{img:sparta-plain-eurotas-river-mountains-taygetus-hoplites-drilling}
In the summer of 432, the peace of Greece is coming to an end.

[pause]

For fifteen years, there has been a peace treaty between Athens and Sparta, the two great powers of Greece. But Sparta is afraid of Athens, and of its growing power, and its navy, and its empire. And Athens has quarrelled with Corinth, an ally of Sparta, over two cities, Corcyra and Potidaea.

[long-pause]

Sparta is very different from Athens. It is a city of soldiers, in the south of Greece, in a green valley, beneath high mountains. The Spartans have no walls around their city: they say that their soldiers are their walls. Every Spartan man is a soldier, trained from the age of seven, in hard discipline, to be brave, and tough, and obedient. The Spartans do not build great temples, or write plays, or argue in assemblies. They are farmers and warriors, and the land is worked for them by the helots, a whole people, held in a kind of slavery.

[pause]

Spartan women are freer than Athenian women: they exercise, and run, and own land, and speak their minds.

[pause]

In the summer of 432, the allies of Sparta meet, at Sparta, and accuse Athens of breaking the peace. And the Spartans vote that the peace has been broken. Thucydides, the historian, says that the real cause of the war was the growth of the power of Athens, and the fear that this caused in Sparta.

[long-pause]

In Athens, Pericles persuades the assembly not to give in to the demands of Sparta.

[pause]

He has a plan. Sparta has the best army in Greece, on land. Athens has the best navy. So, Pericles says, if the Spartans invade Attica, the Athenians should not fight them on land. They should bring everyone, all the people of the countryside, inside the walls of Athens, and the Long Walls, and let the Spartans burn the farms, and cut down the olive trees. Athens will be fed by its ships, from the sea. And the navy will raid the coasts of the Peloponnese. And in the end, the Spartans will grow tired, and make peace.

[pause]

{img:country-people-moving-into-city-carts-belongings-long-walls}
In the spring of 431, the war begins. The Spartans invade Attica. And the people of the countryside leave their farms, and their homes, and their temples, sadly, and bring their families, and their furniture, and even the wooden parts of their houses, into the city, and send their sheep and cattle to the island of Euboea. They camp in the city, wherever they can, in the temples, and between the Long Walls, and in the towers of the walls.

[long-pause]

At the end of the first year of the war, in the winter, Pericles gives a famous speech, at the funeral of the Athenians who have fallen in the first year of the war. Thucydides wrote it down, or wrote what he remembered of it. It is called the Funeral Oration. And it is one of the most famous speeches in history.

[pause]

In it, Pericles does not talk much about the fallen. He talks about Athens, and what it means. He says that Athens is a democracy, where power is in the hands not of a few, but of the many. That everyone is equal before the law. That people are chosen for public service not because of their wealth, or their family, but because of their merit. That the Athenians live freely, and tolerantly, and do not mind if their neighbours enjoy themselves in their own way. That they love beauty, without extravagance, and wisdom, without weakness. That Athens is the school of Greece.

[pause]

{img:funeral-oration-pericles-speaking-crowd-cypress-coffins-winter}
These words have been read, and quoted, ever since, by people who believe in freedom, and democracy.

[long-pause]

And then, in the second summer of the war, in 430, a terrible plague comes to Athens, perhaps on a ship, from Egypt, into the crowded city, full of people from the countryside.

[pause]

We will not describe it. It is enough to say that a great many people were lost, perhaps a quarter, or a third, of the people of Athens. Thucydides himself caught the illness, and recovered, and described it carefully, so that, he said, if it ever came again, people would recognize it.

[pause]

And in the autumn of 429, Pericles himself fell ill, and passed away. His two sons by his first wife had been lost in the plague before him. His son by Aspasia, the young Pericles, survived.

[long-pause]

The war between Athens and Sparta, which historians call the Peloponnesian War, lasted, with a few years of peace, for twenty-seven years, until 404. And in the end, Athens lost. Its fleet was destroyed, and its walls were pulled down, and its empire was gone.

[pause]

But Athens survived. Its democracy came back, a year later. And its great age of art, and thought, and poetry, went on, for many years more: Plato, and Aristotle, and many others, were still to come.

[pause]

{img:athens-rebuilding-walls-citizens-working-hopeful-morning}
And the Parthenon still stood, on the Acropolis, white and gold, above the city, as it does today.

[long-pause]

{img:acropolis-today-parthenon-ruins-blue-sky-athens-city-around}
Today, Athens is a great modern city, the capital of Greece, with more than three million people, spreading out across the whole plain of Attica, from the mountains to the sea.

[pause]

And in the middle of it all, the Acropolis still rises, as it did in 432, with the Parthenon on top, its columns shining, golden white, in the sunshine.

[long-pause]

The Parthenon is a ruin now. Over two and a half thousand years, it has been a temple of Athena, and a Christian church, and a mosque. In 1687, during a war between Venice and the Ottoman Turks, it was being used to store gunpowder, and a Venetian cannonball struck it, and the gunpowder exploded, and blew out the middle of the temple. Many of its sculptures were later taken to London, by Lord Elgin, in the early nineteenth century, and are in the British Museum, where the Greeks would very much like them back.

[pause]

But the building still stands, with its columns, and its gentle curves, and every year, millions of people from all over the world climb up the old path, through the gateway of the Propylaea, to see it.

[pause]

{img:acropolis-museum-glass-floor-frieze-gallery-light}
At the foot of the Acropolis, there is a new museum, the Acropolis Museum, opened in 2009, with great glass walls, and glass floors, through which you can see the ruins of the ancient city below. On the top floor, the frieze of the Parthenon is laid out, the parts that remain in Athens, and copies of the parts in London, in the same order as on the temple, so that you can walk around it, and see the great procession, the horsemen, and the young women, and the gods, with the real Parthenon, outside the windows, beyond.

[long-pause]

The Agora is still there, below the Acropolis, a peaceful park, of ruins, and trees, and grass, where tortoises wander among the stones. The temple of Hephaestus, on its hill, is still standing, almost complete, with its roof. And the Stoa of Attalos, a great colonnade, built two hundred years after 432, has been rebuilt, by American archaeologists, as it was, and is now the museum of the Agora.

[pause]

In it, you can see the things found in the Agora: the ostraka, with the names of famous Athenians, scratched on them. The kleroterion, the machine for choosing jurors. A water clock, for timing speeches in the courts. Little bronze ballots. Children's toys. And cooking pots, and lamps, and coins, from the everyday life of the people.

[pause]

The theatre of Dionysus is still there too, on the south slope of the Acropolis, with its stone seats, built a century after 432, and the marble thrones of the priests, in the front row. And nearby, there is another theatre, the Odeon of Herodes Atticus, built by the Romans, where, on summer evenings, ancient Greek plays are still performed, under the stars, with the Acropolis lit up above.

[long-pause]

On the hill of the Pnyx, where the assembly met, you can still see the stone speaker's platform, cut into the rock. It is a quiet place, with pine trees, and a view of the Acropolis, where few tourists come. You can stand there, where Pericles stood, and imagine the six thousand citizens, sitting on the slope, raising their hands to vote.

[pause]

And at the Piraeus, the ferries still sail out, every day, to the islands, past the old harbours where the triremes once lay.

[pause]

{img:pnyx-hill-pine-trees-acropolis-view-sunset-quiet}
And in the evening, when the sun goes down, the marble of the Parthenon turns golden, and then pink, and then soft grey, and the lights of the city come on, all across the plain, and the Acropolis is lit up, glowing, above the city, as it has for two and a half thousand years.

[long-pause]

So we leave Athens tonight, in its last peaceful summer, with the new temple shining on its rock, and the people talking in the Agora as the evening comes.

[pause]

The potter at his wheel, the farmer in his olive grove, the barefoot philosopher with his questions, and a city that tried, for the first time, to let its people rule themselves.
