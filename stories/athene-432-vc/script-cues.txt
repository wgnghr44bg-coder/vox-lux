{img:athens-432bc-acropolis-parthenon-new-marble-city-below-morning}
Tonight we are going nearly two thousand five hundred years back, to the city of Athens in ancient Greece, in the year 432 before Christ.

[long-pause]

On a great rock above the city, a new temple has just been finished. It is made of shining white marble, and it is the most beautiful building the Greeks have ever made. Below it, in the streets and the market, in the theatre and the assembly, the people of Athens are talking and arguing and voting. They are writing plays and asking questions that people will still be asking two and a half thousand years later.

[pause]

It is the golden age of Athens. Tonight we will spend a little time there, in what will turn out to be its last peaceful summer.

[pause]

If you enjoy these slow evenings in the distant past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, and come back for another one.

[long-pause]

{img:greek-coast-blue-sea-islands-olive-hills-summer-haze}
Let us picture Greece as it is in 432 before Christ.

[pause]

It is a land of mountains and sea. Steep, rocky mountains rise everywhere, covered with pine trees and wild herbs like thyme and sage. In the summer heat, the air smells of them. Between the mountains lie little valleys with fields, olive groves and vineyards. And the sea is never far away, deep blue and clear, scattered with islands.

[long-pause]

The Greeks do not live in one country with one king. They live in hundreds of small cities, each with its own government, its own laws and its own army. Each city, with the land around it, is called a polis. Some are big, like Athens and Sparta. Some are no bigger than a village on a small island.

[pause]

They often quarrel and fight each other. But they all speak Greek and worship the same gods. They all know the same old poems about the war at Troy. And every four years, they all come together at Olympia for the Olympic Games.

[pause]

Greek cities are scattered all around the Mediterranean, wherever Greek sailors have settled. A wise man called Plato will later say that the Greeks live around the sea like frogs around a pond.

[long-pause]

And of all the Greek cities in 432, the richest, the strongest and the most brilliant is Athens.

[pause]

Athens leads a great alliance of cities around the Aegean Sea. Every year, they pay it silver in return for protection. Athens has the greatest navy in the world, three hundred warships. It has silver mines in the south of its land. And it has a great port full of ships and goods from all over the Mediterranean.

[pause]

{img:piraeus-harbour-triremes-merchant-ships-warehouses-sunlight}
And Athens has something new, something no other people has tried in quite this way before. It is called democracy. That means: rule by the people.

[long-pause]

In Athens, ordinary citizens meet on a hill every few days. Farmers, shoemakers, potters and sailors sit side by side. Together they vote on all the big questions. War or peace, laws and taxes, new buildings, and who will lead them.

[pause]

It is not a perfect democracy. Women cannot vote. Slaves cannot vote. Foreigners cannot vote. Perhaps only one person in five or six has a say. But for its time, it is something remarkable.

[pause]

{img:pnyx-hill-assembly-citizens-seated-speaker-platform-morning}
And under this democracy, Athens has become the most extraordinary city in the world. A city of builders and sculptors, poets and playwrights, thinkers and doctors. Some of their names are still known everywhere today. But across the mountains, in the city of Sparta, people are watching Athens grow. And they do not like what they see.

[long-pause]

{img:attica-plain-olive-groves-mountains-hymettus-athens-distance}
Athens is not just a city. It is a whole little country, called Attica. It is a triangle of land jutting out into the sea, about the size of a small Dutch province.

[pause]

Attica has mountains. From one of them comes white marble. Another is famous for its honey, and for the purple colour it turns at sunset. Between the mountains lie plains with olive groves, fields of barley and little villages.

[long-pause]

Perhaps two or three hundred thousand people live in Attica. About half of them live in the city of Athens and its port. The rest live in the countryside, in about a hundred and forty villages.

[pause]

The city lies in the middle of the plain, about seven kilometres from the sea. A great wall surrounds it. And from the city to the port, two long walls run side by side, all the way to the sea, with a road between them. The idea is simple. Even if an enemy army comes into Attica, the city can always reach its ships, and get food. The Athenians hope they will never need those walls. Soon, they will find out whether they work.

[pause]

{img:long-walls-athens-to-piraeus-road-between-plain-sea}
In the middle of the city rises a great rock with cliffs on every side and a flat top. It is called the Acropolis, which means the high city. On top stand the temples of the gods, and above all, the temple of Athena, the goddess of the city.

[long-pause]

Around the Acropolis, the city spreads out in every direction. It is a great jumble of houses, streets, temples, markets, workshops and fountains.

[pause]

Ordinary houses are small and plain. They are made of mud bricks on stone foundations, with red clay tiles on the roofs and whitewashed walls. The streets are narrow and winding. They are dusty in summer and muddy in winter. And they often smell, because there are no drains, and people throw their rubbish into the street.

[pause]

{img:narrow-athens-street-whitewashed-walls-tiles-donkey-shade}
Even the rich live in quite simple houses. The Athenians believe that private homes should be modest. The city's money, they say, should go to beautiful buildings for everyone, like temples, theatres and fountains.

[long-pause]

In summer, the weather is hot and dry, with clear blue skies, day after day. From May to September it hardly rains. The light is very bright and the shadows are sharp. In the afternoon, the cicadas sing in the olive trees, loud and endless.

[pause]

In winter it is cooler, with rain and wind, and sometimes snow on the mountains.

[pause]

{img:olive-grove-cicadas-midday-heat-light-shade-stone-wall}
The Athenians love their land. The great playwright Sophocles will one day write a famous song about it. He sings of the village where he was born, where the nightingale sings among the ivy, and the river never runs dry, and the grey-green olive trees grow, which no enemy has ever been able to destroy. When he writes those words, the Athenians will know just how precious that promise is.

[long-pause]

{img:greek-house-courtyard-altar-rooms-around-well-tiled-roof}
Let us step inside an ordinary Athenian house in 432 before Christ.

[pause]

From the street there is not much to see. A plain white wall with a few small, high windows, and a wooden door. Beside the door stands a little stone pillar for the god Hermes, with a few flowers in front of it. The house turns its back on the street, and looks inward.

[long-pause]

Through the door, a short passage leads to the heart of the house. It is a courtyard, open to the sky. In the middle there is an altar to Zeus, who protects the household, and perhaps a well that collects rain from the roofs. A vine may grow over a frame for shade, or there may be a fig tree and a few pots of herbs.

[pause]

All the rooms open onto the courtyard. There is a kitchen with a hearth and a clay oven. There are storerooms with big jars of oil, wine and grain. There is a room for weaving, with a tall wooden loom. There are bedrooms. And near the front door there is a special dining room, where the husband entertains his friends.

[pause]

{img:greek-kitchen-hearth-clay-pots-oven-woman-cooking-light}
Many houses have a second floor, reached by a wooden ladder or stairs. The women and children often live and sleep up there, away from visitors.

[long-pause]

The rooms are small and simple. There are wooden couches, which are beds at night and seats by day. There are stools, low tables and wooden chests for clothes. There are baskets of wool, and pottery of every kind, for water, oil and wine.

[pause]

The walls are plastered, sometimes painted red or white. The floors are beaten earth, or in better houses, small pebbles set in patterns. There is no glass in the windows, only wooden shutters. In winter the house is cold. It is heated only by little pans of glowing charcoal, carried from room to room.

[pause]

{img:greek-bedroom-wooden-couch-blankets-chest-lamp-shutters}
At night the house is lit by little clay lamps filled with olive oil. They give a small, soft, flickering light. People go to bed early, and get up with the sun.

[long-pause]

It is a simple house. But it is home. The family and their slaves live here together, perhaps with a dog, a few hens, and even a cat, which the Greeks are just beginning to keep as pets. They all live around the courtyard, under the bright Greek sky. They have no idea yet how crowded the city will soon become, when people from the countryside come flooding in behind the walls.

[long-pause]

{img:pericles-bust-helmet-bearded-calm-statesman-portrait}
The leading man of Athens in 432 before Christ is Pericles.

[pause]

He is about sixty-three years old. He is not a king. He has no special title, except that every year the people choose him as one of the ten generals who lead the army and the navy. They have chosen him almost every year for fifteen years. He leads the city by persuading the people with his speeches.

[long-pause]

Pericles comes from one of the oldest and noblest families of Athens. His mother's family helped to found the democracy, about seventy-five years before.

[pause]

There is a story that before he was born, his mother dreamed she gave birth to a lion. He was a serious, clever child, taught by the best teachers in Athens. One of them was a thinker called Anaxagoras. He taught Pericles to think about the world and the stars, and not to be afraid of eclipses, because they have natural causes.

[pause]

{img:philosopher-teaching-young-noble-courtyard-stars-diagram-sand}
Pericles is tall and dignified, with a calm face and a quiet voice. But his head is rather long and high. The comedy writers make fun of it and call him onion head. That is why his statues always show him wearing a helmet, pushed back to hide the shape.

[long-pause]

He is famous for staying calm. There is a story that once a man followed him all day through the market, shouting insults at him. Pericles took no notice. In the evening, the man followed him all the way home, still shouting. And Pericles, without a word, told his servant to take a torch and light the man's way home, because it was dark.

[pause]

He is famous for his honesty too. He does not take bribes or grow rich from his power. He lives simply and hardly ever goes to parties.

[pause]

And above all, he is famous as a speaker. One poet wrote that persuasion sat on his lips. People called him the Olympian, because when he spoke, it was like thunder and lightning from Zeus himself.

[long-pause]

Under Pericles, Athens has grown rich and beautiful.

[pause]

He has persuaded the people to spend the alliance's silver on rebuilding the temples of the Acropolis. The Persians burned them nearly fifty years before. He has hired the greatest builders and sculptors, and paid thousands of workers, to build the Parthenon and much more.

[pause]

{img:workers-carrying-marble-blocks-acropolis-ramp-ox-carts}
Some Athenians criticise him. They say he is using the allies' money, meant for defence, to decorate Athens like a vain woman with jewels. Pericles answers that as long as Athens protects its allies, it may spend the money as it likes. And the building gives work to thousands of ordinary Athenians.

[long-pause]

Pericles also pays citizens a small daily wage for serving on juries and in the council. That way, poor men can take part in government too, not only the rich.

[pause]

And he has a great dream for Athens. He wants it to be, as he says, the school of Greece. A city where people are free and equal before the law, where they love beauty and wisdom, and live openly and bravely.

[pause]

{img:pericles-speaking-bema-crowd-listening-pnyx-afternoon}
But in 432, dark clouds are gathering. Sparta, the other great power of Greece, is growing afraid of Athens, and angry. Messages are going back and forth between the cities. And Pericles, calm as ever, is quietly getting ready for the war he fears is coming.

[long-pause]

{img:aspasia-elegant-greek-woman-chiton-himation-courtyard-thoughtful}
In the house of Pericles lives a remarkable woman. Her name is Aspasia.

[pause]

She is not from Athens. She comes from Miletus, a Greek city on the coast of what is now Turkey, famous for its thinkers. She is about thirty-eight. She is clever, well educated, witty and beautiful. People say she speaks better than most men.

[long-pause]

Pericles divorced his first wife many years before, and has lived with Aspasia for about twelve years. By the law of Athens they cannot marry, because she was not born an Athenian. But they love each other and live as husband and wife. An old writer says Pericles kissed her every morning when he left, and every evening when he came home. The Athenians found that very surprising.

[pause]

They have a son, also called Pericles.

[pause]

{img:greek-house-doorway-morning-man-leaving-woman-farewell}
Aspasia is unusual in Athens. Respectable Athenian women live very quiet lives, mostly at home. They do not talk with men outside their family. But Aspasia talks with the most famous men in Athens, who come to visit Pericles. Even the great thinker Socrates came to talk with her, it is said, and admired her.

[long-pause]

The comedy writers make cruel jokes about her. Her enemies say she controls Pericles and makes him go to war. Some even take her to court, saying she does not respect the gods. That is a serious charge. Pericles defends her himself, and weeps in front of the judges, the only time anyone ever saw him weep. And she is set free.

[pause]

We do not know much about her for certain. None of her own words have survived. But she is remembered as one of the most remarkable women of the ancient world.

[pause]

So what is life like for other women in Athens?

[long-pause]

A girl from a citizen family is raised at home by her mother and the household slaves. She learns to spin, weave, cook and run a home. Some learn to read a little. She marries young, at fourteen or fifteen, to a man her father chooses, often about thirty years old.

[pause]

After marriage she runs her husband's household. She looks after the slaves, the stores and the children. She spins and weaves the family's clothes. She spends most of her time at home. When her husband's friends come to dinner, she does not join them.

[pause]

{img:greek-women-fountain-house-water-jars-chatting-morning}
But women do go out. They visit friends and family. They meet at the fountains to fetch water and talk. And above all, they take part in the great religious festivals, with processions, dances and festivals for women only, where men are not allowed.

[long-pause]

Poorer women must work. They sell bread and vegetables and ribbons in the market. They are nurses and midwives and washerwomen.

[pause]

The great playwright Euripides will soon put on a play called Medea. In it, a woman gives a famous speech about how hard life is for women. She says she would rather stand in battle three times than give birth once.

[pause]

{img:woman-sitting-courtyard-evening-oil-lamp-quiet-thought}
The audience in the theatre, mostly men, will listen in silence. And perhaps some of them will think about it afterwards, on the walk home in the dark.

[long-pause]

{img:acropolis-rock-cliffs-propylaea-gateway-parthenon-erechtheion-site}
Let us climb up to the Acropolis on a summer morning in the year 432 before Christ.

[pause]

The Acropolis is a great flat-topped rock in the middle of the city, about a hundred and fifty metres high. It has cliffs on every side except the west. People have lived and prayed here for thousands of years. Long ago it was the fortress of the kings of Athens. Now it is the home of the gods.

[long-pause]

The only way up is from the west, by a long zigzag ramp. The great processions use it on festival days.

[pause]

At the top of the ramp stands the new gateway to the Acropolis. In 432 it is still being built, of white marble. It looks like the front of a temple, with tall columns and five doorways. The middle doorway is wide enough for the processions to pass through, with the animals for the sacrifice.

[pause]

{img:propylaea-marble-columns-steps-workers-scaffolding-sunlight}
To the right of the gateway, a few years later, a small and lovely temple will be built to Athena of Victory.

[long-pause]

Then you pass through the gateway, out onto the top of the rock. And there, in front of you, on the highest point, shining white and gold and coloured in the sunshine, stands the Parthenon. It is the temple of Athena the Maiden.

[pause]

But before we go there, let us look around.

[pause]

Just past the gate stands a huge bronze statue of Athena, nine metres tall, with a spear, a shield and a helmet. Sailors coming toward Athens can see the gleam of her spear tip from far out at sea. For them, it is the first sign that they are nearly home.

[long-pause]

To the left stands an old temple, the holiest one on the rock. The Persians burned it, and it has been partly repaired. Inside is a very old wooden statue of Athena. The Athenians believe it fell from heaven. Every four years, the women of Athens weave a new robe for it.

[pause]

Near it grows a sacred olive tree. The Athenians tell a story about it. Long ago, Athena and Poseidon, the god of the sea, both wanted to be the god of this city. Poseidon struck the rock with his trident, and a spring of salt water came out. Athena gave the people an olive tree. The people chose the olive tree, and Athena became their goddess.

[pause]

{img:sacred-olive-tree-acropolis-old-temple-marble-sunlight}
When the Persians burned the Acropolis, the story goes, the sacred olive tree burned too. But the very next day, a new green shoot had grown from the black stump. Then the Athenians knew their city would live. They still tell that story whenever times look dark.

[long-pause]

The top of the Acropolis is crowded. There are altars and shrines everywhere, and hundreds of statues given to the gods over the years. There are statues of gods and heroes, of athletes and horsemen and young women. There are stones carved with the laws of the city.

[pause]

It is not quiet and empty like the ruins we see today. It is busy and colourful, full of priests and visitors and workmen, with smoke rising from the altars.

[pause]

{img:acropolis-crowded-statues-altars-smoke-priests-visitors-morning}
From the edge of the rock, you can see the whole of Athens spread out below. The red roofs and the market, the theatre and the hills. The olive groves and the long walls running down to the shining sea. On a clear day, everything looks safe and calm from up here. You would never guess how soon that will change.

[long-pause]

{img:parthenon-new-white-marble-painted-sculptures-colours-sunlight-432bc}
In 432 before Christ, the Parthenon is finished at last.

[pause]

It has taken fifteen years. The building itself was ready six years ago. But the sculptors have kept working on the great statues for the triangles at each end of the roof. Now, in 432, the last of them are in place.

[long-pause]

The Parthenon is made almost entirely of white marble. The marble comes from a mountain about sixteen kilometres away. It was dragged down on wooden sledges, carried to the city on ox carts, and hauled up the ramp. More than twenty thousand tons of it.

[pause]

The temple is about seventy metres long and thirty metres wide. All around it stand forty-six tall columns. Each column is made of round blocks of marble stacked on top of each other, with shallow grooves running up and down.

[pause]

{img:column-drums-marble-masons-fluting-chisels-workshop-acropolis}
The builders did something very clever. In the Parthenon there are almost no straight lines. The floor curves up a tiny bit in the middle. The columns lean a tiny bit inward, and swell a little in the middle. You cannot see these curves. But they make the building look perfectly straight and balanced and alive, instead of stiff and heavy.

[long-pause]

And the marble is not plain white, as it looks today.

[pause]

In 432, the statues and parts of the building are painted in bright colours. Red and blue, green and gold. The figures have coloured clothes, painted eyes and golden weapons. The whole temple glows in the bright Greek sun.

[pause]

At the east end, over the main door, the statues show the birth of Athena. The story says she sprang fully grown, in armour, from the head of her father Zeus. At the west end, they show the contest between Athena and Poseidon.

[pause]

Around the top of the outside walls there are ninety-two square carved panels. They show great battles from old stories. Gods fight giants, and Greeks fight centaurs, which are half man and half horse.

[long-pause]

Higher up, inside the row of columns, a long band of carving runs all the way around the temple walls. It is a hundred and sixty metres long. It shows a great procession, the festival of Athena. There are horsemen and chariots, young men and old men, young women and musicians, all moving toward the gods, who sit waiting at the end.

[pause]

It shows the people of Athens themselves, honouring their goddess. Perhaps for the first time ever, ordinary people are shown on a temple, beside the gods.

[pause]

{img:frieze-horsemen-procession-marble-relief-young-riders-flowing}
The horses and young riders, with their cloaks blowing in the wind, are among the most beautiful carvings ever made.

[long-pause]

And inside the temple, in the great dim hall, stands the statue of Athena. It was made by the sculptor Phidias, a friend of Pericles.

[pause]

It is twelve metres tall, almost touching the roof. It is made of a wooden frame, covered with ivory for the skin and gold for the clothes. More than a ton of gold. She wears a helmet, with a shield beside her. In her right hand she holds a little winged figure of Victory, as tall as a man.

[pause]

{img:chryselephantine-athena-statue-dim-temple-hall-gold-ivory-pool}
In front of her lies a shallow pool of water. It reflects the light from the door onto the gold and ivory, and it keeps the air damp, so the ivory does not crack.

[pause]

The Athenians come to look at her, and bow, and pray. Pericles has told the assembly something important. The gold of her robe can be taken off and melted down to pay for a war, if the city ever needs it. Everyone hopes that day will never come. But Pericles does not say things without a reason.

[long-pause]

{img:athenian-agora-stoa-colonnade-market-stalls-crowds-morning}
Below the Acropolis lies the heart of everyday life in Athens: the Agora.

[pause]

The Agora is a great open square. It is the market, the meeting place and the centre of government, all at once. Every morning it is crowded with shoppers and sellers, politicians and thinkers, slaves and foreigners. They walk and talk and buy and argue in the sunshine.

[long-pause]

Around the edges of the square stand long buildings with a row of columns in front and a roof on top. They are called stoas. In them, people can walk and talk in the shade, out of the sun and rain.

[pause]

The most famous one is the Painted Stoa. Its back wall is covered with big paintings of the great battles of Athens, like the battle of Marathon against the Persians. Much later, a thinker will teach here, and his followers will be called Stoics, after this building.

[pause]

There is the council house, where five hundred citizens meet every day to prepare business for the assembly. There is a round building where some of them stay day and night, so that someone is always ready if danger comes. There are law courts, temples and fountains.

[long-pause]

On a little hill beside the Agora, a new temple is being built to Hephaestus, the god of fire and of craftsmen. It still stands today, almost complete. It is the best preserved Greek temple in the world.

[pause]

Around it are the workshops of the smiths and bronze workers. The ring of their hammers sounds across the square.

[pause]

{img:temple-hephaestus-hill-agora-smiths-workshops-smoke}
In the middle of the Agora are the market stalls, with tables and baskets and awnings. Each kind of goods has its own corner.

[long-pause]

The fish market is the most popular. The Athenians love fish. The fish sellers are famous for being rude and greedy, and for refusing to bargain.

[pause]

There are stalls with bread and garlic, onions and beans, figs and grapes. There is olive oil, wine, honey and cheese. There are sellers of perfume and flowers, shoes and cloth, and even books, written on rolls of papyrus.

[pause]

{img:agora-fish-stall-seller-arguing-customer-baskets-octopus}
There is a slave market too. Because Athens, like all ancient cities, depends on the work of slaves.

[long-pause]

Slaves are everywhere in Athens. Perhaps one person in three is a slave. They were captured in war, or sold by pirates, or born to slave mothers, from all around the Mediterranean.

[pause]

Some work in the houses of the rich, as cooks and nurses and teachers. Some work in workshops beside their masters. Some work in the fields. The unluckiest work in the silver mines, in terrible conditions. Even the city's police are slaves, archers from the far north, owned by the city.

[pause]

Life for a slave is often hard. But many are treated as part of the household, and some are set free. A visitor once complained that in Athens you cannot tell a slave from a free man in the street. They dress the same, he said, and slaves are not afraid to speak their minds.

[long-pause]

And above all, the Agora is where people come to talk.

[pause]

The Athenians love talking more than anything. About politics and news, about trials and plays, about the price of fish and the meaning of life. They stand in groups in the shade, or sit on temple steps, and argue for hours.

[pause]

{img:men-talking-in-groups-stoa-steps-afternoon-shade-gestures}
And among them walks a short, round, rather ugly man with a snub nose and bulging eyes. He goes barefoot, wears the same old cloak summer and winter, and asks everyone questions. His name is Socrates. Many people find his questions annoying. A few find them dangerous.

[long-pause]

{img:athens-dawn-rooftops-acropolis-pink-light-roosters}
Let us spend an ordinary day in Athens in the summer of 432 before Christ, with an ordinary family.

[pause]

The father is a citizen and a potter. He has a little workshop in the potters' quarter, near the northwest gate of the city. He has a wife, three children and two slaves, who help in the workshop and the house.

[long-pause]

The day begins at dawn. Athenians get up at first light, to make the most of the cool morning before the heat.

[pause]

The father washes his face in cold water from a jar. He does not shave, because Athenian men wear beards. He puts on his tunic. It is a simple piece of wool or linen, pinned at the shoulders and tied at the waist. If it is cool, he wraps a big woollen cloak around himself as well.

[pause]

{img:greek-man-wrapping-himation-cloak-courtyard-morning-light}
Breakfast is simple. A piece of barley bread dipped in watered wine, and perhaps a few figs or olives. Then he goes to work.

[long-pause]

In the workshop, the potters are already busy.

[pause]

Athenian pottery is famous all over the Mediterranean. The pots are made of fine red clay, shaped on a wheel that a boy turns by hand. They are painted with a special black glaze. When they are fired in the kiln, the clay turns red and the glaze turns shiny black.

[pause]

The painters leave the figures red and paint the background black. Then they draw in the details with very fine lines, using a brush sometimes made of a single hair. The pictures show gods and heroes, athletes and women at the fountain, and everyday life. Many pots are signed by their maker. And they are sold far away, in Italy, by the Black Sea and in Egypt.

[pause]

But lately, the potter has heard worrying talk in the market. If war comes, will the ships still sail? Will his pots still reach their buyers? He keeps working, and tries not to think about it.

[long-pause]

At home, his wife runs the household.

[pause]

She tells the slave woman what to clean and cook. Water must be fetched from the fountain in a jar carried on the head. She spins wool, and weaves cloth on a tall loom in the courtyard, for the family's clothes and blankets.

[pause]

{img:woman-upright-loom-weaving-wool-courtyard-daughter-spinning}
The little children play in the courtyard with clay dolls that have moving arms and legs, little carts, spinning tops and balls. Some have a pet bird or a tortoise. The older son goes to school.

[long-pause]

At midday the family eats a light meal. In the heat of the early afternoon, everyone rests in the shade. The streets are empty and quiet, except for the cicadas.

[pause]

Later, the father finishes work and goes to the sports ground to exercise and wash and talk with his friends. Or he goes to the Agora for the news. Or, if it is a day of the assembly, he goes to vote.

[pause]

{img:athens-street-afternoon-quiet-heat-shadows-cat-doorway}
In the evening, as the air cools, the family eats its main meal together. There is barley porridge or bread, vegetables and lentils, cheese and olives. On good days there is a little fish. Meat is rare. It is mostly eaten at festivals, after a sacrifice.

[long-pause]

When darkness falls, the little oil lamps are lit, and the family sits in the courtyard under the stars. The father tells stories about Theseus, the hero of Athens, who killed the Minotaur. And about the battle of Marathon, where their grandfather fought.

[pause]

Then they go to bed on simple wooden beds with mattresses of wool. In the hottest weather, some sleep on the flat roof under the stars. And Athens lies quiet under the moon, with the great white temple glowing faintly above the sleeping city.

[long-pause]

{img:greek-woman-peplos-chiton-pinned-shoulders-himation-veil-courtyard}
What do the Athenians wear in 432 before Christ?

[pause]

Their clothes are very simple. Almost every garment is just a big rectangle of cloth, woven at home on the family loom. It is draped around the body and held with pins and a belt. There is very little sewing, and no buttons at all.

[long-pause]

Men wear a tunic made of two pieces of cloth, pinned at the shoulders and belted at the waist. Workmen and soldiers wear a short one, to the knees, so they can move freely. Older and richer men wear a long one, to the ankles.

[pause]

Over it goes a big woollen cloak, wrapped around the body and over the left shoulder. Wearing it gracefully, so it hangs in nice folds, is the sign of a gentleman. Young horsemen wear a short cloak pinned at one shoulder.

[pause]

Women wear a long, light dress of linen, falling to the feet, or a heavier one of wool, folded over at the top and pinned at the shoulders. When they go out, they pull a cloak over their heads like a veil.

[long-pause]

The clothes are often brightly coloured. Saffron yellow, red, green and blue, with patterned borders. The finest purple dye is made from sea snails. It is so costly that only the rich can wear it.

[pause]

At home, and often in the street, people go barefoot. Otherwise they wear leather sandals, or boots for travelling. In the sun they wear a wide hat of felt or straw.

[pause]

{img:sandal-maker-workshop-leather-sandals-hanging-street-front}
In 432, men wear their hair short and their beards neatly trimmed. Women wear their hair long and tied up in elegant styles, with ribbons and scarves. They use make-up too. White powder to make their skin pale, and red from plants for their cheeks and lips.

[long-pause]

Rich women wear fine jewellery of gold and silver. Earrings, necklaces and bracelets, with tiny flowers and animals. They carry fans, and little bottles of perfume.

[pause]

Everyone smells of olive oil and perfume. After bathing, and before parties, Athenians rub scented oil into their skin and hair. There are perfume shops in the Agora where people meet to gossip.

[pause]

{img:perfume-shop-agora-small-bottles-oils-customers-chatting}
For festivals and parties, people wear wreaths of leaves and flowers, of myrtle, ivy, violets and roses. A famous poet once called Athens violet-crowned. Perhaps he meant the purple hills at sunset. Or perhaps he meant the wreaths of violets its people wore, on happy days, when nobody yet thought of war.

[long-pause]

{img:symposium-men-reclining-couches-wine-krater-lamps-andron}
In the evening, richer Athenians hold dinner parties called symposia.

[pause]

The word means drinking together. It is a party for men only. The host and a handful of friends meet in a special dining room, with couches along the walls and a floor of little pebbles in patterns.

[long-pause]

The guests arrive in the evening and take off their sandals at the door. A slave washes their feet. They lie down on the couches, two to a couch, leaning on their left elbow on soft cushions. Little tables with food are set in front of them.

[pause]

First they eat. Bread, fish, vegetables, cheese, olives and perhaps a little meat. They eat with their fingers and wipe their hands on pieces of bread, which they throw to the dogs under the tables.

[pause]

{img:slave-boy-pouring-wine-from-krater-into-kylix-cups-lamps}
Then the tables are cleared and the floor is swept. Now the drinking begins. The guests put on wreaths of flowers or ivy. They pour a little wine on the floor as a gift to the gods, and sing a hymn.

[long-pause]

The wine is always mixed with water in a big bowl. The Greeks think only barbarians drink wine without water. The guests choose a leader for the evening. He decides how strong the wine should be, and how many bowls they will drink. Slaves fill the cups with a ladle.

[pause]

The cups are wide and shallow, with two handles. Many are painted inside. As you drink, a picture appears at the bottom, perhaps a dolphin or a funny face.

[pause]

Then the guests talk. About politics, about love, about poetry. They tell jokes and riddles. They take turns to sing, passing a sprig of myrtle from one singer to the next. And these days, more and more often, the talk turns to Sparta, and what might happen if war comes.

[long-pause]

There is entertainment too. A girl plays the double flute. There are dancers and acrobats. And the guests play games.

[pause]

Their favourite game is called kottabos. Lying on his couch, each guest holds his cup by one handle and flicks the last drops of wine at a target. The target is a little bronze disc balanced on top of a tall stand. If he knocks it down with a clang, he wins. As he throws, he says the name of the person he loves. If he hits, it is a good sign.

[pause]

{img:kottabos-game-wine-drops-flicked-bronze-stand-laughter}
The party can go on late into the night. Sometimes it gets noisy. At the end, the guests stagger out into the streets with torches, singing, and wake up the neighbours.

[long-pause]

But the best parties are about conversation.

[pause]

Much later, the thinker Plato will write a famous book about a dinner party like this. In it, Socrates and other famous men decide not to drink too much. Instead they take turns making speeches about love. Socrates gives the most beautiful speech of all. He says that loving beauty can lead the soul upward, step by step, toward wisdom and goodness itself.

[pause]

{img:dawn-light-symposium-room-guests-asleep-one-man-leaving}
At the end of that book, as dawn breaks, all the guests have fallen asleep on their couches, except Socrates. He gets up, walks out into the morning, washes, and spends the day as usual, talking with anyone he meets. And in the evening, he goes home to bed.

[long-pause]

{img:greek-schoolroom-boys-wax-tablets-teacher-lyre-scrolls}
At about seven years old, the son of an Athenian citizen goes to school.

[pause]

There are no free schools paid for by the city. Parents pay small fees to private teachers, who teach in rooms or in the open air. Rich families send their sons for many years. Poor families send them for a few years, or not at all. Most girls do not go to school. They learn at home.

[long-pause]

Every morning, the boy walks to school with an old slave who looks after him. The slave carries his things, makes sure he behaves, and even sits with him during lessons. Our word for teaching, pedagogy, comes from the name of this slave.

[pause]

The boy has three teachers.

[pause]

{img:paidagogos-old-slave-walking-boy-to-school-street-morning}
The first teaches him to read, write and count. The boy writes on a wooden board covered with wax, using a pointed stick. He can rub out his mistakes with the flat end. He learns his letters, then words, then long pieces of poetry, especially Homer. He learns hundreds of lines by heart. Every educated Athenian knows much of Homer by heart.

[long-pause]

The second teacher teaches him music. He learns to play the lyre, a small harp made with a tortoise shell, and to sing. The Greeks believe that music shapes a person's character and makes the soul gentle.

[pause]

The third teacher teaches him sport at the wrestling school. Wrestling, running, jumping, throwing the discus and the javelin.

[pause]

The boys exercise naked, as Greek athletes always do. They rub their bodies with olive oil, and afterwards scrape it off with a curved metal tool. The Greeks believe a strong, healthy body is just as important as a good mind.

[long-pause]

Older boys and men go to the gymnasium.

[pause]

There are three great gymnasiums in Athens, outside the city walls, in pleasant places with trees and streams. They have running tracks and wrestling grounds, baths, and shady paths under plane trees and olive trees.

[pause]

{img:academy-gymnasium-plane-trees-olive-grove-running-track-shade}
Men go there in the afternoon to exercise, wash and talk. Thinkers go there too, to talk with the young men and teach them. One of these places is called the Academy. Later, Plato will start his famous school there, and all the academies in the world will be named after it.

[long-pause]

At eighteen, a young Athenian begins two years of training as a soldier. He guards the borders of Attica, in little forts in the mountains. After that he is a full citizen. He can vote in the assembly and serve in the army as a foot soldier, with a bronze helmet, a big round shield and a spear. If he is poor, he serves as a rower in the navy.

[pause]

{img:young-men-oath-temple-ephebes-shields-spears-morning-solemn}
The young men swear an oath in a temple on the slope of the Acropolis. They promise never to disgrace their weapons, and never to leave a friend alone in battle. They promise to leave their homeland not smaller, but greater and better than they found it. In 432, the young men say these words with special seriousness. They know that soon, they may have to keep them.

[long-pause]

{img:socrates-snub-nose-beard-barefoot-cloak-agora-talking-young-men}
In 432 before Christ, Socrates is about thirty-seven years old.

[pause]

His father was a stonemason and his mother a midwife. As a young man, he learned to work stone himself. But he has given up that work. Now he spends every day talking.

[long-pause]

By Greek standards, he is very ugly. The Greeks love beauty, and Socrates has a snub nose, thick lips, bulging eyes and a big belly. His friends say he walks like a pelican, looking from side to side. He goes barefoot summer and winter, and always wears the same old cloak. He is poor, and he does not care.

[pause]

But something about him draws people in. His friends say he is like the little ugly statues sold in the market. You open them up, and inside there are small golden figures of the gods.

[pause]

Socrates goes everywhere and talks with everyone. Politicians and poets, craftsmen and generals, young and old, rich and poor. And he asks them questions.

[long-pause]

He asks about things they think they know. What is courage? What is justice? What is a good life? What is friendship? Can goodness be taught?

[pause]

When they answer, he asks more questions, gently and politely. Step by step, he shows them that they do not really know what they thought they knew. Their answers are confused, or contradict each other. Then he says: I do not know either. Let us look for the answer together.

[pause]

{img:philosopher-walking-with-young-men-path-plane-trees-river}
This way of teaching by asking questions is still used by teachers all over the world today.

[long-pause]

Socrates says he knows only one thing: that he knows nothing. And perhaps that makes him a little wiser than people who think they know things they do not.

[pause]

He says the most important thing in life is not money or fame or power. It is to look after your soul, and try to become a good person. And he says: a life without asking questions is not worth living.

[pause]

{img:socrates-standing-still-thinking-dawn-soldiers-camp-watching}
Socrates is also a brave soldier. And very soon, he will march off to war in the north of Greece. His friends will later tell how he walked barefoot over the ice in a bitter winter. Once, they say, he stood still, thinking about a problem, from one dawn to the next, while the other soldiers stared at him. And in a battle, he saved the life of a young friend.

[long-pause]

Socrates never writes anything down. He believes wisdom is found in talking, between living people, not in books. Everything we know about him comes from his friends and students, above all Plato.

[pause]

He has a wife called Xanthippe. The stories say she has a sharp temper, and scolds him because he earns no money and is always out talking. Socrates jokes that if he can live with her, he can get along with anyone.

[pause]

Many young men love him and follow him around. Their fathers are sometimes less pleased. Because Socrates teaches the young to question everything, even the laws and the gods of their city.

[long-pause]

And in the end, that will cost him his life. Many years later, when he is seventy, the Athenians will put him on trial for it. The jury will condemn him.

[pause]

He could escape, but he will refuse, out of respect for the laws of Athens. He will spend his last day talking calmly with his friends about the soul, and then die peacefully among them.

[pause]

{img:quiet-evening-prison-room-friends-gathered-calm-light}
But in 432, all that is far away. He is a man in his prime, walking barefoot through the Agora in the morning sun, looking for someone to talk to.

[long-pause]

{img:theatre-of-dionysus-hillside-seats-acropolis-slope-audience}
On the south slope of the Acropolis lies the theatre of Dionysus, the god of wine and of theatre.

[pause]

It is an open-air theatre built into the hillside. The audience sits on wooden benches rising up the slope in a great half circle. Below them is a round, flat dancing floor, where the chorus sings and dances. Behind it is a low building where the actors change. It is painted to look like a palace or a temple.

[long-pause]

The theatre holds perhaps fifteen thousand people, almost all the citizens of Athens.

[pause]

Plays are not shown all year round, like today. They are performed at festivals for Dionysus. The biggest one is in spring, in March, when the sea is calm again and visitors come from all over Greece.

[pause]

{img:procession-statue-dionysus-city-dionysia-spring-crowds-ivy}
The festival lasts five days. There are processions and choirs, and then three days of plays, from dawn to dusk. It is a competition. At the end, judges choose the best playwright and the best actor. The prize is a crown of ivy, and great honour.

[long-pause]

The serious plays are called tragedies. They tell the old stories of gods and heroes. The war at Troy, King Oedipus, Medea, Antigone and many more. They are sad and full of great poetry. They ask deep questions about fate and justice, about suffering, and about what happens when people make terrible mistakes.

[pause]

Two of the three great writers of tragedy are alive in these years. Sophocles is about sixty-five. Around this time he may be writing his most famous play, about King Oedipus. Euripides is about fifty, and his plays question everything, and sometimes shock the Athenians.

[pause]

All the actors are men, even those who play women. Each play has only three actors. Each one plays several parts by changing masks. The masks have big open mouths, so the voices carry, and so that even people at the back can see who is who.

[long-pause]

The chorus of a dozen or so men sings and dances between the scenes. They comment on what is happening, give advice, pray and mourn. A musician plays the double flute.

[pause]

Historians still argue whether women were allowed to watch. Perhaps some did.

[pause]

{img:comic-actors-padded-costumes-funny-masks-audience-laughing}
And then there are the comedies. They are rude, silly and very funny. They make jokes about politics and about famous men, who are often sitting in the audience being laughed at. In a few years, a great comic writer will put Socrates in a play, hanging in a basket in the air, studying the clouds. It is said that Socrates stood up in the theatre, so everyone could see how much he looked like his mask.

[long-pause]

Going to the theatre is part of religion, and part of being a citizen. Poor citizens even get money from the city to pay for their seats.

[pause]

The audience brings cushions, food and wine, and spends the whole day there in the spring sunshine. They cheer and weep and laugh. If they do not like an actor, they throw nuts and figs at him.

[pause]

{img:sunset-theatre-empty-seats-acropolis-above-golden-quiet}
Behind the stage, the audience can see the plain of Attica, the mountains and the sea. So the old stories of gods and heroes are played out against the real land of Greece, under the open sky. Next spring, the plays will go on as usual. But by then, the land behind the stage may look very different.

[long-pause]

{img:pnyx-assembly-thousands-citizens-hillside-speaker-bema-dawn}
About forty times a year, early in the morning, the citizens of Athens climb a hill west of the Acropolis, for a meeting of the assembly.

[pause]

The hill is a wide slope with a stone platform at the bottom, where the speakers stand. The citizens sit on the slope, on the ground or on cushions they have brought. From the top they can see the Parthenon, shining in the morning sun.

[long-pause]

Any male citizen over eighteen may come, speak and vote. About six thousand usually come. The meeting begins at dawn with a prayer, and goes on until the business is done, usually by midday.

[pause]

Before the meeting, the city police go around the market with a rope dipped in red paint, and herd the citizens toward the hill. Anyone caught with red paint on his clothes, who has not gone to the assembly, must pay a fine.

[pause]

{img:scythian-archers-red-rope-herding-citizens-agora-morning}
A herald calls out: who wishes to speak? Anyone may stand up, walk to the platform, put a wreath on his head, and speak to the people. Usually the speakers are experienced men like Pericles. But anyone may try. If a speaker is boring or foolish, the crowd shouts him down and laughs.

[long-pause]

The assembly decides everything. War or peace, laws and taxes, buildings and ambassadors. And in 432, there is one question that comes up again and again. What to do about Sparta?

[pause]

They vote by raising their hands. Officials look across the hillside and judge which side has more. For some important matters, they vote with pebbles dropped into jars.

[pause]

The business of the assembly is prepared by a council of five hundred citizens. They are not elected. They are chosen by lot, drawn by chance, like a lottery. The Athenians think this is the fairest way, because it gives every citizen an equal chance. Most officials are chosen by lot too. Only the generals, and a few others who need special skills, are elected.

[long-pause]

There are law courts too, with huge juries of hundreds of citizens, chosen by lot each morning. There are no professional judges and no lawyers. Each side speaks for itself, for a fixed time. The time is measured by a water clock, which is a pot with a small hole that slowly lets the water out. Then the jury votes straight away.

[pause]

To choose the jurors, the Athenians use a kind of stone machine. Citizens slot their little bronze name tickets into rows of holes. Then black and white balls are dropped down a tube at random, to decide which rows will serve. Archaeologists have found some of these machines.

[pause]

{img:kleroterion-stone-slots-bronze-tickets-allotment-machine-citizens}
And there is ostracism. Once a year, the assembly can decide to send one man away for ten years, if he seems to be getting too powerful. Each citizen scratches a name on a broken piece of pottery. If enough votes are cast, the man with the most votes must leave Athens for ten years. He keeps his property, and may come back afterwards.

[long-pause]

Archaeologists have found thousands of these pottery pieces, with famous names scratched on them. Even the name of Pericles.

[pause]

There is a famous story about a man called Aristides. Everyone called him Aristides the Just, because he was so honest. At an ostracism, a farmer who could not write, and did not know him, asked him to scratch a name on his piece of pottery: Aristides. Aristides asked what harm Aristides had ever done to him. None, said the farmer. I do not even know him. I am just tired of everyone calling him the Just. And Aristides wrote his own name, and handed it back.

[pause]

{img:broken-pottery-sherds-names-scratched-ostraka-museum-light}
The democracy of Athens has many faults. It leaves out women, slaves and foreigners. Sometimes the assembly makes rash or cruel choices, swayed by clever speakers. But it is the first great experiment in government by ordinary people. And soon it will face its hardest test of all: whether a city ruled by its people can survive a long war.

[long-pause]

{img:panathenaic-procession-sacred-way-young-women-baskets-horsemen-dawn}
The greatest festival of Athens is the Panathenaia. It is the festival of all the Athenians, held every summer on Athena's birthday.

[pause]

Every year there is a small festival. And every four years there is the Great Panathenaia, which lasts about a week, with games, music, sacrifices and a great procession.

[long-pause]

The games are like the Olympic Games, but with even more events. There are foot races, horse races and chariot races. There is wrestling and boxing. There are music contests, singing to the lyre and playing the flute. And there are contests for reciting Homer. Professional reciters compete to tell the old stories of Troy most beautifully.

[pause]

There are boat races in the harbour. And there is a torch race at night. It is a relay, from an altar outside the city to the altar of Athena on the Acropolis. The runners pass burning torches from hand to hand. If the flame goes out, the team loses. So everyone watches the little flames bobbing through the dark streets, holding their breath.

[pause]

{img:torch-relay-race-night-runners-flames-crowds-cheering}
There is even a dance in full armour, and a contest of strength and good looks between the tribes.

[long-pause]

The prizes for the athletes are wonderful. They are big jars filled with olive oil from Athena's sacred olive trees. Each jar holds about forty litres. A winner of the chariot race might win a hundred and forty jars. That is a fortune.

[pause]

The jars are painted. On one side Athena strides forward with her spear and shield. On the other side is a picture of the event, like runners or wrestlers. And the words: one of the prizes from Athens. Many of these jars are still in museums today.

[pause]

On the last great day, Athena's birthday, comes the great procession.

[long-pause]

At dawn, everyone gathers at the big double gate in the northwest of the city.

[pause]

Then the procession moves off along a broad road that crosses the market and climbs up to the Acropolis. Everyone is there. Priests and priestesses. Young women from noble families, carrying baskets on their heads. Old men with olive branches. Young men leading cattle and sheep for the sacrifice. Musicians, horsemen and chariots. Even the foreigners who live in Athens walk along with offerings.

[pause]

{img:new-peplos-robe-carried-like-sail-ship-on-wheels-procession}
In the middle is the most important thing of all. It is a new robe for Athena, woven by the women and girls of Athens over the past nine months. It is saffron yellow, with pictures woven into it. At the Great Panathenaia, the robe is so big that it hangs like a sail from the mast of a ship on wheels, pulled along the road.

[long-pause]

The procession climbs up through the great gateway. The robe is given to the priestess of Athena, to dress the ancient wooden statue in the old temple.

[pause]

Then comes the great sacrifice, a hundred cattle or more, at the altar of Athena. Afterwards the meat is roasted and shared among all the people of Athens in a great feast. For many poor families, it is the best meal of the whole year.

[pause]

{img:great-feast-athenians-sharing-roasted-meat-bread-evening-joy}
Perhaps this is the procession that is carved around the Parthenon. The horsemen, the young women, the old men and the cattle, and the gods waiting to receive them. The people of Athens, honouring their goddess, in marble, forever. As they eat and laugh together this summer, they hope there will be many more festivals like it.

[long-pause]

{img:piraeus-port-harbours-ship-sheds-triremes-merchant-ships-sun}
Seven kilometres from the city, along the road between the long walls, lies the port of Athens. It is called the Piraeus.

[pause]

It is a busy, noisy town built around three natural harbours. About twenty years ago it was laid out by a famous planner, with straight streets crossing each other in a neat grid. It was one of the first towns of its kind in Greece.

[long-pause]

The biggest harbour is for trade. It is full of merchant ships from all over the Mediterranean and the Black Sea. They bring grain from the Black Sea, Egypt and Sicily, because Attica cannot grow enough to feed all its people. They bring timber for shipbuilding, and fish, wine, papyrus, spices and carpets.

[pause]

Pericles says that because Athens is so great, the goods of the whole world flow into the city. The Athenians enjoy foreign things as easily as their own.

[pause]

{img:merchants-unloading-grain-amphoras-quay-piraeus-porters}
Along the quays there are warehouses and a great market hall. Bankers sit at their tables changing money. There are taverns for the sailors. And there are people from everywhere, speaking every language.

[pause]

But there is a worry hidden in all this. Athens needs that grain from far away. If an enemy ever cut off the ships, the city would go hungry. That is why the navy matters so much.

[long-pause]

The other two harbours belong to the navy.

[pause]

Around them stand hundreds of ship sheds. They are long, narrow buildings with sloping floors running down into the water. The warships are pulled up into them when they are not at sea, so their wooden hulls stay dry and do not rot.

[pause]

The warships are called triremes. They are long, slim and fast, about thirty-seven metres long but only five or six metres wide. They have a square sail for cruising. But in battle they are rowed by a hundred and seventy rowers, sitting in three rows, one above the other. At the front, at the waterline, there is a heavy bronze ram for smashing into enemy ships. And on each side of the bow there is a painted eye, so the ship can see its way.

[long-pause]

The rowers are not slaves. They are free men, mostly poor citizens of Athens, and they are paid a day's wage. A flute player plays a steady beat, so they all pull together. A well-rowed trireme can go as fast as a running man, and turn very quickly.

[pause]

Because the navy depends on these poor citizens, they have great power in the assembly. That is one reason why Athens is a democracy. The rich fight on land. But the poor row the ships that make Athens great.

[pause]

{img:rowers-benches-trireme-piper-rhythm-pulling-oars-together}
Every year, from spring to autumn, the fleet goes out to patrol the seas, protect the merchant ships and show the world the power of Athens. This year, the captains check their ships more carefully than usual.

[long-pause]

In 1987, Greek and British scientists and sailors built a full-size copy of an Athenian trireme. They rowed it on the sea with volunteers, to see how it worked. It went very fast and turned very quickly, just as the old writers said. It is now kept in a museum near the Piraeus.

[pause]

{img:evening-piraeus-harbour-ships-moored-lamps-quiet-water}
In the evening, the sailors come ashore. They eat fish and drink wine in the taverns, and tell stories of the sea and the islands. The ships rock gently in the harbour, and the sun sets behind the island of Salamis.

[long-pause]

{img:athenian-silver-tetradrachm-owl-athena-coins-hand-close}
In the markets of Athens, and all over the Mediterranean, people pay with Athenian coins. They are small, thick silver coins. On one side is the head of Athena in her helmet. On the other side is an owl, Athena's bird, with big round eyes and a little olive twig.

[pause]

People call these coins owls. A big one is worth four drachmas. One drachma is about a day's pay for a skilled worker. The smallest coins are so tiny that people carry them in their mouths at the market, tucked in their cheeks, because they have no pockets.

[long-pause]

The owls of Athens are the most trusted coins in the Greek world. Their silver is always pure and their weight is always right. They are used everywhere, from Italy to Egypt and Persia. Some have even been found as far away as Afghanistan. The Greeks have a saying for doing something useless: bringing owls to Athens.

[pause]

The silver comes from mines in the south of Attica.

[pause]

{img:laurion-hills-mine-entrances-washing-tables-ore-cisterns}
The mines are deep, narrow, dark tunnels in the hills. Thousands of slaves work there by the light of little oil lamps. They dig out the ore with picks and carry it up in baskets. It is terrible, dangerous work. Above ground, the ore is crushed and washed, and then melted in furnaces to get the silver.

[long-pause]

Fifty years ago, the Athenians found a rich new vein of silver in these mines. A clever leader called Themistocles persuaded them not to share the money out, but to build two hundred warships with it. Three years later, those ships beat the great Persian fleet at the battle of Salamis, and saved Greece. They were the start of the great navy and the wealth of Athens.

[pause]

So the silver dug by slaves in the dark paid for the Parthenon, the fleet and much of the glory of Athens. The Athenians hope it will be enough to pay for whatever is coming next.

[pause]

{img:temple-of-poseidon-sounion-cliff-sea-sunset-columns}
At the tip of Attica, high on a cliff above the sea, stands a white marble temple to Poseidon, god of the sea. It was built just a few years ago. Sailors coming home see it shining on its cliff from far out at sea, and know they are almost home.

[pause]

It still stands today. At sunset, people come out from Athens to sit on the rocks beside its columns and watch the sun go down into the sea. On one of the columns, more than two hundred years ago, the English poet Lord Byron carved his name.

[long-pause]

{img:olympia-sanctuary-temple-zeus-valley-alpheus-river-pines}
Every four years, in the middle of summer, Greeks from every city travel to a quiet valley in the west of Greece, beside a river, among pine trees and olive groves. The place is called Olympia.

[pause]

Olympia is not a city. It is a holy place of Zeus, the king of the gods. And every four years, the Olympic Games are held there in his honour.

[long-pause]

The Games are very old. The Greeks believe the first ones were held more than three hundred years before our story.

[pause]

Before the Games, messengers travel all over Greece to announce a sacred truce. All wars between Greek cities must stop, so that athletes and visitors can travel safely to Olympia and home again.

[pause]

{img:herald-announcing-sacred-truce-town-square-crowd-listening}
And the next Games are this very summer, in 432. Thousands of people come. Athletes and trainers, families and merchants, poets and ambassadors. They camp in tents by the river in the summer heat. Athenians and Spartans walk past each other among the tents. For now, the truce holds. But everyone knows that when the Games are over, the truce will end too.

[long-pause]

In the middle of Olympia is a sacred grove with temples, altars and hundreds of statues of winning athletes.

[pause]

There is the great temple of Zeus. And inside it, there will soon be one of the Seven Wonders of the World. It is a huge statue of Zeus on his throne, more than twelve metres high, made of gold and ivory. The sculptor is Phidias, the same man who made the Athena in the Parthenon. He is working on it in these years, in a workshop at Olympia. Archaeologists have found that workshop. They even found a little cup with his name scratched on the bottom: I belong to Phidias.

[pause]

The statue is so big, the Greeks said, that if Zeus stood up, he would go through the roof.

[long-pause]

The Games last five days. There are foot races in the stadium, a long running track with grassy banks where the crowd sits. The most famous race is one length of the track. The winner of that race gives his name to the whole four years until the next Games.

[pause]

There are longer races, and even a race in armour. There is wrestling and boxing. There is the pentathlon, five events in one. And there are horse races and chariot races, with four horses thundering around the track.

[pause]

{img:olympia-stadium-runners-starting-line-crowd-banks-sun}
Only free Greek men may compete. Married women may not even watch. But unmarried girls have their own games at Olympia, with races in honour of Hera, queen of the gods.

[long-pause]

The prize for an Olympic victory is a simple crown of olive leaves, cut from the sacred olive tree behind the temple of Zeus.

[pause]

But the glory is enormous. The winner's name is announced to all of Greece. His home city gives him a hero's welcome. Poets write songs about him. In Athens, an Olympic winner gets free meals from the city for the rest of his life.

[pause]

{img:olive-wreath-crowning-victor-temple-steps-crowd-cheering}
Today, the ruins of Olympia still lie in their quiet valley among the pines. And before each modern Olympic Games, the Olympic flame is lit there from the rays of the sun, with a curved mirror. Then runners carry it all over the world.

[long-pause]

{img:greek-household-altar-courtyard-offering-wine-cakes-family}
The Athenians live with their gods every day.

[pause]

There are the twelve great gods of Mount Olympus. Zeus, the king of the gods, with his thunderbolt. Hera, his wife. Athena, goddess of wisdom and of the city. Apollo, god of music and the sun. Artemis, goddess of hunting and the moon. Poseidon, god of the sea. Aphrodite, goddess of love. Hermes, the messenger. And others besides, like Demeter, goddess of the grain, and Dionysus, god of wine.

[long-pause]

And there are many smaller gods and spirits, in every spring and river and tree and mountain.

[pause]

The Greek gods are very human. They love and quarrel and get jealous. They can be kind, and they can be cruel. The Greeks tell endless stories about them. Children learn them early. They are painted on pots, carved on temples and acted in plays.

[pause]

{img:herm-pillar-street-corner-doorway-hermes-head-flowers}
In every house there is a little altar to Zeus in the courtyard. At the door stands a stone pillar with the head of Hermes, to protect the house. Before every meal, a little food is offered to the gods. Before every drink, a few drops of wine are poured on the ground.

[long-pause]

The Greeks pray standing up, with their arms raised to the sky. They make promises to the gods. If the god helps them, they will give a gift. So the temples are full of gifts. Statues, little figures, painted plaques, and even clay models of arms or legs that a god has healed.

[pause]

The great act of worship is the sacrifice. An animal is offered at the altar outside the temple. The meat is shared among the people in a feast. It is a joyful occasion, with music and garlands and processions.

[pause]

{img:temple-of-asclepius-healing-dreams-sleepers-colonnade-night}
And there are oracles, places where people ask the gods about the future. The most famous is at Delphi, high in the mountains. There, a priestess of Apollo sits in the temple and answers questions in the god's name, in strange and mysterious words. Cities send messengers to ask whether to go to war. Ordinary people ask about marriage, money and illness.

[long-pause]

And in this very year, the Spartans send messengers to Delphi with a question. Should they go to war with Athens? The answer comes back, the old historian says, that if they fight with all their strength, they will win. And that the god himself will help them, whether they ask him or not.

[pause]

When the Athenians hear about that answer, many of them grow quiet.

[pause]

Over the door of the temple at Delphi there are two famous sayings carved in the stone. The thinkers love to talk about them. Know yourself. And: nothing too much.

[long-pause]

{img:herodotus-reading-histories-aloud-crowd-festival-scroll}
In 432, Athens pulls clever people toward it like a magnet. They come from all over the Greek world, because Athens is rich and free, and full of people who love to talk and listen and argue.

[pause]

One of them is Herodotus. He comes from a Greek city on the coast of what is now Turkey. He is about fifty-two. He has travelled more than almost anyone in the Greek world. He has been to Egypt and Babylon, to Persia and far north of the Black Sea.

[long-pause]

And he has written a great book. It is about the wars between the Greeks and the Persians, fifty years before. And it is about all the peoples and wonders he has seen on his travels. It is the first great history book ever written. He calls it his Inquiries. In Greek that word is historiai, and that is where our word history comes from.

[pause]

He begins his book by saying he wrote it so that the great deeds of people would not be forgotten with time.

[pause]

{img:herodotus-traveller-egypt-pyramids-notes-guide-pointing}
Herodotus has read parts of his book aloud in Athens to big audiences, and the Athenians loved it. He is a wonderful storyteller. He writes about the pyramids and the crocodiles of the Nile. He writes about ants that dig for gold, and many other strange things. Some of his stories are true and some are not. He often says: I tell what I was told, but I do not have to believe it.

[long-pause]

There is also a younger Athenian, about thirty, called Thucydides. When the war with Sparta begins, he will write its history. He will write carefully and exactly, without gods or marvels. His book will become one of the greatest history books ever written. He does not know yet what a sad story he will have to tell.

[pause]

On the island of Cos, across the sea, there is a doctor of about thirty called Hippocrates.

[pause]

Hippocrates and his followers are changing medicine. They teach that illnesses have natural causes. Not the anger of the gods, but bad food, bad water, the weather or the way people live. Doctors, they say, must watch their patients carefully and write down what they see. And the body has its own power to heal itself, which the doctor should help with rest, good food and fresh air.

[long-pause]

Doctors who follow Hippocrates take an oath. Doctors still remember it today. They promise to help the sick as well as they can, and never to do harm. They promise to keep their patients' secrets, and to treat rich and poor alike.

[pause]

And there are thinkers who ask questions about the nature of the world.

[pause]

{img:philosopher-anaxagoras-looking-at-sun-moon-sky-students}
Anaxagoras, the teacher of Pericles, taught that the sun is not a god at all, but a huge burning stone. He taught that the moon reflects the light of the sun. For saying this, he was accused of not respecting the gods, and had to leave Athens. In Athens, even in its golden age, a new idea can be dangerous.

[pause]

Another young thinker will soon teach that everything is made of tiny pieces, too small to see, that cannot be cut. He calls them atoms, from the Greek word for uncuttable. More than two thousand years later, scientists will find that he was, in a way, right.

[long-pause]

There are travelling teachers too. They teach rich young men, for money, how to speak well and win arguments in the assembly and the courts. The most famous one says: man is the measure of all things. And about the gods, he says he cannot know whether they exist, because the question is hard and life is short.

[pause]

{img:sophist-teacher-young-men-colonnade-gesturing-debate}
And there are sculptors, builders, painters and musicians. There is even an astronomer who has just worked out a clever calendar. He noticed that nineteen years of the sun fit almost exactly into two hundred and thirty-five months of the moon. That idea is still used today, to work out the date of Easter.

[pause]

So many great minds in one small city, all at the same time. It has hardly ever happened before or since. And none of them knows how little time is left before the war changes everything.

[long-pause]

{img:attic-farm-stone-house-olive-trees-terraces-goats-hills}
In 432, most Athenians do not live in the city. They live in the countryside of Attica, in villages and on farms. They come into the city only for the assembly, the festivals and the markets.

[pause]

The historian Thucydides says that most Athenians had always lived in the country, and loved it. They loved their old houses, their little temples and their way of life.

[long-pause]

A typical farm is small. There are a few fields of barley and wheat, on the plain or on terraces on the hillsides. The terraces have stone walls, so the rain does not wash the soil away. There is a vineyard, an olive grove and a few fig trees. There are sheep and goats, chickens, and an ox or a donkey to plough and carry loads.

[pause]

The farmhouse is made of stone and mud brick, with a courtyard and storerooms. Outside there is a threshing floor. It is a round, flat, paved space, where animals walk round and round over the grain to loosen it.

[pause]

{img:threshing-floor-mules-circling-grain-farmer-winnowing-summer}
The farmer works hard all year with his family, and perhaps a slave or two. An old poet called Hesiod wrote a famous poem about a farmer's year. Every Greek knows it.

[long-pause]

In autumn, when the cranes fly south crying high in the sky, it is time to plough and sow. In winter, the farmer stays by the fire and mends his tools. In spring, when the swallows return, he digs around his vines. In early summer, when the snails climb up the plants to escape the heat, it is time to harvest the grain.

[pause]

In the hottest days, when the cicadas sing, the farmer may rest in the shade of a rock. He drinks wine and eats bread and cheese, with his face turned toward the cool west wind.

[pause]

And in September comes the grape harvest. The grapes are picked, carried in baskets and trodden by foot in big tubs, to the music of flutes. The juice is poured into great jars to become wine.

[long-pause]

In late autumn and winter, the olives are harvested. They are knocked from the trees with long sticks, gathered from the ground, and taken to the press to be crushed for oil.

[pause]

Olive oil is the most important thing Attica makes. It is used for cooking and eating, for lamps, for washing and for medicine. It is sold all over the Mediterranean in painted jars.

[pause]

{img:olive-harvest-sticks-nets-family-trees-winter-light}
Olive trees grow slowly. A young tree takes many years to give fruit, and it can live for hundreds of years. So the olive groves are precious. They are passed down from father to son, for generations. A farmer's olive trees are his family's whole future. That is why, if an enemy ever cut them down, it would be the cruellest blow of all.

[long-pause]

The countryside has its own festivals. In winter, the villages hold processions with songs and dances. There is a game where young men try to stand on a greasy, blown-up wineskin, and everyone laughs when they fall off.

[pause]

And there are old country shrines. There are caves for the nymphs, near springs. There are shrines to Pan, the god of shepherds, with his pipes. And every autumn, thousands of people walk in procession from Athens to the town of Eleusis, for a great secret ceremony. It promises a happy life after death. Nobody ever told what happened there. The secret has been kept for two and a half thousand years.

[pause]

{img:shrine-of-nymphs-cave-spring-offerings-shepherd-goats}
In 432, the countryside of Attica is beautiful and peaceful. But very soon, the farmers will have to leave it all behind.

[long-pause]

{img:sparta-plain-eurotas-river-mountains-taygetus-hoplites-drilling}
In the summer of 432, the peace of Greece is coming to an end.

[pause]

For fifteen years, there has been a peace treaty between Athens and Sparta, the two great powers of Greece. But Sparta is afraid of Athens. It fears its growing power, its navy and its empire. And Athens has quarrelled with Corinth, a friend of Sparta, over two other cities.

[long-pause]

Sparta is very different from Athens. It is a city of soldiers in the south of Greece, in a green valley below high mountains. Sparta has no walls. The Spartans say their soldiers are their walls. Every Spartan man is trained as a soldier from the age of seven, to be tough, brave and obedient. The Spartans do not build great temples or write plays. Their land is farmed for them by a whole people kept in a kind of slavery.

[pause]

Spartan women are freer than Athenian women. They exercise and run, they own land, and they speak their minds.

[pause]

In the summer of 432, Sparta's friends meet at Sparta. They accuse Athens of breaking the peace. And the Spartans vote that the peace is broken. The historian Thucydides says the real cause of the war was simple. Athens was growing stronger, and Sparta was afraid.

[long-pause]

In Athens, Pericles persuades the assembly not to give in to Sparta.

[pause]

He has a plan. Sparta has the best army on land. Athens has the best navy. So, Pericles says, if the Spartans march into Attica, the Athenians should not fight them on land. Everyone from the countryside should come inside the walls of Athens. Let the Spartans burn the farms. The city will be fed by its ships. The navy will raid the coasts of Sparta's lands. And in the end, the Spartans will grow tired, and make peace.

[pause]

{img:country-people-moving-into-city-carts-belongings-long-walls}
In the spring of 431, the war begins. The Spartans march into Attica. And the country people sadly leave their farms and homes and little temples. They bring their families and their furniture into the city, and even the wooden doors of their houses. They send their sheep and cattle across the water to an island. They camp wherever they can, in temples, between the long walls and in the towers.

[long-pause]

At the end of the first year of the war, Pericles gives a famous speech at the funeral of the Athenians who have fallen. Thucydides wrote it down, as well as he could remember it. It is one of the most famous speeches in history.

[pause]

In it, Pericles talks about Athens and what it means. Power belongs not to a few, but to the many. Everyone is equal before the law. People are chosen for public work because of their ability, not their wealth or their family. Athenians live freely and do not mind if their neighbours enjoy themselves in their own way. They love beauty and wisdom. Athens, he says again, is the school of Greece.

[pause]

{img:funeral-oration-pericles-speaking-crowd-cypress-coffins-winter}
People who believe in freedom and democracy have quoted these words ever since.

[long-pause]

Then, in the second summer of the war, a terrible sickness comes to Athens. Perhaps it arrived on a ship. It spreads quickly through the crowded city, full of people from the countryside.

[pause]

We will not describe it. It is enough to say that very many people were lost, perhaps a quarter or a third of the people of Athens. Thucydides himself fell ill and recovered. He wrote down exactly what he saw, so that people would know it if it ever came again.

[pause]

In the autumn of 429, Pericles himself fell ill and died. His two older sons had died before him. His son with Aspasia survived.

[long-pause]

The war between Athens and Sparta lasted, with a few short pauses, for twenty-seven years. And in the end, Athens lost. Its fleet was destroyed, its walls were pulled down and its empire was gone.

[pause]

But Athens survived. A year later, its democracy came back. And its great age of art and thought went on for many years. Plato and Aristotle were still to come.

[pause]

{img:athens-rebuilding-walls-citizens-working-hopeful-morning}
And the Parthenon still stood on the Acropolis, white and gold above the city, as it does today.

[long-pause]

{img:acropolis-today-parthenon-ruins-blue-sky-athens-city-around}
Today, Athens is a big modern city, the capital of Greece, with more than three million people. It spreads across the whole plain of Attica, from the mountains to the sea.

[pause]

And in the middle of it all, the Acropolis still rises, as it did in 432. On top stands the Parthenon, its columns shining golden white in the sunshine.

[long-pause]

The Parthenon is a ruin now. Over two and a half thousand years it has been a temple, a church and a mosque. In 1687, during a war, it was being used to store gunpowder. A cannonball struck it, and the explosion blew out the middle of the temple. Many of its sculptures were later taken to London, and they are now in the British Museum. The Greeks would very much like them back.

[pause]

But the building still stands, with its columns and its gentle curves. Every year, millions of people from all over the world climb the old path through the gateway to see it.

[pause]

{img:acropolis-museum-glass-floor-frieze-gallery-light}
At the foot of the Acropolis there is a new museum with great glass walls and glass floors. Through the floors you can see the ruins of the old city below. On the top floor, the great carved procession of the Parthenon is laid out in its old order. You can walk all around it, with the real Parthenon just outside the windows.

[long-pause]

The Agora is still there below the Acropolis. It is a peaceful park of ruins, trees and grass, where tortoises wander among the stones. The temple of Hephaestus still stands on its hill, almost complete. And one of the long covered buildings has been rebuilt, and is now the museum of the Agora.

[pause]

In it you can see things found there. The pottery pieces with famous names scratched on them. The stone machine for choosing jurors. A water clock for timing speeches. Children's toys, cooking pots, lamps and coins from everyday life.

[pause]

The theatre of Dionysus is still there on the slope of the Acropolis, with its stone seats. Nearby is another old theatre, where on summer evenings, ancient Greek plays are still performed under the stars, with the Acropolis lit up above.

[long-pause]

On the hill where the assembly met, you can still see the stone speaker's platform cut into the rock. It is a quiet place with pine trees and a view of the Acropolis. You can stand where Pericles stood, and imagine six thousand citizens on the slope, raising their hands to vote.

[pause]

And from the Piraeus, ferries still sail out every day to the islands, past the old harbours where the warships once lay.

[pause]

{img:pnyx-hill-pine-trees-acropolis-view-sunset-quiet}
In the evening, when the sun goes down, the marble of the Parthenon turns golden, then pink, then soft grey. The lights of the city come on across the plain. And the Acropolis glows above the city, as it has for two and a half thousand years.

[long-pause]

So we leave Athens tonight, in its last peaceful summer, with the new temple shining on its rock, and the people talking in the Agora as the evening comes.

[pause]

The potter at his wheel, the farmer in his olive grove, the barefoot thinker with his questions, and a city that tried, for the first time, to let its people rule themselves.

[pause]

{img:parthenon-lit-at-night-athens-lights-below-calm}
If you enjoyed this evening in ancient Greece, you are very welcome to subscribe to the channel, so that you can find the next quiet story.

[pause]

There should be another quiet story waiting on the screen now, if you are still awake.
