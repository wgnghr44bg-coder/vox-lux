Let us spend an ordinary day in Athens in the summer of 432 before Christ, with an ordinary family.

The father is a citizen and a potter. He has a little workshop in the potters' quarter, near the northwest gate of the city. He has a wife, three children and two slaves, who help in the workshop and the house.

--

The day begins at dawn. Athenians get up at first light, to make the most of the cool morning before the heat.

The father washes his face in cold water from a jar. He does not shave, because Athenian men wear beards. He puts on his tunic. It is a simple piece of wool or linen, pinned at the shoulders and tied at the waist. If it is cool, he wraps a big woollen cloak around himself as well.

{img:greek-man-wrapping-himation-cloak-courtyard-morning-light}
Breakfast is simple. A piece of barley bread dipped in watered wine, and perhaps a few figs or olives. Then he goes to work.

--

In the workshop, the potters are already busy.

Athenian pottery is famous all over the Mediterranean. The pots are made of fine red clay, shaped on a wheel that a boy turns by hand. They are painted with a special black glaze. When they are fired in the kiln, the clay turns red and the glaze turns shiny black.

The painters leave the figures red and paint the background black. Then they draw in the details with very fine lines, using a brush sometimes made of a single hair. The pictures show gods and heroes, athletes and women at the fountain, and everyday life. Many pots are signed by their maker. And they are sold far away, in Italy, by the Black Sea and in Egypt.

But lately, the potter has heard worrying talk in the market. If war comes, will the ships still sail? Will his pots still reach their buyers? He keeps working, and tries not to think about it.

--

At home, his wife runs the household.

She tells the slave woman what to clean and cook. Water must be fetched from the fountain in a jar carried on the head. She spins wool, and weaves cloth on a tall loom in the courtyard, for the family's clothes and blankets.

{img:woman-upright-loom-weaving-wool-courtyard-daughter-spinning}
The little children play in the courtyard with clay dolls that have moving arms and legs, little carts, spinning tops and balls. Some have a pet bird or a tortoise. The older son goes to school.

--

At midday the family eats a light meal. In the heat of the early afternoon, everyone rests in the shade. The streets are empty and quiet, except for the cicadas.

Later, the father finishes work and goes to the sports ground to exercise and wash and talk with his friends. Or he goes to the Agora for the news. Or, if it is a day of the assembly, he goes to vote.

{img:athens-street-afternoon-quiet-heat-shadows-cat-doorway}
In the evening, as the air cools, the family eats its main meal together. There is barley porridge or bread, vegetables and lentils, cheese and olives. On good days there is a little fish. Meat is rare. It is mostly eaten at festivals, after a sacrifice.

--

When darkness falls, the little oil lamps are lit, and the family sits in the courtyard under the stars. The father tells stories about Theseus, the hero of Athens, who killed the Minotaur. And about the battle of Marathon, where their grandfather fought.

Then they go to bed on simple wooden beds with mattresses of wool. In the hottest weather, some sleep on the flat roof under the stars. And Athens lies quiet under the moon, with the great white temple glowing faintly above the sleeping city.
