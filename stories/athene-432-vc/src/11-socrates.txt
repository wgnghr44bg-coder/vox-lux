In 432 before Christ, Socrates is about thirty-seven years old.

He is the son of a stonemason, Sophroniscus, and a midwife, Phaenarete. He was trained as a stonemason himself, as a young man, and some people said that he carved a statue of the Graces, which stood at the entrance to the Acropolis. But he has given up his trade, and spends his days, every day, talking.

--

He is very ugly, by the standards of the Greeks, who love beauty. He has a snub nose, and thick lips, and bulging eyes, and a big belly, and he walks with a strange, rolling walk, like a pelican, his friends say, looking from side to side. He walks barefoot, summer and winter, and wears the same old, worn cloak, always. He is poor, and does not care.

But he has something that draws people to him, like a magnet. His friends say that he is like those little statues of Silenus, ugly satyrs, which are sold in the market, and which open up, and inside, there are little golden figures of the gods.

Socrates goes to the Agora, and the gymnasia, and the workshops, and the parties, and talks to everyone: politicians, and poets, and craftsmen, and generals, and young men, and old men, rich and poor. And he asks them questions.

--

He asks them about things they think they know. What is courage? What is justice? What is beauty? What is a good life? What is friendship? What is virtue, and can it be taught?

And when they answer, he asks them more questions, gently, politely, about their answers. And slowly, step by step, he shows them that they do not really know what they thought they knew. That their answers are confused, or contradict each other. And then he says: I do not know either. Let us look for the answer together.

{img:philosopher-walking-with-young-men-path-plane-trees-river}
This is called the Socratic method. It is still used today, by teachers, all over the world.

--

Socrates says that he knows only one thing: that he knows nothing. And that, perhaps, this makes him a little wiser than other people, who think they know things that they do not.

He says that the most important thing in life is not money, or fame, or power, but to take care of your soul, to try to become a good person, and to understand what goodness is. And he says: the unexamined life is not worth living.

{img:socrates-standing-still-thinking-dawn-soldiers-camp-watching}
Socrates is also a brave soldier. He has fought for Athens, as a hoplite. And very soon, in 432, he will go to war, at Potidaea, in the north of Greece. There, his friends will tell later, he walked barefoot over the ice in the bitter winter, and once stood still, thinking about a problem, from one dawn to the next, while the other soldiers watched him in wonder. And in the battle, he saved the life of a young man, Alcibiades.

--

Socrates never writes anything down. He believes that wisdom is found in conversation, between living people, not in books. Everything we know about him comes from his friends and his students, who wrote about him after his death: above all Plato, and Xenophon.

He has a wife, Xanthippe, who, the stories say, is quick-tempered, and scolds him, because he does not earn any money, and is always out, talking. Socrates says, jokingly, that if he can live with her, he can get along with anyone.

Many young men of Athens love him, and follow him around, and listen to him, and try to argue with him. Their fathers, sometimes, are less pleased. Because Socrates teaches the young to question everything, even the laws, and the gods, and the customs of their city.

--

And that, in the end, will cost him his life. More than thirty years later, in 399, when he is seventy, the Athenians will put him on trial, for not believing in the gods of the city, and for corrupting the young. And the jury will condemn him.

He could escape, but he refuses, because he respects the laws of Athens. And he spends his last day talking with his friends, calmly, about the soul, and what happens after death, and then drinks the cup of hemlock, and passes away, peacefully, among his friends.

{img:quiet-evening-prison-room-friends-gathered-calm-light}
But in 432, all that is far in the future. He is a man in the prime of life, walking barefoot through the Agora, in the morning sunshine, looking for someone to talk to.
