In the house of Pericles, there lives a remarkable woman. Her name is Aspasia.

She is not from Athens. She comes from Miletus, a great Greek city on the coast of what is now Turkey, famous for its philosophers, and its learning. She is about thirty-eight, in 432. She is clever, and well educated, and witty, and beautiful, and she speaks well, better than most men, people say.

--

Pericles divorced his first wife, many years before, and has been living with Aspasia for about twelve years. They cannot marry, under the law of Athens, because she is not an Athenian citizen. But they love each other, and live together, as husband and wife. Plutarch says that Pericles kissed her every morning, when he left the house, and every evening, when he came home, which the Athenians found very surprising.

They have a son, also called Pericles.

{img:greek-house-doorway-morning-man-leaving-woman-farewell}
Aspasia is unusual, in Athens. Respectable Athenian women, in 432, live very quiet lives, mostly at home, and do not take part in public life, or talk with men who are not their relatives. But Aspasia talks with the most famous men of Athens, philosophers, and politicians, and artists, who come to the house of Pericles. Socrates himself, it is said, came to talk with her, and admired her, and said that she had taught him about rhetoric, and about love.

--

The comic poets make cruel jokes about her, in their plays, and call her names. Her enemies say that she controls Pericles, and makes him go to war. Some of them even take her to court, accusing her of not respecting the gods. Pericles defends her himself, in court, and weeps, it is said, the only time anyone ever saw him weep, and she is acquitted.

We do not know very much about her for certain. None of her own words have survived. But she is remembered, more than two thousand years later, as one of the most remarkable women of the ancient world.

What is life like for other women in Athens?

--

An Athenian girl, of a citizen family, is brought up at home, by her mother, and the household slaves. She learns to spin, and weave, and cook, and run a household. Some learn to read, a little. She is married young, at fourteen or fifteen, to a husband chosen by her father, often a man of thirty.

After marriage, she runs her husband's household: the slaves, and the stores, and the cooking, and the spinning and weaving of the family's clothes, and the care of the children. She spends most of her time at home, in the women's rooms, at the back, or upstairs. When her husband's friends come to dinner, she does not join them.

{img:greek-women-fountain-house-water-jars-chatting-morning}
But women do go out. They visit their relatives, and their friends. They go to the fountain houses, to fetch water, and talk. They go to funerals, and weddings. And above all, they take part in the great religious festivals of the city: the processions, and the sacrifices, and the dances, and the women's festivals, like the Thesmophoria, in honour of the goddess Demeter, from which men are excluded.

--

And poorer women, of course, must work: in the market, selling bread, and vegetables, and ribbons, and perfume. As nurses, and midwives, and washerwomen, and wool workers. Many of them are widows, or foreigners, or freed slaves.

The great playwright Euripides, in one of his plays, Medea, which will be performed the very next year, in 431, gives a woman a famous speech about the hard life of women: that they must buy a husband, with a dowry, and take him as master of their body, and that they would rather stand three times in the battle line than give birth once.

The audience, in the theatre of Dionysus, most of them men, will listen in silence. And some of them, perhaps, will think about it, afterwards.
