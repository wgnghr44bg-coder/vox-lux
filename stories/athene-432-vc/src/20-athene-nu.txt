Today, Athens is a great modern city, the capital of Greece, with more than three million people, spreading out across the whole plain of Attica, from the mountains to the sea.

And in the middle of it all, the Acropolis still rises, as it did in 432, with the Parthenon on top, its columns shining, golden white, in the sunshine.

--

The Parthenon is a ruin now. Over two and a half thousand years, it has been a temple of Athena, and a Christian church, and a mosque. In 1687, during a war between Venice and the Ottoman Turks, it was being used to store gunpowder, and a Venetian cannonball struck it, and the gunpowder exploded, and blew out the middle of the temple. Many of its sculptures were later taken to London, by Lord Elgin, in the early nineteenth century, and are in the British Museum, where the Greeks would very much like them back.

But the building still stands, with its columns, and its gentle curves, and every year, millions of people from all over the world climb up the old path, through the gateway of the Propylaea, to see it.

{img:acropolis-museum-glass-floor-frieze-gallery-light}
At the foot of the Acropolis, there is a new museum, the Acropolis Museum, opened in 2009, with great glass walls, and glass floors, through which you can see the ruins of the ancient city below. On the top floor, the frieze of the Parthenon is laid out, the parts that remain in Athens, and copies of the parts in London, in the same order as on the temple, so that you can walk around it, and see the great procession, the horsemen, and the young women, and the gods, with the real Parthenon, outside the windows, beyond.

--

The Agora is still there, below the Acropolis, a peaceful park, of ruins, and trees, and grass, where tortoises wander among the stones. The temple of Hephaestus, on its hill, is still standing, almost complete, with its roof. And the Stoa of Attalos, a great colonnade, built two hundred years after 432, has been rebuilt, by American archaeologists, as it was, and is now the museum of the Agora.

In it, you can see the things found in the Agora: the ostraka, with the names of famous Athenians, scratched on them. The kleroterion, the machine for choosing jurors. A water clock, for timing speeches in the courts. Little bronze ballots. Children's toys. And cooking pots, and lamps, and coins, from the everyday life of the people.

The theatre of Dionysus is still there too, on the south slope of the Acropolis, with its stone seats, built a century after 432, and the marble thrones of the priests, in the front row. And nearby, there is another theatre, the Odeon of Herodes Atticus, built by the Romans, where, on summer evenings, ancient Greek plays are still performed, under the stars, with the Acropolis lit up above.

--

On the hill of the Pnyx, where the assembly met, you can still see the stone speaker's platform, cut into the rock. It is a quiet place, with pine trees, and a view of the Acropolis, where few tourists come. You can stand there, where Pericles stood, and imagine the six thousand citizens, sitting on the slope, raising their hands to vote.

And at the Piraeus, the ferries still sail out, every day, to the islands, past the old harbours where the triremes once lay.

{img:pnyx-hill-pine-trees-acropolis-view-sunset-quiet}
And in the evening, when the sun goes down, the marble of the Parthenon turns golden, and then pink, and then soft grey, and the lights of the city come on, all across the plain, and the Acropolis is lit up, glowing, above the city, as it has for two and a half thousand years.

--

So we leave Athens tonight, in its last peaceful summer, with the new temple shining on its rock, and the people talking in the Agora as the evening comes.

The potter at his wheel, the farmer in his olive grove, the barefoot philosopher with his questions, and a city that tried, for the first time, to let its people rule themselves.
