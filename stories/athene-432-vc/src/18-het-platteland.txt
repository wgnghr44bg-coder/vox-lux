In 432, most Athenians do not live in the city. They live in the countryside of Attica, in villages and on farms. They come into the city only for the assembly, the festivals and the markets.

The historian Thucydides says that most Athenians had always lived in the country, and loved it. They loved their old houses, their little temples and their way of life.

--

A typical farm is small. There are a few fields of barley and wheat, on the plain or on terraces on the hillsides. The terraces have stone walls, so the rain does not wash the soil away. There is a vineyard, an olive grove and a few fig trees. There are sheep and goats, chickens, and an ox or a donkey to plough and carry loads.

The farmhouse is made of stone and mud brick, with a courtyard and storerooms. Outside there is a threshing floor. It is a round, flat, paved space, where animals walk round and round over the grain to loosen it.

{img:threshing-floor-mules-circling-grain-farmer-winnowing-summer}
The farmer works hard all year with his family, and perhaps a slave or two. An old poet called Hesiod wrote a famous poem about a farmer's year. Every Greek knows it.

--

In autumn, when the cranes fly south crying high in the sky, it is time to plough and sow. In winter, the farmer stays by the fire and mends his tools. In spring, when the swallows return, he digs around his vines. In early summer, when the snails climb up the plants to escape the heat, it is time to harvest the grain.

In the hottest days, when the cicadas sing, the farmer may rest in the shade of a rock. He drinks wine and eats bread and cheese, with his face turned toward the cool west wind.

And in September comes the grape harvest. The grapes are picked, carried in baskets and trodden by foot in big tubs, to the music of flutes. The juice is poured into great jars to become wine.

--

In late autumn and winter, the olives are harvested. They are knocked from the trees with long sticks, gathered from the ground, and taken to the press to be crushed for oil.

Olive oil is the most important thing Attica makes. It is used for cooking and eating, for lamps, for washing and for medicine. It is sold all over the Mediterranean in painted jars.

{img:olive-harvest-sticks-nets-family-trees-winter-light}
Olive trees grow slowly. A young tree takes many years to give fruit, and it can live for hundreds of years. So the olive groves are precious. They are passed down from father to son, for generations. A farmer's olive trees are his family's whole future. That is why, if an enemy ever cut them down, it would be the cruellest blow of all.

--

The countryside has its own festivals. In winter, the villages hold processions with songs and dances. There is a game where young men try to stand on a greasy, blown-up wineskin, and everyone laughs when they fall off.

And there are old country shrines. There are caves for the nymphs, near springs. There are shrines to Pan, the god of shepherds, with his pipes. And every autumn, thousands of people walk in procession from Athens to the town of Eleusis, for a great secret ceremony. It promises a happy life after death. Nobody ever told what happened there. The secret has been kept for two and a half thousand years.

{img:shrine-of-nymphs-cave-spring-offerings-shepherd-goats}
In 432, the countryside of Attica is beautiful and peaceful. But very soon, the farmers will have to leave it all behind.
