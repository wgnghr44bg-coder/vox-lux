Most Athenians, in 432, do not live in the city. They live in the countryside of Attica, in the villages, and on their farms, and they come into the city only for the assembly, and the festivals, and the markets.

The historian Thucydides says that, before the war, most Athenians had always lived in the country, and loved it, and their old houses, and their temples, and their way of life there.

--

A typical Athenian farm is small: a few fields, of barley and wheat, on the plain, or on terraces, on the hillsides, built with walls of stone, to stop the soil being washed away. A vineyard. An olive grove. A few fig trees. A vegetable garden. Some sheep, and goats, and pigs, and chickens, and an ox, or a donkey, or a mule, to plough, and to carry loads.

The farmhouse is built of stone, and mud brick, with a courtyard, and a tower, sometimes, for safety, and storerooms, and a threshing floor, a round, flat, paved area, outside, where the grain is threshed, by animals walking round and round on it.

{img:threshing-floor-mules-circling-grain-farmer-winnowing-summer}
The farmer works hard, all year round, with his family, and perhaps a slave or two. A farmer's year is described by an old Greek poet, Hesiod, who lived three hundred years before, in a poem called Works and Days, which every Greek knows.

--

In the autumn, when the cranes fly south, crying, high in the sky, it is time to plough, and sow the barley and the wheat. In the winter, the farmer stays by the fire, and mends his tools, and prunes his vines. In the spring, when the swallows come back, he digs around the vines. In early summer, when the snail climbs up the plants, to escape the heat, it is time to harvest the grain, with sickles, and thresh it, and store it.

In the hottest days of summer, when the thistle blooms, and the cicada sings in the trees, the farmer may rest, in the shade of a rock, and drink wine, and eat bread and milk, and goat's meat, with his face turned to the cool west wind.

And in September, there is the grape harvest, the vintage. The grapes are picked, and carried in baskets, and trodden by foot, in great vats, to the music of pipes, and the juice is poured into great jars, to become wine.

--

And in the late autumn and winter, the olives are harvested: beaten from the trees with long sticks, and gathered from the ground, and taken to the press, where they are crushed, and the oil is squeezed out.

Olive oil is the most important product of Attica. It is used for everything: for cooking, and for eating, with bread, and for lamps, to give light, and for washing, and for the skin, after exercise, and for medicines, and perfumes. And it is sold, all over the Mediterranean, in painted jars.

{img:olive-harvest-sticks-nets-family-trees-winter-light}
The olive trees grow slowly. A new tree takes many years to give fruit, and lives for hundreds of years. So the olive groves are precious, and passed down from father to son, for generations.

--

In the country, there are festivals too: the Rural Dionysia, in the winter, in the villages, with processions, and songs, and dances, and plays, and a game in which young men try to stand on a greasy, inflated wineskin, and everyone laughs when they fall off.

And there are the old shrines of the country: of the nymphs, in caves, by springs. Of Pan, the god of shepherds, with his pipes. Of Artemis, the goddess of wild things, and hunting. Of Demeter, the goddess of the grain, and her daughter, Persephone, at Eleusis, where, every autumn, thousands of people walk in procession from Athens, along the Sacred Way, to be initiated into the Mysteries, the great secret ceremony, which promises a happy life after death. Nobody ever revealed what happened there. The secret has been kept, for two and a half thousand years.

{img:shrine-of-nymphs-cave-spring-offerings-shepherd-goats}
The countryside of Attica is beautiful, and peaceful, in 432. But very soon, the farmers will have to leave it.
