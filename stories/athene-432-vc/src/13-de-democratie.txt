About forty times a year, early in the morning, the citizens of Athens climb a hill west of the Acropolis, for a meeting of the assembly.

The hill is a wide slope with a stone platform at the bottom, where the speakers stand. The citizens sit on the slope, on the ground or on cushions they have brought. From the top they can see the Parthenon, shining in the morning sun.

--

Any male citizen over eighteen may come, speak and vote. About six thousand usually come. The meeting begins at dawn with a prayer, and goes on until the business is done, usually by midday.

Before the meeting, the city police go around the market with a rope dipped in red paint, and herd the citizens toward the hill. Anyone caught with red paint on his clothes, who has not gone to the assembly, must pay a fine.

{img:scythian-archers-red-rope-herding-citizens-agora-morning}
A herald calls out: who wishes to speak? Anyone may stand up, walk to the platform, put a wreath on his head, and speak to the people. Usually the speakers are experienced men like Pericles. But anyone may try. If a speaker is boring or foolish, the crowd shouts him down and laughs.

--

The assembly decides everything. War or peace, laws and taxes, buildings and ambassadors. And in 432, there is one question that comes up again and again. What to do about Sparta?

They vote by raising their hands. Officials look across the hillside and judge which side has more. For some important matters, they vote with pebbles dropped into jars.

The business of the assembly is prepared by a council of five hundred citizens. They are not elected. They are chosen by lot, drawn by chance, like a lottery. The Athenians think this is the fairest way, because it gives every citizen an equal chance. Most officials are chosen by lot too. Only the generals, and a few others who need special skills, are elected.

--

There are law courts too, with huge juries of hundreds of citizens, chosen by lot each morning. There are no professional judges and no lawyers. Each side speaks for itself, for a fixed time. The time is measured by a water clock, which is a pot with a small hole that slowly lets the water out. Then the jury votes straight away.

To choose the jurors, the Athenians use a kind of stone machine. Citizens slot their little bronze name tickets into rows of holes. Then black and white balls are dropped down a tube at random, to decide which rows will serve. Archaeologists have found some of these machines.

{img:kleroterion-stone-slots-bronze-tickets-allotment-machine-citizens}
And there is ostracism. Once a year, the assembly can decide to send one man away for ten years, if he seems to be getting too powerful. Each citizen scratches a name on a broken piece of pottery. If enough votes are cast, the man with the most votes must leave Athens for ten years. He keeps his property, and may come back afterwards.

--

Archaeologists have found thousands of these pottery pieces, with famous names scratched on them. Even the name of Pericles.

There is a famous story about a man called Aristides. Everyone called him Aristides the Just, because he was so honest. At an ostracism, a farmer who could not write, and did not know him, asked him to scratch a name on his piece of pottery: Aristides. Aristides asked what harm Aristides had ever done to him. None, said the farmer. I do not even know him. I am just tired of everyone calling him the Just. And Aristides wrote his own name, and handed it back.

{img:broken-pottery-sherds-names-scratched-ostraka-museum-light}
The democracy of Athens has many faults. It leaves out women, slaves and foreigners. Sometimes the assembly makes rash or cruel choices, swayed by clever speakers. But it is the first great experiment in government by ordinary people. And soon it will face its hardest test of all: whether a city ruled by its people can survive a long war.
