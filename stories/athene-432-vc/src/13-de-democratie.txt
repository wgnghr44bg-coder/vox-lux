About forty times a year, early in the morning, the citizens of Athens climb up a hill, west of the Acropolis, called the Pnyx, for a meeting of the assembly, the ekklesia.

The Pnyx is a great sloping hillside, with a platform of stone at the bottom, called the bema, where the speakers stand. The citizens sit on the slope, on the ground, or on cushions they have brought, facing the speakers. From the top of the hill, they can see the Acropolis, and the Parthenon, shining in the morning sun.

--

Any male citizen over eighteen may come, and speak, and vote. About six thousand usually come. The meeting begins at dawn, with a prayer, and a sacrifice of a little pig, and goes on until the business is finished, usually by midday.

Scythian archers, the city police, go around the Agora, before the meeting, with a rope dipped in red paint, and herd the citizens toward the Pnyx. Anyone caught with red paint on his clothes, who has not gone to the assembly, has to pay a fine.

{img:scythian-archers-red-rope-herding-citizens-agora-morning}
The herald calls out: who wishes to speak? And anyone may stand up, go to the bema, put a wreath of myrtle on his head, as a sign that he is speaking, and address the people. Usually, the speakers are the politicians, the experienced men, like Pericles. But anyone may speak. If a speaker is boring, or foolish, the crowd shouts him down, and laughs, and the archers lead him away.

--

The assembly decides everything: whether to go to war, or make peace. Which laws to make. How much tax to raise. Which buildings to build. Who will be sent as ambassadors. What to do with allies who rebel.

They vote by raising their hands. The officials look at the hillside, and judge which side has more hands. On some important matters, they vote with pebbles, or little bronze discs, dropped into urns.

The business of the assembly is prepared by the council, the boule, of five hundred citizens, fifty from each of the ten tribes of Athens, chosen by lot, for one year. Chosen by lot: not elected, but drawn by chance, like a lottery. The Athenians believe that this is the most democratic way, because it gives every citizen an equal chance. Most officials of the city are chosen by lot too. Only the generals, and a few others, who need special skills, are elected.

--

There are law courts too, with juries, huge juries, of two hundred, or five hundred, or even a thousand, or more citizens, chosen by lot, every morning, from those who have volunteered. There are no professional judges, and no lawyers. Each side speaks for itself, for a fixed time, measured by a water clock: a pot with a hole in it, through which the water runs out. Then the jury votes, at once, without discussion, by dropping little bronze ballots into urns.

To choose the jurors, the Athenians use a machine, called a kleroterion: a slab of stone, with rows of slots, into which the citizens put their little bronze identity tickets, and a tube, down which black and white balls are dropped, at random, to choose which rows will serve. Some of these machines have been found, by archaeologists, in the Agora.

{img:kleroterion-stone-slots-bronze-tickets-allotment-machine-citizens}
And there is ostracism. Once a year, the assembly may decide to hold an ostracism, to send one man into exile, for ten years, if he is becoming too powerful, and might become a tyrant. Each citizen scratches the name of the man he wants to send away, on a broken piece of pottery, called an ostrakon. If more than six thousand votes are cast, the man with the most votes must leave Athens, for ten years. He keeps his property, and can come back afterwards.

--

Archaeologists have found thousands of these ostraka, in the Agora, with names scratched on them: Themistocles, and Aristides, and Cimon, and Pericles himself, although he was never ostracized. Some of them, it seems, were written in advance, by the same hand, for voters who could not write.

There is a famous story about Aristides, called the Just, because he was so honest. At an ostracism, an illiterate farmer, who did not recognize him, asked him to write a name on his ostrakon for him: Aristides. Aristides asked him what harm Aristides had ever done to him. None, said the farmer. I do not even know him. But I am tired of hearing everybody call him the Just. And Aristides wrote his own name on the potsherd, and gave it back.

{img:broken-pottery-sherds-names-scratched-ostraka-museum-light}
The democracy of Athens has many faults. It excludes women, and slaves, and foreigners. Sometimes the assembly makes rash, cruel, or foolish decisions, swayed by clever speakers. But it is the first great experiment in government by ordinary people, and it has inspired people who believe in freedom, all over the world, ever since.
