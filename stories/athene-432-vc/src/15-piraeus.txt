Seven kilometres from the city, along the road between the long walls, lies the port of Athens. It is called the Piraeus.

It is a busy, noisy town built around three natural harbours. About twenty years ago it was laid out by a famous planner, with straight streets crossing each other in a neat grid. It was one of the first towns of its kind in Greece.

--

The biggest harbour is for trade. It is full of merchant ships from all over the Mediterranean and the Black Sea. They bring grain from the Black Sea, Egypt and Sicily, because Attica cannot grow enough to feed all its people. They bring timber for shipbuilding, and fish, wine, papyrus, spices and carpets.

Pericles says that because Athens is so great, the goods of the whole world flow into the city. The Athenians enjoy foreign things as easily as their own.

{img:merchants-unloading-grain-amphoras-quay-piraeus-porters}
Along the quays there are warehouses and a great market hall. Bankers sit at their tables changing money. There are taverns for the sailors. And there are people from everywhere, speaking every language.

But there is a worry hidden in all this. Athens needs that grain from far away. If an enemy ever cut off the ships, the city would go hungry. That is why the navy matters so much.

--

The other two harbours belong to the navy.

Around them stand hundreds of ship sheds. They are long, narrow buildings with sloping floors running down into the water. The warships are pulled up into them when they are not at sea, so their wooden hulls stay dry and do not rot.

The warships are called triremes. They are long, slim and fast, about thirty-seven metres long but only five or six metres wide. They have a square sail for cruising. But in battle they are rowed by a hundred and seventy rowers, sitting in three rows, one above the other. At the front, at the waterline, there is a heavy bronze ram for smashing into enemy ships. And on each side of the bow there is a painted eye, so the ship can see its way.

--

The rowers are not slaves. They are free men, mostly poor citizens of Athens, and they are paid a day's wage. A flute player plays a steady beat, so they all pull together. A well-rowed trireme can go as fast as a running man, and turn very quickly.

Because the navy depends on these poor citizens, they have great power in the assembly. That is one reason why Athens is a democracy. The rich fight on land. But the poor row the ships that make Athens great.

{img:rowers-benches-trireme-piper-rhythm-pulling-oars-together}
Every year, from spring to autumn, the fleet goes out to patrol the seas, protect the merchant ships and show the world the power of Athens. This year, the captains check their ships more carefully than usual.

--

In 1987, Greek and British scientists and sailors built a full-size copy of an Athenian trireme. They rowed it on the sea with volunteers, to see how it worked. It went very fast and turned very quickly, just as the old writers said. It is now kept in a museum near the Piraeus.

{img:evening-piraeus-harbour-ships-moored-lamps-quiet-water}
In the evening, the sailors come ashore. They eat fish and drink wine in the taverns, and tell stories of the sea and the islands. The ships rock gently in the harbour, and the sun sets behind the island of Salamis.
