In 432, Athens pulls clever people toward it like a magnet. They come from all over the Greek world, because Athens is rich and free, and full of people who love to talk and listen and argue.

One of them is Herodotus. He comes from a Greek city on the coast of what is now Turkey. He is about fifty-two. He has travelled more than almost anyone in the Greek world. He has been to Egypt and Babylon, to Persia and far north of the Black Sea.

--

And he has written a great book. It is about the wars between the Greeks and the Persians, fifty years before. And it is about all the peoples and wonders he has seen on his travels. It is the first great history book ever written. He calls it his Inquiries. In Greek that word is historiai, and that is where our word history comes from.

He begins his book by saying he wrote it so that the great deeds of people would not be forgotten with time.

{img:herodotus-traveller-egypt-pyramids-notes-guide-pointing}
Herodotus has read parts of his book aloud in Athens to big audiences, and the Athenians loved it. He is a wonderful storyteller. He writes about the pyramids and the crocodiles of the Nile. He writes about ants that dig for gold, and many other strange things. Some of his stories are true and some are not. He often says: I tell what I was told, but I do not have to believe it.

--

There is also a younger Athenian, about thirty, called Thucydides. When the war with Sparta begins, he will write its history. He will write carefully and exactly, without gods or marvels. His book will become one of the greatest history books ever written. He does not know yet what a sad story he will have to tell.

On the island of Cos, across the sea, there is a doctor of about thirty called Hippocrates.

Hippocrates and his followers are changing medicine. They teach that illnesses have natural causes. Not the anger of the gods, but bad food, bad water, the weather or the way people live. Doctors, they say, must watch their patients carefully and write down what they see. And the body has its own power to heal itself, which the doctor should help with rest, good food and fresh air.

--

Doctors who follow Hippocrates take an oath. Doctors still remember it today. They promise to help the sick as well as they can, and never to do harm. They promise to keep their patients' secrets, and to treat rich and poor alike.

And there are thinkers who ask questions about the nature of the world.

{img:philosopher-anaxagoras-looking-at-sun-moon-sky-students}
Anaxagoras, the teacher of Pericles, taught that the sun is not a god at all, but a huge burning stone. He taught that the moon reflects the light of the sun. For saying this, he was accused of not respecting the gods, and had to leave Athens. In Athens, even in its golden age, a new idea can be dangerous.

Another young thinker will soon teach that everything is made of tiny pieces, too small to see, that cannot be cut. He calls them atoms, from the Greek word for uncuttable. More than two thousand years later, scientists will find that he was, in a way, right.

--

There are travelling teachers too. They teach rich young men, for money, how to speak well and win arguments in the assembly and the courts. The most famous one says: man is the measure of all things. And about the gods, he says he cannot know whether they exist, because the question is hard and life is short.

{img:sophist-teacher-young-men-colonnade-gesturing-debate}
And there are sculptors, builders, painters and musicians. There is even an astronomer who has just worked out a clever calendar. He noticed that nineteen years of the sun fit almost exactly into two hundred and thirty-five months of the moon. That idea is still used today, to work out the date of Easter.

So many great minds in one small city, all at the same time. It has hardly ever happened before or since. And none of them knows how little time is left before the war changes everything.
