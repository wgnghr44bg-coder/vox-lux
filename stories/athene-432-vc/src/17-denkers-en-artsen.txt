Athens, in 432, is a magnet for clever people, from all over the Greek world. They come to Athens, because it is rich, and free, and full of people who love to talk, and listen, and argue.

One of them is Herodotus, from Halicarnassus, on the coast of what is now Turkey. He is about fifty-two. He has travelled more than almost anyone in the Greek world: to Egypt, and Babylon, and Persia, and Scythia, north of the Black Sea, and Italy.

--

And he has written a great book, about the wars between the Greeks and the Persians, fifty years before, and about all the peoples, and lands, and customs, and wonders, he has seen and heard of on his travels. It is the first great work of history ever written. He calls it his Inquiries, in Greek, his historiai, from which our word history comes.

He begins: this is the account of the inquiries of Herodotus of Halicarnassus, so that the deeds of men may not be forgotten with time, and that the great and wonderful things done by Greeks and barbarians may not lose their glory.

{img:herodotus-traveller-egypt-pyramids-notes-guide-pointing}
Herodotus has read parts of his book aloud, in Athens, to great audiences, and the Athenians loved it, and gave him a reward. He is a wonderful storyteller, and he writes about everything: the pyramids, and the crocodiles of the Nile, and the hippopotamus, and the customs of the Persians, and the gold-digging ants of India, and the people who eat their dead, and the people who wear the skins of their enemies. Some of his stories are true, and some are not, and he often says: I tell what I was told, but I do not have to believe it.

--

And there is a younger man, an Athenian, about thirty, who has heard Herodotus, it is said, and wept, and resolved to write history himself. His name is Thucydides. In a year or two, when the war with Sparta begins, he will begin to write its history, carefully, accurately, without stories of gods or marvels, and his book will be one of the greatest history books ever written.

On the island of Cos, across the sea, there is a doctor, also about thirty, called Hippocrates.

Hippocrates and his followers are changing medicine. They teach that illnesses have natural causes, not supernatural ones: not the anger of the gods, but bad food, or bad water, or the weather, or the way people live. They teach that doctors must observe their patients carefully, and write down what they see, and learn from experience. And that the body has its own power to heal itself, which the doctor must help, with rest, and good food, and exercise, and fresh air.

--

The doctors who follow Hippocrates take an oath, which is still remembered, and in some form still taken by doctors today: that they will help the sick, according to their ability and judgment, and never do harm. That they will keep the secrets of their patients. That they will treat everyone, rich or poor, free or slave.

And there are philosophers, who ask questions about the nature of the world.

{img:philosopher-anaxagoras-looking-at-sun-moon-sky-students}
Anaxagoras, the teacher of Pericles, taught that the sun is not a god, but a great burning stone, larger than the Peloponnese, and that the moon reflects the light of the sun, and that eclipses happen when the moon passes in front of the sun, or the earth's shadow falls on the moon. For saying this, he was accused of not respecting the gods, and had to leave Athens.

And a young man, Democritus, from Abdera, in the north, will soon teach that everything in the world is made of tiny particles, too small to see, which cannot be cut, and which he calls atoms, from the Greek word for uncuttable. Two thousand three hundred years later, scientists will discover that he was, in a way, right.

--

There are the Sophists, too: travelling teachers, who come to Athens, and teach rich young men, for money, how to speak well, and persuade people, and win arguments, in the assembly, and the courts. The most famous is Protagoras, who says: man is the measure of all things. And that, about the gods, he cannot know whether they exist or not, because the question is difficult, and life is short.

{img:sophist-teacher-young-men-colonnade-gesturing-debate}
And there is Phidias, the sculptor, and Ictinus, the architect, and Polygnotus, the painter, and Damon, the musician, and Meton, the astronomer, who has just, in 432, worked out a calendar, based on the observation that nineteen years of the sun are almost exactly equal to two hundred and thirty-five months of the moon. It is still used, today, to work out the date of Easter, and the Jewish calendar.

So many great minds, in one small city, at one time. It has hardly ever happened before, or since.
