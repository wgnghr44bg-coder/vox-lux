The Athenians live with their gods every day.

There are the twelve great gods of Mount Olympus. Zeus, the king of the gods, with his thunderbolt. Hera, his wife. Athena, goddess of wisdom and of the city. Apollo, god of music and the sun. Artemis, goddess of hunting and the moon. Poseidon, god of the sea. Aphrodite, goddess of love. Hermes, the messenger. And others besides, like Demeter, goddess of the grain, and Dionysus, god of wine.

--

And there are many smaller gods and spirits, in every spring and river and tree and mountain.

The Greek gods are very human. They love and quarrel and get jealous. They can be kind, and they can be cruel. The Greeks tell endless stories about them. Children learn them early. They are painted on pots, carved on temples and acted in plays.

{img:herm-pillar-street-corner-doorway-hermes-head-flowers}
In every house there is a little altar to Zeus in the courtyard. At the door stands a stone pillar with the head of Hermes, to protect the house. Before every meal, a little food is offered to the gods. Before every drink, a few drops of wine are poured on the ground.

--

The Greeks pray standing up, with their arms raised to the sky. They make promises to the gods. If the god helps them, they will give a gift. So the temples are full of gifts. Statues, little figures, painted plaques, and even clay models of arms or legs that a god has healed.

The great act of worship is the sacrifice. An animal is offered at the altar outside the temple. The meat is shared among the people in a feast. It is a joyful occasion, with music and garlands and processions.

{img:temple-of-asclepius-healing-dreams-sleepers-colonnade-night}
And there are oracles, places where people ask the gods about the future. The most famous is at Delphi, high in the mountains. There, a priestess of Apollo sits in the temple and answers questions in the god's name, in strange and mysterious words. Cities send messengers to ask whether to go to war. Ordinary people ask about marriage, money and illness.

--

And in this very year, the Spartans send messengers to Delphi with a question. Should they go to war with Athens? The answer comes back, the old historian says, that if they fight with all their strength, they will win. And that the god himself will help them, whether they ask him or not.

When the Athenians hear about that answer, many of them grow quiet.

Over the door of the temple at Delphi there are two famous sayings carved in the stone. The thinkers love to talk about them. Know yourself. And: nothing too much.
