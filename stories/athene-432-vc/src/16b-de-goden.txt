The Athenians live with their gods every day.

There are the twelve great gods of Mount Olympus: Zeus, the king of the gods, with his thunderbolt. Hera, his wife, the queen. Athena, the goddess of wisdom, and crafts, and the city. Apollo, the god of the sun, and music, and prophecy. Artemis, his twin sister, the goddess of the hunt, and the moon. Poseidon, the god of the sea. Aphrodite, the goddess of love. Ares, the god of war. Hermes, the messenger, the god of travellers, and merchants, and thieves. Hephaestus, the smith. Demeter, the goddess of the grain. And Dionysus, the god of wine, and the theatre.

--

And there are many more: little gods, and nymphs, and heroes, and spirits, of every spring, and river, and tree, and mountain, and house, and crossroads.

The gods of the Greeks are very human. They love, and quarrel, and are jealous, and proud, and kind, and cruel. They have families, and favourites. The Greeks tell endless stories about them, which they learn as children, and which are painted on their pots, and carved on their temples, and told in their plays.

{img:herm-pillar-street-corner-doorway-hermes-head-flowers}
In every house, there is a little altar, in the courtyard, to Zeus, the protector of the household. At the door, there is a herm, a stone pillar, with the head of the god Hermes on top, to protect the house. In the kitchen, the hearth is sacred to Hestia, the goddess of the hearth. Before every meal, a little food is offered to the gods. Before every drink, a few drops of wine are poured on the ground.

--

The Greeks pray standing, with their arms raised to the sky, for the gods above, or stretched down to the ground, for the gods below. They make vows: if the god helps them, they will give a gift. And the temples are full of these gifts: statues, and little figures, and painted plaques, and models of the parts of the body that the god has healed.

The great act of worship is the sacrifice, at the altar, outside the temple, when an animal is offered to the god, and the meat is shared among the people, at a feast. It is a joyful occasion, with music, and garlands, and processions.

{img:temple-of-asclepius-healing-dreams-sleepers-colonnade-night}
And there are the oracles, where people go to ask the gods for advice about the future. The most famous is at Delphi, in the mountains, where the priestess of Apollo, the Pythia, sitting on a tripod, in the temple, answers questions, in the name of the god, in strange, mysterious words, which the priests interpret. Cities send ambassadors to Delphi, to ask whether they should go to war, or found a colony. And ordinary people go too, to ask about marriage, and money, and journeys, and illness.

--

When the Spartans asked the oracle, in 432, whether they should go to war with Athens, the god answered, Thucydides says, that if they fought with all their might, they would win, and that he himself would help them, whether they asked him or not.

Over the door of the temple at Delphi, there are two famous words, carved in the stone, which the philosophers love to talk about: Know thyself. And: Nothing too much.
