At about seven years old, the son of an Athenian citizen goes to school.

There are no public schools, paid for by the city. Parents pay small fees to private teachers, who keep little schools, in rooms, or in the open air. Rich families send their sons for many years. Poor families, only for a few, or not at all. Girls, mostly, do not go to school, but learn at home.

--

Every morning, the boy walks to school, accompanied by a slave, called a paidagogos, a leader of the boy, an older man, who carries his things, and looks after him, and makes sure he behaves, and sits with him in the lessons. Our word pedagogy, teaching, comes from his name.

The boy has three teachers.

{img:paidagogos-old-slave-walking-boy-to-school-street-morning}
The grammatistes teaches him to read and write, and to count. The boy writes on a wooden tablet, covered with wax, with a stylus, a pointed stick of bone, or metal, which has a flat end, for rubbing out. He learns his letters, and then words, and then whole passages of poetry, especially Homer, which he learns by heart, long passages, of hundreds of lines. Every educated Athenian knows much of Homer by heart.

--

The kitharistes teaches him music. He learns to play the lyre, a stringed instrument, made with the shell of a tortoise, and the horns of a goat, and to sing, and to recite poetry to music. The Greeks believe that music shapes the character, and makes the soul gentle, and harmonious.

And the paidotribes teaches him physical exercise, at the palaestra, the wrestling school: wrestling, and running, and jumping, and throwing the discus, and the javelin, and boxing.

The boys exercise naked, as Greek athletes always do, their bodies rubbed with olive oil, which they scrape off afterwards, with a curved metal tool, called a strigil. The Greeks believe that a beautiful, healthy, strong body is as important as a good mind.

--

When they are older, young men, and grown men, go to the gymnasium.

There are three great gymnasia in Athens, outside the city walls, in pleasant places, with trees, and streams, and gardens: the Academy, the Lyceum, and the Cynosarges. They are large open spaces, with running tracks, and wrestling grounds, and baths, and shady walks, under plane trees and olive trees, and colonnades, and altars.

{img:academy-gymnasium-plane-trees-olive-grove-running-track-shade}
The men go there in the afternoon, to exercise, and bathe, and talk. And the philosophers go there too, to talk with the young men, and teach them. A few years later, Plato will found his famous school, at the Academy, which will give its name to all the academies of the world. And later still, Aristotle will teach at the Lyceum.

--

At eighteen, a young Athenian becomes an ephebe. For two years, he trains as a soldier, and guards the frontiers of Attica, in the forts on the mountains. And then he becomes a full citizen, and may go to the assembly, and vote, and serve in the army, as a hoplite, a heavily armed foot soldier, with a bronze helmet, and a great round shield, and a spear, or, if he is poor, as a rower, in the navy.

{img:young-men-oath-temple-ephebes-shields-spears-morning-solemn}
The young men take an oath, in the temple of Aglauros, on the slope of the Acropolis: I will not disgrace these sacred arms, or desert my comrade in the battle line. I will defend the sacred and holy things. I will leave my fatherland not smaller, but greater, and better, than I received it.
