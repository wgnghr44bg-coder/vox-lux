Tonight we are going nearly two thousand five hundred years back, to the city of Athens, in ancient Greece, in the year 432 before Christ.

--

On a great rock above the city, a new temple has just been finished, of shining white marble, the most beautiful building the Greeks have ever made. Below it, in the streets and the market place, in the theatre and the assembly, the people of Athens are talking, and arguing, and voting, and writing plays, and asking questions that people will still be asking two and a half thousand years later.

It is the golden age of Athens. And tonight, we will spend a little time there, in its last peaceful summer.

If you enjoy these slow evenings in the distant past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, and come back for another one.

--

{img:greek-coast-blue-sea-islands-olive-hills-summer-haze}
Let us imagine Greece, as it is in 432 before Christ.

It is a land of mountains and sea. Everywhere, there are steep, rocky mountains, grey and brown and green, covered with pine trees, and scrub, and wild herbs, thyme and sage and oregano, which fill the air with their scent in the summer heat. Between the mountains, there are little plains, and valleys, with fields, and olive groves, and vineyards. And everywhere, the sea is close: a deep, clear, shining blue, scattered with islands.

--

The Greeks do not live in one country, with one king. They live in hundreds of little independent cities, each with its own government, and its own laws, and its own gods, and its own army, and its own coins. Each city, with the countryside around it, is called a polis. There are big ones, like Athens, and Sparta, and Corinth, and Thebes. And small ones, no more than a village, on a little island, or in a mountain valley.

They often quarrel, and fight each other. But they all speak Greek, and worship the same gods, and know the same stories, the poems of Homer, about the war at Troy. And they all come together, every four years, at Olympia, for the Olympic Games, in honour of Zeus, the king of the gods.

Greek cities are scattered all around the Mediterranean, and the Black Sea, wherever Greek sailors and settlers have gone: in southern Italy, and Sicily, and the south of France, and North Africa, and the coast of Turkey. A philosopher, Plato, will later say that the Greeks live around the sea like frogs around a pond.

--

And of all the Greek cities, in 432, the richest, and the most powerful, and the most brilliant, is Athens.

Athens is the leader of a great alliance of Greek cities, around the Aegean Sea, which pay it tribute, in silver, every year, for protection. It has the greatest navy in the world: three hundred warships. It has silver mines, at Laurion, in the south of its country. It has a great port, the Piraeus, full of ships, and merchants, and goods, from all over the Mediterranean.

{img:piraeus-harbour-triremes-merchant-ships-warehouses-sunlight}
And it has something new, something that no other people in the world has ever tried before, in quite this way: democracy. The government of the people.

--

In Athens, the ordinary citizens, the farmers, and the shoemakers, and the potters, and the sailors, all meet together, on a hill, every few days, and decide, by voting, all the great questions of the city: war and peace, and laws, and taxes, and buildings, and who will lead them.

It is not a perfect democracy. Women cannot vote. Slaves cannot vote. Foreigners cannot vote. Perhaps only one person in five or six in Athens has a vote. But for the time, it is a remarkable thing.

{img:pnyx-hill-assembly-citizens-seated-speaker-platform-morning}
And under this democracy, in these years, Athens has become the most extraordinary city in the world: a city of builders, and sculptors, and painters, and poets, and playwrights, and philosophers, and historians, and doctors, and scientists. Their names are still known, all over the world, today: Pericles, and Phidias, and Sophocles, and Euripides, and Socrates, and Herodotus.
