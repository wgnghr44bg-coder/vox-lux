Below the Acropolis lies the heart of everyday life in Athens: the Agora.

The Agora is a great open square. It is the market, the meeting place and the centre of government, all at once. Every morning it is crowded with shoppers and sellers, politicians and thinkers, slaves and foreigners. They walk and talk and buy and argue in the sunshine.

--

Around the edges of the square stand long buildings with a row of columns in front and a roof on top. They are called stoas. In them, people can walk and talk in the shade, out of the sun and rain.

The most famous one is the Painted Stoa. Its back wall is covered with big paintings of the great battles of Athens, like the battle of Marathon against the Persians. Much later, a thinker will teach here, and his followers will be called Stoics, after this building.

There is the council house, where five hundred citizens meet every day to prepare business for the assembly. There is a round building where some of them stay day and night, so that someone is always ready if danger comes. There are law courts, temples and fountains.

--

On a little hill beside the Agora, a new temple is being built to Hephaestus, the god of fire and of craftsmen. It still stands today, almost complete. It is the best preserved Greek temple in the world.

Around it are the workshops of the smiths and bronze workers. The ring of their hammers sounds across the square.

{img:temple-hephaestus-hill-agora-smiths-workshops-smoke}
In the middle of the Agora are the market stalls, with tables and baskets and awnings. Each kind of goods has its own corner.

--

The fish market is the most popular. The Athenians love fish. The fish sellers are famous for being rude and greedy, and for refusing to bargain.

There are stalls with bread and garlic, onions and beans, figs and grapes. There is olive oil, wine, honey and cheese. There are sellers of perfume and flowers, shoes and cloth, and even books, written on rolls of papyrus.

{img:agora-fish-stall-seller-arguing-customer-baskets-octopus}
There is a slave market too. Because Athens, like all ancient cities, depends on the work of slaves.

--

Slaves are everywhere in Athens. Perhaps one person in three is a slave. They were captured in war, or sold by pirates, or born to slave mothers, from all around the Mediterranean.

Some work in the houses of the rich, as cooks and nurses and teachers. Some work in workshops beside their masters. Some work in the fields. The unluckiest work in the silver mines, in terrible conditions. Even the city's police are slaves, archers from the far north, owned by the city.

Life for a slave is often hard. But many are treated as part of the household, and some are set free. A visitor once complained that in Athens you cannot tell a slave from a free man in the street. They dress the same, he said, and slaves are not afraid to speak their minds.

--

And above all, the Agora is where people come to talk.

The Athenians love talking more than anything. About politics and news, about trials and plays, about the price of fish and the meaning of life. They stand in groups in the shade, or sit on temple steps, and argue for hours.

{img:men-talking-in-groups-stoa-steps-afternoon-shade-gestures}
And among them walks a short, round, rather ugly man with a snub nose and bulging eyes. He goes barefoot, wears the same old cloak summer and winter, and asks everyone questions. His name is Socrates. Many people find his questions annoying. A few find them dangerous.
