Below the Acropolis, to the north west, there is the heart of everyday life in Athens: the Agora.

The Agora is a great open square, the market place, and the meeting place, and the centre of government, and business, and gossip, all at once. Every morning, it is crowded with people: shoppers, and sellers, and politicians, and philosophers, and lawyers, and slaves, and foreigners, and idle men, walking, and talking, and buying, and arguing, in the sunshine.

--

Around the edges of the square, there are long buildings, called stoas: covered colonnades, with a row of columns along the front, and a wall at the back, and a roof, where people can walk, and talk, in the shade, out of the sun and the rain.

The most famous is the Painted Stoa, the Stoa Poikile, whose back wall is covered with great paintings, by famous painters, of the great battles of Athens: the battle of Marathon, against the Persians, and the war against the Amazons, and the fall of Troy. More than a hundred years later, a philosopher, Zeno, will teach in this stoa, and his followers will be called the Stoics, after it.

There is the council house, the Bouleuterion, where the council of five hundred meets, every day, to prepare business for the assembly. There is the round building, the Tholos, where fifty members of the council live, eat, and sleep, day and night, so that someone is always on duty, to deal with any emergency. There are law courts. And temples, and altars, and fountain houses, and statues.

--

On a little hill, beside the Agora, there is a new temple, of Hephaestus, the god of fire, and of smiths, and craftsmen, which is being built at the same time as the Parthenon. It still stands today, almost complete, the best preserved Greek temple in the world.

Around it, there are the workshops of the smiths, and the bronze workers, whose god Hephaestus is. The sound of their hammers rings out across the Agora.

{img:temple-hephaestus-hill-agora-smiths-workshops-smoke}
And in the middle of the Agora, there are the market stalls: wooden tables, and booths, and baskets, and awnings, where everything is sold. Each kind of goods has its own place, its own circle, as the Athenians call it.

--

There is the fish market, the most popular of all, because the Athenians love fish, and the fish sellers are famous for being rude, and greedy, and for charging high prices, and refusing to bargain.

There are the sellers of bread, and of vegetables, garlic, and onions, and cabbages, and lettuces, and beans, and lentils, and of fruit, figs, and grapes, and pomegranates, and apples, and pears. There are sellers of olive oil, and wine, and honey, and cheese, and eggs, and chickens, and little birds. There are sellers of perfumes, and wreaths of flowers, and ribbons, and pots, and pans, and lamps, and shoes, and cloth, and furniture, and books, written on rolls of papyrus.

{img:agora-fish-stall-seller-arguing-customer-baskets-octopus}
There is a slave market, too. Because Athens, like all ancient cities, depends on the work of slaves.

--

Slaves are everywhere in Athens. Perhaps one person in three, or one in four, in Attica, is a slave. They are people captured in war, or sold by pirates, or born to slave mothers, from all around the Mediterranean and the Black Sea: Thracians, and Scythians, and Syrians, and Carians, and many others.

Some work in the houses of the rich, as cooks, and maids, and nurses, and tutors. Some work in workshops, beside their masters, making pots, and shoes, and furniture. Some work in the fields. Some, the most unlucky, work in the silver mines of Laurion, in terrible conditions. And some belong to the city itself, and work as clerks, and policemen: the city's police are Scythian archers, slaves, owned by the state, who keep order in the assembly, and the streets.

Life for a slave in Athens is often hard. But many are treated as members of the household, and some are set free, by their masters. And a visitor from another city once complained that in Athens, you cannot tell a slave from a free man, in the street, because they dress the same, and slaves are not afraid to speak their minds.

--

And the Agora is where people come to talk.

The Athenians love talking, more than anything. They talk about politics, and the latest news, and the decisions of the assembly, and the trials in the courts, and the new plays at the theatre, and the price of fish, and the gods, and the meaning of life. They stand in groups, in the shade of the stoas, or sit on the steps of the temples, and argue, for hours.

{img:men-talking-in-groups-stoa-steps-afternoon-shade-gestures}
And among them, there is a short, round, ugly man, with a snub nose, and bulging eyes, and a beard, who walks barefoot, and wears the same old cloak, summer and winter, and asks everyone questions. His name is Socrates.
