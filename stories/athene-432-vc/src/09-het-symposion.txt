In the evening, in the houses of richer Athenians, there are dinner parties, called symposia.

The word symposion means drinking together. It is a party for men only: the host, and his friends, perhaps seven, or nine, or eleven of them. They meet in a special room in the house, the andron, the men's room, with couches along the walls, and a floor of pebble mosaic.

--

The guests arrive, in the evening, and take off their sandals, at the door, and a slave washes their feet. They lie down on the couches, two to a couch, leaning on their left elbows, on cushions. Little tables are set in front of them, with the food.

First, they eat: bread, and fish, and vegetables, and cheese, and olives, and perhaps a little meat. They eat with their fingers, and wipe their hands on pieces of bread, and throw the bread to the dogs, under the tables.

{img:slave-boy-pouring-wine-from-krater-into-kylix-cups-lamps}
Then the tables are taken away, and the floor is swept, and the drinking begins. The guests put on wreaths of flowers, or ivy, or myrtle, on their heads. They pour a little wine on the floor, as an offering to the gods, and sing a hymn.

--

The wine is always mixed with water, in a great bowl, called a krater. The Greeks think that only barbarians drink wine without water. The guests choose a leader, the symposiarch, who decides how strong the wine should be, perhaps three parts water to one part wine, and how many bowls they will drink. And slaves fill the cups, from the krater, with a ladle.

The cups are wide, shallow bowls, with two handles, called kylikes, often beautifully painted inside, so that as you drink, a picture appears at the bottom: a dolphin, or a satyr, or a young man, or a gorgon's face.

And then the guests talk. They talk about politics, and philosophy, and love, and poetry. They tell jokes, and riddles. They take turns to sing songs, with a branch of myrtle, passed from hand to hand, from one singer to the next. They recite poems, from Homer, and other poets.

--

There is entertainment too. A girl plays the double pipes, the aulos. There are dancers, and acrobats, and jugglers. And the guests play games.

The favourite game is kottabos. Each guest, lying on his couch, holds his cup by one handle, and flicks the last drops of wine at a target: a little bronze disc, balanced on top of a tall stand, or little saucers floating in a bowl of water. If he knocks the disc down, with a clang, or sinks a saucer, he wins. And as he throws, he says the name of the person he loves. If he hits the target, it is a good sign.

{img:kottabos-game-wine-drops-flicked-bronze-stand-laughter}
The party can go on late into the night. Sometimes, it becomes noisy, and drunken, and at the end, the guests stagger out into the streets, with torches, singing, and knock on the doors of their friends, and wake up the neighbours.

--

But the best symposia are about conversation.

The philosopher Plato, who is not yet born in 432, will later write a famous book, called the Symposium, about a dinner party in Athens, a few years later, at which Socrates, and the comic poet Aristophanes, and other famous men, decide not to drink too much, but to take turns to make speeches about love. Each one gives a different speech. And at the end, Socrates gives the most beautiful of all, about how love of beauty can lead the soul upward, from the love of one beautiful person, to the love of beauty itself, and of wisdom, and of the good.

{img:dawn-light-symposium-room-guests-asleep-one-man-leaving}
And at the end of the book, as dawn comes, all the guests have fallen asleep on their couches, except Socrates, who gets up, and walks out into the morning, and goes to the Lyceum, to wash, and spends the day as usual, talking with anyone he meets. And in the evening, he goes home to bed.
