On the south slope of the Acropolis lies the theatre of Dionysus, the god of wine and of theatre.

It is an open-air theatre built into the hillside. The audience sits on wooden benches rising up the slope in a great half circle. Below them is a round, flat dancing floor, where the chorus sings and dances. Behind it is a low building where the actors change. It is painted to look like a palace or a temple.

--

The theatre holds perhaps fifteen thousand people, almost all the citizens of Athens.

Plays are not shown all year round, like today. They are performed at festivals for Dionysus. The biggest one is in spring, in March, when the sea is calm again and visitors come from all over Greece.

{img:procession-statue-dionysus-city-dionysia-spring-crowds-ivy}
The festival lasts five days. There are processions and choirs, and then three days of plays, from dawn to dusk. It is a competition. At the end, judges choose the best playwright and the best actor. The prize is a crown of ivy, and great honour.

--

The serious plays are called tragedies. They tell the old stories of gods and heroes. The war at Troy, King Oedipus, Medea, Antigone and many more. They are sad and full of great poetry. They ask deep questions about fate and justice, about suffering, and about what happens when people make terrible mistakes.

Two of the three great writers of tragedy are alive in these years. Sophocles is about sixty-five. Around this time he may be writing his most famous play, about King Oedipus. Euripides is about fifty, and his plays question everything, and sometimes shock the Athenians.

All the actors are men, even those who play women. Each play has only three actors. Each one plays several parts by changing masks. The masks have big open mouths, so the voices carry, and so that even people at the back can see who is who.

--

The chorus of a dozen or so men sings and dances between the scenes. They comment on what is happening, give advice, pray and mourn. A musician plays the double flute.

Historians still argue whether women were allowed to watch. Perhaps some did.

{img:comic-actors-padded-costumes-funny-masks-audience-laughing}
And then there are the comedies. They are rude, silly and very funny. They make jokes about politics and about famous men, who are often sitting in the audience being laughed at. In a few years, a great comic writer will put Socrates in a play, hanging in a basket in the air, studying the clouds. It is said that Socrates stood up in the theatre, so everyone could see how much he looked like his mask.

--

Going to the theatre is part of religion, and part of being a citizen. Poor citizens even get money from the city to pay for their seats.

The audience brings cushions, food and wine, and spends the whole day there in the spring sunshine. They cheer and weep and laugh. If they do not like an actor, they throw nuts and figs at him.

{img:sunset-theatre-empty-seats-acropolis-above-golden-quiet}
Behind the stage, the audience can see the plain of Attica, the mountains and the sea. So the old stories of gods and heroes are played out against the real land of Greece, under the open sky. Next spring, the plays will go on as usual. But by then, the land behind the stage may look very different.
