In the markets of Athens, and all over the Mediterranean, people pay with Athenian coins. They are small, thick silver coins. On one side is the head of Athena in her helmet. On the other side is an owl, Athena's bird, with big round eyes and a little olive twig.

People call these coins owls. A big one is worth four drachmas. One drachma is about a day's pay for a skilled worker. The smallest coins are so tiny that people carry them in their mouths at the market, tucked in their cheeks, because they have no pockets.

--

The owls of Athens are the most trusted coins in the Greek world. Their silver is always pure and their weight is always right. They are used everywhere, from Italy to Egypt and Persia. Some have even been found as far away as Afghanistan. The Greeks have a saying for doing something useless: bringing owls to Athens.

The silver comes from mines in the south of Attica.

{img:laurion-hills-mine-entrances-washing-tables-ore-cisterns}
The mines are deep, narrow, dark tunnels in the hills. Thousands of slaves work there by the light of little oil lamps. They dig out the ore with picks and carry it up in baskets. It is terrible, dangerous work. Above ground, the ore is crushed and washed, and then melted in furnaces to get the silver.

--

Fifty years ago, the Athenians found a rich new vein of silver in these mines. A clever leader called Themistocles persuaded them not to share the money out, but to build two hundred warships with it. Three years later, those ships beat the great Persian fleet at the battle of Salamis, and saved Greece. They were the start of the great navy and the wealth of Athens.

So the silver dug by slaves in the dark paid for the Parthenon, the fleet and much of the glory of Athens. The Athenians hope it will be enough to pay for whatever is coming next.

{img:temple-of-poseidon-sounion-cliff-sea-sunset-columns}
At the tip of Attica, high on a cliff above the sea, stands a white marble temple to Poseidon, god of the sea. It was built just a few years ago. Sailors coming home see it shining on its cliff from far out at sea, and know they are almost home.

It still stands today. At sunset, people come out from Athens to sit on the rocks beside its columns and watch the sun go down into the sea. On one of the columns, more than two hundred years ago, the English poet Lord Byron carved his name.
