In the markets of Athens, and all over the Mediterranean, people pay with the coins of Athens: little thick silver coins, with the head of Athena on one side, wearing her helmet, and on the other side, an owl, the bird of Athena, with big round eyes, and a little sprig of olive, and the letters ATHE, for the Athenians.

People call them owls. A big coin, worth four drachmas, is a tetradrachm. A drachma is about a day's wages for a skilled worker. And there are smaller coins, obols, six to a drachma, so small that people carry them in their mouths, in the market, tucked in their cheeks, because they have no pockets.

--

The owls of Athens are the most trusted coins in the Greek world, because their silver is always pure, and their weight is always right. They are used everywhere, from Italy to Egypt to Persia. Archaeologists have found them as far away as Afghanistan. The Greeks have a saying, about doing something useless: bringing owls to Athens, like bringing coals to Newcastle.

The silver comes from the mines of Laurion, in the south of Attica, near Cape Sounion.

{img:laurion-hills-mine-entrances-washing-tables-ore-cisterns}
The mines are deep tunnels, dug into the hills, narrow, and low, and dark, where thousands of slaves work, by the light of little oil lamps, digging out the silver ore, with picks and hammers, and carrying it up to the surface, in baskets. It is terrible, hard, dangerous work. The ore is crushed, and washed, on great sloping tables, with water from cisterns, and then smelted, in furnaces, to get the silver.

--

Fifty years before, in 483, the Athenians found a rich new vein of silver at Laurion. And a wise politician, Themistocles, persuaded them not to share the money out among the citizens, but to build a fleet of two hundred warships with it. Three years later, those ships defeated the great fleet of the Persians, at the battle of Salamis, and saved Greece. And they became the beginning of the great navy, and the empire, and the wealth, of Athens.

So the silver of Laurion, dug by slaves in the dark, paid for the Parthenon, and the fleet, and much of the glory of Athens.

{img:temple-of-poseidon-sounion-cliff-sea-sunset-columns}
At the tip of Attica, near the mines, on a high cliff above the sea, there is a temple of Poseidon, the god of the sea, built just a few years before, of white marble. Sailors, coming home to Athens, see it from far out at sea, shining on its cliff, and know that they are nearly home.

It still stands today, at Cape Sounion. And at sunset, people come from Athens to sit on the rocks, beside its columns, and watch the sun go down into the sea, among the islands. On one of its columns, in 1810, the English poet Lord Byron carved his name.
