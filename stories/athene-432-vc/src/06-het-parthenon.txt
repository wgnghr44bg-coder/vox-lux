In 432 before Christ, the Parthenon is finished at last.

It has taken fifteen years to build. The building itself was finished six years before, in 438. But the sculptors have been working on it ever since, on the sculptures that fill the triangular gables at each end, the pediments. And now, in 432, the last of them have been put in place.

--

The Parthenon is made almost entirely of marble, white marble, from the quarries of Mount Pentelikon, about sixteen kilometres away, brought down the mountain, on wooden sledges, and in ox carts, to the city, and hauled up the ramp, to the top of the Acropolis. More than twenty thousand tons of marble.

It is about seventy metres long, and thirty metres wide. All around it, there is a row of tall columns, forty-six of them, each made of drums of marble, placed one on top of the other, and fluted, with shallow grooves, running up and down. On top of the columns, there is the roof, of wood, covered with marble tiles.

{img:column-drums-marble-masons-fluting-chisels-workshop-acropolis}
The architects were Ictinus and Callicrates. And they did something very clever. In the Parthenon, there are almost no straight lines. The floor curves up slightly in the middle. The columns lean slightly inward, and swell slightly in the middle. The corner columns are a little thicker. All these little curves are so small that you cannot see them. But they make the building look perfectly straight, and balanced, and alive, instead of stiff and heavy.

--

The marble is not plain white, as it looks today.

In 432, the sculptures, and parts of the building, are painted, in bright colours: red, and blue, and green, and gold. The figures in the sculptures have coloured clothes, and painted eyes, and lips, and gilded weapons, and jewellery of bronze. The building glows, in the bright Greek sunlight.

On the pediments, there are great groups of statues, larger than life, of the gods. At the east end, over the main door, the birth of Athena, who sprang fully grown, and in armour, from the head of her father, Zeus. At the west end, the contest between Athena and Poseidon, for the land of Athens.

Around the top of the outside walls, there are ninety-two square carved panels, called metopes, showing battles: gods against giants, Greeks against Amazons, Greeks against Trojans, and the Lapiths, a people of Greek legend, against the centaurs, half man and half horse.

--

And inside the colonnade, high up around the top of the walls of the temple itself, there is a long band of carving, a frieze, a hundred and sixty metres long, showing a great procession: the Panathenaic procession, the great festival of Athena, with horsemen, and chariots, and young men, and old men, and young women, and musicians, and priests, and animals for sacrifice, all moving toward the gods, who sit, at the east end, waiting to receive them.

It shows the people of Athens themselves, honouring their goddess. It is perhaps the first time, in Greek art, that ordinary people have been shown, on a temple, beside the gods.

{img:frieze-horsemen-procession-marble-relief-young-riders-flowing}
The horses, and the young riders, flowing along, with their cloaks blowing, are some of the most beautiful sculptures ever made.

--

And inside the temple, in the great dim hall, there stands the statue of Athena Parthenos, made by the great sculptor Phidias, the friend of Pericles, who was in charge of all the sculptures of the Parthenon.

The statue is twelve metres tall, almost reaching the roof. It is made of a wooden frame, covered with ivory, for the skin, and gold, for the clothes, and the helmet, and the shield. More than a ton of gold. She stands, in her helmet, with her shield beside her, and a great snake, and in her right hand, a little figure of Victory, with wings, as tall as a man.

{img:chryselephantine-athena-statue-dim-temple-hall-gold-ivory-pool}
In front of her, there is a shallow pool of water, which reflects the light from the door, onto the gold and ivory, and keeps the air moist, so that the ivory does not crack.

The Athenians come to look at her, and bow, and pray. The gold of her robe can be taken off, in an emergency, and melted down, to pay for war. Pericles says so himself, in the assembly. Athena will help her city, if it needs her.
