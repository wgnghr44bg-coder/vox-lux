The greatest festival of Athens is the Panathenaia, the festival of all the Athenians, in honour of Athena, on her birthday, in the middle of summer, in July or August.

Every year, there is a small festival. And every four years, there is the Great Panathenaia, which lasts for about a week, with games, and contests, and music, and sacrifices, and a great procession.

--

The games are like the Olympic Games, but with even more events. There are races, on foot, and on horseback, and in chariots. There is wrestling, and boxing, and the pankration, a kind of all-in wrestling. There are contests of music: singing to the lyre, and playing the pipes. And contests of reciting Homer, by professional reciters, called rhapsodes, who compete to recite the Iliad and the Odyssey most beautifully.

There are boat races, in the harbour. There is a torch race, at night, a relay, from an altar outside the city, to the altar of Athena on the Acropolis, with the runners carrying burning torches, passing them from hand to hand, trying to keep the flame alight.

{img:torch-relay-race-night-runners-flames-crowds-cheering}
And there are contests for the tribes: a beauty contest for men, called the euandria, the contest of manliness, and a dance in armour, called the pyrrhic.

--

The prizes for the athletes are wonderful: great jars, called Panathenaic amphoras, filled with olive oil, from the sacred olive trees of Athena. Hundreds of jars, each holding about forty litres. A winner of the chariot race might win a hundred and forty jars of oil, a fortune, which he could sell.

The jars are painted, on one side, with a picture of Athena, striding forward, with her spear and her shield, and on the other side, with a picture of the event that was won: runners, or wrestlers, or chariots. And with the words: one of the prizes from Athens. Many of these jars still exist, in museums, all over the world.

And on the last great day of the festival, the twenty-eighth day of the month, Athena's birthday, there is the great procession.

--

At dawn, the procession gathers at the Dipylon Gate, the double gate, in the north west of the city.

And then it moves off, along the Panathenaic Way, a broad road, which crosses the Agora, and climbs up to the Acropolis. Everyone is there. Priests, and priestesses. Magistrates. Young women of noble families, carrying baskets on their heads, with the sacred barley, and the knives for the sacrifice. Old men, carrying olive branches. Young men, leading the cattle and the sheep for the sacrifice. Musicians, playing pipes, and lyres. Horsemen, the young knights of Athens, riding their fine horses. Chariots. And the allies of Athens, and the foreigners who live in Athens, carrying offerings.

{img:new-peplos-robe-carried-like-sail-ship-on-wheels-procession}
And in the middle of the procession, there is the most important thing of all: a new robe for Athena, the peplos, woven by the women and girls of Athens, during the past nine months, of saffron yellow wool, with pictures woven into it, of the battle of the gods and giants. At the Great Panathenaia, the robe is so large that it is hung, like a sail, from the mast of a ship, on wheels, which is pulled along the road.

--

The procession climbs up to the Acropolis, through the gateway, and the robe is given to the priestess of Athena, to dress the ancient wooden statue of the goddess, in her old temple.

And then there is the great sacrifice: a hundred cattle, or more, at the great altar of Athena, on the Acropolis. And afterward, the meat is roasted, and shared out among all the people of Athens, in the Kerameikos, in a great feast, the biggest meal of the year, for rich and poor alike.

{img:great-feast-athenians-sharing-roasted-meat-bread-evening-joy}
It is this procession, perhaps, that the frieze of the Parthenon shows: the horsemen, and the young women, and the old men, and the cattle, and the gods, waiting to receive them. The people of Athens, honouring their goddess, carved in marble, forever.
