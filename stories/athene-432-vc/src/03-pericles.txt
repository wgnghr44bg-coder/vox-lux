The leading man of Athens in 432 before Christ is Pericles.

He is about sixty-three years old. He is not a king. He has no special title, except that every year the people choose him as one of the ten generals who lead the army and the navy. They have chosen him almost every year for fifteen years. He leads the city by persuading the people with his speeches.

--

Pericles comes from one of the oldest and noblest families of Athens. His mother's family helped to found the democracy, about seventy-five years before.

There is a story that before he was born, his mother dreamed she gave birth to a lion. He was a serious, clever child, taught by the best teachers in Athens. One of them was a thinker called Anaxagoras. He taught Pericles to think about the world and the stars, and not to be afraid of eclipses, because they have natural causes.

{img:philosopher-teaching-young-noble-courtyard-stars-diagram-sand}
Pericles is tall and dignified, with a calm face and a quiet voice. But his head is rather long and high. The comedy writers make fun of it and call him onion head. That is why his statues always show him wearing a helmet, pushed back to hide the shape.

--

He is famous for staying calm. There is a story that once a man followed him all day through the market, shouting insults at him. Pericles took no notice. In the evening, the man followed him all the way home, still shouting. And Pericles, without a word, told his servant to take a torch and light the man's way home, because it was dark.

He is famous for his honesty too. He does not take bribes or grow rich from his power. He lives simply and hardly ever goes to parties.

And above all, he is famous as a speaker. One poet wrote that persuasion sat on his lips. People called him the Olympian, because when he spoke, it was like thunder and lightning from Zeus himself.

--

Under Pericles, Athens has grown rich and beautiful.

He has persuaded the people to spend the alliance's silver on rebuilding the temples of the Acropolis. The Persians burned them nearly fifty years before. He has hired the greatest builders and sculptors, and paid thousands of workers, to build the Parthenon and much more.

{img:workers-carrying-marble-blocks-acropolis-ramp-ox-carts}
Some Athenians criticise him. They say he is using the allies' money, meant for defence, to decorate Athens like a vain woman with jewels. Pericles answers that as long as Athens protects its allies, it may spend the money as it likes. And the building gives work to thousands of ordinary Athenians.

--

Pericles also pays citizens a small daily wage for serving on juries and in the council. That way, poor men can take part in government too, not only the rich.

And he has a great dream for Athens. He wants it to be, as he says, the school of Greece. A city where people are free and equal before the law, where they love beauty and wisdom, and live openly and bravely.

{img:pericles-speaking-bema-crowd-listening-pnyx-afternoon}
But in 432, dark clouds are gathering. Sparta, the other great power of Greece, is growing afraid of Athens, and angry. Messages are going back and forth between the cities. And Pericles, calm as ever, is quietly getting ready for the war he fears is coming.
