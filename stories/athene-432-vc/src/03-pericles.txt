The leading man of Athens, in 432 before Christ, is Pericles.

He is about sixty-three years old. He is not a king, or a tyrant. He holds no special office, except that every year, the people elect him as one of the ten generals of the city, the strategoi, who lead the army and the navy. He has been elected general almost every year, for fifteen years. And he leads the city by persuading the people, in the assembly, with his speeches.

--

Pericles comes from one of the oldest and noblest families of Athens. His mother, Agariste, was the niece of Cleisthenes, the man who founded the democracy of Athens, about seventy-five years before.

The story goes that, before Pericles was born, his mother dreamed that she gave birth to a lion. He was a serious, clever child, and was taught by the best teachers in Athens: musicians, and philosophers. One of them, Anaxagoras, a philosopher from Ionia, taught him to think about the nature of the world, and the stars, and not to be afraid of omens, or eclipses, because they had natural causes.

{img:philosopher-teaching-young-noble-courtyard-stars-diagram-sand}
Pericles is tall, and handsome, and dignified, with a calm face, and a quiet voice. But his head is unusually long, and high, which the comic poets make fun of, calling him onion head, or squill head. That is why, in his statues, he is always shown wearing a helmet, pushed back on his head, to hide its shape.

--

He is famous for his calm. Plutarch tells a story that, once, a man followed him all day, through the market place, shouting insults at him, and Pericles took no notice, and went on with his business. And in the evening, when he went home, the man followed him to his door, still shouting. And Pericles, without a word, told his servant to take a torch, and light the man home, because it was dark.

He is famous for his honesty, too. He does not take bribes, or make himself rich from his power. He lives simply, and carefully. He hardly ever goes to parties, or dinners. The only time he went to a wedding feast, people say, he left before the drinking began.

And he is famous, above all, as a speaker. The comic poet Eupolis wrote that persuasion sat on his lips, and that he was the only speaker who left his sting in his listeners. They called him the Olympian, because when he spoke, it was like thunder and lightning, like Zeus on Mount Olympus.

--

Under Pericles, Athens has become rich, and powerful, and beautiful.

He has persuaded the people to spend the money of the alliance, the tribute paid by the allied cities, on rebuilding the temples of the Acropolis, which were burned by the Persians, nearly fifty years before. He has hired the greatest architects, and sculptors, and craftsmen, and paid thousands of workers, to build the Parthenon, and the great gateway of the Acropolis, and other temples, and buildings, all over Athens.

{img:workers-carrying-marble-blocks-acropolis-ramp-ox-carts}
Some people in Athens criticize him. They say that he is using the money of the allies, which was meant for defence against Persia, to decorate Athens, like a vain woman, with precious stones and statues and temples. But Pericles says that as long as Athens protects the allies, it may spend the money as it likes. And that the buildings give work, and wages, to thousands of ordinary Athenians.

--

Pericles also pays the citizens who serve on juries, and in the council, a small daily wage, so that poor men can take part in the government, and not only the rich. This is one of the most important things he has done for the democracy.

And he has a great dream, for Athens: that it will be, as he says, the school of Greece. A city where people are free, and equal before the law, and where they love beauty, and wisdom, and live generously, and openly, and bravely.

{img:pericles-speaking-bema-crowd-listening-pnyx-afternoon}
But in 432, there are dark clouds on the horizon. Sparta, the other great power of Greece, is growing afraid of Athens, and angry. And Pericles knows that war may be coming.
