Long before any ship arrived, this coast belonged to the Wampanoag, the People of the First Light.

[pause]

Picture yourself standing on a sandy shore in the far east of what we now call Massachusetts. The sun is just rising out of the sea, and the first light touches the marsh grass and turns it gold. That's why the people who lived here called themselves the Wampanoag, the People of the First Light. They were the first to see the sun come up over the land each morning. Their homeland stretched from the shores of Cape Cod to the islands of Martha's Vineyard and Nantucket, and inland across rivers, ponds and wide forests. They'd lived here for more than twelve thousand years. They knew every cove, every spring and every path through the woods. Now, you might think of this as an empty wilderness. It wasn't. It was a home, carefully looked after, full of villages, fields and familiar places with old names. Can you feel the cool salt air on your face?

{img:02-wampanoag-family-path-forest}

The Wampanoag moved with the seasons, the way you might move between a summer house and a winter one.

[pause]

In spring and summer, families lived close to the coast and the rivers. There, they planted their fields, gathered shellfish and caught fish in the shallow bays. When autumn came and the harvest was in, many of them moved inland, into the shelter of the forest. The trees kept off the worst of the winter wind, and there was firewood all around. You'd see them walking along well-worn paths, carrying baskets, mats and cooking pots on their backs. Children carried smaller bundles and ran ahead. I've always liked this way of living. Nobody was in a hurry to own one single spot forever. Instead, people went where the land offered food and shelter at that time of year. It was a rhythm as old as the tides. Each season had its work, and each place had its moment. Wouldn't it be lovely to live by the seasons like that?

{img:03-wetu-dome-house-woven-mats}

A Wampanoag home, called a wetu, was a dome of bent young trees covered with bark or woven mats.

[pause]

To build a wetu, people cut long, thin saplings and pushed both ends into the ground, bending them over into arches. Then they tied the arches together to make a rounded frame. Over this frame they laid sheets of bark, or mats woven from cattail reeds, which shed the rain beautifully. A small hole at the top let the smoke out. Inside, you'd find low sleeping platforms along the walls, covered with furs and soft mats. A fire burned gently in the middle. It's surprisingly cosy in there. In summer, the mats could be rolled up to let the breeze through. In winter, families lived in larger longhouses, where several households shared the warmth. Can you picture yourself lying on a fur by the fire, watching the smoke drift up through the hole in the roof, towards the stars?

[long-pause]

{img:04-canoe-dugout-river-mist}

Wampanoag canoes were made from a single tree, hollowed out slowly with fire and stone tools.

[pause]

The Wampanoag called these canoes mishoon. To make one, a team chose a tall, straight tree, often a chestnut or a pine. They felled it and then laid small fires along the top of the trunk. As each patch of wood burned and softened, they scraped it away with shells and stone tools. Then they burned a little more, and scraped a little more. It took a long time and a great deal of patience. But when it was done, you'd have a strong, smooth boat, big enough to carry several people and their goods. You'd glide down rivers and along the coast in it, fishing, trading and visiting relatives. A good mishoon could last for years. Today, Wampanoag craftspeople still make them in the old way, and you can watch them at work. I find that rather wonderful, don't you?

{img:05-fishing-weir-river-autumn}

In spring, rivers filled with so many fish that people could catch them by the basketful.

[pause]

Every spring, great shoals of herring swam up the rivers from the sea to lay their eggs in the ponds. The Wampanoag built fish traps, called weirs, across the streams. These were fences of wooden stakes and woven branches that let the water through but stopped the fish. You'd stand in the cold, clear water with a basket and simply scoop them up. There were also cod and bass in the bays, eels in the muddy creeks, and clams, oysters and lobsters along the shore. Some of these lobsters were so large, early English writers claimed, that one could feed several people. Fish were eaten fresh, or dried and smoked to keep for winter. Nothing was wasted. Now, you might think this was easy living. It took real skill. You had to know exactly when and where the fish would come. Would you have known where to look?

[long-pause]

{img:06-autumn-forest-deer-clearing}

The Wampanoag kept parts of the forest open by burning the undergrowth, so deer could graze and walking was easy.

[pause]

When the first English settlers walked into the woods, they were surprised. The forest wasn't a dark, tangled jungle. In many places, it looked more like a park, with big trees standing apart and open grass beneath them. That was no accident. Each year, the Wampanoag carefully burned away the bushes and dead branches on the forest floor. The fresh grass that grew back attracted deer, and the open spaces made hunting and travelling much easier. You could see a long way between the trunks. You'd walk on soft ground, with the sunlight falling in long golden stripes. Hunters knew these places well. They'd wait quietly at the edge of a clearing in the early morning. A good hunter might bring home venison for the whole family, and a deerskin for clothing. Can you imagine walking through a forest that people had gently shaped for thousands of years?

[long-pause]

{img:07-sachem-gathering-council-fire}

Each Wampanoag community had a leader called a sachem, who listened before deciding.

[pause]

A sachem was a leader, but not quite a king. He or she was expected to be generous, wise and fair. If someone was in need, the sachem was expected to help. Before making an important choice, a sachem met with respected elders and advisers, and listened carefully to what they said. Decisions were talked over, often for a long time, around a fire in the evening. You'd see people sitting in a circle, speaking one at a time, while the others listened in silence. Women could be sachems too, which surprised many European visitors. In 1621, the most important sachem in this region was a man called Ousamequin. You might know him by his title, Massasoit, which means something like great sachem. His home village was called Sowams, beside a quiet bay to the west. We'll meet him again later in our story. I think you'll like him.

{img:08-patuxet-town-brook-harbour}

The council fire is burning low now. Let's walk east together, towards the sea, to a quiet harbour where a small river comes tumbling down to the shore.

[pause]

The English village of Plymouth was built on the site of an older Wampanoag village called Patuxet.

[pause]

Patuxet means something like "at the little falls." The name came from the small stream that tumbled down over rocks into the harbour. For generations, a Wampanoag community had lived here, fishing in the bay and planting corn on the gentle slopes above the shore. You'd have heard children laughing by the stream and smelled wood smoke from the houses. But a few years before the English came, a terrible sickness swept along this coast. It had been brought by European sailors and traders, and the people here had no protection against it. Very many died, and Patuxet was left empty. When the English arrived, they found cleared fields waiting, with nobody to plant them. It's a sad beginning, and I'll keep it gentle. But it's important to remember. The village of Plymouth grew on land that had already been somebody's home for a very long time.

{img:09-tisquantum-portrait-shore}

One man from Patuxet had seen more of the world than almost anyone in the English village.

[pause]

His name was Tisquantum, though the English usually called him Squanto. In 1614, years before the Pilgrims arrived, an English captain named Thomas Hunt tricked him and other men onto his ship and carried them away across the ocean. Tisquantum ended up in Spain, and later in England, where he learned to speak English. After many adventures, he finally found a ship home in 1619. But when he walked back to Patuxet, the village where he'd grown up was empty. His family and neighbours were gone. Can you imagine coming home after so long, and finding only silence? Yet Tisquantum didn't turn away from the English. He chose to live among them, and helped them. He showed them where to fish, and how to plant corn so that it grew well in this soil. Without him, the little village might not have survived its first year.

{img:10-hobbamock-family-wetu-near-village}

By the autumn of 1621, a Wampanoag family lived right beside the English village.

[pause]

Massasoit sent one of his trusted men, Hobbamock, to live near Plymouth. Hobbamock was a pniese, a respected warrior and counsellor, and he came with his family. They built their own home a short walk from the English houses. You'd see their smoke rising in the morning, beside the smoke from the English chimneys. Hobbamock helped the settlers with advice and translation, and he was an important link between the two peoples. Tisquantum and Hobbamock didn't always agree, and sometimes there was a little rivalry between them. That's very human, isn't it? But together, they helped the English understand the land and the people around them. For the Wampanoag, it was also a way of keeping an eye on these strange newcomers. Friendship and caution went hand in hand. And Hobbamock's family must have watched the English struggling with their first harvest with great interest.

{img:11-wampanoag-women-gardens-planting}

In Wampanoag communities, the fields belonged to the women.

[pause]

The women planted, tended and harvested the crops. They decided which seeds to keep for next year and where to plant them. They worked with hoes made from wood, stone or large clam shells. Their knowledge was passed down from grandmother to mother to daughter, over many generations. You'd see them working together in the fields, talking and singing, with small children playing nearby or sleeping in a cradleboard in the shade. Older girls learned by helping. The men hunted, fished, built canoes and cleared new fields, and they helped carry heavy loads. Now, you might think the farming was the lesser job. It wasn't. The crops fed everyone for much of the year, so the women's work was the very heart of the community. When the English arrived, they were surprised to see women in charge of the fields. In England, it was mostly men who ploughed and sowed.

{img:12-cornfield-mounds-three-sisters}

Corn, beans and squash were planted together in the same small hills, and people called them the Three Sisters.

[pause]

Here's a clever idea. In each small mound of earth, the women planted a few kernels of corn. When the corn had grown a little, they planted beans around it. The beans climbed up the corn stalks, using them like poles. Then they planted squash between the mounds. Its broad leaves shaded the ground, kept the soil moist and kept weeds down. Beans also help to feed the soil, so the corn grew better. Each plant helped the others, like sisters looking after one another. That's why the Haudenosaunee and other peoples called them the Three Sisters. You'd walk between the mounds and brush past big green leaves and twisting bean vines. And when the three were cooked together, they made a very good meal too. Isn't it lovely when something useful is also beautiful? Many gardeners still plant this way today.

{img:13-nasaump-cooking-pot-fire}

A favourite Wampanoag dish was nasaump, a warm porridge of ground corn with berries and nuts.

[pause]

To make nasaump, you'd grind dried corn into a coarse meal between two stones. Then you'd simmer it slowly in water in a pot over the fire, and stir in whatever was in season. In summer, that might be strawberries or blueberries. In autumn, crushed walnuts or chestnuts. It's a little like a sweet, nutty porridge. The English learned to make it too, and they called it samp. There was also a stew of corn and beans cooked together with a little fish or meat, which the Narragansett neighbours called msickquatash. We know it today as succotash. You can still eat it in New England. Food was cooked in clay pots or in bark containers. Sometimes, hot stones were dropped into a container of water to make it boil. I love that little trick. Would you like a bowl of nasaump on a cold morning?

The pot is scraped clean, and the fire burns low. Now let's walk up from the shore to see the little English village, and the people who'd only just arrived.

[pause]

In the autumn of 1621, Plymouth was just a single dirt street with a few small houses.

[pause]

If you walked up from the harbour, you'd find one street climbing the hill, with a few houses along each side. The houses were small, made of rough timber, with thatched roofs of grass and reeds. Each family had a garden plot behind its house, fenced in to keep out animals. At the top of the hill there was a flat place where the colonists planned to build a fort. Below, the little stream ran down to the harbour, giving fresh water. It was tiny. {img:15-settlers-house-interior-hearth}

Inside a Plymouth house, a whole family lived, cooked and slept in one or two rooms.

[pause]

You'd step through a low wooden door into a dim room. The floor was packed earth. The walls were made of timber, and the gaps were filled with clay and straw. There was a wide fireplace at one end, with a chimney made of wood plastered thickly with clay. Over the fire hung an iron pot. That fire was the heart of the house. It gave warmth, light and every hot meal. Furniture was simple: a table made from boards, a few benches or stools, perhaps one chair for the head of the family, chests for clothes and a bed or two. {img:16-pilgrims-colourful-clothing-village}

The Pilgrims didn't wear black clothes with big buckles on their hats.

[pause]

You might picture them all in black, with tall hats and shiny buckles. That's a picture from much later paintings and cards. In fact, buckles on hats and shoes became fashionable decades afterwards. {img:17-pilgrims-name-book-candle}

The settlers didn't call themselves Pilgrims.

[pause]

That name came much later. Many of them simply called themselves saints, or brethren, or the separatists, because they had separated from the Church of England. The word Pilgrims came from a line in William Bradford's own history of the colony. Writing about the day they left Holland, he said that they knew they were pilgrims, and didn't look much on earthly things, but lifted up their eyes to heaven. More than a hundred and fifty years later, people read that line and began calling them the Pilgrims. The name stuck. And the other passengers, who came for work or adventure rather than faith, were known at the time as strangers. So when you hear the word Pilgrims, you're hearing a beautiful old phrase that became a name. I've always liked that. Have you ever been given a nickname that stuck?

{img:18-governor-bradford-writing-table}

The governor of Plymouth in 1621 was William Bradford, who was only about thirty-one years old.

[pause]

The first governor, John Carver, had died that spring. The colonists then chose William Bradford to lead them. He was a quiet, serious man who loved books and had taught himself several languages. He'd grown up an orphan in a farming village in Yorkshire, and had joined the separatists as a teenager. Now, he was responsible for the whole colony. You'd find him at his table in the evenings, writing letters, keeping accounts and thinking about food, houses and peace with the Wampanoag. Years later, he wrote a long history of the colony, called Of Plymouth Plantation. Without it, we'd know much less about these years. Bradford was elected governor again and again, for most of the next thirty-five years. Can you imagine being chosen to lead a whole village at thirty-one, in a strange new land?

{img:19-myles-standish-musket-hill}

The colony's military leader was a small, red-haired soldier called Myles Standish.

[pause]

Myles Standish had fought as a soldier in the Netherlands before joining the voyage. He wasn't a member of the church group, but the settlers hired him to organise their defence. He was short, and people said he had a quick temper, which earned him the nickname Captain Shrimp, though probably not to his face. You'd see him on the hill above the village, looking out over the harbour and the woods, planning where to put the guns. He trained the men to use their muskets and kept watch over the colony. He could be stern and suspicious. Yet he was also loyal and hard-working, and when many settlers were sick during the first winter, he helped nurse them. {img:20-four-women-hearth-cooking}

By the autumn of 1621, only four of the grown women who had sailed on the Mayflower were still alive.

[pause]

The first winter had been very hard on everyone, and the women had suffered most of all. When the harvest came, only four married women remained: Eleanor Billington, Mary Brewster, Elizabeth Hopkins and Susanna Winslow. Think of what that meant. These four, with the help of the girls and servants, did most of the cooking for the whole colony. They baked, boiled, roasted, salted and stored food for around fifty people. You'd see them working from before sunrise, with their sleeves rolled up, sharing out tasks and calling to the children to fetch water or turn the meat. {img:21-pilgrim-children-names-village-lane}

Some of the Plymouth children had wonderful names, like Love, Wrestling, Remember and Humility.

[pause]

Many children in the colony had ordinary names, like John, Mary or Elizabeth. But some families chose names that expressed their faith or hopes. Love Brewster and his little brother Wrestling Brewster lived on the street. So did Remember Allerton and Humility Cooper. {img:22-village-dawn-rooster-smoke}

The children have been called inside, one by one, and the street is quiet. Tomorrow, though, there's work to be done, so let's be up early with them.

[pause]

In the village, the day began at first light, with fires to light and animals to feed.

[pause]

You'd wake in the half-dark to the sound of someone stirring the embers. The fire had to be coaxed back to life before anything else could happen. Then there was water to fetch from the brook, and the few animals to see to. The colony had goats, pigs and chickens, and two dogs, a mastiff and a spaniel, that had come over on the ship. There were no cows yet. Those wouldn't arrive for a few more years. So there was no fresh milk or butter, which the English missed terribly. Breakfast was usually simple: a bowl of corn porridge, a piece of bread or something left over from yesterday. Everyone ate quickly. {img:23-children-fetching-water-brook}

Children had their own jobs, and fetching water was one of the most important.

[pause]

Every day, someone had to walk down to Town Brook with buckets and carry water back up the hill. It was heavy work, and children did much of it. You'd see them in little groups, wooden buckets in their hands, picking their way down the path and trying not to spill too much on the way back. Children also gathered firewood, fed the chickens, weeded the gardens and turned the meat on the spit by the fire. {img:24-men-felling-trees-clapboard}

The men spent long days cutting timber, partly to build houses and partly to pay their debts in England.

[pause]

The colonists had borrowed money from a group of London merchants to pay for their voyage. In return, they'd promised to send back goods to sell, such as furs, fish and timber. So, alongside building their own homes, the men cut trees and split them into boards called clapboard. You'd hear the steady chop of axes from the edge of the forest all day long. It was hard, sweaty work, even on cool autumn days. They also hunted, fished, mended tools and built fences. Everything had to be done by hand. There were no horses yet to pull carts, so logs were dragged or carried. I find it hard to imagine how tired they must have been each evening. And yet, house by house, the village grew. {img:25-fishing-boat-bay-cod}

The bay was full of cod and bass, but the settlers weren't very good at catching them at first.

[pause]

You might think that people from a seafaring country would be expert fishermen. Most of these settlers had been farmers, weavers, printers and craftsmen in England and Holland. They'd brought the wrong hooks, too big for the fish in the bay. So in their first months, they often came home with very little. Bit by bit, with help from Tisquantum and from trial and error, they learned. By autumn, they were catching cod and bass more often. {img:26-gardens-autumn-herbs-pottage}

Behind every house was a garden with herbs and vegetables brought from England.

[pause]

The colonists had carried seeds with them across the ocean, wrapped up carefully in cloth. In their little gardens behind the houses, they grew herbs like parsley, sage and thyme, and vegetables such as cabbages, turnips, onions and carrots. Some of these did well in the new soil, and some didn't. You'd see a woman bending over the rows with her basket, picking what was ready for supper. {img:27-hearth-cooking-iron-pot-spit}

All the cooking in Plymouth was done over an open fire, with just a few iron pots and pans.

[pause]

There were no ovens in the village yet, not even for bread at first. So everything was boiled, stewed, fried or roasted over the hearth. A large iron pot hung from a hook over the flames. A smaller pan stood on short legs among the coals. Meat or birds were roasted on a long iron rod called a spit, which someone had to turn slowly so it cooked evenly. That was often a child's job, and it was hot work. You'd sit on a stool by the fire, turning and turning, with your face glowing red. Bread was sometimes baked in a covered pot buried in the embers. {img:28-sabbath-meeting-house-quiet}

On Sundays, all work stopped, and the whole village gathered for worship.

[pause]

For the Plymouth settlers, Sunday, which they called the Sabbath, was a holy day. Nobody worked, cooked big meals, hunted or played games. In the morning, everyone walked to the meeting place together. In 1621, they didn't yet have a proper meeting house, so they gathered in the common house or another large room. You'd sit on hard benches for many hours, listening to prayers, readings and a long sermon. William Brewster, the respected elder, usually led them, because their own minister had stayed behind in Holland. They sang psalms from a special book, without any musical instruments. The singing was slow and deep. You might find it very long. But for them, it was a precious time of rest, peace and togetherness after a hard week. Would you be able to sit still for that long?

{img:29-wampanoag-village-autumn-harvest}

The psalms are sung, and the English families rest. Let's slip quietly back into the woods, to see how autumn felt in a Wampanoag village not far away.

[pause]

Autumn was the busiest and happiest time of year in a Wampanoag village.

[pause]

In the autumn, the fields were full and the forest was generous. You'd see women and girls carrying heavy baskets of corn, beans and squash back from the fields, and spreading them out to dry in the sun. Men came back from the hunt with deer and from the shore with fish. Children gathered nuts under the chestnut and walnut trees. Everybody was busy, but it was a cheerful busyness, because there was plenty. Each family prepared for the winter move inland, packing food into baskets and storage pits. In the evenings, people gathered around the fires to eat and talk. Can you smell the roasting chestnuts? The Wampanoag held their own harvest ceremonies of thanks, with songs, dancing and feasting. Giving thanks for the harvest wasn't a new idea to them at all. It was an old and familiar part of the year.

{img:30-green-corn-ceremony-dance}

The Wampanoag gave thanks many times a year, not just once.

[pause]

You might think that giving thanks was something the English brought with them. In fact, the Wampanoag held ceremonies of thanksgiving throughout the year. They gave thanks when the first strawberries ripened, when the corn was green and ready, and when the harvest was gathered in. In their way of thinking, the plants, animals and waters were gifts that deserved respect and gratitude. You'd hear drums and singing drift through the trees in the evening, and see people dancing in a slow circle around the fire. Elders would speak, and food would be shared with everyone, including visitors and those who had little. Some of these ceremonies are still held by Wampanoag people today. I find that very beautiful. {img:31-wampanoag-woman-weaving-mat}

Women wove mats, baskets and bags from reeds, grasses and bark, with beautiful patterns.

[pause]

Weaving was an important skill, and a very old one. Women gathered cattail reeds and grasses in the late summer, dried them and then wove them into long mats for the houses, sleeping mats, baskets and bags. Some bags were woven so finely, with patterns in colours from natural dyes, that they were treasured for years. You'd see a woman sitting outside her wetu in the afternoon light, her hands moving quickly, while a child watched and tried to copy. The mats for one house could take many weeks to make. When a family moved for the winter, they rolled up the mats and carried them along. In the new place, they simply laid them over a new frame of saplings. It's a little like taking your walls with you. I've always found that a clever idea. Would you have had the patience for such fine weaving?

{img:32-deerskin-clothing-preparing-hide}

Wampanoag clothing was made mostly from soft deerskin.

[pause]

After a hunt, nothing was wasted. The meat was eaten, the bones made into tools and the skin turned into clothing. Preparing a deerskin took days. It was stretched on a frame, scraped clean, soaked, worked and smoked until it was soft and supple. You'd see someone pulling and stretching the hide for hours to keep it from going stiff. Men wore breechcloths and leggings, and women wore skirts and wraps. In cold weather, everyone wore mantles of fur or deerskin over their shoulders. Moccasins kept their feet warm and quiet on the forest paths. Clothing could be decorated with paint, shells or porcupine quills. Some people also wore necklaces and belts of wampum beads. The English, in their wool and linen, often admired how practical these clothes were. Can you imagine the feel of soft deerskin on a cool autumn morning?

[long-pause]

{img:33-wampum-beads-shells-making}

Wampum beads were made from the shells of whelks and quahog clams, and they took great skill to make.

[pause]

Along the shore, people collected the shells of whelks, which gave white beads, and quahog clams, which gave rare purple ones. Making each tiny bead was slow work. The shell was broken into small pieces, ground smooth into a little tube and then drilled through the middle with a stone drill. You'd see a craftsman bent over his work for hours, making just a handful of beads. The beads were strung into necklaces and woven into belts with patterns that recorded important agreements and events. Wampum was precious, and it was given as a gift to honour guests and to seal promises. Later, English and Dutch traders began using it almost like money, which changed its meaning a great deal. But to the Wampanoag, it was much more than money. I love that a tiny bead from a clam shell could hold a promise. Would you be able to keep such a small bead steady while drilling it?

[long-pause]

{img:34-children-playing-wampanoag-village}

Wampanoag children learned by watching, helping and playing.

[pause]

There were no schools like the English ones, but children were always learning. Girls followed their mothers and grandmothers into the fields and learned how to plant, weave and cook. Boys followed the men, learning to fish, track animals and make bows. You'd see small children playing games of skill with sticks, stones and balls in the clearings. They raced each other and practised throwing and shooting at targets. In the evenings, elders told stories about the world, the animals and the old heroes. Children learned that every hill and pond had a story. I think that's a wonderful way to grow up, surrounded by stories about the place you live. You'd know your home not just as a map, but as a long, living tale. Do you know any old stories about the place where you grew up?

[long-pause]

{img:35-maushop-giant-legend-dunes-sunset}

In Wampanoag stories, a kind giant called Maushop shaped the islands and the coast.

[pause]

According to old Wampanoag stories, a gentle giant named Maushop once lived on this coast. He was so big that he could wade out into the sea to catch whales, and he shared them with the people. Some stories say that when he emptied the sand from his moccasins, it made the islands of Nantucket and Martha's Vineyard. Others say that the colourful clay cliffs at Aquinnah, on Martha's Vineyard, got their colours from his cooking fires. When the wind blew mist across the water, people said it was smoke from Maushop's pipe. You'd hear these stories by the fire on a long winter evening. Can you picture a giant striding through the waves, with mist drifting from his pipe? Those coloured cliffs are still there today, glowing red, white and yellow in the evening light. I find that a lovely kind of story to fall asleep to.

[long-pause]

{img:36-moving-inland-winter-camp-forest}

When the first frosts came, families moved inland to their sheltered winter homes.

[pause]

Late in the autumn, when the nights grew cold and the first frost silvered the grass, it was time to move. Families packed up their mats, baskets and tools, and walked inland along the forest paths to their winter places in sheltered valleys. There, they lived in long houses shared by several families, with fires burning down the middle. You'd smell wood smoke and dried fish, and hear the soft murmur of voices as people settled down for the long, dark evenings. In winter, the men hunted deer in the snow, and everyone mended, wove, carved and told stories. The stored corn and beans, dried fish and berries kept everyone fed until spring. It was a quieter time, a time for rest and for stories. Can you imagine falling asleep in a long house, with the fires glowing and the snow falling softly outside?

[long-pause]

{img:37-twenty-acres-corn-harvest}

The long houses are quiet in the woods. Back in the little English village, though, an important week is beginning, because out in the fields, the corn is turning gold.

[pause]

That spring, the settlers had planted about twenty acres of corn, and it grew well.

[pause]

In the spring of 1621, with Tisquantum's help, the colonists had planted around twenty acres of corn in the old Patuxet fields. Twenty acres is roughly the size of fifteen football pitches. Now, in the autumn, it stood tall and golden. You'd walk between the rows and hear the dry leaves whisper as you passed. Edward Winslow wrote home that their corn did prove well. That was the most important news in the whole colony. Corn meant bread, porridge and survival through the winter. The settlers had also sown about six acres of barley and peas from England. The barley was fairly good, Winslow said. But the peas weren't worth the gathering. They'd been planted too late, and the sun had scorched them. Can you imagine walking through that field, knowing that this harvest was the difference between a hungry winter and a safe one?

[long-pause]

{img:38-fish-in-corn-mounds-planting}

Tisquantum had shown them how to feed the corn with fish.

[pause]

This is one of the best known stories about Plymouth, and it's true. In the spring, Tisquantum showed the settlers how to plant corn in small hills of earth, and how to put fish into the ground with the seeds. Each spring, the herring came up the brook in great numbers. The colonists caught them and buried two or three in each mound. As the fish broke down, they fed the soil, and the corn grew much better. You'd smell the fields for a while, I'm afraid. And the settlers had to guard them at night, because wolves and dogs tried to dig up the fish. Some historians think this method might have been learned from Europeans, while others think it was an old local practice. Either way, it worked. You might think a fish is an odd thing to plant. But the harvest said otherwise.

[long-pause]

{img:39-husking-corn-evening-barn}

Once the corn was picked, every ear had to be husked and dried before it could be stored.

[pause]

The harvest wasn't over when the ears came in from the field. Each one had to be husked, which means peeling away the dry green leaves around it. You'd sit in a circle with the others, an ear in your lap, pulling the husks back with a satisfying rustle. It was slow work, but it was easier together. People talked and told stories as their hands moved. Children were good at it too. Afterwards, the ears were hung up or spread out to dry, so they wouldn't go mouldy in the winter. Dried corn could be kept for many months. When it was needed, the kernels were rubbed off the cob and pounded into meal in a wooden mortar. I like to think of these evenings, with the rustling husks and the soft talk. Would you have stayed up late to help finish the pile?

[long-pause]

{img:40-corn-storage-pits-baskets}

Some of the corn was stored in pits in the ground, just as the Wampanoag stored theirs.

[pause]

The Wampanoag had a clever way of keeping food through the winter. They dug pits in dry, sandy ground and lined them with grass and bark mats. Then they filled them with baskets of dried corn and beans, and covered them over with more mats and earth. The food stayed dry and safe for months. In fact, the settlers had found such stores on their very first explorations, back in the autumn before. They'd taken some of that seed corn, and later paid the owners back. Now they had their own corn to store. Some went into pits, some into baskets in the houses and common storehouse. You'd see them counting the baskets carefully. Every one mattered. I find it rather touching that the settlers' first winter supplies followed a Wampanoag pattern. Can you imagine opening a pit in midwinter and finding the corn still golden?

[long-pause]

{img:41-governor-sends-fowlers-marsh}

When the harvest was in, Governor Bradford sent four men out to hunt birds for a celebration.

[pause]

Edward Winslow described it in a letter he wrote home that December. Our harvest being gotten in, he wrote, our governor sent four men on fowling, so that we might after a special manner rejoice together. In other words, they wanted to celebrate. Harvest festivals were an old custom in England, and the settlers had a great deal to be thankful for. So four men took their long fowling guns down to the marshes and ponds. You'd hear the ducks and geese calling before you saw them. Autumn was the time of great flocks, flying south along the coast. Winslow wrote that the four men killed so many birds in one day that, with a little help, they fed the whole company for almost a week. That's quite a day's hunting, isn't it? I imagine they came home very muddy and very proud.

[long-pause]

{img:42-ducks-geese-marsh-sunset}

The birds they brought home were probably ducks and geese, and perhaps a few turkeys.

[pause]

Winslow simply wrote fowl, which meant any kind of wild bird. In the autumn, the marshes and ponds around Plymouth were full of ducks and geese, resting on their long journey south. There were also wild turkeys in the woods. Bradford later wrote that besides waterfowl, there was great store of wild turkeys, of which they took many. So it's quite possible that turkeys were on the menu. But we can't be sure they were the main dish. You might think the turkey was always the star of the first Thanksgiving. The truth is, it may have been a duck or a goose instead. Wild turkeys were also leaner and more active than the big farm turkeys we know today. They could even fly short distances. Can you imagine a wild turkey flapping up into a tree to roost for the night?

[long-pause]

{img:43-venison-roasting-spit-outdoor}

The meat we know for certain was eaten at the celebration was venison, a gift from the Wampanoag.

[pause]

Here's a detail that's often forgotten. During the celebration, Winslow wrote, the Wampanoag men went out and killed five deer, which they brought back to the plantation. They gave them to the governor, to Captain Standish and to others. So the biggest share of meat at this famous meal was deer, a gift from the Wampanoag hunters. Five deer is a lot of meat. You'd see it roasting on long spits over outdoor fires, with the smoke drifting into the trees and the fat sizzling as it dripped into the flames. Some was probably stewed in pots, too. I love that this gift is at the heart of the story. It was more than just food. In Wampanoag culture, giving gifts was a way of showing friendship, respect and goodwill. Can you smell the wood smoke and the roasting meat?

[long-pause]

{img:44-massasoit-arrives-ninety-men}

The birds are plucked and the fires are being built. And then, from the edge of the woods, you'd see a long line of people walking towards the village.

[pause]

Massasoit came to the celebration with about ninety of his men.

[pause]

Winslow wrote that many of the Indians came among them, and among the rest their greatest king, Massasoit, with some ninety men. Imagine the scene. The little English village had only about fifty people in it. Suddenly, ninety Wampanoag men walked out of the woods. Nobody knows exactly why they came. Some historians think they heard the gunfire from the hunting and came to see whether their allies were in trouble. Others think they were invited, or simply came as friends do at harvest time. Either way, they stayed. You'd have seen them arriving in their deerskin clothes, some with painted faces or feathers, carrying bows and their own food. For the English, it must have been a little overwhelming. For the Wampanoag, it was a visit to allies who were still very new, and very few. How would you have felt, watching them come?

[long-pause]

{img:45-massasoit-portrait-calm}

Massasoit was a careful leader who had chosen to make peace with the newcomers.

[pause]

Ousamequin, known as Massasoit, was probably in his early forties in 1621. His people had lost many lives in the recent sickness, and he had powerful rivals to the west, especially the Narragansett. In the spring, he'd come to Plymouth and agreed to a treaty of peace and mutual help with the settlers. Each side promised not to harm the other, and to help if the other was attacked. You might think this was simple friendship. It was also a thoughtful choice. The English had guns, and they could be useful friends. Massasoit kept his word for the rest of his life, through some very difficult years. When he fell seriously ill a couple of years later, Edward Winslow travelled to his village and nursed him back to health with broth and care. I find that a very gentle part of this story, don't you?

[long-pause]

{img:46-two-peoples-language-gesture}

Only a few people at the celebration could speak both languages.

[pause]

Picture yourself at the edge of the crowd. You'd hear English, spoken with accents from different parts of England. You'd hear Wampanoag, a language of the Algonquian family, full of long, flowing words. But very few people could understand both. Tisquantum and Hobbamock translated, moving from group to group. Some of the English had learned a few words, and some of the Wampanoag men knew a little English. Much of the time, people must have used gestures, smiles and pointing. Can you imagine trying to share a meal and a joke with someone when you only know ten words of their language? It takes patience, and goodwill. And perhaps a little laughter at your own mistakes. Many place names in New England today, like Massachusetts, Nantucket and Mattapoisett, come from the Wampanoag language and its close relatives. You've probably heard some of them without knowing it.

[long-pause]

{img:47-three-days-celebration-village}

The celebration lasted three days.

[pause]

It wasn't a single dinner at a long table. Winslow wrote that they entertained and feasted Massasoit and his men for three days. You'd see fires burning all over the little village, with food cooking from morning until night. People ate when food was ready, sitting on the ground, on benches or on logs, wherever there was room. There may have been some boards set up as tables, but there weren't enough chairs for a hundred and fifty people, or even for fifty. Guests probably slept in the open or in simple shelters at night. By day, there was eating, talking, games and showing off skills. By night, there were fires under the stars. Doesn't three days of food and company sound rather nice? It must have been the busiest and noisiest the little village had ever been. And for the four women and the children, it must have been very tiring too.

[long-pause]

{img:48-muskets-drill-village-green}

During the celebration, the English men showed off their muskets with marching and firing.

[pause]

Winslow wrote that amongst other recreations, they exercised their arms. That means Myles Standish probably had the men march and fire their muskets in a little display. You'd hear the loud bangs echo off the hills and see clouds of grey smoke drifting across the village. It was partly entertainment, and partly a quiet message. It showed the guests that the English were well armed and could defend themselves. You might think that sounds a bit unfriendly at a party. But displays like this were very common between allies at the time, a little like a parade. The Wampanoag men may well have shown their own skills too, perhaps with bows or in races and games. We don't know exactly. But I like to imagine young men on both sides trying to impress each other, the way young people always have.

[long-pause]

{img:49-games-running-race-field}

There were probably games and sports, though nobody wrote down which ones.

[pause]

Winslow only says amongst other recreations. So we have to imagine a little. The English loved games like running races, wrestling, jumping and a rough sort of football. Children played with balls, hoops and spinning tops. The Wampanoag also had many games, including ball games played on long fields and games of skill and chance with small pieces. Young men on both sides were strong and competitive. It's easy to picture a race across the field at the edge of the village, with people shouting and cheering on both sides. Perhaps someone tripped and everyone laughed. Perhaps a child won a race against a grown man. We'll never know for sure. Would you have joined in the race, or watched from the edge with a piece of roast duck in your hand?

[long-pause]

{img:50-children-sharing-food-fire}

For the children of Plymouth, it must have been the most exciting three days of the year.

[pause]

Think how it must have felt to be a child in the village that week. Suddenly there were dozens of visitors, with clothes, hair and ornaments you'd never seen before. There were fires everywhere, and food you'd never tasted. There were games, gunfire and stories in two languages. Children are curious, and I'm sure they watched everything with wide eyes. Some of them may have tried out a few Wampanoag words, or been shown how to string a bow. Some of the Wampanoag men may have brought their own sons, who'd have watched the English children just as curiously. And at night, lying in bed and listening to the voices around the fires outside, it must have been hard to fall asleep. Do you remember a day from childhood that felt that big and exciting?

[long-pause]

{img:51-harvest-feast-table-food-1621}

The fires burn on into the night, and the smell of roasting meat drifts over the rooftops. So let's look closely at what you'd actually have eaten, and at a few things you wouldn't.

[pause]

The only description of the 1621 harvest feast is a few lines in a single letter.

[pause]

Everything we know for certain comes from Edward Winslow's letter of December 1621, which was printed in London the next year. It's only a few sentences long. It mentions the fowl, the five deer, Massasoit and his ninety men, the three days and the recreations. That's all. Everything else is a careful guess. Historians look at what grew and lived around Plymouth in the autumn, what the settlers wrote about at other times, and what the Wampanoag ate. From that, they can say what was probably there. So when you imagine the table, think of it as a picture painted from a few small clues. I find it rather amazing that such a famous event rests on one short letter. Isn't it strange how a few lines, written by candlelight in a little timber house, can become part of a whole country's story?

[long-pause]

{img:52-corn-bread-pottage-wooden-bowls}

Corn was almost certainly on the table, but not as corn on the cob.

[pause]

The corn of 1621 wasn't the sweet, juicy corn you'd buy at a market today. It was hard flint corn, best dried and ground. So the cooks would have pounded it into meal and made it into bread, or a thick porridge, or added it to stews. You'd tear off a piece of coarse, golden corn bread and dip it into the juices of the meat. Or you'd eat a bowl of warm samp, sweetened perhaps with a few dried berries. There may also have been boiled pumpkin or squash, which both peoples ate. The settlers may have stewed it with a little vinegar or spice, the English way. Simple food, really. But after the hungry months of the first winter, it must have tasted wonderful. Would you have liked a bowl of warm corn porridge on a cool autumn evening?

[long-pause]

{img:53-shellfish-eels-lobster-shore}

There may well have been seafood at the feast, such as eels, clams, mussels and lobster.

[pause]

The bay at Plymouth was full of shellfish. At low tide, you could walk out on the mudflats and dig up clams with your hands. Mussels clung to the rocks in dark blue clusters. Eels lived in the muddy creeks, and lobsters crawled among the rocks offshore. The settlers wrote about all of these at other times, so they could easily have been cooked for the celebration. Funnily enough, the English weren't always very excited about lobster and mussels. In England, they were often thought of as plain, poor people's food. You might find that hard to believe today. But fresh from the cold bay, roasted on the fire or boiled in a pot, they must have been delicious. Can you picture someone wading back to shore with a basket of clams, the water cold around their ankles?

[long-pause]

{img:54-no-potatoes-garden-turnips-onions}

There were almost certainly no potatoes at the first Thanksgiving.

[pause]

Today, mashed potatoes are one of the most loved Thanksgiving dishes. But in 1621, potatoes hadn't yet become a normal crop in New England. They'd come from South America and were still a bit of a curiosity in England too. The colonists certainly weren't growing them yet. Sweet potatoes weren't grown here either. So instead, the vegetables on the table were probably things from the English gardens, like onions, turnips, carrots and herbs, along with the local squash, pumpkins and beans. Many people are surprised by this. You might think of the potatoes as essential. Yet the most famous Thanksgiving meal in history went happily without them. Isn't it funny how dishes we think of as ancient are sometimes quite new? I suspect the cooks of 1621 wouldn't have missed them at all.

[long-pause]

{img:55-pumpkins-squash-field-autumn}

There were pumpkins, but no pumpkin pie.

[pause]

Pumpkins and squash grew well around Plymouth, and both the Wampanoag and the settlers ate them. But pumpkin pie as we know it needs a crust of butter and flour, plenty of sugar and an oven. In the autumn of 1621, the colony had very little sugar, no cows for butter and probably no proper oven yet. So there was no pie. Instead, the pumpkins were most likely cut into pieces and boiled or stewed, or roasted in the ashes of the fire. You might find that a little disappointing. I must admit, I'd miss the pie. Later, as the colony grew and settled, English cooks began to make pumpkin into all sorts of puddings and pies. Within a few generations, pumpkin pie had become a proud New England tradition. But on that first autumn, the pumpkins were simple and warm. Would you like a slice of roasted pumpkin, straight from the ashes?

[long-pause]

{img:56-cranberries-bog-autumn-red}

Cranberries grew wild nearby, but there was no cranberry sauce.

[pause]

Bright red cranberries grow in the wet, sandy bogs of this part of New England. The Wampanoag gathered them in the autumn, ate them, used them for dye and added them to dishes. They called them sasumuneash. So cranberries may well have been part of the feast in some way. But cranberry sauce as we know it is made with lots of sugar, because the berries are very sour on their own. In 1621, there simply wasn't enough sugar for that. If you'd bitten into a raw cranberry, you'd have screwed up your whole face. Can you imagine it? Cranberry sauce came along much later, once sugar became cheaper. Today, cranberries are still grown commercially in Massachusetts, and at harvest time the bogs are flooded so the berries float to the surface. Whole lakes turn red. That's a sight I'd love to see one day.

[long-pause]

{img:57-spoons-knives-fingers-napkins}

Nobody at the feast ate with a fork.

[pause]

Forks were still very rare in England in 1621, and the settlers didn't use them. Instead, people ate with spoons, knives and their fingers. Food was often served in shared dishes in the middle of the table, and people took what they wanted. That's why napkins were large, more like small towels. You'd wipe your fingers on yours, or throw it over your shoulder to keep your clothes clean. Plates were often just thick slices of bread or wooden boards called trenchers, sometimes shared between two people. Drinks came in shared cups too. The Wampanoag guests ate in much the same way, with their hands and with spoons carved from wood or shell. Table manners were simply different. You might find it a bit messy. But I think it sounds rather friendly, everyone reaching into the same dish. Would you mind eating roast duck with your fingers?

[long-pause]

{img:58-beer-water-cups-wooden-table}

The settlers probably drank water and a little homemade beer.

[pause]

In England, people often drank weak beer, because the water in towns could be unsafe. The Mayflower had carried barrels of beer across the ocean. By the autumn of 1621, that beer was long gone. Bradford wrote, with some pride, that the water at Plymouth was as good as any in the world, even if it wasn't quite as pleasant as beer. So most people drank water from the brook. The settlers may have brewed a little beer from their barley harvest, which had grown fairly well. There was no tea or coffee in New England yet. No milk either, because there were still no cows. You'd raise a wooden cup of cold, clear brook water to your lips at the end of a long, busy day. Would you have missed your tea or coffee, if you'd been there? I'm afraid I would have.

[long-pause]

{img:59-village-evening-after-feast-embers}

The last of the venison is eaten, and the guests have walked home into the woods. Let's stay a little longer in the village, you and I, as the autumn evenings grow longer and calmer.

[pause]

After three busy days, the village fell quiet again.

[pause]

When the last guests had gone, there was a great deal of tidying up to do. Fire pits had to be raked out, pots scrubbed, bones cleared away and borrowed benches carried back inside. You'd see the children yawning as they helped, still full of excitement and roast venison. Then, one by one, the families went back into their small houses and closed the doors. The smoke curled up from the chimneys into the evening air. The village was quiet, apart from a dog barking somewhere and the soft lapping of the harbour. I imagine everyone slept very well that night. They'd worked hard, eaten well and made new friends. Have you ever felt that kind of happy tiredness after a big family gathering, when the house is suddenly quiet again?

[long-pause]

{img:60-candle-making-tallow-evening}

Light was precious, so the settlers made their own candles and often used firelight instead.

[pause]

In the autumn evenings, the days grew short, and darkness came early. Light was precious. The settlers made candles by dipping wicks again and again into melted animal fat, called tallow, until they grew thick enough. It was smelly, sticky work. Candles were used carefully, never wasted. Often, families simply sat by the light of the fire. Some used thin sticks of pitch pine, which burned brightly and were sometimes called candlewood, a trick they learned in the new land. You'd sit in the warm glow, mending a shirt or carving a spoon, while the shadows danced on the walls. Children played quiet games or listened to stories. Can you imagine an evening lit only by a fire and a single candle? I find that rather peaceful, don't you?

[long-pause]

{img:61-spinning-wheel-evening-hearth}

Women and girls spent many evenings spinning and sewing by the fire.

[pause]

Every piece of clothing had to be made or mended at home, and there was no shop to buy cloth. So in the evenings, women and girls sat by the fire with their needles, patching knees and elbows, turning collars and sewing new shirts from the cloth they'd brought from England. When there was wool, they spun it into yarn on a spinning wheel, which made a soft whirring sound. You'd hear it humming gently in the background as people talked. Older girls taught younger ones to sew straight seams and neat little stitches. Some girls made samplers, small pieces of cloth stitched with letters and patterns, to practise their skills. One such sampler, made by a girl called Loara Standish, Myles Standish's daughter, still survives today. I love that little detail. Would you have had the patience for those tiny stitches?

[long-pause]

{img:62-reading-bible-family-firelight}

Many of the settlers could read, and books were treasured.

[pause]

For people of their time, a large number of the Plymouth settlers could read, because they wanted to read the Bible themselves. William Brewster had brought a whole library of books across the ocean, more than three hundred of them, which was a great deal for the time. In the evenings, a father or mother might read aloud by the fire, while the children listened. You'd hear the slow, careful reading of familiar old words, and the crackle of the fire. Children learned their letters from a little board called a hornbook, with the alphabet on it, covered by a thin, clear sheet of horn to protect it. Some families also had almanacs, books of psalms and stories. Isn't it lovely to imagine a little library in a timber house at the edge of a vast forest?

[long-pause]

{img:63-night-watch-hilltop-stars}

Every night, men took turns to stand watch over the sleeping village.

[pause]

Even in peaceful times, the settlers kept a watch through the night. Each evening, a few men took their muskets and lanterns up to their posts, and stayed awake while the others slept. You'd see a small light moving slowly up on the hill, and hear footsteps on the frosty ground. The watchmen listened for anything unusual, like a fire, a ship in the bay or a wolf near the animals. Mostly, nothing happened. They'd stand in the cold, looking up at a sky full of stars, far brighter than anything in an English town, and listen to the owls in the woods. I imagine it could feel very lonely, and very beautiful. At dawn, they'd walk down, stiff and tired, to warm themselves by the fire. Would you have liked a night on watch, under all those stars?

[long-pause]

{img:64-letters-home-england-ship-hope}

People in Plymouth thought often of the family and friends they'd left behind.

[pause]

Most of the settlers had left parents, brothers, sisters or even children behind in England or Holland. Some hoped those loved ones would come over on a later ship. Letters could take many months to cross the ocean, and only when a ship happened to sail. So every letter was read again and again, until the paper was soft and worn. You'd see someone sitting by the window, reading a letter for the tenth time, smiling at a familiar turn of phrase. William Brewster's daughters Fear and Patience were still in Holland, and they came over a couple of years later. Can you imagine waiting so long for news, without any way to call or send a message? It makes me appreciate a simple letter in the post a little more. Who would you write to, if you were there?

[long-pause]

{img:65-first-snow-village-morning-quiet}

One morning in late autumn, the settlers woke to find the first snow.

[pause]

As November turned towards December, the nights grew colder. Then, one morning, you'd open the door and see the whole village white and silent. The thatched roofs had soft caps of snow. The fences and the garden rows were outlined in white. The harbour was grey and still, and the smoke from the chimneys rose straight up into the cold air. Children ran out to make footprints and throw snowballs. The adults looked at the woodpiles and the storehouse and thought about the long months ahead. This time, they were ready. There were houses, firewood, stored corn and friends in the woods. It was very different from the year before. I like to think of them standing in the doorway, breathing the cold, clean air, and feeling quietly grateful. Can you feel the hush of the snow?

[long-pause]

{img:66-corn-pounding-mortar-winter}

All winter, corn had to be pounded into meal by hand, a little every day.

[pause]

There was no mill in Plymouth yet. So every day, someone had to turn dried corn into meal for bread and porridge. The kernels were put into a hollowed log called a mortar and pounded with a heavy wooden pole called a pestle. Thump, thump, thump. You'd hear the sound from several houses each morning, like a slow drum. It was hard on the arms, and children often took turns. The Wampanoag pounded their corn the same way, and the English had learned from them. The coarse meal was then sifted and cooked. A few years later, the colony built a water mill on the brook, which made life much easier. Until then, it was thump, thump, thump, every single day. I find that rhythm rather soothing, don't you? Could you have pounded corn every morning before breakfast?

{img:67-ship-fortune-arrives-harbour}

Let's go back a few weeks now, to a grey November morning, before the first snow. Down in the harbour, someone is pointing out to sea, where a small sail has appeared.

[pause]

In November 1621, a small ship called the Fortune arrived with thirty-five new settlers.

[pause]

One day in November, the lookouts saw a ship coming into the bay. At first, people feared it might be French, or pirates. Then they saw it was English. It was the Fortune, a small ship sent by the London merchants. On board were thirty-five new settlers, many of them young men, and a few relatives of people already in Plymouth. You can imagine the excitement. There were letters from home, news from England and old friends to greet. William Brewster's eldest son Jonathan was among them. But there was a problem. The newcomers had brought almost no food, no extra clothing and very few supplies. Suddenly, the corn that had seemed so plentiful had to feed many more mouths through the winter. Bradford had to cut everyone's food in half. Can you imagine the mixed feelings in the village that night?

[long-pause]

{img:68-winslow-letter-candle-quill}

Edward Winslow wrote his famous letter just before the Fortune sailed home.

[pause]

The Fortune stayed for only a couple of weeks before sailing back to England, loaded with clapboard and beaver skins. Before she left, the settlers wrote letters to friends and family. Edward Winslow wrote one, dated the eleventh of December, 1621, telling what had happened in their first year. You'd see him sitting by the fire late at night with a quill, choosing his words carefully. In it, he described the harvest, the birds, the three days with Massasoit and the five deer. He also wrote warmly about the land and its people, and encouraged others to come. He said that, by the goodness of God, they were so far from want that they often wished their friends could share in their plenty. Those few lines became the whole story of the first Thanksgiving. Sadly, the Fortune was captured by French pirates on the way home. But the letter got through.

[long-pause]

{img:69-winter-village-snow-smoke}

The second winter was hungry, but much calmer than the first.

[pause]

With all the extra mouths to feed, the winter of 1621 into 1622 was lean. People ate small portions and were often hungry. But it wasn't like the terrible first winter, when so many had died. Now everyone had a house, a fire and the knowledge they'd gained in their first year. You'd see snow settling softly on the thatched roofs, and smoke curling out of every chimney. Inside, families mended clothes, sharpened tools and told stories by the fire. The men still went out to fish and hunt when the weather allowed. Children slid on the ice of the frozen brook. The days were short and the nights very long and quiet. I imagine people often thought back to the autumn feast, and the taste of roast venison, and hoped there'd be another one. Can you hear the wind outside, and the fire crackling gently within?

[long-pause]

{img:70-drought-rain-field-1623}

The settlers' first official day of thanksgiving came two years later, when rain ended a long drought.

[pause]

You might think the harvest feast of 1621 was what the settlers called a thanksgiving. For them, it wasn't. A day of thanksgiving was a solemn religious day, spent in church and prayer, often after a special blessing from God. In the summer of 1623, the colony suffered a long drought. Week after week, no rain fell, and the corn began to wither. So the settlers held a day of fasting and prayer. Bradford wrote that the very same evening, the clouds gathered, and soft, gentle rain began to fall, and kept falling for days. The corn recovered, and the harvest was saved. Afterwards, they held a day of thanksgiving. Can you imagine standing at the edge of a dry field and feeling the first raindrops on your face? Many people in New England later held days like this every autumn.

[long-pause]

{img:71-plymouth-fort-meeting-house-hill}

Within a couple of years, the settlers built a fort on the hill that was also their meeting house.

[pause]

In 1622, the colonists built a sturdy timber fort on top of the hill above the village. It was square, with thick walls, and cannons mounted on its flat roof. From up there, you could see far out over the bay and deep into the woods. But the fort wasn't only for defence. On Sundays, the ground floor became their meeting house, where the whole colony gathered to worship. Imagine sitting in church with cannons on the roof above your head. You'd hear the wind whistle around the walls during the sermon. I find that a funny picture, I must admit. The hill is still there today, and it's called Burial Hill, because the settlers later used it as a cemetery. Some of the oldest gravestones in New England stand on it, looking out over the harbour.

[long-pause]

{img:72-plymouth-grows-more-houses-1627}

Slowly, the tiny village of 1621 grew into a busy little town.

[pause]

More ships came, bringing more settlers, tools and, at last, cows. In 1624, the first cattle arrived, and children could finally drink milk again. Within a few years, the single street had grown into a small town with more than thirty houses, a second street and a busy harbour. Families planted orchards. People traded corn and goods with the Wampanoag and with other colonies. Plymouth was never very large or very rich. Soon much bigger towns, like Boston, grew up nearby. But it had been first, and its people were proud of that. If you'd walked down the street in 1627, you'd have heard cows lowing, hammers ringing and children chasing chickens between the gardens. It must have felt like a very different place from the cold, sick little village of that first winter. Isn't it amazing what a few years of patience can do?

{img:73-new-england-autumn-thanksgiving-days}

Let's step forward now, you and I, past many winters and many harvests. Because the story of Thanksgiving didn't end in 1621. In a way, it had only just begun.

[pause]

For many years, each colony and later each state chose its own day of thanksgiving.

[pause]

In the decades after 1621, the towns and colonies of New England held days of thanksgiving whenever they felt they'd been blessed, after a good harvest, a victory or the end of a sickness. Over time, it became a habit to hold one every autumn. Families went to church in the morning, and then came home to a big meal. Relatives travelled from far away to be together. You'd see wagons rolling along the country roads on a crisp November morning, filled with grandparents, children and baskets of pies. But each state chose its own date. So Thanksgiving in one state might fall on a different day from Thanksgiving in the next. Some states, especially in the South, hardly celebrated it at all. It was a much loved custom, but not yet a national holiday. Can you smell the pies in those baskets?

[long-pause]

{img:74-sarah-josepha-hale-writing-desk}

A determined magazine editor named Sarah Josepha Hale spent seventeen years asking for a national Thanksgiving.

[pause]

Sarah Josepha Hale was a writer and the editor of a very popular women's magazine. You may know one thing she wrote without realising it. She's the author of the nursery rhyme Mary Had a Little Lamb. Hale believed that a shared Thanksgiving day could bring the whole country together. So, starting in the 1840s, she wrote editorials about it year after year. She sent letters to governors, senators and presidents, one after another, asking them to make it a national holiday. Most of them politely ignored her. She didn't give up. You can picture her at her desk, writing yet another letter by lamplight, year after year. I really admire that kind of patience. Would you have kept writing for seventeen years, after so many polite refusals? She did.

[long-pause]

{img:75-lincoln-proclamation-1863-desk}

In 1863, in the middle of the Civil War, President Lincoln made Thanksgiving a national holiday.

[pause]

In September 1863, Sarah Josepha Hale wrote to President Abraham Lincoln. The country was torn apart by the Civil War. Hale argued that a national day of thanksgiving might help to heal it. A few days later, Lincoln issued a proclamation. He asked all Americans to set apart the last Thursday of November as a day of thanksgiving. It was a quiet, hopeful act in a very dark time. You might think a holiday was a strange thing to create during a war. But perhaps that was exactly when people needed it most. From then on, every president issued a Thanksgiving proclamation each year. Hale had finally won. She lived to see Thanksgiving celebrated across the whole country, every autumn, for the rest of her long life. I find it lovely that one stubborn editor and one tired president gave the country this gentle day.

[long-pause]

{img:76-pilgrim-story-books-nineteenth-century}

The idea that the 1621 harvest feast was the first Thanksgiving became popular only in the 1800s.

[pause]

For a long time, almost nobody connected Thanksgiving with that harvest at Plymouth. Winslow's letter had been nearly forgotten. Then, in 1841, a historian reprinted it and added a little note, calling the event the first Thanksgiving. The idea caught on. Later in the century, schoolbooks, magazines and paintings began to show the Pilgrims and the Wampanoag sitting down together to a feast. Sometimes the pictures got things wrong, with black clothes and buckles, or tepees that the Wampanoag never used. You've probably seen pictures like that. Still, the story became part of how Americans thought about the holiday. Children acted it out in school plays every November. It's a good reminder of how stories grow. A few lines in an old letter became a picture that millions of people share. Isn't it interesting how history and memory slowly weave together?

[long-pause]

{img:77-mourts-relation-book-london-1622}

Winslow's letter about the harvest was printed in London in 1622, in a small book now called Mourt's Relation.

[pause]

When the Fortune's letters reached England, the merchants who had paid for the voyage were eager to share the news. So in 1622, a little book was printed in London. It told the story of the colony's first year, from the landing at Cape Cod to the treaty with Massasoit. At the end, it included Edward Winslow's letter, with those few lines about the harvest, the fowling and the three days with Massasoit and his men. Today, people call it Mourt's Relation, after a name printed in its preface. You'd hold a thin book, with old-fashioned letters and long, winding sentences. Only a handful of copies survive. For a long time, almost nobody read it. Yet without that small book, we'd know almost nothing about the harvest of 1621. Isn't it lovely that such a famous story was saved by such a modest little book? I find that rather comforting.

[long-pause]

{img:78-plymouth-family-cornfield-plots-1623}

Two years after the harvest feast, Bradford gave every family its own field of corn.

[pause]

In the first years, the colonists worked the fields together, and the harvest went into a common store. Everyone took food from it, whether they'd worked hard or not. Bradford noticed that this made people grumble, and that some didn't work as hard as they might. So in the spring of 1623, he tried something new. He gave each family its own piece of land to plant for itself. You'd see families out in their own small fields, with even the women and children helping, because what they grew was theirs to keep. Bradford wrote that much more corn was planted than before. You might think this was a small change. But it changed how the little colony worked, and the harvests that followed were better. Can you picture a whole family bent over their own green rows on a spring morning?

[long-pause]

{img:79-family-thanksgiving-table-modern}

Today, Thanksgiving is one of the busiest travel days of the year in America.

[pause]

Every November, tens of millions of Americans travel to be with their families. Roads, trains and airports are packed. They come for a meal that often includes roast turkey, stuffing, mashed potatoes, cranberry sauce and, of course, pumpkin pie. Many of those dishes, as you now know, weren't at the feast of 1621. But the heart of it is similar. A good harvest, a long table, friends and family, and a moment to be grateful. Some families go around the table, and everyone says one thing they're thankful for. Others watch football, or take a long walk after the meal. And every year, the President of the United States pardons a turkey, which then gets to live out its life on a farm. I love that little tradition. What would you say, if it were your turn at the table?

{img:80-national-day-of-mourning-hill-quiet}

For many Wampanoag and other Native people, Thanksgiving is also a day of remembrance.

[pause]

Since 1970, on Thanksgiving Day, a group of Native Americans and their friends have gathered on Cole's Hill in Plymouth, above the harbour. They call it the National Day of Mourning. They come to remember their ancestors, the lands that were lost and the hard years that followed the arrival of the English. The friendship of 1621 didn't last. Within fifty years, there was a terrible war between the colonists and the Wampanoag and their allies, and much of their land was taken. That's a sad part of this story, and I'll touch it only gently. But it matters. For the Wampanoag people, the harvest celebration is only one chapter of a much longer story. Many families honour both the gratitude of the day and the memory of what came after. Isn't it right that there's room for both, on the same quiet hill?

{img:81-plymouth-harbour-today-autumn}

The hill above the harbour is quiet now, and the visitors have gone home. So what would you find if you came to Plymouth yourself, on a crisp autumn morning?

[pause]

Plymouth is now a peaceful seaside town, and the first street still climbs the hill.

[pause]

If you visit Plymouth today, you'll find a friendly small town by the sea, with boats bobbing in the harbour and white wooden houses among the trees. The original street, now called Leyden Street, still runs up the hill from the shore, just where the first seven houses stood. You can walk up it slowly, imagining the timber houses and fenced gardens on either side. At the top is Burial Hill, with its old gravestones and its view over the bay. Down by the water, the little brook still runs into the harbour. In autumn, the maple trees turn bright red and gold, and the air smells of salt and fallen leaves. Thanksgiving is a big occasion here, with a parade and many visitors. Would you like to stand at the top of Leyden Street one cool morning, and look out at the bay?

{img:82-living-museum-patuxet-homesite}

At a museum near Plymouth, you can step into a Wampanoag homesite and an English village of 1627.

[pause]

A few miles south of the town, there's a living history museum now called Plimoth Patuxet. On one side, by a river, there's a Wampanoag homesite, with wetu houses, gardens and a canoe being made by burning and scraping. The people there are Native, and they talk about their ancestors' lives and their own lives today. On the hill above, there's a reconstruction of the English village in 1627. Actors dressed in the clothes of the time go about their work, and they speak in the old English accents of the people they represent. {img:84-wampanoag-language-classroom-today}

The Wampanoag language, which had not been spoken for generations, is being brought back to life.

[pause]

For a long time, the Wampanoag language wasn't spoken in daily life by anyone. But it hadn't completely disappeared. In the 1600s, a missionary named John Eliot worked with Native translators to make a Bible in their language. It was printed in 1663, and it was the first Bible ever printed in North America. Many other letters, deeds and records were written in the language too. In the 1990s, a Wampanoag woman named Jessie Little Doe Baird began studying these old writings. With other scholars and her community, she pieced the language back together. Today, there are classes, a dictionary and a school, and some children are growing up speaking it again. You might think a sleeping language can never wake up. This one did. I find that one of the most hopeful stories I know. Isn't it wonderful to imagine the old words spoken again by children?

{img:85-wampanoag-community-today-gathering}

The Wampanoag are still here, and many of them live on Cape Cod and Martha's Vineyard.

[pause]

It's easy to tell this story as though the Wampanoag belong only to the past. They don't. Today, thousands of Wampanoag people live in southern New England. The Mashpee Wampanoag on Cape Cod and the Aquinnah Wampanoag on Martha's Vineyard are two tribes with their own governments. They hold gatherings called powwows, with drumming, dancing, food and families coming together, some of which have taken place every summer for many decades. Wampanoag people work as teachers, fishermen, artists, nurses and builders, like anyone else. And they keep the old knowledge alive, from planting the Three Sisters to making canoes and telling the old stories. You might pass a Wampanoag family on a beach on Cape Cod without knowing it. {img:86-town-brook-park-autumn-today}

The little brook that gave Patuxet its name still runs through Plymouth today.

[pause]

If you follow the path down from Leyden Street, you'll come to Town Brook, the same small stream that tumbled over the little falls at Patuxet. In recent years, people have removed old dams so the fish can pass more easily again. You'd sit on a bench and listen to the water, and watch the leaves drift by on the current. It's a peaceful spot. {img:87-cranberry-harvest-flooded-bog}

Every autumn, the cranberry bogs near Plymouth are flooded, and whole fields turn bright red.

[pause]

The wild cranberries that the Wampanoag once gathered are now grown on farms all around Plymouth. In the autumn, farmers flood the bogs with water. Workers in tall waders walk through the water, pushing the berries together with long floating booms. It's one of the most beautiful sights of the New England autumn. A statue of Massasoit stands on the hill above Plymouth harbour.

On Cole's Hill, above the water, there's a bronze statue of Massasoit. It was made by the sculptor Cyrus Dallin and put up in 1921, three hundred years after that first harvest.
