In the autumn of 1621, about fifty English colonists and around ninety Wampanoag men shared a harvest celebration at Plymouth, on the coast of what is now Massachusetts. It lasted three days. Only a few lines about it were ever written down. Yet it later became one of the best-known events in American history. In this story, we will look at the Wampanoag homeland, the small English village, the harvest itself, and how a modest feast became a national holiday.

Long before any English ship arrived, this coast was the homeland of the Wampanoag, the People of the First Light. The Wampanoag had lived in what is now southeastern Massachusetts for more than twelve thousand years. Their name is usually translated as the People of the First Light, or the People of the Dawn. It fitted their home well. They lived on the eastern edge of the land, where the sun rose first out of the sea. Their country stretched from Cape Cod and the islands of Martha's Vineyard and Nantucket inland across rivers, ponds and forests. Around the year 1600, they lived in dozens of villages, each with its own leaders and fields. Archaeologists have found their shell heaps, tools and hearths all along the coast, some of them thousands of years old. Yet this was not an empty wilderness waiting to be discovered. It was a homeland, full of named places, family fields and well-known paths. When the English arrived in 1620, they stepped into a country that had been carefully lived in and looked after for a very long time.

[long-pause]

{img:02-wampanoag-family-path-forest}

The Wampanoag moved between summer and winter homes, following the seasons. Wampanoag families did not stay in one place all year. In spring and summer, they lived near the coast and the rivers. There they planted their fields, gathered shellfish and caught fish in the shallow bays. When the harvest was finished in autumn, many families moved inland to sheltered valleys in the forest. The trees broke the force of the winter wind, and firewood was close at hand. Families carried mats, baskets and cooking pots along well-worn paths between the two places. English writers in the 1620s described this pattern with some surprise, because English farmers lived in one house all their lives. Yet the Wampanoag way made good sense. Each season, people went where the land offered the most food and the best shelter. Their year had a steady rhythm, shaped by planting, fishing, hunting and harvest. It was a way of living that fitted closely to the land and its changes.

[long-pause]

{img:03-wetu-dome-house-woven-mats}

A Wampanoag house, called a wetu, was a dome of bent saplings covered with bark or woven mats. The wetu was a simple and clever kind of house. Builders pushed long, flexible saplings into the ground in a circle and bent them over into arches. They tied the arches together to make a strong, rounded frame. Then they covered it with sheets of bark, or with mats woven from cattail reeds, which shed the rain well. A small opening at the top let smoke escape from the fire in the middle. Inside were low platforms along the walls, covered with mats and furs for sitting and sleeping. In summer, mats could be rolled up to let the breeze through. In winter, families often lived in larger longhouses, where several households shared the warmth of more than one fire. English visitors wrote that these houses were warmer and drier than they expected. Reconstructed wetus can be seen today at living history museums in Massachusetts. They show how well a house of wood, bark and reeds could suit the New England seasons.

[long-pause]

{img:04-canoe-dugout-river-mist}

Wampanoag canoes, called mishoon, were made from a single tree trunk hollowed out with fire. Making a mishoon took skill and patience. Builders chose a tall, straight tree, often a pine or a chestnut, and felled it. Then they lit small, controlled fires along the top of the log. As each patch of wood burned and softened, they scraped it away with shells and stone tools. The process was repeated again and again until the log became a long, smooth boat. A large mishoon could carry several people and their goods along rivers and the coast. Canoes were used for fishing, travelling, trading and visiting relatives in other villages. Archaeologists have found old dugout canoes preserved in the mud of New England ponds. Some are hundreds of years old. Wampanoag craftspeople still make mishoon in the traditional way today, burning and scraping them by hand. The canoe was more than a tool. It connected villages, families and fishing grounds across a land shaped by water.

[long-pause]

{img:05-fishing-weir-river-autumn}

Every spring, rivers filled with fish, and the Wampanoag caught them with traps called weirs. In spring, great numbers of herring swam up the rivers from the sea to lay their eggs in freshwater ponds. The Wampanoag built weirs across the streams to catch them. A weir was a fence of wooden stakes and woven branches. It let the water through but stopped the fish. People could then scoop them up in baskets. The bays and shores offered much more as well. There were cod and bass in the deeper water, eels in the muddy creeks, and clams, oysters and lobsters along the shore. Early English writers described the coast as remarkably rich in fish. Fish were eaten fresh, or dried and smoked for the winter. Knowing when and where each kind would appear was important knowledge, passed down through generations. Even so, it was not easy living. It depended on skill, planning and close attention to the seasons. The rivers and the sea were a pantry that had to be understood.

[long-pause]

{img:06-autumn-forest-deer-clearing}

The Wampanoag regularly burned the undergrowth in the forest, keeping it open for deer and for travel. When English settlers first walked through the New England woods, many were surprised by what they saw. In many places, the forest was open and park-like, with large trees standing well apart and grass beneath them. This was the result of careful work. Native peoples set controlled fires to clear away brush and fallen branches. Fresh grass grew back afterwards and attracted deer, which made hunting easier. The open ground also made walking and travelling much simpler. Several early English writers mentioned this burning in their accounts. Historians now see it as a form of land management, practised for a very long time. Hunters knew the clearings well and waited at their edges in the early morning. A successful hunt brought venison for food and deerskin for clothing. The forest, in other words, was not untouched. It had been gently shaped by people for countless generations to provide what they needed.

[long-pause]

{img:07-sachem-gathering-council-fire}

Each Wampanoag community was led by a sachem, who governed with the advice of respected elders. A sachem was a leader, but not a king in the European sense. Sachems were expected to be generous, wise and fair, and to help people in need. Before making important decisions, a sachem met with elders and counsellors and listened to their views. Matters were often discussed at length before agreement was reached. Women could also be sachems, which surprised many European visitors. In 1621, the most powerful sachem in the region was Ousamequin, often known by his title, Massasoit, meaning great sachem. His home village was Sowams, near present-day Warren in Rhode Island. Several smaller communities looked to him for leadership and protection. English writers described him as a dignified man who spoke carefully. Even so, his position was not unlimited. A sachem kept his authority by keeping the trust of his people. Leadership among the Wampanoag rested on respect and generosity as much as on power.

[long-pause]

{img:08-patuxet-town-brook-harbour}

The English settlement of Plymouth was built on the site of an older Wampanoag village called Patuxet. Patuxet is usually translated as "at the little falls." The name came from a small stream that tumbled over rocks into the harbour. For generations, a Wampanoag community lived there, fishing in the bay and growing corn on the slopes above the shore. A few years before the English arrived, an epidemic swept along the coast between about 1616 and 1619. It had been brought by European sailors and traders, and the local people had no resistance to it. The death toll was very high, and Patuxet was left almost empty. When the Mayflower passengers came in December 1620, they found cleared fields and no one living there. They built their village on that land. Historians still debate exactly which disease caused the epidemic. Yet its effect is clear. Plymouth did not grow on untouched ground. It grew on the fields of a community that had been there for a very long time.

[long-pause]

{img:09-tisquantum-portrait-shore}

Tisquantum, a man from Patuxet, had been taken across the Atlantic years before the Pilgrims arrived. Tisquantum is often called Squanto in English. In 1614, an English captain named Thomas Hunt lured him and about twenty other men onto his ship. Hunt carried them to Spain, intending to sell them. Tisquantum later reached England, where he lived for some time and learned to speak English. Around 1619, he finally found a ship back to New England. When he returned to Patuxet, he found that his village had been emptied by the epidemic. Even so, he chose to help the English who settled there in 1620. According to William Bradford, he showed them where to catch fish and how to plant corn so that it grew well. He also acted as an interpreter between the colonists and Massasoit. Bradford called him a special instrument sent by God for their good. His life shows how deeply Europe and New England were already connected, years before the Mayflower sailed.

[long-pause]

{img:10-hobbamock-family-wetu-near-village}

From 1621, a Wampanoag counsellor named Hobbamock lived with his family beside Plymouth. Massasoit sent one of his trusted men, Hobbamock, to live near the English settlement. Hobbamock was a pniese, a respected warrior and adviser. He came with his family and built his own home a short distance from the English houses. English writers, including Edward Winslow, mentioned him often. He helped the colonists as a guide and interpreter, and he was an important link between the two peoples. Hobbamock and Tisquantum did not always agree, and the records suggest some rivalry between them. For the Wampanoag, having Hobbamock nearby was also a way to keep watch on the newcomers. Friendship and caution existed side by side. Hobbamock stayed close to the English for many years and later became a Christian. His family's home beside the English village is a reminder that Plymouth was not an isolated English outpost. From its earliest years, Wampanoag people lived, worked and talked alongside it every day.

[long-pause]

{img:11-wampanoag-women-gardens-planting}

In Wampanoag communities, women were responsible for the fields and the crops. Wampanoag women planted, tended and harvested the crops. They decided which seeds to save for the next year and where to plant them. Their tools included hoes made of wood, stone or large clam shells. Agricultural knowledge passed from grandmothers to mothers to daughters over many generations. Women often worked together in the fields, with small children close by. Men hunted, fished, built canoes and helped clear new fields, and they assisted with heavy work. English observers were surprised by this division of labour, because in England men usually did most of the ploughing and sowing. Some English writers misunderstood it entirely. Yet the women's work was the foundation of the community. The crops they grew fed families for much of the year, and stored corn carried them through the winter. Historians today emphasise how central women's knowledge was to life in the region. The fields of New England were, in a very real sense, women's work.

[long-pause]

{img:12-cornfield-mounds-three-sisters}

Corn, beans and squash were grown together in the same small hills, a method often called the Three Sisters. Native farmers in much of eastern North America planted these three crops together. A few kernels of corn were placed in a small mound of earth. When the corn had grown a little, beans were planted around it. The beans climbed the corn stalks as if they were poles. Squash was planted between the mounds. Its broad leaves shaded the soil, kept in moisture and held back weeds. Beans also help to enrich the soil, which benefits the corn. The name Three Sisters comes from the Haudenosaunee, who told stories of three sisters who supported one another. Modern agricultural scientists have studied the method and found it productive and well balanced. Together, the three crops also made a nourishing diet. Even so, the system required careful timing and constant attention through the summer. It was a sophisticated way of farming, developed over many centuries, and it is still used by some gardeners today.

[long-pause]

{img:13-nasaump-cooking-pot-fire}

A common Wampanoag dish was nasaump, a porridge of ground corn often mixed with berries or nuts. To make nasaump, dried corn was pounded into a coarse meal. The meal was simmered slowly in water over a fire. Depending on the season, cooks added strawberries, blueberries, walnuts or chestnuts. The English settlers learned to make a similar dish, which they called samp. Another common dish was a stew of corn and beans, sometimes cooked with fish or meat. The Narragansett, neighbours of the Wampanoag, called it msickquatash, the origin of the English word succotash. Food was cooked in clay pots, or in containers of bark into which heated stones were dropped to bring the water to a boil. Roger Williams, an English writer who lived among the Narragansett, recorded many of these food words in a book published in 1643. Even so, much everyday cooking knowledge was never written down. It survived through families. Several of these dishes are still made in New England kitchens today.

[long-pause]

{img:14-plymouth-village-1621-street-houses}

In the autumn of 1621, Plymouth consisted of a single street with a handful of small houses. The English settlement was very small in its first year. Edward Winslow wrote in December 1621 that the colonists had built seven dwelling houses and four buildings for common use. The street ran up a hill from the harbour, with houses on each side. The houses were small and made of rough timber, with thatched roofs of grass and reeds. Each family had a garden plot behind its house, fenced to keep out animals. At the top of the hill was a site the settlers planned to use for a fort. A small stream, later called Town Brook, ran down to the harbour and supplied fresh water. Only about fifty of the original Mayflower passengers were still alive. Even so, the little street marked a real beginning. After a long voyage and a very hard winter, the colonists now had houses, gardens and a harvest. The modern street on the same line is called Leyden Street.

[long-pause]

{img:15-settlers-house-interior-hearth}

A Plymouth house usually had one or two rooms, where the whole family cooked, ate and slept. Early Plymouth houses were simple. The floors were packed earth. The walls were timber, with gaps filled with clay and straw. At one end was a wide fireplace, with a chimney of wood plastered thickly with clay to resist the heat. The hearth provided warmth, light and every hot meal, so the fire was kept going almost constantly. Furniture was limited: a board table, benches or stools, perhaps one chair, chests for clothing and a bed or two. Children often slept on straw mattresses on the floor. Windows were small and usually had no glass. They were covered with oiled paper or wooden shutters. Archaeologists working in Plymouth have found post holes, hearth stones and broken pottery from these early houses. Reconstructions at living history museums are based on such evidence. The houses were cramped and smoky by modern standards. Yet for people who had spent a winter aboard ship, they were a great improvement.

[long-pause]

{img:16-pilgrims-colourful-clothing-village}

The Plymouth colonists did not dress only in black, and they did not wear buckles on their hats. The familiar image of the Pilgrims in black clothes with buckled hats comes from later paintings and illustrations. Buckles on hats and shoes became fashionable later in the seventeenth century. Records of the colonists' possessions, such as inventories made after their deaths, mention clothing in many colours. Red, green, brown, blue and violet all appear. Men wore linen shirts, woollen breeches, stockings and doublets. Women wore long skirts, fitted bodices and aprons, with linen caps. Black cloth was expensive to dye and was often kept for formal occasions. Everyday clothes were practical and were mended many times, because new cloth had to come from England. William Brewster's inventory, for example, included a violet coat and a green waistcoat. Historians and museum costume makers rely on these lists today. They show a community that dressed much like other English people of modest means. The colourful reality is quite different from the solemn picture in later prints.

[long-pause]

{img:17-pilgrims-name-book-candle}

The settlers did not call themselves Pilgrims; the name came from a phrase in William Bradford's history. The religious members of the Plymouth colony usually called themselves saints, brethren or separatists. They had separated from the Church of England. The word Pilgrims came from William Bradford's history of the colony. Describing the day they left Leiden in Holland, he wrote that they knew they were pilgrims and lifted their eyes to heaven. Bradford's manuscript was not widely known for many years. In the late eighteenth century, writers began using the word Pilgrims for the whole group, and the name stuck. Other passengers on the Mayflower, who came for work or opportunity rather than for religious reasons, were known at the time as strangers. Bradford's original manuscript, Of Plymouth Plantation, survived a long and complicated journey. It was eventually returned from England to Massachusetts in 1897. Today it is kept in the State Library in Boston. A single phrase in that handwritten book gave the colonists the name by which most people now know them.

[long-pause]

{img:18-governor-bradford-writing-table}

William Bradford became governor of Plymouth in 1621, when he was about thirty-one years old. The colony's first governor, John Carver, died in the spring of 1621. The colonists then chose William Bradford to lead them. Bradford had been born in Yorkshire around 1590 and was orphaned as a child. As a teenager, he joined a separatist congregation and later went with it to Holland. He was largely self-taught and read widely, learning several languages. As governor, he was responsible for food supplies, relations with the Wampanoag and the colony's debts to its investors in London. He was re-elected many times and served as governor for most of the next three decades. Bradford also wrote Of Plymouth Plantation, a detailed history of the colony from its origins to 1647. Historians rely on it heavily, though they also note that it reflects his own point of view. Even so, few founders of any settlement left such a full record. Much of what is known about early Plymouth comes from his patient writing.

[long-pause]

{img:19-myles-standish-musket-hill}

The colony's military leader was Myles Standish, a professional soldier hired for the voyage. Myles Standish had served as a soldier in the Netherlands before joining the Mayflower passengers. He was not a member of the religious congregation. The colonists hired him to organise their defence. Contemporary accounts describe him as short and quick-tempered. A critic of the colony, Thomas Morton, mockingly called him Captain Shrimp. Standish trained the men in the use of their muskets and planned the colony's fortifications. He also led expeditions and negotiations with Native communities, some of which became violent in later years. During the first winter, when many colonists were ill, Bradford recorded that Standish was one of the few who stayed healthy and helped nurse the sick. Historians describe him in mixed terms, as both loyal and harsh. His sword, said to have belonged to him, is now held by a historical society. Like many figures of early Plymouth, he is remembered both for service and for conflict.

[long-pause]

{img:20-four-women-hearth-cooking}

By the autumn of 1621, only four of the adult women who sailed on the Mayflower were still alive. The first winter in Plymouth was very hard, and the adult women suffered especially heavy losses. By the time of the harvest, only four married women remained. They were Eleanor Billington, Mary Brewster, Elizabeth Hopkins and Susanna Winslow. With the help of girls and servants, they did most of the cooking for the whole colony. That meant baking, boiling, roasting, salting and storing food for around fifty people. During the harvest celebration, there were also about ninety Wampanoag guests. Historians have pointed out how much work this must have meant for so few women. Their names appear in Bradford's list of passengers, with brief notes on who died and who survived. Very little else was written about their daily lives. Even so, their work underpinned the whole settlement. The famous meal of 1621 depended on the labour of four women and a group of girls, working over open fires.

[long-pause]

{img:21-pilgrim-children-names-village-lane}

Some Plymouth children had unusual names, including Love, Wrestling, Remember and Humility. Many children in early Plymouth had ordinary names such as John, Mary or Elizabeth. Some families, however, chose names that expressed religious ideas or hopes. Love Brewster and his younger brother Wrestling Brewster were sons of William Brewster. Remember Allerton was a daughter of Isaac Allerton. Humility Cooper was a young girl travelling with relatives. Resolved White was a small boy, and his brother Peregrine was born aboard the Mayflower in Cape Cod Harbour in November 1620. These names are all recorded in Bradford's list of passengers. Children and teenagers made up a large part of the colony's population after the first winter. By some estimates, they were close to half of the survivors. Children fetched water, gathered firewood, weeded gardens and watched animals. They were also part of a lively village life. Their names, preserved in a few lines of Bradford's list, are among the most personal details to survive from the colony's first years.

[long-pause]

{img:22-village-dawn-rooster-smoke}

In early Plymouth, the working day began at first light with fires to tend and animals to feed. Each morning, the hearth fire had to be revived from the embers of the night before. Water was fetched from the brook and animals were fed. The colony had goats, pigs and chickens, and two dogs that had come on the Mayflower, a mastiff and a spaniel. Bradford's history mentions both dogs during an early expedition. There were no cattle yet, so there was no fresh milk or butter. The first cows did not arrive until 1624. Breakfast was usually simple, often a bowl of corn porridge or bread with whatever remained from the day before. In the autumn, daylight hours were growing shorter, and work had to be fitted into them. Candles and lamp oil were precious and used carefully. So the rhythm of the day followed the sun closely. The settlers' lives were shaped by the same forces as those of farmers everywhere at the time: light, weather and the needs of animals and crops.

[long-pause]

{img:23-children-fetching-water-brook}

Children in early Plymouth carried out many daily tasks, including fetching water from the brook. Water for cooking, drinking and washing came from Town Brook, the small stream that ran down to the harbour. Carrying it up the hill in wooden buckets was heavy and frequent work, and children did much of it. They also gathered firewood, fed chickens, weeded gardens and turned meat on the spit. Younger children kept birds away from the growing crops. Older girls helped with cooking, spinning and caring for babies. Older boys worked in the fields with the men. This was normal in England at the time, where children were expected to contribute to the household from an early age. Historians of childhood note that work and play were often mixed together. Even so, there was time for games, and toys such as tops and dolls are known from the period. In a small colony where every pair of hands mattered, children were an essential part of the workforce, and their efforts helped the settlement survive.

[long-pause]

{img:24-men-felling-trees-clapboard}

Plymouth's men cut timber both to build houses and to send back to England to pay the colony's debts. The colonists had financed their voyage by agreement with a group of London investors, often called the Merchant Adventurers. In return, the settlers promised to send back goods for sale, such as furs, fish and timber. So alongside building their own homes, the men cut trees and split them into boards known as clapboard. All of this was done by hand, with axes, wedges and saws. There were no horses or oxen yet to haul logs. When the ship Fortune left Plymouth in December 1621, it carried a cargo of clapboard and beaver skins. Bradford estimated the value of this cargo in his history. The investors in London were often disappointed with the returns, and the debt remained a burden for years. Even so, the work shows how closely the small colony was tied to the economy of England. Every board sent home was part of a long and difficult effort to pay for the voyage.

[long-pause]

{img:25-fishing-boat-bay-cod}

Although the bay was rich in fish, the settlers at first had little success catching them. Most of the Plymouth colonists had been farmers, artisans and townspeople in England and the Netherlands rather than fishermen. Bradford noted that they had brought hooks that were too large for the fish in the bay. In their first months, catches were often poor. Over time, with advice from Tisquantum and through trial and error, they improved. By the autumn of 1621, Bradford recorded that they had good supplies of cod and bass. Tisquantum also showed them how to catch eels by treading them out of the mud in the creeks. Edward Winslow later described the abundance of fish and shellfish in letters home. The irony was noted at the time. New England's waters were among the richest in the Atlantic, and fishing fleets from Europe had worked the coast for years. Even so, a small group of inexperienced settlers needed time and help to learn how to use those riches.

[long-pause]

{img:26-gardens-autumn-herbs-pottage}

Behind their houses, the colonists grew herbs and vegetables from seeds brought from England. The settlers carried seeds across the Atlantic, carefully wrapped. In the small gardens behind their houses, they grew herbs such as parsley, sage and thyme, and vegetables such as cabbages, turnips, onions and carrots. Some of these did well in New England soil, and others struggled. Much of what the gardens produced went into pottage, a thick stew that simmered in the iron pot for much of the day. Meat, beans, grains and vegetables were added as they became available. Pottage was eaten with bread and was a staple of ordinary English diets at the time. Gardens also supplied plants used as medicines, since there were no apothecaries' shops nearby. English gardening books of the period describe these plants and their uses. Even so, gardening in a new climate involved many failures. The colonists learned which plants thrived and which did not. Their small gardens combined English habits with the conditions of a new land.

[long-pause]

{img:27-hearth-cooking-iron-pot-spit}

All cooking in early Plymouth was done over an open hearth with a few iron pots and pans. The colonists had no proper bake ovens in their first months, so food was boiled, stewed, fried or roasted over the hearth. A large iron pot hung from a hook or crane above the flames. Smaller pans with short legs stood among the coals. Meat and birds were roasted on an iron spit that had to be turned steadily so the food cooked evenly. This was often a job for children. Bread could be baked in a covered pot buried in the embers. Cooking over an open fire required constant attention to the heat, the fuel and the food. Archaeologists in Plymouth have found fragments of iron pots and pottery cooking vessels from the period. English cookbooks of the early 1600s describe the same methods. Even so, cooking for a family, let alone for a whole colony at harvest time, was slow and laborious work. Much of each day revolved around the fire.

[long-pause]

{img:28-sabbath-meeting-house-quiet}

On Sundays, all ordinary work stopped in Plymouth and the colonists gathered for worship. For the Plymouth separatists, the Sabbath was a holy day set apart from ordinary life. No one worked, hunted or played games. In the first years, there was no separate meeting house, so services were held in the common house and later in the fort on the hill. Services were long, with prayers, Bible readings and sermons. Because their minister, John Robinson, had stayed behind in Leiden, the colony's elder, William Brewster, usually led worship. The congregation sang psalms from a book called the Ainsworth Psalter, without musical instruments. A copy of this psalter is said to have come over on the Mayflower. Visitors from other English settlements sometimes commented on the strictness of these Sundays. Even so, for the colonists themselves, the Sabbath was a time of rest and shared purpose after a week of hard labour. It reflected the religious convictions that had brought many of them across the ocean in the first place.

[long-pause]

{img:29-wampanoag-village-autumn-harvest}

Autumn was the busiest season of the year in a Wampanoag village. In the autumn, the fields were full and the forests offered nuts, game and firewood. Women and girls harvested corn, beans and squash and spread them out to dry in the sun. Men returned from hunting with deer and from the shore with fish. Children gathered chestnuts and walnuts. Families prepared for the move inland by packing food into baskets and storage pits. Harvest was also a time of gatherings and ceremonies of thanks, with songs, dances and shared meals. English observers in the 1620s and 1630s described such gatherings, though often without fully understanding them. Wampanoag oral tradition today also speaks of many seasonal ceremonies of thanksgiving. Giving thanks for the harvest was therefore not a new idea brought by the English. It was already an old and familiar part of the Wampanoag year. The busy autumn of 1621 at Plymouth took place alongside this much older pattern of harvest and gratitude.

[long-pause]

{img:30-green-corn-ceremony-dance}

The Wampanoag held ceremonies of thanksgiving at several points in the year, not only at harvest. Wampanoag tradition includes ceremonies that mark the turning of the seasons. Thanks were given when strawberries ripened, when the green corn was ready and when the main harvest was gathered. In this way of thinking, plants, animals and waters were gifts that deserved respect and gratitude. Ceremonies often included songs, dances, speeches by elders and the sharing of food with all present, including visitors and those in need. Some of these ceremonies continue among Wampanoag communities today. Historians note that seventeenth-century English writers rarely understood these events well, and their descriptions are brief. Much of what is known comes from Wampanoag oral tradition. Even so, the evidence makes one point clear. Gratitude was woven into the whole Wampanoag year, rather than reserved for a single day. When people today speak of the first Thanksgiving, Wampanoag scholars often point out that their ancestors had been giving thanks for a very long time before 1621.

[long-pause]

{img:31-wampanoag-woman-weaving-mat}

Wampanoag women wove mats, baskets and bags from reeds, grasses and bark. Weaving was an important and very old skill. Women gathered cattail reeds and grasses in late summer, dried them and wove them into long mats. These mats covered house frames, lined sleeping platforms and served as floor coverings. Smaller items included baskets for storage and carrying, and finely woven bags with patterns made from natural dyes. Some of these bags were treasured for many years. A few early examples survive in museum collections, showing the fine twining techniques used. When families moved between summer and winter homes, they rolled up the house mats and carried them along. At the new site, the mats were laid over a fresh frame of saplings. English observers admired this practical system. Wampanoag artists still practise traditional weaving today and teach it to younger generations. The craft shows how closely household life, building and the seasonal round were connected. A single mat could be part of a home, a bed and a journey.

[long-pause]

{img:32-deerskin-clothing-preparing-hide}

Most Wampanoag clothing was made from carefully prepared deerskin. After a hunt, nearly every part of the deer was used. The meat was eaten, the bones were made into tools, and the skin was turned into clothing. Preparing a hide took several days. It was stretched on a frame, scraped clean, soaked and worked by hand until it became soft. Smoking helped preserve it and keep it supple. Men wore breechcloths and leggings, and women wore skirts and wraps. In cold weather, people wore mantles of fur or deerskin over their shoulders. Moccasins protected the feet and made little noise on forest paths. Clothing was often decorated with paint, shells or porcupine quills. Some English writers commented that this clothing was warm and practical. Even so, after European trade expanded, many Native people also began to use woollen cloth obtained through trade. Wampanoag clothing in the 1620s therefore reflected both old skills and new materials, as the region's economy began to change.

[long-pause]

{img:33-wampum-beads-shells-making}

Wampum beads were made from whelk and quahog shells and took great skill to produce. Along the shore, people collected the shells of whelks, which gave white beads, and quahog clams, which gave purple ones. The purple beads were rarer and more valued. Making a bead was slow work. A piece of shell was ground into a small cylinder and then drilled through the middle with a stone drill. Beads were strung into necklaces and woven into belts. Some belts used patterns to record agreements and important events. Wampum was given as a gift to honour guests and to confirm promises. In the early seventeenth century, Dutch and English traders began to use wampum almost as a currency in their trade with Native peoples. This changed its role in the regional economy. Historians and Wampanoag scholars emphasise that wampum carried meanings far beyond money. Wampanoag artists still work with quahog shell today, making jewellery and art. The small beads remain a symbol of skill, memory and agreement.

[long-pause]

{img:34-children-playing-wampanoag-village}

Wampanoag children learned mainly by watching, helping and playing. There were no schools like those in England, but learning was constant. Girls followed their mothers and grandmothers into the fields and learned planting, weaving and cooking. Boys followed the men and learned to fish, track animals and make tools and bows. Children played games of skill with sticks, stones and balls, raced one another and practised throwing and shooting at targets. English writers such as Roger Williams noted that Native parents were very affectionate towards their children. In the evenings, elders told stories about the world, the animals and important ancestors. Through these stories, children learned the history of their people and the meaning of the land around them. Many places had stories attached to them. Even so, much of this knowledge was passed on orally and was never written down by outsiders. Wampanoag families today continue to teach children through stories, crafts and community gatherings, much as their ancestors did.

[long-pause]

{img:35-maushop-giant-legend-dunes-sunset}

In Wampanoag tradition, a giant named Maushop shaped the islands and coast of the region. Wampanoag stories tell of Maushop, a kind giant who lived along the coast long ago. He was so large that he could wade into the sea to catch whales, which he shared with the people. According to one story, when he emptied the sand from his moccasins, it formed the islands of Nantucket and Martha's Vineyard. Another story says that the colourful clay cliffs at Aquinnah, on Martha's Vineyard, took their colours from his cooking fires. When fog drifted across the water, people said it was smoke from his pipe. These stories were passed down orally for generations. Some were recorded by English writers in later centuries, often in simplified form. Wampanoag storytellers still tell them today. The cliffs at Aquinnah are now a protected landmark, managed with the involvement of the Aquinnah Wampanoag. The legends show how the land itself was part of the people's history. Every cliff and island had its own story.

[long-pause]

{img:36-moving-inland-winter-camp-forest}

When the first frosts came, many Wampanoag families moved inland to sheltered winter homes. In late autumn, as the nights grew cold, families packed their mats, baskets and tools. They walked inland along forest paths to winter sites in sheltered valleys. There they often lived in longhouses shared by several families, with fires burning along the centre. Winter was a time for hunting deer in the snow, and for repairing tools, weaving and making clothing. Stored corn and beans, dried fish and dried berries fed the family until spring. Long evenings were also a time for storytelling. English writers in the seventeenth century described these seasonal moves, though some misunderstood them as wandering. Archaeological sites inland, with remains of hearths and storage pits, confirm the pattern. Even so, winters could be difficult, especially if harvests had been poor. The move inland was a practical way of reducing the hardships of the cold season. It had been part of life in the region for many centuries before 1621.

[long-pause]

{img:37-twenty-acres-corn-harvest}

In the spring of 1621, the Plymouth colonists planted about twenty acres of corn, and it grew well. The colony's most important crop in its first year was Native corn. With guidance from Tisquantum, the settlers planted about twenty acres of it in the old fields of Patuxet. Edward Winslow wrote in December 1621 that their corn had done well. They also sowed about six acres of barley and peas brought from England. According to Winslow, the barley was reasonably good, but the peas were not worth gathering. They had been planted too late and were scorched by the summer sun. Corn mattered more than anything else. It provided bread and porridge, and it could be dried and stored through the winter. A good corn harvest meant the colony could face the cold months with some confidence. Historians estimate that twenty acres could feed the small population for a year, if nothing went wrong. Even so, the margin was narrow. The success of that first crop was a turning point for the struggling settlement.

[long-pause]

{img:38-fish-in-corn-mounds-planting}

Tisquantum showed the colonists how to fertilise their corn with fish. One of the best-known details of early Plymouth concerns fish in the cornfields. According to the colonists' accounts, Tisquantum taught them to plant corn in small hills and to bury fish in the ground with the seeds. Each spring, herring ran up Town Brook in large numbers. The settlers caught them and placed two or three in each hill. As the fish decayed, they enriched the soil. Winslow reported that without this method, the corn would have yielded little. The fields had to be guarded for some days, because wolves and dogs tried to dig up the fish. Historians have debated where the practice came from. Some argue it was a long-standing Native method in this area. Others suggest Tisquantum may have learned it during his years in Europe or from other coastal peoples. Whatever its origin, the method worked. It helped to produce the harvest that made the autumn of 1621 a time of relief.

[long-pause]

{img:39-husking-corn-evening-barn}

After the harvest, every ear of corn had to be husked and dried before it could be stored. Picking the corn was only the first step. Each ear had to be husked, which meant peeling away the dry outer leaves. Husking was slow work and was often done in groups, with people sitting together around a pile of ears. The ears were then hung or spread out to dry thoroughly, so they would not rot during the winter. Dried corn could be stored for many months. When needed, the kernels were rubbed off the cob and pounded into meal in a wooden mortar. In later New England, husking became a social event, known as a husking bee, with food and games. The early colonists probably worked in a similar shared way, though no detailed account of it survives from 1621. Corn was a new crop for most English settlers. They had to learn not only how to grow it, but also how to dry, store and cook it. Each stage was essential to the colony's survival.

[long-pause]

{img:40-corn-storage-pits-baskets}

Some of the harvest was stored in lined pits in the ground, a Wampanoag method. The Wampanoag had long stored food in pits dug into dry, sandy ground. They lined the pits with grass and bark mats, filled them with baskets of dried corn and beans, and covered them with more mats and earth. Food stored this way stayed dry for months. The English had encountered such stores during their first explorations of Cape Cod in November 1620. They took some of the seed corn they found, and Bradford recorded that they later paid the owners back. By the autumn of 1621, the colonists had their own corn to store. Some went into pits, some into baskets in their houses and some into a common storehouse. Archaeologists have excavated storage pits at Native sites across southern New England, confirming the descriptions in early accounts. The method shows how much the English depended on local knowledge. Their first winter supplies followed a pattern developed by the Wampanoag over many generations.

[long-pause]

{img:41-governor-sends-fowlers-marsh}

When the harvest was gathered, Governor Bradford sent four men to hunt wild birds for a celebration. Edward Winslow described the event in a letter dated the eleventh of December 1621. He wrote that after the harvest was gathered, the governor sent four men out fowling, so that they might rejoice together in a special manner after the fruits of their labour. Harvest celebrations were a familiar custom in England, and the colonists had good reason to be thankful. The four men went to the marshes and ponds with their fowling guns. Autumn was the season when large flocks of ducks and geese passed along the coast. Winslow wrote that in one day, the four men killed enough birds to feed the whole company for almost a week, with a little help besides. This short passage is the main source for the beginning of the celebration. Historians note that it says nothing about the exact date. The event probably took place sometime between late September and early November 1621.

[long-pause]

{img:42-ducks-geese-marsh-sunset}

The birds brought back by the hunters were probably mostly ducks and geese, and possibly wild turkeys. Winslow used the word fowl, which in his time meant wild birds of many kinds. In autumn, the marshes and ponds around Plymouth were full of migrating ducks and geese. Wild turkeys also lived in the surrounding woods. William Bradford later wrote that during that first autumn there was a great store of wild turkeys, of which they took many. So turkey may well have been eaten during the celebration. Historians cannot be certain that it was the main dish, however. Waterfowl were more plentiful and easier to hunt in large numbers. Wild turkeys were also different from modern farm turkeys. They were leaner and faster, and they could fly short distances to roost in trees. The idea of turkey as the centrepiece of Thanksgiving developed much later, in the nineteenth century. In 1621, the table was more likely filled with a mixture of birds from the marshes and the woods.

[long-pause]

{img:43-venison-roasting-spit-outdoor}

The one meat certainly eaten at the 1621 celebration was venison from five deer given by Wampanoag hunters. Winslow's letter contains one specific detail about the meat. During the celebration, he wrote, the Wampanoag men went out and killed five deer. They brought them to the plantation and gave them to the governor, to Captain Standish and to others. Five deer would have provided a large amount of meat. It was probably roasted over open fires and stewed in pots. This detail is often overlooked in popular images of the event. It shows that the largest single contribution of meat came from the Wampanoag guests. In Wampanoag culture, gifts were an important way of expressing friendship, respect and obligation. Historians emphasise that the celebration was not simply English hosts feeding Native guests. Both groups contributed, and the Wampanoag outnumbered the English. The gift of venison is one of the few firm facts about the menu. It places Wampanoag hunters at the very centre of what later became a national story.

[long-pause]

{img:44-massasoit-arrives-ninety-men}

Massasoit came to the celebration with about ninety of his men. Winslow wrote that many of the Native people came among them, including their greatest king, Massasoit, with some ninety men. The English settlement had only about fifty people at the time. So the Wampanoag guests outnumbered their hosts almost two to one. The reason for their arrival is not recorded. Some historians suggest that the Wampanoag heard gunfire from the English hunting or from a musket display and came to see whether their allies were in danger. Others think they had been invited or came as part of normal diplomatic visits. Whatever the reason, they stayed, and they were entertained. The arrival of so many visitors must have changed the scale of the celebration entirely. Food, space and cooking all had to stretch to more than three times the colony's normal population. The meeting reflected the alliance agreed earlier that year, and the careful diplomacy that both sides were practising.

[long-pause]

{img:45-massasoit-portrait-calm}

Massasoit had made a treaty of peace and mutual help with the Plymouth colonists in the spring of 1621. Ousamequin, known as Massasoit, was probably in his early forties in 1621. His people had suffered heavy losses in the epidemic of the previous years. He also faced powerful rivals, especially the Narragansett to the west. In March 1621, he met the Plymouth leaders and agreed a treaty. Each side promised not to harm the other and to come to the other's aid if attacked. The text of the agreement was recorded by the English. Historians view the treaty as a practical decision for both sides. The English gained a powerful ally, and Massasoit gained support against his rivals. He kept the agreement for the rest of his life, through several difficult periods. When he fell seriously ill in 1623, Edward Winslow travelled to his village and helped nurse him back to health. Massasoit died around 1661. Within a few years of his death, relations between the English and the Wampanoag broke down.

[long-pause]

{img:46-two-peoples-language-gesture}

Only a few people at the celebration could speak both English and the Wampanoag language. The English colonists spoke English, with accents from different regions of England. The Wampanoag spoke a language of the Algonquian family. Very few people understood both. Tisquantum and Hobbamock acted as interpreters. Some colonists had learned a few Wampanoag words, and some Wampanoag men knew a little English from earlier contact with traders and fishermen. Much communication must have relied on gestures and simple words. Edward Winslow and Roger Williams later recorded Native words and phrases, which shows how much effort went into learning. Many place names in New England today come from Algonquian languages, including Massachusetts, Nantucket and Mattapoisett. These names are a lasting sign of the original languages of the region. Even so, the language gap meant that misunderstandings were always possible. The celebration of 1621 took place across a real barrier of words, managed through interpreters, goodwill and careful diplomacy on both sides.

[long-pause]

{img:47-three-days-celebration-village}

According to Winslow, the celebration lasted three days. Winslow wrote that the colonists entertained and feasted Massasoit and his men for three days. This was not a single sit-down dinner at one long table. Food was cooked over many fires and eaten as it was ready. People probably sat on benches, logs or the ground, since the colony could not have had enough tables or chairs for so many. At night, the guests likely slept in temporary shelters or in the open. Between meals there was talk, recreation and displays of skill. Harvest celebrations in England could also last several days, so the length was not unusual. The scale, however, was remarkable for such a small settlement. Historians point out how much the four surviving women and the girls of the colony must have cooked during those days. The event was later remembered as the first Thanksgiving. At the time, it was an extended harvest feast shared between allies.

[long-pause]

{img:48-muskets-drill-village-green}

During the celebration, the English men performed a display with their muskets. Winslow wrote that among other recreations, they exercised their arms. This most likely means that Myles Standish led the men in marching and firing their muskets. Such displays were common in England at festivals and on training days. They were partly entertainment and partly a demonstration of strength. For the English, it showed their guests that the colony could defend itself. Military displays between allies were also a normal part of diplomacy at the time. Some historians suggest that the sound of the guns may have brought Massasoit and his men to the settlement in the first place. The Wampanoag men may also have shown their own skills, though no record of this survives. The muskets used were heavy, slow to load and loud. Even so, they impressed many Native observers in the early years. The display was a reminder that friendship in 1621 existed alongside caution and a careful balance of power.

[long-pause]

{img:49-games-running-race-field}

Games and sports were probably part of the 1621 celebration, though no one recorded which ones. Winslow mentioned only other recreations, without details. English harvest festivals of the period often included running races, wrestling, jumping and early forms of football. Children played with balls, hoops and spinning tops. The Wampanoag also had many games, including ball games played on long fields and games of chance played with marked pieces. Seventeenth-century writers such as Roger Williams described some of these Native games. It is likely that some sort of shared contest took place, since young men on both sides were strong and competitive. Historians are careful not to invent details that the sources do not support. Even so, the general picture is reasonable. A three-day gathering of about a hundred and fifty people would hardly have passed without games of some kind. The lack of detail is typical of the period. Winslow was writing a short letter, not a full description of the festivities.

[long-pause]

{img:50-children-sharing-food-fire}

The celebration would have been a rare and memorable event for the children of Plymouth. For the children of the colony, the arrival of about ninety visitors was extraordinary. The Wampanoag guests wore clothing, ornaments and hairstyles unlike anything the English children had known. There were fires and food throughout the day, games, gunfire and conversation in two languages. Historians of childhood note that children in the period often moved freely among adults at such gatherings. Some English children may have learned a few Wampanoag words. Wampanoag men may also have brought sons, though the sources do not say so. Several of the Plymouth children lived into old age and some of their recollections were recorded much later, though not in detail about this event. Even so, the scale of the gathering makes it likely to have been remembered. For a small community that had suffered greatly the winter before, the celebration marked a time of plenty. That contrast would have been felt by young and old alike.

[long-pause]

{img:51-harvest-feast-table-food-1621}

The only eyewitness description of the 1621 harvest feast is a short passage in one letter. Everything known for certain about the celebration comes from Edward Winslow's letter of December 1621. The letter was published in London in 1622. The relevant passage is only a few sentences long. It mentions the fowling, the five deer, Massasoit and his ninety men, the three days of feasting and the recreations. William Bradford's history adds general information about the food available that autumn, but it does not describe the celebration itself. Everything else is careful reconstruction. Historians and food historians study what grew and lived around Plymouth in autumn, what the colonists ate at other times and what the Wampanoag ate. From this, they estimate what was probably served. Even so, many popular details have no basis in the sources. The modern picture of the first Thanksgiving was built up mainly in the nineteenth century. The actual record is remarkably brief, and much of the event remains unknown.

[long-pause]

{img:52-corn-bread-pottage-wooden-bowls}

Corn was almost certainly eaten at the feast, but in the form of bread or porridge rather than on the cob. The corn grown at Plymouth was a hard flint variety, not the sweet corn common today. It was best dried and ground into meal. Cooks would have made it into flat bread, a thick porridge or an ingredient in stews. The English called the porridge samp, after the Wampanoag nasaump. Boiled pumpkin or squash was probably eaten as well, since both peoples grew and cooked it. English cooks of the period sometimes stewed pumpkin with vinegar, butter or spices, though butter was not yet available at Plymouth. Food historians base these suggestions on recipes from English cookbooks and on accounts of Native cooking. The meal would have seemed plain by modern standards. Even so, after the hunger of the first winter, a plentiful supply of corn bread and porridge was a significant comfort. Corn had become the foundation of the colony's diet, as it had long been for the Wampanoag.

[long-pause]

{img:53-shellfish-eels-lobster-shore}

Seafood such as eels, clams, mussels and lobster may well have been eaten during the celebration. The waters around Plymouth were rich in shellfish. At low tide, clams could be dug from the mudflats. Mussels grew on rocks, eels lived in the muddy creeks and lobsters were found among the rocks offshore. The colonists wrote about all of these at various times in their first years. It is therefore likely that some were cooked during the harvest celebration, though Winslow did not mention them. Interestingly, many English people of the time regarded lobster and mussels as humble food. Early accounts describe lobsters so plentiful that they were sometimes considered food for the poor. Attitudes changed greatly in later centuries. Food historians list seafood among the probable dishes because of its abundance and seasonality. Even so, they stress that it remains a reasonable guess rather than a recorded fact. The shore was a constant source of food for both the Wampanoag and the English throughout the year.

[long-pause]

{img:54-no-potatoes-garden-turnips-onions}

There were almost certainly no potatoes at the 1621 harvest feast. Potatoes originated in South America and were not yet grown in New England in 1621. In England, they were still something of a novelty. Sweet potatoes were not grown in the region either. The vegetables available at Plymouth would have included onions, turnips, carrots and herbs from the English gardens, along with the corn, beans, squash and pumpkins grown by the Wampanoag and the colonists. Mashed potatoes, now a standard Thanksgiving dish, became associated with the holiday much later. Food historians often point to this as an example of how modern Thanksgiving menus differ from the original event. White potatoes became common in New England only in the eighteenth century, after they were brought by Scots-Irish settlers. The 1621 meal therefore had a different character. It relied on local crops and a few European garden vegetables. Many of the dishes now considered traditional have no connection to the first harvest at Plymouth.

[long-pause]

{img:55-pumpkins-squash-field-autumn}

Pumpkins were grown at Plymouth, but there was no pumpkin pie in 1621. Pumpkins and other squash grew well around Plymouth, and both the Wampanoag and the colonists ate them. Pumpkin pie, however, requires a pastry crust made with butter and flour, sugar and an oven. In the autumn of 1621, the colony had very little sugar, no cows to provide butter and probably no proper bake oven. Pumpkin was therefore most likely boiled, stewed or roasted in the embers of a fire. Within a few decades, English settlers in New England began making pumpkin into puddings and pies. Recipes for pumpkin pie appear in English cookbooks from the later seventeenth century. By the time of the American Revolution, pumpkin pie was well established in New England cooking. The pie later became one of the symbols of the holiday. Even so, it belongs to a later chapter of the story. In 1621, pumpkins were simply one of the season's useful vegetables, cooked in plain and practical ways.

[long-pause]

{img:56-cranberries-bog-autumn-red}

Cranberries grew wild near Plymouth, but cranberry sauce was not part of the 1621 feast. Cranberries grow in the wet, sandy bogs of southeastern Massachusetts. The Wampanoag gathered them in autumn, ate them and used them in dishes and as a dye. Their name for the berries was sasumuneash. Cranberries may have been eaten during the celebration in some form. Cranberry sauce, however, requires a large amount of sugar, because the berries are very sour. In 1621, the colony had almost no sugar. Recipes for cranberry sauce appear in English and American sources later in the seventeenth century and beyond. Commercial cultivation of cranberries began in Massachusetts in the early nineteenth century. Today, the state remains one of the major cranberry producers in the United States. At harvest time, growers flood the bogs so that the ripe berries float to the surface. Fields of red berries on the water have become a familiar autumn sight. Yet the sauce served at modern Thanksgiving tables was unknown in 1621.

[long-pause]

{img:57-spoons-knives-fingers-napkins}

No forks were used at the 1621 feast; people ate with knives, spoons and their fingers. Forks were rare in England in 1621 and were not used by the Plymouth colonists. People ate with spoons, knives and fingers. Food was often served in shared dishes, and people took portions from them. Napkins were large, more like small towels, and were used to wipe hands and protect clothing. Plates were often wooden boards called trenchers, sometimes shared between two people. Drinks were shared from common cups. The Wampanoag guests also ate with their hands and with spoons carved from wood or shell. Table manners of the period followed their own careful rules, even without forks. Etiquette books from the time advise people to keep their fingers clean and to take food politely from the shared dish. The fork came into general use in England later in the seventeenth century. Museum reconstructions of the 1621 meal now show these older customs, which give a very different picture from later paintings.

[long-pause]

{img:58-beer-water-cups-wooden-table}

The colonists probably drank mostly water during the celebration, and perhaps a little home-brewed beer. In England, people commonly drank weak beer, partly because water in towns could be unsafe. The Mayflower had carried beer across the Atlantic, but by the autumn of 1621 those supplies were long gone. Bradford noted that the water at Plymouth was very good, though he admitted that it was not as pleasant as beer. Most people at the celebration therefore probably drank water from Town Brook. The colonists may have brewed a little beer from their barley harvest, which Winslow described as reasonably good. There was no tea or coffee in New England at the time. Neither drink had yet become common even in England. There was no milk either, since the colony had no cows until 1624. Food historians include water and possibly beer in their reconstructions of the meal. The drinks of 1621 were simple. Even so, a reliable supply of clean water was an important advantage for the small settlement.

[long-pause]

{img:59-village-evening-after-feast-embers}

After the three days of celebration, the village returned to its normal quiet routine. When the guests had gone, there was work to be done. Fire pits had to be cleaned, pots scrubbed and borrowed furniture returned. Then the colony resumed its ordinary pattern of work. Winslow's letter does not describe the days after the feast. However, other records show what life in Plymouth was like in the late autumn. The colonists continued to fish, hunt, cut timber and prepare for winter. Relations with the Wampanoag continued through regular visits, trade and diplomacy. Historians note that the celebration was not seen at the time as an especially important event. Winslow mentioned it briefly, as one item in a longer letter about the colony's progress. Its later fame came from nineteenth-century writers who saw it as a symbol. For the colonists in 1621, it was a welcome pause after a difficult year. Then the short days of November arrived, and everyday life went on.

[long-pause]

{img:60-candle-making-tallow-evening}

Light was precious in early Plymouth, and candles were made at home from animal fat. As the autumn days shortened, artificial light became important. The colonists made candles by dipping wicks repeatedly into melted animal fat called tallow, until the candles were thick enough to use. The process was smelly and messy, and the candles burned with a smoky flame. Candles were used sparingly. Families often relied on the light of the fire instead. Early New England settlers also burned slivers of resinous pitch pine, sometimes called candlewood, which gave a bright light. Several seventeenth-century writers mention this practice. Evenings were spent mending, carving, spinning or talking by the hearth. Historians of daily life emphasise how much the lack of light shaped routines. People generally went to bed soon after dark and rose with the sun. Even so, winter evenings were long, and the fire was the centre of family life. The careful use of light was part of the careful use of all resources in the colony.

[long-pause]

{img:61-spinning-wheel-evening-hearth}

Women and girls in early Plymouth spent many evenings spinning, sewing and mending clothes. Every piece of clothing in the colony had to be made or repaired at home. New cloth had to come from England, and it was expensive and slow to arrive. So women and girls spent evenings patching knees and elbows, turning worn collars and sewing new garments. When wool was available, it was spun into yarn on a spinning wheel. Older girls taught younger ones to sew neat seams. Some girls made samplers, pieces of cloth stitched with letters and patterns to practise their skills. A sampler made by Loara Standish, daughter of Myles Standish, survives from the mid-seventeenth century. It is considered one of the oldest surviving American samplers and is kept by the Pilgrim Hall Museum in Plymouth. Needlework was an essential household skill. Even so, it also allowed for some creativity and pride. The careful stitching of a young girl in Plymouth is one of the few personal objects to survive from the early colony.

[long-pause]

{img:62-reading-bible-family-firelight}

Many Plymouth colonists could read, and books were highly valued in the colony. Literacy was unusually high among the Plymouth separatists, because they believed it was important to read the Bible for themselves. William Brewster brought a large library across the Atlantic. An inventory made after his death in 1644 listed nearly four hundred books, a very large collection for the time. Books included Bibles, psalm books, works of theology, history and practical guides. In the evenings, a family member might read aloud by the fire. Children learned their letters from hornbooks, small wooden boards printed with the alphabet and covered by a thin, transparent sheet of horn. Historians use book inventories to understand what people in the colony read and valued. Even so, not everyone could read, especially among the servants and some of the passengers known as strangers. Reading, writing and religious study were nevertheless central to the colony's identity. The surviving inventories suggest a community that treasured its books.

[long-pause]

{img:63-night-watch-hilltop-stars}

Every night, Plymouth's men took turns standing watch over the sleeping village. Even in peaceful times, the colonists kept a night watch. Men took turns standing guard with muskets and lanterns, while the others slept. Watchmen looked out for fire, for ships in the bay and for wild animals near the livestock. Fire was a constant danger in a village of wooden, thatched houses. In fact, the common house had caught fire in January 1621, as Bradford recorded. Night watches were a normal part of town life in England, so the practice was familiar. Colony records from later years include rules about the watch and penalties for neglecting it. Most nights, nothing happened. Watchmen stood in the cold, listened to owls and wind and waited for dawn. Even so, the watch was taken seriously. In a small and isolated settlement, early warning of a fire or an unexpected arrival could make a great difference. Keeping watch was a shared duty that every able man was expected to perform.

[long-pause]

{img:64-letters-home-england-ship-hope}

Many Plymouth colonists had left close family members behind in England or the Netherlands. Most of the settlers had left parents, brothers, sisters and sometimes children behind. Some hoped their relatives would join them on later ships. Letters could take months to cross the Atlantic, and they travelled only when a ship happened to be sailing. Each letter was therefore precious. Several later ships brought families together. William Brewster's daughters Fear and Patience, for example, arrived in 1623 aboard the ship Anne. Correspondence between Plymouth and Leiden survives in part, including letters from the minister John Robinson to the colonists. Historians use these letters to understand the colonists' hopes, worries and religious views. They show how strongly the settlers still felt connected to their community in Europe. Even so, the distance was enormous. A question asked in a letter might not receive an answer for a year. Patience, in every sense, was essential to life in the early colony.

[long-pause]

{img:65-first-snow-village-morning-quiet}

By the end of 1621, the Plymouth colonists faced their second winter better prepared than their first. As November gave way to December, the weather turned colder, and snow began to fall. The village now had houses, firewood, stored corn and established relations with its Wampanoag neighbours. This was very different from the previous winter. In 1620 and 1621, the colonists had arrived with no shelter, and about half of them had died before spring. The second winter was still difficult, especially after the arrival of new settlers with few supplies. Yet the colony was no longer facing the cold without houses or knowledge. Bradford's history reflects this change in tone. Historians often see the autumn of 1621 as the moment when Plymouth became a lasting settlement rather than a desperate camp. The harvest, the treaty and the houses were all part of that shift. The first snow of that winter fell on a village that had learned a great deal in a single year.

[long-pause]

{img:66-corn-pounding-mortar-winter}

Throughout the winter, corn had to be pounded into meal by hand every day. There was no mill in Plymouth in its first years. Dried corn was turned into meal by pounding it in a mortar, often a hollowed log, with a heavy wooden pestle. This was tiring work that had to be done regularly, because meal did not keep as well as whole kernels. Children often took turns at it. The Wampanoag processed their corn in the same way, and the English learned the method from them. The coarse meal was then sifted and cooked as bread or porridge. Within a few years, the colony built a water-powered mill on Town Brook. Records indicate that a gristmill was operating there by the 1630s. A reconstructed mill stands near the site today. Even so, for the first winters, the steady thump of mortar and pestle was part of daily life. Grinding corn by hand connected English households to a technique used in the region for many centuries.

[long-pause]

{img:67-ship-fortune-arrives-harbour}

In November 1621, a small ship called the Fortune arrived at Plymouth with thirty-five new settlers. The Fortune was sent by the colony's investors in London. When it was first sighted, the colonists feared it might be a French or hostile ship, until they saw that it was English. On board were thirty-five new settlers, many of them young men. Some were relatives of people already in the colony, including Jonathan Brewster, the eldest son of William Brewster. The ship also brought letters and news from England. However, the newcomers arrived with very little food and few supplies. Bradford recorded that they had not a biscuit-cake or any other victuals, and few clothes or bedding. Suddenly, the corn harvest that had seemed plentiful had to feed many more people through the winter. Bradford reduced everyone's rations by half. The arrival of the Fortune shows both the colony's connection to England and its continued vulnerability. Growth brought hope for the future, but also immediate strain on limited resources.

[long-pause]

{img:68-winslow-letter-candle-quill}

Edward Winslow wrote his account of the harvest celebration in December 1621, before the Fortune sailed home. The Fortune remained at Plymouth for only a few weeks. It was loaded with clapboard and beaver skins for the investors in London. Before it left, colonists wrote letters to friends and relatives. Edward Winslow's letter was dated the eleventh of December, 1621. In it, he described the colony's first year, including the harvest, the fowling, the visit of Massasoit and his men and the gift of five deer. He also praised the land and encouraged others to come. He wrote that, by the goodness of God, they were so far from want that they often wished their friends could share in their plenty. On its voyage home, the Fortune was captured by French privateers, who took much of its cargo. Even so, the letters reached England. Winslow's account was printed the following year. Without that single letter, almost nothing would be known about the celebration now remembered as the first Thanksgiving.

[long-pause]

{img:69-winter-village-snow-smoke}

The colony's second winter was a time of short rations, but far fewer deaths than the first. With the extra settlers from the Fortune to feed, the winter of 1621 to 1622 was lean. Rations had been cut in half, and hunger was common. Bradford's history records the difficulty of these months. Yet this winter was very different from the first. In the winter of 1620 to 1621, about half of the original passengers had died from disease and exposure. Now, every family had a house and a hearth. The colonists also had a year's experience of the land, the weather and the waters. Men continued to fish and hunt when the weather allowed. Inside, families mended clothes, repaired tools and kept their fires burning. Historians generally describe the second winter as hard but survivable. The settlement's basic structures were in place. The worst danger had passed, even if comfort and plenty were still some years away. The colony's survival now depended more on supplies and harvests than on shelter.

[long-pause]

{img:70-drought-rain-field-1623}

In 1623, the colonists held a day of thanksgiving after rain ended a long summer drought. For the Plymouth colonists, a day of thanksgiving was a religious occasion. It was spent in prayer and worship, usually in response to a particular blessing. The harvest feast of 1621 was not described by them in these terms. In the summer of 1623, a long drought threatened the colony's crops. Weeks passed without rain, and the corn began to wither. The colonists held a day of fasting and prayer. Bradford wrote that before the day was over, clouds gathered and gentle rain began to fall. It continued for several days, and the crops recovered. Afterwards, the colony set apart a day of thanksgiving. Historians note that this event matches the religious meaning of the word more closely than the feast of 1621. Days of fasting and thanksgiving became a regular part of life in New England. They were declared in response to droughts, harvests, victories and recoveries from illness.

[long-pause]

{img:71-plymouth-fort-meeting-house-hill}

In 1622, the colonists built a timber fort on the hill above Plymouth that also served as a meeting house. Concerned about security, the colonists built a sturdy fort at the top of the hill above their village in 1622. It was square and made of thick timber, with cannons mounted on its flat roof. From there, guards could watch the harbour and the surrounding land. The ground floor was used as the colony's meeting house for Sunday worship. Isaac de Rasières, a Dutch visitor in 1627, described the fort and the procession of colonists walking to church with their weapons. The hill later became the town's burial ground and is now called Burial Hill. Some of the oldest gravestones in New England stand there, though most date from the late seventeenth century or later. A small replica of the fort has been built at the living history museum near Plymouth. The combination of fort and church reflected the colonists' priorities. Defence and faith shared the same building at the heart of the settlement.

[long-pause]

{img:72-plymouth-grows-more-houses-1627}

By 1627, Plymouth had grown into a small town with more than thirty houses. More ships arrived in the years after 1621, bringing settlers, tools and livestock. The first cattle came in 1624, providing milk, butter and meat. Isaac de Rasières described Plymouth in 1627 as having about twenty houses, with gardens enclosed by fences, along a street that crossed another at the centre. Other estimates suggest a slightly larger number. The colony also traded with the Wampanoag and with Dutch traders from New Netherland. Plymouth never became large or wealthy. Within a decade, the much larger Massachusetts Bay Colony was founded nearby, with Boston as its centre. Plymouth was eventually merged into Massachusetts in 1691. Even so, its early growth showed that the colony had become permanent. The modern living history museum near Plymouth recreates the village as it looked in 1627, based on descriptions such as de Rasières's letter. The small settlement of 1621 had become an established English town.

[long-pause]

{img:73-new-england-autumn-thanksgiving-days}

For many years after 1621, each New England colony and later each state chose its own days of thanksgiving. In the decades after the harvest at Plymouth, colonies and towns across New England held days of thanksgiving. They were declared after good harvests, victories or the end of epidemics. Over time, an autumn thanksgiving became a regular custom in many places. Families attended church in the morning and then gathered for a large meal. Relatives often travelled to be together. Each colony, and after independence each state, chose its own date. As a result, Thanksgiving might fall on different days in neighbouring states. Some states, especially in the South, celebrated it rarely or not at all. During the American Revolution, the Continental Congress declared several national days of thanksgiving. George Washington proclaimed one in 1789. Yet these were occasional rather than annual. For much of the nineteenth century, Thanksgiving remained a regional custom. It was strongly associated with New England, where it had become one of the most important days of the year.

[long-pause]

{img:74-sarah-josepha-hale-writing-desk}

The magazine editor Sarah Josepha Hale campaigned for many years to make Thanksgiving a national holiday. Sarah Josepha Hale was a writer and the long-serving editor of Godey's Lady's Book, one of the most widely read magazines in nineteenth-century America. She is also known as the author of the nursery rhyme Mary Had a Little Lamb. Hale believed that a shared national day of thanksgiving would help unite the country. Beginning in the 1840s, she wrote editorials in favour of the idea year after year. She also sent letters to governors, members of Congress and several presidents. Her campaign lasted about seventeen years. Many of her appeals received polite replies or none at all. Hale also published recipes and descriptions of Thanksgiving meals, which helped shape the image of the holiday. Historians credit her with turning a regional custom into a national cause. Her persistence was remarkable. Without her long campaign, Thanksgiving might have remained a mainly New England tradition for much longer.

[long-pause]

{img:75-lincoln-proclamation-1863-desk}

In 1863, during the Civil War, President Abraham Lincoln proclaimed a national day of Thanksgiving. In September 1863, Sarah Josepha Hale wrote to President Abraham Lincoln, asking him to establish a national day of thanksgiving. The country was then deep in the Civil War. On the third of October, 1863, Lincoln issued a proclamation. It invited Americans to set apart the last Thursday of November as a day of thanksgiving and praise. The text, largely drafted by Secretary of State William Seward, acknowledged the suffering of the war while giving thanks for continued blessings. Historians see the proclamation as an attempt to encourage national unity at a time of division. From then on, presidents issued Thanksgiving proclamations every year. The holiday gradually became established across the whole country, including in the South after the war. Hale lived until 1879 and saw her goal achieved. The proclamation transformed a regional tradition into a national one. It marked the beginning of Thanksgiving as it is known in the United States today.

[long-pause]

{img:76-pilgrim-story-books-nineteenth-century}

The idea of the 1621 harvest feast as the first Thanksgiving became popular in the nineteenth century. For a long time, few people linked Thanksgiving with the harvest at Plymouth. Winslow's letter was little known. In 1841, the historian Alexander Young reprinted it in a collection of early documents. He added a note describing the event as the first Thanksgiving. The phrase caught on. In the later nineteenth century, schoolbooks, magazines and paintings began to show Pilgrims and Native people sharing a feast. Artists such as Jennie Augusta Brownscombe painted scenes of the event in the early twentieth century. Many of these images contained errors, such as black clothing with buckles or tepees, which the Wampanoag never used. Historians note that the story became popular partly because it offered a peaceful and unifying origin story for the nation. School pageants and plays spread it further. The process shows how historical memory develops. A few lines in a letter gradually became a national image, shaped by the needs and tastes of later generations.

[long-pause]

{img:77-mourts-relation-book-london-1622}

Winslow's letter about the harvest was printed in London in 1622, in a small book now called Mourt's Relation. When letters from Plymouth reached England, the colony's supporters published an account of its first year. The small book appeared in London in 1622. It described the voyage, the landing at Cape Cod, the founding of Plymouth and the treaty with Massasoit. Near the end, it included Edward Winslow's letter with its brief description of the harvest celebration. The book is now known as Mourt's Relation, after a name, George Morton, signed in its preface as G. Mourt. Its main authors are believed to be Winslow and Bradford. Only a small number of original copies survive today. For a long time, the book attracted little attention. It was rediscovered by historians in the nineteenth century and has been reprinted many times since. Historians consider it one of the most important sources on early Plymouth. A short book printed for investors and supporters became the main record of a famous event.

[long-pause]

{img:78-plymouth-family-cornfield-plots-1623}

In 1623, Governor Bradford assigned each family its own plot of land to plant corn. In its first years, Plymouth worked its fields in common. The harvest went into a shared store, from which everyone received food. Bradford later wrote that this system caused discontent. Some colonists felt they worked harder than others for the same reward. In the spring of 1623, Bradford and the colony's leaders decided to assign each family a parcel of land for its own use. Bradford recorded that the change made everyone more industrious. He wrote that much more corn was planted than before, and that women and children went into the fields as well. The arrangement applied to that season's crops at first, and land division continued in later years. Historians have discussed this episode at length. Some see it as an important lesson in incentives. Others point out that the colony's improving knowledge and conditions also played a role. Either way, harvests improved in the following years.

[long-pause]

{img:79-family-thanksgiving-table-modern}

Today, Thanksgiving is one of the most widely celebrated holidays in the United States. Thanksgiving is celebrated on the fourth Thursday of November. A law passed by Congress in 1941 fixed that date. Every year, tens of millions of Americans travel to spend the holiday with family and friends. Travel surveys regularly list the days around Thanksgiving among the busiest of the year. The traditional meal usually includes roast turkey, stuffing, mashed potatoes, cranberry sauce and pumpkin pie. Many of these dishes were not part of the meal in 1621. They developed over the following centuries, especially in the nineteenth. Other customs have grown up as well, including parades, football games and a ceremony in which the President pardons a turkey. Many families share what they are thankful for during the meal. Historians note that the holiday has changed considerably over time. Yet its central themes remain close to the original harvest celebrations. A good harvest, a shared meal and a moment of gratitude are still at its heart.

[long-pause]

{img:80-national-day-of-mourning-hill-quiet}

Since 1970, some Native Americans have observed Thanksgiving Day in Plymouth as a National Day of Mourning. Every year since 1970, Native Americans and their supporters have gathered on Cole's Hill in Plymouth on Thanksgiving Day. The event began when Wamsutta Frank James, a Wampanoag leader, was invited to speak at an anniversary celebration. His speech, which reflected on the history of his people, was not accepted. In response, he and others gathered to mark the day as a National Day of Mourning. Participants remember their ancestors, the loss of land and the hardships that followed colonisation. The alliance of 1621 did not last. In 1675 and 1676, a devastating conflict known as King Philip's War broke out between the English colonists and the Wampanoag and their allies. Thousands died, and Native communities suffered great losses of land and life. Historians today stress the importance of including this history. For many Native families, Thanksgiving is a day of both gratitude and remembrance. A plaque on Cole's Hill now records the meaning of the gathering.

[long-pause]

{img:81-plymouth-harbour-today-autumn}

Plymouth today is a seaside town, and its first street still follows the route laid out in 1620. Modern Plymouth is a town of about sixty thousand people on the coast of Massachusetts. Its harbour is busy with fishing boats and pleasure craft. The original street of the colony, now called Leyden Street, still runs up the hill from the shore along the same line. It is often described as one of the oldest continuously inhabited streets in English-speaking North America. At the top of the hill is Burial Hill, with its old gravestones and a view over the bay. Town Brook still flows into the harbour nearby. Plymouth Rock, traditionally identified as the landing place of the Mayflower passengers, is displayed under a granite canopy by the water. Historians note that the rock was first identified as the landing place only in 1741, more than a century later. Plymouth holds a large Thanksgiving celebration each year, with a parade and many visitors. Its streets connect the modern town directly to its earliest years.

[long-pause]

{img:82-living-museum-patuxet-homesite}

Near Plymouth, a living history museum recreates a Wampanoag homesite and the English village of 1627. The museum now called Plimoth Patuxet was founded in 1947. Beside a river, it presents a Wampanoag homesite, with wetu houses, gardens and a mishoon being shaped by burning and scraping. The staff there are Native people, who speak in their own voices about their ancestors and their communities today. On a hill nearby, the museum has reconstructed the English village as it appeared in 1627. Role players in period clothing portray specific colonists and speak in seventeenth-century English accents. They answer visitors' questions as though it were still 1627. The reconstructions are based on archaeology, documents and studies of buildings and clothing. In 2020, the museum changed its name from Plimoth Plantation to Plimoth Patuxet, to reflect both histories of the site. The museum also cares for Mayflower II and a reconstructed gristmill. It has become one of the most visited historical sites in New England.

[long-pause]

{img:83-mayflower-replica-ship-harbour}

A full-size reproduction of the Mayflower, called Mayflower II, is moored in Plymouth harbour. Mayflower II was built in Brixham, England, in the 1950s. Its designers worked from research into ships of the early seventeenth century, since no plans of the original Mayflower survive. In 1957, the ship sailed across the Atlantic to Plymouth, where it was presented as a gift of friendship from Britain. It is now operated by the Plimoth Patuxet museum. Visitors can go on board and see the cramped space below deck where the passengers of 1620 lived during their voyage of more than two months. The ship underwent a major restoration that was completed in 2020, in time for the four-hundredth anniversary of the voyage. It occasionally sails in the harbour and along the coast. Historians use the ship to help explain the conditions of the crossing. Standing on its deck gives a sense of how small the vessel was. More than a hundred people and their belongings once crossed an ocean in a ship of this size.

[long-pause]

{img:84-wampanoag-language-classroom-today}

The Wampanoag language, once no longer spoken, is being revived through a community project. For more than a century, the Wampanoag language was not spoken as a daily language. Yet it had been written down extensively in earlier times. In the seventeenth century, the missionary John Eliot worked with Native translators to produce a Bible in the closely related Massachusett language. Printed in 1663, it was the first complete Bible printed in North America. Wampanoag people also used their language in letters, deeds and records. In the 1990s, Jessie Little Doe Baird, a Mashpee Wampanoag, began studying these documents. She later earned a degree in linguistics and helped found the Wôpanâak Language Reclamation Project. The project has produced a dictionary, classes and a language immersion school. Some children are now growing up as speakers. In 2010, Baird received a MacArthur Fellowship for this work. Linguists regard the project as a remarkable example of language revival. Old written records have helped bring a sleeping language back into everyday use.

[long-pause]

{img:85-wampanoag-community-today-gathering}

The Wampanoag remain a living people, with tribal governments on Cape Cod and Martha's Vineyard. Thousands of Wampanoag people live in southern New England today. The Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe on Cape Cod and the Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head (Aquinnah) on Martha's Vineyard are federally recognised tribes with their own governments. Other Wampanoag communities are also active in the region. Annual powwows bring families together for drumming, dancing, food and ceremony. The Mashpee Wampanoag powwow has been held for many decades. Wampanoag people work in every profession and also maintain traditional knowledge, from farming and canoe building to storytelling. In recent years, Wampanoag historians and cultural leaders have played a growing role in telling the story of 1621, particularly around the four-hundredth anniversary in 2020 and 2021. Museums and schools increasingly include their perspectives. Historians emphasise that the Wampanoag are not only part of the past. Their communities continue on the same lands where their ancestors lived when the Mayflower arrived.

[long-pause]

{img:86-town-brook-park-autumn-today}

Town Brook, the stream that gave Patuxet its name, still flows through Plymouth today. Town Brook runs from a pond inland down to Plymouth harbour. It was the water source for both the Wampanoag village of Patuxet and the English settlement. Today, part of its course runs through Brewster Gardens, a park near the town centre. Over the centuries, several dams and mills were built along the brook, which blocked the migration of herring. In recent decades, local projects have removed or modified many of these dams. As a result, herring can once again swim upstream in spring to reach the ponds where they spawn. Scientists and volunteers count the fish each year to monitor the population. The restoration is often described as one of the most important river projects in the region. It links the modern town to the springs of the 1620s, when herring from the same brook fertilised the colony's first cornfields. The small stream has been at the centre of life on this site for centuries.

[long-pause]

{img:87-cranberry-harvest-flooded-bog}

Each autumn, cranberry bogs around Plymouth are flooded for the harvest, turning whole fields red. The wild cranberries once gathered by the Wampanoag are now cultivated on farms throughout southeastern Massachusetts. Commercial cranberry growing began in the region in the early nineteenth century. In the most common method of harvesting, growers flood the bogs with water in autumn. Machines then loosen the berries from the vines, and the ripe fruit floats to the surface. Workers gather the floating berries together with long booms before they are collected. The result is a striking sight: fields covered with bright red fruit floating on the water. Massachusetts remains one of the leading cranberry-producing states in the United States. Much of the crop is used for juice and sauce. Some of it is served at Thanksgiving meals across the country, as cranberry sauce. That sauce was not part of the 1621 celebration. Even so, the berries themselves link today's harvest to the wild fruit that grew in the bogs of the region long ago.

[long-pause]

{img:88-statue-massasoit-hill-harbour}

A bronze statue of Massasoit stands on Cole's Hill, overlooking Plymouth harbour. The statue was created by the sculptor Cyrus Dallin and unveiled in 1921, three hundred years after the harvest celebration. It shows Massasoit standing upright, with one hand extended, looking out over the harbour where English ships once anchored. The monument was commissioned by the Improved Order of Red Men, a fraternal organisation. Castings of the same statue were later placed in other locations, including Salt Lake City in Utah and Kansas City in Missouri. A plaque at the base identifies Massasoit as a protector and preserver of the Pilgrims. Some Wampanoag people have expressed mixed feelings about how he is presented, while others see the statue as an important recognition of his role. Visitors sometimes leave small offerings at its base. Historians point out that Massasoit's decisions were made in the interests of his own people. His alliance was a careful political choice. The statue remains a prominent reminder of his part in the colony's survival.

[long-pause]

{img:89-autumn-forest-sunset-bay-calm}

Each autumn, large flocks of geese still migrate along the coast near Plymouth Bay. Some features of the landscape have changed little since 1621. Every autumn, migrating geese and ducks still travel down the Atlantic coast. Many rest on the marshes and ponds around Plymouth, much as they did when the colonists went fowling before the harvest celebration. The marshes are now partly protected as conservation land. Birdwatchers record the arrivals each year. Cranberries ripen in the bogs, and corn is still grown on farms not far from the shore. In late afternoon, sunlight falls across the marsh grass along the coast that the Wampanoag called home. Historians of the environment note that many parts of the landscape have been greatly altered by roads, towns and development. Even so, the seasons follow their ancient pattern. The migrations, the harvest and the shortening days of November connect the present to the autumn of 1621. Long after the feast ended, the same rhythms of the year continue along this coast.
