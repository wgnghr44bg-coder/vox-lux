TOPIC: A ship called Fortune, and the winter after
===
IMG: ship-fortune-arrives-harbour | a small sailing ship anchored in a quiet harbour in late autumn, a rowing boat heading to shore, grey sky and bare trees
HEAD: In November 1621, a small ship called the Fortune arrived at Plymouth with thirty-five new settlers.
BODY: The Fortune was sent by the colony's investors in London. When it was first sighted, the colonists feared it might be a French or hostile ship, until they saw that it was English. On board were thirty-five new settlers, many of them young men. Some were relatives of people already in the colony, including Jonathan Brewster, the eldest son of William Brewster. The ship also brought letters and news from England. However, the newcomers arrived with very little food and few supplies. Bradford recorded that they had not a biscuit-cake or any other victuals, and few clothes or bedding. Suddenly, the corn harvest that had seemed plentiful had to feed many more people through the winter. Bradford reduced everyone's rations by half. The arrival of the Fortune shows both the colony's connection to England and its continued vulnerability. Growth brought hope for the future, but also immediate strain on limited resources.
===
IMG: winslow-letter-candle-quill | a man writing a letter with a quill by candlelight in a small dark timber room, a folded paper and sealing wax on the table
HEAD: Edward Winslow wrote his account of the harvest celebration in December 1621, before the Fortune sailed home.
BODY: The Fortune remained at Plymouth for only a few weeks. It was loaded with clapboard and beaver skins for the investors in London. Before it left, colonists wrote letters to friends and relatives. Edward Winslow's letter was dated the eleventh of December, 1621. In it, he described the colony's first year, including the harvest, the fowling, the visit of Massasoit and his men and the gift of five deer. He also praised the land and encouraged others to come. He wrote that, by the goodness of God, they were so far from want that they often wished their friends could share in their plenty. On its voyage home, the Fortune was captured by French privateers, who took much of its cargo. Even so, the letters reached England. Winslow's account was printed the following year. Without that single letter, almost nothing would be known about the celebration now remembered as the first Thanksgiving.
===
IMG: winter-village-snow-smoke | a small timber village under light snow at dusk, smoke rising from chimneys, a lantern in one window, bare trees
HEAD: The colony's second winter was a time of short rations, but far fewer deaths than the first.
BODY: With the extra settlers from the Fortune to feed, the winter of 1621 to 1622 was lean. Rations had been cut in half, and hunger was common. Bradford's history records the difficulty of these months. Yet this winter was very different from the first. In the winter of 1620 to 1621, about half of the original passengers had died from disease and exposure. Now, every family had a house and a hearth. The colonists also had a year's experience of the land, the weather and the waters. Men continued to fish and hunt when the weather allowed. Inside, families mended clothes, repaired tools and kept their fires burning. Historians generally describe the second winter as hard but survivable. The settlement's basic structures were in place. The worst danger had passed, even if comfort and plenty were still some years away. The colony's survival now depended more on supplies and harvests than on shelter.
===
IMG: drought-rain-field-1623 | a parched cornfield under grey clouds as the first rain begins to fall, settlers standing quietly at the field edge
HEAD: In 1623, the colonists held a day of thanksgiving after rain ended a long summer drought.
BODY: For the Plymouth colonists, a day of thanksgiving was a religious occasion. It was spent in prayer and worship, usually in response to a particular blessing. The harvest feast of 1621 was not described by them in these terms. In the summer of 1623, a long drought threatened the colony's crops. Weeks passed without rain, and the corn began to wither. The colonists held a day of fasting and prayer. Bradford wrote that before the day was over, clouds gathered and gentle rain began to fall. It continued for several days, and the crops recovered. Afterwards, the colony set apart a day of thanksgiving. Historians note that this event matches the religious meaning of the word more closely than the feast of 1621. Days of fasting and thanksgiving became a regular part of life in New England. They were declared in response to droughts, harvests, victories and recoveries from illness.
===
IMG: plymouth-fort-meeting-house-hill | a square timber fort with a flat roof on a hilltop above a small village and harbour, cannons on top, morning light
HEAD: In 1622, the colonists built a timber fort on the hill above Plymouth that also served as a meeting house.
BODY: Concerned about security, the colonists built a sturdy fort at the top of the hill above their village in 1622. It was square and made of thick timber, with cannons mounted on its flat roof. From there, guards could watch the harbour and the surrounding land. The ground floor was used as the colony's meeting house for Sunday worship. Isaac de Rasières, a Dutch visitor in 1627, described the fort and the procession of colonists walking to church with their weapons. The hill later became the town's burial ground and is now called Burial Hill. Some of the oldest gravestones in New England stand there, though most date from the late seventeenth century or later. A small replica of the fort has been built at the living history museum near Plymouth. The combination of fort and church reflected the colonists' priorities. Defence and faith shared the same building at the heart of the settlement.
===
IMG: plymouth-grows-more-houses-1627 | a larger village of timber houses with fenced gardens along two crossing streets, cows grazing, a harbour with small boats, warm light
HEAD: By 1627, Plymouth had grown into a small town with more than thirty houses.
BODY: More ships arrived in the years after 1621, bringing settlers, tools and livestock. The first cattle came in 1624, providing milk, butter and meat. Isaac de Rasières described Plymouth in 1627 as having about twenty houses, with gardens enclosed by fences, along a street that crossed another at the centre. Other estimates suggest a slightly larger number. The colony also traded with the Wampanoag and with Dutch traders from New Netherland. Plymouth never became large or wealthy. Within a decade, the much larger Massachusetts Bay Colony was founded nearby, with Boston as its centre. Plymouth was eventually merged into Massachusetts in 1691. Even so, its early growth showed that the colony had become permanent. The modern living history museum near Plymouth recreates the village as it looked in 1627, based on descriptions such as de Rasières's letter. The small settlement of 1621 had become an established English town.

TOPIC: How a harvest became a holiday
===
IMG: new-england-autumn-thanksgiving-days | a white wooden church on a village green in autumn, families in eighteenth century clothes walking to it, maple trees glowing red
HEAD: For many years after 1621, each New England colony and later each state chose its own days of thanksgiving.
BODY: In the decades after the harvest at Plymouth, colonies and towns across New England held days of thanksgiving. They were declared after good harvests, victories or the end of epidemics. Over time, an autumn thanksgiving became a regular custom in many places. Families attended church in the morning and then gathered for a large meal. Relatives often travelled to be together. Each colony, and after independence each state, chose its own date. As a result, Thanksgiving might fall on different days in neighbouring states. Some states, especially in the South, celebrated it rarely or not at all. During the American Revolution, the Continental Congress declared several national days of thanksgiving. George Washington proclaimed one in 1789. Yet these were occasional rather than annual. For much of the nineteenth century, Thanksgiving remained a regional custom. It was strongly associated with New England, where it had become one of the most important days of the year.
===
IMG: sarah-josepha-hale-writing-desk | a woman in a nineteenth century dress writing at a desk covered with letters and magazines, an oil lamp glowing, evening
HEAD: The magazine editor Sarah Josepha Hale campaigned for many years to make Thanksgiving a national holiday.
BODY: Sarah Josepha Hale was a writer and the long-serving editor of Godey's Lady's Book, one of the most widely read magazines in nineteenth-century America. She is also known as the author of the nursery rhyme Mary Had a Little Lamb. Hale believed that a shared national day of thanksgiving would help unite the country. Beginning in the 1840s, she wrote editorials in favour of the idea year after year. She also sent letters to governors, members of Congress and several presidents. Her campaign lasted about seventeen years. Many of her appeals received polite replies or none at all. Hale also published recipes and descriptions of Thanksgiving meals, which helped shape the image of the holiday. Historians credit her with turning a regional custom into a national cause. Her persistence was remarkable. Without her long campaign, Thanksgiving might have remained a mainly New England tradition for much longer.
===
IMG: lincoln-proclamation-1863-desk | a tall bearded man reading a document at a desk by a window in the White House, autumn light, a quill and inkstand
HEAD: In 1863, during the Civil War, President Abraham Lincoln proclaimed a national day of Thanksgiving.
BODY: In September 1863, Sarah Josepha Hale wrote to President Abraham Lincoln, asking him to establish a national day of thanksgiving. The country was then deep in the Civil War. On the third of October, 1863, Lincoln issued a proclamation. It invited Americans to set apart the last Thursday of November as a day of thanksgiving and praise. The text, largely drafted by Secretary of State William Seward, acknowledged the suffering of the war while giving thanks for continued blessings. Historians see the proclamation as an attempt to encourage national unity at a time of division. From then on, presidents issued Thanksgiving proclamations every year. The holiday gradually became established across the whole country, including in the South after the war. Hale lived until 1879 and saw her goal achieved. The proclamation transformed a regional tradition into a national one. It marked the beginning of Thanksgiving as it is known in the United States today.
===
IMG: pilgrim-story-books-nineteenth-century | a family reading a picture book by the fireside in the nineteenth century, an illustration of settlers and a feast on the page
HEAD: The idea of the 1621 harvest feast as the first Thanksgiving became popular in the nineteenth century.
BODY: For a long time, few people linked Thanksgiving with the harvest at Plymouth. Winslow's letter was little known. In 1841, the historian Alexander Young reprinted it in a collection of early documents. He added a note describing the event as the first Thanksgiving. The phrase caught on. In the later nineteenth century, schoolbooks, magazines and paintings began to show Pilgrims and Native people sharing a feast. Artists such as Jennie Augusta Brownscombe painted scenes of the event in the early twentieth century. Many of these images contained errors, such as black clothing with buckles or tepees, which the Wampanoag never used. Historians note that the story became popular partly because it offered a peaceful and unifying origin story for the nation. School pageants and plays spread it further. The process shows how historical memory develops. A few lines in a letter gradually became a national image, shaped by the needs and tastes of later generations.
===
IMG: mourts-relation-book-london-1622 | a small printed book from 1622 lying open on a wooden table beside a candle, old English type and a simple woodcut, soft light
HEAD: Winslow's letter about the harvest was printed in London in 1622, in a small book now called Mourt's Relation.
BODY: When letters from Plymouth reached England, the colony's supporters published an account of its first year. The small book appeared in London in 1622. It described the voyage, the landing at Cape Cod, the founding of Plymouth and the treaty with Massasoit. Near the end, it included Edward Winslow's letter with its brief description of the harvest celebration. The book is now known as Mourt's Relation, after a name, George Morton, signed in its preface as G. Mourt. Its main authors are believed to be Winslow and Bradford. Only a small number of original copies survive today. For a long time, the book attracted little attention. It was rediscovered by historians in the nineteenth century and has been reprinted many times since. Historians consider it one of the most important sources on early Plymouth. A short book printed for investors and supporters became the main record of a famous event.
===
IMG: plymouth-family-cornfield-plots-1623 | several small fenced cornfields beside a timber village in spring, each family working its own plot, soft morning light
HEAD: In 1623, Governor Bradford assigned each family its own plot of land to plant corn.
BODY: In its first years, Plymouth worked its fields in common. The harvest went into a shared store, from which everyone received food. Bradford later wrote that this system caused discontent. Some colonists felt they worked harder than others for the same reward. In the spring of 1623, Bradford and the colony's leaders decided to assign each family a parcel of land for its own use. Bradford recorded that the change made everyone more industrious. He wrote that much more corn was planted than before, and that women and children went into the fields as well. The arrangement applied to that season's crops at first, and land division continued in later years. Historians have discussed this episode at length. Some see it as an important lesson in incentives. Others point out that the colony's improving knowledge and conditions also played a role. Either way, harvests improved in the following years.
===
IMG: family-thanksgiving-table-modern | a warm family dining table with a roast turkey, pies and candles, grandparents and children sitting together, soft golden light
HEAD: Today, Thanksgiving is one of the most widely celebrated holidays in the United States.
BODY: Thanksgiving is celebrated on the fourth Thursday of November. A law passed by Congress in 1941 fixed that date. Every year, tens of millions of Americans travel to spend the holiday with family and friends. Travel surveys regularly list the days around Thanksgiving among the busiest of the year. The traditional meal usually includes roast turkey, stuffing, mashed potatoes, cranberry sauce and pumpkin pie. Many of these dishes were not part of the meal in 1621. They developed over the following centuries, especially in the nineteenth. Other customs have grown up as well, including parades, football games and a ceremony in which the President pardons a turkey. Many families share what they are thankful for during the meal. Historians note that the holiday has changed considerably over time. Yet its central themes remain close to the original harvest celebrations. A good harvest, a shared meal and a moment of gratitude are still at its heart.
===
IMG: national-day-of-mourning-hill-quiet | a quiet group of people gathered on a hilltop above a harbour on a grey November day, a statue in the background, calm and respectful
HEAD: Since 1970, some Native Americans have observed Thanksgiving Day in Plymouth as a National Day of Mourning.
BODY: Every year since 1970, Native Americans and their supporters have gathered on Cole's Hill in Plymouth on Thanksgiving Day. The event began when Wamsutta Frank James, a Wampanoag leader, was invited to speak at an anniversary celebration. His speech, which reflected on the history of his people, was not accepted. In response, he and others gathered to mark the day as a National Day of Mourning. Participants remember their ancestors, the loss of land and the hardships that followed colonisation. The alliance of 1621 did not last. In 1675 and 1676, a devastating conflict known as King Philip's War broke out between the English colonists and the Wampanoag and their allies. Thousands died, and Native communities suffered great losses of land and life. Historians today stress the importance of including this history. For many Native families, Thanksgiving is a day of both gratitude and remembrance. A plaque on Cole's Hill now records the meaning of the gathering.

TOPIC: Plymouth and the Wampanoag today
===
IMG: plymouth-harbour-today-autumn | the harbour of a small New England town today on a calm autumn morning, boats at their moorings, white houses and red trees on the hillside
HEAD: Plymouth today is a seaside town, and its first street still follows the route laid out in 1620.
BODY: Modern Plymouth is a town of about sixty thousand people on the coast of Massachusetts. Its harbour is busy with fishing boats and pleasure craft. The original street of the colony, now called Leyden Street, still runs up the hill from the shore along the same line. It is often described as one of the oldest continuously inhabited streets in English-speaking North America. At the top of the hill is Burial Hill, with its old gravestones and a view over the bay. Town Brook still flows into the harbour nearby. Plymouth Rock, traditionally identified as the landing place of the Mayflower passengers, is displayed under a granite canopy by the water. Historians note that the rock was first identified as the landing place only in 1741, more than a century later. Plymouth holds a large Thanksgiving celebration each year, with a parade and many visitors. Its streets connect the modern town directly to its earliest years.
===
IMG: living-museum-patuxet-homesite | a reconstructed Wampanoag homesite with dome houses covered in mats and bark beside a river, a person scraping a canoe, autumn trees
HEAD: Near Plymouth, a living history museum recreates a Wampanoag homesite and the English village of 1627.
BODY: The museum now called Plimoth Patuxet was founded in 1947. Beside a river, it presents a Wampanoag homesite, with wetu houses, gardens and a mishoon being shaped by burning and scraping. The staff there are Native people, who speak in their own voices about their ancestors and their communities today. On a hill nearby, the museum has reconstructed the English village as it appeared in 1627. Role players in period clothing portray specific colonists and speak in seventeenth-century English accents. They answer visitors' questions as though it were still 1627. The reconstructions are based on archaeology, documents and studies of buildings and clothing. In 2020, the museum changed its name from Plimoth Plantation to Plimoth Patuxet, to reflect both histories of the site. The museum also cares for Mayflower II and a reconstructed gristmill. It has become one of the most visited historical sites in New England.
===
IMG: mayflower-replica-ship-harbour | a full-size wooden replica of a seventeenth century sailing ship moored at a harbour quay, masts against a clear autumn sky
HEAD: A full-size reproduction of the Mayflower, called Mayflower II, is moored in Plymouth harbour.
BODY: Mayflower II was built in Brixham, England, in the 1950s. Its designers worked from research into ships of the early seventeenth century, since no plans of the original Mayflower survive. In 1957, the ship sailed across the Atlantic to Plymouth, where it was presented as a gift of friendship from Britain. It is now operated by the Plimoth Patuxet museum. Visitors can go on board and see the cramped space below deck where the passengers of 1620 lived during their voyage of more than two months. The ship underwent a major restoration that was completed in 2020, in time for the four-hundredth anniversary of the voyage. It occasionally sails in the harbour and along the coast. Historians use the ship to help explain the conditions of the crossing. Standing on its deck gives a sense of how small the vessel was. More than a hundred people and their belongings once crossed an ocean in a ship of this size.
===
IMG: wampanoag-language-classroom-today | a bright classroom where children and teachers read from books, the walls decorated with words in an indigenous language, calm afternoon light
HEAD: The Wampanoag language, once no longer spoken, is being revived through a community project.
BODY: For more than a century, the Wampanoag language was not spoken as a daily language. Yet it had been written down extensively in earlier times. In the seventeenth century, the missionary John Eliot worked with Native translators to produce a Bible in the closely related Massachusett language. Printed in 1663, it was the first complete Bible printed in North America. Wampanoag people also used their language in letters, deeds and records. In the 1990s, Jessie Little Doe Baird, a Mashpee Wampanoag, began studying these documents. She later earned a degree in linguistics and helped found the Wôpanâak Language Reclamation Project. The project has produced a dictionary, classes and a language immersion school. Some children are now growing up as speakers. In 2010, Baird received a MacArthur Fellowship for this work. Linguists regard the project as a remarkable example of language revival. Old written records have helped bring a sleeping language back into everyday use.
===
IMG: wampanoag-community-today-gathering | a Wampanoag community gathering outdoors in summer, people in traditional regalia and everyday clothes, drums and dancers, trees and sea behind
HEAD: The Wampanoag remain a living people, with tribal governments on Cape Cod and Martha's Vineyard.
BODY: Thousands of Wampanoag people live in southern New England today. The Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe on Cape Cod and the Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head (Aquinnah) on Martha's Vineyard are federally recognised tribes with their own governments. Other Wampanoag communities are also active in the region. Annual powwows bring families together for drumming, dancing, food and ceremony. The Mashpee Wampanoag powwow has been held for many decades. Wampanoag people work in every profession and also maintain traditional knowledge, from farming and canoe building to storytelling. In recent years, Wampanoag historians and cultural leaders have played a growing role in telling the story of 1621, particularly around the four-hundredth anniversary in 2020 and 2021. Museums and schools increasingly include their perspectives. Historians emphasise that the Wampanoag are not only part of the past. Their communities continue on the same lands where their ancestors lived when the Mayflower arrived.
===
IMG: town-brook-park-autumn-today | a small clear brook running through a green park with old stone walls and red maple trees, autumn afternoon
HEAD: Town Brook, the stream that gave Patuxet its name, still flows through Plymouth today.
BODY: Town Brook runs from a pond inland down to Plymouth harbour. It was the water source for both the Wampanoag village of Patuxet and the English settlement. Today, part of its course runs through Brewster Gardens, a park near the town centre. Over the centuries, several dams and mills were built along the brook, which blocked the migration of herring. In recent decades, local projects have removed or modified many of these dams. As a result, herring can once again swim upstream in spring to reach the ponds where they spawn. Scientists and volunteers count the fish each year to monitor the population. The restoration is often described as one of the most important river projects in the region. It links the modern town to the springs of the 1620s, when herring from the same brook fertilised the colony's first cornfields. The small stream has been at the centre of life on this site for centuries.
===
IMG: cranberry-harvest-flooded-bog | a flooded cranberry bog covered with floating bright red berries, workers in waders gathering them, autumn trees, soft morning light
HEAD: Each autumn, cranberry bogs around Plymouth are flooded for the harvest, turning whole fields red.
BODY: The wild cranberries once gathered by the Wampanoag are now cultivated on farms throughout southeastern Massachusetts. Commercial cranberry growing began in the region in the early nineteenth century. In the most common method of harvesting, growers flood the bogs with water in autumn. Machines then loosen the berries from the vines, and the ripe fruit floats to the surface. Workers gather the floating berries together with long booms before they are collected. The result is a striking sight: fields covered with bright red fruit floating on the water. Massachusetts remains one of the leading cranberry-producing states in the United States. Much of the crop is used for juice and sauce. Some of it is served at Thanksgiving meals across the country, as cranberry sauce. That sauce was not part of the 1621 celebration. Even so, the berries themselves link today's harvest to the wild fruit that grew in the bogs of the region long ago.
===
IMG: statue-massasoit-hill-harbour | a bronze statue of a Wampanoag leader standing on a grassy hill overlooking a harbour, calm autumn evening light
HEAD: A bronze statue of Massasoit stands on Cole's Hill, overlooking Plymouth harbour.
BODY: The statue was created by the sculptor Cyrus Dallin and unveiled in 1921, three hundred years after the harvest celebration. It shows Massasoit standing upright, with one hand extended, looking out over the harbour where English ships once anchored. The monument was commissioned by the Improved Order of Red Men, a fraternal organisation. Castings of the same statue were later placed in other locations, including Salt Lake City in Utah and Kansas City in Missouri. A plaque at the base identifies Massasoit as a protector and preserver of the Pilgrims. Some Wampanoag people have expressed mixed feelings about how he is presented, while others see the statue as an important recognition of his role. Visitors sometimes leave small offerings at its base. Historians point out that Massasoit's decisions were made in the interests of his own people. His alliance was a careful political choice. The statue remains a prominent reminder of his part in the colony's survival.
===
IMG: autumn-forest-sunset-bay-calm | a calm bay at sunset in late autumn, a forest of bare and golden trees on the shore, the first stars appearing, peaceful
HEAD: Each autumn, large flocks of geese still migrate along the coast near Plymouth Bay.
BODY: Some features of the landscape have changed little since 1621. Every autumn, migrating geese and ducks still travel down the Atlantic coast. Many rest on the marshes and ponds around Plymouth, much as they did when the colonists went fowling before the harvest celebration. The marshes are now partly protected as conservation land. Birdwatchers record the arrivals each year. Cranberries ripen in the bogs, and corn is still grown on farms not far from the shore. In late afternoon, sunlight falls across the marsh grass along the coast that the Wampanoag called home. Historians of the environment note that many parts of the landscape have been greatly altered by roads, towns and development. Even so, the seasons follow their ancient pattern. The migrations, the harvest and the shortening days of November connect the present to the autumn of 1621. Long after the feast ended, the same rhythms of the year continue along this coast.
