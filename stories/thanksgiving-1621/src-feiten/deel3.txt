HEAD: In November 1621, a small ship called the Fortune arrived with thirty-five new settlers.
BODY: One day in November, the lookouts saw a ship coming into the bay. At first, people feared it might be French, or pirates. Then they saw it was English. It was the Fortune, a small ship sent by the London merchants. On board were thirty-five new settlers, many of them young men, and a few relatives of people already in Plymouth. You can imagine the excitement. There were letters from home, news from England and old friends to greet. William Brewster's eldest son Jonathan was among them. But there was a problem. The newcomers had brought almost no food, no extra clothing and very few supplies. Suddenly, the corn that had seemed so plentiful had to feed many more mouths through the winter. Bradford had to cut everyone's food in half. Can you imagine the mixed feelings in the village that night?

IMG: winslow-letter-candle-quill | a man writing a letter with a quill by candlelight in a small dark timber room, a folded paper and sealing wax on the table
HEAD: Edward Winslow wrote his famous letter just before the Fortune sailed home.
BODY: The Fortune stayed for only a couple of weeks before sailing back to England, loaded with clapboard and beaver skins. Before she left, the settlers wrote letters to friends and family. Edward Winslow wrote one, dated the eleventh of December, 1621, telling what had happened in their first year. You'd see him sitting by the fire late at night with a quill, choosing his words carefully. In it, he described the harvest, the birds, the three days with Massasoit and the five deer. He also wrote warmly about the land and its people, and encouraged others to come. He said that, by the goodness of God, they were so far from want that they often wished their friends could share in their plenty. Those few lines became the whole story of the first Thanksgiving. Sadly, the Fortune was captured by French pirates on the way home. But the letter got through.

IMG: winter-village-snow-smoke | a small timber village under light snow at dusk, smoke rising from chimneys, a lantern in one window, bare trees
HEAD: The second winter was hungry, but much calmer than the first.
BODY: With all the extra mouths to feed, the winter of 1621 into 1622 was lean. People ate small portions and were often hungry. But it wasn't like the terrible first winter, when so many had died. Now everyone had a house, a fire and the knowledge they'd gained in their first year. You'd see snow settling softly on the thatched roofs, and smoke curling out of every chimney. Inside, families mended clothes, sharpened tools and told stories by the fire. The men still went out to fish and hunt when the weather allowed. Children slid on the ice of the frozen brook. The days were short and the nights very long and quiet. I imagine people often thought back to the autumn feast, and the taste of roast venison, and hoped there'd be another one. Can you hear the wind outside, and the fire crackling gently within?

IMG: drought-rain-field-1623 | a parched cornfield under grey clouds as the first rain begins to fall, settlers standing quietly at the field edge
HEAD: The settlers' first official day of thanksgiving came two years later, when rain ended a long drought.
BODY: You might think the harvest feast of 1621 was what the settlers called a thanksgiving. For them, it wasn't. A day of thanksgiving was a solemn religious day, spent in church and prayer, often after a special blessing from God. In the summer of 1623, the colony suffered a long drought. Week after week, no rain fell, and the corn began to wither. So the settlers held a day of fasting and prayer. Bradford wrote that the very same evening, the clouds gathered, and soft, gentle rain began to fall, and kept falling for days. The corn recovered, and the harvest was saved. Afterwards, they held a day of thanksgiving. Can you imagine standing at the edge of a dry field and feeling the first raindrops on your face? Many people in New England later held days like this every autumn.

IMG: plymouth-fort-meeting-house-hill | a square timber fort with a flat roof on a hilltop above a small village and harbour, cannons on top, morning light
HEAD: Within a couple of years, the settlers built a fort on the hill that was also their meeting house.
BODY: In 1622, the colonists built a sturdy timber fort on top of the hill above the village. It was square, with thick walls, and cannons mounted on its flat roof. From up there, you could see far out over the bay and deep into the woods. But the fort wasn't only for defence. On Sundays, the ground floor became their meeting house, where the whole colony gathered to worship. Imagine sitting in church with cannons on the roof above your head. You'd hear the wind whistle around the walls during the sermon. I find that a funny picture, I must admit. The hill is still there today, and it's called Burial Hill, because the settlers later used it as a cemetery. Some of the oldest gravestones in New England stand on it, looking out over the harbour.

IMG: plymouth-grows-more-houses-1627 | a larger village of timber houses with fenced gardens along two crossing streets, cows grazing, a harbour with small boats, warm light
HEAD: Slowly, the tiny village of 1621 grew into a busy little town.
BODY: More ships came, bringing more settlers, tools and, at last, cows. In 1624, the first cattle arrived, and children could finally drink milk again. Within a few years, the single street had grown into a small town with more than thirty houses, a second street and a busy harbour. Families planted orchards. People traded corn and goods with the Wampanoag and with other colonies. Plymouth was never very large or very rich. Soon much bigger towns, like Boston, grew up nearby. But it had been first, and its people were proud of that. If you'd walked down the street in 1627, you'd have heard cows lowing, hammers ringing and children chasing chickens between the gardens. It must have felt like a very different place from the cold, sick little village of that first winter. Isn't it amazing what a few years of patience can do?

TOPIC: How a harvest became a holiday
BRIDGE: Let's step forward now, you and I, past many winters and many harvests. Because the story of Thanksgiving didn't end in 1621. In a way, it had only just begun.
===
IMG: new-england-autumn-thanksgiving-days | a white wooden church on a village green in autumn, families in eighteenth century clothes walking to it, maple trees glowing red
HEAD: For many years, each colony and later each state chose its own day of thanksgiving.
BODY: In the decades after 1621, the towns and colonies of New England held days of thanksgiving whenever they felt they'd been blessed, after a good harvest, a victory or the end of a sickness. Over time, it became a habit to hold one every autumn. Families went to church in the morning, and then came home to a big meal. Relatives travelled from far away to be together. You'd see wagons rolling along the country roads on a crisp November morning, filled with grandparents, children and baskets of pies. But each state chose its own date. So Thanksgiving in one state might fall on a different day from Thanksgiving in the next. Some states, especially in the South, hardly celebrated it at all. It was a much loved custom, but not yet a national holiday. Can you smell the pies in those baskets?
===
IMG: sarah-josepha-hale-writing-desk | a woman in a nineteenth century dress writing at a desk covered with letters and magazines, an oil lamp glowing, evening
HEAD: A determined magazine editor named Sarah Josepha Hale spent seventeen years asking for a national Thanksgiving.
BODY: Sarah Josepha Hale was a writer and the editor of a very popular women's magazine. You may know one thing she wrote without realising it. She's the author of the nursery rhyme Mary Had a Little Lamb. Hale believed that a shared Thanksgiving day could bring the whole country together. So, starting in the 1840s, she wrote editorials about it year after year. She sent letters to governors, senators and presidents, one after another, asking them to make it a national holiday. Most of them politely ignored her. She didn't give up. You can picture her at her desk, writing yet another letter by lamplight, year after year. I really admire that kind of patience. Would you have kept writing for seventeen years, after so many polite refusals? She did.
===
IMG: lincoln-proclamation-1863-desk | a tall bearded man reading a document at a desk by a window in the White House, autumn light, a quill and inkstand
HEAD: In 1863, in the middle of the Civil War, President Lincoln made Thanksgiving a national holiday.
BODY: In September 1863, Sarah Josepha Hale wrote to President Abraham Lincoln. The country was torn apart by the Civil War. Hale argued that a national day of thanksgiving might help to heal it. A few days later, Lincoln issued a proclamation. He asked all Americans to set apart the last Thursday of November as a day of thanksgiving. It was a quiet, hopeful act in a very dark time. You might think a holiday was a strange thing to create during a war. But perhaps that was exactly when people needed it most. From then on, every president issued a Thanksgiving proclamation each year. Hale had finally won. She lived to see Thanksgiving celebrated across the whole country, every autumn, for the rest of her long life. I find it lovely that one stubborn editor and one tired president gave the country this gentle day.

IMG: pilgrim-story-books-nineteenth-century | a family reading a picture book by the fireside in the nineteenth century, an illustration of settlers and a feast on the page
HEAD: The idea that the 1621 harvest feast was the first Thanksgiving became popular only in the 1800s.
BODY: For a long time, almost nobody connected Thanksgiving with that harvest at Plymouth. Winslow's letter had been nearly forgotten. Then, in 1841, a historian reprinted it and added a little note, calling the event the first Thanksgiving. The idea caught on. Later in the century, schoolbooks, magazines and paintings began to show the Pilgrims and the Wampanoag sitting down together to a feast. Sometimes the pictures got things wrong, with black clothes and buckles, or tepees that the Wampanoag never used. You've probably seen pictures like that. Still, the story became part of how Americans thought about the holiday. Children acted it out in school plays every November. It's a good reminder of how stories grow. A few lines in an old letter became a picture that millions of people share. Isn't it interesting how history and memory slowly weave together?

IMG: franksgiving-1939-calendar-shops | a busy department store street in 1939 with shoppers in coats and hats, autumn decorations in the windows, soft evening light
HEAD: In 1939, President Franklin Roosevelt moved Thanksgiving a week earlier, and many people were not pleased.
BODY: In 1939, the last Thursday of November fell very late, on the thirtieth. Shopkeepers worried that the Christmas shopping season would be too short. So President Roosevelt announced that Thanksgiving would be a week earlier. The country was rather confused. Some states followed the new date, and others kept the old one. For two years, some families celebrated on one Thursday and their cousins in the next state on another. People jokingly called the new holiday Franksgiving. Football teams had to reschedule their games. Calendars were wrong. Finally, in 1941, Congress settled it by law. Thanksgiving would always be on the fourth Thursday of November. And so it has been ever since. You might smile at all the fuss. But it shows how much people cared about their Thanksgiving traditions. Would you have been cross if someone moved your favourite holiday?

IMG: parade-balloons-city-morning | huge colourful parade balloons floating between tall buildings on a cold November morning, crowds in warm coats watching
HEAD: The famous New York Thanksgiving parade began in 1924, with animals borrowed from a zoo.
BODY: In 1924, the employees of Macy's department store in New York put on a parade on Thanksgiving morning. It was meant to start the Christmas shopping season. There were floats, bands, people in costumes and even real animals, like camels, elephants and bears, borrowed from the Central Park Zoo. The parade ended at the store, where Santa Claus arrived to great cheers. It was such a success that it became a yearly tradition. A few years later, the animals were replaced by giant balloons. Today, millions of people watch it on television every year, often while the turkey roasts in the oven. You'd see enormous cartoon characters floating between the skyscrapers, held down by dozens of people with ropes. On windy days, that can be quite a struggle. Can you picture a giant balloon bobbing gently past a high window?

HEAD: Today, Thanksgiving is one of the busiest travel days of the year in America.
BODY: Every November, tens of millions of Americans travel to be with their families. Roads, trains and airports are packed. HEAD: For many Wampanoag and other Native people, Thanksgiving is also a day of remembrance.

IMG: wampanoag-language-classroom-today | a bright classroom where children and teachers read from books, the walls decorated with words in an indigenous language, calm afternoon light
HEAD: The Wampanoag language, which had not been spoken for generations, is being brought back to life.
BODY: For a long time, the Wampanoag language wasn't spoken in daily life by anyone. But it hadn't completely disappeared. In the 1600s, a missionary named John Eliot worked with Native translators to make a Bible in their language. It was printed in 1663, and it was the first Bible ever printed in North America. Many other letters, deeds and records were written in the language too. In the 1990s, a Wampanoag woman named Jessie Little Doe Baird began studying these old writings. With other scholars and her community, she pieced the language back together. Today, there are classes, a dictionary and a school, and some children are growing up speaking it again. You might think a sleeping language can never wake up. This one did. I find that one of the most hopeful stories I know. Isn't it wonderful to imagine the old words spoken again by children?

HEAD: The Wampanoag are still here, and many of them live on Cape Cod and Martha's Vineyard.
BODY: It's easy to tell this story as though the Wampanoag belong only to the past. They don't. Today, thousands of Wampanoag people live in southern New England. The Mashpee Wampanoag on Cape Cod and the Aquinnah Wampanoag on Martha's Vineyard are two tribes with their own governments. They hold gatherings called powwows, with drumming, dancing, food and families coming together, some of which have taken place every summer for many decades. Wampanoag people work as teachers, fishermen, artists, nurses and builders, like anyone else. And they keep the old knowledge alive, from planting the Three Sisters to making canoes and telling the old stories. You might pass a Wampanoag family on a beach on Cape Cod without knowing it.
