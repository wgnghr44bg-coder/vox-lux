TOPIC: The land of the People of the First Light
===
IMG: wampanoag-coast-autumn-dawn | a quiet New England coastline at dawn in autumn, salt marsh, golden grass, a calm bay and forest edge, soft mist
HEAD: Long before any English ship arrived, this coast was the homeland of the Wampanoag, the People of the First Light.
BODY: The Wampanoag had lived in what is now southeastern Massachusetts for more than twelve thousand years. Their name is usually translated as the People of the First Light, or the People of the Dawn. It fitted their home well. They lived on the eastern edge of the land, where the sun rose first out of the sea. Their country stretched from Cape Cod and the islands of Martha's Vineyard and Nantucket inland across rivers, ponds and forests. Around the year 1600, they lived in dozens of villages, each with its own leaders and fields. Archaeologists have found their shell heaps, tools and hearths all along the coast, some of them thousands of years old. Yet this was not an empty wilderness waiting to be discovered. It was a homeland, full of named places, family fields and well-known paths. When the English arrived in 1620, they stepped into a country that had been carefully lived in and looked after for a very long time.
===
IMG: wampanoag-family-path-forest | a Wampanoag family walking along a forest path in autumn colours, carrying baskets, soft afternoon light through tall trees
HEAD: The Wampanoag moved between summer and winter homes, following the seasons.
BODY: Wampanoag families did not stay in one place all year. In spring and summer, they lived near the coast and the rivers. There they planted their fields, gathered shellfish and caught fish in the shallow bays. When the harvest was finished in autumn, many families moved inland to sheltered valleys in the forest. The trees broke the force of the winter wind, and firewood was close at hand. Families carried mats, baskets and cooking pots along well-worn paths between the two places. English writers in the 1620s described this pattern with some surprise, because English farmers lived in one house all their lives. Yet the Wampanoag way made good sense. Each season, people went where the land offered the most food and the best shelter. Their year had a steady rhythm, shaped by planting, fishing, hunting and harvest. It was a way of living that fitted closely to the land and its changes.
===
IMG: wetu-dome-house-woven-mats | a round dome-shaped house covered in woven cattail mats and bark in a forest clearing, smoke rising from the roof hole
HEAD: A Wampanoag house, called a wetu, was a dome of bent saplings covered with bark or woven mats.
BODY: The wetu was a simple and clever kind of house. Builders pushed long, flexible saplings into the ground in a circle and bent them over into arches. They tied the arches together to make a strong, rounded frame. Then they covered it with sheets of bark, or with mats woven from cattail reeds, which shed the rain well. A small opening at the top let smoke escape from the fire in the middle. Inside were low platforms along the walls, covered with mats and furs for sitting and sleeping. In summer, mats could be rolled up to let the breeze through. In winter, families often lived in larger longhouses, where several households shared the warmth of more than one fire. English visitors wrote that these houses were warmer and drier than they expected. Reconstructed wetus can be seen today at living history museums in Massachusetts. They show how well a house of wood, bark and reeds could suit the New England seasons.
===
IMG: canoe-dugout-river-mist | a long dugout canoe made from a single tree trunk gliding on a misty river at dawn, two paddlers, autumn trees on the banks
HEAD: Wampanoag canoes, called mishoon, were made from a single tree trunk hollowed out with fire.
BODY: Making a mishoon took skill and patience. Builders chose a tall, straight tree, often a pine or a chestnut, and felled it. Then they lit small, controlled fires along the top of the log. As each patch of wood burned and softened, they scraped it away with shells and stone tools. The process was repeated again and again until the log became a long, smooth boat. A large mishoon could carry several people and their goods along rivers and the coast. Canoes were used for fishing, travelling, trading and visiting relatives in other villages. Archaeologists have found old dugout canoes preserved in the mud of New England ponds. Some are hundreds of years old. Wampanoag craftspeople still make mishoon in the traditional way today, burning and scraping them by hand. The canoe was more than a tool. It connected villages, families and fishing grounds across a land shaped by water.
===
IMG: fishing-weir-river-autumn | a fish trap of woven wooden stakes across a shallow river, people standing in the water, golden autumn light
HEAD: Every spring, rivers filled with fish, and the Wampanoag caught them with traps called weirs.
BODY: In spring, great numbers of herring swam up the rivers from the sea to lay their eggs in freshwater ponds. The Wampanoag built weirs across the streams to catch them. A weir was a fence of wooden stakes and woven branches. It let the water through but stopped the fish. People could then scoop them up in baskets. The bays and shores offered much more as well. There were cod and bass in the deeper water, eels in the muddy creeks, and clams, oysters and lobsters along the shore. Early English writers described the coast as remarkably rich in fish. Fish were eaten fresh, or dried and smoked for the winter. Knowing when and where each kind would appear was important knowledge, passed down through generations. Even so, it was not easy living. It depended on skill, planning and close attention to the seasons. The rivers and the sea were a pantry that had to be understood.
===
IMG: autumn-forest-deer-clearing | deer standing at the edge of an open forest clearing in autumn, scattered oaks, low golden sunlight, smoke haze
HEAD: The Wampanoag regularly burned the undergrowth in the forest, keeping it open for deer and for travel.
BODY: When English settlers first walked through the New England woods, many were surprised by what they saw. In many places, the forest was open and park-like, with large trees standing well apart and grass beneath them. This was the result of careful work. Native peoples set controlled fires to clear away brush and fallen branches. Fresh grass grew back afterwards and attracted deer, which made hunting easier. The open ground also made walking and travelling much simpler. Several early English writers mentioned this burning in their accounts. Historians now see it as a form of land management, practised for a very long time. Hunters knew the clearings well and waited at their edges in the early morning. A successful hunt brought venison for food and deerskin for clothing. The forest, in other words, was not untouched. It had been gently shaped by people for countless generations to provide what they needed.
===
IMG: sachem-gathering-council-fire | a group of Wampanoag men seated around a council fire under tall trees at dusk, calm faces, feathers and deerskin clothing
HEAD: Each Wampanoag community was led by a sachem, who governed with the advice of respected elders.
BODY: A sachem was a leader, but not a king in the European sense. Sachems were expected to be generous, wise and fair, and to help people in need. Before making important decisions, a sachem met with elders and counsellors and listened to their views. Matters were often discussed at length before agreement was reached. Women could also be sachems, which surprised many European visitors. In 1621, the most powerful sachem in the region was Ousamequin, often known by his title, Massasoit, meaning great sachem. His home village was Sowams, near present-day Warren in Rhode Island. Several smaller communities looked to him for leadership and protection. English writers described him as a dignified man who spoke carefully. Even so, his position was not unlimited. A sachem kept his authority by keeping the trust of his people. Leadership among the Wampanoag rested on respect and generosity as much as on power.
===
TOPIC: Patuxet, the place of the little falls
===
IMG: patuxet-town-brook-harbour | a small stream flowing over little falls into a calm harbour, cleared fields on a gentle hillside, autumn trees
HEAD: The English settlement of Plymouth was built on the site of an older Wampanoag village called Patuxet.
BODY: Patuxet is usually translated as "at the little falls." The name came from a small stream that tumbled over rocks into the harbour. For generations, a Wampanoag community lived there, fishing in the bay and growing corn on the slopes above the shore. A few years before the English arrived, an epidemic swept along the coast between about 1616 and 1619. It had been brought by European sailors and traders, and the local people had no resistance to it. The death toll was very high, and Patuxet was left almost empty. When the Mayflower passengers came in December 1620, they found cleared fields and no one living there. They built their village on that land. Historians still debate exactly which disease caused the epidemic. Yet its effect is clear. Plymouth did not grow on untouched ground. It grew on the fields of a community that had been there for a very long time.
===
IMG: tisquantum-portrait-shore | a Wampanoag man in deerskin standing on a quiet shore looking out to sea, calm expression, soft evening light
HEAD: Tisquantum, a man from Patuxet, had been taken across the Atlantic years before the Pilgrims arrived.
BODY: Tisquantum is often called Squanto in English. In 1614, an English captain named Thomas Hunt lured him and about twenty other men onto his ship. Hunt carried them to Spain, intending to sell them. Tisquantum later reached England, where he lived for some time and learned to speak English. Around 1619, he finally found a ship back to New England. When he returned to Patuxet, he found that his village had been emptied by the epidemic. Even so, he chose to help the English who settled there in 1620. According to William Bradford, he showed them where to catch fish and how to plant corn so that it grew well. He also acted as an interpreter between the colonists and Massasoit. Bradford called him a special instrument sent by God for their good. His life shows how deeply Europe and New England were already connected, years before the Mayflower sailed.
===
IMG: hobbamock-family-wetu-near-village | a Wampanoag family's dome house standing near a small English village of timber houses, smoke rising from both, morning light
HEAD: From 1621, a Wampanoag counsellor named Hobbamock lived with his family beside Plymouth.
BODY: Massasoit sent one of his trusted men, Hobbamock, to live near the English settlement. Hobbamock was a pniese, a respected warrior and adviser. He came with his family and built his own home a short distance from the English houses. English writers, including Edward Winslow, mentioned him often. He helped the colonists as a guide and interpreter, and he was an important link between the two peoples. Hobbamock and Tisquantum did not always agree, and the records suggest some rivalry between them. For the Wampanoag, having Hobbamock nearby was also a way to keep watch on the newcomers. Friendship and caution existed side by side. Hobbamock stayed close to the English for many years and later became a Christian. His family's home beside the English village is a reminder that Plymouth was not an isolated English outpost. From its earliest years, Wampanoag people lived, worked and talked alongside it every day.
===
IMG: wampanoag-women-gardens-planting | Wampanoag women working in a field of small earth mounds, planting with wooden hoes, baskets beside them, soft spring light
HEAD: In Wampanoag communities, women were responsible for the fields and the crops.
BODY: Wampanoag women planted, tended and harvested the crops. They decided which seeds to save for the next year and where to plant them. Their tools included hoes made of wood, stone or large clam shells. Agricultural knowledge passed from grandmothers to mothers to daughters over many generations. Women often worked together in the fields, with small children close by. Men hunted, fished, built canoes and helped clear new fields, and they assisted with heavy work. English observers were surprised by this division of labour, because in England men usually did most of the ploughing and sowing. Some English writers misunderstood it entirely. Yet the women's work was the foundation of the community. The crops they grew fed families for much of the year, and stored corn carried them through the winter. Historians today emphasise how central women's knowledge was to life in the region. The fields of New England were, in a very real sense, women's work.
===
IMG: cornfield-mounds-three-sisters | rows of small earth mounds with tall corn, climbing beans and broad squash leaves growing together, golden late summer light
HEAD: Corn, beans and squash were grown together in the same small hills, a method often called the Three Sisters.
BODY: Native farmers in much of eastern North America planted these three crops together. A few kernels of corn were placed in a small mound of earth. When the corn had grown a little, beans were planted around it. The beans climbed the corn stalks as if they were poles. Squash was planted between the mounds. Its broad leaves shaded the soil, kept in moisture and held back weeds. Beans also help to enrich the soil, which benefits the corn. The name Three Sisters comes from the Haudenosaunee, who told stories of three sisters who supported one another. Modern agricultural scientists have studied the method and found it productive and well balanced. Together, the three crops also made a nourishing diet. Even so, the system required careful timing and constant attention through the summer. It was a sophisticated way of farming, developed over many centuries, and it is still used by some gardeners today.
===
IMG: nasaump-cooking-pot-fire | a clay pot of cornmeal porridge simmering over a small fire inside a bark house, berries and nuts in a basket nearby
HEAD: A common Wampanoag dish was nasaump, a porridge of ground corn often mixed with berries or nuts.
BODY: To make nasaump, dried corn was pounded into a coarse meal. The meal was simmered slowly in water over a fire. Depending on the season, cooks added strawberries, blueberries, walnuts or chestnuts. The English settlers learned to make a similar dish, which they called samp. Another common dish was a stew of corn and beans, sometimes cooked with fish or meat. The Narragansett, neighbours of the Wampanoag, called it msickquatash, the origin of the English word succotash. Food was cooked in clay pots, or in containers of bark into which heated stones were dropped to bring the water to a boil. Roger Williams, an English writer who lived among the Narragansett, recorded many of these food words in a book published in 1643. Even so, much everyday cooking knowledge was never written down. It survived through families. Several of these dishes are still made in New England kitchens today.

TOPIC: A small village on the hill
===
IMG: plymouth-village-1621-street-houses | a small village of rough timber houses with thatched roofs along a dirt street climbing a hill, gardens and fences, autumn light
HEAD: In the autumn of 1621, Plymouth consisted of a single street with a handful of small houses.
BODY: The English settlement was very small in its first year. Edward Winslow wrote in December 1621 that the colonists had built seven dwelling houses and four buildings for common use. The street ran up a hill from the harbour, with houses on each side. The houses were small and made of rough timber, with thatched roofs of grass and reeds. Each family had a garden plot behind its house, fenced to keep out animals. At the top of the hill was a site the settlers planned to use for a fort. A small stream, later called Town Brook, ran down to the harbour and supplied fresh water. Only about fifty of the original Mayflower passengers were still alive. Even so, the little street marked a real beginning. After a long voyage and a very hard winter, the colonists now had houses, gardens and a harvest. The modern street on the same line is called Leyden Street.
===
IMG: settlers-house-interior-hearth | a simple one-room timber house interior with a wide clay hearth, iron pot, wooden benches and a chest, soft firelight
HEAD: A Plymouth house usually had one or two rooms, where the whole family cooked, ate and slept.
BODY: Early Plymouth houses were simple. The floors were packed earth. The walls were timber, with gaps filled with clay and straw. At one end was a wide fireplace, with a chimney of wood plastered thickly with clay to resist the heat. The hearth provided warmth, light and every hot meal, so the fire was kept going almost constantly. Furniture was limited: a board table, benches or stools, perhaps one chair, chests for clothing and a bed or two. Children often slept on straw mattresses on the floor. Windows were small and usually had no glass. They were covered with oiled paper or wooden shutters. Archaeologists working in Plymouth have found post holes, hearth stones and broken pottery from these early houses. Reconstructions at living history museums are based on such evidence. The houses were cramped and smoky by modern standards. Yet for people who had spent a winter aboard ship, they were a great improvement.
===
IMG: pilgrims-colourful-clothing-village | English settlers in colourful seventeenth century clothing, red, green, brown and violet, working in a village street, warm light
HEAD: The Plymouth colonists did not dress only in black, and they did not wear buckles on their hats.
BODY: The familiar image of the Pilgrims in black clothes with buckled hats comes from later paintings and illustrations. Buckles on hats and shoes became fashionable later in the seventeenth century. Records of the colonists' possessions, such as inventories made after their deaths, mention clothing in many colours. Red, green, brown, blue and violet all appear. Men wore linen shirts, woollen breeches, stockings and doublets. Women wore long skirts, fitted bodices and aprons, with linen caps. Black cloth was expensive to dye and was often kept for formal occasions. Everyday clothes were practical and were mended many times, because new cloth had to come from England. William Brewster's inventory, for example, included a violet coat and a green waistcoat. Historians and museum costume makers rely on these lists today. They show a community that dressed much like other English people of modest means. The colourful reality is quite different from the solemn picture in later prints.
===
IMG: pilgrims-name-book-candle | an open old book and a quill on a wooden table by candlelight in a small timber room
HEAD: The settlers did not call themselves Pilgrims; the name came from a phrase in William Bradford's history.
BODY: The religious members of the Plymouth colony usually called themselves saints, brethren or separatists. They had separated from the Church of England. The word Pilgrims came from William Bradford's history of the colony. Describing the day they left Leiden in Holland, he wrote that they knew they were pilgrims and lifted their eyes to heaven. Bradford's manuscript was not widely known for many years. In the late eighteenth century, writers began using the word Pilgrims for the whole group, and the name stuck. Other passengers on the Mayflower, who came for work or opportunity rather than for religious reasons, were known at the time as strangers. Bradford's original manuscript, Of Plymouth Plantation, survived a long and complicated journey. It was eventually returned from England to Massachusetts in 1897. Today it is kept in the State Library in Boston. A single phrase in that handwritten book gave the colonists the name by which most people now know them.
===
IMG: governor-bradford-writing-table | a thoughtful young man in a simple doublet writing at a wooden table by a small window, autumn light
HEAD: William Bradford became governor of Plymouth in 1621, when he was about thirty-one years old.
BODY: The colony's first governor, John Carver, died in the spring of 1621. The colonists then chose William Bradford to lead them. Bradford had been born in Yorkshire around 1590 and was orphaned as a child. As a teenager, he joined a separatist congregation and later went with it to Holland. He was largely self-taught and read widely, learning several languages. As governor, he was responsible for food supplies, relations with the Wampanoag and the colony's debts to its investors in London. He was re-elected many times and served as governor for most of the next three decades. Bradford also wrote Of Plymouth Plantation, a detailed history of the colony from its origins to 1647. Historians rely on it heavily, though they also note that it reflects his own point of view. Even so, few founders of any settlement left such a full record. Much of what is known about early Plymouth comes from his patient writing.
===
IMG: myles-standish-musket-hill | a short sturdy man in a breastplate holding a matchlock musket on a grassy hilltop above a small harbour, wind in the grass
HEAD: The colony's military leader was Myles Standish, a professional soldier hired for the voyage.
BODY: Myles Standish had served as a soldier in the Netherlands before joining the Mayflower passengers. He was not a member of the religious congregation. The colonists hired him to organise their defence. Contemporary accounts describe him as short and quick-tempered. A critic of the colony, Thomas Morton, mockingly called him Captain Shrimp. Standish trained the men in the use of their muskets and planned the colony's fortifications. He also led expeditions and negotiations with Native communities, some of which became violent in later years. During the first winter, when many colonists were ill, Bradford recorded that Standish was one of the few who stayed healthy and helped nurse the sick. Historians describe him in mixed terms, as both loyal and harsh. His sword, said to have belonged to him, is now held by a historical society. Like many figures of early Plymouth, he is remembered both for service and for conflict.
===
IMG: four-women-hearth-cooking | four English women in aprons and linen caps preparing food together around a large hearth, children helping, warm firelight
HEAD: By the autumn of 1621, only four of the adult women who sailed on the Mayflower were still alive.
BODY: The first winter in Plymouth was very hard, and the adult women suffered especially heavy losses. By the time of the harvest, only four married women remained. They were Eleanor Billington, Mary Brewster, Elizabeth Hopkins and Susanna Winslow. With the help of girls and servants, they did most of the cooking for the whole colony. That meant baking, boiling, roasting, salting and storing food for around fifty people. During the harvest celebration, there were also about ninety Wampanoag guests. Historians have pointed out how much work this must have meant for so few women. Their names appear in Bradford's list of passengers, with brief notes on who died and who survived. Very little else was written about their daily lives. Even so, their work underpinned the whole settlement. The famous meal of 1621 depended on the labour of four women and a group of girls, working over open fires.
===
IMG: pilgrim-children-names-village-lane | children in seventeenth century clothes playing in a village lane between timber houses, a dog running beside them, golden light
HEAD: Some Plymouth children had unusual names, including Love, Wrestling, Remember and Humility.
BODY: Many children in early Plymouth had ordinary names such as John, Mary or Elizabeth. Some families, however, chose names that expressed religious ideas or hopes. Love Brewster and his younger brother Wrestling Brewster were sons of William Brewster. Remember Allerton was a daughter of Isaac Allerton. Humility Cooper was a young girl travelling with relatives. Resolved White was a small boy, and his brother Peregrine was born aboard the Mayflower in Cape Cod Harbour in November 1620. These names are all recorded in Bradford's list of passengers. Children and teenagers made up a large part of the colony's population after the first winter. By some estimates, they were close to half of the survivors. Children fetched water, gathered firewood, weeded gardens and watched animals. They were also part of a lively village life. Their names, preserved in a few lines of Bradford's list, are among the most personal details to survive from the colony's first years.

TOPIC: Busy autumn days
===
IMG: village-dawn-rooster-smoke | dawn over a small timber village, smoke rising from chimneys, a rooster on a fence, mist over the harbour below
HEAD: In early Plymouth, the working day began at first light with fires to tend and animals to feed.
BODY: Each morning, the hearth fire had to be revived from the embers of the night before. Water was fetched from the brook and animals were fed. The colony had goats, pigs and chickens, and two dogs that had come on the Mayflower, a mastiff and a spaniel. Bradford's history mentions both dogs during an early expedition. There were no cattle yet, so there was no fresh milk or butter. The first cows did not arrive until 1624. Breakfast was usually simple, often a bowl of corn porridge or bread with whatever remained from the day before. In the autumn, daylight hours were growing shorter, and work had to be fitted into them. Candles and lamp oil were precious and used carefully. So the rhythm of the day followed the sun closely. The settlers' lives were shaped by the same forces as those of farmers everywhere at the time: light, weather and the needs of animals and crops.
===
IMG: children-fetching-water-brook | children carrying wooden buckets down to a small clear stream among autumn trees, morning mist, soft light
HEAD: Children in early Plymouth carried out many daily tasks, including fetching water from the brook.
BODY: Water for cooking, drinking and washing came from Town Brook, the small stream that ran down to the harbour. Carrying it up the hill in wooden buckets was heavy and frequent work, and children did much of it. They also gathered firewood, fed chickens, weeded gardens and turned meat on the spit. Younger children kept birds away from the growing crops. Older girls helped with cooking, spinning and caring for babies. Older boys worked in the fields with the men. This was normal in England at the time, where children were expected to contribute to the household from an early age. Historians of childhood note that work and play were often mixed together. Even so, there was time for games, and toys such as tops and dolls are known from the period. In a small colony where every pair of hands mattered, children were an essential part of the workforce, and their efforts helped the settlement survive.
===
IMG: men-felling-trees-clapboard | men splitting logs and shaping boards with axes at the edge of a forest, piles of timber, autumn leaves
HEAD: Plymouth's men cut timber both to build houses and to send back to England to pay the colony's debts.
BODY: The colonists had financed their voyage by agreement with a group of London investors, often called the Merchant Adventurers. In return, the settlers promised to send back goods for sale, such as furs, fish and timber. So alongside building their own homes, the men cut trees and split them into boards known as clapboard. All of this was done by hand, with axes, wedges and saws. There were no horses or oxen yet to haul logs. When the ship Fortune left Plymouth in December 1621, it carried a cargo of clapboard and beaver skins. Bradford estimated the value of this cargo in his history. The investors in London were often disappointed with the returns, and the debt remained a burden for years. Even so, the work shows how closely the small colony was tied to the economy of England. Every board sent home was part of a long and difficult effort to pay for the voyage.
===
IMG: fishing-boat-bay-cod | a small open wooden boat with two men fishing for cod on a calm bay, rocky shore and forest behind, morning haze
HEAD: Although the bay was rich in fish, the settlers at first had little success catching them.
BODY: Most of the Plymouth colonists had been farmers, artisans and townspeople in England and the Netherlands rather than fishermen. Bradford noted that they had brought hooks that were too large for the fish in the bay. In their first months, catches were often poor. Over time, with advice from Tisquantum and through trial and error, they improved. By the autumn of 1621, Bradford recorded that they had good supplies of cod and bass. Tisquantum also showed them how to catch eels by treading them out of the mud in the creeks. Edward Winslow later described the abundance of fish and shellfish in letters home. The irony was noted at the time. New England's waters were among the richest in the Atlantic, and fishing fleets from Europe had worked the coast for years. Even so, a small group of inexperienced settlers needed time and help to learn how to use those riches.
===
IMG: gardens-autumn-herbs-pottage | a small fenced kitchen garden behind a timber house with herbs, cabbages and beans in autumn light, a woman with a basket
HEAD: Behind their houses, the colonists grew herbs and vegetables from seeds brought from England.
BODY: The settlers carried seeds across the Atlantic, carefully wrapped. In the small gardens behind their houses, they grew herbs such as parsley, sage and thyme, and vegetables such as cabbages, turnips, onions and carrots. Some of these did well in New England soil, and others struggled. Much of what the gardens produced went into pottage, a thick stew that simmered in the iron pot for much of the day. Meat, beans, grains and vegetables were added as they became available. Pottage was eaten with bread and was a staple of ordinary English diets at the time. Gardens also supplied plants used as medicines, since there were no apothecaries' shops nearby. English gardening books of the period describe these plants and their uses. Even so, gardening in a new climate involved many failures. The colonists learned which plants thrived and which did not. Their small gardens combined English habits with the conditions of a new land.
===
IMG: hearth-cooking-iron-pot-spit | an iron pot hanging over a hearth fire and a roasting spit with birds, a woman stirring with a long wooden spoon, warm light
HEAD: All cooking in early Plymouth was done over an open hearth with a few iron pots and pans.
BODY: The colonists had no proper bake ovens in their first months, so food was boiled, stewed, fried or roasted over the hearth. A large iron pot hung from a hook or crane above the flames. Smaller pans with short legs stood among the coals. Meat and birds were roasted on an iron spit that had to be turned steadily so the food cooked evenly. This was often a job for children. Bread could be baked in a covered pot buried in the embers. Cooking over an open fire required constant attention to the heat, the fuel and the food. Archaeologists in Plymouth have found fragments of iron pots and pottery cooking vessels from the period. English cookbooks of the early 1600s describe the same methods. Even so, cooking for a family, let alone for a whole colony at harvest time, was slow and laborious work. Much of each day revolved around the fire.
===
IMG: sabbath-meeting-house-quiet | a small group of settlers walking quietly up a hill to a simple timber building on a Sunday morning, misty autumn light
HEAD: On Sundays, all ordinary work stopped in Plymouth and the colonists gathered for worship.
BODY: For the Plymouth separatists, the Sabbath was a holy day set apart from ordinary life. No one worked, hunted or played games. In the first years, there was no separate meeting house, so services were held in the common house and later in the fort on the hill. Services were long, with prayers, Bible readings and sermons. Because their minister, John Robinson, had stayed behind in Leiden, the colony's elder, William Brewster, usually led worship. The congregation sang psalms from a book called the Ainsworth Psalter, without musical instruments. A copy of this psalter is said to have come over on the Mayflower. Visitors from other English settlements sometimes commented on the strictness of these Sundays. Even so, for the colonists themselves, the Sabbath was a time of rest and shared purpose after a week of hard labour. It reflected the religious convictions that had brought many of them across the ocean in the first place.

TOPIC: Autumn in a Wampanoag village
===
IMG: wampanoag-village-autumn-harvest | a Wampanoag village of dome houses among autumn trees, women carrying baskets of corn, smoke rising, golden afternoon light
HEAD: Autumn was the busiest season of the year in a Wampanoag village.
BODY: In the autumn, the fields were full and the forests offered nuts, game and firewood. Women and girls harvested corn, beans and squash and spread them out to dry in the sun. Men returned from hunting with deer and from the shore with fish. Children gathered chestnuts and walnuts. Families prepared for the move inland by packing food into baskets and storage pits. Harvest was also a time of gatherings and ceremonies of thanks, with songs, dances and shared meals. English observers in the 1620s and 1630s described such gatherings, though often without fully understanding them. Wampanoag oral tradition today also speaks of many seasonal ceremonies of thanksgiving. Giving thanks for the harvest was therefore not a new idea brought by the English. It was already an old and familiar part of the Wampanoag year. The busy autumn of 1621 at Plymouth took place alongside this much older pattern of harvest and gratitude.

IMG: green-corn-ceremony-dance | Wampanoag people dancing in a circle around a fire at dusk, drummers seated nearby, autumn trees, warm glow
HEAD: The Wampanoag held ceremonies of thanksgiving at several points in the year, not only at harvest.
BODY: Wampanoag tradition includes ceremonies that mark the turning of the seasons. Thanks were given when strawberries ripened, when the green corn was ready and when the main harvest was gathered. In this way of thinking, plants, animals and waters were gifts that deserved respect and gratitude. Ceremonies often included songs, dances, speeches by elders and the sharing of food with all present, including visitors and those in need. Some of these ceremonies continue among Wampanoag communities today. Historians note that seventeenth-century English writers rarely understood these events well, and their descriptions are brief. Much of what is known comes from Wampanoag oral tradition. Even so, the evidence makes one point clear. Gratitude was woven into the whole Wampanoag year, rather than reserved for a single day. When people today speak of the first Thanksgiving, Wampanoag scholars often point out that their ancestors had been giving thanks for a very long time before 1621.

IMG: wampanoag-woman-weaving-mat | a Wampanoag woman weaving a long mat of cattail reeds outside her house, a child watching, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Wampanoag women wove mats, baskets and bags from reeds, grasses and bark.
BODY: Weaving was an important and very old skill. Women gathered cattail reeds and grasses in late summer, dried them and wove them into long mats. These mats covered house frames, lined sleeping platforms and served as floor coverings. Smaller items included baskets for storage and carrying, and finely woven bags with patterns made from natural dyes. Some of these bags were treasured for many years. A few early examples survive in museum collections, showing the fine twining techniques used. When families moved between summer and winter homes, they rolled up the house mats and carried them along. At the new site, the mats were laid over a fresh frame of saplings. English observers admired this practical system. Wampanoag artists still practise traditional weaving today and teach it to younger generations. The craft shows how closely household life, building and the seasonal round were connected. A single mat could be part of a home, a bed and a journey.

IMG: deerskin-clothing-preparing-hide | a person scraping and softening a deerskin stretched on a wooden frame outside a bark house, autumn leaves, soft light
HEAD: Most Wampanoag clothing was made from carefully prepared deerskin.
BODY: After a hunt, nearly every part of the deer was used. The meat was eaten, the bones were made into tools, and the skin was turned into clothing. Preparing a hide took several days. It was stretched on a frame, scraped clean, soaked and worked by hand until it became soft. Smoking helped preserve it and keep it supple. Men wore breechcloths and leggings, and women wore skirts and wraps. In cold weather, people wore mantles of fur or deerskin over their shoulders. Moccasins protected the feet and made little noise on forest paths. Clothing was often decorated with paint, shells or porcupine quills. Some English writers commented that this clothing was warm and practical. Even so, after European trade expanded, many Native people also began to use woollen cloth obtained through trade. Wampanoag clothing in the 1620s therefore reflected both old skills and new materials, as the region's economy began to change.

IMG: wampum-beads-shells-making | small purple and white shell beads being drilled and strung on a cord, quahog shells on a wooden board, soft light
HEAD: Wampum beads were made from whelk and quahog shells and took great skill to produce.
BODY: Along the shore, people collected the shells of whelks, which gave white beads, and quahog clams, which gave purple ones. The purple beads were rarer and more valued. Making a bead was slow work. A piece of shell was ground into a small cylinder and then drilled through the middle with a stone drill. Beads were strung into necklaces and woven into belts. Some belts used patterns to record agreements and important events. Wampum was given as a gift to honour guests and to confirm promises. In the early seventeenth century, Dutch and English traders began to use wampum almost as a currency in their trade with Native peoples. This changed its role in the regional economy. Historians and Wampanoag scholars emphasise that wampum carried meanings far beyond money. Wampanoag artists still work with quahog shell today, making jewellery and art. The small beads remain a symbol of skill, memory and agreement.

IMG: children-playing-wampanoag-village | Wampanoag children playing a game with sticks and a ball in a clearing by their village, adults watching, golden light
HEAD: Wampanoag children learned mainly by watching, helping and playing.
BODY: There were no schools like those in England, but learning was constant. Girls followed their mothers and grandmothers into the fields and learned planting, weaving and cooking. Boys followed the men and learned to fish, track animals and make tools and bows. Children played games of skill with sticks, stones and balls, raced one another and practised throwing and shooting at targets. English writers such as Roger Williams noted that Native parents were very affectionate towards their children. In the evenings, elders told stories about the world, the animals and important ancestors. Through these stories, children learned the history of their people and the meaning of the land around them. Many places had stories attached to them. Even so, much of this knowledge was passed on orally and was never written down by outsiders. Wampanoag families today continue to teach children through stories, crafts and community gatherings, much as their ancestors did.

IMG: maushop-giant-legend-dunes-sunset | coloured clay cliffs and dunes beside the sea at sunset, mist over the water, a peaceful wild coast
HEAD: In Wampanoag tradition, a giant named Maushop shaped the islands and coast of the region.
BODY: Wampanoag stories tell of Maushop, a kind giant who lived along the coast long ago. He was so large that he could wade into the sea to catch whales, which he shared with the people. According to one story, when he emptied the sand from his moccasins, it formed the islands of Nantucket and Martha's Vineyard. Another story says that the colourful clay cliffs at Aquinnah, on Martha's Vineyard, took their colours from his cooking fires. When fog drifted across the water, people said it was smoke from his pipe. These stories were passed down orally for generations. Some were recorded by English writers in later centuries, often in simplified form. Wampanoag storytellers still tell them today. The cliffs at Aquinnah are now a protected landmark, managed with the involvement of the Aquinnah Wampanoag. The legends show how the land itself was part of the people's history. Every cliff and island had its own story.

IMG: moving-inland-winter-camp-forest | a family carrying rolled mats and baskets along a forest trail to a sheltered valley with a long bark house, late autumn, first frost
HEAD: When the first frosts came, many Wampanoag families moved inland to sheltered winter homes.
BODY: In late autumn, as the nights grew cold, families packed their mats, baskets and tools. They walked inland along forest paths to winter sites in sheltered valleys. There they often lived in longhouses shared by several families, with fires burning along the centre. Winter was a time for hunting deer in the snow, and for repairing tools, weaving and making clothing. Stored corn and beans, dried fish and dried berries fed the family until spring. Long evenings were also a time for storytelling. English writers in the seventeenth century described these seasonal moves, though some misunderstood them as wandering. Archaeological sites inland, with remains of hearths and storage pits, confirm the pattern. Even so, winters could be difficult, especially if harvests had been poor. The move inland was a practical way of reducing the hardships of the cold season. It had been part of life in the region for many centuries before 1621.
