TOPIC: The land of the People of the First Light
===
IMG: wampanoag-coast-autumn-dawn | a quiet New England coastline at dawn in autumn, salt marsh, golden grass, a calm bay and forest edge, soft mist
HEAD: Long before any ship arrived, this coast belonged to the Wampanoag, the People of the First Light.
BODY: Picture yourself standing on a sandy shore in the far east of what we now call Massachusetts. The sun is just rising out of the sea, and the first light touches the marsh grass and turns it gold. That's why the people who lived here called themselves the Wampanoag, the People of the First Light. They were the first to see the sun come up over the land each morning. Their homeland stretched from the shores of Cape Cod to the islands of Martha's Vineyard and Nantucket, and inland across rivers, ponds and wide forests. They'd lived here for more than twelve thousand years. They knew every cove, every spring and every path through the woods. Now, you might think of this as an empty wilderness. It wasn't. It was a home, carefully looked after, full of villages, fields and familiar places with old names. Can you feel the cool salt air on your face?
===
IMG: wampanoag-family-path-forest | a Wampanoag family walking along a forest path in autumn colours, carrying baskets, soft afternoon light through tall trees
HEAD: The Wampanoag moved with the seasons, the way you might move between a summer house and a winter one.
BODY: In spring and summer, families lived close to the coast and the rivers. There, they planted their fields, gathered shellfish and caught fish in the shallow bays. When autumn came and the harvest was in, many of them moved inland, into the shelter of the forest. The trees kept off the worst of the winter wind, and there was firewood all around. You'd see them walking along well-worn paths, carrying baskets, mats and cooking pots on their backs. Children carried smaller bundles and ran ahead. I've always liked this way of living. Nobody was in a hurry to own one single spot forever. Instead, people went where the land offered food and shelter at that time of year. It was a rhythm as old as the tides. Each season had its work, and each place had its moment. Wouldn't it be lovely to live by the seasons like that?
===
IMG: wetu-dome-house-woven-mats | a round dome-shaped house covered in woven cattail mats and bark in a forest clearing, smoke rising from the roof hole
HEAD: A Wampanoag home, called a wetu, was a dome of bent young trees covered with bark or woven mats.
BODY: To build a wetu, people cut long, thin saplings and pushed both ends into the ground, bending them over into arches. Then they tied the arches together to make a rounded frame. Over this frame they laid sheets of bark, or mats woven from cattail reeds, which shed the rain beautifully. A small hole at the top let the smoke out. Inside, you'd find low sleeping platforms along the walls, covered with furs and soft mats. A fire burned gently in the middle. It's surprisingly cosy in there. In summer, the mats could be rolled up to let the breeze through. In winter, families lived in larger longhouses, where several households shared the warmth. Can you picture yourself lying on a fur by the fire, watching the smoke drift up through the hole in the roof, towards the stars?
===
IMG: canoe-dugout-river-mist | a long dugout canoe made from a single tree trunk gliding on a misty river at dawn, two paddlers, autumn trees on the banks
HEAD: Wampanoag canoes were made from a single tree, hollowed out slowly with fire and stone tools.
BODY: The Wampanoag called these canoes mishoon. To make one, a team chose a tall, straight tree, often a chestnut or a pine. They felled it and then laid small fires along the top of the trunk. As each patch of wood burned and softened, they scraped it away with shells and stone tools. Then they burned a little more, and scraped a little more. It took a long time and a great deal of patience. But when it was done, you'd have a strong, smooth boat, big enough to carry several people and their goods. You'd glide down rivers and along the coast in it, fishing, trading and visiting relatives. A good mishoon could last for years. Today, Wampanoag craftspeople still make them in the old way, and you can watch them at work. I find that rather wonderful, don't you?
===
IMG: fishing-weir-river-autumn | a fish trap of woven wooden stakes across a shallow river, people standing in the water, golden autumn light
HEAD: In spring, rivers filled with so many fish that people could catch them by the basketful.
BODY: Every spring, great shoals of herring swam up the rivers from the sea to lay their eggs in the ponds. The Wampanoag built fish traps, called weirs, across the streams. These were fences of wooden stakes and woven branches that let the water through but stopped the fish. You'd stand in the cold, clear water with a basket and simply scoop them up. There were also cod and bass in the bays, eels in the muddy creeks, and clams, oysters and lobsters along the shore. Some of these lobsters were so large, early English writers claimed, that one could feed several people. Fish were eaten fresh, or dried and smoked to keep for winter. Nothing was wasted. Now, you might think this was easy living. It took real skill. You had to know exactly when and where the fish would come. Would you have known where to look?
===
IMG: autumn-forest-deer-clearing | deer standing at the edge of an open forest clearing in autumn, scattered oaks, low golden sunlight, smoke haze
HEAD: The Wampanoag kept parts of the forest open by burning the undergrowth, so deer could graze and walking was easy.
BODY: When the first English settlers walked into the woods, they were surprised. The forest wasn't a dark, tangled jungle. In many places, it looked more like a park, with big trees standing apart and open grass beneath them. That was no accident. Each year, the Wampanoag carefully burned away the bushes and dead branches on the forest floor. The fresh grass that grew back attracted deer, and the open spaces made hunting and travelling much easier. You could see a long way between the trunks. You'd walk on soft ground, with the sunlight falling in long golden stripes. Hunters knew these places well. They'd wait quietly at the edge of a clearing in the early morning. A good hunter might bring home venison for the whole family, and a deerskin for clothing. Can you imagine walking through a forest that people had gently shaped for thousands of years?
===
IMG: sachem-gathering-council-fire | a group of Wampanoag men seated around a council fire under tall trees at dusk, calm faces, feathers and deerskin clothing
HEAD: Each Wampanoag community had a leader called a sachem, who listened before deciding.
BODY: A sachem was a leader, but not quite a king. He or she was expected to be generous, wise and fair. If someone was in need, the sachem was expected to help. Before making an important choice, a sachem met with respected elders and advisers, and listened carefully to what they said. Decisions were talked over, often for a long time, around a fire in the evening. You'd see people sitting in a circle, speaking one at a time, while the others listened in silence. Women could be sachems too, which surprised many European visitors. In 1621, the most important sachem in this region was a man called Ousamequin. You might know him by his title, Massasoit, which means something like great sachem. His home village was called Sowams, beside a quiet bay to the west. We'll meet him again later in our story. I think you'll like him.
===
TOPIC: Patuxet, the place of the little falls
BRIDGE: The council fire is burning low now. Let's walk east together, towards the sea, to a quiet harbour where a small river comes tumbling down to the shore.
===
IMG: patuxet-town-brook-harbour | a small stream flowing over little falls into a calm harbour, cleared fields on a gentle hillside, autumn trees
HEAD: The English village of Plymouth was built on the site of an older Wampanoag village called Patuxet.
BODY: Patuxet means something like "at the little falls." The name came from the small stream that tumbled down over rocks into the harbour. For generations, a Wampanoag community had lived here, fishing in the bay and planting corn on the gentle slopes above the shore. You'd have heard children laughing by the stream and smelled wood smoke from the houses. But a few years before the English came, a terrible sickness swept along this coast. It had been brought by European sailors and traders, and the people here had no protection against it. Very many died, and Patuxet was left empty. When the English arrived, they found cleared fields waiting, with nobody to plant them. It's a sad beginning, and I'll keep it gentle. But it's important to remember. The village of Plymouth grew on land that had already been somebody's home for a very long time.
===
IMG: tisquantum-portrait-shore | a Wampanoag man in deerskin standing on a quiet shore looking out to sea, calm expression, soft evening light
HEAD: One man from Patuxet had seen more of the world than almost anyone in the English village.
BODY: His name was Tisquantum, though the English usually called him Squanto. In 1614, years before the Pilgrims arrived, an English captain named Thomas Hunt tricked him and other men onto his ship and carried them away across the ocean. Tisquantum ended up in Spain, and later in England, where he learned to speak English. After many adventures, he finally found a ship home in 1619. But when he walked back to Patuxet, the village where he'd grown up was empty. His family and neighbours were gone. Can you imagine coming home after so long, and finding only silence? Yet Tisquantum didn't turn away from the English. He chose to live among them, and helped them. He showed them where to fish, and how to plant corn so that it grew well in this soil. Without him, the little village might not have survived its first year.
===
IMG: hobbamock-family-wetu-near-village | a Wampanoag family's dome house standing near a small English village of timber houses, smoke rising from both, morning light
HEAD: By the autumn of 1621, a Wampanoag family lived right beside the English village.
BODY: Massasoit sent one of his trusted men, Hobbamock, to live near Plymouth. Hobbamock was a pniese, a respected warrior and counsellor, and he came with his family. They built their own home a short walk from the English houses. You'd see their smoke rising in the morning, beside the smoke from the English chimneys. Hobbamock helped the settlers with advice and translation, and he was an important link between the two peoples. Tisquantum and Hobbamock didn't always agree, and sometimes there was a little rivalry between them. That's very human, isn't it? But together, they helped the English understand the land and the people around them. For the Wampanoag, it was also a way of keeping an eye on these strange newcomers. Friendship and caution went hand in hand. And Hobbamock's family must have watched the English struggling with their first harvest with great interest.
===
IMG: wampanoag-women-gardens-planting | Wampanoag women working in a field of small earth mounds, planting with wooden hoes, baskets beside them, soft spring light
HEAD: In Wampanoag communities, the fields belonged to the women.
BODY: The women planted, tended and harvested the crops. They decided which seeds to keep for next year and where to plant them. They worked with hoes made from wood, stone or large clam shells. Their knowledge was passed down from grandmother to mother to daughter, over many generations. You'd see them working together in the fields, talking and singing, with small children playing nearby or sleeping in a cradleboard in the shade. Older girls learned by helping. The men hunted, fished, built canoes and cleared new fields, and they helped carry heavy loads. Now, you might think the farming was the lesser job. It wasn't. The crops fed everyone for much of the year, so the women's work was the very heart of the community. When the English arrived, they were surprised to see women in charge of the fields. In England, it was mostly men who ploughed and sowed.
===
IMG: cornfield-mounds-three-sisters | rows of small earth mounds with tall corn, climbing beans and broad squash leaves growing together, golden late summer light
HEAD: Corn, beans and squash were planted together in the same small hills, and people called them the Three Sisters.
BODY: Here's a clever idea. In each small mound of earth, the women planted a few kernels of corn. When the corn had grown a little, they planted beans around it. The beans climbed up the corn stalks, using them like poles. Then they planted squash between the mounds. Its broad leaves shaded the ground, kept the soil moist and kept weeds down. Beans also help to feed the soil, so the corn grew better. Each plant helped the others, like sisters looking after one another. That's why the Haudenosaunee and other peoples called them the Three Sisters. You'd walk between the mounds and brush past big green leaves and twisting bean vines. And when the three were cooked together, they made a very good meal too. Isn't it lovely when something useful is also beautiful? Many gardeners still plant this way today.
===
IMG: nasaump-cooking-pot-fire | a clay pot of cornmeal porridge simmering over a small fire inside a bark house, berries and nuts in a basket nearby
HEAD: A favourite Wampanoag dish was nasaump, a warm porridge of ground corn with berries and nuts.
BODY: To make nasaump, you'd grind dried corn into a coarse meal between two stones. Then you'd simmer it slowly in water in a pot over the fire, and stir in whatever was in season. In summer, that might be strawberries or blueberries. In autumn, crushed walnuts or chestnuts. It's a little like a sweet, nutty porridge. The English learned to make it too, and they called it samp. There was also a stew of corn and beans cooked together with a little fish or meat, which the Narragansett neighbours called msickquatash. We know it today as succotash. You can still eat it in New England. Food was cooked in clay pots or in bark containers. Sometimes, hot stones were dropped into a container of water to make it boil. I love that little trick. Would you like a bowl of nasaump on a cold morning?
===
TOPIC: A small village on the hill
BRIDGE: The pot is scraped clean, and the fire burns low. Now let's walk up from the shore to see the little English village, and the people who'd only just arrived.
===
IMG: plymouth-village-1621-street-houses | a small village of rough timber houses with thatched roofs along a dirt street climbing a hill, gardens and fences, autumn light
HEAD: In the autumn of 1621, Plymouth was just a single dirt street with a few small houses.
BODY: If you walked up from the harbour, you'd find one street climbing the hill, with a few houses along each side. Edward Winslow, one of the settlers, wrote that they had seven dwelling houses and four buildings for shared use. That's all. The houses were small, made of rough timber, with thatched roofs of grass and reeds. Each family had a garden plot behind its house, fenced in to keep out animals. At the top of the hill there was a flat place where the colonists planned to build a fort. Below, the little stream ran down to the harbour, giving fresh water. It was tiny. Yet to the people living there, it meant everything. After a long voyage and a very hard first winter, these few houses were home. Can you hear a hammer tapping somewhere, and a child calling for her mother?
===
IMG: settlers-house-interior-hearth | a simple one-room timber house interior with a wide clay hearth, iron pot, wooden benches and a chest, soft firelight
HEAD: Inside a Plymouth house, a whole family lived, cooked and slept in one or two rooms.
BODY: You'd step through a low wooden door into a dim room. The floor was packed earth. The walls were made of timber, and the gaps were filled with clay and straw. There was a wide fireplace at one end, with a chimney made of wood plastered thickly with clay. Over the fire hung an iron pot. That fire was the heart of the house. It gave warmth, light and every hot meal. Furniture was simple: a table made from boards, a few benches or stools, perhaps one chair for the head of the family, chests for clothes and a bed or two. Children often slept on straw mattresses on the floor. Windows were small and had no glass. They were covered with oiled paper or wooden shutters. On a chilly autumn evening, the whole family would sit close to the fire. Would you have minded sharing one room with everyone?
===
IMG: pilgrims-colourful-clothing-village | English settlers in colourful seventeenth century clothing, red, green, brown and violet, working in a village street, warm light
HEAD: The Pilgrims didn't wear black clothes with big buckles on their hats.
BODY: You might picture them all in black, with tall hats and shiny buckles. That's a picture from much later paintings and cards. In fact, buckles on hats and shoes became fashionable decades afterwards. The people of Plymouth wore clothes of many colours. Records of what they owned mention red, green, brown, blue and even violet. Men wore linen shirts, woollen breeches, stockings and doublets. Women wore long skirts, bodices and aprons, with linen caps on their heads. Black cloth was expensive and kept for special occasions, a bit like a best suit today. Everyday clothes were practical, patched and mended again and again, because there was no shop to buy new ones. If you'd walked down that street in 1621, you'd have seen quite a colourful little crowd. Doesn't that change the picture in your mind a little?
===
IMG: pilgrims-name-book-candle | an open old book and a quill on a wooden table by candlelight in a small timber room
HEAD: The settlers didn't call themselves Pilgrims.
BODY: That name came much later. Many of them simply called themselves saints, or brethren, or the separatists, because they had separated from the Church of England. The word Pilgrims came from a line in William Bradford's own history of the colony. Writing about the day they left Holland, he said that they knew they were pilgrims, and didn't look much on earthly things, but lifted up their eyes to heaven. More than a hundred and fifty years later, people read that line and began calling them the Pilgrims. The name stuck. And the other passengers, who came for work or adventure rather than faith, were known at the time as strangers. So when you hear the word Pilgrims, you're hearing a beautiful old phrase that became a name. I've always liked that. Have you ever been given a nickname that stuck?
===
IMG: governor-bradford-writing-table | a thoughtful young man in a simple doublet writing at a wooden table by a small window, autumn light
HEAD: The governor of Plymouth in 1621 was William Bradford, who was only about thirty-one years old.
BODY: The first governor, John Carver, had died that spring. The colonists then chose William Bradford to lead them. He was a quiet, serious man who loved books and had taught himself several languages. He'd grown up an orphan in a farming village in Yorkshire, and had joined the separatists as a teenager. Now, he was responsible for the whole colony. You'd find him at his table in the evenings, writing letters, keeping accounts and thinking about food, houses and peace with the Wampanoag. Years later, he wrote a long history of the colony, called Of Plymouth Plantation. Without it, we'd know much less about these years. Bradford was elected governor again and again, for most of the next thirty-five years. Can you imagine being chosen to lead a whole village at thirty-one, in a strange new land?
===
IMG: myles-standish-musket-hill | a short sturdy man in a breastplate holding a matchlock musket on a grassy hilltop above a small harbour, wind in the grass
HEAD: The colony's military leader was a small, red-haired soldier called Myles Standish.
BODY: Myles Standish had fought as a soldier in the Netherlands before joining the voyage. He wasn't a member of the church group, but the settlers hired him to organise their defence. He was short, and people said he had a quick temper, which earned him the nickname Captain Shrimp, though probably not to his face. You'd see him on the hill above the village, looking out over the harbour and the woods, planning where to put the guns. He trained the men to use their muskets and kept watch over the colony. He could be stern and suspicious. Yet he was also loyal and hard-working, and when many settlers were sick during the first winter, he helped nurse them. I find that a nice reminder that people are rarely just one thing. Would you have wanted him as a neighbour?
===
IMG: four-women-hearth-cooking | four English women in aprons and linen caps preparing food together around a large hearth, children helping, warm firelight
HEAD: By the autumn of 1621, only four of the grown women who had sailed on the Mayflower were still alive.
BODY: The first winter had been very hard on everyone, and the women had suffered most of all. When the harvest came, only four married women remained: Eleanor Billington, Mary Brewster, Elizabeth Hopkins and Susanna Winslow. Think of what that meant. These four, with the help of the girls and servants, did most of the cooking for the whole colony. They baked, boiled, roasted, salted and stored food for around fifty people. You'd see them working from before sunrise, with their sleeves rolled up, sharing out tasks and calling to the children to fetch water or turn the meat. When the great harvest celebration came, they must have cooked for a hundred and fifty. Can you imagine feeding all those people over an open fire? I think they deserve a quiet round of applause tonight.
===
IMG: pilgrim-children-names-village-lane | children in seventeenth century clothes playing in a village lane between timber houses, a dog running beside them, golden light
HEAD: Some of the Plymouth children had wonderful names, like Love, Wrestling, Remember and Humility.
BODY: Many children in the colony had ordinary names, like John, Mary or Elizabeth. But some families chose names that expressed their faith or hopes. Love Brewster and his little brother Wrestling Brewster lived on the street. So did Remember Allerton and Humility Cooper. Resolved White was a small boy, and his baby brother Peregrine had been born on the ship itself. You'd hear their mothers calling these names across the yards at supper time. Wrestling, come inside! Remember, where are you? I love that little detail. There were about thirty children and teenagers in the colony in 1621, which was more than half of the whole population. So the village was a lively place, full of young voices. Can you imagine having a name like Wrestling at school?

TOPIC: Busy autumn days
BRIDGE: The children have been called inside, one by one, and the street is quiet. Tomorrow, though, there's work to be done, so let's be up early with them.
===
IMG: village-dawn-rooster-smoke | dawn over a small timber village, smoke rising from chimneys, a rooster on a fence, mist over the harbour below
HEAD: In the village, the day began at first light, with fires to light and animals to feed.
BODY: You'd wake in the half-dark to the sound of someone stirring the embers. The fire had to be coaxed back to life before anything else could happen. Then there was water to fetch from the brook, and the few animals to see to. The colony had goats, pigs and chickens, and two dogs, a mastiff and a spaniel, that had come over on the ship. There were no cows yet. Those wouldn't arrive for a few more years. So there was no fresh milk or butter, which the English missed terribly. Breakfast was usually simple: a bowl of corn porridge, a piece of bread or something left over from yesterday. Everyone ate quickly. The days in autumn were growing shorter, and every hour of daylight mattered. Could you get used to starting your day like that, every single morning?
===
IMG: children-fetching-water-brook | children carrying wooden buckets down to a small clear stream among autumn trees, morning mist, soft light
HEAD: Children had their own jobs, and fetching water was one of the most important.
BODY: Every day, someone had to walk down to Town Brook with buckets and carry water back up the hill. It was heavy work, and children did much of it. You'd see them in little groups, wooden buckets in their hands, picking their way down the path and trying not to spill too much on the way back. Children also gathered firewood, fed the chickens, weeded the gardens and turned the meat on the spit by the fire. Smaller children kept birds and animals away from the growing corn, waving their arms and shouting. Older girls helped with cooking, spinning and looking after babies. Older boys worked in the fields with the men. Nobody was too young to help in some small way. Still, there was time for play too. Would you have liked fetching water from a clear little brook every morning?
===
IMG: men-felling-trees-clapboard | men splitting logs and shaping boards with axes at the edge of a forest, piles of timber, autumn leaves
HEAD: The men spent long days cutting timber, partly to build houses and partly to pay their debts in England.
BODY: The colonists had borrowed money from a group of London merchants to pay for their voyage. In return, they'd promised to send back goods to sell, such as furs, fish and timber. So, alongside building their own homes, the men cut trees and split them into boards called clapboard. You'd hear the steady chop of axes from the edge of the forest all day long. It was hard, sweaty work, even on cool autumn days. They also hunted, fished, mended tools and built fences. Everything had to be done by hand. There were no horses yet to pull carts, so logs were dragged or carried. I find it hard to imagine how tired they must have been each evening. And yet, house by house, the village grew. Would you have chosen the axe, or the fishing line?
===
IMG: fishing-boat-bay-cod | a small open wooden boat with two men fishing for cod on a calm bay, rocky shore and forest behind, morning haze
HEAD: The bay was full of cod and bass, but the settlers weren't very good at catching them at first.
BODY: You might think that people from a seafaring country would be expert fishermen. Most of these settlers had been farmers, weavers, printers and craftsmen in England and Holland. They'd brought the wrong hooks, too big for the fish in the bay. So in their first months, they often came home with very little. Bit by bit, with help from Tisquantum and from trial and error, they learned. By autumn, they were catching cod and bass more often. Bradford later wrote of good store of cod and bass in the bay that season. You'd see the little boat heading out in the early morning mist, and coming back in the afternoon with a few gleaming fish in the bottom. Eels, too, could be trodden out of the mud in the creeks with bare feet. Squanto showed them that trick. Would you have tried it?
===
IMG: gardens-autumn-herbs-pottage | a small fenced kitchen garden behind a timber house with herbs, cabbages and beans in autumn light, a woman with a basket
HEAD: Behind every house was a garden with herbs and vegetables brought from England.
BODY: The colonists had carried seeds with them across the ocean, wrapped up carefully in cloth. In their little gardens behind the houses, they grew herbs like parsley, sage and thyme, and vegetables such as cabbages, turnips, onions and carrots. Some of these did well in the new soil, and some didn't. You'd see a woman bending over the rows with her basket, picking what was ready for supper. Most of it went into pottage, a thick stew that bubbled in the iron pot all day. Bits of meat, beans, grains and vegetables were added as they came, and the pottage was eaten with bread. It wasn't fancy, but it was filling and warm. Gardens also grew plants used as medicines, because there was no doctor's shop. I like to think of those little gardens, so far from England. What would you have planted first?
===
IMG: hearth-cooking-iron-pot-spit | an iron pot hanging over a hearth fire and a roasting spit with birds, a woman stirring with a long wooden spoon, warm light
HEAD: All the cooking in Plymouth was done over an open fire, with just a few iron pots and pans.
BODY: There were no ovens in the village yet, not even for bread at first. So everything was boiled, stewed, fried or roasted over the hearth. A large iron pot hung from a hook over the flames. A smaller pan stood on short legs among the coals. Meat or birds were roasted on a long iron rod called a spit, which someone had to turn slowly so it cooked evenly. That was often a child's job, and it was hot work. You'd sit on a stool by the fire, turning and turning, with your face glowing red. Bread was sometimes baked in a covered pot buried in the embers. Cooking was slow, and the fire needed constant care. Wood had to be cut and carried every day. Could you have cooked a whole meal like that, with only the fire to help you?
===
IMG: sabbath-meeting-house-quiet | a small group of settlers walking quietly up a hill to a simple timber building on a Sunday morning, misty autumn light
HEAD: On Sundays, all work stopped, and the whole village gathered for worship.
BODY: For the Plymouth settlers, Sunday, which they called the Sabbath, was a holy day. Nobody worked, cooked big meals, hunted or played games. In the morning, everyone walked to the meeting place together. In 1621, they didn't yet have a proper meeting house, so they gathered in the common house or another large room. You'd sit on hard benches for many hours, listening to prayers, readings and a long sermon. William Brewster, the respected elder, usually led them, because their own minister had stayed behind in Holland. They sang psalms from a special book, without any musical instruments. The singing was slow and deep. You might find it very long. But for them, it was a precious time of rest, peace and togetherness after a hard week. Would you be able to sit still for that long?

TOPIC: Autumn in a Wampanoag village
BRIDGE: The psalms are sung, and the English families rest. Let's slip quietly back into the woods, to see how autumn felt in a Wampanoag village not far away.
===
IMG: wampanoag-village-autumn-harvest | a Wampanoag village of dome houses among autumn trees, women carrying baskets of corn, smoke rising, golden afternoon light
HEAD: Autumn was the busiest and happiest time of year in a Wampanoag village.
BODY: In the autumn, the fields were full and the forest was generous. You'd see women and girls carrying heavy baskets of corn, beans and squash back from the fields, and spreading them out to dry in the sun. Men came back from the hunt with deer and from the shore with fish. Children gathered nuts under the chestnut and walnut trees. Everybody was busy, but it was a cheerful busyness, because there was plenty. Each family prepared for the winter move inland, packing food into baskets and storage pits. In the evenings, people gathered around the fires to eat and talk. Can you smell the roasting chestnuts? The Wampanoag held their own harvest ceremonies of thanks, with songs, dancing and feasting. Giving thanks for the harvest wasn't a new idea to them at all. It was an old and familiar part of the year.
===
IMG: green-corn-ceremony-dance | Wampanoag people dancing in a circle around a fire at dusk, drummers seated nearby, autumn trees, warm glow
HEAD: The Wampanoag gave thanks many times a year, not just once.
BODY: You might think that giving thanks was something the English brought with them. In fact, the Wampanoag held ceremonies of thanksgiving throughout the year. They gave thanks when the first strawberries ripened, when the corn was green and ready, and when the harvest was gathered in. In their way of thinking, the plants, animals and waters were gifts that deserved respect and gratitude. You'd hear drums and singing drift through the trees in the evening, and see people dancing in a slow circle around the fire. Elders would speak, and food would be shared with everyone, including visitors and those who had little. Some of these ceremonies are still held by Wampanoag people today. I find that very beautiful. Gratitude woven into the whole year, not saved up for one day. Wouldn't it be lovely to stop and say thank you for the first strawberries of summer?
===
IMG: wampanoag-woman-weaving-mat | a Wampanoag woman weaving a long mat of cattail reeds outside her house, a child watching, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Women wove mats, baskets and bags from reeds, grasses and bark, with beautiful patterns.
BODY: Weaving was an important skill, and a very old one. Women gathered cattail reeds and grasses in the late summer, dried them and then wove them into long mats for the houses, sleeping mats, baskets and bags. Some bags were woven so finely, with patterns in colours from natural dyes, that they were treasured for years. You'd see a woman sitting outside her wetu in the afternoon light, her hands moving quickly, while a child watched and tried to copy. The mats for one house could take many weeks to make. When a family moved for the winter, they rolled up the mats and carried them along. In the new place, they simply laid them over a new frame of saplings. It's a little like taking your walls with you. I've always found that a clever idea. Would you have had the patience for such fine weaving?

HEAD: Wampanoag clothing was made mostly from soft deerskin.
BODY: After a hunt, nothing was wasted. The meat was eaten, the bones made into tools and the skin turned into clothing. Preparing a deerskin took days. It was stretched on a frame, scraped clean, soaked, worked and smoked until it was soft and supple. You'd see someone pulling and stretching the hide for hours to keep it from going stiff. Men wore breechcloths and leggings, and women wore skirts and wraps. In cold weather, everyone wore mantles of fur or deerskin over their shoulders. Moccasins kept their feet warm and quiet on the forest paths. Clothing could be decorated with paint, shells or porcupine quills. Some people also wore necklaces and belts of wampum beads. The English, in their wool and linen, often admired how practical these clothes were. Can you imagine the feel of soft deerskin on a cool autumn morning?

HEAD: Wampum beads were made from the shells of whelks and quahog clams, and they took great skill to make.
BODY: Along the shore, people collected the shells of whelks, which gave white beads, and quahog clams, which gave rare purple ones. Making each tiny bead was slow work. The shell was broken into small pieces, ground smooth into a little tube and then drilled through the middle with a stone drill. You'd see a craftsman bent over his work for hours, making just a handful of beads. The beads were strung into necklaces and woven into belts with patterns that recorded important agreements and events. Wampum was precious, and it was given as a gift to honour guests and to seal promises. Later, English and Dutch traders began using it almost like money, which changed its meaning a great deal. But to the Wampanoag, it was much more than money. I love that a tiny bead from a clam shell could hold a promise. Would you be able to keep such a small bead steady while drilling it?

HEAD: Wampanoag children learned by watching, helping and playing.
BODY: There were no schools like the English ones, but children were always learning. Girls followed their mothers and grandmothers into the fields and learned how to plant, weave and cook. Boys followed the men, learning to fish, track animals and make bows. You'd see small children playing games of skill with sticks, stones and balls in the clearings. They raced each other and practised throwing and shooting at targets. In the evenings, elders told stories about the world, the animals and the old heroes. Children learned that every hill and pond had a story. I think that's a wonderful way to grow up, surrounded by stories about the place you live. You'd know your home not just as a map, but as a long, living tale. Do you know any old stories about the place where you grew up?

HEAD: In Wampanoag stories, a kind giant called Maushop shaped the islands and the coast.
BODY: According to old Wampanoag stories, a gentle giant named Maushop once lived on this coast. He was so big that he could wade out into the sea to catch whales, and he shared them with the people. Some stories say that when he emptied the sand from his moccasins, it made the islands of Nantucket and Martha's Vineyard. Others say that the colourful clay cliffs at Aquinnah, on Martha's Vineyard, got their colours from his cooking fires. When the wind blew mist across the water, people said it was smoke from Maushop's pipe. You'd hear these stories by the fire on a long winter evening. Can you picture a giant striding through the waves, with mist drifting from his pipe? Those coloured cliffs are still there today, glowing red, white and yellow in the evening light. I find that a lovely kind of story to fall asleep to.

HEAD: When the first frosts came, families moved inland to their sheltered winter homes.
BODY: Late in the autumn, when the nights grew cold and the first frost silvered the grass, it was time to move. Families packed up their mats, baskets and tools, and walked inland along the forest paths to their winter places in sheltered valleys. There, they lived in long houses shared by several families, with fires burning down the middle. You'd smell wood smoke and dried fish, and hear the soft murmur of voices as people settled down for the long, dark evenings. In winter, the men hunted deer in the snow, and everyone mended, wove, carved and told stories. The stored corn and beans, dried fish and berries kept everyone fed until spring. It was a quieter time, a time for rest and for stories. Can you imagine falling asleep in a long house, with the fires glowing and the snow falling softly outside?
