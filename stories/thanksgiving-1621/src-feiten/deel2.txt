TOPIC: The first English harvest
BRIDGE: The long houses are quiet in the woods. Back in the little English village, though, an important week is beginning, because out in the fields, the corn is turning gold.
===
IMG: twenty-acres-corn-harvest | a wide field of ripe golden corn on a gentle slope above a harbour, English settlers picking ears into baskets, autumn sky
HEAD: That spring, the settlers had planted about twenty acres of corn, and it grew well.
BODY: In the spring of 1621, with Tisquantum's help, the colonists had planted around twenty acres of corn in the old Patuxet fields. Twenty acres is roughly the size of fifteen football pitches. Now, in the autumn, it stood tall and golden. You'd walk between the rows and hear the dry leaves whisper as you passed. Edward Winslow wrote home that their corn did prove well. That was the most important news in the whole colony. Corn meant bread, porridge and survival through the winter. The settlers had also sown about six acres of barley and peas from England. The barley was fairly good, Winslow said. But the peas weren't worth the gathering. They'd been planted too late, and the sun had scorched them. Can you imagine walking through that field, knowing that this harvest was the difference between a hungry winter and a safe one?
===
IMG: fish-in-corn-mounds-planting | a man placing small fish into earth mounds in a spring field before planting corn, a Wampanoag man showing him, soft light
HEAD: Tisquantum had shown them how to feed the corn with fish.
BODY: This is one of the best known stories about Plymouth, and it's true. In the spring, Tisquantum showed the settlers how to plant corn in small hills of earth, and how to put fish into the ground with the seeds. Each spring, the herring came up the brook in great numbers. The colonists caught them and buried two or three in each mound. As the fish broke down, they fed the soil, and the corn grew much better. You'd smell the fields for a while, I'm afraid. And the settlers had to guard them at night, because wolves and dogs tried to dig up the fish. Some historians think this method might have been learned from Europeans, while others think it was an old local practice. Either way, it worked. You might think a fish is an odd thing to plant. But the harvest said otherwise.
===
IMG: husking-corn-evening-barn | settlers and children sitting on a wooden floor husking ears of corn by lantern light, baskets of golden ears around them
HEAD: Once the corn was picked, every ear had to be husked and dried before it could be stored.
BODY: The harvest wasn't over when the ears came in from the field. Each one had to be husked, which means peeling away the dry green leaves around it. You'd sit in a circle with the others, an ear in your lap, pulling the husks back with a satisfying rustle. It was slow work, but it was easier together. People talked and told stories as their hands moved. Children were good at it too. Afterwards, the ears were hung up or spread out to dry, so they wouldn't go mouldy in the winter. Dried corn could be kept for many months. When it was needed, the kernels were rubbed off the cob and pounded into meal in a wooden mortar. I like to think of these evenings, with the rustling husks and the soft talk. Would you have stayed up late to help finish the pile?
===
IMG: corn-storage-pits-baskets | woven baskets of dried corn kernels being placed into a lined pit in the ground, bark and grass lining, autumn light
HEAD: Some of the corn was stored in pits in the ground, just as the Wampanoag stored theirs.
BODY: The Wampanoag had a clever way of keeping food through the winter. They dug pits in dry, sandy ground and lined them with grass and bark mats. Then they filled them with baskets of dried corn and beans, and covered them over with more mats and earth. The food stayed dry and safe for months. In fact, the settlers had found such stores on their very first explorations, back in the autumn before. They'd taken some of that seed corn, and later paid the owners back. Now they had their own corn to store. Some went into pits, some into baskets in the houses and common storehouse. You'd see them counting the baskets carefully. Every one mattered. I find it rather touching that the settlers' first winter supplies followed a Wampanoag pattern. Can you imagine opening a pit in midwinter and finding the corn still golden?
===
IMG: governor-sends-fowlers-marsh | four men with long fowling guns walking towards a marsh at dawn, flocks of ducks rising from the water, golden mist
HEAD: When the harvest was in, Governor Bradford sent four men out to hunt birds for a celebration.
BODY: Edward Winslow described it in a letter he wrote home that December. Our harvest being gotten in, he wrote, our governor sent four men on fowling, so that we might after a special manner rejoice together. In other words, they wanted to celebrate. Harvest festivals were an old custom in England, and the settlers had a great deal to be thankful for. So four men took their long fowling guns down to the marshes and ponds. You'd hear the ducks and geese calling before you saw them. Autumn was the time of great flocks, flying south along the coast. Winslow wrote that the four men killed so many birds in one day that, with a little help, they fed the whole company for almost a week. That's quite a day's hunting, isn't it? I imagine they came home very muddy and very proud.
===
IMG: ducks-geese-marsh-sunset | large flocks of wild ducks and geese settling on a wide salt marsh at sunset, reeds and still water, soft colours
HEAD: The birds they brought home were probably ducks and geese, and perhaps a few turkeys.
BODY: Winslow simply wrote fowl, which meant any kind of wild bird. In the autumn, the marshes and ponds around Plymouth were full of ducks and geese, resting on their long journey south. There were also wild turkeys in the woods. Bradford later wrote that besides waterfowl, there was great store of wild turkeys, of which they took many. So it's quite possible that turkeys were on the menu. But we can't be sure they were the main dish. You might think the turkey was always the star of the first Thanksgiving. The truth is, it may have been a duck or a goose instead. Wild turkeys were also leaner and more active than the big farm turkeys we know today. They could even fly short distances. Can you imagine a wild turkey flapping up into a tree to roost for the night?
===
IMG: venison-roasting-spit-outdoor | large pieces of venison roasting on wooden spits over a long outdoor fire, smoke drifting, people moving around it, autumn trees
HEAD: The meat we know for certain was eaten at the celebration was venison, a gift from the Wampanoag.
BODY: Here's a detail that's often forgotten. During the celebration, Winslow wrote, the Wampanoag men went out and killed five deer, which they brought back to the plantation. They gave them to the governor, to Captain Standish and to others. So the biggest share of meat at this famous meal was deer, a gift from the Wampanoag hunters. Five deer is a lot of meat. You'd see it roasting on long spits over outdoor fires, with the smoke drifting into the trees and the fat sizzling as it dripped into the flames. Some was probably stewed in pots, too. I love that this gift is at the heart of the story. It was more than just food. In Wampanoag culture, giving gifts was a way of showing friendship, respect and goodwill. Can you smell the wood smoke and the roasting meat?
===
TOPIC: Massasoit arrives
BRIDGE: The birds are plucked and the fires are being built. And then, from the edge of the woods, you'd see a long line of people walking towards the village.
===
IMG: massasoit-arrives-ninety-men | a long line of Wampanoag men walking out of an autumn forest towards a small timber village, their leader in front, calm afternoon light
HEAD: Massasoit came to the celebration with about ninety of his men.
BODY: Winslow wrote that many of the Indians came among them, and among the rest their greatest king, Massasoit, with some ninety men. Imagine the scene. The little English village had only about fifty people in it. Suddenly, ninety Wampanoag men walked out of the woods. Nobody knows exactly why they came. Some historians think they heard the gunfire from the hunting and came to see whether their allies were in trouble. Others think they were invited, or simply came as friends do at harvest time. Either way, they stayed. You'd have seen them arriving in their deerskin clothes, some with painted faces or feathers, carrying bows and their own food. For the English, it must have been a little overwhelming. For the Wampanoag, it was a visit to allies who were still very new, and very few. How would you have felt, watching them come?
===
IMG: massasoit-portrait-calm | a dignified Wampanoag leader in his forties seated on a mat, deerskin mantle and simple necklace, calm wise expression, warm firelight
HEAD: Massasoit was a careful leader who had chosen to make peace with the newcomers.
BODY: Ousamequin, known as Massasoit, was probably in his early forties in 1621. His people had lost many lives in the recent sickness, and he had powerful rivals to the west, especially the Narragansett. In the spring, he'd come to Plymouth and agreed to a treaty of peace and mutual help with the settlers. Each side promised not to harm the other, and to help if the other was attacked. You might think this was simple friendship. It was also a thoughtful choice. The English had guns, and they could be useful friends. Massasoit kept his word for the rest of his life, through some very difficult years. When he fell seriously ill a couple of years later, Edward Winslow travelled to his village and nursed him back to health with broth and care. I find that a very gentle part of this story, don't you?
===
IMG: two-peoples-language-gesture | an English man and a Wampanoag man talking with gestures under a tree, another man translating, calm autumn light
HEAD: Only a few people at the celebration could speak both languages.
BODY: Picture yourself at the edge of the crowd. You'd hear English, spoken with accents from different parts of England. You'd hear Wampanoag, a language of the Algonquian family, full of long, flowing words. But very few people could understand both. Tisquantum and Hobbamock translated, moving from group to group. Some of the English had learned a few words, and some of the Wampanoag men knew a little English. Much of the time, people must have used gestures, smiles and pointing. Can you imagine trying to share a meal and a joke with someone when you only know ten words of their language? It takes patience, and goodwill. And perhaps a little laughter at your own mistakes. Many place names in New England today, like Massachusetts, Nantucket and Mattapoisett, come from the Wampanoag language and its close relatives. You've probably heard some of them without knowing it.
===
IMG: three-days-celebration-village | a small village green with outdoor fires, tables made of boards, English settlers and Wampanoag guests mingling, autumn afternoon light
HEAD: The celebration lasted three days.
BODY: It wasn't a single dinner at a long table. Winslow wrote that they entertained and feasted Massasoit and his men for three days. You'd see fires burning all over the little village, with food cooking from morning until night. People ate when food was ready, sitting on the ground, on benches or on logs, wherever there was room. There may have been some boards set up as tables, but there weren't enough chairs for a hundred and fifty people, or even for fifty. Guests probably slept in the open or in simple shelters at night. By day, there was eating, talking, games and showing off skills. By night, there were fires under the stars. Doesn't three days of food and company sound rather nice? It must have been the busiest and noisiest the little village had ever been. And for the four women and the children, it must have been very tiring too.
===
IMG: muskets-drill-village-green | English men in breastplates firing matchlock muskets in a line on a village green, smoke drifting, Wampanoag men watching
HEAD: During the celebration, the English men showed off their muskets with marching and firing.
BODY: Winslow wrote that amongst other recreations, they exercised their arms. That means Myles Standish probably had the men march and fire their muskets in a little display. You'd hear the loud bangs echo off the hills and see clouds of grey smoke drifting across the village. It was partly entertainment, and partly a quiet message. It showed the guests that the English were well armed and could defend themselves. You might think that sounds a bit unfriendly at a party. But displays like this were very common between allies at the time, a little like a parade. The Wampanoag men may well have shown their own skills too, perhaps with bows or in races and games. We don't know exactly. But I like to imagine young men on both sides trying to impress each other, the way young people always have.
===
IMG: games-running-race-field | young men racing across an open autumn field, others watching and cheering from the edge, trees glowing orange
HEAD: There were probably games and sports, though nobody wrote down which ones.
BODY: Winslow only says amongst other recreations. So we have to imagine a little. The English loved games like running races, wrestling, jumping and a rough sort of football. Children played with balls, hoops and spinning tops. The Wampanoag also had many games, including ball games played on long fields and games of skill and chance with small pieces. Young men on both sides were strong and competitive. It's easy to picture a race across the field at the edge of the village, with people shouting and cheering on both sides. Perhaps someone tripped and everyone laughed. Perhaps a child won a race against a grown man. We'll never know for sure. Would you have joined in the race, or watched from the edge with a piece of roast duck in your hand?
===
IMG: children-sharing-food-fire | English and Wampanoag children sitting near a fire, curiously looking at each other, sharing a bowl, warm evening glow
HEAD: For the children of Plymouth, it must have been the most exciting three days of the year.
BODY: Think how it must have felt to be a child in the village that week. Suddenly there were dozens of visitors, with clothes, hair and ornaments you'd never seen before. There were fires everywhere, and food you'd never tasted. There were games, gunfire and stories in two languages. Children are curious, and I'm sure they watched everything with wide eyes. Some of them may have tried out a few Wampanoag words, or been shown how to string a bow. Some of the Wampanoag men may have brought their own sons, who'd have watched the English children just as curiously. And at night, lying in bed and listening to the voices around the fires outside, it must have been hard to fall asleep. Do you remember a day from childhood that felt that big and exciting?
===
TOPIC: What was on the table, and what wasn't
BRIDGE: The fires burn on into the night, and the smell of roasting meat drifts over the rooftops. So let's look closely at what you'd actually have eaten, and at a few things you wouldn't.
===
IMG: harvest-feast-table-food-1621 | a rough wooden table outdoors with roast fowl, venison, corn bread, boiled squash, shellfish and wooden bowls, autumn light
HEAD: The only description of the 1621 harvest feast is a few lines in a single letter.
BODY: Everything we know for certain comes from Edward Winslow's letter of December 1621, which was printed in London the next year. It's only a few sentences long. It mentions the fowl, the five deer, Massasoit and his ninety men, the three days and the recreations. That's all. Everything else is a careful guess. Historians look at what grew and lived around Plymouth in the autumn, what the settlers wrote about at other times, and what the Wampanoag ate. From that, they can say what was probably there. So when you imagine the table, think of it as a picture painted from a few small clues. I find it rather amazing that such a famous event rests on one short letter. Isn't it strange how a few lines, written by candlelight in a little timber house, can become part of a whole country's story?
===
IMG: corn-bread-pottage-wooden-bowls | wooden bowls of corn porridge, a round flat corn bread and boiled squash on a board table, a pewter spoon, soft light
HEAD: Corn was almost certainly on the table, but not as corn on the cob.
BODY: The corn of 1621 wasn't the sweet, juicy corn you'd buy at a market today. It was hard flint corn, best dried and ground. So the cooks would have pounded it into meal and made it into bread, or a thick porridge, or added it to stews. You'd tear off a piece of coarse, golden corn bread and dip it into the juices of the meat. Or you'd eat a bowl of warm samp, sweetened perhaps with a few dried berries. There may also have been boiled pumpkin or squash, which both peoples ate. The settlers may have stewed it with a little vinegar or spice, the English way. Simple food, really. But after the hungry months of the first winter, it must have tasted wonderful. Would you have liked a bowl of warm corn porridge on a cool autumn evening?
===
IMG: shellfish-eels-lobster-shore | baskets of clams, mussels and eels and a large lobster on a rocky shore beside a calm bay, golden evening light
HEAD: There may well have been seafood at the feast, such as eels, clams, mussels and lobster.
BODY: The bay at Plymouth was full of shellfish. At low tide, you could walk out on the mudflats and dig up clams with your hands. Mussels clung to the rocks in dark blue clusters. Eels lived in the muddy creeks, and lobsters crawled among the rocks offshore. The settlers wrote about all of these at other times, so they could easily have been cooked for the celebration. Funnily enough, the English weren't always very excited about lobster and mussels. In England, they were often thought of as plain, poor people's food. You might find that hard to believe today. But fresh from the cold bay, roasted on the fire or boiled in a pot, they must have been delicious. Can you picture someone wading back to shore with a basket of clams, the water cold around their ankles?
===
IMG: no-potatoes-garden-turnips-onions | a simple kitchen garden with onions, turnips and herbs in late autumn, a basket of vegetables on the ground, soft light
HEAD: There were almost certainly no potatoes at the first Thanksgiving.
BODY: Today, mashed potatoes are one of the most loved Thanksgiving dishes. But in 1621, potatoes hadn't yet become a normal crop in New England. They'd come from South America and were still a bit of a curiosity in England too. The colonists certainly weren't growing them yet. Sweet potatoes weren't grown here either. So instead, the vegetables on the table were probably things from the English gardens, like onions, turnips, carrots and herbs, along with the local squash, pumpkins and beans. Many people are surprised by this. You might think of the potatoes as essential. Yet the most famous Thanksgiving meal in history went happily without them. Isn't it funny how dishes we think of as ancient are sometimes quite new? I suspect the cooks of 1621 wouldn't have missed them at all.
===
IMG: pumpkins-squash-field-autumn | large orange and green pumpkins and squashes lying among their leaves in a field at sunset, a woven basket nearby
HEAD: There were pumpkins, but no pumpkin pie.
BODY: Pumpkins and squash grew well around Plymouth, and both the Wampanoag and the settlers ate them. But pumpkin pie as we know it needs a crust of butter and flour, plenty of sugar and an oven. In the autumn of 1621, the colony had very little sugar, no cows for butter and probably no proper oven yet. So there was no pie. Instead, the pumpkins were most likely cut into pieces and boiled or stewed, or roasted in the ashes of the fire. You might find that a little disappointing. I must admit, I'd miss the pie. Later, as the colony grew and settled, English cooks began to make pumpkin into all sorts of puddings and pies. Within a few generations, pumpkin pie had become a proud New England tradition. But on that first autumn, the pumpkins were simple and warm. Would you like a slice of roasted pumpkin, straight from the ashes?
===
IMG: cranberries-bog-autumn-red | a low wetland covered with small red berries on creeping plants in autumn, misty morning light, woods behind
HEAD: Cranberries grew wild nearby, but there was no cranberry sauce.
BODY: Bright red cranberries grow in the wet, sandy bogs of this part of New England. The Wampanoag gathered them in the autumn, ate them, used them for dye and added them to dishes. They called them sasumuneash. So cranberries may well have been part of the feast in some way. But cranberry sauce as we know it is made with lots of sugar, because the berries are very sour on their own. In 1621, there simply wasn't enough sugar for that. If you'd bitten into a raw cranberry, you'd have screwed up your whole face. Can you imagine it? Cranberry sauce came along much later, once sugar became cheaper. Today, cranberries are still grown commercially in Massachusetts, and at harvest time the bogs are flooded so the berries float to the surface. Whole lakes turn red. That's a sight I'd love to see one day.
===
IMG: spoons-knives-fingers-napkins | a wooden table with knives, wooden and pewter spoons, large linen napkins and shared dishes, no plates, soft light
HEAD: Nobody at the feast ate with a fork.
BODY: Forks were still very rare in England in 1621, and the settlers didn't use them. Instead, people ate with spoons, knives and their fingers. Food was often served in shared dishes in the middle of the table, and people took what they wanted. That's why napkins were large, more like small towels. You'd wipe your fingers on yours, or throw it over your shoulder to keep your clothes clean. Plates were often just thick slices of bread or wooden boards called trenchers, sometimes shared between two people. Drinks came in shared cups too. The Wampanoag guests ate in much the same way, with their hands and with spoons carved from wood or shell. Table manners were simply different. You might find it a bit messy. But I think it sounds rather friendly, everyone reaching into the same dish. Would you mind eating roast duck with your fingers?
===
IMG: beer-water-cups-wooden-table | wooden and clay cups on a rough table with a large jug, a window with soft afternoon light behind
HEAD: The settlers probably drank water and a little homemade beer.
BODY: In England, people often drank weak beer, because the water in towns could be unsafe. The Mayflower had carried barrels of beer across the ocean. By the autumn of 1621, that beer was long gone. Bradford wrote, with some pride, that the water at Plymouth was as good as any in the world, even if it wasn't quite as pleasant as beer. So most people drank water from the brook. The settlers may have brewed a little beer from their barley harvest, which had grown fairly well. There was no tea or coffee in New England yet. No milk either, because there were still no cows. You'd raise a wooden cup of cold, clear brook water to your lips at the end of a long, busy day. Would you have missed your tea or coffee, if you'd been there? I'm afraid I would have.

TOPIC: Quiet evenings after the feast
BRIDGE: The last of the venison is eaten, and the guests have walked home into the woods. Let's stay a little longer in the village, you and I, as the autumn evenings grow longer and calmer.
===
IMG: village-evening-after-feast-embers | a small timber village at dusk after a celebration, embers glowing in outdoor fire pits, a few people tidying, first stars
HEAD: After three busy days, the village fell quiet again.
BODY: When the last guests had gone, there was a great deal of tidying up to do. Fire pits had to be raked out, pots scrubbed, bones cleared away and borrowed benches carried back inside. You'd see the children yawning as they helped, still full of excitement and roast venison. Then, one by one, the families went back into their small houses and closed the doors. The smoke curled up from the chimneys into the evening air. The village was quiet, apart from a dog barking somewhere and the soft lapping of the harbour. I imagine everyone slept very well that night. They'd worked hard, eaten well and made new friends. Have you ever felt that kind of happy tiredness after a big family gathering, when the house is suddenly quiet again?
===
IMG: candle-making-tallow-evening | a woman dipping candles in a pot of melted fat by the hearth, a row of finished candles hanging on a rod, warm glow
HEAD: Light was precious, so the settlers made their own candles and often used firelight instead.
BODY: In the autumn evenings, the days grew short, and darkness came early. Light was precious. The settlers made candles by dipping wicks again and again into melted animal fat, called tallow, until they grew thick enough. It was smelly, sticky work. Candles were used carefully, never wasted. Often, families simply sat by the light of the fire. Some used thin sticks of pitch pine, which burned brightly and were sometimes called candlewood, a trick they learned in the new land. You'd sit in the warm glow, mending a shirt or carving a spoon, while the shadows danced on the walls. Children played quiet games or listened to stories. Can you imagine an evening lit only by a fire and a single candle? I find that rather peaceful, don't you?
===
IMG: spinning-wheel-evening-hearth | a woman spinning wool on a small wheel beside the fire, a girl holding a basket of wool, soft firelight on timber walls
HEAD: Women and girls spent many evenings spinning and sewing by the fire.
BODY: Every piece of clothing had to be made or mended at home, and there was no shop to buy cloth. So in the evenings, women and girls sat by the fire with their needles, patching knees and elbows, turning collars and sewing new shirts from the cloth they'd brought from England. When there was wool, they spun it into yarn on a spinning wheel, which made a soft whirring sound. You'd hear it humming gently in the background as people talked. Older girls taught younger ones to sew straight seams and neat little stitches. Some girls made samplers, small pieces of cloth stitched with letters and patterns, to practise their skills. One such sampler, made by a girl called Loara Standish, Myles Standish's daughter, still survives today. I love that little detail. Would you have had the patience for those tiny stitches?
===
IMG: reading-bible-family-firelight | a father reading aloud from a large book by the fire to his family, children sitting on the floor listening, warm light
HEAD: Many of the settlers could read, and books were treasured.
BODY: For people of their time, a large number of the Plymouth settlers could read, because they wanted to read the Bible themselves. William Brewster had brought a whole library of books across the ocean, more than three hundred of them, which was a great deal for the time. In the evenings, a father or mother might read aloud by the fire, while the children listened. You'd hear the slow, careful reading of familiar old words, and the crackle of the fire. Children learned their letters from a little board called a hornbook, with the alphabet on it, covered by a thin, clear sheet of horn to protect it. Some families also had almanacs, books of psalms and stories. Isn't it lovely to imagine a little library in a timber house at the edge of a vast forest?
===
IMG: night-watch-hilltop-stars | a man with a musket and a lantern standing watch on a hilltop at night, a sleeping village below, a sky full of stars
HEAD: Every night, men took turns to stand watch over the sleeping village.
BODY: Even in peaceful times, the settlers kept a watch through the night. Each evening, a few men took their muskets and lanterns up to their posts, and stayed awake while the others slept. You'd see a small light moving slowly up on the hill, and hear footsteps on the frosty ground. The watchmen listened for anything unusual, like a fire, a ship in the bay or a wolf near the animals. Mostly, nothing happened. They'd stand in the cold, looking up at a sky full of stars, far brighter than anything in an English town, and listen to the owls in the woods. I imagine it could feel very lonely, and very beautiful. At dawn, they'd walk down, stiff and tired, to warm themselves by the fire. Would you have liked a night on watch, under all those stars?
===
IMG: letters-home-england-ship-hope | a young woman reading a folded letter by a small window, a quill and paper on the table, soft grey morning light
HEAD: People in Plymouth thought often of the family and friends they'd left behind.
BODY: Most of the settlers had left parents, brothers, sisters or even children behind in England or Holland. Some hoped those loved ones would come over on a later ship. Letters could take many months to cross the ocean, and only when a ship happened to sail. So every letter was read again and again, until the paper was soft and worn. You'd see someone sitting by the window, reading a letter for the tenth time, smiling at a familiar turn of phrase. William Brewster's daughters Fear and Patience were still in Holland, and they came over a couple of years later. Can you imagine waiting so long for news, without any way to call or send a message? It makes me appreciate a simple letter in the post a little more. Who would you write to, if you were there?
===
IMG: first-snow-village-morning-quiet | the first light snow on thatched roofs of a small village on an early winter morning, smoke from chimneys, a quiet harbour
HEAD: One morning in late autumn, the settlers woke to find the first snow.
BODY: As November turned towards December, the nights grew colder. Then, one morning, you'd open the door and see the whole village white and silent. The thatched roofs had soft caps of snow. The fences and the garden rows were outlined in white. The harbour was grey and still, and the smoke from the chimneys rose straight up into the cold air. Children ran out to make footprints and throw snowballs. The adults looked at the woodpiles and the storehouse and thought about the long months ahead. This time, they were ready. There were houses, firewood, stored corn and friends in the woods. It was very different from the year before. I like to think of them standing in the doorway, breathing the cold, clean air, and feeling quietly grateful. Can you feel the hush of the snow?
===
IMG: corn-pounding-mortar-winter | a girl pounding dried corn in a tall wooden mortar with a heavy pestle inside a warm timber house, a basket of corn beside her
HEAD: All winter, corn had to be pounded into meal by hand, a little every day.
BODY: There was no mill in Plymouth yet. So every day, someone had to turn dried corn into meal for bread and porridge. The kernels were put into a hollowed log called a mortar and pounded with a heavy wooden pole called a pestle. Thump, thump, thump. You'd hear the sound from several houses each morning, like a slow drum. It was hard on the arms, and children often took turns. The Wampanoag pounded their corn the same way, and the English had learned from them. The coarse meal was then sifted and cooked. A few years later, the colony built a water mill on the brook, which made life much easier. Until then, it was thump, thump, thump, every single day. I find that rhythm rather soothing, don't you? Could you have pounded corn every morning before breakfast?
