TOPIC: The first English harvest
===
IMG: twenty-acres-corn-harvest | a wide field of ripe golden corn on a gentle slope above a harbour, English settlers picking ears into baskets, autumn sky
HEAD: In the spring of 1621, the Plymouth colonists planted about twenty acres of corn, and it grew well.
BODY: The colony's most important crop in its first year was Native corn. With guidance from Tisquantum, the settlers planted about twenty acres of it in the old fields of Patuxet. Edward Winslow wrote in December 1621 that their corn had done well. They also sowed about six acres of barley and peas brought from England. According to Winslow, the barley was reasonably good, but the peas were not worth gathering. They had been planted too late and were scorched by the summer sun. Corn mattered more than anything else. It provided bread and porridge, and it could be dried and stored through the winter. A good corn harvest meant the colony could face the cold months with some confidence. Historians estimate that twenty acres could feed the small population for a year, if nothing went wrong. Even so, the margin was narrow. The success of that first crop was a turning point for the struggling settlement.
===
IMG: fish-in-corn-mounds-planting | a man placing small fish into earth mounds in a spring field before planting corn, a Wampanoag man showing him, soft light
HEAD: Tisquantum showed the colonists how to fertilise their corn with fish.
BODY: One of the best-known details of early Plymouth concerns fish in the cornfields. According to the colonists' accounts, Tisquantum taught them to plant corn in small hills and to bury fish in the ground with the seeds. Each spring, herring ran up Town Brook in large numbers. The settlers caught them and placed two or three in each hill. As the fish decayed, they enriched the soil. Winslow reported that without this method, the corn would have yielded little. The fields had to be guarded for some days, because wolves and dogs tried to dig up the fish. Historians have debated where the practice came from. Some argue it was a long-standing Native method in this area. Others suggest Tisquantum may have learned it during his years in Europe or from other coastal peoples. Whatever its origin, the method worked. It helped to produce the harvest that made the autumn of 1621 a time of relief.
===
IMG: husking-corn-evening-barn | settlers and children sitting on a wooden floor husking ears of corn by lantern light, baskets of golden ears around them
HEAD: After the harvest, every ear of corn had to be husked and dried before it could be stored.
BODY: Picking the corn was only the first step. Each ear had to be husked, which meant peeling away the dry outer leaves. Husking was slow work and was often done in groups, with people sitting together around a pile of ears. The ears were then hung or spread out to dry thoroughly, so they would not rot during the winter. Dried corn could be stored for many months. When needed, the kernels were rubbed off the cob and pounded into meal in a wooden mortar. In later New England, husking became a social event, known as a husking bee, with food and games. The early colonists probably worked in a similar shared way, though no detailed account of it survives from 1621. Corn was a new crop for most English settlers. They had to learn not only how to grow it, but also how to dry, store and cook it. Each stage was essential to the colony's survival.
===
IMG: corn-storage-pits-baskets | woven baskets of dried corn kernels being placed into a lined pit in the ground, bark and grass lining, autumn light
HEAD: Some of the harvest was stored in lined pits in the ground, a Wampanoag method.
BODY: The Wampanoag had long stored food in pits dug into dry, sandy ground. They lined the pits with grass and bark mats, filled them with baskets of dried corn and beans, and covered them with more mats and earth. Food stored this way stayed dry for months. The English had encountered such stores during their first explorations of Cape Cod in November 1620. They took some of the seed corn they found, and Bradford recorded that they later paid the owners back. By the autumn of 1621, the colonists had their own corn to store. Some went into pits, some into baskets in their houses and some into a common storehouse. Archaeologists have excavated storage pits at Native sites across southern New England, confirming the descriptions in early accounts. The method shows how much the English depended on local knowledge. Their first winter supplies followed a pattern developed by the Wampanoag over many generations.
===
IMG: governor-sends-fowlers-marsh | four men with long fowling guns walking towards a marsh at dawn, flocks of ducks rising from the water, golden mist
HEAD: When the harvest was gathered, Governor Bradford sent four men to hunt wild birds for a celebration.
BODY: Edward Winslow described the event in a letter dated the eleventh of December 1621. He wrote that after the harvest was gathered, the governor sent four men out fowling, so that they might rejoice together in a special manner after the fruits of their labour. Harvest celebrations were a familiar custom in England, and the colonists had good reason to be thankful. The four men went to the marshes and ponds with their fowling guns. Autumn was the season when large flocks of ducks and geese passed along the coast. Winslow wrote that in one day, the four men killed enough birds to feed the whole company for almost a week, with a little help besides. This short passage is the main source for the beginning of the celebration. Historians note that it says nothing about the exact date. The event probably took place sometime between late September and early November 1621.
===
IMG: ducks-geese-marsh-sunset | large flocks of wild ducks and geese settling on a wide salt marsh at sunset, reeds and still water, soft colours
HEAD: The birds brought back by the hunters were probably mostly ducks and geese, and possibly wild turkeys.
BODY: Winslow used the word fowl, which in his time meant wild birds of many kinds. In autumn, the marshes and ponds around Plymouth were full of migrating ducks and geese. Wild turkeys also lived in the surrounding woods. William Bradford later wrote that during that first autumn there was a great store of wild turkeys, of which they took many. So turkey may well have been eaten during the celebration. Historians cannot be certain that it was the main dish, however. Waterfowl were more plentiful and easier to hunt in large numbers. Wild turkeys were also different from modern farm turkeys. They were leaner and faster, and they could fly short distances to roost in trees. The idea of turkey as the centrepiece of Thanksgiving developed much later, in the nineteenth century. In 1621, the table was more likely filled with a mixture of birds from the marshes and the woods.
===
IMG: venison-roasting-spit-outdoor | large pieces of venison roasting on wooden spits over a long outdoor fire, smoke drifting, people moving around it, autumn trees
HEAD: The one meat certainly eaten at the 1621 celebration was venison from five deer given by Wampanoag hunters.
BODY: Winslow's letter contains one specific detail about the meat. During the celebration, he wrote, the Wampanoag men went out and killed five deer. They brought them to the plantation and gave them to the governor, to Captain Standish and to others. Five deer would have provided a large amount of meat. It was probably roasted over open fires and stewed in pots. This detail is often overlooked in popular images of the event. It shows that the largest single contribution of meat came from the Wampanoag guests. In Wampanoag culture, gifts were an important way of expressing friendship, respect and obligation. Historians emphasise that the celebration was not simply English hosts feeding Native guests. Both groups contributed, and the Wampanoag outnumbered the English. The gift of venison is one of the few firm facts about the menu. It places Wampanoag hunters at the very centre of what later became a national story.

TOPIC: Massasoit arrives
===
IMG: massasoit-arrives-ninety-men | a long line of Wampanoag men walking out of an autumn forest towards a small timber village, their leader in front, calm afternoon light
HEAD: Massasoit came to the celebration with about ninety of his men.
BODY: Winslow wrote that many of the Native people came among them, including their greatest king, Massasoit, with some ninety men. The English settlement had only about fifty people at the time. So the Wampanoag guests outnumbered their hosts almost two to one. The reason for their arrival is not recorded. Some historians suggest that the Wampanoag heard gunfire from the English hunting or from a musket display and came to see whether their allies were in danger. Others think they had been invited or came as part of normal diplomatic visits. Whatever the reason, they stayed, and they were entertained. The arrival of so many visitors must have changed the scale of the celebration entirely. Food, space and cooking all had to stretch to more than three times the colony's normal population. The meeting reflected the alliance agreed earlier that year, and the careful diplomacy that both sides were practising.
===
IMG: massasoit-portrait-calm | a dignified Wampanoag leader in his forties seated on a mat, deerskin mantle and simple necklace, calm wise expression, warm firelight
HEAD: Massasoit had made a treaty of peace and mutual help with the Plymouth colonists in the spring of 1621.
BODY: Ousamequin, known as Massasoit, was probably in his early forties in 1621. His people had suffered heavy losses in the epidemic of the previous years. He also faced powerful rivals, especially the Narragansett to the west. In March 1621, he met the Plymouth leaders and agreed a treaty. Each side promised not to harm the other and to come to the other's aid if attacked. The text of the agreement was recorded by the English. Historians view the treaty as a practical decision for both sides. The English gained a powerful ally, and Massasoit gained support against his rivals. He kept the agreement for the rest of his life, through several difficult periods. When he fell seriously ill in 1623, Edward Winslow travelled to his village and helped nurse him back to health. Massasoit died around 1661. Within a few years of his death, relations between the English and the Wampanoag broke down.
===
IMG: two-peoples-language-gesture | an English man and a Wampanoag man talking with gestures under a tree, another man translating, calm autumn light
HEAD: Only a few people at the celebration could speak both English and the Wampanoag language.
BODY: The English colonists spoke English, with accents from different regions of England. The Wampanoag spoke a language of the Algonquian family. Very few people understood both. Tisquantum and Hobbamock acted as interpreters. Some colonists had learned a few Wampanoag words, and some Wampanoag men knew a little English from earlier contact with traders and fishermen. Much communication must have relied on gestures and simple words. Edward Winslow and Roger Williams later recorded Native words and phrases, which shows how much effort went into learning. Many place names in New England today come from Algonquian languages, including Massachusetts, Nantucket and Mattapoisett. These names are a lasting sign of the original languages of the region. Even so, the language gap meant that misunderstandings were always possible. The celebration of 1621 took place across a real barrier of words, managed through interpreters, goodwill and careful diplomacy on both sides.
===
IMG: three-days-celebration-village | a small village green with outdoor fires, tables made of boards, English settlers and Wampanoag guests mingling, autumn afternoon light
HEAD: According to Winslow, the celebration lasted three days.
BODY: Winslow wrote that the colonists entertained and feasted Massasoit and his men for three days. This was not a single sit-down dinner at one long table. Food was cooked over many fires and eaten as it was ready. People probably sat on benches, logs or the ground, since the colony could not have had enough tables or chairs for so many. At night, the guests likely slept in temporary shelters or in the open. Between meals there was talk, recreation and displays of skill. Harvest celebrations in England could also last several days, so the length was not unusual. The scale, however, was remarkable for such a small settlement. Historians point out how much the four surviving women and the girls of the colony must have cooked during those days. The event was later remembered as the first Thanksgiving. At the time, it was an extended harvest feast shared between allies.
===
IMG: muskets-drill-village-green | English men in breastplates firing matchlock muskets in a line on a village green, smoke drifting, Wampanoag men watching
HEAD: During the celebration, the English men performed a display with their muskets.
BODY: Winslow wrote that among other recreations, they exercised their arms. This most likely means that Myles Standish led the men in marching and firing their muskets. Such displays were common in England at festivals and on training days. They were partly entertainment and partly a demonstration of strength. For the English, it showed their guests that the colony could defend itself. Military displays between allies were also a normal part of diplomacy at the time. Some historians suggest that the sound of the guns may have brought Massasoit and his men to the settlement in the first place. The Wampanoag men may also have shown their own skills, though no record of this survives. The muskets used were heavy, slow to load and loud. Even so, they impressed many Native observers in the early years. The display was a reminder that friendship in 1621 existed alongside caution and a careful balance of power.
===
IMG: games-running-race-field | young men racing across an open autumn field, others watching and cheering from the edge, trees glowing orange
HEAD: Games and sports were probably part of the 1621 celebration, though no one recorded which ones.
BODY: Winslow mentioned only other recreations, without details. English harvest festivals of the period often included running races, wrestling, jumping and early forms of football. Children played with balls, hoops and spinning tops. The Wampanoag also had many games, including ball games played on long fields and games of chance played with marked pieces. Seventeenth-century writers such as Roger Williams described some of these Native games. It is likely that some sort of shared contest took place, since young men on both sides were strong and competitive. Historians are careful not to invent details that the sources do not support. Even so, the general picture is reasonable. A three-day gathering of about a hundred and fifty people would hardly have passed without games of some kind. The lack of detail is typical of the period. Winslow was writing a short letter, not a full description of the festivities.
===
IMG: children-sharing-food-fire | English and Wampanoag children sitting near a fire, curiously looking at each other, sharing a bowl, warm evening glow
HEAD: The celebration would have been a rare and memorable event for the children of Plymouth.
BODY: For the children of the colony, the arrival of about ninety visitors was extraordinary. The Wampanoag guests wore clothing, ornaments and hairstyles unlike anything the English children had known. There were fires and food throughout the day, games, gunfire and conversation in two languages. Historians of childhood note that children in the period often moved freely among adults at such gatherings. Some English children may have learned a few Wampanoag words. Wampanoag men may also have brought sons, though the sources do not say so. Several of the Plymouth children lived into old age and some of their recollections were recorded much later, though not in detail about this event. Even so, the scale of the gathering makes it likely to have been remembered. For a small community that had suffered greatly the winter before, the celebration marked a time of plenty. That contrast would have been felt by young and old alike.

TOPIC: What was on the table, and what wasn't
===
IMG: harvest-feast-table-food-1621 | a rough wooden table outdoors with roast fowl, venison, corn bread, boiled squash, shellfish and wooden bowls, autumn light
HEAD: The only eyewitness description of the 1621 harvest feast is a short passage in one letter.
BODY: Everything known for certain about the celebration comes from Edward Winslow's letter of December 1621. The letter was published in London in 1622. The relevant passage is only a few sentences long. It mentions the fowling, the five deer, Massasoit and his ninety men, the three days of feasting and the recreations. William Bradford's history adds general information about the food available that autumn, but it does not describe the celebration itself. Everything else is careful reconstruction. Historians and food historians study what grew and lived around Plymouth in autumn, what the colonists ate at other times and what the Wampanoag ate. From this, they estimate what was probably served. Even so, many popular details have no basis in the sources. The modern picture of the first Thanksgiving was built up mainly in the nineteenth century. The actual record is remarkably brief, and much of the event remains unknown.
===
IMG: corn-bread-pottage-wooden-bowls | wooden bowls of corn porridge, a round flat corn bread and boiled squash on a board table, a pewter spoon, soft light
HEAD: Corn was almost certainly eaten at the feast, but in the form of bread or porridge rather than on the cob.
BODY: The corn grown at Plymouth was a hard flint variety, not the sweet corn common today. It was best dried and ground into meal. Cooks would have made it into flat bread, a thick porridge or an ingredient in stews. The English called the porridge samp, after the Wampanoag nasaump. Boiled pumpkin or squash was probably eaten as well, since both peoples grew and cooked it. English cooks of the period sometimes stewed pumpkin with vinegar, butter or spices, though butter was not yet available at Plymouth. Food historians base these suggestions on recipes from English cookbooks and on accounts of Native cooking. The meal would have seemed plain by modern standards. Even so, after the hunger of the first winter, a plentiful supply of corn bread and porridge was a significant comfort. Corn had become the foundation of the colony's diet, as it had long been for the Wampanoag.
===
IMG: shellfish-eels-lobster-shore | baskets of clams, mussels and eels and a large lobster on a rocky shore beside a calm bay, golden evening light
HEAD: Seafood such as eels, clams, mussels and lobster may well have been eaten during the celebration.
BODY: The waters around Plymouth were rich in shellfish. At low tide, clams could be dug from the mudflats. Mussels grew on rocks, eels lived in the muddy creeks and lobsters were found among the rocks offshore. The colonists wrote about all of these at various times in their first years. It is therefore likely that some were cooked during the harvest celebration, though Winslow did not mention them. Interestingly, many English people of the time regarded lobster and mussels as humble food. Early accounts describe lobsters so plentiful that they were sometimes considered food for the poor. Attitudes changed greatly in later centuries. Food historians list seafood among the probable dishes because of its abundance and seasonality. Even so, they stress that it remains a reasonable guess rather than a recorded fact. The shore was a constant source of food for both the Wampanoag and the English throughout the year.
===
IMG: no-potatoes-garden-turnips-onions | a simple kitchen garden with onions, turnips and herbs in late autumn, a basket of vegetables on the ground, soft light
HEAD: There were almost certainly no potatoes at the 1621 harvest feast.
BODY: Potatoes originated in South America and were not yet grown in New England in 1621. In England, they were still something of a novelty. Sweet potatoes were not grown in the region either. The vegetables available at Plymouth would have included onions, turnips, carrots and herbs from the English gardens, along with the corn, beans, squash and pumpkins grown by the Wampanoag and the colonists. Mashed potatoes, now a standard Thanksgiving dish, became associated with the holiday much later. Food historians often point to this as an example of how modern Thanksgiving menus differ from the original event. White potatoes became common in New England only in the eighteenth century, after they were brought by Scots-Irish settlers. The 1621 meal therefore had a different character. It relied on local crops and a few European garden vegetables. Many of the dishes now considered traditional have no connection to the first harvest at Plymouth.
===
IMG: pumpkins-squash-field-autumn | large orange and green pumpkins and squashes lying among their leaves in a field at sunset, a woven basket nearby
HEAD: Pumpkins were grown at Plymouth, but there was no pumpkin pie in 1621.
BODY: Pumpkins and other squash grew well around Plymouth, and both the Wampanoag and the colonists ate them. Pumpkin pie, however, requires a pastry crust made with butter and flour, sugar and an oven. In the autumn of 1621, the colony had very little sugar, no cows to provide butter and probably no proper bake oven. Pumpkin was therefore most likely boiled, stewed or roasted in the embers of a fire. Within a few decades, English settlers in New England began making pumpkin into puddings and pies. Recipes for pumpkin pie appear in English cookbooks from the later seventeenth century. By the time of the American Revolution, pumpkin pie was well established in New England cooking. The pie later became one of the symbols of the holiday. Even so, it belongs to a later chapter of the story. In 1621, pumpkins were simply one of the season's useful vegetables, cooked in plain and practical ways.
===
IMG: cranberries-bog-autumn-red | a low wetland covered with small red berries on creeping plants in autumn, misty morning light, woods behind
HEAD: Cranberries grew wild near Plymouth, but cranberry sauce was not part of the 1621 feast.
BODY: Cranberries grow in the wet, sandy bogs of southeastern Massachusetts. The Wampanoag gathered them in autumn, ate them and used them in dishes and as a dye. Their name for the berries was sasumuneash. Cranberries may have been eaten during the celebration in some form. Cranberry sauce, however, requires a large amount of sugar, because the berries are very sour. In 1621, the colony had almost no sugar. Recipes for cranberry sauce appear in English and American sources later in the seventeenth century and beyond. Commercial cultivation of cranberries began in Massachusetts in the early nineteenth century. Today, the state remains one of the major cranberry producers in the United States. At harvest time, growers flood the bogs so that the ripe berries float to the surface. Fields of red berries on the water have become a familiar autumn sight. Yet the sauce served at modern Thanksgiving tables was unknown in 1621.
===
IMG: spoons-knives-fingers-napkins | a wooden table with knives, wooden and pewter spoons, large linen napkins and shared dishes, no plates, soft light
HEAD: No forks were used at the 1621 feast; people ate with knives, spoons and their fingers.
BODY: Forks were rare in England in 1621 and were not used by the Plymouth colonists. People ate with spoons, knives and fingers. Food was often served in shared dishes, and people took portions from them. Napkins were large, more like small towels, and were used to wipe hands and protect clothing. Plates were often wooden boards called trenchers, sometimes shared between two people. Drinks were shared from common cups. The Wampanoag guests also ate with their hands and with spoons carved from wood or shell. Table manners of the period followed their own careful rules, even without forks. Etiquette books from the time advise people to keep their fingers clean and to take food politely from the shared dish. The fork came into general use in England later in the seventeenth century. Museum reconstructions of the 1621 meal now show these older customs, which give a very different picture from later paintings.
===
IMG: beer-water-cups-wooden-table | wooden and clay cups on a rough table with a large jug, a window with soft afternoon light behind
HEAD: The colonists probably drank mostly water during the celebration, and perhaps a little home-brewed beer.
BODY: In England, people commonly drank weak beer, partly because water in towns could be unsafe. The Mayflower had carried beer across the Atlantic, but by the autumn of 1621 those supplies were long gone. Bradford noted that the water at Plymouth was very good, though he admitted that it was not as pleasant as beer. Most people at the celebration therefore probably drank water from Town Brook. The colonists may have brewed a little beer from their barley harvest, which Winslow described as reasonably good. There was no tea or coffee in New England at the time. Neither drink had yet become common even in England. There was no milk either, since the colony had no cows until 1624. Food historians include water and possibly beer in their reconstructions of the meal. The drinks of 1621 were simple. Even so, a reliable supply of clean water was an important advantage for the small settlement.

TOPIC: Quiet evenings after the feast
===
IMG: village-evening-after-feast-embers | a small timber village at dusk after a celebration, embers glowing in outdoor fire pits, a few people tidying, first stars
HEAD: After the three days of celebration, the village returned to its normal quiet routine.
BODY: When the guests had gone, there was work to be done. Fire pits had to be cleaned, pots scrubbed and borrowed furniture returned. Then the colony resumed its ordinary pattern of work. Winslow's letter does not describe the days after the feast. However, other records show what life in Plymouth was like in the late autumn. The colonists continued to fish, hunt, cut timber and prepare for winter. Relations with the Wampanoag continued through regular visits, trade and diplomacy. Historians note that the celebration was not seen at the time as an especially important event. Winslow mentioned it briefly, as one item in a longer letter about the colony's progress. Its later fame came from nineteenth-century writers who saw it as a symbol. For the colonists in 1621, it was a welcome pause after a difficult year. Then the short days of November arrived, and everyday life went on.
===
IMG: candle-making-tallow-evening | a woman dipping candles in a pot of melted fat by the hearth, a row of finished candles hanging on a rod, warm glow
HEAD: Light was precious in early Plymouth, and candles were made at home from animal fat.
BODY: As the autumn days shortened, artificial light became important. The colonists made candles by dipping wicks repeatedly into melted animal fat called tallow, until the candles were thick enough to use. The process was smelly and messy, and the candles burned with a smoky flame. Candles were used sparingly. Families often relied on the light of the fire instead. Early New England settlers also burned slivers of resinous pitch pine, sometimes called candlewood, which gave a bright light. Several seventeenth-century writers mention this practice. Evenings were spent mending, carving, spinning or talking by the hearth. Historians of daily life emphasise how much the lack of light shaped routines. People generally went to bed soon after dark and rose with the sun. Even so, winter evenings were long, and the fire was the centre of family life. The careful use of light was part of the careful use of all resources in the colony.
===
IMG: spinning-wheel-evening-hearth | a woman spinning wool on a small wheel beside the fire, a girl holding a basket of wool, soft firelight on timber walls
HEAD: Women and girls in early Plymouth spent many evenings spinning, sewing and mending clothes.
BODY: Every piece of clothing in the colony had to be made or repaired at home. New cloth had to come from England, and it was expensive and slow to arrive. So women and girls spent evenings patching knees and elbows, turning worn collars and sewing new garments. When wool was available, it was spun into yarn on a spinning wheel. Older girls taught younger ones to sew neat seams. Some girls made samplers, pieces of cloth stitched with letters and patterns to practise their skills. A sampler made by Loara Standish, daughter of Myles Standish, survives from the mid-seventeenth century. It is considered one of the oldest surviving American samplers and is kept by the Pilgrim Hall Museum in Plymouth. Needlework was an essential household skill. Even so, it also allowed for some creativity and pride. The careful stitching of a young girl in Plymouth is one of the few personal objects to survive from the early colony.
===
IMG: reading-bible-family-firelight | a father reading aloud from a large book by the fire to his family, children sitting on the floor listening, warm light
HEAD: Many Plymouth colonists could read, and books were highly valued in the colony.
BODY: Literacy was unusually high among the Plymouth separatists, because they believed it was important to read the Bible for themselves. William Brewster brought a large library across the Atlantic. An inventory made after his death in 1644 listed nearly four hundred books, a very large collection for the time. Books included Bibles, psalm books, works of theology, history and practical guides. In the evenings, a family member might read aloud by the fire. Children learned their letters from hornbooks, small wooden boards printed with the alphabet and covered by a thin, transparent sheet of horn. Historians use book inventories to understand what people in the colony read and valued. Even so, not everyone could read, especially among the servants and some of the passengers known as strangers. Reading, writing and religious study were nevertheless central to the colony's identity. The surviving inventories suggest a community that treasured its books.
===
IMG: night-watch-hilltop-stars | a man with a musket and a lantern standing watch on a hilltop at night, a sleeping village below, a sky full of stars
HEAD: Every night, Plymouth's men took turns standing watch over the sleeping village.
BODY: Even in peaceful times, the colonists kept a night watch. Men took turns standing guard with muskets and lanterns, while the others slept. Watchmen looked out for fire, for ships in the bay and for wild animals near the livestock. Fire was a constant danger in a village of wooden, thatched houses. In fact, the common house had caught fire in January 1621, as Bradford recorded. Night watches were a normal part of town life in England, so the practice was familiar. Colony records from later years include rules about the watch and penalties for neglecting it. Most nights, nothing happened. Watchmen stood in the cold, listened to owls and wind and waited for dawn. Even so, the watch was taken seriously. In a small and isolated settlement, early warning of a fire or an unexpected arrival could make a great difference. Keeping watch was a shared duty that every able man was expected to perform.
===
IMG: letters-home-england-ship-hope | a young woman reading a folded letter by a small window, a quill and paper on the table, soft grey morning light
HEAD: Many Plymouth colonists had left close family members behind in England or the Netherlands.
BODY: Most of the settlers had left parents, brothers, sisters and sometimes children behind. Some hoped their relatives would join them on later ships. Letters could take months to cross the Atlantic, and they travelled only when a ship happened to be sailing. Each letter was therefore precious. Several later ships brought families together. William Brewster's daughters Fear and Patience, for example, arrived in 1623 aboard the ship Anne. Correspondence between Plymouth and Leiden survives in part, including letters from the minister John Robinson to the colonists. Historians use these letters to understand the colonists' hopes, worries and religious views. They show how strongly the settlers still felt connected to their community in Europe. Even so, the distance was enormous. A question asked in a letter might not receive an answer for a year. Patience, in every sense, was essential to life in the early colony.
===
IMG: first-snow-village-morning-quiet | the first light snow on thatched roofs of a small village on an early winter morning, smoke from chimneys, a quiet harbour
HEAD: By the end of 1621, the Plymouth colonists faced their second winter better prepared than their first.
BODY: As November gave way to December, the weather turned colder, and snow began to fall. The village now had houses, firewood, stored corn and established relations with its Wampanoag neighbours. This was very different from the previous winter. In 1620 and 1621, the colonists had arrived with no shelter, and about half of them had died before spring. The second winter was still difficult, especially after the arrival of new settlers with few supplies. Yet the colony was no longer facing the cold without houses or knowledge. Bradford's history reflects this change in tone. Historians often see the autumn of 1621 as the moment when Plymouth became a lasting settlement rather than a desperate camp. The harvest, the treaty and the houses were all part of that shift. The first snow of that winter fell on a village that had learned a great deal in a single year.
===
IMG: corn-pounding-mortar-winter | a girl pounding dried corn in a tall wooden mortar with a heavy pestle inside a warm timber house, a basket of corn beside her
HEAD: Throughout the winter, corn had to be pounded into meal by hand every day.
BODY: There was no mill in Plymouth in its first years. Dried corn was turned into meal by pounding it in a mortar, often a hollowed log, with a heavy wooden pestle. This was tiring work that had to be done regularly, because meal did not keep as well as whole kernels. Children often took turns at it. The Wampanoag processed their corn in the same way, and the English learned the method from them. The coarse meal was then sifted and cooked as bread or porridge. Within a few years, the colony built a water-powered mill on Town Brook. Records indicate that a gristmill was operating there by the 1630s. A reconstructed mill stands near the site today. Even so, for the first winters, the steady thump of mortar and pestle was part of daily life. Grinding corn by hand connected English households to a technique used in the region for many centuries.
