Close your eyes if you'd like.

[pause]

Tonight, we are going to sea.

[pause]

We are going back more than a hundred years, to the spring of 1912, and to the most famous ship that ever sailed.

[long-pause]

She was called Titanic.

[pause]

We know how her story ends.

[pause]

But tonight, we are not here to be frightened.

[pause]

We are here to walk slowly along her decks, to meet the people who travelled on her, and to listen quietly to the story of her only voyage.

[long-pause]

You don't need to remember any of this.

[pause]

You don't need to follow every name, or every date.

[pause]

Just let the story move past you, the way a great ship moves past a quiet harbour at night, with all its lights glowing.

[pause]

If you fall asleep before the end, that's alright.

[pause]

The sea has kept this story for a very long time.

[pause]

It can keep it a little longer.

[long-pause]

{img:belfast-shipyard-morning}
Our story begins not at sea, but in a city by the water.

[pause]

It begins in Belfast, in the north of Ireland.

[pause]

In those years, Belfast was a busy, noisy, hard-working city, full of mills and factories and smoking chimneys.

[pause]

It made linen, and rope, and tobacco.

[pause]

And most of all, it made ships.

[long-pause]

On the edge of the city, where the River Lagan flows out into a long, calm bay called Belfast Lough, there was a shipyard.

[pause]

It was called Harland and Wolff.

[pause]

It stood on a flat piece of land known as Queen's Island, and it was one of the largest shipyards in the world.

[pause]

Every morning, long before sunrise, thousands of men walked through its gates.

[pause]

They wore flat caps and heavy boots, and many carried a small tin box with their lunch inside.

[pause]

The sound of their footsteps filled the dark streets like the sound of a river.

[long-pause]

At the time of our story, around fifteen thousand people worked at the yard.

[pause]

There were riveters and platers, carpenters and joiners, painters, electricians and boilermakers.

[pause]

There were boys of fourteen, just starting out, who heated rivets in small glowing fires, and tossed them up to the men above.

[pause]

And there were old men who had built ships all their lives, and who could tell a good piece of steel just by the sound it made.

[long-pause]

The yard had its own rhythm.

[pause]

In the morning, a loud horn sounded across the rooftops, calling everyone to work.

[pause]

At midday, it sounded again, and the men sat down on piles of timber or in the shadow of the ships, to eat their bread and drink cold tea.

[pause]

And in the evening, when the horn sounded for the last time, the great river of men flowed out through the gates again, and back into the narrow streets of red brick houses, where their families were waiting for them with supper.

[pause]

On Sundays, the yard was silent.

[pause]

The ships stood still in the grey light, waiting.

[pause]

And the only sound was the wind, and the cry of the seagulls over the water.

[long-pause]

{img:white-star-offices-plans}
In 1907, so the story goes, two men sat down to dinner together in London.

[pause]

One was Lord Pirrie, the head of Harland and Wolff.

[pause]

The other was Bruce Ismay, the head of a shipping company called the White Star Line.

[pause]

White Star carried passengers across the Atlantic Ocean, between Britain and America.

[pause]

Its great rival was another company, Cunard, which had just built two very fast ships.

[pause]

Ismay did not want to build faster ships.

[pause]

He wanted to build bigger ones.

[pause]

Ships so large and so comfortable that people would choose them over any other.

[long-pause]

And so, over dinner, a plan was made.

[pause]

Harland and Wolff would build three enormous sister ships for the White Star Line.

[pause]

The first would be called Olympic.

[pause]

The second would be called Titanic.

[pause]

And the third, which came later, would be called Britannic.

[long-pause]

The man who helped to design them was called Thomas Andrews.

[pause]

He was Lord Pirrie's nephew, and he had started at the yard as a young apprentice, at the age of sixteen.

[pause]

He had worked in every part of it, from the joiners' shop to the drawing office.

[pause]

The workers liked him.

[pause]

He knew many of them by name, and he often walked around the yard in an old bowler hat, with plans rolled up under his arm, and his pockets full of notes.

[pause]

People said that he knew every rivet in his ships.

[long-pause]

{img:arrol-gantry-hull}
The ships were so large that no slipway in the world was big enough to build them.

[pause]

So the yard made new ones.

[pause]

Above them, it built a huge steel frame called the Arrol Gantry.

[pause]

It stood more than sixty metres high, and from far across the bay it looked like a giant cage of iron lace against the sky.

[pause]

Inside it, side by side, the two sisters began to grow.

[long-pause]

The keel of Titanic was laid in the spring of 1909.

[pause]

A keel is the long spine along the bottom of a ship, and everything else is built up from it, like the ribs of a great whale.

[pause]

Slowly, month after month, the steel frames rose up from the keel.

[pause]

Then came the steel plates, fixed to the frames with rivets.

[pause]

There were around three million rivets in the ship.

[pause]

Many of them were hammered in by hand, by teams of men working high up on wooden scaffolding, in wind and in rain.

[long-pause]

The work was hard, and the days were long.

[pause]

But there was pride in it, too.

[pause]

The men knew they were building something that had never been built before.

[pause]

When Titanic was finished, she would be almost two hundred and seventy metres long.

[pause]

If you stood her on her end, she would be taller than any building in the world at that time.

[long-pause]

Her older sister, Olympic, was always a few months ahead.

[pause]

She was launched first, in the autumn of 1910, and then towed away to be finished.

[pause]

For a while, Titanic stood alone under the great gantry, growing taller and more complete, week by week.

[pause]

From the streets of Belfast, people could see her dark hull rising above the rooftops.

[pause]

Children would stop on their way home from school, and point at her, and try to count the rows of portholes along her sides.

[pause]

Families would walk down to the water on a Sunday afternoon, just to look at her.

[pause]

Everyone in the city knew someone who worked on her.

[pause]

A father, an uncle, a brother, a neighbour.

[pause]

In a way, she belonged to all of them.

[long-pause]

{img:titanic-launch-crowd}
On the last day of May, in 1911, the hull of Titanic was ready to be launched.

[pause]

The engines and the rooms were not yet inside her.

[pause]

She was only an empty shell of steel, painted in a pale grey, so that she would look good in the photographs.

[pause]

But she was already enormous.

[long-pause]

Around a hundred thousand people came to watch.

[pause]

They stood along the riverbanks and on the rooftops, and they sat in small boats out on the water.

[pause]

Some had paid for a ticket to stand in a special place near the ship.

[pause]

It was a warm and sunny day.

[long-pause]

To help her slide into the water, the slipway had been covered with more than twenty tons of tallow and soap.

[pause]

A little after midday, a rocket went up into the sky as a warning.

[pause]

Then another.

[pause]

The last supports were knocked away.

[pause]

And slowly, very slowly, the great hull began to move.

[long-pause]

It took just over one minute for her to slide down into the river.

[pause]

The crowd cheered, and the ships in the harbour sounded their horns.

[pause]

Then the water settled again, and Titanic floated there, quiet and grey, in the calm water of the Lagan.

[pause]

Her life on the sea had begun.

[long-pause]

After the launch, the important guests sat down to a celebration lunch.

[pause]

On that same day, Olympic was finished, and handed over to the White Star Line.

[pause]

That afternoon, she sailed away from Belfast for England, with many of the guests on board, to begin her life at sea.

[pause]

And the workers went home through the warm streets, tired and proud, to tell their families all about it.

[pause]

The next morning, the horn sounded again, and they went back to work.

[pause]

Because there was still so much to do.
