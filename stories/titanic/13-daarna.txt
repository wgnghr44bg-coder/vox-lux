In the weeks after Titanic was lost, people everywhere wanted to understand what had happened.

[pause]

How could the largest and finest ship in the world be lost on her very first voyage?

[pause]

And what could be done, to make sure that it never happened again?

[long-pause]

In America, a senator named William Alden Smith led an inquiry.

[pause]

It began the day after the Carpathia arrived, in a grand hotel in New York, and later continued in Washington.

[pause]

Survivors, officers and crew members came one by one to tell their stories.

[pause]

Frederick Fleet, the lookout.

[pause]

Charles Lightoller, the second officer.

[pause]

Harold Bride, the radio operator.

[pause]

Captain Rostron of the Carpathia.

[pause]

And Bruce Ismay, the head of the White Star Line.

[long-pause]

In Britain, there was another inquiry, led by a judge named Lord Mersey.

[pause]

It took place in a large hall in London, and it lasted for many weeks.

[pause]

A huge model of the ship stood at the front of the room, and a large map of the ocean hung on the wall.

[pause]

Once again, the survivors and the crew told their stories, carefully, one detail at a time.

[long-pause]

In Southampton, the news was felt most of all.

[pause]

So many of the crew had come from the same streets, close to the docks, that almost every family knew someone who had been on board.

[pause]

For days, people gathered outside the offices of the White Star Line, waiting for the lists of names.

[pause]

Neighbours helped each other.

[pause]

Churches and towns all over Britain collected money for the families.

[pause]

And in Belfast, too, the shipyard fell silent, as the workers remembered Thomas Andrews, and the men from the yard who had sailed with him.

[long-pause]

In the years that followed, memorials were built in many places.

[pause]

In Southampton, there is a memorial to the engineers, and another to the musicians.

[pause]

In Belfast, there is a white stone memorial beside the City Hall.

[pause]

And in many small towns and villages, from Ireland to Lebanon to Sweden, there are quiet stones and plaques with the names of the people who once set out on that voyage.

{img:lifeboats-for-all-ship}
From these inquiries, and from the stories of the survivors, many important lessons were learned.

[pause]

And they changed life at sea for ever.

[long-pause]

The first and simplest lesson was about lifeboats.

[pause]

From now on, every ship would have to carry enough lifeboats for every single person on board.

[pause]

And there would have to be lifeboat drills, so that passengers and crew knew exactly where to go and what to do.

[long-pause]

The second lesson was about the radio.

[pause]

From now on, the radio on a passenger ship would have to be listened to all day and all night, every hour of every day.

[pause]

No ship would ever again sail past a call for help, simply because its radio operator had gone to sleep.

[long-pause]

And the third lesson was about the ice.

[pause]

In 1914, the countries of the world agreed to create a special service, called the International Ice Patrol.

[pause]

Its ships would sail the cold waters of the North Atlantic every spring, watching for icebergs, and warning other ships where they were.

[pause]

Today, the Ice Patrol still exists, using aeroplanes and satellites.

[pause]

And every year, on the fifteenth of April, they drop a wreath of flowers onto the sea, near the place where Titanic was lost.

[long-pause]

Captain Rostron of the Carpathia became a hero.

[pause]

He was given medals and awards, and a special gold medal by the American Congress.

[pause]

But he always said that he had simply done what any captain would do.

[pause]

Later in his life, he became the commander of some of the largest ships on the Atlantic.

[pause]

And his crew never forgot that cold night, when their old ship had raced faster through the ice than she had ever gone before.

[long-pause]

The Olympic, Titanic's older sister, was changed to make her safer.

[pause]

She was given more lifeboats, and an extra inner wall along her sides.

[pause]

She went on sailing across the Atlantic for more than twenty years, carrying hundreds of thousands of passengers safely across the ocean.

[pause]

Sailors gave her a friendly nickname.

[pause]

They called her Old Reliable.

[long-pause]

Charles Lightoller, the second officer, went back to sea, and served in the navy during the First World War.

[pause]

Many years later, when he was an old man, he sailed his own small motor boat across the English Channel, to help bring soldiers home from the beaches of Dunkirk.

[pause]

Harold Bride, the young radio operator, recovered from the cold, and went back to work at sea for a while, before settling down in Scotland.

[pause]

And Frederick Fleet, the lookout, worked on ships for many more years, and later sold newspapers on a street corner in Southampton.

[long-pause]

And what happened to the passengers?

[long-pause]

Many of them went on to live long, full lives.

[pause]

Lawrence Beesley, the science teacher, wrote his book about the voyage, only a few weeks after he arrived in New York.

[pause]

He later married again, and lived until he was almost ninety.

[long-pause]

Margaret Brown became famous.

[pause]

Newspapers called her the unsinkable Molly Brown, although nobody who knew her ever called her Molly.

[pause]

She spent the rest of her life working to help others, especially women, children and working people.

[pause]

She was even given one of France's highest honours, for her work helping people during the First World War.

[long-pause]

Violet Jessop, the stewardess, kept working at sea.

[pause]

A few years later, she was on board the third sister, the Britannic, when it too was lost, during the First World War.

[pause]

And once again, she was safely rescued.

[pause]

She worked on ships for many more years, and later retired to a small cottage in the English countryside, where she kept chickens, and grew flowers in her garden.

[long-pause]

The two little French boys, Lolo and Momon, were soon reunited with their mother, who travelled from France to fetch them.

[pause]

Michel, the older one, grew up to become a teacher of philosophy.

[pause]

He lived until the year 2001, and he was the last man who had survived Titanic.

The youngest survivor of all was a baby girl called Millvina Dean.

[pause]

She was only nine weeks old when her family boarded the ship in Southampton, travelling in third class, on their way to start a new life in Kansas.

[pause]

On that cold night, she was wrapped in a blanket and carried to a lifeboat, and she slept most of the way.

[pause]

Her mother brought her safely back to England, and she grew up there, never remembering the ship at all.

[long-pause]

For many years, she hardly spoke about it.

[pause]

But later in her life, she became very well known, and she received letters from all over the world.

[pause]

She was very kind, and patient, and she answered many of them.

[pause]

She lived until the year 2009, when she was ninety-seven years old.

[pause]

She was the very last survivor of Titanic.
