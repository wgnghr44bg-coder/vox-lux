Now let's meet some of the people who worked on board.

[long-pause]

At the top of it all was the captain, Edward Smith.

[pause]

He was sixty-two years old, with a white beard and a calm, kind face.

[pause]

He had been at sea for more than forty years, and he had sailed many of the White Star Line's largest ships.

[pause]

The passengers liked him very much.

[pause]

Some wealthy travellers would only cross the ocean on a ship that he commanded.

[pause]

Some people said that this was to be his last voyage before he retired, although nobody knows for certain.

[long-pause]

Below the captain were his officers.

[pause]

There was the chief officer, Henry Wilde, who had joined the ship at the last moment.

[pause]

There was the first officer, William Murdoch, a careful and experienced sailor from Scotland.

[pause]

And there was the second officer, Charles Lightoller, a tall, strong man who had been at sea since he was thirteen, and had already survived a shipwreck, and a fire, and even a spell as a gold miner in Canada.

[long-pause]

Then there were the younger officers.

[pause]

Herbert Pitman, from a farming family in the west of England.

[pause]

Joseph Boxhall, who was very good with maps and charts.

[pause]

Harold Lowe, a young Welshman who had run away to sea as a boy.

[pause]

And James Moody, the youngest of them all, only twenty-four years old.

[pause]

They took turns on the bridge, day and night, four hours on and eight hours off, watching the sea and guiding the great ship across it.

[long-pause]

The bridge itself was a wide, open place at the front of the upper deck, with large windows looking out over the bow.

[pause]

In the middle stood the wheel, made of polished wood and brass.

[pause]

Beside it were the compasses, and the telegraphs, which sent orders down to the engine room with the ring of a bell.

[pause]

Behind the bridge was the chart room, where the officers worked out the ship's position with pencils, rulers and maps.

[pause]

At noon each day, they measured the height of the sun with an instrument called a sextant, and at night, they did the same with the stars.

[pause]

It was quiet up there, most of the time.

[pause]

Only the soft sound of the wind, and the low voices of the men on watch.

[long-pause]

{img:boiler-room-stokers}
Deep below the waterline, in a completely different world, were the engine rooms and the boiler rooms.

[pause]

Here, it was hot, and loud, and dark, lit only by electric lamps and the glow of the fires.

[pause]

The firemen, who were also called stokers, shovelled coal into the furnaces beneath the boilers, hour after hour.

[pause]

There were more than a hundred and fifty furnaces to feed.

[pause]

The trimmers brought the coal to them in heavy iron wheelbarrows, from the bunkers along the sides of the ship.

[long-pause]

It was very hard work.

[pause]

The men worked in shifts of four hours, and when they came off duty, their faces were black with coal dust, and their shirts were wet with sweat.

[pause]

They would climb up a long steel stairway to their own quarters, wash, eat, and sleep, before going down again.

[pause]

But they were proud of their work, and proud of their ship.

[pause]

And without them, Titanic could not have moved at all.

[long-pause]

Their leader was the chief engineer, Joseph Bell.

[pause]

He and his team of engineers looked after the enormous engines, the pumps, and the electric generators that gave the ship her light and her heat.

[pause]

They worked almost without rest, and they knew every sound that the machines made.

[long-pause]

{img:stewardess-corridor}
Most of the crew did not work with engines or with ropes.

[pause]

They worked with the passengers.

[pause]

There were hundreds of stewards, who cleaned the cabins, made the beds, carried the luggage, and served the meals.

[pause]

There were cooks and bakers, who worked through the night in the ship's great kitchens, making fresh bread and rolls for the morning.

[pause]

There were barbers, and a man who ran the ship's post office, and a man who looked after the swimming pool and the Turkish bath.

[long-pause]

And there were around twenty women on the crew.

[pause]

Most of them were stewardesses, who looked after the female passengers in first and second class.

[pause]

One of them was a young woman from Argentina, with Irish parents, named Violet Jessop.

[pause]

She had already been working on ships for several years.

[pause]

She was patient and cheerful, and she worked long hours, bringing tea, running baths, and helping the ladies with their dresses.

[pause]

Her story, too, will come back later.

[long-pause]

The kitchens were a world of their own.

[pause]

There were chefs from France and from England, and pastry cooks, and butchers, and a man whose only job was to make the soup.

[pause]

Every day, they baked thousands of loaves and rolls, and cooked meals for more than two thousand people.

[pause]

One of the chief bakers was a cheerful man named Charles Joughin.

[pause]

He had a team of bakers working under him, and they worked through the night, so that there would always be fresh bread in the morning.

[pause]

The smell of warm bread drifted up through the ship in the early hours, and reached the cabins of the passengers as they woke.

[long-pause]

{img:marconi-radio-room}
Up near the top of the ship, just behind the bridge, there was a small room with a very important job.

[pause]

This was the radio room, which people in those days called the Marconi room, after the inventor of the wireless telegraph.

[long-pause]

Two young men worked there.

[pause]

Jack Phillips, who had just turned twenty-five, and Harold Bride, who was twenty-two.

[pause]

They wore headphones, and they tapped out messages in Morse code, in long and short beeps, that travelled through the air to other ships and to stations on land.

[pause]

The radio was still quite new, and passengers loved it.

[pause]

Only a few years before, a ship in the middle of the ocean had been completely alone, with no way to speak to anyone, for days at a time.

[pause]

Now, messages could fly across the sea in a few moments.

[pause]

People on land could even read news from the ships in their morning newspapers.

[pause]

They could send short messages, called Marconigrams, to friends at home, or on other ships.

[pause]

Happy birthday, see you soon, a wonderful ship, the weather is fine.

[pause]

The two young men were very busy, and worked in turns, day and night.

[long-pause]

{img:ship-orchestra-playing}
And finally, there were the musicians.

[pause]

There were eight of them, led by a young violinist from the north of England, named Wallace Hartley.

[pause]

They were not really part of the crew, because they were employed by an agency, and travelled as second-class passengers.

[pause]

But they played every day, in the reception room, in the restaurant, and on the stairs.

[pause]

Five of them played together as one group, with piano, violins, cello and bass.

[pause]

The other three played as a small trio, near the restaurant and the little café.

[long-pause]

Each of them had a book with more than three hundred tunes in it, and they knew them all by heart.

[pause]

Passengers could ask for their favourite, just by calling out its number.

[pause]

And so, all day, and late into the evening, music drifted softly through the first- and second-class rooms of the ship, mixing with the sound of voices, and the clinking of glasses, and the gentle hum of the engines far below.
