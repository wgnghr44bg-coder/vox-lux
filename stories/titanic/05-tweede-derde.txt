Let's walk a little further now, down a few steps, to the second class.

[long-pause]

The people who travelled in second class were not rich, but most of them were comfortable.

[pause]

There were teachers and clergymen, shopkeepers and engineers, young couples and families with children.

[pause]

Some were going to America for work, some to visit family, and some just for the adventure.

[long-pause]

One of them was a young science teacher from London, named Lawrence Beesley.

[pause]

He had recently lost his wife, and he had decided to take a holiday, and to visit his brother in America.

[pause]

He was a quiet, careful man, who liked to observe things.

[pause]

He noticed how smoothly the ship moved, and how steady she was, even when the sea was rough.

[pause]

He liked to stand at the stern and watch the long white trail that the ship left behind her on the water.

[pause]

And he spent many hours in the second-class library, reading, and writing, and talking with the other passengers.

[pause]

Later, he would write a book about everything he saw on the voyage.

[pause]

Much of what we know about life on board comes from his careful notes.

[long-pause]

{img:second-class-deck-children}
Life in second class was quieter than in first, but it was pleasant.

[pause]

There were three good meals a day in the dining room, with its long tables and polished chairs.

[pause]

There was a promenade deck where people could walk and sit in the sun.

[pause]

The children played games on the deck, and ran up and down the stairs.

[long-pause]

Among them were two little boys from France, three and two years old.

[pause]

Their names were Michel and Edmond, but their father called them Lolo and Momon.

[pause]

They were travelling with their father, and they spent their days playing happily on the deck.

[pause]

We will meet them again, later in the story.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, the passengers in second class would gather in their dining room, or on deck if it was not too cold.

[pause]

Some played cards.

[pause]

Some read.

[pause]

And sometimes, someone would sit down at the piano, and the others would gather around to sing.

[long-pause]

There were many different people in second class.

[pause]

There were young couples, just married, going to start a new life together.

[pause]

There were families with small children, going to join relatives in cities like Detroit and Chicago.

[pause]

There were miners from Cornwall, and dressmakers, and young men who were going to work in offices and factories.

[pause]

And there were several clergymen, of different churches, travelling to new homes and new work in America.

[pause]

They sat together at dinner, and talked about their homes, and their plans, and the wonderful ship that was carrying them.

[pause]

And the ship's musicians came to play for them too, in the evenings, so that the music drifted along the corridors, and out onto the decks.

{img:third-class-general-room}
Now let's go further down still, to the third class.

[long-pause]

Around seven hundred passengers travelled in third class.

[pause]

They came from all over the world.

[pause]

From Ireland and England, from Sweden, Finland and Norway, from Belgium and Italy, from Lebanon and Syria, and from many other countries.

[pause]

Most of them were young.

[pause]

Many of them were leaving home for the very first time.

[long-pause]

Some were travelling with their whole families, with parents and grandparents, and many children.

[pause]

Some were young men and women travelling alone, with a letter in their pocket from a brother, or a cousin, or a friend who was already waiting for them in America.

[pause]

They were going to work on farms, in factories, or in shops.

[pause]

Some were going to be married.

[pause]

And all of them were hoping for a better life.

[long-pause]

They had packed their whole lives into a few small bags and wooden boxes.

[pause]

Warm clothes, and a good pair of shoes for Sundays.

[pause]

A family Bible, or a small photograph of their parents.

[pause]

A knitted blanket, made by a grandmother.

[pause]

A few tools, for work.

[pause]

And, sewn carefully into the lining of a coat, a little money, saved up over many years, to help them begin again.

[long-pause]

Many of the Irish passengers came from small villages in the west of Ireland, where there was very little work.

[pause]

Some of them travelled together in groups, with friends and neighbours from the same village.

[pause]

From one small village in County Mayo, fourteen people were travelling together.

[pause]

And from Lebanon, there were families who had walked and ridden for days, over the mountains and down to the sea, before taking a ship to France, and then a train to Cherbourg.

[pause]

For all of them, this great ship was the last step of a very long journey.

[long-pause]

{img:third-class-dining-tables}
For these passengers, Titanic was a wonder.

[pause]

The food was simple, but good, and there was plenty of it.

[pause]

For breakfast, there was porridge with milk, smoked herrings and jacket potatoes, bread and butter, and marmalade.

[pause]

For lunch, there might be soup, and roast beef, and boiled potatoes, and plum pudding.

[pause]

And for tea in the evening, there was bread and cheese, and pickles, and tea with sugar.

[pause]

Some of the passengers had never eaten so well in their lives.

[long-pause]

Many of the passengers wrote letters home, to be posted when they reached New York.

[pause]

They wrote about the size of the ship, and the white walls of their cabins, and the good food, and the kind stewards.

[pause]

One young man wrote to his family that the ship was like a palace.

[long-pause]

During the day, the third class passengers had their own open decks, at the front and at the back of the ship.

[pause]

On sunny mornings, they sat there on wooden benches, wrapped in shawls and coats, and watched the sea go by.

[pause]

Young mothers rocked their babies.

[pause]

Old men smoked their pipes, and talked about the farms and the villages they had left behind.

[pause]

And the children, who had quickly made friends with each other, even without speaking the same language, played chasing games around the steel deck fittings, and climbed on the cranes, until a sailor gently sent them down again.

[long-pause]

{img:third-class-music-dancing}
In the evenings, the third class was the liveliest place on the whole ship.

[pause]

In the general room at the back of the ship, people gathered on the long wooden benches.

[pause]

Someone would play the piano.

[pause]

Someone else would bring out a fiddle, or a set of pipes, or a small accordion.

[pause]

And soon, people were singing songs from their homelands, and dancing between the tables.

[long-pause]

The songs were in many different languages.

[pause]

Irish songs, and Swedish songs, and songs in Arabic and Italian.

[pause]

Most people did not understand the words of each other's songs.

[pause]

But they understood the music, and they clapped along, and laughed together, and made new friends.

[long-pause]

Out on the open deck at the back of the ship, some of the young people played games, or just sat and talked in the fresh cold air.

[pause]

Children chased each other between the benches, until their mothers called them in for bed.

[pause]

And later, when the music had stopped, people went back to their small cabins, and climbed into their narrow bunks, and fell asleep to the gentle hum of the engines.

[pause]

Dreaming, perhaps, of the new country that was waiting for them, somewhere far away, beyond the edge of the sea.
