[pause]

But nobody knew exactly where.

[long-pause]

The sea there is very deep.

[pause]

Almost four kilometres deep.

[pause]

If you could stand on the seabed and look up, there would be more than two and a half miles of water above your head.

[pause]

It is completely dark down there.

[pause]

No sunlight ever reaches it.

[pause]

The water is very cold, just a little above freezing, and very, very still.

[long-pause]

Over the years, many people dreamed of finding the ship.

[pause]

There were plans to raise her back to the surface, with balloons, or with magnets, or even by filling her with ping-pong balls.

[pause]

Some expeditions set out to search for her with special equipment.

[pause]

But the ocean is vast, and the position that Boxhall had worked out on that night was not quite exact.

[pause]

For a long time, nobody found anything.

{img:research-ship-knorr-night}
Then, in the summer of 1985, a French and American team set out together.

[pause]

The French team was led by an explorer named Jean-Louis Michel.

[pause]

The American team was led by an ocean scientist named Robert Ballard.

[long-pause]

First, the French ship searched a large area of the seabed with sound, sending signals down into the depths and listening for their echoes.

[pause]

Then the American ship, called Knorr, took over.

[pause]

She towed a special camera sledge called Argo, deep below her on a very long cable.

[pause]

Argo carried bright lights and cameras, and it sent its pictures up the cable to a room full of screens on the ship.

[long-pause]

Day and night, the scientists took turns watching the screens.

[pause]

For days, they saw nothing but mud and sand.

[pause]

The screens glowed softly in the dark room, showing the grey, empty seabed, sliding slowly past.

[pause]

Now and then, a small fish swam by, or a strange pale creature crawled across the mud.

[pause]

The scientists drank cups of coffee, and told each other stories to stay awake.

[pause]

And the ship moved slowly back and forth across the sea, in long straight lines, like a farmer ploughing a field.

[pause]

Time was running out.

[pause]

In a few days, they would have to go home.

[long-pause]

Then, very early in the morning of the first of September, 1985, something appeared on the screens.

[pause]

At first, it was just small pieces of metal lying on the sand.

[pause]

Then, suddenly, a large round shape came into view.

[pause]

It was one of Titanic's great boilers, lying on the seabed.

[pause]

The same kind of boilers that the stokers had fed with coal, day and night, so long ago.

[long-pause]

The team on the ship was filled with joy.

[pause]

They had found her.

[pause]

But then, they remembered where they were, and what had happened here.

[pause]

So they went quietly up onto the deck of their ship, in the cold early morning, and stood together in silence, to remember the people who had been lost.

[pause]

[pause]

The journey down took more than two hours, in total darkness.

[pause]

And then, in the lights of the submarine, the great bow of Titanic appeared out of the dark.

[long-pause]

She was still standing upright on the seabed, as if she was still sailing.

[pause]

[pause]

[pause]

[pause]

The bow and the stern lay about half a kilometre apart, facing in different directions.

[pause]

Between them, there was a wide field of objects scattered across the sand.

[pause]

Plates and cups.

[pause]

Bottles of wine, still sealed.

[pause]

Pieces of the beautiful grand staircase.

[pause]

A small leather shoe.

[long-pause]

Ballard did not take anything away from the wreck.

[pause]

He felt that it should be left in peace.

[pause]

[pause]

Later, other expeditions did bring up some objects from the seabed.

[pause]

Today, many of them can be seen in museums.

[pause]

[pause]

[pause]

The ship is slowly changing.

[pause]

Tiny living things, too small to see, are feeding on the iron of her hull.

[pause]

They create long, soft, rust-coloured shapes that hang down from her sides, like icicles.

[pause]

The scientists call them rusticles.

[pause]

Slowly, over many years, the great ship is becoming part of the sea floor.

[pause]

Soft corals and small, pale creatures live on her decks.

[pause]

Fish swim slowly through her windows.

[pause]

One day, perhaps many years from now, she will be gone altogether.

[long-pause]

Today, the wreck is protected.

[pause]

In 2012, a hundred years after she sank, Titanic came under the protection of a special agreement of the United Nations, to protect places of history beneath the sea.

[pause]

People are asked to treat her gently, and with respect, because she is a memorial to all who were lost.

[long-pause]

And in 2023, two small underwater robots spent weeks gliding slowly around the wreck, taking hundreds of thousands of photographs.

[pause]

[pause]

[pause]

Quiet, and still, and resting peacefully in the deep.
