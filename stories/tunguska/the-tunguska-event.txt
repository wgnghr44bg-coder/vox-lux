Let us begin with the place itself.

[long-pause]

Siberia is enormous.

[pause]

It stretches across the top of Asia, from the Ural Mountains in the west all the way to the Pacific Ocean in the east.

[pause]

It is so large that you could set the whole of Europe down inside it, and still have room to spare.

[long-pause]

Most of Siberia is covered by forest.

[pause]

It is the largest forest on our planet, a green belt that runs for thousands of kilometres.

[pause]

Seen from far above, it looks like a deep, dark, soft carpet that goes on and on, with no end in sight.

[long-pause]

The people who live there have a simple word for it.

[pause]

They call it the taiga.

[long-pause]

The taiga is made mostly of trees that like the cold.

[pause]

There are larches, which are strange and lovely, because they are cones that shed their needles every autumn, and turn a soft golden colour before they do.

[pause]

There are tall spruces, and firs, and pines, with long needles that smell sweet and sharp in the sun.

[pause]

And along the rivers, and in the places where the light can reach the ground, there are birches, with their pale papery bark, and their small leaves that tremble in even the faintest breeze.

[long-pause]

Walk into the taiga in summer, and the first thing you notice is the quiet.

[pause]

It is not a silence, exactly.

[pause]

There is the hum of insects, and the whisper of wind high in the needles, and now and then the call of a bird.

[pause]

But it is a wide and gentle sound, the sound of a very big place that is not in any hurry.

[long-pause]

The ground is soft under your feet.

[pause]

It is covered in moss, and in low green plants, and in cranberries and bilberries that ripen in the late summer.

[pause]

In some places the moss is so deep and so springy that every step feels like walking on a mattress.

[long-pause]

And under the moss, not very far down, the earth is frozen.

[pause]

In much of Siberia the ground stays frozen all year round, summer and winter, for hundreds of metres deep.

[pause]

People call this permafrost.

[pause]

It is like a great cold sleeper, lying under the forest, keeping the water in the soil from sinking away.

[long-pause]

That is why, in the summer, so much of the taiga is dotted with small lakes, and wet places, and bogs.

[pause]

The warm sun thaws the top of the ground a little, but the water has nowhere to go, because the frozen earth lies just below.

[pause]

So it gathers in the hollows, and shines there in the light, like pieces of the sky that have fallen among the trees.

Through this forest, long ago, the rivers carved their roads.

The great rivers of central Siberia flow north, towards the Arctic Ocean.

[pause]

Among them is the Yenisei, one of the longest and widest rivers in the world.

[pause]

[long-pause]

The Yenisei has many helpers.

[pause]

There are smaller rivers that bring their waters to it from the east and from the west.

[pause]

One of the helpers, coming in from the east, is a long and winding river called the Tunguska.

In fact there are three Tunguskas.

[pause]

There is the Lower Tunguska, the Upper Tunguska, and the one that matters for our story, the Stony Tunguska.

[pause]

In Russian, it is called the Podkamennaya Tunguska.

The name means something like, the Tunguska that flows beneath the rocks.

[long-pause]

The Stony Tunguska is a quiet river with many bends.

[pause]

It runs through low hills and thick forest, over shallow places and deep pools, past rocks and gravel banks.

[pause]

In the summer, its water is clear and cool, and in the long evenings it turns the colours of the sky, pink, and gold, and pale blue.

[long-pause]

In the winter, it freezes so thickly that people can travel along it on sledges, as if it were a white road laid through the trees.

[long-pause]

Along the Stony Tunguska, and in the forest around it, there are very few towns.

[pause]

[pause]

Many of the people who live here have lived here for a very long time, because their families have lived here for hundreds of years.

[long-pause]

And it is in this region, far from any city, far from any railway station, in the deep green quiet of central Siberia, that our story takes place.

{img:evenki-camp-chum-evening}

Before we go to the summer of 1908, let us spend a little while with the people who lived in this forest.

[long-pause]

They are called the Evenki.

[pause]

In the past, outsiders sometimes called them Tungus, which is the word that gave the river and the region its name.

[pause]

But they call themselves Evenki, and that is the name we will use tonight.

[long-pause]

The Evenki are among the oldest peoples of Siberia.

[pause]

Their homeland stretches across a huge part of the taiga, from the Yenisei River in the west to the Pacific coast in the east.

[pause]

For many centuries, their families have followed the seasons through the forest, travelling lightly, and living gently on the land.

[long-pause]

Their lives were built around reindeer.

[long-pause]

The reindeer of the Evenki are not wild, but they are not quite tame, either.

[pause]

They are gentle, quiet animals, with soft grey and brown coats, and wide, branching antlers.

[pause]

They are used to people, and they follow them through the forest, but they also love to wander, and to find their own food.

[long-pause]

An Evenki family might keep a small herd.

[pause]

The reindeer carried bags and bundles on their backs.

[pause]

They gave milk, and skins, and warmth.

[pause]

In the old days, a person could even ride a reindeer, sitting well back on its shoulders, with a light saddle, and a stick to keep balance.

[pause]

This is quite different from riding a horse, and it takes a little practice, but the reindeer are calm and patient, and they can walk through places where a horse would sink in the bog.

[long-pause]

Because the reindeer must always find fresh food, the families moved often.

[pause]

In the summer, they went to the higher ground and the open places near the rivers, where the breeze kept away some of the biting insects.

[pause]

In the winter, they moved to sheltered places in the forest, where there was food under the snow.

[long-pause]

Their homes moved with them.

[pause]

The traditional home of the Evenki is a tent, shaped like a cone, and made of long thin poles leaned together at the top.

[pause]

In the summer, these poles were covered with sheets of birch bark, sewn together with thread, and laid over the frame, like the scales of a fish.

[pause]

In the winter, they used animal skins, which kept the warmth in.

[pause]

The tent is called a chum.

[long-pause]

Inside a chum, there is a fire in the middle, and a small opening at the top to let the smoke out.

[pause]

Around the fire, the family sleeps on soft furs, and keeps its cups, and its pots, and its sewing.

[pause]

At night, when the fire burns low, the tent glows faintly orange, like a lantern in the dark forest.

[long-pause]

{img:evenki-hunter-forest-river}

The Evenki were also hunters and fishermen.

[long-pause]

In the autumn, the men went out to hunt for elk and for wild reindeer, and for the small furry animals whose fur is so warm that it has been valued all over the world.

[pause]

One of these is the sable, a small, quick, dark-brown animal that lives in the forest and moves between the trees like a shadow.

[pause]

In those years, sable fur was very precious, and was traded far to the west, to the cities of Russia and beyond.

[long-pause]

In the rivers and lakes, they caught fish.

[pause]

In the summer, there were berries to pick, and mushrooms, and the young green shoots of plants.

[pause]

Every part of the forest gave something, and the families took only what they needed, because they knew they would come back the next year.

[long-pause]

They knew the forest very well.

[pause]

They knew every river and every ridge by name.

[pause]

They knew which hill had good grass for the reindeer, and which marsh was safe to cross, and where the bears liked to feed on berries in the late summer.

[pause]

Children learned these things from their parents and grandparents, not from books, but from walking beside them through the woods.

[long-pause]

And they knew the sky.

[pause]

In a place with no city lights, the night sky is very bright.

[pause]

On a clear winter night, the stars seem to be close enough to touch, and sometimes the northern lights dance in slow green curtains.

[pause]

The Evenki had stories about the stars, and about the moon, and about the long journey of the sun.

[long-pause]

{img:evenki-storyteller-by-fire}

Like many peoples of the north, the Evenki had a rich tradition of stories and of belief.

[long-pause]

They believed that the world was full of spirits.

[pause]

There were spirits of the rivers, and of the mountains, and of the animals.

[pause]

There were spirits of the sky, too, and of the thunder and the lightning.

[long-pause]

The Evenki respected these spirits, and tried to live in harmony with them.

[pause]

When a hunter took an animal, he thanked the forest.

[pause]

When a family moved camp, they left things in good order, so that the place would be welcoming if they returned.

[long-pause]

Some people among the Evenki were shamans.

[pause]

A shaman was a kind of guide between the world of people and the world of spirits.

[pause]

The shaman would sing, and play a drum, and in this way would help the community with sickness, and trouble, and hard decisions.

[pause]

The drum was important, and its sound, slow and deep and steady, was like a heartbeat.

[long-pause]

The Evenki also told stories of a powerful being who lived in the sky, and who was linked with thunder, and with fire.

[pause]

The name of this being was Agdy, and sometimes the name Ogdy is used.

But in many of the stories, the great spirit of the sky was shown as powerful, and a little frightening, and not to be disturbed.

It has a great many insects.

In June and July, the warm, damp air fills with mosquitoes and midges and biting flies.

Even the reindeer are troubled by them, and in the worst weeks, they gather around the smoke, and stand close to the fires, flicking their ears.

For this reason, the families often moved to windy places, along the rivers and on the open ridges.

There, the breeze carried the insects away, and the reindeer could graze in peace.

So when we picture the Evenki in the summer of 1908, we should picture them scattered widely over the land, in small camps, each one a little home in the green.

Each living quietly, beside the river, with their reindeer, their smoke, and their simple daily work.

It was an old way of life, and a good one, and it had gone on for a very long time.

In the year 1908, Russia was a vast empire.

[long-pause]

It stretched from the Baltic Sea in the west to the Pacific Ocean in the east, across eleven time zones, and it was ruled by an emperor, called the Tsar.

[pause]

His name was Nicholas the Second.

Travellers had to go by horse and cart, or by boat along the rivers, or by sledge in the winter.

By around 1904, the line was open, and you could travel by train all the way from the west of Russia to the shores of the Pacific.

For the first time, Siberia felt a little closer.

[pause]

The trains brought goods and letters and newspapers.

One of the railway's stops was a town called Krasnoyarsk, on the Yenisei River.

This small place was called Vanavara.

[long-pause]

It stood on a hill above the Stony Tunguska River.

[pause]

In 1908, it was a trading post, a modest little settlement with a handful of wooden houses, built by farmers and traders who had made the long journey from the south.

[pause]

Its position was a good one, because the river was a natural road for the Evenki, and the traders came to meet them there.

[long-pause]

Here, in the warm months, the Evenki families brought their furs, and their meat, and the things they had made.

[pause]

And the traders offered tea, and flour, and salt, and iron pots, and knives, and cloth, and needles.

[pause]

It was a place of quiet bargaining, with polite words and long pauses, and cups of hot tea, and sometimes a good deal of laughing.

[long-pause]

The people of Vanavara were farmers, and traders, and a few families who had come to start a new life.

[pause]

They had small fields, with barley and potatoes, and sheds for horses and cattle.

[pause]

They cut hay in the summer, and put up wood for the winter.

[pause]

They knew their neighbours, and they knew the forest edge, and they knew the river.

[long-pause]

Many of them lived by the rhythm of the seasons.

In the spring, the ice broke up on the river, with a crack, and a rumble, and floated away downstream in great white plates.

[pause]

In the summer, the days grew very long.

[pause]

At that northern latitude, it never got properly dark in June.

[pause]

The sun dipped below the horizon late at night, and then rose again after only a few hours, and in between, the sky glowed softly, pink and green and silver, in what people sometimes call the white nights.

[pause]

A horse is shifting in the stable, and the hens are walking about, pecking at the ground.

[pause]

[pause]

He has no idea that this day is going to be remembered, in books, for more than a century.

The year 1908 was a hopeful time in many parts of the world.

In Britain, the king was Edward the Seventh, and the years of his reign are often called the Edwardian age.

The newspapers mattered a great deal in those days.

[pause]

There was no radio yet, and no television, and of course no internet.

[pause]

If you wanted to know what was happening in the world, you read the paper, or you waited for a letter.

But news could now travel quickly, because of the telegraph.

[pause]

Long cables ran under the oceans, and along railway lines, carrying short messages in a code of dots and dashes.

A report from one side of the world could reach the other in a few hours.

Science was moving fast.

In the year 1908, people were discussing electricity, and X-rays, and the strange new idea of radioactivity.

In that same summer, people were learning to fly.

In July of that same year, London was hosting the Olympic Games.

In the autumn, in the city of Detroit, a company led by Henry Ford began to produce a simple, sturdy car called the Model T.

{img:observatory-dome-night-stars-1908}

In the world of astronomy, 1908 was a year of great interest.

[long-pause]

Astronomers had been studying the sky for thousands of years, but in the early twentieth century, they were beginning to understand it in a new way.

[pause]

They had photographic plates, which could capture faint light over long hours.

[pause]

They had spectroscopes, which could split light into its colours, and so reveal what distant stars are made of.

[pause]

They had large telescopes, built on quiet mountaintops, far from the city smoke.

[long-pause]

They also knew that space was not entirely empty.

[pause]

They knew about comets, the long-tailed visitors that appear from the dark and swing around the sun.

[pause]

They knew about shooting stars, the brief streaks of light that cross the sky on summer nights.

Collectors and museums prized them.

[pause]

But in 1908, most people believed that meteorites were small things, which came down one at a time, now and then, in odd places, and were mostly of interest to scientists.

Nobody was seriously worried about the sky falling.

[long-pause]

There was one celestial event that everyone was looking forward to.

Halley's Comet, the most famous comet of all, was expected to return in 1910.

[pause]

When it came, the Earth would even pass through its tail.

[pause]

Newspapers wrote excited articles about it, and some enterprising people sold pills and umbrellas to protect against the comet's gases, which, of course, were harmless.

{img:european-garden-summer-evening-1908}

So, in the summer of 1908, the world was looking at the sky in a calm and curious way.

[long-pause]

It was a time of long evenings, and open windows, and gardens in bloom.

[pause]

A time of long train journeys, and brass bands in the park, and newspapers read slowly, with a cup of tea.

[pause]

A time when most people, in most places, expected the next day to be a great deal like the last.

[long-pause]

And far away in Siberia, as the middle of the year approached, the days grew longer and longer.

By the end of June, the sun stayed up for about nineteen hours at that latitude, and the nights were no more than a short blue twilight.

In the forest, the larches were in fresh green needle.

The rivers were running full from the melted snow.

[pause]

The insects were rising in clouds.

[long-pause]

[pause]

{img:night-sky-meteor-over-field}

Let us look up at the sky for a little while, and think about where the thing came from.

[long-pause]

Our solar system is not as empty as it looks.

[pause]

Out beyond the sun and its planets, beyond even the farthest of them, there are countless small bodies, made of rock, and metal, and ice, all travelling quietly in their own long orbits.

[pause]

Most of them have been circling in the dark for more than four and a half billion years, since the planets themselves were formed.

[long-pause]

Between Mars and Jupiter, there is a wide belt of rocky pieces, called the asteroid belt.

[pause]

Some of them are tiny, like pebbles.

[pause]

A few are vast, hundreds of kilometres across.

[pause]

Most travel peacefully in the belt for ages.

[long-pause]

But now and then, a gentle tug from a planet, or a small nudge from a collision between two rocks, sends one of them on a new path.

[pause]

Over millions of years, some of these wanderers drift inward, towards the sun, and cross the path of the Earth.

[long-pause]

Comets live farther out.

[pause]

They are made of ice and dust, like great dirty snowballs, and they spend most of their lives in the deep cold, a long way from the sun.

[pause]

When one of them swings in close, the sunlight warms it, and its ice turns to gas, and streams out behind it in a shining tail.

[pause]

That is the beautiful sight that people have always loved to watch.

[long-pause]

The Earth, meanwhile, moves on its own track.

[pause]

It travels around the sun at about thirty kilometres every second.

[pause]

That is so fast that it is hard to imagine, and yet we never feel it, because everything around us is moving at the same speed.

[long-pause]

So when a small rock from space meets our planet, it meets it at great speed.

[pause]

Not at the speed of a car, or even of a jet plane, but at tens of thousands of kilometres an hour.

[pause]

It is this speed that makes the difference.

[long-pause]

{img:meteor-streak-over-mountains-dawn}

When a meteoroid, which is the name for such a piece of rock or metal in space, enters the Earth's atmosphere, something remarkable happens.

[long-pause]

The air in front of it cannot get out of the way quickly enough.

[pause]

It is squeezed, and squeezed, and as it is squeezed it grows extremely hot.

[pause]

The rock does not burn, exactly, in the way that wood burns.

[pause]

[long-pause]

That is what we see as a shooting star, a thin streak of light across a night sky.

[pause]

Most shooting stars are caused by tiny grains, no bigger than a grain of sand, which burn away completely, high above our heads, in less than a second.

[long-pause]

Larger pieces make brighter streaks.

[pause]

A piece the size of a fist can make a brilliant flash, called a fireball, which may light up the whole landscape for a moment.

[pause]

A piece the size of a car can make a fireball bright enough to be seen from hundreds of kilometres away, and it can end with a rumble that rolls across the countryside, like far-off thunder.

[long-pause]

Sometimes the object breaks apart.

[pause]

The great pressure of the air builds up on one side, and the stone, which may be full of tiny cracks, splits, and the pieces spread out.

[pause]

Then the heat and pressure grow even more suddenly.

[pause]

The whole thing can end in an enormous burst of energy, high in the sky.

[pause]

This is called an airburst.

[long-pause]

[pause]

Often they are small.

[pause]

[pause]

[long-pause]

An airburst is different.

[pause]

In an airburst, the object does not need to land at all.

[pause]

It does its work in the air.

[long-pause]

[long-pause]

[pause]

Something very bright crossed the sky.

There was no scientist in Vanavara.

[pause]

[long-pause]

[pause]

[long-pause]

[pause]

[long-pause]

[pause]

[pause]

[pause]

[pause]

[pause]

[pause]

At that time, no one connected them with Siberia.

[pause]

[long-pause]

[pause]

An enormous sound, made in a Siberian forest in the morning, had spread out across the sky, as a very low and slow ripple, and had passed over Britain, and over the quiet English gardens, in the afternoon.

[pause]

Nobody heard it.

[pause]

Some even said that the sky had turned red.

[long-pause]

This is not surprising.

[pause]

People were in different places, and saw different parts of the sky.

[pause]

They were frightened, or startled, or were looking in another direction.

[pause]

And over the years, as they told their story, it grew and changed, as stories do.

[long-pause]

But if you collect many of them, and compare them carefully, as the scientists later did, a pattern appears.

[pause]

It tells you the direction the object was travelling, and how steeply it came down, and roughly how high it was when it burst.

[pause]

It is like a puzzle, in which each person has one small piece.

[long-pause]

That work took years, and it was begun by a man whose name is now known to everyone who studies the Tunguska event.

[pause]

His name was Leonid Kulik.

[long-pause]

{img:young-scientist-museum-meteorite-collection}

Leonid Alekseyevich Kulik was born in 1883, in a small town near the city of Saint Petersburg.

[pause]

He grew up in a family that valued learning, and as a young man he trained as a teacher.

[pause]

But he was drawn to the world of the sky and the stones, and he found his way to the study of minerals, and then to something even more particular, which was the study of meteorites.

[long-pause]

He was a patient, determined, rather stubborn person.

[pause]

Those who knew him described him as a man of great energy, who could march for days through the wilderness, and sleep in a damp tent, and never lose his enthusiasm.

[pause]

He had a fine, thick moustache, and a pair of quick, curious eyes.

[long-pause]

After the revolution, Kulik worked for the Russian Academy of Sciences, in a department that looked after the national collection of meteorites.

[pause]

He was in charge of a small museum in Petrograd, as Saint Petersburg was then called, which held stones that had fallen from space.

[pause]

He loved those stones.

[pause]

He would take them in his hands, and turn them gently, and read their surface as if it were a very old book.

[long-pause]

His task was to find more.

[pause]

The Soviet government wanted to know about meteorites that had fallen on its land, and Kulik was the person for the job.

[pause]

He travelled to many places, collecting reports, and talking to farmers, and studying maps.

[long-pause]

{img:old-map-siberia-lamplight-notes}

In the early 1920s, Kulik was working in the east, in Siberia.

[long-pause]

He had come across a number of old reports, in the archives and the newspapers, about a great fall of stones in the summer of 1908.

[pause]

Some of these reports said that a bright body had crossed the sky, and then an immense noise had followed.

[pause]

Some said the ground had shaken, and that a huge cloud of smoke had risen in the north.

[long-pause]

He was intrigued.

[pause]

It seemed to him that this might be a very large meteorite, much larger than the famous ones in the museums.

[pause]

It might have fallen somewhere in the taiga, where nobody had yet looked.

[long-pause]

He began to collect accounts.

[pause]

He talked to railway workers, and teachers, and traders.

[pause]

In some of the villages near the great river, he heard stories of a place in the north where the forest had been knocked flat, and where the earth was said to be burned.

[pause]

He was told about the Evenki families who had run away from the north, and about the odd quiet that lay over that land.

[long-pause]

At first, he did not know if he believed them.

[pause]

Some of the stories sounded exaggerated.

[pause]

But there were so many of them, and they agreed so closely, that he could not set them aside.

[pause]

He made up his mind to find out for himself.

[long-pause]

{img:expedition-sledges-frozen-river-snow}

It took a long time to organise.

[pause]

Money was short in those years, and travel was hard.

[pause]

But in 1927, the Academy agreed to support a small expedition, and Kulik set out.

[long-pause]

The journey was long.

[pause]

He took the railway across Russia and into Siberia, to a station in the south.

[pause]

From there, he went north with a few companions, and a handful of supplies, to the village of Vanavara.

[pause]

The route was difficult.

[pause]

There were rivers to cross, and marshes to wade through, and thick forest to push through with axes.

[long-pause]

It was a land of mosquitoes and mud, and little else.

[pause]

The expedition members hired local guides, some of them Evenki, who knew the paths and the water.

[pause]

Some of the guides were reluctant to go too near the place they had heard about.

[pause]

They said it was a bad place, a place of power, and best left alone.

[pause]

But some agreed to take the party as far as they dared.

[long-pause]

Kulik talked with the farmers of Vanavara, and he heard the story of Semyon Semyonov, and of others who had been there on the morning.

[pause]

The villagers told him of the flash, and the roar, and the hot wind.

[pause]

They told him about the forest to the north, and how, in the years since, travellers had come back with tales of trees lying on the ground, like matchsticks.

[long-pause]

He was more eager than ever.

[pause]

He packed his supplies, hired a few horses, and set off north.

[long-pause]

{img:ridge-top-view-over-flattened-forest-valley}

After several days of hard travel, the expedition came to a high ridge.

[pause]

It is called the Makirta ridge, and from its top, one can look out over a wide stretch of the land.

[long-pause]

Kulik stood at the top, in the clear air, and looked north.

[pause]

What he saw was beyond his imagination.

[long-pause]

In front of him, as far as he could see, the forest lay flat.

[pause]

Thousands upon thousands of trees, tall spruces and larches, each many metres long, lay on the ground, all pointing the same way, all pointing away from a distant centre, as if a great hand had combed them smooth.

[pause]

They were silvery grey, and many of them were stripped of their branches.

[pause]

The whole landscape was like a vast, silent, orderly graveyard of trees, stretching to the horizon.

[long-pause]

He wrote in his diary that it was a scene he would never forget.

[pause]

It was so large, and so quiet.

[pause]

It was not chaotic, like the ruins of a storm.

[pause]

It was strangely neat.

[long-pause]

There was no sound but the wind.

[pause]

The air smelled of damp wood and moss.

[pause]

The sun glinted on the grey trunks, and a few small birds hopped between them.

[long-pause]

For the first time, Kulik understood how big the event had been.

[pause]

It was not a small meteorite.

[pause]

It was something of a very different scale.

[long-pause]

He stood for a long time on the ridge, gazing out at the silent forest.

[pause]

And then he took a deep breath, and set off down the slope, towards the centre.

[long-pause]

{img:expedition-camp-tents-fallen-trees-evening}

The journey from the ridge to the centre of the fallen forest was hard.

[long-pause]

The ground was wet and uneven, covered with moss, and with the long grey trunks lying criss-cross over it.

[pause]

The party had to climb over them, one after another, step by step, pulling themselves along by their hands.

[pause]

The horses could not follow, so supplies had to be carried.

[pause]

The mosquitoes were fierce.

[long-pause]

Each night, they made camp in a small open place, and lit a smoky fire, and cooked a simple supper of tea and bread and dried meat.

[pause]

Each morning they woke, stiff and damp, and went on.

[long-pause]

As they went, they noticed a curious thing.

[pause]

The trees all lay with their tops pointing away from a single place, in front of them.

[pause]

It was as if the whole forest had been blown outward from a centre, like the spokes of an enormous wheel.

[pause]

And as the party got closer to this centre, they saw an even stranger thing.

[long-pause]

Near the middle, the trees were not lying down at all.

[pause]

They were standing.

[pause]

Hundreds of tall, straight trunks stood upright, with every branch and all their bark stripped away, like a forest of bare telegraph poles.

[long-pause]

Scientists later explained why.

[pause]

At the very centre, directly beneath the explosion, the blast wave came straight down from above.

[pause]

It pushed down on the treetops, and stripped away the branches, but it could not push the trunks over, for there was no sideways force.

[pause]

Farther away, the wave came in at an angle, and pushed the trees flat.

[pause]

It is a beautiful pattern, and it is one of the best clues we have to where the explosion took place.

[long-pause]

{img:standing-bare-trunks-in-misty-bog-dawn}

Kulik was thrilled.

[pause]

He thought that, in the middle of the standing trees, he would find the crater of a great meteorite.

[pause]

That was what he expected, and what he hoped.

[long-pause]

He searched.

[pause]

In the centre of the devastated area, he found a number of round, shallow holes in the ground, some of them full of water, and ringed with a lip of moss.

[pause]

They looked like small craters.

[pause]

Some were as wide as a hundred metres across.

[long-pause]

He decided that these must be places where the fragments of the meteorite had hit the ground, and he spent a great deal of effort on them.

[pause]

He measured them, and photographed them, and pondered them.

[long-pause]

But when he looked more closely, they did not behave like impact craters.

[pause]

They were not surrounded by throwing-out of earth.

[pause]

They had no pieces of meteorite in them.

[pause]

Many of them, as later study showed, were simply the kind of thaw hollows, or marshy depressions, that are common in the permafrost lands of Siberia, and had been there long before 1908.

[long-pause]

Kulik was puzzled, and a little disappointed.

[pause]

But he did not give up.

[long-pause]

He wrote that there must be a hidden meteorite, buried deep in the ground, perhaps under the swamp.

[pause]

He decided to return, with more people, and better tools, and look again.

[long-pause]

{img:scientists-drilling-wet-bog-summer}

And return he did.

[pause]

In 1928, he came back with a larger group.

[pause]

In 1929 and 1930, he made two more journeys.

[pause]

Each time, he and his team worked with great determination.

[long-pause]

They dug trenches.

[pause]

They drained marshy pools by cutting channels, and looked at the mud.

[pause]

They drilled long holes with hand tools, and looked at what came up.

[pause]

They dragged boats and tools and sacks of supplies across the swamp.

[pause]

They fought off the insects.

[pause]

They slept in damp tents, and ate rough meals, and were often cold and often tired.

[long-pause]

Through it all, Kulik kept hoping.

[pause]

He imagined a huge iron meteorite lying beneath the peat, a shining mass of metal, which would be the greatest in the world.

[pause]

He sent reports back to Leningrad, and told his colleagues that he was close.

[long-pause]

Yet nothing was found.

[pause]

No giant stone.

[pause]

No iron mass.

[pause]

No crater, in the ordinary sense.

[long-pause]

It was a puzzle.

[pause]

The forest had been flattened over a huge area, so something very powerful had happened.

[pause]

But the thing that did it was not there.

[long-pause]

Today, we understand the answer.

[pause]

The object did not hit the ground.

[pause]

It burst in the air, and the pieces, small and hot, were scattered, or turned to vapour, or to dust.

[pause]

But in the late 1920s, nobody had yet worked this out.

[pause]

The idea of an airburst was new, and Kulik, with his heart set on a great meteorite, was not ready to accept it.

[long-pause]

{img:forest-photograph-expedition-large-format-camera}

Still, his work was of great value.

[long-pause]

He and his team made maps of the flattened forest.

[pause]

They measured the directions of the fallen trees, and plotted them on paper.

[pause]

They photographed the scene, and made careful notes.

[pause]

They collected stories from every eyewitness they could find.

[long-pause]

And they told the world about it.

[pause]

In the late 1920s, newspapers in Russia, and then in other countries, began to print stories about the mysterious devastation in the Siberian forest.

[pause]

Scientists in Europe and America began to read about it, and to wonder.

[long-pause]

In the 1930s, aeroplanes were used for the first time, and in 1938, Kulik arranged for aerial photographs to be taken.

[pause]

From the air, the pattern was even clearer.

[pause]

The fallen trees formed a great shape, like the wings of a butterfly, spread out over the forest.

[pause]

It covered an area of about two thousand square kilometres, an area larger than many a modern city.

[long-pause]

It was a remarkable picture.

[pause]

It showed that the blast had not come from a single point on the ground, but from above, from a body that was moving as it burst.

[pause]

This gave the first strong hint to what had really happened.

[long-pause]

The photographs were carefully stored, and they are still studied today.

[pause]

They are a kind of portrait of the forest, taken from the sky, in the pale light of an ordinary northern day.

[long-pause]

{img:evenki-elder-tea-table-warm-tent-interior}

While Kulik was searching the swamp, other people were listening.

[long-pause]

Over the years, researchers travelled through the region to talk with the Evenki and with the settlers, and collected their memories of the morning.

[pause]

Some of these stories were written down in the late 1920s, and others in the 1930s, and later.

[pause]

The people who told them were, by then, middle-aged or old.

[pause]

They remembered what they had felt, and seen, and heard, with great clarity, as one remembers a day that changes one's life.

[long-pause]

One family told how they had been sleeping in their tent, when a great force lifted it, and threw them about.

[pause]

They said the tent was torn, and blown away.

[pause]

The father was thrown, and woke up lying on the ground, far from where he had been.

[pause]

They thought at first that it must be the end of the world.

[long-pause]

Another man told how he had been far from the centre, with his reindeer, and had seen a bright light and felt the earth shake.

[pause]

He said that the reindeer had all run away, and that it took many days to find them.

[pause]

Some of the animals, he said, were never found.

[long-pause]

A third person recalled that it had been so hot, for a moment, that the skin of the face felt burned.

[pause]

Another told that the trees were all falling, one by one, like a row of tall, slow dominoes, and that the noise was louder than anything they had ever heard.

[long-pause]

These stories are very moving, and their strength comes from how ordinary they are.

[pause]

These are not the accounts of scientists.

[pause]

They are the stories of families, with their tents and their animals, and their daily lives, caught up in an event that they had no way of understanding.

[long-pause]

{img:evenki-camp-after-storm-people-rebuilding}

We must be careful, though, about one thing.

[pause]

Some of the stories told about the Evenki in connection with this event have been told and retold in the wrong way, and have grown into legends of their own.

[long-pause]

You may sometimes read that the Evenki saw the event as a kind of divine punishment.

[pause]

There are accounts where it is said that the sky god, Agdy, had come down, and had cursed the forest, and that the people were afraid to go there for years.

[pause]

Some versions of this story are told in books and on television.

[long-pause]

It is hard to know how much of this comes from the Evenki themselves, and how much was added by outsiders.

[pause]

Many of the accounts that we have were gathered by Russian scientists, who were writing in a hurry, in difficult conditions, and who did not always understand the language or the traditions.

[pause]

Some people have argued that the stories were shaped, over time, by the way they were asked for.

[long-pause]

What does seem true is that the area became a place of caution.

[pause]

It was a place of great power, as many people said, and one did not go there without good reason.

[pause]

That sort of feeling is very common in the world, around places that have seen strange or powerful events.

[pause]

It does not need any more explanation than that.

[long-pause]

We might say simply that the Evenki, who had lived in this forest for centuries, knew that something very big had happened, and felt that the right thing was to treat the place with respect.

[pause]

And that is a wise thing to do, for any place that has been touched by something larger than ourselves.

[long-pause]

{img:soviet-expedition-aerial-survey-plane-meadow}

After Kulik's last expedition in 1939, the story took a sad turn.

[long-pause]

In 1941, the war came.

[pause]

Germany invaded the Soviet Union, and the whole country was drawn into one of the hardest struggles in its history.

[pause]

Kulik, who was by then in his late fifties, joined a volunteer unit of the army.

[pause]

In the fighting around Moscow in the autumn of 1941, he was taken prisoner.

[pause]

He fell ill in a prison camp, and he died in the spring of 1942, at the age of fifty-eight.

[long-pause]

He never found his meteorite.

[pause]

He never knew that he had, in fact, found something even more extraordinary.

[long-pause]

But he had done what no one else had done.

[pause]

He had gone to the place, and seen it with his own eyes, and measured it, and told the world.

[pause]

Without his stubbornness, and his enthusiasm, and his patient labour in the swamps, the story might have been lost.

[long-pause]

Today, Kulik is remembered with great respect.

[pause]

In the Tunguska region, there are places named after him.

[pause]

A crater on the Moon bears his name.

[pause]

And scientists who study the event still use the maps and the photographs and the notes that he made, almost a hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

His work reminds us of something.

[pause]

That curiosity, and effort, and care, even when they do not lead to the answer we expected, can lead to an answer that is just as good, or better, for those who come after.

[long-pause]

{img:quiet-wartime-library-lamp-notes-evening}

During the war years, and for a while after, research on the Tunguska event stopped.

[long-pause]

But the notes, and the photographs, and the maps stayed safe in the archives.

[pause]

And as the war ended, and the country slowly rebuilt, a new generation of scientists began to take an interest.

[long-pause]

In the late 1940s, a writer of science stories named Alexander Kazantsev published a tale about the event.

[pause]

In his story, the explosion in Siberia was not caused by a rock at all, but by a visiting spaceship that had blown up in the air.

[pause]

It was only a work of fiction, written for fun, and Kazantsev himself did not claim it was true.

[pause]

But the idea caught on, and for years afterwards, it was repeated in books and magazines.

[long-pause]

It is worth saying clearly that there is no evidence for this.

[pause]

The science that has been done since then points to a natural cause.

[pause]

But the fiction is a sign of how much the event had captured the public imagination.

[pause]

People wanted there to be a great mystery, and it was a kind of tribute to the power of the story that so many wanted to explain it in their own ways.

[long-pause]

Meanwhile, the real scientists got on with the real work.

[pause]

In the 1950s and 1960s, they began to travel to the forest in larger groups, with better instruments, and a much clearer idea of what they were looking for.

[long-pause]

{img:soviet-scientists-sieving-soil-forest-camp}

In 1958, a new expedition set out for the Tunguska forest.

[long-pause]

It was led by a geochemist named Kirill Florensky, and it was the first of many that would be organised by the Academy of Sciences in the following years.

[pause]

The team was larger than Kulik's had been, and better equipped, with trucks, radios, and modern instruments.

[pause]

They were not looking for a big stone.

[pause]

They were looking for small clues.

[long-pause]

They made maps with great care.

[pause]

They studied the fallen trees, tree by tree, and measured the directions in which they lay.

[pause]

They collected soil, and sifted it, and looked at it under microscopes.

[pause]

They took samples of the peat, and of the resin in the trees, and of the water in the little pools.

[long-pause]

In the soil, they found something remarkable.

[pause]

Mixed into the top layers of the ground, in the area of the explosion, there were tiny round particles, like microscopic beads, made of metal and glass.

[pause]

They were smaller than grains of sand.

[pause]

Some were made of iron and nickel, and some of silicate, a kind of glassy stone.

[long-pause]

These beads, called spherules, are formed when a body from space melts in the air and breaks into droplets, which cool and fall as a fine rain of tiny balls.

[pause]

They are typical of meteoritic dust.

[pause]

Their presence in the Tunguska soil was strong evidence that the object had indeed been a natural body from space, and that it had broken up high in the atmosphere, scattering its remains over the landscape.

[long-pause]

It was a very satisfying discovery.

[pause]

And it was only the beginning.

[long-pause]

{img:tree-ring-cross-section-closeup-sunlit-lab}

Over the following decades, many more studies were carried out.

[long-pause]

Scientists looked at the rings of trees that had survived the blast, and found that the rings from the year 1908 told a story.

[pause]

The trees that were still standing, after the event, had grown more slowly in the next few years, as if they had been shocked.

[pause]

But some of them, after a period of recovery, grew faster than before, perhaps because the surrounding trees had been cleared away, and there was more light and space.

[pause]

Rings are like a diary, written in wood.

[long-pause]

{img:peat-bog-core-sample-researcher-hands}

They studied the peat in the bogs, which laid down a layer each year, like pages in a book.

[pause]

In the layer for 1908, they found small changes in the chemistry, which fit with the dust of a meteoroid, and with a short rise in the amount of certain elements.

[pause]

They even found traces of the same elements in ice cores from the polar regions, from the year 1908, thousands of kilometres away.

[long-pause]

They studied the fallen trees, and worked out, from the angle at which the trunks lay, and from how the bark was scorched, the exact place where the explosion had happened.

[pause]

They found that the blast had come from a point about eight kilometres above the ground, though newer studies give a range of between five and ten.

[long-pause]

They collected more accounts, and compared them with the measurements, and slowly the whole picture came together.

[pause]

It was patient work, done in the summers, in the mud and the mosquitoes, by dozens of people.

[pause]

In the winters, they sat in their offices, in the cities, and studied the numbers.

[pause]

And it all added up.

[long-pause]

{img:laboratory-microscope-tiny-silver-spheres}

By the end of the twentieth century, the story had been pieced together with much more care.

[long-pause]

The object had come in at a shallow angle, from the south-east, travelling at a speed of perhaps fifteen to thirty kilometres a second.

[pause]

It was most likely about fifty to sixty metres across, roughly the size of a large building, though some estimates are smaller or larger.

[pause]

It exploded about five to ten kilometres above the forest, releasing energy of about ten to fifteen megatons.

[long-pause]

It flattened some eighty million trees over an area of about two thousand one hundred and fifty square kilometres.

[pause]

It threw out heat, and a shock wave, and a roar that was heard hundreds of kilometres away.

[pause]

It set some of the forest on fire, in places, though the fires did not spread widely.

[pause]

And it left no great crater, only a landscape of fallen trees, tiny beads, and a quiet that lasted a very long time.

[long-pause]

All of this was learned by people who walked through that forest, sat beside those bogs, and looked closely at things that most would have passed by.

[pause]

It is a good example of how science works, with slow, careful, modest steps.

[long-pause]

And still, some questions remain open.

[long-pause]

{img:comet-bright-head-tail-over-dark-hills}

One of the biggest questions is what the object actually was.

[long-pause]

For a long time, scientists have disagreed.

[pause]

There have been two main ideas.

[pause]

Both are about natural objects, and both fit much of the evidence.

[pause]

Let us look at them calmly, one by one.

[long-pause]

The first idea is that it was a comet.

[pause]

Remember that a comet is a ball of ice and dust, a bit like a very large, loose snowball.

[pause]

If a small comet entered the atmosphere, it would be heated rapidly, and the ice would turn to steam.

[pause]

The whole thing would blow apart in the air, in a great burst, leaving no stony remains.

[long-pause]

This idea fits several of the facts.

[pause]

It explains why no large fragments were found, because ice melts, and the dust spreads away.

[pause]

It might explain the bright night skies, because a comet carries a lot of water, and the water would have gone high into the sky, to form clouds that shine.

[pause]

This idea was suggested in the 1930s by the British scientist who studied the barograph records, and by others.

[long-pause]

But there are difficulties.

[pause]

A comet is very fragile.

[pause]

It would probably break up much higher in the atmosphere, long before reaching the height where the explosion took place.

[pause]

Some scientists think a comet would not penetrate deeply enough.

[pause]

And the tiny spherules found in the soil seem to point to rock and metal, not ice.

[long-pause]

{img:rocky-asteroid-in-space-quiet-stars}

The second idea is that it was a stony asteroid.

[long-pause]

An asteroid is a piece of rock, and it is much stronger than a comet.

[pause]

If one of about fifty metres entered the air, it would plunge much deeper into the atmosphere before breaking up.

[pause]

It would then burst at a height of a few kilometres, with a shockwave strong enough to knock down trees, just as was seen in the forest.

[long-pause]

This idea fits the tiny beads of metal and glass.

[pause]

It also fits the calculations of how deep the object went before bursting.

[pause]

Many scientists today favour it, and a number of detailed studies, using computers, point the same way.

[long-pause]

It also explains why no large pieces were found.

[pause]

In the explosion, the stone was shattered into countless small fragments, and most of the material was turned into very fine dust, or vapour, and spread through the air.

[pause]

Only a small portion may have landed in the form of tiny particles.

[long-pause]

So today, most researchers think it was probably a stony asteroid that exploded in the air.

[pause]

But there is no single piece of evidence that settles the matter entirely, and some scientists still think a comet is possible.

[long-pause]

{img:small-forest-lake-still-water-morning}

In 2007, a team of Italian scientists from the University of Bologna suggested something new.

[long-pause]

They had been studying a small lake in the Tunguska region, called Lake Cheko.

[pause]

It lies about eight kilometres north-west of the centre of the fallen forest, and it is a small, almost perfectly oval lake, about five hundred metres across.

[pause]

It is quiet and deep, and ringed with trees.

[long-pause]

The Italian scientists proposed that the lake might be a crater, made by a fragment of the object that had survived and fallen to the ground in 1908.

[pause]

They studied the shape of the lake, and the layers of mud on its floor, and they found some features that seemed to fit.

[long-pause]

It was an exciting idea.

[pause]

If true, it would mean that a piece of the object had been found, hidden beneath the water, for all those years.

[long-pause]

Other scientists, however, were not convinced.

[pause]

Some argued that the lake was much older than 1908, and pointed to the age of the sediments, and to the shape of the basin.

[pause]

Others said it was simply one of many small lakes in the region.

[pause]

Studies since have gone back and forth.

[long-pause]

At the time of this recording, the question of Lake Cheko has not been settled.

[pause]

It may be a crater, and it may not.

[pause]

It is one of those quiet puzzles that are left for people in the future to solve, perhaps with better drills, and a more careful look at the mud.

[long-pause]

{img:night-sky-aurora-over-lonely-forest-cabin}

There have also been many wilder theories.

[long-pause]

Over the years, people have suggested that the explosion was caused by a tiny black hole, passing through the Earth.

[pause]

Some said it was a lump of antimatter.

[pause]

Some said it was a burst of gas from deep underground.

[pause]

Some said that the great inventor Nikola Tesla had done it with an experiment, though this is simply not possible, since Tesla's machines could not do anything remotely like it.

[pause]

And some, as we have said, have suggested that it was a spaceship.

[long-pause]

None of these ideas is supported by evidence.

[pause]

Most are rather like fairy stories, told for fun on long winter nights.

[pause]

They are not harmful, and they are part of the charm of the Tunguska story, but they are not what the scientists think happened.

[long-pause]

It is good to remember that a mystery does not have to be a miracle.

[pause]

Something can be wonderful, and strange, and still have a natural explanation.

[pause]

In fact, the true explanation, an object from space, no bigger than a block of flats, travelling fast enough to cross a continent in minutes, and bursting in the sky with the energy of millions of tonnes of explosive, is every bit as marvellous as anything made up.

[long-pause]

And the best part is that we are still learning.

[long-pause]

{img:supercomputer-hall-soft-blue-light-screens}

In the early years of the twenty-first century, scientists found a new way to look at the Tunguska event.

[long-pause]

They used computers.

[pause]

Very powerful ones, of the kind that can carry out billions of calculations every second.

[pause]

With these machines, researchers at several laboratories, including one in New Mexico, in America, built detailed simulations of an object entering the atmosphere.

[long-pause]

The idea is simple, though the numbers are not.

[pause]

You tell the computer what the object is made of, how big it is, how fast it is moving, and at what angle it comes in.

[pause]

Then you let the computer work out, step by step, how the air presses on it, how it heats, how it breaks up, and how the energy is released.

[pause]

It is like running a very detailed film of the explosion, frame by frame, in slow motion.

[long-pause]

One surprising result of these simulations was that the object may have been smaller than people had thought.

[pause]

In the simulations, the fragments of the broken object did not simply fall as a cloud of dust.

[pause]

They travelled together, as a kind of fiery jet, which pushed deeper into the air, and released its energy lower down, closer to the ground.

[pause]

This made the explosion more efficient at knocking down trees.

[long-pause]

So, an object of perhaps thirty to fifty metres, and an energy of about three to five megatons, could have flattened the forest in the way that was seen.

[pause]

That is smaller than the older estimates, and it makes the event a little less rare than people feared.

[long-pause]

{img:digital-map-fan-shaped-pattern-fallen-trees}

The simulations also reproduced the butterfly pattern.

[long-pause]

When the computer models were run with an object coming in at a shallow angle, from the south-east, the pattern of blast damage on the ground came out in the shape of two wings, spreading out from the burst point, with a quiet zone in the middle.

[pause]

This was just what Kulik's aerial photographs had shown, almost seventy years earlier.

[long-pause]

It must have been a strange and wonderful moment for the scientists.

[pause]

They had set up a virtual world, and run a virtual meteoroid, and watched a pattern appear on their screens that matched, tree for tree, what a small team had mapped by hand in the swamps long ago.

[long-pause]

This is the quiet joy of science.

[pause]

It is when a modern machine and an old notebook agree.

[pause]

It means that the people who came before us did good work, and that our understanding, slowly and carefully, is on the right track.

[long-pause]

We should say that the simulations do not settle every question.

[pause]

Different teams use different assumptions, and get different numbers.

[pause]

There is still much debate about the exact size of the object, and its exact makeup.

[pause]

But the broad picture, an asteroid, a shallow angle, an airburst, a flattened forest, seems solid.

[long-pause]

{img:forest-research-station-evening-light-wooden-cabin}

Over the years, expeditions have continued.

[long-pause]

In the 1990s, and in the 2000s, groups of scientists from Russia and from other countries, including Italy and Germany and the United States, returned to the forest.

[pause]

They carried lighter equipment, and satellite navigation, and cameras.

[pause]

They studied the bogs, the lakes, the trees and the soil, and gathered more and more evidence.

[long-pause]

Some of them stayed for weeks, in simple camps, in tents or in wooden cabins.

[pause]

They would get up with the sun, and walk long distances through the forest, carrying heavy bags, and measure and record.

[pause]

In the evenings, they would sit around a small fire, and talk about their day, and drink tea.

[long-pause]

They were joined, at times, by local people, and by the Evenki, who helped as guides, and who shared their own knowledge of the land.

[pause]

It was a small, friendly kind of science, based on shared curiosity, patience, and respect.

[long-pause]

Today, much of the area is protected.

[pause]

In the 1990s, the Russian government set up a nature reserve, called the Tunguska Nature Reserve, which covers a large part of the region.

[pause]

Its purpose is to look after the forest, and the rivers, and the wildlife, and to allow scientists to continue their work in peace.

[long-pause]

It is a fitting thing, we might say.

[pause]

A place that was struck by a force from the sky is now kept as a place of calm.

[long-pause]

{img:desert-crater-arizona-sunrise-wide}

The Tunguska event was a large one.

[pause]

But it was not the only time the Earth has met a visitor from space.

[long-pause]

In the desert of Arizona, in the United States, there is a great round hollow in the land, called Meteor Crater.

[pause]

It is about one and a quarter kilometres across, and nearly two hundred metres deep.

[pause]

It was made about fifty thousand years ago, when an iron meteorite, perhaps fifty metres wide, struck the ground at high speed.

[pause]

Today, you can stand at its edge, in the dry desert air, and look out across it, and feel how large the world is, and how old.

[long-pause]

In February of 1947, in the far east of Russia, in the Sikhote-Alin mountains, a bright fireball crossed the morning sky, and a shower of iron meteorites fell on the snowy forest.

[pause]

Many people saw it, and some of them were able to paint and draw what they had seen.

[pause]

Scientists collected thousands of pieces, and found a number of small craters.

[pause]

It was a very large fall, but it came apart in the air, and fell as a rain of metal pieces over a wide place, so that none of them was large enough to cause great harm.

[long-pause]

And in more recent times, there have been others.

[pause]

Observation networks, and cameras, and satellites now record dozens of small events each year, as bright fireballs flash in the sky, and are noticed from space.

[pause]

Most are small, and burn away without a sound.

[pause]

A few are brighter, and are seen by many people, and make a boom that rolls through the countryside.

[long-pause]

{img:winter-morning-sky-bright-trail-over-city-skyline}

One of these, in particular, was widely seen.

[long-pause]

On a cold morning in February of 2013, over the city of Chelyabinsk in Russia, a bright fireball appeared in the sky.

[pause]

It was about twenty metres across, much smaller than the Tunguska object.

[pause]

It entered the air at a shallow angle, and exploded about thirty kilometres above the ground.

[long-pause]

It was seen by thousands of people.

[pause]

Many of them were on their way to work or to school.

[pause]

They looked up, and saw a brilliant white streak, brighter than the sun, with a long trail of smoke curling behind it.

[pause]

And then, a minute or two later, the shock wave arrived.

[pause]

It rolled across the city, and broke a great many windows, and rattled doors and cars and buildings.

[long-pause]

Because it was a morning in a city, with lots of cars and cameras, the event was filmed from every angle.

[pause]

Within hours, the videos were seen all over the world.

[pause]

It was the first time that an event of this kind had been recorded in such detail.

[long-pause]

Scientists used the videos, and other data, to work out exactly where the object had come from, and what it was made of.

[pause]

It turned out to be a stony asteroid, which had been travelling in a path that had never been noticed.

[pause]

Nobody had seen it coming, because it was too small and too dark to detect, and it had come in from the direction of the sun.

[long-pause]

Chelyabinsk was a useful reminder.

[pause]

It showed that objects from space do reach the Earth, not just in ancient times, but now.

[pause]

And it showed that, with careful study, we can learn a great deal from them.

[long-pause]

{img:gentle-meteor-shower-over-quiet-lake-night}

Now, let us put all of this in proportion.

[long-pause]

The Earth is struck by small objects every day.

[pause]

Some, no bigger than grains of dust, fall in their millions, and drift softly down through the air, as a fine, invisible rain.

[pause]

Perhaps forty tonnes of material, from space, lands on our planet each day, most of it as very fine dust, and no one notices.

[long-pause]

Objects the size of a pebble make the shooting stars that you can see on a summer night.

[pause]

An object the size of a car, entering the atmosphere, makes a bright fireball a few times a year, somewhere in the world, usually over the ocean or empty land.

[pause]

One the size of the Chelyabinsk object comes along every few decades.

[long-pause]

Objects the size of the Tunguska body, around fifty metres, are far rarer.

[pause]

Scientists estimate that one such object strikes the Earth about once in several hundred years, or once in a thousand years, or perhaps more.

[pause]

The exact figure is uncertain, and more recent studies suggest that these events may be less frequent than we thought.

[pause]

But the point is clear.

[pause]

It is a rare event, and most likely it will not happen again in our lifetime, or in the lifetime of anyone we know.

[long-pause]

And even if it did, there is a good chance it would fall in the sea, or in empty land, as the Tunguska body did.

[pause]

The Earth is a very large place, and most of it is quiet.

[long-pause]

{img:observatory-telescope-dome-open-night-sky}

There is a hopeful part to this story, and it is the part that comes next.

[long-pause]

Because of Tunguska, and because of other events like it, people began to take the sky more seriously.

[pause]

Over the last few decades, astronomers have begun to watch for objects that might cross the path of the Earth.

[pause]

They are called near-Earth objects, and they are the small asteroids and comets whose orbits bring them close to our planet.

[long-pause]

In the 1990s, a number of observatories began to search for them, patiently, night after night, using telescopes and cameras that can detect very faint, slow-moving points of light.

[pause]

Each night, they compare their pictures with those from the night before, and look for any dot that has moved.

[pause]

When they find one, they measure its position again and again, and work out its orbit, as precisely as they can.

[long-pause]

The work has been very successful.

[pause]

Today, we know of tens of thousands of near-Earth asteroids.

[pause]

We have found nearly all of the very big ones, those a kilometre across or more, and we know that none of them is going to hit us for a very long time.

[pause]

We are now working our way down to the smaller ones, which are harder to see, and more numerous.

[long-pause]

New telescopes are coming into use.

[pause]

A great observatory in the mountains of Chile, named after the American astronomer Vera Rubin, began to take its first images in 2025, and will photograph the whole southern sky again and again, finding many new moving objects.

[pause]

And space agencies are building telescopes that will fly in space, and look for asteroids that approach from the direction of the sun, where ground telescopes cannot see.

[long-pause]

{img:spacecraft-approaching-small-asteroid-quiet-space}

And there is something else, which would have astonished the people of 1908.

[pause]

We have begun to test ways of moving an asteroid out of the way.

[long-pause]

In September of 2022, a small spacecraft named DART, built by the American space agency, travelled for ten months through space, and met a tiny asteroid called Dimorphos.

[pause]

Dimorphos is a little moon, about a hundred and sixty metres wide, that circles a larger asteroid, called Didymos.

[pause]

Neither of them was a danger to the Earth.

[pause]

They were chosen because they made a good test.

[long-pause]

The spacecraft was not large, about the size of a vending machine.

[pause]

It travelled all that way, and then, on purpose, flew straight into the little moon, at a speed of around six kilometres a second.

[pause]

It was a gentle experiment, in a sense, because it was done in complete safety, a very long way from home.

[long-pause]

Telescopes on Earth watched the result.

[pause]

They saw that the little moon, after the impact, took slightly less time to circle its partner.

[pause]

Before, it had taken about eleven hours and fifty-five minutes.

[pause]

Afterwards, it took about thirty-two minutes less.

[long-pause]

{img:telescopes-watching-dimorphos-night-observatory}

It was a small change, but it was exactly what the scientists had hoped for.

[pause]

It showed that, if we find a dangerous asteroid early enough, with years of warning, we can nudge it gently, so that it misses us.

[pause]

For the first time in history, human beings had changed the motion of a natural body in space.

[long-pause]

{img:small-probe-in-orbit-over-asteroid-surface}

The story continues.

[long-pause]

A second spacecraft, built by the European Space Agency, and called Hera, was launched in October of 2024, and is travelling to the same pair of asteroids, to study the place of the impact in close detail.

[pause]

It will measure the size and the shape of the crater, and the makeup of the little moon, and so help us understand exactly what happened.

[pause]

Scientists want to know everything they can, so that, if the day ever comes, they will be ready.

[long-pause]

Meanwhile, astronomers have been keeping a friendly eye on another asteroid, named Apophis.

[pause]

It is about three hundred and forty metres wide, and in the year 2004, it was briefly thought to have a small chance of hitting the Earth.

[pause]

Careful measurements since then have shown that it will not.

[long-pause]

On the thirteenth of April, in the year 2029, Apophis will pass close by our planet, closer than many of our satellites, at a distance of about thirty-two thousand kilometres.

[pause]

It will be bright enough to see with the naked eye, a small, quick star, moving across the sky, in the evening, over Europe and Africa.

[pause]

Millions of people will be able to look up, and watch it.

[long-pause]

It is a lovely thing to think about.

[pause]

An asteroid, passing safely by, in the dark, on a spring evening, while ordinary people look up and wonder.

[pause]

It is one of those visits that remind us we are part of a great and gentle solar system.

[long-pause]

{img:night-sky-family-looking-up-hillside-stars}

There is even a special day, now, to remember all this.

[long-pause]

In the year 2016, the United Nations declared the thirtieth of June to be International Asteroid Day.

[pause]

It was chosen because it is the anniversary of the Tunguska event.

[pause]

Each year, on that day, schools and museums and observatories around the world hold talks, and events, and quiet evenings of watching the sky.

[long-pause]

It is a day to learn about asteroids, and about how we can watch for them.

[pause]

It is a day to remember that the sky is not just a distant roof, but a part of our home.

[pause]

And it is a day to give thanks, a little, for all the people who spend their nights looking at faint dots of light, so that the rest of us can sleep.

[long-pause]

When you think about it, that is a rather beautiful job.

[pause]

Somewhere, tonight, an astronomer is sitting in a small, quiet room, in front of a screen, watching the stars move, with a cup of tea in her hand.

[pause]

She is looking for something that is very probably not there.

[pause]

And if it is there, she will notice it, and write it down, and tell the world.

[long-pause]

So tonight, we can rest a little easier.

[pause]

The Tunguska event was a wonder, and a warning, and also a kind of gift.

[pause]

It taught us to look up, and it taught us to care for the world we share.

[long-pause]

{img:tunguska-forest-today-green-regrowth-fallen-trunks}

Let us go back, now, to the forest, and see how it looks today.

[long-pause]

More than a hundred years have passed.

[pause]

If you were to walk in the area beneath the explosion now, you would find that it has changed a great deal.

[pause]

The forest has grown back.

[long-pause]

Where the great trees fell, new trees have risen, larches and birches and spruces, tall and strong.

The young trees grew up between the old fallen trunks, and over the years the old ones rotted slowly into the ground, softened by moss and ferns.

[pause]

Nature has a great power of healing.

Given time, and a little patience, a forest can grow back from almost anything.

The Tunguska Nature Reserve has a small base, with wooden buildings, where researchers stay, and where, now and then, a few travellers are welcomed.

In the village of Vanavara, too, life goes on.
