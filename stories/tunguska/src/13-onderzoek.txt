In 1958, a new expedition set out for the Tunguska forest.

It was led by a geochemist named Kirill Florensky, and it was the first of many that would be organised by the Academy of Sciences in the following years.
The team was larger than Kulik's had been, and better equipped, with trucks, radios, and modern instruments.
They were not looking for a big stone.
They were looking for small clues.

They made maps with great care.
They studied the fallen trees, tree by tree, and measured the directions in which they lay.
They collected soil, and sifted it, and looked at it under microscopes.
They took samples of the peat, and of the resin in the trees, and of the water in the little pools.

In the soil, they found something remarkable.
Mixed into the top layers of the ground, in the area of the explosion, there were tiny round particles, like microscopic beads, made of metal and glass.
They were smaller than grains of sand.
Some were made of iron and nickel, and some of silicate, a kind of glassy stone.

These beads, called spherules, are formed when a body from space melts in the air and breaks into droplets, which cool and fall as a fine rain of tiny balls.
They are typical of meteoritic dust.
Their presence in the Tunguska soil was strong evidence that the object had indeed been a natural body from space, and that it had broken up high in the atmosphere, scattering its remains over the landscape.

It was a very satisfying discovery.
And it was only the beginning.

Over the following decades, many more studies were carried out.

Scientists looked at the rings of trees that had survived the blast, and found that the rings from the year 1908 told a story.
The trees that were still standing, after the event, had grown more slowly in the next few years, as if they had been shocked.
But some of them, after a period of recovery, grew faster than before, perhaps because the surrounding trees had been cleared away, and there was more light and space.
Rings are like a diary, written in wood.

{img:peat-bog-core-sample-researcher-hands}
They studied the peat in the bogs, which laid down a layer each year, like pages in a book.
In the layer for 1908, they found small changes in the chemistry, which fit with the dust of a meteoroid, and with a short rise in the amount of certain elements.
They even found traces of the same elements in ice cores from the polar regions, from the year 1908, thousands of kilometres away.

They studied the fallen trees, and worked out, from the angle at which the trunks lay, and from how the bark was scorched, the exact place where the explosion had happened.
They found that the blast had come from a point about eight kilometres above the ground, though newer studies give a range of between five and ten.

They collected more accounts, and compared them with the measurements, and slowly the whole picture came together.
It was patient work, done in the summers, in the mud and the mosquitoes, by dozens of people.
In the winters, they sat in their offices, in the cities, and studied the numbers.
And it all added up.

{img:laboratory-microscope-tiny-silver-spheres}
By the end of the twentieth century, the story had been pieced together with much more care.

The object had come in at a shallow angle, from the south-east, travelling at a speed of perhaps fifteen to thirty kilometres a second.
It was most likely about fifty to sixty metres across, roughly the size of a large building, though some estimates are smaller or larger.
It exploded about five to ten kilometres above the forest, releasing energy of about ten to fifteen megatons.

It flattened some eighty million trees over an area of about two thousand one hundred and fifty square kilometres.
It threw out heat, and a shock wave, and a roar that was heard hundreds of kilometres away.
It set some of the forest on fire, in places, though the fires did not spread widely.
And it left no great crater, only a landscape of fallen trees, tiny beads, and a quiet that lasted a very long time.

All of this was learned by people who walked through that forest, sat beside those bogs, and looked closely at things that most would have passed by.
It is a good example of how science works, with slow, careful, modest steps.

And still, some questions remain open.
