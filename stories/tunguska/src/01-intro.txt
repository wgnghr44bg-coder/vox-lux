Close your eyes if you'd like.

Tonight, we are going north and east, to a very big and very quiet place.
It is a place of tall trees, slow rivers, and long, pale summer evenings.
It is the great forest of Siberia, which people call the taiga.

We are going back more than a hundred years, to the summer of 1908.
And we are going to listen to the story of one calm summer morning in that forest, a morning that nobody who lived through it ever forgot.

It is a story about the sky.
It is a story about a sudden light, and a great and distant sound.
It is a story about a forest, and the people who live in it, and the scientists who came, years later, to ask what had happened.

But tonight, we are not here to be frightened.
We are here to walk slowly through the trees, to look up at the sky, and to listen.

You don't need to remember any of this.
You don't need to follow every name, or every date.
Just let the story drift past you, the way a slow river drifts through a quiet forest.

If you fall asleep before the end, that's alright.
The forest has kept this story for a very long time.
It can keep it a little longer.

Let us begin with the place itself.

Siberia is enormous.
It stretches across the top of Asia, from the Ural Mountains in the west all the way to the Pacific Ocean in the east.
It is so large that you could set the whole of Europe down inside it, and still have room to spare.

Most of Siberia is covered by forest.
It is the largest forest on our planet, a green belt that runs for thousands of kilometres.
Seen from far above, it looks like a deep, dark, soft carpet that goes on and on, with no end in sight.

The people who live there have a simple word for it.
They call it the taiga.

The taiga is made mostly of trees that like the cold.
There are larches, which are strange and lovely, because they are cones that shed their needles every autumn, and turn a soft golden colour before they do.
There are tall spruces, and firs, and pines, with long needles that smell sweet and sharp in the sun.
And along the rivers, and in the places where the light can reach the ground, there are birches, with their pale papery bark, and their small leaves that tremble in even the faintest breeze.

Walk into the taiga in summer, and the first thing you notice is the quiet.
It is not a silence, exactly.
There is the hum of insects, and the whisper of wind high in the needles, and now and then the call of a bird.
But it is a wide and gentle sound, the sound of a very big place that is not in any hurry.

The ground is soft under your feet.
It is covered in moss, and in low green plants, and in cranberries and bilberries that ripen in the late summer.
In some places the moss is so deep and so springy that every step feels like walking on a mattress.

And under the moss, not very far down, the earth is frozen.
In much of Siberia the ground stays frozen all year round, summer and winter, for hundreds of metres deep.
People call this permafrost.
It is like a great cold sleeper, lying under the forest, keeping the water in the soil from sinking away.

That is why, in the summer, so much of the taiga is dotted with small lakes, and wet places, and bogs.
The warm sun thaws the top of the ground a little, but the water has nowhere to go, because the frozen earth lies just below.
So it gathers in the hollows, and shines there in the light, like pieces of the sky that have fallen among the trees.

Through this forest, long ago, the rivers carved their roads.

The great rivers of central Siberia flow north, towards the Arctic Ocean.
Among them is the Yenisei, one of the longest and widest rivers in the world.
It begins in the mountains near Mongolia, and flows for thousands of kilometres, past towns and villages and wide empty shores, until at last it reaches the icy northern sea.

The Yenisei has many helpers.
There are smaller rivers that bring their waters to it from the east and from the west.
One of the helpers, coming in from the east, is a long and winding river called the Tunguska.

In fact there are three Tunguskas.
There is the Lower Tunguska, the Upper Tunguska, and the one that matters for our story, the Stony Tunguska.
In Russian, it is called the Podkamennaya Tunguska.
The name means something like, the Tunguska that flows beneath the rocks.

The Stony Tunguska is a quiet river with many bends.
It runs through low hills and thick forest, over shallow places and deep pools, past rocks and gravel banks.
In the summer, its water is clear and cool, and in the long evenings it turns the colours of the sky, pink, and gold, and pale blue.

In the winter, it freezes so thickly that people can travel along it on sledges, as if it were a white road laid through the trees.

Along the Stony Tunguska, and in the forest around it, there are very few towns.
There are small settlements, and trading places, and camps.
Many of the people who live here have lived here for a very long time, because their families have lived here for hundreds of years.

And it is in this region, far from any city, far from any railway station, in the deep green quiet of central Siberia, that our story takes place.
