For many years, the forest was left alone.

The seasons turned.
The summer of 1908 ended, and the larches turned gold, and dropped their needles.
The first snow came, and the rivers froze, and the long Siberian winter closed over the land.
Then the spring came again, and the ice cracked, and the river ran.

In the part of the forest beneath the explosion, something very strange was happening.
The trees that had been knocked down lay where they had fallen.
Over the years, the moss grew over them, and the rain softened their bark.
New seedlings sprouted among them, small and green.
The animals came back.
Birds nested in the broken branches.

It was a landscape that nobody visited.
The Evenki, who knew the forest best, kept away from it, as we have said.
The few Russian settlers did not go that way, because there was no reason to.
It was a long, hard, wet journey, through swamp and thicket, to a place where there was nothing to hunt, and little to gather.

And the rest of the world, as we have seen, did not know.
It had other things to think about.

{img:european-city-1914-crowd-summer-morning}
The years that followed were hard ones in that part of the world.

In 1914, a great war began in Europe.
It was a war that drew in many countries, and lasted for four years, and changed everything.
Russia was deeply involved, and the whole country was shaken.
In 1917, there was a revolution.
The Tsar was no longer in power.
A new government, and then another, took charge, and there followed a long and troubled period of change, struggle, and rebuilding.

In those years, almost no one had the time or the means to go looking for a meteorite in the far north.
Scientists were busy with other things, or had left, or had found their work interrupted.
Many of the old records were lost.

But a few people remembered.
In Siberian villages along the great rivers, the old story of the morning of the great fire in the sky was told and retold.
Families would say, on certain summer evenings, "do you remember the day the sky split?"
The older people would nod, and begin to talk.

And in some places, a few people wrote things down.
Teachers, and local officials, and curious travellers, took notes, and put them in little notebooks and folders.
They asked questions of those who had been there, and wrote down what they were told.
It was patient, humble work, and it turned out to be extremely valuable, because it kept the memory safe.

{img:small-school-classroom-siberian-village-lamp}
One thing that the early notebooks make clear is how different the stories were.

Some witnesses said that the fiery object had moved from the south to the north.
Others said that it had come from the east, or the south-east.
Some said there had been a single great flash.
Some said there had been two or three loud bangs, spread over a minute or so.
Some said the sound was like thunder.
Others said it was like gunfire.
Some even said that the sky had turned red.

This is not surprising.
People were in different places, and saw different parts of the sky.
They were frightened, or startled, or were looking in another direction.
And over the years, as they told their story, it grew and changed, as stories do.

But if you collect many of them, and compare them carefully, as the scientists later did, a pattern appears.
It tells you the direction the object was travelling, and how steeply it came down, and roughly how high it was when it burst.
It is like a puzzle, in which each person has one small piece.

That work took years, and it was begun by a man whose name is now known to everyone who studies the Tunguska event.
His name was Leonid Kulik.
