At about a quarter past seven in the morning, local time, the first people saw it.

Those who looked up at that moment saw a streak of bright light moving across the sky.
Some described it as a brilliant, blue-white column, as bright as the sun, or even brighter.
Others said it looked like a flying fire, or a long, glowing pipe, or a sheet of light that grew larger as it came.

It seemed to move from the south towards the north, over a very wide area.
People who saw it from more than a hundred kilometres away, in different villages and camps along the river, all described something similar, a bright body, a long tail of light, and a feeling that the whole sky was changing.

Some people said they saw it clearly, and that the brightness hurt their eyes.
Others only noticed that the light of the morning suddenly seemed very strange.
Some heard a low rumbling, like thunder, even before anything else happened.

It is very hard to know, from this distance, exactly what each person saw.
These accounts were written down later, and in some cases many years later.
But there are many of them, from many different places, and they tell a similar tale.
Something very bright crossed the sky.

{img:bright-sky-over-river-valley-midmorning}
Then, over the forest to the north of Vanavara, there was a flash.

Scientists now believe that the object, somewhere between five and ten kilometres above the ground, reached the point where the pressure of the air on its front became too great to bear.
It broke apart.
In a fraction of a second, the whole of its energy, which had been stored in its great speed, was released into the air.

The energy was enormous.
Most estimates say that it was equal to the explosion of between ten and fifteen million tonnes of ordinary explosive, though some researchers think it was a little less, and some a little more.
It is a very large number, and it is hard to picture.
It was about a thousand times more energy than the first atomic bomb, though we will not talk further about that tonight.
It is enough to say that it was one of the biggest natural blasts that people have ever recorded.

The explosion happened in the air, well above the ground.
There was a huge ball of heat, glowing and expanding, for a moment brighter than the sun, high above the forest.
It sent out a wave of pressure in every direction, rolling outward through the air, like the ripple that spreads when a stone is thrown into a still pond.

This wave was invisible, but its effects were very real.
And because it began high above the trees, it reached the ground over a wide circle, and pressed down on the forest like an enormous hand.

Now let us go back to the farmer on the porch.

His name was Semyon Semyonov.
He was a peasant farmer, and he was sitting on the steps of a trading post in Vanavara, about sixty kilometres from the place beneath the explosion.
It was a bright morning, and he was relaxing, perhaps waiting for a friend, perhaps simply enjoying the sun.

Years later, he described what he remembered to the scientists who came to ask.
We should say that he spoke years after the event, and that memory changes with time.
But his account has been told and retold, and it is one of the most important we have.

He remembered that, suddenly, he saw a great light in the north.
The sky seemed to split in two, and a sheet of fire appeared, high above the forest, and spread wide across the northern horizon.

At that moment, he felt a heat on his skin.
It was so strong that he said it seemed as if his shirt had caught fire.
He wanted to tear it off, and throw it away.
But just then, the sky seemed to close up again, and there was a loud thump.

The blast wave arrived.
It threw him from the porch, a few metres across the ground, and for a moment he lost his senses.

His wife ran out and helped him back to the house.
And then came the sound.
It was a great crashing, as if rocks were falling from the sky, or as if cannons were being fired all around.
The earth shook beneath them.
He lay on the ground, and held his head, afraid that stones might fall on it.

Afterwards, a hot wind blew between the houses, like the breath from a great cannon.
It left marks on the ground, like paths, and it flattened parts of the barley crop.
Many windows in the village were broken.
The iron lock on a barn door was snapped in two.

That was what happened to a man, sixty kilometres away.
By the grace of distance, he was unhurt, apart from a bruise or two, and a great fright.

{img:horses-and-houses-vanavara-after-wind}
All around Vanavara, people felt the same things.

There was a flash.
There was a wave of heat.
There was a roar.
And then there was a strong shaking of the ground, and gusts of wind, which shook the houses, and rattled the dishes in the cupboards.

Horses panicked.
Cattle bolted.
Dogs ran for cover.
Many people, up and down the river valley, ran out of their houses, thinking that a storm had come, or that the world was ending.

In the next few minutes, as the dust settled and the sounds died away, the strange thing was how quickly the world went back to a kind of quiet.
The sky was clear again.
The birds were silent for a while.
But the sun was still shining, and the river was still flowing, and the forest to the north was hidden behind a very high, slowly spreading column of smoke and dust.

People in Vanavara talked about it all day.
They told each other what they had seen and heard.
Some believed it was an earthquake.
Some thought a great thunderstorm had passed.
Some may have wondered about a volcano, though there are none anywhere near.
Others had no idea at all, and simply shook their heads, and set about repairing what was broken.

In those days, there was no one to ask.
There was no scientist in Vanavara.
There was no radio station to report the news.
There was only the quiet, and the question, and the long summer day still stretching ahead.

Further north, closer to the centre, there were Evenki families in their summer camps.

They were closer than the villagers, and they felt the blast more strongly.
We must be gentle in telling this part, because it is a place where the accounts are less clear, and where some of the stories were told many years later, and shaped by many retellings.

What we know is this.
A number of the Evenki reported that they had been thrown to the ground by the wave of air.
Their chums, their tents, were blown down, or lifted, or scattered.
Reindeer ran off in fright, and in some places a number of the animals were lost.
A few people were hurt, mostly by falling, or by being thrown.
But there are no certain records that anyone was killed, which is a remarkable thing, given the size of the event, and one that is often told as a kind of small miracle.

Because the explosion was in the air, and not on the ground, and because the region was so thinly inhabited, the harm to people was far smaller than it might have been.
The forest took the force of it.

In the days that followed, the families who lived in the area gathered what they could, and looked for their reindeer, and made their camps anew.
Many of them left that part of the forest, and for a long time, they avoided it.
It was no longer the same place.
It had been touched by something large, and strange, and powerful, and it seemed wise to leave it alone.

And above it all, as the morning went on, the great cloud of dust that the explosion had thrown up was beginning to drift, slowly, high in the air, to the east, and to the west, and to the north.
