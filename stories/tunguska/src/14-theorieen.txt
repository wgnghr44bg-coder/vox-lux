One of the biggest questions is what the object actually was.

For a long time, scientists have disagreed.
There have been two main ideas.
Both are about natural objects, and both fit much of the evidence.
Let us look at them calmly, one by one.

The first idea is that it was a comet.
Remember that a comet is a ball of ice and dust, a bit like a very large, loose snowball.
If a small comet entered the atmosphere, it would be heated rapidly, and the ice would turn to steam.
The whole thing would blow apart in the air, in a great burst, leaving no stony remains.

This idea fits several of the facts.
It explains why no large fragments were found, because ice melts, and the dust spreads away.
It might explain the bright night skies, because a comet carries a lot of water, and the water would have gone high into the sky, to form clouds that shine.
This idea was suggested in the 1930s by the British scientist who studied the barograph records, and by others.

But there are difficulties.
A comet is very fragile.
It would probably break up much higher in the atmosphere, long before reaching the height where the explosion took place.
Some scientists think a comet would not penetrate deeply enough.
And the tiny spherules found in the soil seem to point to rock and metal, not ice.

{img:rocky-asteroid-in-space-quiet-stars}
The second idea is that it was a stony asteroid.

An asteroid is a piece of rock, and it is much stronger than a comet.
If one of about fifty metres entered the air, it would plunge much deeper into the atmosphere before breaking up.
It would then burst at a height of a few kilometres, with a shockwave strong enough to knock down trees, just as was seen in the forest.

This idea fits the tiny beads of metal and glass.
It also fits the calculations of how deep the object went before bursting.
Many scientists today favour it, and a number of detailed studies, using computers, point the same way.

It also explains why no large pieces were found.
In the explosion, the stone was shattered into countless small fragments, and most of the material was turned into very fine dust, or vapour, and spread through the air.
Only a small portion may have landed in the form of tiny particles.

So today, most researchers think it was probably a stony asteroid that exploded in the air.
But there is no single piece of evidence that settles the matter entirely, and some scientists still think a comet is possible.

In 2007, a team of Italian scientists from the University of Bologna suggested something new.

They had been studying a small lake in the Tunguska region, called Lake Cheko.
It lies about eight kilometres north-west of the centre of the fallen forest, and it is a small, almost perfectly oval lake, about five hundred metres across.
It is quiet and deep, and ringed with trees.

The Italian scientists proposed that the lake might be a crater, made by a fragment of the object that had survived and fallen to the ground in 1908.
They studied the shape of the lake, and the layers of mud on its floor, and they found some features that seemed to fit.

It was an exciting idea.
If true, it would mean that a piece of the object had been found, hidden beneath the water, for all those years.

Other scientists, however, were not convinced.
Some argued that the lake was much older than 1908, and pointed to the age of the sediments, and to the shape of the basin.
Others said it was simply one of many small lakes in the region.
Studies since have gone back and forth.

At the time of this recording, the question of Lake Cheko has not been settled.
It may be a crater, and it may not.
It is one of those quiet puzzles that are left for people in the future to solve, perhaps with better drills, and a more careful look at the mud.

There have also been many wilder theories.

Over the years, people have suggested that the explosion was caused by a tiny black hole, passing through the Earth.
Some said it was a lump of antimatter.
Some said it was a burst of gas from deep underground.
Some said that the great inventor Nikola Tesla had done it with an experiment, though this is simply not possible, since Tesla's machines could not do anything remotely like it.
And some, as we have said, have suggested that it was a spaceship.

None of these ideas is supported by evidence.
Most are rather like fairy stories, told for fun on long winter nights.
They are not harmful, and they are part of the charm of the Tunguska story, but they are not what the scientists think happened.

It is good to remember that a mystery does not have to be a miracle.
Something can be wonderful, and strange, and still have a natural explanation.
In fact, the true explanation, an object from space, no bigger than a block of flats, travelling fast enough to cross a continent in minutes, and bursting in the sky with the energy of millions of tonnes of explosive, is every bit as marvellous as anything made up.

And the best part is that we are still learning.
