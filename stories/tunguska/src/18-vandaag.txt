Let us go back, now, to the forest, and see how it looks today.

More than a hundred years have passed.
If you were to walk in the area beneath the explosion now, you would find that it has changed a great deal.
The forest has grown back.

Where the great trees fell, new trees have risen, larches and birches and spruces, tall and strong.
The young trees grew up between the old fallen trunks, and over the years the old ones rotted slowly into the ground, softened by moss and ferns.
In places, you can still see them, lying in the same direction, grey and silvery, half hidden in the green.

The ground is covered with a thick carpet of moss, and cranberries, and small white flowers.
In the summer, the air hums with insects.
Birds sing in the branches.
Small animals go about their business, among the roots and the fallen logs.
Now and then, a deer, or an elk, steps out of the trees, and stands still, listening.

It is a peaceful place.
It is hard to believe that, once, it was a place of fire and roaring wind.
Nature has a great power of healing.
Given time, and a little patience, a forest can grow back from almost anything.

{img:researchers-walking-boardwalk-through-forest}
Visitors do come, though not many.

The Tunguska Nature Reserve has a small base, with wooden buildings, where researchers stay, and where, now and then, a few travellers are welcomed.
The journey is still not easy.
It takes some days from the nearest big town, along rough roads and rivers, and one has to go with a guide.

In the summer, small groups of scientists, and students, and enthusiasts walk through the forest, following narrow paths and wooden walkways laid over the marshy ground.
They pass the old fallen trees, and the little thaw hollows that Kulik once took for craters.
They stand quietly at the foot of the hills, and look out over the green.

Some of them leave small signs, and remembrances.
At certain points, there are simple wooden markers, put up by earlier expeditions, with names and dates.
Here and there, the names of the people who worked in the area are remembered.
In the evenings, the visitors sit together in the cabins, and drink tea, and talk about what they have seen.

It is a modest kind of pilgrimage.
There are no grand buildings, and no crowds.
Only the quiet forest, and the sky, and the people who come to listen.

{img:evenki-family-reindeer-summer-meadow-today}
And the Evenki are still here.

Some of them live in the villages, along the rivers, in wooden houses with electric lights, and a satellite dish, and a snowmobile in the yard.
Others keep their old way of life, with reindeer, in the forest, in the summer, as their grandparents did.
Many families combine both, living in a village in the winter, and moving out to the forest in the warm season.

Their language is still spoken, though it is less common than it used to be, and many people are working to teach it to the children.
Their songs, and their stories, and their crafts, such as beadwork and fur sewing, are still treasured.
The memory of the morning of the thirtieth of June is still part of the family history in some of those families.
Grandparents tell the young ones what their own grandparents told them, about a flash, and a roar, and a day that was never forgotten.

It is a good thing to remember that this is not a story about an empty place.
It is a story about a place that has always been lived in, by people who know the land well, and who care for it.
They were here before the event.
They are here now.
They will, we hope, be here long into the future.

{img:vanavara-village-evening-quiet-street-today}
In the village of Vanavara, too, life goes on.

It is still a small place, with wooden houses, and gardens, and a few shops.
There is a school, and a little museum, where visitors can learn about the event, and see photographs and old maps.
There are children who go to school there, and farmers who keep cows, and grandmothers who sit on benches in the evening sun.

Sometimes, in the summer, a visitor will stop at the house where the farmer from our story once lived, or at least at the place where it was.
They will stand at the porch, and look to the north, where the forest begins.
It is just a quiet porch, on a quiet street, in the warm light of a northern evening.

And it is easy to imagine a man sitting there, once, on a bright summer morning, with his cup of tea, looking up at the sky.
He had no idea what was about to happen.
He was only a farmer, in a small village, on an ordinary day.
But because of him, and others like him, who told what they saw, we know what happened on that morning.
That is something worth remembering.
