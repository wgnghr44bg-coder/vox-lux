Far away from the forest, in other parts of the world, instruments were quietly noticing that something had happened.

In 1908, scientists had two useful kinds of machine for this.
The first was the seismograph.
A seismograph is a very sensitive instrument that records the shaking of the ground.
In its simplest form, it has a heavy weight hanging from a spring, so that when the ground moves, the weight stays still, and a pen attached to it draws a line on a slowly turning drum of paper.

On the calmest days, the line is almost flat.
When the earth shakes, the line begins to dance.

On the thirtieth of June, in the city of Irkutsk, about nine hundred kilometres to the south-east, a seismograph in an observatory recorded a series of small tremors.
The people there noticed the marks on the paper, and wrote them down in their records.
They were not large, but they were clear, and they were unusual.
They matched no earthquake that anyone knew of.

Other seismographs, farther away, also recorded faint signals.
In some stations in Europe, tiny ripples appeared on the paper, arriving a little after the time of the event.
At that time, no one connected them with Siberia.
They were simply part of the great quiet record of a restless planet.

{img:barograph-drum-paper-english-observatory}
The second instrument was the barograph.
A barograph records the pressure of the air.

It has a thin, sealed metal box, which swells slightly when the pressure is high, and shrinks when it is low.
A long arm connected to this box moves a pen across a slowly turning roll of paper, and so, hour by hour, day by day, it draws a gentle line that rises and falls with the weather.
In 1908, many weather stations around the world had barographs, and kept careful records of them.

Some years afterwards, a British scientist named Francis Whipple, who was the director of an observatory at Kew, near London, looked back through the records from that day.
He found something rather wonderful.
On the thirtieth of June, 1908, several barographs in England had recorded a tiny, quick bump in the pressure, a little blip on the line, which came in the afternoon.

It was very small.
It was far too small to be noticed by anyone in the street.
But it was clear, and the timing made sense.
It was the pressure wave from the explosion in Siberia, travelling through the air around the world, and reaching England, many hours later.

Think of that for a moment.
An enormous sound, made in a Siberian forest in the morning, had spread out across the sky, as a very low and slow ripple, and had passed over Britain, and over the quiet English gardens, in the afternoon.
Nobody heard it.
But the instruments felt it, and wrote it down.

Meanwhile, back in Siberia, news was beginning to move, but slowly.

In the days after the event, a few of the local newspapers printed short reports.
Some told about the bright flash and the loud sounds, and about the shaking of the ground.
There was a newspaper in Irkutsk, and there was one in Krasnoyarsk, and in them, a small number of people wrote about what they had seen.

One report, from a town to the south, described a great ball of fire moving in the sky, and a sound like gunfire.
Another described how a train driver on the new railway had stopped his train, because he thought the ground was shaking, or that something had gone wrong with the track.
We cannot be sure about each of these small stories, but together they give a sense of how widely the event was noticed, along the whole length of the great railway, and across a huge region.

But then, as the days passed, the reports faded.
No one in the towns knew what had happened in the northern forest.
They knew that the sky had flashed, and the earth had trembled, and that something had come down somewhere.
They guessed that it was a meteorite, a large one, and that it had landed in some distant place.
And then they went back to their work.

There was a great deal else going on.
There were harvests to bring in, and bills to pay, and a whole political world to worry about.
Nobody was in a position to send an expedition into the Siberian wilderness, to look for a stone.

It is worth pausing, here, to wonder at this.

Nowadays, if an object exploded over a forest, thousands of people would film it with their phones.
Satellites would pick it up within seconds.
Scientists would be on the radio within the hour.
Aeroplanes would fly over the spot the next day.

In 1908, none of this existed.
The event was a great mystery, in a place with no roads, no telephones, and almost no witnesses.
It took the world nearly twenty years to find out what had really happened.

And yet the world did notice something, in its own gentle way.
Because in the days after the thirtieth of June, far to the west, over the cities of Europe, a strange thing began to happen to the sky.
