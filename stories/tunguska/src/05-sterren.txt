Let us look up at the sky for a little while, and think about where the thing came from.

Our solar system is not as empty as it looks.
Out beyond the sun and its planets, beyond even the farthest of them, there are countless small bodies, made of rock, and metal, and ice, all travelling quietly in their own long orbits.
Most of them have been circling in the dark for more than four and a half billion years, since the planets themselves were formed.

Between Mars and Jupiter, there is a wide belt of rocky pieces, called the asteroid belt.
Some of them are tiny, like pebbles.
A few are vast, hundreds of kilometres across.
Most travel peacefully in the belt for ages.

But now and then, a gentle tug from a planet, or a small nudge from a collision between two rocks, sends one of them on a new path.
Over millions of years, some of these wanderers drift inward, towards the sun, and cross the path of the Earth.

Comets live farther out.
They are made of ice and dust, like great dirty snowballs, and they spend most of their lives in the deep cold, a long way from the sun.
When one of them swings in close, the sunlight warms it, and its ice turns to gas, and streams out behind it in a shining tail.
That is the beautiful sight that people have always loved to watch.

The Earth, meanwhile, moves on its own track.
It travels around the sun at about thirty kilometres every second.
That is so fast that it is hard to imagine, and yet we never feel it, because everything around us is moving at the same speed.

So when a small rock from space meets our planet, it meets it at great speed.
Not at the speed of a car, or even of a jet plane, but at tens of thousands of kilometres an hour.
It is this speed that makes the difference.

{img:meteor-streak-over-mountains-dawn}
When a meteoroid, which is the name for such a piece of rock or metal in space, enters the Earth's atmosphere, something remarkable happens.

The air in front of it cannot get out of the way quickly enough.
It is squeezed, and squeezed, and as it is squeezed it grows extremely hot.
The rock does not burn, exactly, in the way that wood burns.
Instead, the air in front of it becomes a glowing cushion of heat, and this heat strips the surface of the rock away, layer by layer, in a shower of sparks.

That is what we see as a shooting star, a thin streak of light across a night sky.
Most shooting stars are caused by tiny grains, no bigger than a grain of sand, which burn away completely, high above our heads, in less than a second.

Larger pieces make brighter streaks.
A piece the size of a fist can make a brilliant flash, called a fireball, which may light up the whole landscape for a moment.
A piece the size of a car can make a fireball bright enough to be seen from hundreds of kilometres away, and it can end with a rumble that rolls across the countryside, like far-off thunder.

Sometimes the object breaks apart.
The great pressure of the air builds up on one side, and the stone, which may be full of tiny cracks, splits, and the pieces spread out.
Then the heat and pressure grow even more suddenly.
The whole thing can end in an enormous burst of energy, high in the sky.
This is called an airburst.

Meteorites, the pieces that reach the ground, are what is left over.
Often they are small.
Sometimes, though, a large body will survive much of the way down, and make a crater where it lands.
There is a famous one in the desert of Arizona, a bowl in the ground more than a kilometre wide, made about fifty thousand years ago.

An airburst is different.
In an airburst, the object does not need to land at all.
It does its work in the air.

For many years, people had very little idea how often such things happened.

Throughout history, ordinary people have occasionally seen great lights in the sky.
There are records, in many cultures, of fireballs, and loud bangs, and showers of stones.
Some of these records are very old.
In one case, a stone that fell in a field near the town of Ensisheim, in what is now eastern France, in the year 1492, was carried to a church and kept there for centuries.

But before the twentieth century, scientists were not sure that stones really fell from the sky at all.
It was only in the early 1800s that this idea became fully accepted.
Before that, many learned people thought that stories of falling stones were simply country legends.

By 1908, the idea of meteorites was well understood.
But nobody knew how large an object could be, or what would happen if a big one came in over a populated area.
Nobody had ever recorded it.
It was a possibility that scientists could imagine, but that nobody had seen.

That was about to change, in a place that no scientist had thought to watch.

There is something rather lovely about this.
The sky is very big, and the Earth is mostly empty.
Most of its surface is ocean, and much of the rest is forest or desert or ice.
If something were to arrive from space, it would most likely fall where nobody would see it.

And on the morning of the thirtieth of June, that is more or less what happened.
The object came in over a thinly settled, forested region of central Siberia, where only a few hundred people lived within a day's journey.
Had it arrived a few hours earlier or later, the Earth would have turned, and the same object might have crossed over the sea, or a different land.
But the world was in just this position, at just this hour, and so the story belongs to the taiga.

{img:wide-taiga-sunrise-clear-sky}
So here is how that morning began.

It was the thirtieth of June.
According to the old calendar, which Russia still used at that time, the date was the seventeenth of June.
That is a small detail, but it explains why you will sometimes read two different dates in old accounts.

In the central Siberian forest, the sun had risen early.
It was a fine, still, clear morning.
The sky, over the Stony Tunguska river, was a pale and cloudless blue.

The air was already warm.
There was a light haze over the river, rising slowly, as the sun heated the water.
The reindeer were up, and some of the Evenki families were already moving about their camps, tending fires, and checking their animals.
In Vanavara, the farmers and traders were beginning the day.

Everything was ordinary.
Everything was as it should be.

Somewhere above them, high up and a very long way off, a body from space was already falling towards the Earth.
It was moving at an astonishing speed, many kilometres every second, and it was coming in from the south-east, at a fairly shallow angle, crossing the sky above Siberia from one side to the other.

It had been travelling through the dark for a very long time, perhaps for millions of years, and in a few moments, its journey would end.
