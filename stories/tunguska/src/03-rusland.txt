In the year 1908, Russia was a vast empire.

It stretched from the Baltic Sea in the west to the Pacific Ocean in the east, across eleven time zones, and it was ruled by an emperor, called the Tsar.
His name was Nicholas the Second.
He lived in a palace near the capital, Saint Petersburg, a city of canals and bridges and pale pastel-coloured buildings, a very long way from the Siberian forest.

For a long time, getting from Saint Petersburg to the far east of the empire had been very difficult.
Travellers had to go by horse and cart, or by boat along the rivers, or by sledge in the winter.
A journey across Siberia could take many months, and in the spring and the autumn, when the roads turned to mud, it could take longer still.

But a great change had come.
In the 1890s, the Russian government began to build a railway across the whole of Siberia.
It was called the Trans-Siberian Railway, and it became one of the great building projects of its age.

Thousands of workers laid track through forest and marsh and over wide rivers.
They built bridges, and stations, and water towers, and little wooden towns along the way.
By around 1904, the line was open, and you could travel by train all the way from the west of Russia to the shores of the Pacific.

For the first time, Siberia felt a little closer.
The trains brought goods and letters and newspapers.
They also brought people.

{img:siberian-village-wooden-houses-street}
In the years after the railway opened, many thousands of families moved to Siberia from the west.
They were farmers, mostly, looking for land.
The government encouraged them, and offered them help, because there was so much empty space and so much fertile soil.

They built small villages of wooden houses, with carved window frames, and fences, and vegetable gardens.
They grew wheat and rye and potatoes.
They kept cows and chickens and horses.
In the evenings, they sat on their porches and watched the long sunsets, and in the winters, they sat close to their great brick stoves, and told stories, and mended tools.

One of the railway's stops was a town called Krasnoyarsk, on the Yenisei River.
It was a busy trading town, with merchants and sailors and craftsmen.
Not far to the east was another railway place called Kansk, and further along the line was Taishet, and then the big town of Irkutsk, near the shining blue waters of Lake Baikal.

From the railway, there were no more rails going north.
If you wanted to go into the deep forest, to the country of the Stony Tunguska, you had to leave the train, and take a rough track, or a river, and go on slowly by horse, or by boat, or on foot.

It was a journey of several hundred kilometres.
And at the end of it, you would find a very small place indeed.

{img:vanavara-trading-post-river-summer}
This small place was called Vanavara.

It stood on a hill above the Stony Tunguska River.
In 1908, it was a trading post, a modest little settlement with a handful of wooden houses, built by farmers and traders who had made the long journey from the south.
Its position was a good one, because the river was a natural road for the Evenki, and the traders came to meet them there.

Here, in the warm months, the Evenki families brought their furs, and their meat, and the things they had made.
And the traders offered tea, and flour, and salt, and iron pots, and knives, and cloth, and needles.
It was a place of quiet bargaining, with polite words and long pauses, and cups of hot tea, and sometimes a good deal of laughing.

The people of Vanavara were farmers, and traders, and a few families who had come to start a new life.
They had small fields, with barley and potatoes, and sheds for horses and cattle.
They cut hay in the summer, and put up wood for the winter.
They knew their neighbours, and they knew the forest edge, and they knew the river.

{img:vanavara-spring-ice-breaking-river}
Many of them lived by the rhythm of the seasons.
In the spring, the ice broke up on the river, with a crack, and a rumble, and floated away downstream in great white plates.
In the summer, the days grew very long.
At that northern latitude, it never got properly dark in June.
The sun dipped below the horizon late at night, and then rose again after only a few hours, and in between, the sky glowed softly, pink and green and silver, in what people sometimes call the white nights.

{img:farmer-porch-morning-tea}
Imagine a farmer in Vanavara, getting up early on a summer morning.

He wakes before the sun is high.
He puts on his boots, and a rough cotton shirt.
He walks out to the yard, where the grass is wet with dew, and the air smells of smoke from the stove and of damp earth.

A rooster is calling somewhere.
A horse is shifting in the stable, and the hens are walking about, pecking at the ground.
The sky is already pale and bright, and there are only a few high clouds, drifting slowly.

He has work to do.
There is a harrow to mend, there is hay to cut, and there are a few things to buy at the trading post.
He thinks about the day ahead, in the ordinary way that anyone does, on any ordinary morning.

He has no idea that this day is going to be remembered, in books, for more than a century.
He is simply a man, standing on his porch, in the warm morning air, with the whole of the Siberian summer in front of him.

We will meet this man again.
But first, we should look further out, at the world around him, and at the year in which he lived.
