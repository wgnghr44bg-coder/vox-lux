It began on the night of the thirtieth of June, and it went on for several nights.

In many parts of Europe, and in western Siberia, the night sky stayed bright.
It did not get dark.
Instead, even after midnight, a soft, pale glow lingered across the northern sky, as if the sun had not quite gone down.

At that time of year in northern places, the nights are short, and the sky never gets completely dark anyway.
But this was different, and people noticed.
The glow was brighter than usual, and it was spread across a wider part of the sky.
It had a silvery, pearly, slightly pink quality, and there were soft clouds in it, which seemed to shine on their own.

People in London were amazed.
Newspapers in England reported that the sky was so bright, at midnight, that it was possible to read a newspaper outdoors without a lamp.
Some people took photographs at night, which was very difficult in those days, and found that they could do it, with a short exposure, as if it were dusk.

In Germany, in Sweden, and in Russia, there were similar reports.
Observers in different places wrote that the northern sky was glowing with a strange, steady light, and that the clouds were lit from below, long after the sun should have gone.
Some people, in the countryside, went out into the fields to look.
They stood quietly, in the warm night air, and gazed at the sky.

{img:glowing-clouds-over-swedish-lake-night}
The glow lasted several nights, and then faded.

At the time, nobody could explain it.
Some people thought it was an unusual kind of aurora, the northern lights.
Others suggested that it might be dust from a volcano, or the tail of a comet.
There were many guesses, but no one could prove any of them.

{img:noctilucent-clouds-silver-blue-high-sky}
Nowadays, scientists think they have a good idea.
When the object exploded over Siberia, it threw a great deal of fine dust and water vapour high up into the atmosphere, far above the clouds.
The very high air, in the region called the mesosphere, is cold and thin, and the dust and ice crystals that reached it spread slowly around the northern part of the Earth.

When the sun is just below the horizon, its light can still reach these very high clouds, even though the ground below is in shadow.
So the clouds glowed, softly, in the night sky.
These are sometimes called noctilucent clouds, which means, night-shining clouds.
They are real, and you can still see them today, on some summer evenings, very high and very thin and blue-white, like a thin silk veil.

But in 1908, the glow was far brighter than usual, and it seemed that something special had happened.

{img:astronomer-notebook-lamplit-desk-midnight}
At least one thoughtful observer noticed a link.

Some astronomers in Europe wrote down that the unusual glow in the sky, in early July, was something they had never seen so strongly before.
They recorded the times, and the directions, and the colours.
They made notes, in tidy handwriting, and put them in drawers and archives, where they stayed for a long time.

And here, again, is a lovely thing about the story.
The sky itself, in a gentle way, carried the news around the world.
A great event had taken place in Siberia, and a few days later, in Britain, and in Sweden, and in Germany, people were standing in their gardens at midnight, looking up in wonder at a sky that glowed.
They did not know why.
They only knew it was beautiful.

Some of them said it was one of the loveliest things they had ever seen.
Some wrote poems about it.
Others simply stood quietly, and did not say anything at all.

{img:quiet-european-meadow-pale-night-sky}
We can imagine one person among them.

She is a woman, in an English village, stepping out of her back door at midnight, because she cannot sleep.
She wears a shawl over her nightdress.
The grass is cool and wet under her slippers, and the air smells of roses and cut hay.

She looks up, and sees that the whole northern sky is glowing, pale green and pearl and gold, with thin clouds floating in it, silvery and still.
There are no stars in that part of the sky, only light.
It is so bright that she can see her shadow, faint but clear, on the garden path.

She stands there for a long time.
Somewhere, a late bird sings one or two soft notes.
Somewhere, far off, a church clock strikes the hour, slowly.

She does not know that, across a continent, and a mountain range, and half of Russia, a great forest lies flattened and silent, in the pale light of the same sky.
She only knows that the night is beautiful, and that for a moment the world feels very large, and very gentle.

Then she goes back to bed.
And the glow in the northern sky fades, night by night, until by the middle of July, it is gone.

And so the world forgot.
