Edo is a city of water, built around rivers and canals. The great river Sumida flows through the eastern part of the city to the sea. Many smaller rivers and canals run through the streets. There are more than a hundred bridges, made of wood. And boats are everywhere, carrying people and goods from one part of the city to another.

In the middle of the city stands the shogun's castle, on a low hill. Around it, in big quiet districts, live the daimyo and the samurai. Their houses are behind long walls, with big wooden gates and gardens inside. These samurai districts take up more than half of the city. But they are quiet and green, with few people in the streets.

--

The townspeople live in the low parts of the city, near the rivers and the bay. Here, the streets are narrow and crowded and full of life. There are shops of every kind, side by side. There are fish shops and rice shops, cloth shops and paper shops. There are noodle stalls and tea houses, bookshops and toy shops. Peddlers walk through the streets calling out what they sell.

{img:narrow-edo-street-shops-signs-peddlers-calling-children-playing}
Most townspeople live in long wooden buildings called nagaya. A nagaya is like a row of small houses joined together, along a narrow alley. Each family has a small room, perhaps three or four metres across. At the end of the alley, there is a shared well and a shared toilet. The neighbours live very close together. They know everything about each other. They argue sometimes, but they also help each other.

--

At night, the streets of Edo are dark, except for a few lanterns. Each neighbourhood has wooden gates at its ends. At night, the gates are closed, and a watchman sits beside them. If you want to pass through late at night, you must knock and ask the watchman to let you through. The watchman walks around the streets with wooden clappers. He claps them together and calls out: Beware of fire! Beware of fire!

{img:night-watchman-wooden-clappers-lantern-dark-edo-street-gate}
Fire is the great danger in Edo. The houses are made of wood and paper, and they stand very close together. In winter, a strong dry wind blows from the north-west. If a fire starts, it can spread very quickly. The people of Edo have a saying: fires and quarrels are the flowers of Edo. They say it with a smile. But everyone is careful with fire, every day.

--

There are many temples and shrines in Edo. The temples are Buddhist, with big wooden halls, bronze bells and peaceful gardens. The shrines are Shinto, the old religion of Japan, with red gates called torii. People visit them to pray for good luck, good health and good business. On festival days, the temples and shrines are crowded with people, and there are stalls selling food and toys.

{img:asakusa-temple-1800-great-lantern-gate-crowds-incense-smoke}
The most famous temple in Edo is the great temple of Asakusa, beside the Sumida river. Its gate has a giant red paper lantern hanging in the middle. Behind the gate, there is a long street of little shops and stalls. They sell sweets and toys, fans and combs and little lucky charms. Behind the shops is the great temple hall, where a cloud of incense smoke always rises. People wave the smoke over themselves, because they believe it brings good health.

Along the canals, there are big warehouses with thick white walls of plaster. These are called kura. Merchants store rice and sake, cloth and other goods in them. The thick walls protect everything inside from fire. When there is a fire, the merchants close the heavy doors and seal them with mud. Often the kura survives, even when everything around it burns.

In the alleys of the nagaya, life is lived very much in the open. Women wash clothes and cook at the doors. Children play in the narrow passage between the houses. Old people sit on little benches and watch the world go by. Cats sleep on the warm stones. And everyone shares the well, the gossip, and sometimes a little rice when a neighbour has none.
