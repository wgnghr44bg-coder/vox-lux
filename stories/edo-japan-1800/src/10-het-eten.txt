The people of Edo love to eat. And Edo is full of places to eat, from fine restaurants to little stalls in the street.

Many people in Edo are men who have come from the countryside to work. They live alone, without a family to cook for them. So they eat out a lot. There are hundreds of little food stalls in the streets. They sell quick, cheap, tasty food that you can eat standing up. It is a little like fast food today.

--

One of the most popular foods is soba. Soba are thin noodles made from buckwheat flour. They are served in a hot broth, or cold with a dipping sauce. There are soba stalls on almost every street. Some soba sellers carry their whole kitchen on a pole across their shoulders. They walk through the streets at night, with a little lantern, calling out. People run out of their houses to buy a bowl of hot noodles.

{img:night-soba-peddler-portable-stall-lantern-customer-eating-bowl-steam}
Another popular food is tempura. Tempura is fish or vegetables, dipped in a light batter and fried in hot oil. It is crispy and golden and delicious. In 1800, tempura is sold at little stalls in the street, on wooden skewers. Because of the danger of fire, the stalls must be outside, not inside houses. People eat the tempura hot, dipped in a little sauce.

--

And there is sushi. In 1800, sushi is a little different from the sushi of today. In Edo, the cooks are just starting to make a new kind. They press a little ball of rice with vinegar, and put a slice of fresh fish on top. It is quick to make and quick to eat. It is sold at stalls near the fish market. People eat it with their fingers, standing at the counter. This new Edo-style sushi will one day become famous all over the world.

{img:sushi-stall-1800-edo-cook-pressing-rice-fish-wooden-counter}
At home, the family eats simpler food. Rice is the most important part of every meal. With it, there is miso soup, pickles and a little fish or tofu. On special days, there might be a little chicken or duck. Most people in Edo eat very little meat, because of their Buddhist faith. But they eat a lot of fish, because the sea is close.

--

Sweets are very popular too. There are little cakes made of rice flour and sweet bean paste. There are dumplings on sticks, called dango, grilled and covered in sweet sauce. There are crackers made of rice, and sweets made of sugar shaped like flowers. Shinta's favourite sweet is a little rice cake filled with sweet red beans. He buys one whenever he has a coin, from the old peddler at the corner.

{img:boy-buying-dango-sweet-dumplings-stick-old-peddler-street-corner}
And of course, there is tea. The people of Edo drink green tea all day long. In every house, there is a kettle of hot water on the fire. There are tea houses everywhere, in the streets and in the gardens and beside the temples. People stop there to rest and drink a cup of tea, and perhaps eat a little sweet. Pretty young women serve the tea, and some of them become famous for their beauty. Their pictures are printed and sold all over the city.

At festivals, there are even more food stalls. They sell grilled squid on sticks, sweet potatoes roasted in hot stones, and little cakes shaped like fish. The smell of the food drifts through the crowds. Children tug at their parents' sleeves and point. Shinta always chooses the sweet potato. He holds it in both hands and eats it slowly, while it is still steaming.

Oyuki is a good cook. She knows how to make simple food taste wonderful. She makes miso soup with little cubes of tofu and green onions. She grills fish with a little salt until the skin is crispy. She makes pickles of radish and cucumber in big wooden tubs. And on special days, she makes a dish of rice cooked with chestnuts or mushrooms. The whole street can smell it.
