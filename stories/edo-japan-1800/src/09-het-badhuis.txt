Every evening, most of the people of Edo go to the public bath. It is called a sento. There is one in almost every neighbourhood. And it is one of the most important places in daily life.

Very few houses in Edo have their own bath. Heating water needs a lot of fuel, and fuel is expensive. Fire is dangerous in a city of wood. So people go to the public bath instead. It costs only a few copper coins. And it is a lovely way to end the day.

--

At the entrance, there is a little raised desk, where the owner sits and takes the money. Inside, there are separate rooms for men and women. People take off their clothes and leave them in baskets or on shelves. Then they go into the washing room. There, they wash themselves carefully with buckets of warm water, sitting on little wooden stools. Only when they are completely clean do they get into the bath.

{img:bathhouse-washing-area-wooden-buckets-stools-steam-soft-light}
The bath is a big wooden tub, full of very hot water. It is so hot that people get in slowly, little by little. Then they sit in the water up to their necks, and they sigh with happiness. The heat soaks into their tired muscles. They close their eyes and relax. After a long day of work, there is nothing better.

--

But the bath is not only for getting clean. It is also a place to meet people and talk. Neighbours chat about the news and the gossip of the street. Craftsmen talk about their work. Old men tell jokes. Women talk about their children and their husbands. Upstairs, in some bathhouses, there is a room where men can drink tea and play games after their bath.

{img:bathhouse-upstairs-room-men-playing-go-board-drinking-tea-relaxed}
Shinta goes to the bath with his father and Sukeroku. He likes to sit in the hot water and listen to the men talking. Sometimes he splashes, and his father tells him to be quiet. Ohana goes with her mother and grandmother. Oume knows everyone at the bath, and everyone knows her. She spends a long time talking to her old friends, while the girls get bored and wrinkly in the water.

--

On the walls of the bathhouses, there are often big paintings. Sometimes there is a picture of Mount Fuji, rising above the sea. Sometimes there is a picture of a waterfall or a pine forest. The people sitting in the hot water look at the painting and imagine they are far away, in the mountains.

{img:family-walking-home-from-bath-evening-street-lanterns-towels-steam}
After the bath, the family walks home together through the evening streets. They are warm and clean and relaxed. Their cheeks are pink from the hot water. They carry their little towels over their shoulders. The lanterns are being lit along the street, and the shops are closing. Shinta skips ahead. Ohana holds her grandmother's arm. And at home, Oyuki serves a simple supper of rice and soup and grilled fish. Then everyone goes to bed early, and sleeps very well.

The bath owner gets up very early to heat the water. He burns wood and old scraps in a big stove under the bath. It takes hours to make the water hot enough. During the day, he collects firewood from all over the neighbourhood. People give him broken boxes and old wood. In return, he lets the children in for free sometimes.

In the women's bath, the talk is mostly about children and husbands, prices and neighbours. Oume tells everyone about the play she saw at the kabuki. She acts out the best parts, standing in the water and waving her arms. The other women laugh and clap. Ohana is a little embarrassed. But she is also very proud of her grandmother.

On the fifth day of the fifth month, the bathhouses put iris leaves in the water. People believe the leaves keep away illness. In winter, on the shortest day of the year, they put little yellow citrus fruits called yuzu into the bath. The fruits float on the water and fill the room with a fresh, sweet smell. Everyone looks forward to the yuzu bath. It makes the cold winter feel a little brighter.
