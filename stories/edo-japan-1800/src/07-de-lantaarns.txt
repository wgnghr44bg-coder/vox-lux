Let us watch Tokichi make a paper lantern. It is a craft that takes many years to learn.

First, he makes the frame. He takes a piece of bamboo and splits it into very thin strips, thinner than a pencil. Bamboo is strong and light, and it bends easily without breaking. He winds the strips around a wooden mould, in a long spiral, from bottom to top. The mould is made of several pieces of wood, fitted together like a puzzle. It gives the lantern its shape, round or long or oval.

--

Then he covers the frame with paper. Japanese paper is called washi. It is made by hand from the bark of a special tree. It is thin but very strong, and it lets light through softly. Tokichi brushes glue onto the bamboo strips. The glue is made from rice. Then he lays the paper over the frame, piece by piece, smoothing it with his hands. He works slowly and carefully, so there are no wrinkles.

{img:pasting-washi-paper-onto-lantern-frame-rice-glue-brush-careful}
When the glue is dry, he takes the wooden mould out from inside. He pulls out the pieces one by one, through the opening at the top. Now the lantern stands on its own, light and delicate. Its paper is stretched tight over the bamboo spiral. And it can be folded flat, like an accordion, when it is not being used.

--

Next comes the painting. This is the part Ohana loves best. Tokichi paints the name of a shop, or a family crest, in bold black letters. Or he paints a picture: a branch of plum blossom, a carp jumping out of the water, a crane flying across the moon. He uses a soft brush and ink, and sometimes red and blue paint. Ohana watches carefully. Sometimes he lets her paint the small leaves and petals.

{img:father-daughter-painting-lantern-crane-moon-brush-ink-shop}
Last of all, he coats the lantern with a thin layer of oil. That helps the paper to keep out the rain and the damp. Then he fits a little wooden top and bottom. And the lantern is finished. Inside, there will be a little candle, or a small dish of oil with a wick. When it is lit, the whole lantern glows warm and golden.

--

Tokichi also makes umbrellas, of paper and bamboo. A paper umbrella has a long bamboo handle and many thin ribs, like the spokes of a wheel. The ribs are covered with oiled paper, often in bright colours. When it rains, the streets of Edo are full of paper umbrellas, red and blue and yellow. And the rain makes a soft drumming sound on them.

{img:edo-street-rain-colourful-oiled-paper-umbrellas-people-walking}
Lanterns are used everywhere in Edo. People carry small lanterns when they walk at night. Shops hang lanterns outside their doors, with their names written on them. Temples and shrines hang rows of lanterns for festivals. Tea houses decorate their gardens with them. And in summer, at the festival for the ancestors, families hang lanterns to welcome the spirits home. So Tokichi is always busy.

In the evening, when the work is done, Tokichi sometimes makes a little lantern just for fun. Last week, he made a tiny one shaped like a goldfish, for Shinta. It has red paper and painted eyes and a curling tail. Shinta hung it above his futon. And every night, before he falls asleep, he looks at the little goldfish glowing softly in the dark.

Different lanterns are made for different purposes. There are big round lanterns for the gates of temples, as tall as a man. There are long thin lanterns for the doors of shops. There are small lanterns on sticks, for people to carry at night. There are little folding lanterns that travellers can put in their pockets. Tokichi can make them all. But his favourite is the small round lantern, with a picture of the moon.

Sukeroku is learning slowly. At first, he could only split the bamboo. Then he learned to wind the strips around the mould. Now he is learning to paste the paper. His fingers are still clumsy, and the paper sometimes tears. Tokichi does not get angry. He just says, again, and shows him once more. Little by little, the apprentice's hands are learning.

Washi paper is made in villages in the mountains, where there is clean cold water. The bark of the paper tree is soaked, boiled and beaten until it is soft. Then it is mixed with water in a big tub. The paper maker dips a bamboo screen into the tub and lifts it out, covered with a thin layer of fibres. When it dries, it becomes a sheet of paper. It is slow work, done mostly in winter, when the water is coldest.
