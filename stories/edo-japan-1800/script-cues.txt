Tonight we travel more than two hundred years back in time. We go to Japan, to a great city called Edo. The year is 1800. Today, this city has a different name. We call it Tokyo.

[long-pause]

It is early morning in spring, and the air is cool and fresh. A wooden bridge arches gracefully over a river in the middle of the city. It is called Nihonbashi, the Bridge of Japan. Far away to the west, above the rooftops, rises a beautiful mountain with a white peak. It is Mount Fuji, catching the pink light of the dawn.

[pause]

On the river, small wooden boats are already moving. Boatmen push them along with long poles. Some boats are full of vegetables, and some are full of fish. Along the riverbank, the shops are opening their wooden shutters. A man walks past with two baskets hanging from a pole across his shoulders. A cat stretches on a doorstep. And the smell of cooking rice drifts out from a hundred kitchens.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:edo-street-morning-shops-noren-curtains-townspeople-kimono-peddlers}
Edo in 1800 is one of the biggest cities in the whole world. About a million people live here. That is more than London or Paris at the same time. It is a city of wooden houses and paper windows, of narrow streets and busy markets. It is a city of temples and gardens, of rivers and canals and hundreds of bridges. And it is the city of the shogun, the military ruler of Japan.

[pause]

Japan in 1800 is a peaceful country. There has been no war for almost two hundred years. The people of Edo work hard, but they also love to enjoy themselves. They go to the theatre and to the sumo wrestling. They visit the cherry blossoms in spring and watch fireworks in summer. They go to the public bath every evening and chat with their neighbours. They buy colourful prints of actors and beautiful landscapes.

[pause]

Tonight we will meet a family who makes paper lanterns and umbrellas in a little shop in Edo. We will meet the father, who is a skilled craftsman, and the mother, who runs the shop. We will meet their daughter, who goes to a little school to learn to write. We will meet their son, who loves to watch the boats on the river. And we will meet a grandmother who loves the theatre more than anything.

[long-pause]

We will follow them through the seasons of the year. We will picnic under the cherry blossoms in spring. We will watch the fireworks over the river in summer. We will visit the public bath and the fish market, the theatre and the sumo wrestlers. We will learn about the samurai and the great road to Kyoto. And in winter, we will see how the people of Edo face the thing they fear most: fire.

{img:sumida-river-morning-mist-boats-wooden-houses-cherry-trees-budding}
So let us begin beside the river, on a spring morning in Edo. The cherry trees along the bank are just beginning to bud. The water is calm and silver. A heron stands in the shallows, perfectly still. A temple bell rings slowly, somewhere across the city. And a boy is sitting on the riverbank, watching the boats go by, waiting for his mother to call him in for breakfast.

[pause]

On the bridge, people are already crossing in both directions. There are porters carrying heavy bundles, and merchants in neat dark kimonos. There are women with babies tied on their backs, and old men with walking sticks. A monk in a grey robe walks slowly, holding a little bell. Everyone is busy, but nobody seems in a hurry. It is the start of an ordinary day in Edo.

[pause]

The boy's name is Shinta. He comes to the river every morning, before breakfast, to see the first boats. He knows them all by their shapes and their colours. That is the rice boat, he says to himself. That is the boat with the old boatman who sings. And there, coming round the bend, is the boat that carries firewood. He counts them, one by one, until his mother calls.

[long-pause]

{img:edo-castle-1800-white-walls-moats-pine-trees-gate-samurai-guards}
Who rules Japan in 1800? The answer is a little complicated.

[pause]

Japan has an emperor, who lives in the old capital, Kyoto, in a quiet palace with gardens. The emperor is very honoured and very respected. People believe his family goes back to the sun goddess herself. But the emperor does not really rule the country. He spends his days with poetry and ceremonies.

[long-pause]

The real ruler is the shogun. Shogun means great general. The shogun lives in Edo, in a huge castle in the middle of the city. The castle is surrounded by wide moats of water and great stone walls. Inside, there are gardens and palaces and many halls. The shogun's family, the Tokugawa family, has ruled Japan for about two hundred years.

[pause]

{img:shogun-audience-hall-tatami-gold-screens-officials-kneeling-quiet}
Under the shogun, there are about two hundred and sixty lords, called daimyo. Each daimyo rules his own part of Japan, with his own castle and his own samurai. But they must all obey the shogun. And they must spend every other year in Edo, close to the shogun. Their wives and children must live in Edo all the time. In this way, the shogun keeps an eye on them, and keeps the peace.

[long-pause]

Japan in 1800 is also a very closed country. For about a hundred and sixty years, the shogun has kept foreigners out. Japanese people are not allowed to leave Japan. Foreign ships are not allowed to come in. There is only one small exception. Dutch traders are allowed to stay on a tiny island called Dejima, in the harbour of Nagasaki, far to the south. Chinese traders come to Nagasaki too. That is the only door between Japan and the rest of the world.

[pause]

{img:dejima-island-nagasaki-dutch-trading-post-small-ships-harbour}
Because Japan is closed, it has developed its own way of life, very different from anywhere else. Japanese art and music and theatre, food and clothes and houses are all very special. The country is at peace, and the cities are growing. Many people are becoming richer. Merchants and craftsmen in the cities have money to spend on beautiful things and on entertainment.

[long-pause]

Japanese society is divided into four main groups. At the top are the samurai, the warrior class. Then come the farmers, who grow the rice that feeds everyone. Then come the craftsmen, who make things. And at the bottom are the merchants, who buy and sell. That is the official order. But in real life, many merchants are much richer than the samurai. And the people of the cities, the craftsmen and the merchants, are the ones who have the most fun.

[pause]

{img:edo-townspeople-chonin-street-festival-lanterns-evening-crowd}
The people of the city are called chonin, which means townspeople. They are proud of being from Edo. They have a special word for a true child of Edo: Edokko. An Edokko is quick and cheerful, generous and a little bit hot-tempered. He spends his money as soon as he gets it, and he never worries about tomorrow. The family in our story are true Edokko, born and raised in the city.

[pause]

Most Japanese people in 1800 are farmers, living in villages in the countryside. They grow rice in flooded fields, called paddies. In spring, they plant the young rice plants by hand, standing in the water up to their knees. In autumn, they cut the golden rice with sickles. Much of the rice goes to the samurai and the daimyo, as tax. The rest feeds the farmers' families and is sold in the towns.

[pause]

The long peace has brought many good things. People can travel safely on the roads. Trade between the towns has grown. Books are printed and sold in great numbers. Many ordinary people can read and write. And there is time and money for art and theatre, poetry and festivals. Some people call this time the great peace of Edo.

[pause]

In the old capital of Kyoto, life is quieter and more elegant than in Edo. There are hundreds of temples and gardens there, and the old crafts of silk weaving and pottery. The people of Kyoto think the people of Edo are rather loud and rough. And the people of Edo think the people of Kyoto are rather slow and old-fashioned. They tease each other about it, as neighbours do.

[long-pause]

{img:edo-city-panorama-1800-rooftops-castle-rivers-bridges-fuji-behind}
Edo is a city of water, built around rivers and canals. The great river Sumida flows through the eastern part of the city to the sea. Many smaller rivers and canals run through the streets. There are more than a hundred bridges, made of wood. And boats are everywhere, carrying people and goods from one part of the city to another.

[pause]

In the middle of the city stands the shogun's castle, on a low hill. Around it, in big quiet districts, live the daimyo and the samurai. Their houses are behind long walls, with big wooden gates and gardens inside. These samurai districts take up more than half of the city. But they are quiet and green, with few people in the streets.

[long-pause]

The townspeople live in the low parts of the city, near the rivers and the bay. Here, the streets are narrow and crowded and full of life. There are shops of every kind, side by side. There are fish shops and rice shops, cloth shops and paper shops. There are noodle stalls and tea houses, bookshops and toy shops. Peddlers walk through the streets calling out what they sell.

[pause]

{img:narrow-edo-street-shops-signs-peddlers-calling-children-playing}
Most townspeople live in long wooden buildings called nagaya. A nagaya is like a row of small houses joined together, along a narrow alley. Each family has a small room, perhaps three or four metres across. At the end of the alley, there is a shared well and a shared toilet. The neighbours live very close together. They know everything about each other. They argue sometimes, but they also help each other.

[long-pause]

At night, the streets of Edo are dark, except for a few lanterns. Each neighbourhood has wooden gates at its ends. At night, the gates are closed, and a watchman sits beside them. If you want to pass through late at night, you must knock and ask the watchman to let you through. The watchman walks around the streets with wooden clappers. He claps them together and calls out: Beware of fire! Beware of fire!

[pause]

{img:night-watchman-wooden-clappers-lantern-dark-edo-street-gate}
Fire is the great danger in Edo. The houses are made of wood and paper, and they stand very close together. In winter, a strong dry wind blows from the north-west. If a fire starts, it can spread very quickly. The people of Edo have a saying: fires and quarrels are the flowers of Edo. They say it with a smile. But everyone is careful with fire, every day.

[long-pause]

There are many temples and shrines in Edo. The temples are Buddhist, with big wooden halls, bronze bells and peaceful gardens. The shrines are Shinto, the old religion of Japan, with red gates called torii. People visit them to pray for good luck, good health and good business. On festival days, the temples and shrines are crowded with people, and there are stalls selling food and toys.

[pause]

{img:asakusa-temple-1800-great-lantern-gate-crowds-incense-smoke}
The most famous temple in Edo is the great temple of Asakusa, beside the Sumida river. Its gate has a giant red paper lantern hanging in the middle. Behind the gate, there is a long street of little shops and stalls. They sell sweets and toys, fans and combs and little lucky charms. Behind the shops is the great temple hall, where a cloud of incense smoke always rises. People wave the smoke over themselves, because they believe it brings good health.

[pause]

Along the canals, there are big warehouses with thick white walls of plaster. These are called kura. Merchants store rice and sake, cloth and other goods in them. The thick walls protect everything inside from fire. When there is a fire, the merchants close the heavy doors and seal them with mud. Often the kura survives, even when everything around it burns.

[pause]

In the alleys of the nagaya, life is lived very much in the open. Women wash clothes and cook at the doors. Children play in the narrow passage between the houses. Old people sit on little benches and watch the world go by. Cats sleep on the warm stones. And everyone shares the well, the gossip, and sometimes a little rice when a neighbour has none.

[long-pause]

{img:edo-craftsman-father-making-paper-lantern-bamboo-frame-shop-floor}
Let us meet the family who lives in a little shop near the river, in the old part of Edo.

[pause]

The father is called Tokichi. He is a lantern maker, and he is about forty years old. He makes paper lanterns and paper umbrellas, by hand, in the front room of the shop. He learned the craft from his father, who learned it from his father. Tokichi is a calm and patient man. He likes his work, and he does it carefully. He does not talk much while he works. But in the evenings, he likes to laugh and tell jokes.

[pause]

The mother is called Oyuki. She runs the shop and talks to the customers. She keeps the money in a little wooden box and writes the accounts with a brush. She also cooks and cleans and sews, and looks after the whole family. She is quick and clever, and she always has a kind word for the neighbours. Everyone in the street likes Oyuki.

[long-pause]

They have two children. The daughter is called Ohana, which means flower. She is eleven years old, with a round face and bright eyes. She goes to a little neighbourhood school in the mornings, where she learns to read and write. She loves to practise her writing with a brush. And she loves to help her father paint pictures on the lanterns.

[pause]

{img:girl-eleven-practising-calligraphy-brush-ink-low-desk-window-light}
The son is called Shinta, and he is eight. He is lively and curious and always asking questions. He loves boats and wants to be a boatman when he grows up. He spends hours on the riverbank, watching the boats go by. He knows the names of all the boatmen on this part of the river. He also loves sweets, especially the little rice cakes sold by the peddler at the corner.

[long-pause]

The grandmother lives with them too. She is Tokichi's mother, and her name is Oume. She is about seventy, small and thin, with her grey hair tied up neatly. She has a sharp tongue and a warm heart. Her great love is the kabuki theatre. She has seen every famous actor in Edo, and she knows all the plays by heart. She keeps a little collection of prints of her favourite actors. She takes them out sometimes and looks at them, and sighs happily.

[pause]

{img:old-japanese-grandmother-looking-at-kabuki-actor-prints-smiling-room}
There is also a young apprentice in the shop, a boy of fifteen called Sukeroku. He came from a village in the countryside to learn lantern making from Tokichi. He lives with the family and eats with them. He sweeps the shop and runs errands, and he is slowly learning the craft. He is a little shy and a little clumsy. But he works hard, and the family is fond of him.

[long-pause]

The shop stands on a street that runs down to the river. It is a two-storey wooden house, with the shop at the front on the ground floor. Above the door hangs a short curtain of blue cloth, called a noren. On it is written the name of the shop in white letters. When the noren is hanging, the shop is open. When it is taken down, the shop is closed for the day.

[pause]

{img:edo-lantern-shop-front-blue-noren-curtain-lanterns-hanging-street}
Outside the shop, there are always a few lanterns hanging on display. There are round ones and long ones, red ones and white ones. Some have the names of shops written on them. Some have pictures of flowers or birds or waves. At night, Tokichi lights one of them, so that people can see the shop from far away. The soft light glows warm on the dark street. And neighbours walking past often stop to admire it, and to chat with Oyuki at the door.

[pause]

The family also has a little dog, a small white dog with brown ears called Shiro. Shiro means white. He sleeps by the kitchen door and follows Shinta everywhere. When Shinta goes to the river, Shiro goes too. He sits beside the boy on the riverbank and watches the boats, with his ears pricked up. When a boatman waves, Shiro wags his tail.

[pause]

Every evening, after supper, the family sits together for a while before bed. Tokichi smokes a little pipe of tobacco. Oyuki sews or mends clothes. Oume tells a story, or sings a little song from the theatre. Ohana shows her writing to her father. Shinta plays with Shiro on the tatami. And Sukeroku sits quietly in the corner, listening to everything, slowly feeling at home.

[long-pause]

{img:japanese-house-interior-1800-tatami-mats-sliding-paper-screens-light}
Let us go inside the family's house. A Japanese house in 1800 is very different from a house in Europe.

[pause]

When you come in, the first thing you do is take off your shoes. You leave them at the edge of a little step, at the entrance. The floors inside are covered with tatami mats. These are thick mats made of rice straw, covered with smooth woven rush. They are soft and warm to walk on, and they smell sweet, like fresh grass. You never walk on them in shoes.

[long-pause]

The walls inside the house are not solid. Many of them are sliding screens, made of thin wood and paper. They are called shoji. In the daytime, they let in a soft white light. You can slide them open to make two rooms into one big room. Or you can slide them shut to make small private rooms. There are also screens of thick paper, called fusuma, painted with pictures.

[pause]

{img:sliding-shoji-screens-open-small-garden-stone-lantern-moss-morning}
There is very little furniture in a Japanese house. There are no chairs and no beds. People sit on cushions on the floor. They eat from small low tables, or from trays. At night, they take out thick soft mattresses, called futons, from a cupboard. They lay them on the tatami and sleep on them. In the morning, they fold them up and put them away again. So the same room is a living room by day, and a bedroom by night.

[long-pause]

In the middle of the main room, there is a square hole in the floor, filled with sand and ashes. It is called an irori. A small fire of charcoal burns in it. An iron kettle hangs over it on a hook, always full of hot water for tea. In winter, the family sits around the irori to keep warm. There is also a hibachi, a box of wood or pottery with hot charcoal inside. People warm their hands over it on cold days.

[pause]

{img:family-sitting-around-sunken-hearth-irori-kettle-hanging-winter-evening}
At the back of the house, there is a tiny garden. It is only a few metres wide. But it is very carefully made. There is a little stone lantern, a small maple tree and some moss. There is a stone basin, where water drips slowly from a bamboo pipe. Oume looks after the garden. She sweeps it every morning and waters the moss. She says that even a tiny garden can hold the whole world.

[long-pause]

The kitchen is at the side of the house, with a floor of beaten earth. There is a stove made of clay, with places for two pots. There is a big jar of water and a wooden sink. Rice is cooked every morning in a heavy iron pot. There are shelves with bowls and plates, chopsticks and little dishes. And there are jars of pickled vegetables, soy sauce and miso, a thick salty paste made of beans.

[pause]

{img:japanese-kitchen-earthen-floor-clay-stove-rice-pot-steam-morning}
Upstairs, there is a small room where the family keeps its best things. There is a chest with the good kimonos for festivals and special days. There is a little alcove called a tokonoma, with a hanging scroll and a vase of flowers. Oume changes the scroll and the flowers with the seasons. In spring, there is a scroll with a picture of cherry blossoms. In autumn, there is one with red maple leaves. It is a small way of bringing the seasons into the house.

[pause]

Every morning, Oyuki opens all the screens to let in the fresh air. She sweeps the tatami with a soft broom of straw, carefully, in the direction of the weave. She wipes the wooden floors of the corridor with a damp cloth. She folds the futons and puts them away in the cupboard. The house is small, but it is always clean and tidy.

[pause]

In winter, the house can be cold. The paper screens let in the draughts, and there is no heating except the little charcoal fires. So the family wears thick quilted kimonos, padded with cotton. They sit close to the irori in the evenings. At night, they sleep with warm stones wrapped in cloth at their feet. And on very cold nights, the children crawl into their mother's futon to keep warm.

[long-pause]

{img:edo-dawn-mother-cooking-rice-clay-stove-steam-family-asleep-futons}
Let us spend an ordinary day with the family, in spring.

[pause]

Oyuki gets up first, before the sun. She folds away her futon quietly, so she will not wake the others. She goes to the well at the end of the street to fetch water. Other women are there too, filling their buckets and chatting softly in the grey dawn. Then she goes back to the kitchen and lights the stove. She washes the rice carefully and puts it on to cook.

[long-pause]

Soon the house begins to wake up. Tokichi rises and goes to wash his face. Oume kneels in front of the little family altar and lights a stick of incense. She says a short prayer for the ancestors and puts a small bowl of rice in front of them. The children are the last to get up. Shinta has to be called three times. Ohana is up and dressed already, practising her writing.

[pause]

{img:family-breakfast-kneeling-low-trays-rice-miso-soup-pickles-morning}
Breakfast is simple. There is a bowl of hot rice, a bowl of miso soup and some pickled vegetables. Sometimes there is a little grilled fish too. The family kneels on the tatami and eats from little trays, with chopsticks. Before eating, they put their hands together and say itadakimasu. It means, I humbly receive. It is a way of saying thank you for the food.

[long-pause]

After breakfast, Ohana goes off to school. Shinta does not go to school yet, so he helps in the shop. Tokichi and Sukeroku sit down in the front room and begin to work on the lanterns. Oyuki hangs up the blue noren outside the door. The shop is open. Soon, the first customers come in. A tea house owner wants ten new lanterns for his garden. A young man wants an umbrella for his sweetheart.

[pause]

{img:peddlers-edo-street-tofu-seller-goldfish-seller-baskets-pole-morning}
All morning, peddlers walk past the shop, calling out their wares. There is the tofu seller, with his white blocks of bean curd floating in water. There is the fish seller, with fresh sardines in his baskets. There is the vegetable seller, the seller of brooms, and the man who mends pots. In summer, there will be a man selling goldfish in glass bowls, and a man selling cool water with sugar. Each peddler has his own special call, so people know who is coming.

[long-pause]

At midday, the family eats a quick lunch. Then Oume takes her afternoon rest, and the work in the shop goes on. In the afternoon, Shinta runs down to the river to watch the boats. Ohana comes home from school and helps her father paint a lantern. Oyuki goes out to the market to buy food for supper.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-riverbank-edo-afternoon-boats-passing-kites}
In the late afternoon, the whole family goes to the public bath, as most people in Edo do every day. Afterwards, they eat supper together at home, and talk about the day. When it gets dark, Tokichi lights the lantern outside the shop. The neighbours sit outside their houses in the cool evening, chatting and fanning themselves. Children play in the street until their mothers call them in. And slowly, the street becomes quiet, and the lights go out one by one.

[pause]

In the middle of the afternoon, Oyuki makes tea for everyone in the shop. She brings it on a little tray, with a few rice crackers. Tokichi and Sukeroku stop work for a few minutes. They sit on the edge of the step and drink their tea, looking out at the street. A customer comes by and joins them for a cup. They talk about the weather and the news from the castle.

[pause]

Shiro the dog has his own little routine too. In the morning, he walks around the street and greets all the neighbours. At midday, he sleeps in the sun by the shop door. In the afternoon, he goes to the river with Shinta. And in the evening, he waits by the door for the family to come back from the bath. He wags his tail so hard that his whole body wiggles.

[long-pause]

{img:lantern-maker-bending-thin-bamboo-strips-frame-workshop-close-hands}
Let us watch Tokichi make a paper lantern. It is a craft that takes many years to learn.

[pause]

First, he makes the frame. He takes a piece of bamboo and splits it into very thin strips, thinner than a pencil. Bamboo is strong and light, and it bends easily without breaking. He winds the strips around a wooden mould, in a long spiral, from bottom to top. The mould is made of several pieces of wood, fitted together like a puzzle. It gives the lantern its shape, round or long or oval.

[long-pause]

Then he covers the frame with paper. Japanese paper is called washi. It is made by hand from the bark of a special tree. It is thin but very strong, and it lets light through softly. Tokichi brushes glue onto the bamboo strips. The glue is made from rice. Then he lays the paper over the frame, piece by piece, smoothing it with his hands. He works slowly and carefully, so there are no wrinkles.

[pause]

{img:pasting-washi-paper-onto-lantern-frame-rice-glue-brush-careful}
When the glue is dry, he takes the wooden mould out from inside. He pulls out the pieces one by one, through the opening at the top. Now the lantern stands on its own, light and delicate. Its paper is stretched tight over the bamboo spiral. And it can be folded flat, like an accordion, when it is not being used.

[long-pause]

Next comes the painting. This is the part Ohana loves best. Tokichi paints the name of a shop, or a family crest, in bold black letters. Or he paints a picture: a branch of plum blossom, a carp jumping out of the water, a crane flying across the moon. He uses a soft brush and ink, and sometimes red and blue paint. Ohana watches carefully. Sometimes he lets her paint the small leaves and petals.

[pause]

{img:father-daughter-painting-lantern-crane-moon-brush-ink-shop}
Last of all, he coats the lantern with a thin layer of oil. That helps the paper to keep out the rain and the damp. Then he fits a little wooden top and bottom. And the lantern is finished. Inside, there will be a little candle, or a small dish of oil with a wick. When it is lit, the whole lantern glows warm and golden.

[long-pause]

Tokichi also makes umbrellas, of paper and bamboo. A paper umbrella has a long bamboo handle and many thin ribs, like the spokes of a wheel. The ribs are covered with oiled paper, often in bright colours. When it rains, the streets of Edo are full of paper umbrellas, red and blue and yellow. And the rain makes a soft drumming sound on them.

[pause]

{img:edo-street-rain-colourful-oiled-paper-umbrellas-people-walking}
Lanterns are used everywhere in Edo. People carry small lanterns when they walk at night. Shops hang lanterns outside their doors, with their names written on them. Temples and shrines hang rows of lanterns for festivals. Tea houses decorate their gardens with them. And in summer, at the festival for the ancestors, families hang lanterns to welcome the spirits home. So Tokichi is always busy.

[pause]

In the evening, when the work is done, Tokichi sometimes makes a little lantern just for fun. Last week, he made a tiny one shaped like a goldfish, for Shinta. It has red paper and painted eyes and a curling tail. Shinta hung it above his futon. And every night, before he falls asleep, he looks at the little goldfish glowing softly in the dark.

[pause]

Different lanterns are made for different purposes. There are big round lanterns for the gates of temples, as tall as a man. There are long thin lanterns for the doors of shops. There are small lanterns on sticks, for people to carry at night. There are little folding lanterns that travellers can put in their pockets. Tokichi can make them all. But his favourite is the small round lantern, with a picture of the moon.

[pause]

Sukeroku is learning slowly. At first, he could only split the bamboo. Then he learned to wind the strips around the mould. Now he is learning to paste the paper. His fingers are still clumsy, and the paper sometimes tears. Tokichi does not get angry. He just says, again, and shows him once more. Little by little, the apprentice's hands are learning.

[pause]

Washi paper is made in villages in the mountains, where there is clean cold water. The bark of the paper tree is soaked, boiled and beaten until it is soft. Then it is mixed with water in a big tub. The paper maker dips a bamboo screen into the tub and lifts it out, covered with a thin layer of fibres. When it dries, it becomes a sheet of paper. It is slow work, done mostly in winter, when the water is coldest.

[long-pause]

{img:nihonbashi-fish-market-1800-early-morning-baskets-tuna-crowd-boats}
Beside the Nihonbashi bridge, along the river, there is a great fish market. It is one of the busiest places in all of Edo.

[pause]

The market starts very early, before the sun comes up. Boats arrive from the bay, loaded with fish caught during the night. The fishermen unload their catch onto the riverbank, in big wooden tubs and baskets. There are sardines and mackerel, sea bream and squid, prawns and clams and octopus. And there are great tuna, as long as a man, lying on wooden boards.

[long-pause]

The fish sellers shout out their prices. The buyers shout back. There are restaurant owners and shopkeepers, peddlers and cooks from the samurai houses. Everyone wants the best fish, and everyone wants it cheap. It is noisy and wet and crowded. The ground is slippery with water and fish scales. And the smell of the sea is everywhere.

[pause]

{img:fish-sellers-buyers-bargaining-wooden-tubs-sea-bream-market-shouting}
By the middle of the morning, most of the fish has been sold. The peddlers carry it away in baskets on their poles, to sell in the streets. The cooks take it back to their kitchens. And the market slowly quietens down. The fishermen wash their boats and their tubs. Then they sit down together to eat a bowl of rice with fresh fish, before they go home to sleep.

[long-pause]

Not far from the fish market, there is a vegetable market too. Here, farmers from the villages around Edo bring their vegetables by boat and on their backs. There are long white radishes, sweet potatoes and eggplants. There are cabbages and leeks, ginger and mushrooms. In spring, there are bamboo shoots, fresh and tender. In autumn, there are chestnuts and persimmons.

[pause]

{img:vegetable-market-edo-farmers-radishes-eggplants-baskets-morning}
Oyuki goes to the market every few days. She takes a basket and a little purse of copper coins. She buys fish and vegetables, tofu and seaweed. She knows all the sellers by name. She chats with them and asks about their families. The sellers like her, and they sometimes give her a little extra. One old woman always gives her a few extra radishes for the grandmother.

[long-pause]

Around Nihonbashi, there are also big shops that sell cloth and kimonos. The biggest one is a famous shop called Echigoya. It is a huge shop, with many rooms full of rolls of silk and cotton. Dozens of clerks work there, showing cloth to customers. Echigoya had a clever new idea. It sells cloth for a fixed price, paid in cash, with no bargaining. Many people liked this, and the shop became very rich.

[pause]

{img:large-kimono-cloth-shop-clerks-showing-silk-rolls-customers-kneeling}
Once a year, Oyuki takes Ohana to a cloth shop to buy material for a new kimono. Ohana loves it. She looks at the rolls of cloth in every colour and pattern. There are cherry blossoms and waves, birds and bamboo, stripes and checks. It takes her a long time to choose. This year, she chooses a cotton cloth of pale blue, with little white plum flowers. Her mother will sew it into a kimono for the New Year. Ohana can hardly wait.

[pause]

On the way home from the market, Oyuki often stops at a little shrine by the road. It is a tiny wooden building with a red gate in front of it. She drops a coin into the box, claps her hands twice and bows. She asks for good health for the family and good business for the shop. Then she goes on her way, her basket full.

[pause]

There are also little markets on festival days at the temples and shrines. Stalls sell toys and sweets, flowers and potted plants. There are goldfish in glass bowls and crickets in little bamboo cages. People keep the crickets as pets, because they like the sound of their singing. Shinta would love to have a cricket. Perhaps, his mother says, next summer.

[long-pause]

{img:edo-public-bathhouse-sento-entrance-noren-curtain-evening-lanterns}
Every evening, most of the people of Edo go to the public bath. It is called a sento. There is one in almost every neighbourhood. And it is one of the most important places in daily life.

[pause]

Very few houses in Edo have their own bath. Heating water needs a lot of fuel, and fuel is expensive. Fire is dangerous in a city of wood. So people go to the public bath instead. It costs only a few copper coins. And it is a lovely way to end the day.

[long-pause]

At the entrance, there is a little raised desk, where the owner sits and takes the money. Inside, there are separate rooms for men and women. People take off their clothes and leave them in baskets or on shelves. Then they go into the washing room. There, they wash themselves carefully with buckets of warm water, sitting on little wooden stools. Only when they are completely clean do they get into the bath.

[pause]

{img:bathhouse-washing-area-wooden-buckets-stools-steam-soft-light}
The bath is a big wooden tub, full of very hot water. It is so hot that people get in slowly, little by little. Then they sit in the water up to their necks, and they sigh with happiness. The heat soaks into their tired muscles. They close their eyes and relax. After a long day of work, there is nothing better.

[long-pause]

But the bath is not only for getting clean. It is also a place to meet people and talk. Neighbours chat about the news and the gossip of the street. Craftsmen talk about their work. Old men tell jokes. Women talk about their children and their husbands. Upstairs, in some bathhouses, there is a room where men can drink tea and play games after their bath.

[pause]

{img:bathhouse-upstairs-room-men-playing-go-board-drinking-tea-relaxed}
Shinta goes to the bath with his father and Sukeroku. He likes to sit in the hot water and listen to the men talking. Sometimes he splashes, and his father tells him to be quiet. Ohana goes with her mother and grandmother. Oume knows everyone at the bath, and everyone knows her. She spends a long time talking to her old friends, while the girls get bored and wrinkly in the water.

[long-pause]

On the walls of the bathhouses, there are often big paintings. Sometimes there is a picture of Mount Fuji, rising above the sea. Sometimes there is a picture of a waterfall or a pine forest. The people sitting in the hot water look at the painting and imagine they are far away, in the mountains.

[pause]

{img:family-walking-home-from-bath-evening-street-lanterns-towels-steam}
After the bath, the family walks home together through the evening streets. They are warm and clean and relaxed. Their cheeks are pink from the hot water. They carry their little towels over their shoulders. The lanterns are being lit along the street, and the shops are closing. Shinta skips ahead. Ohana holds her grandmother's arm. And at home, Oyuki serves a simple supper of rice and soup and grilled fish. Then everyone goes to bed early, and sleeps very well.

[pause]

The bath owner gets up very early to heat the water. He burns wood and old scraps in a big stove under the bath. It takes hours to make the water hot enough. During the day, he collects firewood from all over the neighbourhood. People give him broken boxes and old wood. In return, he lets the children in for free sometimes.

[pause]

In the women's bath, the talk is mostly about children and husbands, prices and neighbours. Oume tells everyone about the play she saw at the kabuki. She acts out the best parts, standing in the water and waving her arms. The other women laugh and clap. Ohana is a little embarrassed. But she is also very proud of her grandmother.

[pause]

On the fifth day of the fifth month, the bathhouses put iris leaves in the water. People believe the leaves keep away illness. In winter, on the shortest day of the year, they put little yellow citrus fruits called yuzu into the bath. The fruits float on the water and fill the room with a fresh, sweet smell. Everyone looks forward to the yuzu bath. It makes the cold winter feel a little brighter.

[long-pause]

{img:edo-street-food-stalls-evening-soba-tempura-sushi-lanterns-customers}
The people of Edo love to eat. And Edo is full of places to eat, from fine restaurants to little stalls in the street.

[pause]

Many people in Edo are men who have come from the countryside to work. They live alone, without a family to cook for them. So they eat out a lot. There are hundreds of little food stalls in the streets. They sell quick, cheap, tasty food that you can eat standing up. It is a little like fast food today.

[long-pause]

One of the most popular foods is soba. Soba are thin noodles made from buckwheat flour. They are served in a hot broth, or cold with a dipping sauce. There are soba stalls on almost every street. Some soba sellers carry their whole kitchen on a pole across their shoulders. They walk through the streets at night, with a little lantern, calling out. People run out of their houses to buy a bowl of hot noodles.

[pause]

{img:night-soba-peddler-portable-stall-lantern-customer-eating-bowl-steam}
Another popular food is tempura. Tempura is fish or vegetables, dipped in a light batter and fried in hot oil. It is crispy and golden and delicious. In 1800, tempura is sold at little stalls in the street, on wooden skewers. Because of the danger of fire, the stalls must be outside, not inside houses. People eat the tempura hot, dipped in a little sauce.

[long-pause]

And there is sushi. In 1800, sushi is a little different from the sushi of today. In Edo, the cooks are just starting to make a new kind. They press a little ball of rice with vinegar, and put a slice of fresh fish on top. It is quick to make and quick to eat. It is sold at stalls near the fish market. People eat it with their fingers, standing at the counter. This new Edo-style sushi will one day become famous all over the world.

[pause]

{img:sushi-stall-1800-edo-cook-pressing-rice-fish-wooden-counter}
At home, the family eats simpler food. Rice is the most important part of every meal. With it, there is miso soup, pickles and a little fish or tofu. On special days, there might be a little chicken or duck. Most people in Edo eat very little meat, because of their Buddhist faith. But they eat a lot of fish, because the sea is close.

[long-pause]

Sweets are very popular too. There are little cakes made of rice flour and sweet bean paste. There are dumplings on sticks, called dango, grilled and covered in sweet sauce. There are crackers made of rice, and sweets made of sugar shaped like flowers. Shinta's favourite sweet is a little rice cake filled with sweet red beans. He buys one whenever he has a coin, from the old peddler at the corner.

[pause]

{img:boy-buying-dango-sweet-dumplings-stick-old-peddler-street-corner}
And of course, there is tea. The people of Edo drink green tea all day long. In every house, there is a kettle of hot water on the fire. There are tea houses everywhere, in the streets and in the gardens and beside the temples. People stop there to rest and drink a cup of tea, and perhaps eat a little sweet. Pretty young women serve the tea, and some of them become famous for their beauty. Their pictures are printed and sold all over the city.

[pause]

At festivals, there are even more food stalls. They sell grilled squid on sticks, sweet potatoes roasted in hot stones, and little cakes shaped like fish. The smell of the food drifts through the crowds. Children tug at their parents' sleeves and point. Shinta always chooses the sweet potato. He holds it in both hands and eats it slowly, while it is still steaming.

[pause]

Oyuki is a good cook. She knows how to make simple food taste wonderful. She makes miso soup with little cubes of tofu and green onions. She grills fish with a little salt until the skin is crispy. She makes pickles of radish and cucumber in big wooden tubs. And on special days, she makes a dish of rice cooked with chestnuts or mushrooms. The whole street can smell it.

[long-pause]

{img:terakoya-school-1800-children-low-desks-brushes-teacher-tatami-room}
In 1800, many children in Edo go to school, more than in many other countries at that time. The schools are called terakoya, which means temple schools. Long ago, they were held in temples. But now most of them are held in the teacher's own house.

[pause]

Ohana goes to a terakoya in the next street. It is a large room with tatami mats on the floor. There are about thirty children, boys and girls, of all ages. They sit at little low wooden desks, kneeling on cushions. The teacher is a kind old man who was once a samurai. He sits at the front, with a long pipe and a cup of tea.

[long-pause]

The children learn to read and write. That is not easy in Japanese. There are two kinds of simple letters, each with about fifty signs. And there are also thousands of Chinese characters, called kanji. Each kanji is a picture-word, with its own meaning. A child must learn many hundreds of them. They practise by writing them again and again, with a brush and black ink.

[pause]

{img:children-grinding-ink-stick-stone-brushes-practice-paper-concentrating}
Each child has a little writing box. Inside there is a brush, an inkstone and a stick of solid ink. First, they put a few drops of water on the inkstone. Then they rub the ink stick on the stone, round and round, until the water turns black. Then they dip their brush and begin to write. The brush must be held straight up. The strokes must be written in the right order. And each character must be beautiful as well as correct.

[long-pause]

The children also learn to count, using a counting frame called a soroban. It has rows of little wooden beads on rods. You move the beads up and down to add and subtract. Shopkeepers and merchants use it every day. A good user can count very fast, faster than you can write. Ohana is quick with the soroban, and her mother is very proud of her.

[pause]

{img:girl-using-soroban-abacus-wooden-beads-school-desk-smiling}
The children learn other things too. They learn good manners and how to bow politely. They learn the names of the provinces of Japan and the stations on the great roads. They learn poems and wise sayings. The girls sometimes learn sewing and the tea ceremony. The teacher is strict but kind. When a child does good work, he praises them. When they are naughty, he makes them stand at the back of the room with a cup of water in their hands.

[long-pause]

The school day starts in the morning and ends around lunchtime. The children bring their own desks and their own writing boxes. When they arrive, they bow to the teacher. When they leave, they bow again. Each child works at their own speed. The teacher walks around, looking at their work, and correcting it with red ink.

[pause]

{img:children-leaving-school-bowing-teacher-doorway-edo-street-noon}
Ohana's best friend at school is a girl called Okiku, whose father sells tofu. They sit together and help each other with difficult characters. On the way home, they stop at a little shrine and clap their hands to make a wish. Okiku wishes for a new hair ornament. Ohana wishes to write as beautifully as her teacher one day. Then they buy a little sweet and share it, walking home slowly in the sunshine.

[pause]

The schools are not free, but they do not cost very much. Parents pay the teacher a small amount each month. They also bring him gifts at New Year and at the summer festival. Sometimes the gift is money, and sometimes it is rice or sake or sweets. Tokichi always gives the teacher a beautiful lantern at New Year. The teacher hangs it in his doorway, proudly.

[pause]

At the end of each year, the children hold a little show of their best writing. They hang their sheets on the walls of the school. Parents and grandparents come to look. Oume goes every year to see Ohana's work. This year, Ohana has written a short poem about the plum blossoms. Oume reads it twice. Then she looks at her granddaughter and nods. That is very fine, she says.

[long-pause]

{img:samurai-1800-two-swords-formal-kimono-walking-edo-street-calm}
In the streets of Edo, you often see samurai. They are easy to recognise. They wear two swords at their waist, a long one and a short one. Only samurai are allowed to wear two swords. They wear their hair in a special style, with the top of the head shaved and a little knot of hair on top.

[pause]

Long ago, the samurai were warriors who fought in battles. But Japan has been at peace for almost two hundred years. So most samurai have not fought in any battle at all. Instead, they work as officials and guards, clerks and teachers. They work for the shogun, or for one of the daimyo lords.

[long-pause]

Samurai are supposed to live by a strict code of honour. They should be loyal to their lord. They should be brave and honest and calm. They should practise the martial arts, like sword fighting and archery. But they should also study books and poetry, calligraphy and the tea ceremony. A good samurai should be gentle as well as strong.

[pause]

{img:samurai-practising-calligraphy-quiet-room-scroll-garden-view}
But many samurai in 1800 are not rich at all. Their salaries are paid in rice, and the price of rice goes up and down. Many samurai families are quite poor. Some of them make umbrellas or lanterns at home, secretly, to earn a little extra money. Some of them borrow money from merchants. It is a strange situation. The samurai are at the top of society, but the merchants often have more money.

[long-pause]

Every year, many daimyo lords travel to Edo with their samurai, or leave Edo to go home. They travel in great processions along the main roads. Hundreds of people walk in a long line. There are samurai on foot and on horseback, carrying spears and banners. There are servants carrying boxes and baggage. And in the middle, there is the lord himself, carried in a beautiful closed box called a palanquin.

[pause]

{img:daimyo-procession-road-pine-trees-samurai-banners-palanquin-farmers-kneeling}
When a daimyo procession passes, ordinary people must step aside and kneel down by the road, with their heads bowed. Shinta saw a procession once, on the edge of the city. It went on for almost an hour. He knelt by the road with his father, peeping up at the samurai in their fine clothes. He thought it was the most exciting thing he had ever seen.

[long-pause]

There is a samurai who lives in a house near the family's shop. His name is Mr Yamada, and he works as a clerk for a daimyo. He is a quiet, polite man with a small family. He sometimes buys lanterns from Tokichi. And sometimes, in the evening, he stops at the shop to talk about poetry with Oume. He writes short poems called haiku, with only seventeen syllables. He reads them to the grandmother, and she tells him honestly whether she likes them.

[pause]

{img:samurai-neighbour-reading-haiku-to-grandmother-shop-step-evening-lantern}
One evening, Mr Yamada reads a new haiku about the spring. It goes something like this. The old pond. A frog jumps in. The sound of water. Oume laughs. That is not yours, she says. That is by the great poet Basho, who lived a hundred years ago. Mr Yamada laughs too. You are right, he says. I was testing you. And you passed.

[pause]

Samurai children are taught very strictly. The boys learn to read the old Chinese books, and to sit very still for a long time. They learn sword fighting with wooden swords, and archery and horse riding. The girls learn to read and write, to sew and to arrange flowers. Mr Yamada's son is the same age as Shinta. The two boys sometimes play together by the river, although their fathers do not quite approve.

[pause]

In the evenings, many samurai like to walk by the river, or to sit in a tea house and talk. They discuss poetry and books, history and the news from the castle. Some of them practise the tea ceremony, a slow and peaceful way of making and drinking tea. Every movement is done with care and calm. It is a way of making the mind still and quiet.

[long-pause]

{img:tokaido-road-1800-pine-trees-travellers-straw-hats-fuji-distance}
From the Nihonbashi bridge in Edo, a great road leads all the way to Kyoto, the old capital. It is called the Tokaido, the Eastern Sea Road. It is about five hundred kilometres long. And it is the busiest road in Japan.

[pause]

The Tokaido runs along the coast for most of the way. On one side is the sea, and on the other side are the mountains. Along the road, there are tall pine trees, planted long ago to give shade to travellers. And there are fifty-three stations, or post towns, where travellers can stop to eat and sleep.

[long-pause]

All kinds of people travel on the Tokaido. There are daimyo with their great processions. There are merchants with their goods, and messengers running with letters. There are pilgrims on their way to famous temples and shrines. There are entertainers and peddlers, monks and nuns. Most people walk, wearing straw sandals and wide straw hats. Rich people are carried in palanquins. Nobody rides in carriages, because there are no carriages in Japan.

[pause]

{img:post-town-inn-tokaido-evening-lanterns-travellers-arriving-maids-welcoming}
In each post town, there are inns where travellers can stay. When you arrive in the evening, the maids of the inn come out to welcome you. They take your pack and help you take off your sandals. They bring you a bucket of warm water to wash your dusty feet. Then you have a hot bath and a good supper. You sleep on a futon on the tatami floor, in a room with other travellers. And in the morning, you set off again early.

[long-pause]

Oyuki's younger brother, Kanbei, is a traveller on the Tokaido. He is a seller of medicines. He walks from town to town with a big box of medicines on his back. He visits the same houses every year and leaves medicines with each family. The next year, he comes back, and they pay only for what they have used. He walks the whole Tokaido twice a year, from Edo to Kyoto and back.

[pause]

{img:medicine-peddler-walking-road-box-on-back-mountain-village-autumn}
When Kanbei comes to Edo, he stays with the family for a few days. The children love it. He tells them about the places he has seen. He tells them about the great river Oi, which has no bridge. Travellers must be carried across it on the shoulders of porters. He tells them about the mountain pass at Hakone, steep and misty, with a checkpoint where everyone must show their papers. He tells them about the sea at Kanagawa, with its great waves.

[long-pause]

This spring, Kanbei brings a present for the children. It is a set of little prints, showing scenes from the Tokaido road. There is a picture of the Nihonbashi bridge, crowded with people. There is a picture of travellers crossing the river on porters' shoulders. There is a picture of Mount Fuji, seen from a pine forest by the sea. The children spread them out on the tatami and look at them for hours.

[pause]

{img:children-looking-at-woodblock-prints-tatami-uncle-pointing-lamp}
Shinta says that when he grows up, he will walk the whole Tokaido, all the way to Kyoto. Ohana says she will come too. Oume laughs and says that she walked part of it once, when she was young. She went with her mother on a pilgrimage to the great shrine of Ise. It took more than a month, she says. It was the best journey of my life. And then she tells them all about it, until it is long past bedtime.

[pause]

In the post towns, there are also little shops selling the famous local foods and gifts of each place. In one town, they sell rice cakes covered in sweet bean flour. In another, they sell dried fish or special pickles. Travellers buy them as presents for their families at home. Kanbei always brings home something from each town. This year, he brings Oume a little box of sweet chestnut cakes.

[pause]

At night in the inns, the travellers sit together after supper and talk. They come from all over Japan, and they speak in different accents. They share news and stories, and they laugh at each other's jokes. Some of them play little games of chance. Some of them write poems about the road. And then they lie down on their futons, side by side, and fall asleep to the sound of the wind in the pines.

[pause]

Along the road, there are little tea houses where travellers can stop and rest. They sit on a bench outside, under a straw roof, and drink a cup of green tea. They eat a rice cake or a dumpling, and look at the view. Some tea houses are famous for their views of Mount Fuji or the sea. The tea house girls call out to travellers to stop and rest their feet.

[long-pause]

{img:cherry-blossom-hanami-ueno-hill-1800-families-picnic-under-trees}
In early April, the cherry trees of Edo burst into flower. And the whole city goes out to see them.

[pause]

The custom of going to look at the cherry blossoms is called hanami, which means flower viewing. It is one of the happiest times of the year. Families and friends go to the places where the cherry trees grow. They spread mats and cloths on the ground under the trees. They eat and drink, sing and dance, and look up at the flowers.

[long-pause]

There are several famous places for hanami in Edo. There is the hill of Ueno, with its temple and its hundreds of cherry trees. There is the bank of the Sumida river, lined with trees for kilometres. And there is a hill called Asukayama, on the edge of the city. The shogun himself had cherry trees planted there, about eighty years ago, so that ordinary people could enjoy them.

[pause]

{img:sumida-riverbank-cherry-blossoms-boats-petals-falling-water-spring}
The family goes to Asukayama on a sunny day in April. It is a long walk, about two hours. They set off early in the morning. Oyuki has prepared a beautiful picnic in a lacquered box. There are rice balls, wrapped in seaweed. There are grilled fish and boiled eggs, pickled vegetables and little sweet cakes. Tokichi carries a jug of sake, rice wine, for the grown-ups.

[long-pause]

When they arrive at the hill, it is already crowded with people. Every space under the trees is taken. There are families and groups of friends, old people and young people. Some have brought musical instruments. Some are dancing. Some are writing poems about the flowers and hanging them on the branches. The family finds a little spot under a tree and spreads out their mat.

[pause]

{img:family-picnic-mat-under-cherry-tree-lacquer-box-food-grandmother-laughing}
The cherry blossoms are beautiful. The trees are covered with pale pink flowers, so many that you can hardly see the branches. When the wind blows, the petals fall like soft pink snow. They fall on the mats and in the food and in people's hair. Shinta tries to catch them as they fall. Ohana collects them in her hand and lets them float away again.

[long-pause]

Oume sits under the tree, looking up at the blossoms. She is quiet for a long time. Then she says something to Ohana. The cherry blossoms are beautiful because they do not last, she says. A few days, and then they are gone. That is why we must look at them now, while we can. Ohana does not quite understand. But she looks up at the flowers very carefully, so she will remember them.

[pause]

{img:evening-cherry-blossoms-paper-lanterns-glowing-people-walking-home}
As the sun goes down, lanterns are lit under the trees. The cherry blossoms glow pale in the lantern light. People sing and laugh, and the music goes on. At last, the family packs up their picnic box and walks home in the dusk. Shinta is so tired that Tokichi carries him on his back. Ohana walks beside her grandmother, holding her hand. There are still cherry petals in her hair.

[pause]

Before the cherry blossoms, there are the plum blossoms. They come at the end of winter, when it is still cold. Plum flowers are white or deep pink, with a sweet smell. People go to see them too, wrapped in warm coats. Oume loves the plum blossoms even more than the cherries. They are brave, she says, because they bloom in the cold, before anything else.

[pause]

After hanami, the petals cover the ground under the trees like a pink carpet. They float on the river, in long drifting patches. Children scoop them up in their hands and throw them in the air. Within a week, the blossoms are all gone, and the trees are green with new leaves. People sigh and say, until next year. And they go back to their work.

[long-pause]

{img:ryogoku-bridge-fireworks-1800-summer-night-boats-crowds-river}
Summer in Edo is hot and humid. The cicadas sing all day in the trees, and people fan themselves to keep cool. But summer also brings one of the most beautiful sights of the year. The fireworks over the Sumida river.

[pause]

The fireworks are held near the Ryogoku bridge, a long wooden bridge across the Sumida. They begin on a summer evening, at the start of the season of cool evenings by the river. They are paid for by the boat owners and the restaurants by the river, who hope to attract customers. And they continue on many evenings through the summer.

[long-pause]

On the evening of the first fireworks, the whole city seems to go down to the river. People crowd onto the bridge and along the banks. The river is covered with boats. There are big pleasure boats, with roofs and lanterns, full of rich merchants having a party. There are small boats, rowed by one boatman, carrying a family or a few friends. And there are little boats that row from one big boat to another, selling food and drinks.

[pause]

{img:pleasure-boats-lanterns-sumida-river-summer-evening-music-feasting}
The family rents a small boat for the evening, as a special treat. Shinta is beside himself with excitement. He sits at the front of the boat and watches the boatman row. He asks the boatman a hundred questions. The boatman, a cheerful old man, answers every one. He even lets Shinta hold the oar for a moment. Shinta thinks it is the best day of his life.

[long-pause]

As it grows dark, the fireworks begin. There is a soft bang, and then a stream of golden sparks rises up into the sky. It bursts open like a flower, and the sparks fall slowly down toward the river. The crowds on the bridge cry out together: Tamaya! Kagiya! These are the names of the two great firework makers of Edo. People shout their names to cheer them on.

[pause]

{img:golden-firework-burst-night-sky-reflection-river-wooden-bridge-silhouettes}
The fireworks of 1800 are not as colourful as fireworks today. They are mostly gold and orange, the colour of sparks. But they are beautiful. There are fireworks like flowers and fireworks like willow trees, with long drooping branches of sparks. There are fireworks that spin and fireworks that crackle. Each one is reflected in the dark water of the river.

[long-pause]

Between the fireworks, the family eats a picnic in the boat. A little boat comes by selling slices of cool watermelon. Tokichi buys some, and they eat it, letting the juice drip into the river. Oume fans herself with a paper fan, painted with a picture of the moon. Oyuki leans back and looks up at the stars between the fireworks.

[pause]

{img:family-small-boat-eating-watermelon-paper-fan-night-river-lanterns}
Late in the evening, the last firework bursts high over the river. The crowds cheer one last time. Then the boats slowly row home, their lanterns bobbing on the dark water. The family's boatman rows them back to the landing near their street. Shinta has fallen asleep with his head in his mother's lap. Ohana is still looking up at the sky, hoping for one more firework. And the summer night is warm and soft and full of the sound of cicadas.

[pause]

In summer, the family also celebrates the festival of the ancestors, called Obon. They believe that the spirits of the ancestors come home to visit for a few days. They clean the family altar and put out fruit and flowers. They light little fires at the door to guide the spirits home. In the evenings, there is dancing in the streets, in big circles, to the music of drums and flutes.

[pause]

At the end of Obon, people place little paper lanterns on the river, with candles inside. The lanterns float slowly away on the dark water, carrying the spirits back to their world. Hundreds of little lights drift down the river together. It is very beautiful and very quiet. Ohana places a lantern that she and her father made together. She watches it float away until she cannot see it anymore.

[long-pause]

{img:kabuki-theatre-1800-stage-hanamichi-walkway-actors-audience-boxes}
The people of Edo love entertainment. And their two greatest loves are the kabuki theatre and sumo wrestling.

[pause]

Kabuki is a kind of theatre with music, dance and very colourful costumes. The actors wear thick white make-up and bold red and black lines on their faces. All the actors are men, even those who play women. Some actors are famous for playing beautiful women, and they do it so well that people forget they are men. The famous actors are the great stars of Edo.

[long-pause]

A kabuki play lasts all day long, from morning until evening. People bring food and drink, and eat during the play. They talk and laugh and shout out to the actors. When a famous actor makes a dramatic pose, the audience shouts his family name. There is a long walkway through the audience, called the hanamichi. The actors walk along it to the stage, so the audience can see them up close.

[pause]

{img:kabuki-actor-dramatic-pose-red-black-face-makeup-costume-stage-lights}
Oume loves the kabuki more than anything. Once or twice a year, the family saves up enough money for her to go. This year, Tokichi takes her for her birthday, with Ohana. They set off early in the morning and walk to the theatre district. The theatres have big colourful signs and banners outside, with pictures of the actors. Crowds of people are arriving, in their best kimonos.

[long-pause]

The play is a famous old story about loyal samurai who avenge their lord. It is long and sad and exciting. Oume knows every word. She whispers the lines along with the actors. When her favourite actor appears, she shouts his name with the rest of the audience. Ohana watches with wide eyes. She does not understand everything. But she loves the costumes and the music and the dancing. At the sad parts, she sees her grandmother wiping her eyes with her sleeve.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-girl-theatre-box-watching-play-eating-bento-delighted}
Sumo wrestling is the other great love of Edo. Sumo is a very old Japanese sport. Two big, strong wrestlers face each other in a ring of sand. They try to push each other out of the ring, or to make the other one touch the ground with anything but his feet. A match often lasts only a few seconds. But before it starts, there is a long ceremony, full of old traditions.

[long-pause]

The wrestlers are huge men, much bigger than ordinary people. They wear their hair in a special knot on top of their heads. Before the match, they stamp their feet and throw salt into the ring to make it pure. They crouch and stare at each other. Then they spring forward, and the crowd roars. The great sumo tournaments are held twice a year, in the grounds of temples, with big crowds watching.

[pause]

{img:sumo-wrestlers-ring-sand-throwing-salt-temple-grounds-crowd-1800}
Shinta's great hero is a famous sumo wrestler called Raiden. Raiden is the strongest wrestler in Edo, perhaps in all of Japan. He is almost two metres tall, and he almost never loses. Shinta has a little print of Raiden, which he keeps under his pillow. One day, he saw Raiden walking along the street near the river, surrounded by admirers. Raiden looked down at the little boy and smiled. Shinta did not sleep at all that night, from excitement.

[pause]

After the play, Oume buys a new print of her favourite actor, from a shop near the theatre. It shows him in his costume from the play they have just seen. She holds it very carefully all the way home. That evening, she adds it to her collection, in a special box lined with silk. She looks through all the prints again, one by one, remembering each play.

[pause]

The sumo wrestlers live together in special houses, called stables, with their teacher. They get up very early and train hard all morning. Then they eat a big stew of fish and vegetables and tofu, to make them strong and heavy. In the afternoon, they sleep. Shinta thinks this sounds like a wonderful life. His mother laughs and says he would have to eat a great deal of stew.

[pause]

Between the acts of the play, there is time to eat. Little boxes of food are sold in the theatre, with rice and fish and pickles, all neatly arranged. People eat them in their boxes, still talking about the play. Oume and Ohana share a box of food and a pot of tea. They are so busy talking about the actors that they almost miss the start of the next act.

[long-pause]

{img:woodblock-print-shop-edo-1800-colourful-prints-hanging-customers-browsing}
In Edo, there are many shops that sell colourful pictures. They are called woodblock prints. And they are one of the most famous art forms of Japan.

[pause]

The prints show many things. They show famous kabuki actors in their roles. They show beautiful women in elegant kimonos. They show sumo wrestlers, and scenes from famous stories. And more and more, they show beautiful landscapes: mountains and rivers, waterfalls and bridges, the sea and Mount Fuji. These pictures are called ukiyo-e, which means pictures of the floating world.

[long-pause]

Making a woodblock print takes the work of several people. First, an artist draws the picture with a brush on thin paper. Then a carver glues the drawing onto a block of smooth cherry wood. He carves away all the wood that is not part of the lines, so the lines stand up from the block. This needs a very sharp knife and a very steady hand. For a colour print, he must carve a separate block for each colour.

[pause]

{img:woodblock-carver-cherry-wood-block-small-knife-careful-lines-workshop}
Then a printer takes over. He brushes ink or paint onto the block. He lays a sheet of damp paper on top. Then he rubs the back of the paper with a round flat pad, called a baren. The ink goes from the block onto the paper. Then he does the same with the next block, and the next colour. Each sheet must be placed exactly right, so the colours fit together. A good printer can make hundreds of copies of one picture.

[long-pause]

Because they can be printed many times, the prints are cheap. Ordinary people can buy them, for about the price of a bowl of noodles. They hang them on their walls or keep them in boxes. They collect prints of their favourite actors, like people today collect pictures of film stars.

[pause]

{img:young-artist-hokusai-drawing-brush-paper-small-room-sketches-everywhere}
In 1800, there is an artist in Edo called Hokusai. He is about forty years old. He is a little strange, and very hard-working. He moves house many times, more than ninety times in his life. He changes his name many times too. He draws all day and every day. He draws people and animals, flowers and birds, ghosts and gods, mountains and waves.

[long-pause]

In 1800, Hokusai is already known in Edo. But his greatest works are still in the future. About thirty years later, when he is an old man, he will make a famous series of prints. It is called Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji. One of those prints will show a great wave, curling over little boats, with Mount Fuji far away behind it. It will become one of the most famous pictures in the world.

[pause]

{img:mount-fuji-seen-from-edo-bay-boats-calm-sea-print-style-landscape}
Ohana loves the prints. She often stops outside the print shop on her way home from school, to look at the new ones. She likes the pictures of beautiful places best. She looks at the mountains and the rivers and the waterfalls. She dreams of seeing them one day. And at home, she tries to draw them herself, with her brush. Tokichi says she has a good hand. Perhaps one day, he says, you will paint lanterns better than I do. Ohana blushes with pleasure.

[pause]

There are also printed books for sale in Edo, with pictures and stories. There are funny books and sad books, books of ghost stories and books of travel. There are lending libraries too. A man walks from house to house with a big pack of books on his back. People can borrow a book for a few days, for a small fee. Oume borrows a new book every week.

[pause]

Some prints are made for New Year as special gifts. They are printed with gold and silver, on very fine paper. Rich people order them and give them to their friends, like greeting cards. Others are calendars, cleverly hidden in a picture. And some prints are made for children, with pictures of animals and games and little stories. Shinta has one with a picture of a fat cat dressed as a samurai. It makes him laugh every time.

[long-pause]

{img:edo-winter-dry-north-wind-streets-people-wrapped-cold-clear-sky}
Winter comes to Edo. The days are short and cold. The sky is clear and blue, and you can see Mount Fuji very clearly, white with snow. But a strong, dry wind blows from the north-west, day after day. And everyone in Edo knows what that wind can bring.

[pause]

In winter, the people of Edo are very careful with fire. They put out their braziers at night. They keep buckets of water by the door. The night watchmen walk the streets more often, clapping their wooden clappers and calling out. Beware of fire. Be careful with your fire. Every neighbourhood has its own fire watchtower, a tall wooden tower with a bell at the top.

[long-pause]

One night in January, Tokichi is woken by the sound of the fire bell. It is ringing fast, again and again. He gets up quickly and opens the shutter. In the distance, to the north, the sky is glowing orange. There is a fire, perhaps a kilometre away. And the wind is blowing toward them.

[pause]

{img:fire-watchtower-bell-ringing-night-orange-glow-distance-edo-rooftops}
Tokichi wakes the whole family. Everyone gets dressed quickly, in warm clothes. Oyuki packs the most important things into bundles. There is the money box and the account book, the family's records and the little family altar. Oume takes her box of actor prints. Ohana takes her writing box. Shinta takes the little goldfish lantern. They carry everything down to the street, ready to leave if they must.

[long-pause]

In the street, the neighbours are all doing the same. Everyone is calm, but quick. They have done this before. The children are wrapped in blankets. The old people are helped along. Mr Yamada, the samurai neighbour, comes to help Oume carry her things. Everyone watches the orange glow in the sky, and the smoke drifting over the roofs.

[pause]

{img:edo-firefighters-matoi-standard-bearer-rooftop-teams-ladders-night}
The fire fighters of Edo are already at work. They are brave men, organised in teams, each team for its own district. Each team has a man who carries a special standard, called a matoi. He climbs onto a roof near the fire and holds up the matoi high, to show where his team is working. The fire fighters cannot put out a big fire with water. Instead, they pull down the houses in the path of the fire, with long hooks and ladders. That makes a gap that the fire cannot cross.

[long-pause]

The family waits in the street for a long time. The night is very cold. Oyuki gives everyone a little hot tea from a pot she has brought. Shinta holds his father's hand tightly. Ohana stands close to her grandmother. The glow in the sky grows brighter, and then it stays the same. Then, very slowly, it begins to grow dimmer. Toward dawn, the wind changes direction. And word comes down the street from house to house. The fire fighters have made a gap. The fire has stopped.

[pause]

{img:dawn-after-fire-smoke-thinning-neighbours-relieved-street-bundles-tea}
Everyone breathes out. Some people laugh, and some cry with relief. The family carries their bundles back into the house. Nobody can sleep. So Oyuki makes rice and soup for everyone, and the neighbours come in to share it. In the morning, Tokichi and Sukeroku go to the burned district to help. Many families there have lost their houses. The people of the neighbourhood bring food and blankets and clothes. Tokichi gives away twenty lanterns, so the families can light their temporary shelters. And within a few weeks, the carpenters of Edo are already building new houses, of fresh pale wood.

[pause]

Every neighbourhood in Edo has its own team of fire fighters, made up of local men. They are mostly carpenters and builders, who know how houses are made. They are very proud of their work. They wear thick padded coats, soaked with water before they go near a fire. Each team has its own coat design and its own matoi. People admire them very much.

[pause]

In the weeks after the fire, the whole neighbourhood helps the families who lost their homes. People share their rooms and their food. The temples open their halls for people to sleep in. The carpenters work from dawn to dusk building new houses. Tokichi and Sukeroku make lanterns for the new houses, for free. And slowly, the burned district comes back to life, with new pale wooden houses smelling of fresh cedar.

[long-pause]

{img:edo-new-year-1800-pine-bamboo-decorations-shop-doors-snow-morning}
The most important holiday of the year in Japan is the New Year, called shogatsu. And the family has been getting ready for weeks.

[pause]

At the end of December, everyone cleans their house from top to bottom. It is called the great cleaning. They sweep the floors and wash the screens. They beat the tatami mats outside to get the dust out. They replace the old torn paper on the shoji with fresh new paper. The whole city is busy cleaning, so that the new year can begin fresh and clean.

[long-pause]

Then they decorate their doors. On each side of the front door, they put a little arrangement of pine branches and bamboo. It is called kadomatsu, the gate pine. Pine and bamboo stay green all winter, so they are signs of long life and strength. Above the door, they hang a rope of twisted straw with white paper strips. It keeps bad luck away.

[pause]

{img:pounding-mochi-rice-cakes-wooden-mortar-mallet-neighbours-courtyard-steam}
A few days before New Year, the neighbours gather to make mochi, rice cakes. They steam sticky rice in big baskets. Then they put it into a big wooden mortar. Two men take turns pounding it with heavy wooden mallets. Bang, bang, bang. A woman quickly turns the rice between the blows, with wet hands. It is noisy and fun, and everyone watches and cheers. Slowly, the rice becomes a soft, smooth, sticky dough. Then it is shaped into round cakes.

[long-pause]

On the last night of the year, the temple bells ring a hundred and eight times. People believe that each ring of the bell takes away one bad thought or worry from the old year. The family sits together at home and eats long soba noodles. Long noodles mean a long life. Then they listen to the bells, ringing slowly through the cold night, near and far, across the whole city.

[pause]

{img:temple-bell-ringing-new-years-eve-night-monks-snow-lanterns-crowd}
On New Year's morning, very early, Tokichi takes the children up to the roof of the house. They want to see the first sunrise of the new year. It is very cold, and they are wrapped in quilted coats. They wait, shivering, as the sky in the east turns pink and gold. Then the sun comes up, red and round, over the rooftops of Edo. They put their hands together and make a wish for the new year.

[long-pause]

Then the whole family eats a special breakfast. There is a soup with mochi rice cakes floating in it. There are special dishes arranged in beautiful lacquered boxes, each one with a lucky meaning. There are black beans for good health, and little dried fish for a good harvest. There is a sweet chestnut dish for wealth. Everyone drinks a little special sake, even the children, just a sip.

[pause]

{img:family-new-year-breakfast-lacquer-boxes-new-kimono-girl-bowing}
Ohana wears her new kimono of pale blue cotton with white plum flowers. She looks at herself in the little mirror and smiles. Then the children bow to their parents and grandmother and wish them a happy new year. The grown-ups give the children little envelopes with a few coins inside. Later, the whole family puts on their best clothes and goes to the shrine. They pray for good luck in the new year. And on the way home, they buy a little paper fortune and tie it to a tree branch, for luck.

[pause]

During the first days of the new year, people visit their friends and relatives. They bow at the door and wish each other a happy new year. They bring small gifts, like sweets or sake or a little towel. Children play special New Year games. The girls play a game with wooden paddles and a shuttlecock, hitting it back and forth. The boys fly kites high over the rooftops.

[pause]

Shinta flies a kite that his father made for him, with a picture of a red carp painted on it. He runs along the riverbank, and the kite rises up into the cold blue sky. It flies higher and higher, until the carp is a little red dot above the city. Shinta holds the string tight and laughs. Shiro the dog runs beside him, barking at the kite.

[long-pause]

{img:american-black-ships-1853-edo-bay-steam-smoke-japanese-boats-watching}
The peaceful world of Edo lasted for about seventy more years after our story. Then, everything changed.

[pause]

In 1853, four strange ships sailed into the bay near Edo. They were American warships, painted black, with tall chimneys pouring out smoke. The Japanese had never seen steamships before. They called them the black ships. The American commander, Commodore Perry, brought a letter from the president of the United States. The Americans wanted Japan to open its harbours to foreign ships and trade.

[long-pause]

After the black ships came, Japan had to open its doors to the world. Foreign traders and diplomats came to Japan. Japanese people began to travel abroad. They saw railways and factories, telegraphs and great cities. There was much argument in Japan about what to do. In the end, the old order came to an end. In 1868, the last shogun gave up his power to the young emperor.

[pause]

{img:meiji-era-tokyo-1870s-mixed-western-japanese-clothes-street-rickshaw}
The emperor moved from Kyoto to Edo. And the city was given a new name: Tokyo, which means Eastern Capital. The emperor moved into the old castle of the shogun. The samurai lost their special place, and they were no longer allowed to wear their swords. Japan began to change very quickly. In a few decades, there were railways, factories, schools and newspapers all over the country.

[long-pause]

But many things from the old Edo days stayed. People still went to the public bath in the evenings. They still ate soba and sushi and tempura. They still went to see the cherry blossoms in spring, and the fireworks over the river in summer. The kabuki theatre and the sumo wrestling were still loved. And families still celebrated the New Year in the old way, with pine at the door and mochi in the soup.

[pause]

{img:ukiyo-e-prints-in-paris-1870s-artists-admiring-japanese-art-studio}
And something interesting happened with the woodblock prints. When Japan opened to the world, the prints began to travel to Europe. At first, they were used as wrapping paper for pottery! But artists in Paris saw them and were amazed. Painters like Van Gogh and Monet loved the bold colours, the simple lines and the unusual views. They collected the prints and learned from them. Van Gogh even painted copies of some of them. Japanese prints changed the way European artists painted.

[long-pause]

Hokusai lived until 1849, almost ninety years old. He went on drawing until the very end of his life. He said that when he was a hundred, he hoped his drawings would come truly alive. Today, his great wave is one of the most famous pictures in the world. It is printed on posters and cups and bags and shirts, everywhere.

[pause]

{img:old-hokusai-ninety-drawing-brush-quiet-room-daughter-beside-him}
And the old craftsmen of Edo? Many of their crafts are still alive today. In Tokyo, there are still lantern makers who make lanterns by hand, with bamboo and washi paper. There are still umbrella makers and woodblock printers. There are still makers of combs and fans, sweets and pickles, in shops that have been run by the same family for many generations. Perhaps one of them is a descendant of Tokichi, still painting cranes and moons on paper lanterns, two hundred years later.

[pause]

The people who had lived in Edo in 1800 did not see these great changes. Most of them lived quiet lives, working and raising their families, in the long peace. They went to the bath and the theatre, they saw the cherry blossoms and the fireworks. Their children grew up and had children of their own. And it was their grandchildren and great-grandchildren who saw the black ships come.

[pause]

In 1868, there was a moment of danger for the city. The armies of the new government came toward Edo, and people feared there would be fighting in the streets. But two wise men, one from each side, met and talked. They agreed that the castle would be handed over peacefully. And so the great city was spared. The people of Edo went on with their lives, under a new name.

[long-pause]

{img:tokyo-today-nihonbashi-modern-city-old-stone-bridge-under-highway-today}
Today, the city of Edo is Tokyo. It is one of the biggest cities in the world, with millions of people. There are tall towers of glass and steel, busy trains and bright lights everywhere.

[pause]

The Nihonbashi bridge is still there, in the middle of the city. It is now a stone bridge, built more than a hundred years ago. Every distance in Japan is still measured from this bridge, just as in the time of the Tokaido. The old shogun's castle is now the home of the emperor. Its great stone walls and wide moats are still there, surrounded by green parks.

[long-pause]

The great temple of Asakusa still stands beside the Sumida river. Its gate still has a giant red lantern hanging in the middle. The long street of little shops still leads to the temple hall. People still buy sweets and toys and lucky charms there. And they still wave the incense smoke over themselves, for good health.

[pause]

{img:asakusa-today-red-lantern-gate-visitors-kimono-incense-today}
In spring, the cherry trees of Ueno and the Sumida river still blossom. And millions of people still go out to see them. They spread their mats under the trees, eat and drink, and look up at the flowers. In summer, there are still fireworks over the Sumida river, every year. Hundreds of thousands of people come to watch. And some of them still shout Tamaya, as people did more than two hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

There are still public baths in Tokyo, though fewer than before. Many of them still have big paintings of Mount Fuji on the walls. Old people still go there every evening to soak and chat. And there are still sumo tournaments in Tokyo, in a big hall near the old Ryogoku bridge. The wrestlers still throw salt into the ring and stamp their feet, just as Raiden did.

[pause]

{img:sumo-hall-ryogoku-today-wrestlers-ring-crowd-ceremony-today}
In some parts of Tokyo, there are still narrow streets with small wooden houses and little shops. There are noodle shops and tea houses and old-fashioned sweet shops. There are little shrines tucked between the houses, with red gates and stone foxes. Old women still water the potted plants outside their doors. And at dusk, some of the shops still hang a paper lantern outside, glowing softly in the evening.

[long-pause]

Mount Fuji is still there too, far to the west of the city. On clear winter days, you can see it from the tall buildings of Tokyo. Its white peak rises above the endless city, just as it rose above the wooden rooftops of Edo. Many people climb it in the summer, to watch the sunrise from the top. And many people still think it is the most beautiful mountain in the world.

[pause]

{img:mount-fuji-winter-sunset-seen-from-tokyo-city-silhouette-today}
At New Year, families in Tokyo still put pine branches by their doors. They still eat long noodles on New Year's Eve, and listen to the temple bells ring a hundred and eight times. They still eat mochi in their soup on New Year's morning. And many of them still go up early, to see the first sunrise of the new year. The great city has changed in almost every way. But in its heart, a little of old Edo still remains.

[pause]

There are museums in Tokyo where you can see how people lived in Edo. In one of them, there is a full-size copy of the Nihonbashi bridge, made of wood, as it was long ago. There are models of nagaya houses and shops, of the kabuki theatre and the fish market. You can walk through them and imagine the busy streets of old Edo. Children love to visit these museums.

[pause]

In the old parts of Tokyo, there are still festivals in summer. People carry portable shrines through the streets on their shoulders, shouting and singing. There are dancers and drummers and food stalls. Old men and young men carry the shrines together, sweating in the heat. And grandmothers watch from their doorways, fanning themselves, just as Oume did long ago.

[long-pause]

{img:edo-late-spring-afternoon-lantern-shop-quiet-street-swallows-1800}
Before we leave Edo, let us go back once more, to an ordinary afternoon in late spring, in the year 1800.

[pause]

The cherry blossoms are over now, and the trees along the river are green with new leaves. Swallows have come back from the south, and they are building their nests under the eaves of the houses. In the lantern shop, Tokichi is working on a big order of lanterns for a temple festival. Sukeroku is helping him, splitting bamboo into thin strips. The apprentice is getting better every week. Today, Tokichi let him paste the paper on a lantern all by himself.

[long-pause]

Oyuki is sitting at the front of the shop, writing in her account book with a brush. A neighbour stops at the door to chat about the weather and the price of rice. Down the street, a peddler is calling out that he has fresh bamboo shoots. Oyuki thinks she might buy some for supper. Bamboo shoots with a little soy sauce and fish flakes is Tokichi's favourite spring dish.

[pause]

{img:mother-shop-front-account-book-brush-neighbour-chatting-afternoon-sun}
Oume is sitting in the little garden at the back, on a cushion in the sun. She is sewing a new cover for a cushion, with tiny stitches. Now and then, she stops and listens to the water dripping in the stone basin. A little brown sparrow comes down to drink from it. She keeps very still, so it will not fly away.

[long-pause]

Ohana has come home from school. She is sitting at her little desk upstairs, by the window, practising her characters. Today she is writing the character for spring. It has many strokes, and it is hard to make it beautiful. She has written it twenty times already. The twenty-first one is the best. She holds it up and looks at it, and decides to show it to her grandmother.

[pause]

{img:girl-upstairs-window-calligraphy-spring-character-afternoon-light-street}
And Shinta is down by the river, as usual. He is sitting on the wooden steps of a landing, with his chin in his hands. The boats go past, one after another. There is the vegetable boat, the fish boat, and the boat that carries rice. The old boatman who rowed them at the fireworks waves to him. Shinta waves back. One day, he thinks, that will be me.

[long-pause]

In the late afternoon, the family goes to the bath together, as always. They soak in the hot water and chat with the neighbours. Then they walk home, warm and pink, through the evening street. They eat supper together around the little low tables. There is rice and soup, grilled fish and bamboo shoots with soy sauce. Tokichi says it is the best supper he has had all year. He says that every spring.

[pause]

{img:family-supper-low-tables-evening-lantern-light-rice-bamboo-shoots}
After supper, Mr Yamada stops by the shop. He has written a new haiku, his own this time. He reads it to Oume. It is about a swallow, building its nest under the roof of a lantern shop. Oume listens, and then she is quiet for a moment. That one, she says, is good. Mr Yamada bows deeply, very pleased. And the evening light fades slowly over the street.

[pause]

Late in the afternoon, a tofu seller comes down the street with his little horn. Oyuki buys a block of fresh tofu for supper. The tofu seller is an old friend. He asks about the children and the grandmother. He tells Oyuki that his daughter is getting married next month. Oyuki is delighted. She promises to make a special lantern for the wedding.

[pause]

Later, when the children are in bed, Tokichi sits by the lamp and finishes the last lantern of the day. He paints a crane flying across a full moon, with long slow strokes of his brush. When it is finished, he holds it up and looks at it. It is good. He puts it on the shelf with the others, ready for the temple festival. Then he blows out the lamp and goes to bed.

[long-pause]

{img:edo-evening-1800-sumida-river-sunset-fuji-silhouette-lanterns-boats}
So we leave Edo tonight, in the year 1800, as the evening falls.

[pause]

The sun is setting behind Mount Fuji, far away to the west. The mountain stands dark against a sky of gold and pink and orange. The Sumida river shines like a ribbon of light. The boats are going home, their small lanterns bobbing on the water. A temple bell rings slowly, somewhere across the city. And the swallows are flying low over the river, catching the last insects of the day.

[long-pause]

In the street by the river, the shops are closing. The blue noren curtains are being taken down, one by one. The night watchman is beginning his rounds, with his wooden clappers. Beware of fire, he calls softly. Beware of fire. In the houses, the lamps are being lit, and the paper screens glow warm and golden.

[pause]

{img:edo-street-dusk-paper-screens-glowing-watchman-clappers-lantern-shop}
In the lantern shop, Tokichi lights the big lantern outside the door. It glows warm and red against the darkening sky. Inside, Oyuki is laying out the futons on the tatami. Sukeroku is sweeping the last bamboo shavings from the floor. Oume is sitting by the brazier, warming her hands, humming a song from her favourite kabuki play.

Ohana is lying on her futon, looking at the little print of Mount Fuji her uncle gave her. She traces the shape of the mountain with her finger. Shinta is already half asleep. Above his futon, the little goldfish lantern glows softly in the dark, red and gold. He looks at it with sleepy eyes. Then his eyes close.

Oyuki slides the paper screen closed, quietly. She sits down for a moment beside Tokichi in the front room. They do not say much. They listen to the sounds of the evening. The clappers of the watchman, far away down the street. A boatman singing on the river. A dog barking once, and then silence. The soft hiss of the charcoal in the brazier.

[long-pause]

Outside, the stars come out over the great city, one by one. They shine over the shogun's castle and the temples. They shine over the wooden houses and the narrow streets. They shine over the river and the bridges and the little boats tied up for the night. And far away to the west, Mount Fuji stands quiet and white in the starlight, watching over the sleeping city of Edo.
