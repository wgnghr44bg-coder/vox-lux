Tonight we travel back more than eight hundred years, to the year 1190. We go to the middle of England, to the county of Nottingham. And there, stretching for many kilometres to the north of the town, there is a great royal forest. Ancient oak trees, quiet glades, heathland and little villages. It is called Sherwood. And in the stories, it is the home of the most famous outlaw in the world. Robin Hood.

[long-pause]

It is early morning in spring. A soft mist lies among the trees. The sun is rising, and its light comes through the branches in long golden beams. Birds are singing everywhere. Blackbirds, thrushes, wrens and robins. A woodpecker is tapping on a dead tree. And in a clearing, a group of deer are grazing quietly, their ears turning this way and that, listening to the sounds of the forest.

[pause]

The oak trees of Sherwood are very old. Some of them are hundreds of years old already. Their trunks are huge and gnarled, and their great branches spread out wide, like the arms of giants. Under them, the ground is covered with bracken and grass, and in spring, with bluebells, a carpet of soft blue flowers stretching away under the trees. It is one of the most beautiful places in England.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

But Sherwood is not an empty wilderness. People live here. In little villages, with thatched houses and small stone churches, surrounded by fields and pastures. They farm the land, keep animals, cut wood and make charcoal. They live in the forest, and from the forest. And they live under special laws, because the forest belongs to the king.

[pause]

Tonight, we will spend a year in Sherwood Forest, with one of these families. We will see how they live and work, among the great oaks. We will meet the foresters, who guard the king's deer, and the monks of the abbey in the woods. We will visit the town and the castle of Nottingham. And we will follow the legend of Robin Hood. The outlaw in green, who lived in the forest with his merry men. Who took from the rich and gave to the poor. Who was the best archer in England. We will meet Little John and Friar Tuck, Maid Marian and the Sheriff of Nottingham. And we will ask the old question. Was there ever a real Robin Hood?

{img:bluebells-carpet-under-ancient-oaks-sherwood-spring-morning-soft-light}
There are some quarrels and some fights in the stories of Robin Hood. We will see them only from a distance, and gently, as if they were a game. Because most of all, the legend of Robin Hood is about other things. About friendship and fairness. About kindness to the poor. And about the green forest, where people could be free. So let us begin, in the misty morning, under the old oaks of Sherwood, in the year 1190.

[long-pause]

{img:old-ballad-singer-medieval-market-crowd-listening-story-of-robin}
Robin Hood is one of the most famous heroes in the world. Everyone knows a little about him. The outlaw in Lincoln green, with his bow and arrows, living in Sherwood Forest. His band of merry men. His enemy, the wicked Sheriff of Nottingham. His love, Maid Marian. His friends, Little John, Friar Tuck and Will Scarlet. And his famous habit of taking from the rich, and giving to the poor.

[long-pause]

But where do these stories come from? Like the stories of King Arthur, the stories of Robin Hood grew slowly, over hundreds of years. They began as songs and rhymes, sung by ordinary people, in villages and taverns, at fairs and festivals. Nobody knows who made them first. They were passed on from person to person, by word of mouth, and changed a little each time.

[pause]

The first time that Robin Hood is mentioned in writing is in a long poem from about the year 1377, called Piers Plowman. In it, a lazy priest says that he does not know his prayers very well. But he knows the rhymes of Robin Hood. So by that time, the songs about Robin were already well known, and very popular. More popular than prayers, it seemed.

[pause]

{img:medieval-manuscript-page-ballad-robin-hood-text-margin-drawings}
The oldest stories about Robin that survive were written down in the fourteen hundreds. They are long poems, called ballads, which were sung or recited. One of the longest is called A Gest of Robyn Hode, which means a tale of Robin Hood. In these early stories, Robin is a yeoman. Not a lord, and not a poor peasant, but a free countryman, who owns a little land. He lives in the forest, with his men, and they are clever, brave and full of tricks.

[pause]

In the early stories, Robin lives mostly in a forest called Barnsdale, in Yorkshire, a little to the north of Sherwood. But he also goes to Sherwood, and to Nottingham, where the sheriff lives. Later, Sherwood became his main home in the stories. And today, everyone connects Robin Hood with Sherwood Forest and Nottingham.

[long-pause]

{img:king-richard-lionheart-returning-legend-forest-meeting-outlaw-later-story}
In the early stories, the king is called simply Edward, our comely king. But later storytellers placed Robin in the time of King Richard the Lionheart and his brother, Prince John. Around the year 1190. Richard was away on crusade, and John was trying to take the throne. Robin became a loyal supporter of the good king Richard, fighting against the wicked John and his sheriff. That is the version most people know today.

[pause]

Was there a real Robin Hood? Historians have searched the old records for hundreds of years. They have found several men called Robin Hood, or Robert Hod, in the twelve and thirteen hundreds. One of them was an outlaw in Yorkshire, in 1225. But nobody can prove that any of them was the Robin of the stories. Robin was a very common name. And Hood was a common surname too, for a man who made or wore hoods.

[pause]

{img:old-oak-tree-forest-hollow-trunk-legend-hiding-place-afternoon-light}
Perhaps there was a real outlaw, long ago, whose adventures were remembered, and made bigger and bigger in the telling. Perhaps several outlaws were mixed together into one hero. Perhaps Robin was always a figure of stories, like a hero of the old May festivals. We will probably never know. But that does not matter very much. Because the real world in which the stories are set, the world of Sherwood Forest in the Middle Ages, was very real. And tonight, we will visit it.

[long-pause]

So let us leave the legend for a while, and walk into the real forest. To a little village, at the edge of the trees, where a family is just waking up, in the spring of 1190. And later, when the evening comes, we will sit by the fire, and listen to the old stories of Robin Hood, as the people of Sherwood would tell them in later centuries.

[long-pause]

{img:sherwood-landscape-1190-heath-birch-oak-woods-open-glades-villages-distant}
What was Sherwood Forest really like, in the year 1190? Today, when we hear the word forest, we think of a place full of trees. But in the Middle Ages, the word forest meant something different. A forest was an area of land that belonged to the king, where he could hunt. It could contain woods, but also heathland, fields, villages, rivers and even small towns.

[long-pause]

Sherwood Forest is very big. It stretches for about thirty kilometres from north to south, and about twelve kilometres from east to west. It covers about a fifth of the whole county of Nottingham. In the south, it reaches almost to the walls of the town of Nottingham. In the north, it reaches as far as the town of Worksop. And within it, there are about twenty villages.

[pause]

Much of Sherwood is covered by woods. Great old oak trees, and birches, with their white bark and their delicate leaves. But there are also wide open heaths, covered with heather, bracken and gorse, with its bright yellow flowers. The soil is sandy and poor, not very good for farming. That is one reason why so much of it has stayed wild. The kings found it a perfect place for hunting.

[pause]

{img:heath-with-heather-gorse-yellow-flowers-birch-trees-sherwood-summer}
The name Sherwood means the wood of the shire, the wood that belongs to the whole county. It has been there for a very long time. Long before the Normans came, the Anglo-Saxons knew it, and used it, and named it. And after the Norman conquest of England in 1066, the new Norman kings made it a royal forest, a hunting ground for the king, with strict laws.

[pause]

The forest is full of animals. Red deer, the biggest wild animals in England, with great antlers on the stags. Smaller roe deer. Wild boar, although they are becoming rare. Foxes, badgers, hares, rabbits and squirrels. Wolves have almost disappeared from this part of England, but people still tell stories about them. There are hawks, owls, woodpeckers and many songbirds. And in the rivers and ponds, there are fish, otters and herons.

[long-pause]

{img:red-deer-stag-antlers-standing-in-bracken-oak-forest-misty-morning}
The most important animals, for the king, are the deer. They are called the king's deer. Nobody else may hunt them, or harm them in any way, on pain of severe punishment. The whole forest is managed for them. Their food is protected. Their resting places are protected. Even their peace and quiet is protected. They are the reason why the forest exists.

[pause]

In the middle of Sherwood, there is a royal hunting lodge, at a place called Clipstone. It is called the King's Houses. When the king comes to hunt in Sherwood, he stays there, with his court. King Henry the Second, the father of Richard and John, came there several times. He had the lodge improved, with new buildings, a chapel and a fishpond. It is quite a grand place, for a hunting lodge, in the middle of the forest.

[pause]

{img:royal-hunting-lodge-clipstone-stone-hall-forest-clearing-horses}
There is also a monastery in Sherwood. Rufford Abbey, founded about forty years before, by monks of the Cistercian order, who wear white robes and live very simply. They farm the land around their abbey, keep sheep, and pray. And in the south of the forest, near Nottingham, a new priory is being built at Newstead, by monks called Augustinian canons. Both of them are quiet, peaceful places, among the trees.

[long-pause]

So Sherwood in 1190 is a living landscape. Woods and heaths, villages and fields, a royal lodge and quiet abbeys. People and animals living side by side. It is not quite the wild green forest of the stories, where outlaws could hide forever. But it is big enough, and wild enough in places, that someone who wanted to disappear could certainly find somewhere to hide.

[pause]

Through the forest run old roads and tracks. The great road from London to the north, which travellers, merchants and messengers use. Smaller paths between the villages. And deer paths, winding through the bracken, which only the foresters and the people of the forest know. Here and there, at a crossing of paths, there is a stone cross, or a little shrine, where travellers stop to say a prayer for a safe journey.

[long-pause]

{img:forest-law-court-medieval-verderers-seated-wooden-hall-villagers-standing}
Living in a royal forest is not easy. Because the forest has its own laws. They are called the forest laws. And they are very different from the ordinary laws of England. They were made by the Norman kings, to protect the deer and the woods for their hunting. And the people of Sherwood must obey them, every day of their lives.

[long-pause]

The most important rule is about the deer. Nobody except the king, and those he gives permission to, may hunt the deer. Not even a nobleman. Anyone caught with a dead deer, or a bow and arrows in the forest without good reason, is in serious trouble. In the past, the punishments were very harsh. By 1190, most offenders pay a heavy fine. But it is still a very serious matter.

[pause]

There are many other rules. People who live in the forest may not keep big dogs, unless the dogs have three claws removed from their front feet, so that they cannot chase deer. People may not cut down trees, or clear new land for farming, without permission. They may not build fences high enough to keep out the deer. They may not carry bows and arrows in the forest. Even picking up fallen branches is controlled.

[pause]

{img:villager-gathering-fallen-wood-bundle-forest-floor-permitted-autumn}
But the forest people also have rights, which are very important to them. They are allowed to gather fallen wood for their fires, but only wood they can take by hook or by crook, which means pulling down dead branches with a hooked stick. They are allowed to let their pigs eat the acorns in the forest in autumn, for a small payment. They are allowed to let their cattle graze on the open heaths. And they can cut bracken and heather for bedding and thatch.

[pause]

The forest is guarded by officials. The most important is the chief forester, who is in charge of the whole forest. Under him, there are foresters, who ride through the woods, watching for poachers and anyone breaking the rules. There are verderers, who are local knights and gentlemen, who look after the forest and hold the forest courts. And there are agisters, who collect the payments for the pigs and the cattle.

[long-pause]

{img:forester-on-horseback-green-tunic-horn-patrolling-woodland-path}
Every few weeks, there is a small forest court, where the verderers judge small offences. A man who cut down a young tree. A woman whose pigs were in the forest at the wrong time. A family that built a fence too high. They pay small fines. And every few years, there is a great forest court, called the Forest Eyre, where judges sent by the king come to deal with the bigger cases. Everyone dreads it.

[pause]

Many people in England hate the forest laws. They think they are unfair. Why should the king's deer be more important than the people's farms? Why should a poor man be punished for catching a hare to feed his hungry children? These feelings run deep. And they help to explain why the stories of an outlaw who hunts the king's deer, and fights against the king's officials, became so popular among ordinary people.

[pause]

{img:villagers-talking-quietly-by-fire-cottage-evening-grumbling-forest-laws}
In 1217, some years after our story, the king's government will publish a great document, called the Charter of the Forest. It will make the forest laws milder, and protect the rights of ordinary people to use the forest. It will become one of the most important laws in English history. But in 1190, that is still in the future. For now, the people of Sherwood must live with the old rules, and find ways to get along.

[long-pause]

And most of them do. They know the foresters by name. Some of the foresters are strict. Others are kind, and look the other way when a poor widow gathers a little too much wood, or a boy catches a rabbit. The people of the forest have learned to live with the king's deer, and with the king's laws. They grumble about them by the fire, in the evenings. But they also love their forest, and would not want to live anywhere else.

[long-pause]

{img:edwinstowe-village-1190-thatched-cottages-church-green-forest-edge-morning}
In the middle of Sherwood Forest, there is a little village called Edwinstowe. It has about twenty houses, a small church, a mill and a village green. Around it, there are fields of oats, barley and rye, and beyond the fields, the great oaks of the forest. The village is very old. The legends say that Robin Hood and Maid Marian were married in its church. But that is a story for later.

[long-pause]

In one of the cottages at the edge of the village lives a family. The father is called Hugh. He is a charcoal burner, which means that he makes charcoal from wood, in the forest. He is also a swineherd, who looks after the pigs of the village in the forest. He is about thirty-five years old, with a black beard and strong arms, and he knows the forest better than almost anyone.

[pause]

The mother is called Agnes. She looks after the house, the garden, the chickens and the cow. She brews ale, bakes bread, spins wool and weaves cloth. She knows all the plants of the forest, which ones are good to eat, and which ones can heal. When someone in the village is ill, they come to Agnes for help. She is calm and wise, with grey eyes and a quiet smile.

[pause]

{img:charcoal-burner-father-son-forest-clearing-smoke-stacked-wood-mound}
They have two children. Wat, who is twelve, and his sister Alys, who is nine. Wat helps his father with the charcoal and the pigs. He is quick and clever, and he loves the forest. He knows every path, every stream and every big tree. He can climb like a squirrel, and move through the woods without making a sound. And he dreams, like all the boys of the village, of becoming a great archer.

[pause]

Alys helps her mother in the house and the garden. She feeds the chickens, milks the cow, and fetches water from the well. She is a gentle, dreamy girl, who loves animals and flowers. She has a pet hedgehog, which she found in the garden, and which she feeds with worms and snails. She also has a very good memory. She knows all the stories and songs that her grandmother taught her, before she passed away.

[long-pause]

{img:inside-medieval-cottage-hearth-family-evening-pottage-pot-straw-beds}
The family's cottage is small and simple. It has one room, with walls made of woven branches covered with clay, and a roof of thick straw thatch. In the middle of the floor, there is a hearth, where a fire burns day and night. There is no chimney. The smoke finds its way out through the roof. The family sleeps on straw mattresses, along the walls, under woollen blankets. At one end of the cottage, separated by a low wall, the cow sleeps in winter, keeping everyone warm.

[pause]

The family is not rich. They have a small strip of land in the village fields, where they grow oats and barley. They have a garden, with cabbages, onions, leeks, beans and herbs. They have a cow, a few chickens and some pigs. And Hugh earns some money from his charcoal, which he sells to the blacksmiths and the townspeople of Nottingham. It is enough to live on, in a good year.

[pause]

{img:family-walking-into-forest-morning-father-axe-children-basket-dog}
Like most villagers, they owe service to a lord. The land around Edwinstowe belongs partly to the king, and partly to the church. Every year, Hugh must work a few days for the lord, ploughing or harvesting. And he must pay some rent, in money and in kind. A few hens at Christmas. Some eggs at Easter. It is the way of things, in 1190.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, the family sits around the fire. Hugh mends his tools. Agnes spins. Wat whittles a piece of wood with his knife, trying to make a bow. And Alys tells stories, to her hedgehog, and to anyone who will listen. Old stories, about the forest and its creatures, about brave men and clever women, and about the green man who lives in the deepest part of the woods. Outside, the wind blows through the great oaks. And the forest whispers its own stories, in the dark.

[pause]

On Sundays, the family goes to the little church of St Mary, in the middle of the village. It is a small stone church, quite new, with thick walls and round arches. The priest is an old man, who has known the family all his life. After Mass, the villagers stand outside on the green, talking about the weather, the crops and the news. And the children run around the churchyard, playing tag among the gravestones.

[long-pause]

{img:charcoal-mound-smoking-forest-clearing-turf-covered-burner-hut-dawn}
Deep in the forest, in a clearing among the oaks, Hugh has his charcoal pit. Charcoal is wood that has been burned slowly, without much air, so that it turns black, light and full of energy. It burns much hotter than ordinary wood, and without smoke. The blacksmiths need it for their forges, to make iron hot enough to work. And the townspeople buy it for cooking and heating.

[long-pause]

Making charcoal is a slow and careful job. First, Hugh and Wat cut wood from the coppice. A coppice is a part of the forest where trees are cut down near the ground, and then grow up again from the stumps, with many thin, straight stems. Every few years, the stems are cut again. It is a very old way of managing woods, and it gives an endless supply of wood, without killing the trees.

[pause]

They cut the wood into pieces of the same length, and stack them carefully, in a big round mound, like a dome, as tall as a man. In the middle, they leave a little chimney. Then they cover the whole mound with turf and earth, so that very little air can get in. Then Hugh drops some burning coals down the chimney, into the middle of the mound. And the slow burning begins.

[pause]

{img:father-and-son-watching-charcoal-mound-night-small-hut-firelight-stars}
The mound must burn slowly, for several days and nights. If it burns too fast, the wood will turn to ash. If it goes out, the work is wasted. So someone must watch it all the time, day and night. Hugh and Wat take turns. They live in a little hut next to the mound, made of branches and turf. They watch the colour of the smoke. White smoke means the wood is still drying. Blue smoke means it is turning into charcoal. When the smoke becomes thin and clear, it is ready.

[pause]

Wat loves the nights at the charcoal pit. He sits by the mound, wrapped in his cloak, watching the thin smoke rising into the darkness. The forest is very quiet. Owls call to each other. A fox barks, far away. Sometimes, a deer comes to the edge of the clearing, and looks at him, with big dark eyes, and then disappears. The stars shine through the gap in the trees. And Wat feels as if he is the only person awake in the whole world.

[long-pause]

{img:charcoal-burner-raking-black-charcoal-sacks-cart-pony-forest}
When the charcoal is ready, Hugh and Wat let the mound cool down. Then they open it carefully, and rake out the charcoal. It is black and shiny and very light, and it rings when you tap it. They put it in sacks, and load the sacks onto a little cart, pulled by a pony. And Hugh takes it to sell, at the market in Nottingham, or to the blacksmiths of the villages around.

[pause]

Charcoal burners have a special place in the old stories. They live in the deep forest, alone, far from the villages. They are a bit mysterious. In the stories, kings sometimes get lost in the forest, and are helped by a charcoal burner, who does not recognise them. The charcoal burner gives the stranger food and shelter, and is rewarded later, when the stranger turns out to be the king. Hugh likes these stories. He says that he is still waiting for his king to get lost.

[pause]

{img:boy-face-smudged-black-charcoal-laughing-mother-washing-cottage-door}
Charcoal burning is a dirty job. When Hugh and Wat come home after several days at the pit, they are covered in black dust, from head to toe. Their faces are black, their hands are black, their clothes are black. Only their eyes and their teeth shine white. Agnes makes them wash in the stream before they come into the house. Alys laughs at them, and calls them the black men of the forest.

[long-pause]

But Hugh is proud of his work. Without charcoal, there would be no iron. No tools, no ploughshares, no horseshoes, no nails, no swords. The charcoal burners of the forest, working quietly in their clearings, are part of the life of the whole country. And the forest, carefully managed, gives its wood again and again, year after year, for hundreds of years.

[long-pause]

{img:pigs-foraging-acorns-oak-forest-autumn-swineherd-boy-horn-golden-leaves}
In the autumn, the forest gives a great gift to the people of Sherwood. Acorns. The great oak trees drop millions of them onto the ground. And the pigs of the villages love acorns more than anything. So every autumn, the pigs are taken into the forest, to eat them. This is called pannage. And it is Wat's favourite time of the year.

[long-pause]

Every family in the village has a few pigs. They are not like the pink pigs of today. They are smaller, thinner and hairy, with long legs and long snouts, brown or black in colour. They look a little like wild boar. They are tough and clever, and they can find food almost anywhere. In the summer, they eat roots, grass and scraps. But in the autumn, in the forest, they grow fat on acorns and beechnuts.

[pause]

The village pays a small fee to the king's officials for each pig that goes into the forest. Then Hugh, as the swineherd, gathers all the pigs of the village together, and drives them into the woods. Wat helps him. Every morning, they take the pigs out, and every evening, they bring them back. Hugh has a horn, made from a cow's horn. When he blows it, the pigs come running, because they know it means a little extra food.

[pause]

{img:swineherd-father-knocking-acorns-down-long-pole-pigs-gathering-below}
Sometimes, Hugh uses a long pole to knock the acorns down from the branches. The pigs crowd around underneath, squealing and pushing, gobbling up the acorns as fast as they fall. It is a funny sight. Alys sometimes comes along to watch, with her hedgehog in a basket. She has given names to all the pigs. Old Grumble. Little Black. Snout. Brownie. And the biggest one, a huge old sow, she calls Queen Matilda.

[pause]

The pannage season lasts for about two months, from late September to the middle of November. During this time, the pigs grow fat and heavy. In November, the month that the Anglo-Saxons once called the month of sacrifice, some of them will be turned into bacon, ham and sausages, to feed the family through the winter. Alys does not like to think about that. She always cries a little. But she knows that it is the way of things.

[long-pause]

{img:autumn-forest-sherwood-golden-oaks-beech-trees-leaves-falling-path}
Autumn in Sherwood is very beautiful. The oak leaves turn golden brown. The beech leaves turn copper and red. The birch leaves turn bright yellow. The bracken turns rusty brown. And the air smells of damp earth, fallen leaves and mushrooms. In the mornings, there is mist among the trees, and dew on the spider webs, which shine like strings of pearls in the sun.

[pause]

Agnes goes into the forest too, at this time of year, to gather food. Hazelnuts, blackberries, crab apples, sloes and rosehips. Mushrooms, which she knows very well, which are good to eat and which are dangerous. Beechnuts, which can be pressed to make oil. And herbs for medicine. She takes Alys with her, and teaches her everything. Alys learns quickly. She has her mother's good eye.

[pause]

{img:mother-daughter-gathering-hazelnuts-blackberries-forest-baskets-autumn}
The forest is like a great pantry for the people of Sherwood. Even though they may not hunt the king's deer, they can gather nuts, berries, fruit and mushrooms, and catch fish in the streams, if they have permission. Rabbits are not protected in the same way as deer, and the boys set little traps for them. The forest gives them wood for their fires, timber for their houses, bracken for their beds, and food for their animals.

[long-pause]

One golden autumn evening, Wat and Hugh are bringing the pigs home through the forest. The sun is setting, and its red light shines through the trees. The pigs are trotting along the path, grunting happily, fat and full. And suddenly, in a clearing, Wat sees a great stag, with huge antlers, standing perfectly still, looking at them. For a long moment, nobody moves. Then the stag turns, and walks slowly into the trees, and disappears. Wat says nothing. But he will remember it all his life.

[long-pause]

{img:forester-green-clothes-hunting-horn-bow-riding-through-sherwood-oaks}
The forest has its guardians. They are the foresters. In Sherwood, there are several of them, each in charge of a part of the forest, called a walk. They ride through the woods, on horseback or on foot, every day, watching over the king's deer and the trees. They wear green clothes, so that they blend into the forest. They carry a horn, to call each other, and a bow, and a sword.

[long-pause]

The forester for the part of Sherwood around Edwinstowe is called Ralph. He is about fifty years old, with a grey beard and a weathered face. He has been a forester for more than twenty years. He knows every tree, every stream and every deer path in his walk. He knows where the deer sleep, where they drink, and where they have their young in the spring. And he knows every person in the village, and what they are likely to be up to.

[pause]

Ralph is strict, but fair. He does his job well. If he catches a poacher, he takes him to the forest court. But he does not make trouble for no reason. He knows that the people of the forest are poor, and that life is hard. When he sees a widow gathering a little more wood than she should, he looks the other way. When a boy is fishing in a stream, he shouts at him, and then laughs when the boy runs away.

[pause]

{img:forester-showing-boy-deer-tracks-mud-forest-path-kneeling-teaching}
Ralph likes Wat. He has seen the boy in the forest many times, with the pigs, or at the charcoal pit. He sees that Wat loves the forest, and moves quietly, and notices things. One day, Ralph stops and talks to him. He shows him the tracks of a deer in the mud, and tells him how to read them. How old the deer is. Whether it is a stag or a hind. How long ago it passed. Wat is fascinated.

[pause]

After that, whenever they meet, Ralph teaches Wat something about the forest. How to read the signs of animals. Which way the wind is blowing. How to walk without making a sound. How to tell the time by the sun. How to find your way when you are lost, by the moss on the trees and the flow of the streams. Wat learns everything eagerly. He thinks that Ralph knows more than anyone in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:fawn-hidden-in-bracken-spotted-coat-forest-floor-sunlight-gentle}
In the spring, Ralph takes Wat to see something special. In a quiet part of the forest, hidden in the tall bracken, there is a newborn fawn, a baby deer. Its coat is reddish brown, with white spots, so that it is almost invisible among the leaves and the sunlight. It lies perfectly still, while its mother is away grazing. Ralph tells Wat that he must never touch it, or the mother might leave it. They watch it from a distance, for a long time, without speaking.

[pause]

The foresters also look after the trees. They mark which trees may be cut, and which must be kept. They make sure that the coppices are cut in turn, so that there is always wood growing. They check that nobody is clearing new land without permission. When the king needs timber, for a castle or a ship, the foresters choose the best oaks. Some of the oaks of Sherwood went to build great buildings, all over England.

[pause]

{img:forester-and-boy-sitting-on-fallen-log-eating-bread-sunset-forest}
Not all foresters were good men. Some were greedy and cruel. They took bribes, or punished people unfairly, or made up offences, to collect fines. In the stories of Robin Hood, the foresters are often the villains, together with the sheriff. People hated them. But there were also good foresters, like Ralph, who loved the forest, and cared for its people as well as its deer.

[long-pause]

One evening, Ralph and Wat sit together on a fallen log, at the edge of a glade, sharing a piece of bread and cheese. The sun is going down. The deer are coming out of the woods, to graze in the glade. Ralph tells Wat that, when he is older, he could become a forester too. It is a good life, he says. Hard, but good. Wat thinks about it. A forester. Riding through Sherwood every day, in green, with a horn and a bow. It sounds like the best life in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:nottingham-castle-1190-on-sandstone-rock-town-below-river-trent-morning}
Once a month, Hugh takes his charcoal to Nottingham, to sell at the market. It is a long journey, about thirty kilometres, through the forest. He leaves before dawn, with the pony and the cart. And one day in the summer, he takes Wat and Alys with him. It is the first time Alys has ever been to a town. She is so excited that she cannot sleep the night before.

[long-pause]

They walk south through the forest, along the old road. The road is just a track of earth and sand, with ruts from the cart wheels. It winds through the oak woods and across the heaths. They meet other travellers. A pedlar with a pack on his back. Two monks on mules. A farmer driving some sheep to market. A group of pilgrims, singing as they walk. Everyone greets each other, and asks for news.

[pause]

In the late morning, they come out of the forest, onto a hill. And there, below them, is Nottingham. A town of about two or three thousand people, with houses of timber and thatch, crowded together, and church towers rising above them. A wall surrounds the town. And on a great rock of red sandstone, at one end of the town, high above the river Trent, there stands the castle. It is the biggest building Alys has ever seen.

[pause]

{img:nottingham-market-square-1190-stalls-crowds-animals-church-tower}
Nottingham Castle is a royal castle. It belongs to the king. It was built by William the Conqueror, more than a hundred years before, and has been made bigger and stronger since. It stands on the rock, with steep cliffs on two sides. Its walls and towers are of stone. It is one of the strongest castles in England. The sheriff of Nottingham lives there, and rules the county for the king.

[pause]

Under the castle, and under the whole town, there are caves. The rock is soft sandstone, easy to dig. So for hundreds of years, the people of Nottingham have dug caves into it. They use them as cellars, for storing food and ale. As workshops. Even as homes. There are hundreds of caves, under the houses and the streets. An old name for the town meant the place of caves.

[long-pause]

{img:sandstone-caves-under-nottingham-cellars-barrels-lantern-light-tunnels}
The market of Nottingham is held in a big open square, in the middle of the town. It is the biggest market square in England. On market days, it is crowded with stalls and people. Farmers sell grain, cheese, butter, eggs and vegetables. Butchers sell meat. Weavers sell cloth. Potters sell pots. There are pedlars, jugglers and musicians. Animals are bought and sold. Sheep, pigs, cattle, horses and geese.

[pause]

Hugh sells his charcoal to a blacksmith, who has a forge near the market. He gets a good price. With the money, he buys salt, a new iron knife, some cloth for Agnes, and a little bag of dried figs, which have come all the way from the warm countries of the south. Figs are a rare treat. Alys has never tasted one. She eats it very slowly, with her eyes closed.

[pause]

{img:girl-tasting-fig-market-stall-wide-eyes-father-smiling-medieval}
They walk through the town. Alys stares at everything. The tall houses. The busy streets. The shops with their signs. The church of St Mary, on its hill, with its tall tower. The river Trent, wide and slow, with boats and barges carrying goods. And always, above everything, the great castle on its rock, with the banner of the king flying from its tower.

[long-pause]

On the way home, in the evening, Alys falls asleep on the cart, among the empty charcoal sacks, holding her bag of figs. Wat walks beside his father, leading the pony. The forest is quiet and golden in the evening light. Hugh tells Wat that the sheriff of Nottingham is a powerful man, and that it is best to stay out of his way. Wat nods. He does not know yet that, in the stories, the sheriff of Nottingham will become the most famous villain in England.

[pause]

Before they leave the town, Hugh takes the children to the church of St Mary, to light a candle. Inside, it is cool and dim, after the bright and noisy market. The candles flicker in front of a painted statue of the Virgin. Alys kneels, and says a prayer for her mother, and for her hedgehog. Wat says a prayer that he may one day become a good archer. And Hugh says a prayer of thanks for the good price he got for his charcoal.

[long-pause]

{img:rufford-abbey-cistercian-white-monks-stone-church-cloister-forest-fields}
On the northern edge of Sherwood, there is a quiet place among the trees. It is Rufford Abbey, a monastery of the Cistercian order. About fourteen monks live there, and many more lay brothers, who do the farm work. The monks wear simple robes of undyed wool, which are pale grey or white. So people call them the white monks.

[long-pause]

The Cistercians are very strict. They live simply, without luxury. Their churches are plain, without paintings or decorations, so that nothing distracts them from prayer. They eat simple food, mostly bread, vegetables and beans, and no meat. They get up in the middle of the night to pray, and pray seven more times during the day. And they work hard, with their own hands, in the fields and the workshops.

[pause]

The Cistercians are also very good farmers. They like to build their monasteries in quiet, wild places, far from towns. And then they clear the land, drain the marshes, and build farms, called granges. They are especially good with sheep. The Cistercian abbeys of England have huge flocks, and sell their wool to merchants from Flanders and Italy. It is the best wool in Europe, and it makes the abbeys rich.

[pause]

{img:lay-brothers-shearing-sheep-abbey-grange-summer-wool-piles}
Agnes sometimes goes to Rufford Abbey with Alys. The monks have a guest house, where they welcome travellers. And they have an infirmary, a hospital, where they care for the sick, and a herb garden, full of medicinal plants. One of the monks, an old man called Brother Simon, is the infirmarer, who looks after the sick. He knows even more about herbs than Agnes. They like to talk together about plants and remedies.

[pause]

Brother Simon shows Alys the herb garden. It is full of plants she has never seen. Lavender, rosemary, sage, fennel, rue and many others. Some of them came from far away, from the gardens of other monasteries in France and Italy. Brother Simon tells her what each one is used for. Sage for sore throats. Lavender for headaches. Fennel for the stomach. Rosemary for the memory. Alys tries to remember them all, and for that, she says, she will need a lot of rosemary.

[long-pause]

{img:monk-showing-girl-herb-garden-abbey-lavender-sage-rows-sunny}
The abbey also helps the poor. Every day, at the gate, the monks give bread and soup to anyone who is hungry. Beggars, travellers, widows, orphans and sick people. In winter, when food is scarce, the crowd at the gate is large. The monks never turn anyone away. It is one of their most important duties. They believe that whoever they help is like Christ himself.

[pause]

The abbey has a little library. A few dozen books, written by hand, on vellum, in Latin. Bibles, prayer books, the lives of the saints, and books about religion. In a small room called the scriptorium, one of the monks copies books by hand, letter by letter, with a quill pen. It is very slow work. A single book can take a year. Alys watches him for a while, through the open door. She cannot read. But she thinks the letters look like little black birds, in long rows.

[pause]

{img:abbey-bells-evening-vespers-monks-walking-to-church-sunset-forest}
In the evening, the bell of the abbey rings for Vespers. The monks come in from the fields and the gardens, and walk in a line to the church. Their singing drifts out through the windows, into the quiet forest. It is a beautiful, peaceful sound. Agnes and Alys stop on the path home, and listen for a while. Then they walk on, through the darkening woods, toward the lights of their own village.

[long-pause]

In the stories of Robin Hood, the monks and abbots are often rich and greedy. Robin takes their money, and gives it to the poor. Some abbots in the real Middle Ages were indeed greedy. But many monks were good men, like Brother Simon, who lived simply, worked hard, prayed for the world, and helped anyone who came to their gate. And in Sherwood, as everywhere, the people knew the difference.

[long-pause]

{img:king-richard-lionheart-1190-departing-crusade-ships-banners-knights}
In the year 1190, England has a new king. His name is Richard. He became king the year before, in 1189, when his father, King Henry the Second, passed away. Richard is thirty-two years old. He is tall and strong, with reddish-golden hair, and he is famous all over Europe as a brave knight and soldier. People call him Richard the Lionheart.

[long-pause]

But Richard does not stay in England. In fact, he spends only about six months of his whole ten-year reign in England. He has promised to go on crusade, to the Holy Land, to win back the city of Jerusalem, which was lost to the Christians a few years before. So, as soon as he is crowned, he begins to raise money for the crusade. He sells offices, lands and titles. He even jokes that he would sell London, if he could find a buyer.

[pause]

In the summer of 1190, Richard leaves England, and sets off for the Holy Land, with a great army and a great fleet of ships. He will not come back for more than four years. While he is away, he leaves England in the hands of his officials. And his younger brother, Prince John, is left behind, with lands and castles of his own. Including, after a while, the castle of Nottingham.

[pause]

{img:prince-john-medieval-castle-hall-scheming-courtiers-candlelight}
Prince John is very different from his brother. He is shorter and darker. He is clever, but not trusted. People say that he is greedy, sly and quick to anger. While Richard is away, John tries to take more and more power for himself. He quarrels with the king's officials. He gathers supporters. And when news comes that Richard has been captured, on his way home from the crusade, John tries to take the crown for himself.

[pause]

Richard was captured in 1192, in Austria, by a duke he had insulted during the crusade. He was held prisoner in a castle in Germany for more than a year. To set him free, the people of England had to pay a huge ransom. Everyone had to give a quarter of their income, and the churches gave their gold and silver. It was a heavy burden. But in the end, the ransom was paid, and Richard came home in 1194.

[long-pause]

{img:nottingham-castle-gate-king-richard-returning-1194-banners-surrender}
When Richard came back, John's supporters gave up, one by one. The last castle to hold out for John was Nottingham Castle. Richard came to Nottingham himself, with an army, and after a few days, the castle surrendered. Then Richard went hunting in Sherwood Forest, at the royal lodge at Clipstone. He was delighted with the forest, the chroniclers say. He had never seen it before.

[pause]

This is the true history behind the later stories of Robin Hood. A good king far away, on crusade. A greedy prince at home, taking power. Officials and sheriffs, collecting heavy taxes. And at last, the return of the good king, who comes to Nottingham and to Sherwood. It is easy to see why the storytellers placed Robin in this time. It fits the story perfectly.

[pause]

{img:villagers-paying-ransom-tax-church-steps-coins-chalice-medieval}
In the village of Edwinstowe, people hear the news slowly, from travellers and from the market in Nottingham. The king has gone on crusade. The king has been captured. The king's ransom must be paid. Hugh has to give a quarter of his earnings from the charcoal, for the ransom. It is hard. The family must go without some things that winter. But Hugh does not complain. He says that a king is a king, and must be brought home.

[long-pause]

And Richard did not stay long, even after he came home. A few months later, he left England again, to fight in France. He never came back. He passed away in France, in 1199. And Prince John became king. In the stories, Robin Hood is always loyal to King Richard, and an enemy of John. But Richard himself, the real Richard, probably never knew that a little forest called Sherwood would one day make his name even more famous than his crusade.

[long-pause]

{img:village-archery-practice-butts-sunday-afternoon-men-boys-bows-green}
In the stories, Robin Hood is the greatest archer in England. He can split an arrow in two. He can hit a target from a distance that nobody else can even reach. And all his men are fine archers too. In the Middle Ages, archery was very important in England. And in the villages, men and boys practised it every week.

[long-pause]

In 1190, the English bow is a simple wooden bow, made from a single piece of wood, with a string of hemp or linen. The best bows are made from yew, a dark evergreen tree, whose wood is strong and springy. Later, in the thirteen hundreds, the English longbow will become famous all over Europe. It will be as tall as a man, very powerful, and the English archers will be the best in the world. But already in 1190, many Englishmen can shoot well.

[pause]

On Sundays, after church, the men of the village go to the archery butts. These are mounds of earth, at the edge of the village green, with targets on them. The men take turns shooting at the targets, from different distances. They compete with each other, for fun, and sometimes for a small prize. The boys watch, and practise with smaller bows. It is one of the great pleasures of village life.

[pause]

{img:boy-learning-to-shoot-bow-father-guiding-arms-target-straw}
Wat has been trying to make a bow for a long time. He has carved several, from hazel and ash. But they are not very good. Then Ralph, the forester, gives him a gift. A small bow of yew, which he made himself, when he was a boy. And a few arrows, with goose feathers. Wat cannot believe it. He thanks Ralph again and again. From that day on, he practises every evening, behind the cottage, shooting at a sack of straw.

[pause]

Shooting a bow well is very difficult. You must stand straight, with your feet apart. You must pull the string back to your ear, with your fingers, not your hand. You must look along the arrow, at the target, and breathe out slowly. And then you must let go, smoothly, without jerking. It takes years of practice to become good. Wat's first arrows go everywhere. Into the hedge. Into the garden. Once, almost into the cow.

[long-pause]

{img:arrow-fletcher-making-arrows-goose-feathers-workshop-bundle}
Arrows are made by craftsmen called fletchers. The shaft is made of straight wood, like ash or birch. At one end, there are feathers, usually from a goose, which make the arrow fly straight. At the other end, there is a point of iron, made by the blacksmith. A good arrow is a work of art. It must be perfectly straight, perfectly balanced, and the feathers must be set at exactly the right angle.

[pause]

Hugh is a good archer too. Better than Wat expected. One Sunday, at the butts, he hits the centre of the target three times in a row. The other men cheer. Wat is amazed. He did not know his father could shoot like that. Hugh laughs, and says that he practised a lot when he was young. And that a charcoal burner needs good eyes, to watch the smoke.

[pause]

{img:sunday-archery-contest-village-green-crowd-cheering-target-hit}
Wat practises all summer. Every evening, until it gets dark. Slowly, his arrows begin to hit the sack of straw. Then the middle of the sack. Then a small mark that he has painted on it. By the autumn, he can hit the mark almost every time. Ralph comes to watch him one evening, and nods. Good, he says. Very good. Keep practising. And never shoot at the king's deer.

[long-pause]

Wat promises. He will never shoot at the king's deer. But he dreams, at night, of the great archery contests in the stories. Of shooting against the best archers in England, in front of a great crowd. And of winning the prize, a golden arrow, or a silver one, from the hand of a lady. Like Robin Hood.

[pause]

Alys watches her brother practise, sitting on the garden wall with her hedgehog. Sometimes she fetches his arrows for him, from the hedge and the garden. And once, when he is not looking, she tries the bow herself. It is very hard to pull. But she gets the arrow to fly a little way, and land in the cabbages. She tells nobody. But secretly, she is very proud.

[long-pause]

{img:storyteller-fireside-cottage-children-listening-winter-evening-medieval}
On winter evenings, in the cottages of Sherwood, people tell stories by the fire. In later centuries, the favourite stories were about Robin Hood. So let us imagine that we are sitting in Hugh's cottage, many years later, by the glowing fire, with Alys, now an old woman, telling the stories to her grandchildren. The first story she tells is always the same. How Robin Hood met Little John.

[long-pause]

One day, Robin Hood was walking through the forest alone, looking for adventure. He came to a stream, where there was a narrow bridge, made of a single log. Just as Robin stepped onto the bridge, a stranger stepped onto the other end. He was a giant of a man, the tallest Robin had ever seen, with a great staff of oak in his hand. Neither of them wanted to step back and let the other pass.

[pause]

Let me pass, said Robin. You let me pass, said the stranger. I was here first. They argued for a while. And then they agreed to settle it in the old English way. With staffs. A friendly contest, to see who could knock the other into the stream. Robin cut himself a staff from an oak tree. And they began, there on the narrow log, swinging their staffs at each other, while the water rushed below.

[pause]

{img:two-men-quarterstaff-on-log-bridge-stream-splash-laughter-forest}
They fought for a long time, back and forth on the log. Neither could win. They were both strong and skilful. And then, at last, the stranger gave a great swing, and Robin lost his balance, and fell, with a huge splash, into the stream. He came up, spluttering and laughing, soaking wet. And the stranger laughed too, so hard that he almost fell in himself.

[pause]

Robin climbed out of the water. He was not angry at all. He admired the stranger's strength and skill. He asked him his name. The stranger said that his name was John Little. Robin laughed even more. John Little, he said, for a man as big as a tree! From now on, you shall be called Little John. And he invited him to join his band of merry men, in the greenwood.

[long-pause]

{img:merry-men-feast-forest-glade-campfire-roasting-laughing-green-clothes}
Little John agreed. He went with Robin to the great oak tree, deep in the forest, where the merry men lived. They welcomed him with a great feast, in a green glade, by a fire. They gave him a new suit of Lincoln green, the green cloth that all the merry men wore, so that they were hidden among the leaves. And from that day on, Little John was Robin's closest friend and right hand, always at his side.

[pause]

The merry men lived in the greenwood, under the trees. In summer, they slept in the open air, or in shelters of branches. They ate venison, which is deer meat, though they should not have. They drank ale and wine. They sang songs and told jokes. They practised archery and wrestling. And they watched the roads, for rich travellers, whom they would invite to dinner, and then ask to pay for it.

[pause]

{img:greenwood-summer-outlaws-lying-under-oak-tree-dappled-light-resting}
The old ballads describe the greenwood in beautiful words. They talk about the merry month of May, when the leaves are green and long, and the birds sing in the trees. They talk about the shade of the great oaks, and the deer running in the glades. For the people who listened to these stories, the greenwood was a dream of freedom. A place without lords and taxes and forest laws, where you could live as you liked.

[long-pause]

The grandchildren laugh at the story of the log bridge. They always ask Alys to tell it again. And the old woman tells it again, by the fire, with the wind howling outside. And she adds a little detail each time. The stream gets deeper. Little John gets taller. And Robin's splash gets bigger and bigger, until it soaks everyone on the bank. That is how stories grow.

[long-pause]

{img:jolly-friar-tuck-brown-robe-round-cheerful-face-river-crossing-legend}
The next story is about another famous member of Robin's band. Friar Tuck. He is a round, cheerful, red-faced friar, a kind of monk who travels around, preaching and helping the poor. He loves good food and good ale. He is also very strong, and handy with a staff and a sword. And he has a wonderful sense of humour.

[long-pause]

One day, the story goes, Robin heard about a strong friar who lived by himself, at a little hermitage beside a river, deep in the forest. Robin wanted to meet him, to test him. So he went to the river. And there, on the bank, he found the friar, sitting on the grass, eating a meat pie and drinking from a big jug of ale. The friar was round and jolly, with a shaved head and a twinkle in his eye.

[pause]

Robin wanted to cross the river, but he did not want to get his feet wet. So he asked the friar to carry him across on his back. The friar agreed, cheerfully. He took off his sandals, lifted Robin onto his back, and waded across the river. When they reached the other side, the friar said, now you must carry me back. Fair is fair. And Robin agreed, and carried the heavy friar back across the river, puffing and panting.

[pause]

{img:friar-carrying-man-on-back-across-river-both-laughing-sunny-day}
Then Robin said, now carry me across again. And the friar picked him up, and waded out into the middle of the river. And then, with a great laugh, he threw Robin into the water. Splash! Robin came up, soaking wet, for the second time in the stories. The friar was roaring with laughter on the bank. And then they had a friendly contest with swords and staffs, which lasted all afternoon, until they were both too tired to go on.

[pause]

In the end, Robin blew his horn, and his merry men came running out of the forest. And the friar whistled, and fifty big dogs came running out of the bushes. Everyone looked at each other. And then they all started laughing. Robin asked the friar to join his band. The friar agreed, as long as he could have good food and good ale every day. And so Friar Tuck became the chaplain of the merry men.

[long-pause]

{img:friar-saying-mass-forest-glade-outlaws-kneeling-morning-light-altar}
Friar Tuck says Mass for the merry men in the forest, at a little altar under the trees. He hears their confessions, and blesses their meals. He also cooks wonderful stews, and brews the best ale in Sherwood. He is kind to the poor, and gives them food and comfort. And he is always ready with a joke, a song or a funny story. The merry men love him.

[pause]

Friar Tuck did not appear in the oldest ballads of Robin Hood. He came into the stories a little later, from the May Games, the spring festivals of the villages, where people dressed up as Robin Hood and his friends, and acted out little plays. A fat, jolly friar was a favourite character in these plays. And he became part of the legend.

[pause]

{img:may-games-village-actors-dressed-as-robin-friar-tuck-crowd-laughing}
There may have been a real man behind the friar too. In the early fourteen hundreds, there was a chaplain in Sussex who became an outlaw, and called himself Friar Tuck. He and his men caused trouble for years. But by then, the name Friar Tuck may already have been part of the stories. Perhaps the chaplain took the name from the stories, rather than the other way around. Nobody knows.

[long-pause]

Alys's grandchildren love Friar Tuck most of all. They love the part where he throws Robin into the river. They want to hear it again and again. And Alys tells it, with all the voices. The friar's big, booming laugh. Robin's surprised splutter. The barking of the fifty dogs. The little cottage rings with laughter. And outside, the snow falls softly on the roofs of Edwinstowe.

[long-pause]

{img:may-day-village-green-maypole-ribbons-dancing-flowers-medieval-spring}
In the real Sherwood, one of the happiest days of the year is the first of May. May Day. It is the great festival of spring. The winter is over. The trees are green again. The flowers are blooming. And everyone in the village celebrates, with dancing, music, games and feasting, from dawn until late at night.

[long-pause]

Before dawn on May morning, the young people of the village go out into the forest. They gather green branches, flowers and blossoms, especially hawthorn, which is called may, because it blooms in May. They bring them back to the village, singing, and decorate the doors and windows of the houses. This is called bringing in the May. Alys goes with the older girls, for the first time. She comes back with her arms full of blossom, and flowers in her hair.

[pause]

They also wash their faces in the dew of May morning. The old people say that May dew makes you beautiful, and keeps you healthy all year. Agnes laughs and says that she has washed in it every year, and it has not done her much good. But Alys washes her face carefully, in the dewy grass, just in case.

[pause]

{img:girls-gathering-hawthorn-blossom-dawn-forest-may-morning-dew}
On the village green, a tall pole is set up, decorated with flowers and ribbons. It is the maypole. The young people dance around it, holding hands, singing. There are musicians, with pipes and drums. There are games and races. There is a May Queen, a pretty girl from the village, chosen to rule over the festival, with a crown of flowers. And there is a lot of good food and ale.

[pause]

In later centuries, the May Games often included little plays about Robin Hood. A young man of the village played Robin, in green. His friends played Little John, Friar Tuck and the other merry men. And a girl played Maid Marian, who was often the May Queen as well. They acted out little scenes, collected money from the crowd for the church, and danced. Robin Hood became one of the most popular figures of the May festivals all over England.

[long-pause]

{img:maid-marian-green-dress-flower-crown-forest-glade-bow-gentle-legend}
That is probably how Maid Marian came into the stories of Robin Hood. She did not appear in the oldest ballads. But in the May Games, there was always a lady, and she became Robin's love. In the later stories, Marian is a noble lady, beautiful, clever and brave. She lives in the forest with Robin, dressed in green, and she is a fine archer too. She often disguises herself as a boy, and has adventures of her own.

[pause]

There is a lovely story about how Robin and Marian found each other again, after being apart. Marian had gone into the forest to look for Robin, dressed as a page boy. Robin, also in disguise, met her on a path. They did not recognise each other. They had a friendly contest with swords, and were evenly matched. Then Robin spoke, and Marian recognised his voice. She threw down her sword, and they embraced, laughing, in the middle of the forest.

[pause]

{img:village-church-edwinstowe-wedding-flowers-couple-door-spring-legend}
And the legend says that Robin Hood and Maid Marian were married in the church of St Mary, in Edwinstowe, the village of our story. Of course, there is no record of it. But the people of Edwinstowe have always believed it. And they are proud of it. Today, the church still stands, and visitors come to see the place where, the story says, the most famous couple of English legend were married.

[long-pause]

On the evening of May Day, the whole village dances on the green, by the light of a big bonfire. Hugh dances with Agnes. Wat dances with the miller's daughter, and blushes. Alys dances with her friends, round and round the maypole, until she is dizzy. Ralph the forester comes by, and stands at the edge of the crowd, smiling, tapping his foot to the music. And over the forest, the first stars come out, on the first night of summer.

[long-pause]

{img:archery-contest-nottingham-field-crowds-tents-banners-targets-sunny}
One of the most famous stories of Robin Hood is the story of the archery contest. Alys tells it to her grandchildren on a summer evening, sitting on the bench outside the cottage, in the last light of the day. It is a story full of excitement, and a little danger, but it ends well, as all the best stories do.

[long-pause]

The Sheriff of Nottingham wanted to catch Robin Hood. But Robin was too clever, and the forest was too big. So the sheriff had an idea. He would hold a great archery contest, in Nottingham. And the prize would be an arrow made of silver, with feathers of gold. He knew that Robin was the best archer in England, and that he would not be able to resist the challenge. And then the sheriff could catch him.

[pause]

The news of the contest spread far and wide. Archers came from all over England. The field outside Nottingham was full of tents and banners. Crowds of people came to watch. The sheriff sat on a high seat, with his officials. And among the crowd of archers, there was a man in a ragged red cloak, with a patch over one eye, and a hood pulled down over his face. Nobody looked at him twice.

[pause]

{img:archer-in-disguise-ragged-cloak-eye-patch-drawing-bow-crowd-watching}
The contest began. The archers shot at the targets, one by one. The best ones went through to the next round. The target was moved further and further away. Fewer and fewer archers were left. In the end, there were only two. A famous archer from the sheriff's own men. And the stranger in the ragged red cloak.

[pause]

The sheriff's archer shot first. His arrow hit the very centre of the target. The crowd cheered. It seemed impossible to do better. Then the stranger stepped forward. He took his time. He looked at the target, and at the sky, and felt the wind. He drew his bow, slowly, all the way back to his ear. And he let the arrow fly. It flew straight and true, and hit the other arrow, right at its end, and split it in two, from end to end.

[long-pause]

{img:arrow-splitting-another-arrow-target-centre-close-dramatic-crowd-gasp}
The crowd was silent for a moment. Then it went wild. People shouted and cheered and threw their hats in the air. Nobody had ever seen such a shot. The sheriff, reluctantly, gave the silver arrow to the stranger. The stranger bowed, took the arrow, and disappeared into the crowd. And the sheriff never found him.

[pause]

That evening, in the greenwood, the stranger took off his ragged cloak and his eye patch. It was Robin Hood, of course. The merry men cheered and laughed. Robin held up the silver arrow, shining in the firelight. And then, to tease the sheriff, Robin shot an arrow over the walls of Nottingham, with a message tied to it. It said, thank you for the silver arrow, from Robin Hood.

[pause]

{img:outlaws-celebrating-forest-campfire-silver-arrow-held-up-firelight}
The grandchildren clap and cheer. They love this story. The disguise. The split arrow. The message over the wall. And the sheriff's face, when he read it. Alys acts out the sheriff's face, red and furious, and they all laugh until they cry. Then she tells them that it is time for bed. They beg for one more story. Just one more. And she says, tomorrow.

[long-pause]

Wat, sitting nearby, smiles at the story. He is a grown man now, a forester himself, with grey in his beard. He remembers the little yew bow that Ralph gave him, long ago, and all the evenings he spent practising behind the cottage. He never split an arrow. But he did win a prize at the archery butts, once, at Nottingham. A pair of good leather gloves. He still has them. And he still thinks that it was the best day of his life.

[pause]

After the grandchildren have gone to bed, Wat and Alys sit together by the dying fire, as they did when they were children. They talk about their parents, and the old days, and the charcoal pit, and the pigs, and old Ralph. Outside, the forest is dark and quiet. And the two old friends sit in the warm glow, remembering, until the fire burns down to embers.

[long-pause]

{img:sad-knight-riding-slowly-forest-road-poor-clothes-thin-horse-legend}
There is one story about Robin Hood that is very old, and very gentle. It comes from the longest of the old ballads, the Gest of Robyn Hode. It is not about fighting or tricks. It is about kindness. And it shows why the people of England loved Robin Hood so much.

[long-pause]

One day, Robin sent Little John and two of his men to the road, to find a guest for dinner. That was Robin's custom. He never ate until a guest had joined him. Usually, the guest was a rich traveller, who would then be asked to pay for the meal. But this time, the guest they found was a sad, quiet knight, riding slowly through the forest, on a thin horse, with his hood pulled down over his face. He looked very unhappy.

[pause]

Little John invited him politely to dinner with Robin Hood. The knight agreed. They took him to the greenwood, where a great feast was waiting. Bread and wine, roast venison, swans and pheasants, and birds of the river. The knight ate and drank, and thanked Robin. Then Robin asked him, as he asked every guest, to pay for the meal. The knight said, sadly, that he had only ten shillings in the whole world.

[pause]

{img:outlaw-host-and-sad-knight-feast-table-under-oak-tree-forest}
Robin did not believe him, at first. He sent Little John to look in the knight's bags. And it was true. There were only ten shillings. Robin was surprised. He asked the knight how a knight could be so poor. And the knight told him his story. His son had got into trouble, and to help him, the knight had borrowed a large sum of money from a rich abbot, four hundred pounds. He had promised his lands and his castle, as security. And the money must be paid back on a certain day. Very soon.

[pause]

If the knight could not pay, the abbot would take his lands and his castle, and he and his wife would have nothing. He had asked all his friends for help. But his friends had all turned away from him, now that he was poor. Nobody would help. He was riding to the abbey now, to beg for more time, though he knew it was hopeless.

[long-pause]

{img:robin-giving-bag-of-gold-to-grateful-knight-merry-men-watching}
Robin listened to the story. He was moved. He talked to his men. And then he lent the knight the four hundred pounds, from the merry men's treasure. He gave him new clothes, of fine cloth, and a good horse, and new boots and spurs. And he sent Little John with him, as his servant, so that he would not ride alone. The knight could hardly believe it. He wept with gratitude.

[pause]

The knight rode to the abbey, and found the greedy abbot waiting, sure that he would get the lands. The knight pretended, at first, that he had no money, and begged for more time. The abbot refused, and laughed at him. And then the knight threw the bag of gold onto the table, and paid every penny. The abbot's face, the story says, was a sight to see.

[pause]

{img:knight-returning-to-forest-year-later-repaying-debt-bows-gift-legend}
A year later, the knight came back to the greenwood, to pay back Robin. He brought the four hundred pounds. And he brought a gift. A hundred fine bows, and a hundred bundles of arrows, with peacock feathers, for the merry men. Robin was delighted. But he would not take the money. Because, by then, he had already been paid, by someone else. By a monk from the same greedy abbey, who had passed through the forest, and been invited to dinner. And who had paid, rather generously.

[long-pause]

Alys likes this story best of all. She says that it is the real heart of Robin Hood. Not the fighting, not the tricks. But the kindness. Helping someone who is in trouble, when everyone else has turned away. Lending money without asking for interest. Giving clothes and a horse to someone who has nothing. And keeping your word. Her grandchildren listen quietly. And the littlest one asks if Robin Hood would help them, if they were in trouble. Alys smiles, and says that he would.

[long-pause]

{img:sherwood-forest-winter-snow-on-ancient-oaks-frozen-stream-quiet-white}
Winter comes to Sherwood. The leaves have fallen. The great oaks stand bare and grey against the sky. The days are short and cold. Sometimes it snows, and the whole forest is white and silent, as if the world were holding its breath. The deer leave tracks in the snow. And the smoke from the cottages of Edwinstowe rises straight up into the frosty air.

[long-pause]

Winter is a hard time for the people of the forest. The fields are bare. There is little fresh food. The family lives on what they have stored. Salted bacon and ham from the pigs. Dried peas and beans. Oats and barley for porridge and bread. Cheese from the summer. Apples from the autumn, stored in straw, which slowly go wrinkly. And whatever Agnes can find. A few winter greens from the garden. A rabbit from Wat's traps.

[pause]

The family spends much of the winter indoors, around the fire. The cow comes into the cottage, at the end of the room, and her warmth helps to keep everyone warm. Hugh mends his tools and makes new ones. He carves wooden spoons and bowls, and weaves baskets from willow. Agnes spins and weaves, all day long. Wat helps his father, and practises his bow when the weather allows. And Alys tells stories.

[pause]

{img:cottage-interior-winter-family-around-fire-cow-at-end-spinning-carving}
But there is still work outside. Firewood must be gathered, every day, from the forest. Hugh and Wat go out into the snow with their axes and their hooks, and bring back loads of dead branches on their backs. The animals must be fed and watered. The ice on the well must be broken. And on fine days, Hugh goes back to the charcoal pit, because the blacksmiths need charcoal even in winter.

[pause]

In the forest, Ralph the forester is busy too. In winter, the deer can be hungry, when the snow covers the grass. So the foresters cut branches of holly and ivy, and leave them for the deer to eat. Ralph takes Wat with him, sometimes, on these winter rounds. They walk through the silent, snowy forest, cutting holly, and leaving it in the clearings. And in the evening, they see the deer come out, and eat.

[long-pause]

{img:christmas-medieval-village-yule-log-holly-ivy-feast-candles-cottage}
The best time of the winter is Christmas. It lasts for twelve days, from Christmas Day to Twelfth Night, in early January. During these days, there is little work. People go to church, visit each other, eat and drink and play games. The houses are decorated with holly, ivy and other evergreen branches. A big log, called the Yule log, is brought in and burned on the fire, as long as possible.

[pause]

The lord of the village gives a feast for his tenants, at Christmas. Hugh and his family go, in their best clothes. There is roast meat, bread, cheese, ale and cakes. There are musicians, and dancing. There are games, like blind man's buff and hunt the slipper. And there is a lord of misrule, a young man chosen to be in charge of the fun, who gives silly orders, which everyone must obey.

[pause]

{img:frosty-night-cottage-window-candle-glow-stars-snow-forest-behind}
On Christmas night, Alys stands at the door of the cottage, looking out at the forest. The snow is glittering in the moonlight. The great oaks stand silent and still. An owl hoots, far away. The stars are very bright and very close. Somewhere, out there, she imagines Robin Hood and his merry men, sitting around a fire in a cave, singing Christmas songs. She knows they are only stories. But tonight, it is a nice thought.

[long-pause]

Then the winter slowly passes. The days get longer. The snow melts. The streams run fast and full. Snowdrops appear under the hedges, little white flowers in the cold earth. Then the primroses, pale yellow. Then the first green leaves on the hawthorn. And one morning, Wat hears a cuckoo, calling from deep in the forest. Spring has come back to Sherwood. And with it, the whole cycle of the year begins again.

[pause]

On the darkest evenings, Hugh sometimes plays a little wooden pipe, which he carved himself. He plays old tunes, slow and sweet, that his own father taught him. Agnes hums along, as she spins. Wat taps the rhythm on his knee. And Alys lies on her straw mattress, with her hedgehog asleep beside her, listening to the music, and to the wind outside, until her eyes close.

[long-pause]

{img:kirklees-priory-old-gatehouse-yorkshire-trees-evening-mist-legend}
All the old stories of Robin Hood end in the same way. Gently, and a little sadly. Alys does not tell this story very often. Only on quiet evenings, when the grandchildren are older, and ask for it. And she tells it softly, as the fire burns low.

[long-pause]

Robin Hood grew old. He had lived in the greenwood for many years. He had had many adventures. But now he was tired, and often ill. His hair was grey, and his bones ached in the cold. One day, he felt very unwell. And he decided to go to a priory called Kirklees, in Yorkshire, where his cousin was the prioress, the head of the nuns. She was known as a skilled healer. He hoped she could help him.

[pause]

Little John went with him, as always. They walked slowly through the forest, to the priory. Robin went inside, to be cared for by his cousin. Little John waited outside. In those days, one of the common treatments for illness was to let a little blood, which people believed could make the body well. The prioress began to treat Robin. But the old stories say that she did not treat him well. And Robin grew weaker, instead of stronger.

[pause]

{img:old-outlaw-at-window-holding-bow-little-john-beside-him-evening-light}
Robin knew that he did not have long. He blew his horn, softly, three times. And Little John heard it, outside, and came running up the stairs, to his friend's room. He found Robin lying on the bed, pale and weak. Little John knelt beside him, and held his hand. He was crying. And Robin smiled at him, and told him not to be sad.

[pause]

Then Robin asked for his bow and an arrow. Little John helped him to sit up, by the open window, from which he could see the green forest outside. Robin drew the bow, one last time, with the last of his strength. And he said, where this arrow falls, there you must bury me. Then he let the arrow fly, out of the window, over the trees. And it fell, far away, in a green glade.

[long-pause]

{img:arrow-flying-out-of-window-over-trees-sunset-green-forest-distance}
Robin lay back, and closed his eyes. And he passed away, peacefully, with his oldest friend beside him, and the green forest outside the window. Little John stayed with him for a long time. Then he carried him out, to the place where the arrow had fallen. And there, under the trees, in the green glade, he buried him, with his bow at his side. As Robin had asked.

[pause]

The old ballad ends with a few simple words. That Robin Hood lies under a stone, by the high road, near Kirklees. And that he was a good outlaw, and did poor men much good. That is all. It is a quiet ending, for a hero who had so many adventures. But it is a fitting one. He ended his life in the green forest he loved, with his friend at his side.

[pause]

{img:old-grave-stone-woodland-glade-moss-ferns-quiet-afternoon-light}
Near Kirklees, in Yorkshire, there is still an old grave, in a wood, behind iron railings. It is called Robin Hood's Grave. Nobody knows who is really buried there, if anyone. The stone has an old inscription, which says that here lies Robert, Earl of Huntingdon, and that never an archer was as good as he. But the inscription is not very old, and nobody believes it is real. Still, people have visited the grave for hundreds of years.

[long-pause]

Alys finishes the story. The fire has burned down to glowing embers. The grandchildren are very quiet. The littlest one has tears in her eyes. Alys hugs her, and says that it is only a story. And that in the stories, Robin Hood never really dies. As long as people tell his stories, he is still there, in the greenwood, under the great oaks, with his bow and his merry men. And the littlest one smiles, and falls asleep in her grandmother's lap.

[long-pause]

{img:older-forester-grey-beard-green-tunic-riding-sherwood-young-apprentice}
The years pass in Sherwood. The seasons turn, spring, summer, autumn and winter, again and again. The great oaks grow a little bigger every year. And the family in Edwinstowe grows older.

[long-pause]

Wat becomes a forester, just as Ralph said he could. When Ralph grows old, and can no longer ride through the forest every day, he asks the chief forester to take Wat as his assistant. And when Ralph retires, Wat takes his place. He rides through Sherwood every day, in green, with a horn and a bow. He watches over the king's deer, and the king's trees. And he is a good forester, strict but fair, like Ralph before him.

[pause]

Wat looks after the people of the forest, as well as the deer. He knows how hard life is for them. He looks the other way when a widow takes a little too much wood. He warns the boys when they are about to get into trouble. He helps families who are in need. And the people of the forest trust him and like him. They say that Wat is the best forester Sherwood has ever had. Ralph, sitting by his fire in his old age, is very proud.

[pause]

{img:old-retired-forester-sitting-by-cottage-door-sun-young-forester-visiting}
Wat marries the miller's daughter, the girl he danced with on May Day. Her name is Mary. They have five children. And Wat teaches them all to love the forest, as he does. To read the tracks of animals, to walk quietly, to find their way by the moss on the trees. And he gives his oldest son the little yew bow that Ralph gave him, long ago.

[pause]

Hugh goes on making charcoal for many years, until his arms are too stiff to cut wood. Then he sits by the fire, and carves spoons and bowls, and tells his grandchildren about the old days. About the year when King Richard went on crusade, and the people of Sherwood had to pay for his ransom. About the day the king came back to Nottingham. About the great stag that he and Wat once saw, in the golden autumn light.

[long-pause]

{img:grandmother-agnes-herb-garden-teaching-granddaughter-plants-sunny}
Agnes becomes the wise woman of the village. Everyone comes to her when they are ill. She knows more about herbs than anyone in the county, except perhaps Brother Simon, at Rufford Abbey. She and Brother Simon remain friends all their lives. When she is very old, she passes away peacefully, in her own bed, in the little cottage, with her family around her.

[pause]

And Alys becomes the storyteller of the village. She marries a carpenter from Edwinstowe, and has children and grandchildren. And every winter evening, by the fire, she tells them the old stories. About the forest and its creatures. About the green man of the woods. About the brave and the clever and the kind. And later, when the songs of Robin Hood spread through England, she tells those too.

[pause]

{img:old-woman-storyteller-fireside-many-grandchildren-listening-cosy-cottage}
She still has her hedgehog, or rather, the great-great-grandson of her first hedgehog, which lives in the garden. She still remembers the taste of the first fig, at the market in Nottingham. And she still goes into the forest in May, very early in the morning, to gather hawthorn blossom, and wash her face in the dew. Just in case.

[long-pause]

The little village of Edwinstowe goes on, year after year, century after century. The houses are rebuilt. The people come and go. But the church stays. The forest stays. The great oaks stay. And the stories stay, passed on from grandmother to grandchild, by the fire, on winter evenings, for hundreds of years. Until, one day, they are written down, and printed in books, and told all over the world.

[long-pause]

{img:printed-chapbook-robin-hood-ballads-woodcut-1500s-later}
After the Middle Ages, the stories of Robin Hood became more and more popular. When printing came to England, in the late fourteen hundreds, the ballads of Robin Hood were among the first things to be printed. Little books, with woodcut pictures, sold for a few pennies. People bought them, read them aloud, and sang them. Robin Hood became one of the best-known heroes in the land.

[long-pause]

In the fifteen hundreds, the May Games with Robin Hood were held all over England. In towns and villages, every spring, young men dressed up as Robin and his men, and Maid Marian, and collected money for the church. Even King Henry the Eighth liked to dress up as Robin Hood. Once, on a May morning, he and his courtiers dressed in green, as outlaws, and surprised the queen with a feast in the woods.

[pause]

Over the centuries, Robin changed. In the early ballads, he was a yeoman, a free countryman. Later writers made him a nobleman, the Earl of Huntingdon, who had lost his lands, and fled to the forest. Later still, in the eighteen hundreds, writers made him a Saxon, fighting against the Norman lords, in the time of King Richard. A writer called Walter Scott put Robin in his famous novel, Ivanhoe, in 1819. And that made him even more famous.

[pause]

{img:sherwood-forest-shrinking-fields-farms-enclosures-1700s-later}
Meanwhile, the real Sherwood Forest was getting smaller. Over the centuries, more and more of it was cleared for farming, for houses and for towns. The kings stopped hunting there. The forest laws were forgotten. Rich landowners built great country houses, with parks, on the old forest land. So many of them that the area became known as the Dukeries, because so many dukes lived there. Later, coal was found under the forest, and mines were dug.

[pause]

By the nineteen hundreds, only a small part of the old Sherwood Forest was left. But in that part, there were still some of the great old oak trees, hundreds of years old. And there was one very special tree. A huge, ancient oak, near Edwinstowe, called the Major Oak. It was named after a Major Rooke, who wrote about the trees of Sherwood in 1790. It may be about a thousand years old.

[long-pause]

{img:major-oak-sherwood-huge-ancient-tree-wooden-supports-spreading-branches}
The Major Oak is enormous. Its trunk is about ten metres around. Its branches spread out about twenty-eight metres wide, so wide that they have to be held up by wooden poles and steel cables. Its trunk is hollow inside. The legend says that Robin Hood and his merry men hid inside it, and slept under its branches. If the tree really is a thousand years old, it was already a big tree in 1190. Perhaps Wat and Alys saw it.

[pause]

In the twentieth century, Robin Hood became a star of films and television. There were dozens of films about him, from the early silent films to colourful Hollywood adventures, with Errol Flynn in green tights, shooting arrows and laughing. There was a famous cartoon film, with Robin Hood as a fox. There were television series, for children and adults. Every generation had its own Robin Hood.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-robin-hood-toy-bows-woods-sunny-later-modern}
And children all over the world played at being Robin Hood. They made bows and arrows from sticks and string. They ran through the woods and parks, hiding behind trees, and ambushing their friends. They took from the rich, and gave to the poor. And they argued about who would be Robin, and who would have to be the sheriff.

[long-pause]

Robin Hood became more than a story. He became a symbol. Of fairness. Of helping the poor. Of standing up to unfair laws and greedy rulers. Of freedom in the green forest. People all over the world, in many languages, know the name of Robin Hood. And when someone takes from the rich to help the poor, people still call them a Robin Hood. The little outlaw of the old English ballads has become a hero of the whole world.

[long-pause]

{img:sherwood-forest-country-park-today-visitors-path-ancient-oaks-summer}
Today, Sherwood Forest still exists. It is much smaller than it was in 1190. But near the village of Edwinstowe, there is a beautiful part of the old forest, protected as a nature reserve. It is called Sherwood Forest Country Park. And every year, hundreds of thousands of people come to visit it, from all over the world.

[long-pause]

The forest today is full of ancient oak trees. More than a thousand of them are thought to be over five hundred years old. They are huge and gnarled, with twisted branches and hollow trunks. Some of them are dead, standing like great grey sculptures among the living trees. They are home to many rare insects, birds and bats. Sherwood is one of the most important ancient oak woods in Europe.

[pause]

The Major Oak still stands, in a clearing in the forest, held up by its poles and cables. A fence keeps visitors at a little distance, to protect its roots. But you can walk all around it, and look up into its great spreading branches. It is green and alive, and every year, it still grows leaves and acorns. Scientists have even grown young trees from its acorns, and planted them all over the world.

[pause]

{img:major-oak-today-visitors-looking-up-autumn-leaves-fence-path}
In the forest, there are walking paths, through the oak woods and across the heaths. In spring, the ground is covered with bluebells. In summer, the bracken grows tall and green. In autumn, the leaves turn golden, and the acorns fall. In winter, sometimes, there is snow on the great old branches. And at every time of year, you can see deer, birds, squirrels and, if you are lucky, a woodpecker.

[pause]

Every summer, there is a Robin Hood Festival in Sherwood Forest. For a week, the forest is full of people dressed as medieval characters. Archers show their skills. Jesters juggle. Musicians play old tunes. Actors play Robin Hood, Little John, Friar Tuck and Maid Marian, and the wicked sheriff. Children can try archery, and learn medieval games. It is like the old May Games, come back to life.

[long-pause]

{img:edwinstowe-st-marys-church-today-stone-tower-village-green-quiet}
In the village of Edwinstowe, the old church of St Mary still stands, built of stone, with a tall tower. Parts of it are from the twelfth century, so it was already there in 1190. People come to see it, because of the legend that Robin Hood and Maid Marian were married there. And outside, in the village, there is a statue of Robin and Marian, holding hands.

[pause]

In Nottingham, there is a famous statue of Robin Hood, in bronze, standing near the walls of the castle, with his bow drawn, aiming an arrow. It is one of the most photographed statues in England. The castle on the rock is no longer the medieval castle. It was pulled down long ago, and a grand house was built in its place, which is now a museum. But the great sandstone rock is still there. And under it, the caves.

[pause]

{img:robin-hood-bronze-statue-nottingham-castle-walls-bow-drawn-today}
Visitors to Nottingham can go down into the caves, under the city, and see where people lived and worked in the Middle Ages. There is also an old inn, built against the castle rock, with rooms cut into the sandstone. It is called Ye Olde Trip to Jerusalem, and it says that it is the oldest inn in England, from the time of the crusades. Whether that is true or not, it is a lovely place to sit, in a cave room, and imagine the knights setting off for the Holy Land with King Richard.

[long-pause]

And at Rufford Abbey, on the northern edge of the forest, the ruins of the old Cistercian abbey still stand, among green lawns and old trees. Part of the old building of the lay brothers survives, with its vaulted cellars. Later, a great country house was built onto it. Today, it is a park, where families walk and have picnics, where the white monks once prayed, worked and gave bread to the poor at their gate.

[long-pause]

{img:sherwood-forest-evening-1190-golden-light-through-oaks-deer-glade-calm}
So we leave Sherwood Forest tonight, in the year 1190, as the evening falls. The sun is going down behind the great oaks. Its last golden light comes through the branches in long beams, and lies on the grass of the glades. The deer are coming out of the woods, to graze in the cool of the evening. And the birds are singing their last songs of the day.

[long-pause]

In the village of Edwinstowe, the smoke is rising from the thatched roofs. The cows have been milked. The chickens are shut in. The pigs are back from the forest, grunting in their sty. In the little church, the priest is lighting a candle. And on the village green, a few children are still playing, until their mothers call them home.

[pause]

{img:cottage-evening-firelight-family-supper-girl-hedgehog-boy-whittling-bow}
In a small cottage at the edge of the village, a family is sitting around the fire. Hugh is mending his axe. Agnes is spinning. Wat is whittling a new arrow, carefully, with his knife. And Alys is sitting on the floor, with her hedgehog in her lap, telling it a story. About the forest, and the deer, and the great old oaks, and the green man who lives in the deepest woods.

[long-pause]

Out in the forest, Ralph the forester is riding slowly home, along a quiet path, his horn at his side. The trees are dark around him. An owl glides silently across the path. A fox stops, and looks at him, and trots on. The forester smiles. Everything is as it should be. The deer are safe. The forest is quiet. And soon he will be home, by his own fire.

[pause]

{img:moonlight-sherwood-oaks-silver-glade-owl-flying-peaceful-night}
The moon rises over Sherwood. It shines through the bare branches and the new leaves, and makes silver patterns on the forest floor. It shines on the charcoal pit in its clearing, where a thin line of blue smoke is rising into the night. It shines on the walls of Rufford Abbey, where the monks are singing Compline. And it shines on the great old oak near Edwinstowe, which is already old, and will grow older still, for a thousand years.

[long-pause]

The forest is quiet now. The wind whispers softly in the leaves. A stream murmurs over its stones. Somewhere far away, a stag calls, once, and then is silent.
