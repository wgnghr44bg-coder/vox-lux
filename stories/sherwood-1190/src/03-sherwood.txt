What was Sherwood Forest really like, in the year 1190? Today, when we hear the word forest, we think of a place full of trees. But in the Middle Ages, the word forest meant something different. A forest was an area of land that belonged to the king, where he could hunt. It could contain woods, but also heathland, fields, villages, rivers and even small towns.

--

Sherwood Forest is very big. It stretches for about thirty kilometres from north to south, and about twelve kilometres from east to west. It covers about a fifth of the whole county of Nottingham. In the south, it reaches almost to the walls of the town of Nottingham. In the north, it reaches as far as the town of Worksop. And within it, there are about twenty villages.

Much of Sherwood is covered by woods. Great old oak trees, and birches, with their white bark and their delicate leaves. But there are also wide open heaths, covered with heather, bracken and gorse, with its bright yellow flowers. The soil is sandy and poor, not very good for farming. That is one reason why so much of it has stayed wild. The kings found it a perfect place for hunting.

{img:heath-with-heather-gorse-yellow-flowers-birch-trees-sherwood-summer}
The name Sherwood means the wood of the shire, the wood that belongs to the whole county. It has been there for a very long time. Long before the Normans came, the Anglo-Saxons knew it, and used it, and named it. And after the Norman conquest of England in 1066, the new Norman kings made it a royal forest, a hunting ground for the king, with strict laws.

The forest is full of animals. Red deer, the biggest wild animals in England, with great antlers on the stags. Smaller roe deer. Wild boar, although they are becoming rare. Foxes, badgers, hares, rabbits and squirrels. Wolves have almost disappeared from this part of England, but people still tell stories about them. There are hawks, owls, woodpeckers and many songbirds. And in the rivers and ponds, there are fish, otters and herons.

--

{img:red-deer-stag-antlers-standing-in-bracken-oak-forest-misty-morning}
The most important animals, for the king, are the deer. They are called the king's deer. Nobody else may hunt them, or harm them in any way, on pain of severe punishment. The whole forest is managed for them. Their food is protected. Their resting places are protected. Even their peace and quiet is protected. They are the reason why the forest exists.

In the middle of Sherwood, there is a royal hunting lodge, at a place called Clipstone. It is called the King's Houses. When the king comes to hunt in Sherwood, he stays there, with his court. King Henry the Second, the father of Richard and John, came there several times. He had the lodge improved, with new buildings, a chapel and a fishpond. It is quite a grand place, for a hunting lodge, in the middle of the forest.

{img:royal-hunting-lodge-clipstone-stone-hall-forest-clearing-horses}
There is also a monastery in Sherwood. Rufford Abbey, founded about forty years before, by monks of the Cistercian order, who wear white robes and live very simply. They farm the land around their abbey, keep sheep, and pray. And in the south of the forest, near Nottingham, a new priory is being built at Newstead, by monks called Augustinian canons. Both of them are quiet, peaceful places, among the trees.

--

So Sherwood in 1190 is a living landscape. Woods and heaths, villages and fields, a royal lodge and quiet abbeys. People and animals living side by side. It is not quite the wild green forest of the stories, where outlaws could hide forever. But it is big enough, and wild enough in places, that someone who wanted to disappear could certainly find somewhere to hide.

Through the forest run old roads and tracks. The great road from London to the north, which travellers, merchants and messengers use. Smaller paths between the villages. And deer paths, winding through the bracken, which only the foresters and the people of the forest know. Here and there, at a crossing of paths, there is a stone cross, or a little shrine, where travellers stop to say a prayer for a safe journey.
