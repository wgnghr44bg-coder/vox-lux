There is one story about Robin Hood that is very old, and very gentle. It comes from the longest of the old ballads, the Gest of Robyn Hode. It is not about fighting or tricks. It is about kindness. And it shows why the people of England loved Robin Hood so much.

--

One day, Robin sent Little John and two of his men to the road, to find a guest for dinner. That was Robin's custom. He never ate until a guest had joined him. Usually, the guest was a rich traveller, who would then be asked to pay for the meal. But this time, the guest they found was a sad, quiet knight, riding slowly through the forest, on a thin horse, with his hood pulled down over his face. He looked very unhappy.

Little John invited him politely to dinner with Robin Hood. The knight agreed. They took him to the greenwood, where a great feast was waiting. Bread and wine, roast venison, swans and pheasants, and birds of the river. The knight ate and drank, and thanked Robin. Then Robin asked him, as he asked every guest, to pay for the meal. The knight said, sadly, that he had only ten shillings in the whole world.

{img:outlaw-host-and-sad-knight-feast-table-under-oak-tree-forest}
Robin did not believe him, at first. He sent Little John to look in the knight's bags. And it was true. There were only ten shillings. Robin was surprised. He asked the knight how a knight could be so poor. And the knight told him his story. His son had got into trouble, and to help him, the knight had borrowed a large sum of money from a rich abbot, four hundred pounds. He had promised his lands and his castle, as security. And the money must be paid back on a certain day. Very soon.

If the knight could not pay, the abbot would take his lands and his castle, and he and his wife would have nothing. He had asked all his friends for help. But his friends had all turned away from him, now that he was poor. Nobody would help. He was riding to the abbey now, to beg for more time, though he knew it was hopeless.

--

{img:robin-giving-bag-of-gold-to-grateful-knight-merry-men-watching}
Robin listened to the story. He was moved. He talked to his men. And then he lent the knight the four hundred pounds, from the merry men's treasure. He gave him new clothes, of fine cloth, and a good horse, and new boots and spurs. And he sent Little John with him, as his servant, so that he would not ride alone. The knight could hardly believe it. He wept with gratitude.

The knight rode to the abbey, and found the greedy abbot waiting, sure that he would get the lands. The knight pretended, at first, that he had no money, and begged for more time. The abbot refused, and laughed at him. And then the knight threw the bag of gold onto the table, and paid every penny. The abbot's face, the story says, was a sight to see.

{img:knight-returning-to-forest-year-later-repaying-debt-bows-gift-legend}
A year later, the knight came back to the greenwood, to pay back Robin. He brought the four hundred pounds. And he brought a gift. A hundred fine bows, and a hundred bundles of arrows, with peacock feathers, for the merry men. Robin was delighted. But he would not take the money. Because, by then, he had already been paid, by someone else. By a monk from the same greedy abbey, who had passed through the forest, and been invited to dinner. And who had paid, rather generously.

--

Alys likes this story best of all. She says that it is the real heart of Robin Hood. Not the fighting, not the tricks. But the kindness. Helping someone who is in trouble, when everyone else has turned away. Lending money without asking for interest. Giving clothes and a horse to someone who has nothing. And keeping your word. Her grandchildren listen quietly. And the littlest one asks if Robin Hood would help them, if they were in trouble. Alys smiles, and says that he would.
