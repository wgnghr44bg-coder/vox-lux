The years pass in Sherwood. The seasons turn, spring, summer, autumn and winter, again and again. The great oaks grow a little bigger every year. And the family in Edwinstowe grows older.

--

Wat becomes a forester, just as Ralph said he could. When Ralph grows old, and can no longer ride through the forest every day, he asks the chief forester to take Wat as his assistant. And when Ralph retires, Wat takes his place. He rides through Sherwood every day, in green, with a horn and a bow. He watches over the king's deer, and the king's trees. And he is a good forester, strict but fair, like Ralph before him.

Wat looks after the people of the forest, as well as the deer. He knows how hard life is for them. He looks the other way when a widow takes a little too much wood. He warns the boys when they are about to get into trouble. He helps families who are in need. And the people of the forest trust him and like him. They say that Wat is the best forester Sherwood has ever had. Ralph, sitting by his fire in his old age, is very proud.

{img:old-retired-forester-sitting-by-cottage-door-sun-young-forester-visiting}
Wat marries the miller's daughter, the girl he danced with on May Day. Her name is Mary. They have five children. And Wat teaches them all to love the forest, as he does. To read the tracks of animals, to walk quietly, to find their way by the moss on the trees. And he gives his oldest son the little yew bow that Ralph gave him, long ago.

Hugh goes on making charcoal for many years, until his arms are too stiff to cut wood. Then he sits by the fire, and carves spoons and bowls, and tells his grandchildren about the old days. About the year when King Richard went on crusade, and the people of Sherwood had to pay for his ransom. About the day the king came back to Nottingham. About the great stag that he and Wat once saw, in the golden autumn light.

--

{img:grandmother-agnes-herb-garden-teaching-granddaughter-plants-sunny}
Agnes becomes the wise woman of the village. Everyone comes to her when they are ill. She knows more about herbs than anyone in the county, except perhaps Brother Simon, at Rufford Abbey. She and Brother Simon remain friends all their lives. When she is very old, she passes away peacefully, in her own bed, in the little cottage, with her family around her.

And Alys becomes the storyteller of the village. She marries a carpenter from Edwinstowe, and has children and grandchildren. And every winter evening, by the fire, she tells them the old stories. About the forest and its creatures. About the green man of the woods. About the brave and the clever and the kind. And later, when the songs of Robin Hood spread through England, she tells those too.

{img:old-woman-storyteller-fireside-many-grandchildren-listening-cosy-cottage}
She still has her hedgehog, or rather, the great-great-grandson of her first hedgehog, which lives in the garden. She still remembers the taste of the first fig, at the market in Nottingham. And she still goes into the forest in May, very early in the morning, to gather hawthorn blossom, and wash her face in the dew. Just in case.

--

The little village of Edwinstowe goes on, year after year, century after century. The houses are rebuilt. The people come and go. But the church stays. The forest stays. The great oaks stay. And the stories stay, passed on from grandmother to grandchild, by the fire, on winter evenings, for hundreds of years. Until, one day, they are written down, and printed in books, and told all over the world.
