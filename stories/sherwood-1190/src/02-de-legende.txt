Robin Hood is one of the most famous heroes in the world. Everyone knows a little about him. The outlaw in Lincoln green, with his bow and arrows, living in Sherwood Forest. His band of merry men. His enemy, the wicked Sheriff of Nottingham. His love, Maid Marian. His friends, Little John, Friar Tuck and Will Scarlet. And his famous habit of taking from the rich, and giving to the poor.

--

But where do these stories come from? Like the stories of King Arthur, the stories of Robin Hood grew slowly, over hundreds of years. They began as songs and rhymes, sung by ordinary people, in villages and taverns, at fairs and festivals. Nobody knows who made them first. They were passed on from person to person, by word of mouth, and changed a little each time.

The first time that Robin Hood is mentioned in writing is in a long poem from about the year 1377, called Piers Plowman. In it, a lazy priest says that he does not know his prayers very well. But he knows the rhymes of Robin Hood. So by that time, the songs about Robin were already well known, and very popular. More popular than prayers, it seemed.

{img:medieval-manuscript-page-ballad-robin-hood-text-margin-drawings}
The oldest stories about Robin that survive were written down in the fourteen hundreds. They are long poems, called ballads, which were sung or recited. One of the longest is called A Gest of Robyn Hode, which means a tale of Robin Hood. In these early stories, Robin is a yeoman. Not a lord, and not a poor peasant, but a free countryman, who owns a little land. He lives in the forest, with his men, and they are clever, brave and full of tricks.

In the early stories, Robin lives mostly in a forest called Barnsdale, in Yorkshire, a little to the north of Sherwood. But he also goes to Sherwood, and to Nottingham, where the sheriff lives. Later, Sherwood became his main home in the stories. And today, everyone connects Robin Hood with Sherwood Forest and Nottingham.

--

{img:king-richard-lionheart-returning-legend-forest-meeting-outlaw-later-story}
In the early stories, the king is called simply Edward, our comely king. But later storytellers placed Robin in the time of King Richard the Lionheart and his brother, Prince John. Around the year 1190. Richard was away on crusade, and John was trying to take the throne. Robin became a loyal supporter of the good king Richard, fighting against the wicked John and his sheriff. That is the version most people know today.

Was there a real Robin Hood? Historians have searched the old records for hundreds of years. They have found several men called Robin Hood, or Robert Hod, in the twelve and thirteen hundreds. One of them was an outlaw in Yorkshire, in 1225. But nobody can prove that any of them was the Robin of the stories. Robin was a very common name. And Hood was a common surname too, for a man who made or wore hoods.

{img:old-oak-tree-forest-hollow-trunk-legend-hiding-place-afternoon-light}
Perhaps there was a real outlaw, long ago, whose adventures were remembered, and made bigger and bigger in the telling. Perhaps several outlaws were mixed together into one hero. Perhaps Robin was always a figure of stories, like a hero of the old May festivals. We will probably never know. But that does not matter very much. Because the real world in which the stories are set, the world of Sherwood Forest in the Middle Ages, was very real. And tonight, we will visit it.

--

So let us leave the legend for a while, and walk into the real forest. To a little village, at the edge of the trees, where a family is just waking up, in the spring of 1190. And later, when the evening comes, we will sit by the fire, and listen to the old stories of Robin Hood, as the people of Sherwood would tell them in later centuries.
