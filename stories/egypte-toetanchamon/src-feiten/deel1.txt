TOPIC: The black land beside the Nile
===
IMG: nile-dawn-reeds-boat | a fisherman in a papyrus boat on the misty Nile at dawn
HEAD: Ancient Egyptians built small boats out of the same reeds they used to make paper.
BODY: Along the edges of the Nile grew thick beds of papyrus, a tall reed with a feathery head. The Egyptians used it for almost everything. They wove it into mats, baskets, sandals and ropes. They pressed thin strips of it together to make sheets for writing. And they tied great bundles of it together to make light little boats. A papyrus boat was easy to build and easy to carry, and it floated well in the calm water near the banks. Fishermen stood in them at dawn, casting their nets while the mist still lay on the river. Hunters used them to glide quietly through the marshes, looking for ducks and geese. Larger wooden boats carried heavy cargo up and down the river, but for everyday work, the simple reed boat was perfect. Thousands of years later, people in some parts of Africa still build boats in almost the same way. The idea was simple, and it lasted.
===
IMG: green-strip-desert-edge | a narrow green strip of fields and palms ending sharply at golden desert
HEAD: In ancient Egypt, green fields ended so suddenly that a person could stand with one foot in crops and the other in desert.
BODY: Egypt is one of the driest countries in the world. Almost no rain falls there. Yet along the Nile lies a narrow strip of dark, fertile land, only a few miles wide in many places. On this strip, the ancient Egyptians grew wheat, barley, flax, vegetables and fruit, and planted tall date palms. Where the water could not reach, the green stopped abruptly and the golden desert began. The Egyptians called their fertile land the Black Land, after its dark soil. The desert was the Red Land. The Black Land was the world of the living, full of villages, fields and noise. The Red Land was quiet, empty and dangerous. It was also where the dead were buried, safe from the yearly flood. This simple geography shaped almost everything about Egyptian life. Nearly every person in the kingdom lived within a short walk of the river, and most could see the desert from their doorstep.
===
IMG: nile-flood-fields-water | fields covered by shallow floodwater with villages standing above it
HEAD: Every summer, the Nile rose and covered the fields, and the Egyptians were glad of it.
BODY: Far to the south, in the mountains of Ethiopia, heavy summer rains swelled the rivers that fed the Nile. Weeks later, the water reached Egypt, and the river began to rise. By late summer, it spread out across the fields, turning the valley into a wide shallow lake. The villages stood on slightly higher ground, like little islands. When the water went down again in the autumn, it left behind a fresh layer of rich black mud, perfect for planting. This yearly flood was the heartbeat of Egypt. A good flood meant a good harvest. A flood that was too low meant hunger, and one that was too high could wash away houses. The Egyptians measured the river carefully with marked stone stairways called nilometers. They even divided their year into three seasons based on the river: the flood, the growing time and the harvest. The whole kingdom lived by its gentle rhythm.
===
IMG: thebes-river-view-temples | the city of Thebes seen from the river with houses, palms and temple gateways
HEAD: In Tutankhamun's time, the great city of Thebes was the religious heart of Egypt.
BODY: Thebes stood on the east bank of the Nile, about four hundred miles south of the sea. Today, the modern city of Luxor stands in the same place. In ancient times, it was a large and busy city of mud-brick houses, gardens, workshops, markets and harbours. Above the low roofs rose the enormous stone gateways of its temples, painted in bright colours and decorated with tall flagpoles. The most important god of Thebes was Amun, the hidden one, and his temple at Karnak was one of the largest religious buildings ever made. For centuries, the kings of Egypt honoured Amun and gave rich gifts to his temples. Across the river, on the west bank, lay the tombs of the kings and the temples built in their memory. Thebes was a city of the living on one side of the river and a city of the dead on the other. Each day, the sun seemed to travel from one to the other.
===
IMG: western-cliffs-golden-light | steep golden cliffs on the west bank above green fields
HEAD: The Egyptians buried their dead on the west bank of the Nile, where the sun goes down.
BODY: Every evening, the sun sank behind the cliffs on the western side of the river. To the Egyptians, this was a daily image of death. They believed that the sun travelled through the underworld at night and was born again each morning in the east. So it seemed natural to bury the dead in the west, in the dry desert hills, where they could join the sun on its nightly journey and rise again. At Thebes, the western cliffs glowed golden and red in the evening light. Hidden among them were the tombs of kings, queens, nobles and ordinary workers. The dry desert air preserved everything placed in the tombs, from linen and wood to bread and flowers. That is why so much of what we know about ancient Egypt comes from burials. It is a slightly strange thought. Our best picture of how the Egyptians lived comes from the places they prepared for the afterlife.
===
IMG: farm-village-mud-houses-palms | a small farming village of mud-brick houses among date palms
HEAD: Most ancient Egyptians lived in small villages and worked as farmers.
BODY: We usually think of pharaohs, pyramids and golden treasures. But most people in ancient Egypt were farmers who lived in small villages along the river. Their houses were built of mud bricks, made from Nile mud mixed with straw and dried in the sun. The walls were thick, keeping the rooms cool in the heat. Roofs were flat, made of wooden beams, palm leaves and mud, and families often slept up there on hot nights. A typical house had a few rooms, a courtyard, a small kitchen with a clay oven and a storage space for grain. Goats, ducks and a donkey might live in the yard. Date palms grew between the houses, giving shade and sweet fruit. Life followed the seasons, the river and the needs of the fields. Most villagers never travelled far from home. Yet their hard work fed the whole kingdom, including the priests, the scribes, the soldiers and the king himself.
===
IMG: woman-grinding-grain-courtyard | a woman kneeling in a courtyard grinding grain on a flat stone
HEAD: Every loaf of bread in ancient Egypt began with someone kneeling on the ground, grinding grain by hand.
BODY: Bread was the most important food in Egypt, eaten by everyone from farmers to kings. But making it took a great deal of work. First, the grain had to be ground into flour. Women knelt behind a flat, sloping stone called a quern and pushed a smaller stone back and forth over the grain, again and again. It was slow, tiring work, done every day. Tiny bits of sand and stone often ended up in the flour. Over a lifetime, this gritty bread wore down people's teeth, and many ancient Egyptian skulls show badly worn teeth because of it. The flour was mixed with water and sometimes with honey, dates or herbs, and baked in clay pots or on the walls of small ovens. The Egyptians had many kinds of bread, in different shapes and sizes. Bread was so central to life that workers were often paid partly in loaves, along with jars of beer.
===
IMG: farmers-ploughing-oxen-field | a farmer guiding a wooden plough pulled by two oxen through wet earth
HEAD: When the floodwater went down, the Egyptian farmers had only a short time to plough and sow.
BODY: As soon as the Nile flood withdrew in the autumn, the fields were left soft, damp and covered with fresh black mud. The farmers had to work quickly, before the earth dried and hardened in the sun. They ploughed with simple wooden ploughs pulled by pairs of oxen, guiding them by hand. Behind them came the sowers, scattering seed from baskets. Then flocks of sheep or goats were sometimes driven across the fields to press the seeds into the soft ground. The main crops were emmer wheat and barley, used for bread and beer, and flax, used to make linen. Farmers also grew onions, lentils, beans, lettuce, cucumbers and melons. Tomb paintings often show these scenes of ploughing and sowing in great detail, with farmers chatting, singing and sometimes complaining. The Egyptians clearly hoped that the fields of the afterlife would be just like the fields they knew, only always fertile and never too hot.
===
IMG: shaduf-irrigation-canal | a man lifting water with a long wooden pole and bucket from a canal
HEAD: A simple wooden lever called a shaduf helped Egyptian gardeners lift water from the river.
BODY: During the dry months, gardens and orchards still needed water. To lift it from the river or the canals, the Egyptians used a clever device called a shaduf. It was a long wooden pole balanced on a frame, with a bucket hanging from one end and a heavy weight, often a lump of clay, on the other. The gardener pulled the bucket down into the water, and the counterweight helped him lift it up again. Then he swung it around and emptied it into a channel that carried the water to the plants. With a shaduf, one person could lift a surprising amount of water in a day. The device appears in tomb paintings from around the time of Tutankhamun, and it worked so well that farmers along the Nile still used it in the twentieth century. Sometimes the best inventions are the simple ones that nobody ever needs to improve.
===
IMG: harvest-golden-wheat-sickles | farmers cutting golden wheat with sickles while women gather the ears
HEAD: At harvest time, the scribes arrived in the fields to count the grain for taxes.
BODY: In spring, the wheat and barley turned golden, and the whole village went out to bring in the harvest. The men cut the stalks with curved sickles, made of wood with sharp flint teeth. Women and children followed behind, gathering the fallen ears in baskets. The grain was carried to a threshing floor, where cattle or donkeys walked over it to separate the seeds from the husks. Then the grain was tossed into the air so that the wind could blow the chaff away. But the farmers did not keep all of it. Scribes from the king or the temples came to measure the fields and count the harvest, and a share was taken as tax. The grain was stored in large granaries, which helped feed the kingdom in years of poor floods. Farmers who could not pay were sometimes punished. Even so, harvest time was also a time of songs, festivals and thanks to the gods.

TOPIC: The city of Thebes
===
IMG: thebes-street-market-morning | a narrow street in Thebes with whitewashed houses and a small market
HEAD: There were no coins in Tutankhamun's Egypt, so people traded goods for goods.
BODY: In the streets and markets of Thebes, people bought and sold bread, fish, vegetables, sandals, pots, linen and jewellery. Yet coins did not yet exist in Egypt. Instead, people bartered, exchanging one item for another. To make this easier, values were often measured in units of copper or silver, or in sacks of grain. A pair of sandals might be worth a certain weight of copper, and a goat a little more. Nobody actually had to hand over metal. People simply agreed on a fair exchange, perhaps a jar of oil and some fish for a basket. Records from the workers' village of Deir el-Medina show people carefully noting what they owed and what was owed to them. Some disputes over such debts even reached local courts. The system sounds complicated, but it worked well for many centuries. Coins only became common in Egypt much later, after contact with the Greeks.
===
IMG: nile-harbour-sailing-boats | a busy harbour on the Nile with wooden cargo boats and square sails
HEAD: On the Nile, the current carried boats north, and the wind carried them south.
BODY: The Nile flows from south to north, toward the Mediterranean Sea. In Egypt, the prevailing wind usually blows from the north. This fortunate combination made the river a perfect highway. Boats travelling north drifted with the current, while boats travelling south raised their sails and let the wind push them upstream. The Egyptian word for travelling north was written with a picture of a boat without a sail. The word for travelling south showed a boat with its sail raised. Large wooden boats carried grain, stone, cattle, wine and people along the length of the kingdom. The harbours of Thebes were busy with sailors, porters and merchants. There were few long-distance roads in Egypt, because the river made them largely unnecessary. Almost everything travelled by water. For the Egyptians, the Nile was not only a source of life and food. It was also the road that held the whole country together.
===
IMG: craftsmen-workshop-jewellery | craftsmen at work in a workshop with a potter's wheel and jewellery
HEAD: Egyptian craftsmen worked in busy workshops, often attached to the temples or the palace.
BODY: Ancient Egypt was full of skilled craftspeople. There were potters, carpenters, stone carvers, metalworkers, jewellers, weavers, leatherworkers and many more. Many of them worked in workshops belonging to the king or to the great temples, which supplied the materials and fed the workers. Tomb paintings show these workshops in lively detail. A potter spins his wheel, a carpenter shapes a chair with a copper adze, and a jeweller threads tiny beads of gold, carnelian and turquoise. Glass was still quite new and precious in Tutankhamun's time, and Egyptian glassmakers made beautiful blue and yellow vessels. Craftsmen usually learned their trade from their fathers, and skills were passed down through families for generations. Their work was often anonymous. Few of them signed their names. Yet when we look at the objects from Tutankhamun's tomb, with their perfect inlays and delicate carvings, we are looking at the patient work of hundreds of unknown hands.
===
IMG: karnak-temple-gateway-flags | the enormous painted stone gateway of Karnak with tall flagpoles
HEAD: The temple of Karnak was so large that it took around two thousand years to build.
BODY: Karnak was the great temple of the god Amun at Thebes. It was not built all at once. Instead, king after king added new halls, gateways, courtyards, statues and obelisks, over a period of about two thousand years. By Tutankhamun's time, it was already enormous, with massive stone gateways called pylons, covered in carved and brightly painted scenes. In front of the gateways stood tall wooden flagpoles, with coloured cloth banners fluttering at the top. Inside lay courtyards and dim halls filled with huge columns. Ordinary people were not allowed into the inner parts of the temple. Only priests could enter the sacred rooms where the statue of the god was kept. Yet the temple was also a busy place, owning land, cattle, workshops and boats, and employing thousands of people. Today, the ruins of Karnak still cover a vast area, and walking among its columns, people often feel very small indeed.
===
IMG: priests-temple-hall-columns | priests with shaved heads walking through a dim hall of painted columns
HEAD: Egyptian priests shaved their heads and bodies, and washed several times a day, to stay pure for the gods.
BODY: Priests served the gods in the temples, and they were expected to be very clean. They shaved off all their hair, washed in sacred pools several times a day and wore only fine white linen and simple sandals. Every morning, the most important priests entered the inner shrine, where the statue of the god stood in darkness. They woke the god with hymns, washed and dressed the statue, offered it food and drink, burned incense and sealed the shrine again. Many priests did not serve full time. They worked in shifts, a month at a time, and spent the rest of the year at home with their families and ordinary jobs. There were also priestesses, singers and musicians who served in the temples. The great temple halls were dim and cool, lit only by small windows high in the walls. In the soft light, the painted columns and the smell of incense must have made the place feel truly sacred.

TOPIC: The tomb-makers, the scribes and their children
===
IMG: deir-el-medina-village-valley | a small walled village of mud-brick houses in a narrow valley
HEAD: The men who built the royal tombs lived in their own small village, hidden in the desert hills.
BODY: On the west bank at Thebes, in a narrow valley behind the hills, lay a small walled village. Today it is known as Deir el-Medina. In Tutankhamun's time, it was home to the skilled workmen who cut and decorated the royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings. There were stonecutters, plasterers, sculptors, painters and scribes, along with their wives and children. The village had about seventy houses, packed closely along a single main street. It was surrounded by a wall, and the families lived somewhat apart from the rest of Thebes, perhaps to help keep the location of the royal tombs secret. Water, food and supplies were brought to the village by porters and donkeys. The workmen were paid by the state in grain, fish, vegetables, oil and clothing. Because they were well educated, many of the villagers could read and write. This makes Deir el-Medina one of the best documented communities in the whole ancient world.
===
IMG: workers-walking-path-cliffs-dawn | tomb workers carrying tools walking along a path in the cliffs at dawn
HEAD: The tomb builders worked a ten-day week, and they often slept near their work during the week.
BODY: The workmen of Deir el-Medina had a regular routine. The Egyptian week lasted ten days, and the men usually worked for eight of them, with days off in between and extra holidays for festivals. Each working day, they walked over the hills on a steep path to the Valley of the Kings, carrying their tools and baskets. The path is still there today. The work was divided into two shifts, the left side and the right side of the tomb, each led by a foreman. During the working week, the men often slept in small stone huts near the valley, rather than walking home every night. Records from the village tell us when men were absent and why. Some were ill, some were stung by scorpions, and some had to help with a family event, like a birthday or a funeral. One man was even excused because he was brewing beer. It all sounds surprisingly familiar.
===
IMG: ostracon-scribe-limestone-flake | a scribe writing with a reed pen on a white limestone flake
HEAD: Instead of expensive papyrus, the villagers often wrote on smooth flakes of white limestone.
BODY: Papyrus was costly, so for everyday notes, the people of Deir el-Medina used something they had plenty of. When the workmen cut tombs into the rock, they produced countless smooth, white flakes of limestone. These made excellent writing surfaces. Archaeologists call such pieces ostraca. Thousands of them have been found around the village. They contain all sorts of writing: lists of tools, work records, letters, laundry lists, receipts, legal disputes, prayers, poems, school exercises and even little sketches and jokes. One note asks a neighbour to return a borrowed item. Another records a complaint about a donkey. There are drawings of cats, monkeys and kings, made by artists practising their skills. These small stones are wonderfully personal. Unlike the grand inscriptions on temple walls, they show us ordinary people dealing with ordinary life, writing quick notes to each other, and sometimes grumbling, just as people do today.
===
IMG: painters-tomb-wall-lamplight | painters filling in colourful figures on a tomb wall by lamplight
HEAD: Egyptian tomb painters worked deep underground, by the light of small oil lamps.
BODY: Decorating a royal tomb was a long and careful process. First, the walls were smoothed and covered with plaster. Then an artist drew the outlines of the scenes in red paint, often using a grid of lines to keep the proportions right. A master artist checked the drawing and corrected mistakes in black. Then the sculptors carved the outlines, and finally the painters filled in the colours. Their paints were made from natural minerals: red and yellow from ochre, white from chalk, black from soot and a brilliant blue from a special pigment made by heating sand, copper and other ingredients. Deep inside the tomb, there was no daylight at all. The painters worked by the light of small lamps burning oil, and it is thought that they added a little salt to the oil so that it would make less smoke. In the soft flickering light, the gods and kings slowly appeared on the walls, in colours that still glow today.
===
IMG: village-evening-families-roofs | a village at dusk with families sitting on the flat roofs
HEAD: On warm evenings, Egyptian families went up onto their flat roofs to enjoy the cool air.
BODY: The houses of ancient Egypt had flat roofs, and these were useful spaces. During the day, people dried fish, fruit and grain up there, or stored firewood and fodder. In the evening, when the heat of the day faded, families climbed the stairs or ladders to sit on the roof. The air was cooler, and a breeze often came across the river. Neighbours could call to one another from roof to roof. Children played, and the adults talked about the day. Smoke from the evening cooking fires drifted across the village. On the hottest nights, many families slept on their roofs, on mats under light linen covers, beneath the enormous starry sky. In the village of the tomb builders, the sounds of the evening would have been gentle: a donkey braying, a dog barking, a baby crying, and somewhere, perhaps, a harp or a song. It was a simple pleasure, and it is still enjoyed in many hot countries today.
===
IMG: scribe-seated-papyrus-palette | a scribe sitting cross-legged with a papyrus roll and a palette
HEAD: Only a small number of ancient Egyptians could read and write, and being a scribe was a respected career.
BODY: Writing in Egypt used hieroglyphs, small pictures that stood for sounds or ideas, and a faster, flowing script called hieratic for everyday use. Learning them took years. Perhaps only a few people in every hundred could read and write. Those who could became scribes, and they were highly valued. Scribes kept the records of the kingdom. They counted harvests, recorded taxes, wrote letters, copied religious texts and managed the accounts of temples and estates. A scribe sat cross-legged, with a roll of papyrus stretched across his knees. He carried a palette with two little hollows for red and black ink, and reed pens chewed at the end to make a brush. Statues often show scribes in this pose, alert and calm. Egyptian texts praise the scribe's life, saying that he never has to carry heavy loads or work in the mud. It was clean work, it paid well, and it could lead to great positions.
===
IMG: school-boys-writing-boards | schoolboys copying hieroglyphs onto white boards
HEAD: Egyptian schoolboys learned to write by copying old texts again and again.
BODY: Boys who were training to become scribes usually began their lessons at around the age of five or ten. They studied at schools attached to temples or government offices, or with a scribe who taught a few students. They practised on boards covered with white plaster, which could be wiped clean, and on flakes of limestone. Their main method was copying. They copied lists of words, letters, prayers and famous old texts, again and again, until they knew them by heart. Many of these school exercises survive, sometimes with the teacher's corrections in red ink. Some of the texts the boys copied were written to encourage them. One famous text describes all the hard and dirty jobs in Egypt, from the fisherman to the brick maker, and then says how much better it is to be a scribe. Teachers could be strict. One old text says that a boy's ear is on his back, meaning that he listens when he is beaten.
===
IMG: girls-weaving-mother-courtyard | a mother and daughter weaving linen on a loom in a courtyard
HEAD: Almost all clothing in ancient Egypt was made of linen, spun and woven from the flax plant.
BODY: Flax grew well in the fields along the Nile. From its long fibres, the Egyptians made linen. The stalks were soaked, beaten and combed, and the fibres were spun into thread. Spinning and weaving were often done by women at home, and girls learned from their mothers. In Tutankhamun's time, many households wove cloth on horizontal looms laid on the ground. Larger workshops were beginning to use upright looms. The finest Egyptian linen was extremely thin, almost transparent, and highly valued. It was even used as a form of payment. Most clothes were simple. Men wore linen kilts wrapped around the waist, and women wore long straight dresses. Wealthy people wore finely pleated garments and fringed shawls. Linen was cool in the heat, and it was usually left white or pale. When a pharaoh was buried, he was wrapped in hundreds of yards of the finest linen the kingdom could produce.
===
IMG: children-playing-ball-courtyard | children playing with a ball and a wooden toy animal in a courtyard
HEAD: Egyptian children played with balls, dolls, spinning tops and wooden animals with moving parts.
BODY: Children in ancient Egypt had toys much like children today. Archaeologists have found leather balls stuffed with straw or reeds, spinning tops, rattles, dolls of wood and cloth and little animals made of clay. Some toys were remarkably clever. One wooden cat has a jaw that opens and closes when a string is pulled, and there are toy animals on little wheels. Paintings show children playing leapfrog, tug of war, ball games and juggling. Girls and boys played together when they were small. Children did not usually wear many clothes in the heat, and young boys and girls often had their heads shaved except for one long lock of hair at the side, called the sidelock of youth. By the age of about ten or twelve, though, most children were already helping their families, working in the fields, the kitchen or the workshop. Childhood was short, but the toys they left behind show that it was also playful.
===
IMG: cat-sleeping-house-niche | a slender cat sleeping on a cushion in a niche of a house
HEAD: The ancient Egyptians were among the first people in the world to keep cats in their homes.
BODY: Cats were very welcome in ancient Egypt. They kept mice and rats away from the grain stores, and they hunted snakes and scorpions. But Egyptians also simply liked them. Paintings show cats sitting under their owners' chairs, wearing collars, playing and even helping with bird hunting in the marshes. The Egyptian word for cat was miu, which sounds much like a cat's own voice. Cats were connected with the goddess Bastet, a gentle goddess of the home, of mothers and of joy. In later centuries, millions of cats were mummified and offered to her at her temple. In Tutankhamun's time, a much-loved cat belonging to a prince was even given her own carved stone coffin, with an inscription naming her as the Osiris Tamit, which means she cat. When a family cat died, it is said that the people of the house shaved their eyebrows as a sign of mourning. Few animals have ever been so honoured.

TOPIC: The king of the sun
===
IMG: amarna-city-desert-river | a new city with long straight streets on an open desert plain by the Nile
HEAD: Just before Tutankhamun's time, a pharaoh built a completely new capital city in the empty desert.
BODY: The pharaoh Akhenaten, who was probably Tutankhamun's father, made one of the most surprising decisions in Egyptian history. He decided to worship one god above all others: the Aten, the shining disc of the sun. To honour the Aten, he built a brand new capital city on an empty stretch of desert beside the Nile, halfway between Thebes and Memphis. He called it Akhetaten, the Horizon of the Aten. Today, the place is known as Amarna. The city was built very quickly, within a few years. It had wide straight streets, palaces, temples, government offices, workshops and neighbourhoods of houses with gardens. Tens of thousands of people moved there from the old cities. It must have been an extraordinary experience, to arrive in a brand new city on a bare desert plain, with fresh white walls and gardens just beginning to grow. Tutankhamun was most likely born there, around 1341 BC.
===
IMG: sun-disk-rays-relief | an open-air temple courtyard full of altars in the sunshine
HEAD: The temples of the sun god at Amarna had no roofs, so that the sunlight could fall directly on the altars.
BODY: Traditional Egyptian temples were dark and mysterious inside, with the god's statue hidden in a shrine at the back. Akhenaten's temples to the Aten were very different. They were open to the sky. The sun itself was the god, so there was no need for a statue. Instead, the temples had wide courtyards filled with hundreds of small stone altars, where offerings of bread, meat, fruit, flowers and incense were laid out in the bright sunlight. Carvings from this time show the Aten as a sun disc with long rays reaching down, each ray ending in a small human hand. Some of the hands hold the sign of life toward the king and queen. Akhenaten wrote a beautiful hymn to the Aten, praising the sun for waking the world each morning and caring for every living thing. Some lines of it are remarkably similar to a psalm in the Bible, written many centuries later. Its tone is gentle and full of wonder.
===
IMG: amarna-palace-garden-pool | a palace garden with a blue pool, lotus flowers and trees
HEAD: The palaces of Amarna had gardens with pools, flowers and painted floors showing birds and marshes.
BODY: Archaeologists who excavated Amarna found the remains of lovely palaces and villas. Many had gardens with rectangular pools, surrounded by trees, flowers and shady walkways. Blue lotus flowers floated on the water, and fish swam beneath them. Tall papyrus plants and date palms grew around the edges. Inside the palaces, the walls and even the floors were painted with scenes of nature. One famous painted floor shows a marsh full of reeds, flowers, flying ducks, fish and calves leaping in the fields. It was meant to bring the beauty of the living world indoors. Art from Amarna is more relaxed and natural than older Egyptian art. Plants seem to move in the breeze, and animals look lively and real. For the young prince Tutankhamun, growing up in these surroundings must have been very pleasant. He would have played in sunlit courtyards and shady gardens, with the sounds of water, birds and music all around him.
===
IMG: royal-family-sun-rays-art | a king and queen with their little daughters in a palace hall
HEAD: Art from Amarna shows the royal family in tender, everyday moments, something never seen before in Egypt.
BODY: For many centuries, Egyptian kings had been shown as stiff, powerful and godlike. But at Amarna, the artists did something new. They showed Akhenaten and his queen, Nefertiti, with their daughters in warm, private moments. In one carving, the king kisses one of his little girls. In another, the queen holds a daughter on her lap, while another child climbs on her shoulder. The family sits together under the rays of the sun disc, relaxed and affectionate. Even the bodies of the king and queen were shown in a new way, with long faces, slender necks and rounded bellies. Nobody knows exactly why. It may have been a new religious style. Nefertiti herself became famous for her beauty, and a painted bust of her, found at Amarna, is one of the best-known works of art in the world. Tutankhamun's future wife, Ankhesenpaaten, was one of the daughters in these tender family scenes.
===
IMG: palace-nursery-baby-nurse | a nurse holding a small baby boy in a cool palace room
HEAD: We know the name of Tutankhamun's wet nurse, because her tomb was discovered in 1997.
BODY: Royal children in ancient Egypt were often cared for by nurses, who fed and looked after them in the palace. Being the nurse of a future king was a great honour, and it brought respect and rewards for life. In 1997, archaeologists working at Saqqara, near Memphis, found the tomb of a woman named Maia. Inscriptions in her tomb call her the wet nurse of the king, the one who fed the body of the god. And one carving shows her holding a young king on her lap, almost certainly Tutankhamun himself. Maia was clearly a woman of importance, with a beautifully decorated tomb. We know nothing about her personality, of course. Yet it is touching to think of her holding a small boy in a cool palace room, long before he became king. It reminds us that even pharaohs began life as babies, who needed to be fed, comforted and rocked to sleep by someone who cared for them.
===
IMG: amarna-workers-village-evening | an ordinary family praying before a small shrine in their house in the evening
HEAD: Even when the king worshipped only the sun disc, many ordinary Egyptians quietly kept their old household gods.
BODY: Akhenaten's new religion was centred on the royal family and the Aten. But for ordinary people, especially those living outside the court, the change may not have gone very deep. Archaeologists working in the workmen's village at Amarna found small amulets and figures of traditional household gods. There was Bes, a cheerful dwarf god who protected mothers and children, and Taweret, a goddess shaped like a hippopotamus, who watched over childbirth. People kept small shrines in their homes and prayed for health, safe births and good luck, much as their parents and grandparents had done. Official religion might change by royal command, but the habits of families changed more slowly. It is a gentle reminder that in every age, most people's faith has been personal and practical. They prayed for the things that mattered most to them: their children, their health and a good night's sleep in a safe home.
