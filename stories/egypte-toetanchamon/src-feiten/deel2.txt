TOPIC: The boy king and his queen
BRIDGE: The city of the sun disc was emptying. And on the throne of Egypt sat a child.
===
IMG: boy-king-coronation-temple | a boy king of about nine wearing the blue crown in a great temple hall
HEAD: Tutankhamun became king of Egypt when he was only about nine years old.
BODY: Around the year 1332 before Christ, a young boy was crowned king of the Two Lands. At first, his name was Tutankhaten, which means the living image of the Aten, the sun disc his family had worshipped. He was probably a son of Akhenaten, the king who had turned Egypt's religion upside down. Now that king was gone, and the throne passed to a child. At the coronation, the boy would have worn fine white linen and a broad golden collar. Priests placed crowns on his head and put the crook and the flail in his small hands, the old signs of a king who guides and protects his people. Of course, a nine-year-old could not rule a great kingdom alone. Experienced officials stood close beside him and made most of the decisions. Yet in the eyes of the Egyptians, he was now something more than a boy. He was the living link between the gods and the people, and the whole land depended on him.
===
IMG: ay-elderly-official-court | an elderly official in fine pleated linen standing beside a throne
HEAD: One of the most powerful men at the young king's side was an old courtier called Ay.
BODY: Ay had served at the royal court for many years, perhaps since the days of Akhenaten's father. He carried a curious title: God's Father. Nobody is completely sure what it meant, but it showed that he was very close to the royal family. Some historians think he may even have been a relative of Queen Nefertiti. When the boy king came to the throne, Ay was already an old man by the standards of his time. He knew how the palace worked, which officials could be trusted, and how the great temples were run. Along with the army commander Horemheb, he helped to guide the kingdom while the king was young. We cannot hear their conversations, and we do not know how much the boy listened to them. Yet the decisions made in those years carry their fingerprints. Ay would stay close to the throne until the very end of Tutankhamun's life, and, as we will see, a little beyond it.
===
IMG: treasurer-maya-scribe-office | a treasurer checking papyrus accounts in a storeroom of sealed jars
HEAD: The man in charge of Tutankhamun's treasury left him a gift that was found in the tomb.
BODY: His name was Maya, and he was the overseer of the treasury. That meant he looked after the king's wealth: the gold, the linen, the grain, the oil and the many goods that flowed into the royal storerooms. It was a job that needed patience and careful accounts, kept by teams of scribes on papyrus rolls. Maya also organised building work, including repairs in the royal cemeteries. When the young king died, Maya did something personal. He gave a small wooden figure of the king lying on a funeral bed, and inscribed it with his own name as a gift. Another of his gifts was a lifelike figure of the king to serve him in the afterlife. Both were placed in the tomb. More than three thousand years later, Maya's own beautiful tomb was rediscovered at Saqqara, near Memphis, in 1986. It is a quiet thought. A careful official, who spent his life counting treasures, chose to leave a small one of his own.
===
IMG: abandoned-city-sand-drifting | an empty city of white mud-brick palaces with sand drifting through the doorways
HEAD: Within a few years of the young king's reign, a whole royal city was left empty.
BODY: Akhenaten had built a brand new capital in the middle of Egypt, on a wide plain beside the Nile. Today we call it Amarna. It had palaces, temples open to the sky, wide roads, gardens and thousands of houses. But it had been built quickly, for one king and one religion. When that king died, the city lost its purpose. Under Tutankhamun, the court moved away, and the people slowly followed. Craftsmen packed their tools. Families loaded their belongings onto donkeys. Officials left their fine villas with their painted walls. In time, the empty rooms filled with sand, blown in by the desert wind. Because nobody built a new town on top of it, Amarna stayed almost untouched for more than three thousand years. For archaeologists, this is a rare gift. They can walk along its streets and see the outline of a whole city, frozen at the moment its people walked away.
===
IMG: memphis-palace-river-pyramids | a palace among palm gardens at Memphis with the pyramids on the horizon
HEAD: The young king's court moved to Memphis, an ancient city that already had pyramids on its horizon.
BODY: Memphis lay near the point where the Nile valley opens out into the wide green delta. It was one of the oldest cities in Egypt, already more than a thousand years old in Tutankhamun's time. From its palaces and gardens, you could see the great pyramids of Giza and Saqqara standing on the desert edge. To the young king, those pyramids would have looked as ancient as the castles of the Middle Ages look to us. Memphis was a busy city of harbours, workshops, temples and government offices. Ships arrived with timber, copper, wine and oil from distant lands. Many of the kingdom's most important officials lived there. Moving the court to Memphis was a sign that Egypt was turning back to its old ways and its old centres of power. Thebes in the south stayed the religious heart of the land. But much of the day-to-day running of the kingdom now happened here, in the shadow of the oldest monuments on Earth.
===
IMG: young-king-chariot-desert-hunt | a young pharaoh driving a light chariot across the desert with a hunting dog
HEAD: Six chariots were found in Tutankhamun's tomb, taken apart so they would fit through the doors.
BODY: Chariots were the sports cars of the ancient world. They were light, fast and beautifully made, with two wheels and a small platform for the driver. Tutankhamun owned several of them. Some were covered in gold and decorated for ceremonies. Others were simpler, made for hunting or for driving across the desert. Hunting was a favourite pastime of Egyptian kings. They chased gazelles, hares, wild bulls and ostriches, often with dogs running beside the wheels. One fan from the tomb once held ostrich feathers, and its handle tells us that the king caught the birds himself in the desert east of the city of Heliopolis. The feathers have mostly crumbled away. Yet the little inscription survives, like a note left behind after a good day out. When the king was buried, his chariots were carefully taken apart and placed in the tomb in pieces, ready for him to drive again in the fields of the afterlife.
===
IMG: queen-ankhesenamun-palace-garden | a young queen in pleated white linen walking among lotus pools
HEAD: Tutankhamun's queen was a daughter of Akhenaten and Nefertiti.
BODY: Her name was Ankhesenamun. Like her husband, she had been given a different name at birth: Ankhesenpaaten, which honoured the sun disc. When the court turned back to the old gods, both of them changed their names to honour Amun instead. She was probably a few years older than the king, and she had grown up in the palaces of Amarna, among her many sisters. In ancient Egypt, it was not unusual for royal brothers and sisters, or half-brothers and half-sisters, to marry. It kept the royal line close and protected. To us, this seems very strange, but at the time it was seen as fitting for a family that was believed to be close to the gods. Ankhesenamun appears beside the king in several of the treasures from his tomb. In these scenes, she is always graceful, calm and close to him. It is one of the few royal marriages from ancient Egypt that still feels quietly personal to us.
===
IMG: golden-throne-queen-anointing | a young queen touching her husband's shoulder with perfumed oil in a palace room
HEAD: On the back of Tutankhamun's golden throne, his queen is shown gently rubbing perfume onto his shoulder.
BODY: The golden throne is one of the most famous objects from the tomb. It is made of wood covered in gold and silver, inlaid with coloured glass, stone and faience. But the most remarkable thing about it is a small scene on the backrest. The young king sits relaxed on a cushioned chair. His queen stands in front of him. In one hand she holds a little cup, and with the other she gently touches his shoulder, as if smoothing perfumed oil onto his skin. Above them, the sun disc shines down, its rays ending in tiny hands. It is a soft, private moment, quite unlike the usual images of kings striding into battle. The scene is in the relaxed style of Amarna, where the royal family was often shown in tender, everyday poses. Most royal art was meant to impress. This picture seems meant to remember a feeling.
===
IMG: palace-hall-painted-floor | a palace hall with papyrus columns and a painted floor showing a pool with fish
HEAD: In Egyptian palaces of this time, even the floors were painted like a garden pond.
BODY: Archaeologists working at royal palaces of this period found the remains of painted plaster floors. Instead of plain stone or tiles, the floors showed a pool of water, full of fish and lotus flowers, surrounded by reeds where ducks and birds flew up. Walking across such a floor must have felt like stepping into a cool marsh on a hot day. The walls were painted too, with plants, birds and patterns in bright blue, green, red and yellow. Wooden columns were carved and painted to look like bundles of papyrus. High windows with stone grilles let in light and air, while keeping out the worst of the sun. Servants stood with long fans made of ostrich feathers to move the warm air around. For the Egyptians, the marsh was a place of new life, where the world had first risen out of the water. To live inside a painted marsh was to live inside a calm, endless beginning.
===
IMG: musicians-harp-lute-banquet | women playing a harp, a lute and a double flute at an evening banquet
HEAD: Two trumpets from Tutankhamun's tomb were played on the radio in 1939, and millions of people heard them.
BODY: Music filled Egyptian life. At banquets, women played harps, lutes and double flutes, while singers performed songs about love, wine and the shortness of life. Guests wore garlands of flowers and sat on low chairs, listening in the soft light of oil lamps. Among the treasures in Tutankhamun's tomb were two trumpets, one made of silver and one of bronze, each with a wooden core to protect its shape. They were probably used in ceremonies or for the army. In 1939, more than three thousand years after they were last heard, a musician played them in a broadcast from the Cairo Museum. Their sound was loud, rough and strange, very different from modern brass instruments. Some people later claimed that the trumpets brought bad luck, but that is only a story. What remains is a recording of a sound from the time of the pharaohs, crackling through the air into living rooms around the world.
===
IMG: royal-couple-boat-marsh-lotus | a young king and queen in a papyrus boat among reeds and lotus flowers
HEAD: A small golden shrine from the tomb shows the king and queen together in a boat, hunting birds in the marshes.
BODY: This shrine is only about half a metre tall, made of wood and covered in thin gold. Every side is decorated with little scenes of the royal couple. In one, the king sits on a chair with his bow, shooting at birds, while the queen sits at his feet and hands him an arrow. In another, she ties a necklace around his neck. In others, they glide through the marshes in a light boat. The king holds a curved throwing stick, the Egyptian way of hunting ducks, and the queen holds a lotus flower. Real throwing sticks were found in the tomb too, some of them gilded. The scenes on the shrine are full of small, warm gestures, a hand on an arm, a flower offered, a look exchanged. They were probably meant to show the queen's role in keeping the king alive and well. Yet to us, they look like pages from a family album, left in gold.
===
IMG: palace-terrace-sunset-couple | a young king and queen on a palace roof looking at the Nile at sunset
HEAD: In Tutankhamun's tomb lay two tiny coffins, holding the daughters he and his queen had lost.
BODY: The young royal couple did not have any children who survived. When archaeologists explored the tomb, they found a plain wooden box in the treasury. Inside were two small coffins, each holding the mummy of a baby girl. One was born far too early. The other was born closer to term, but did not live. DNA studies suggest that they were most likely the daughters of Tutankhamun himself. The babies had been carefully preserved and wrapped, just like adults, and placed beside their father for the journey into the afterlife. They had no names, as far as we know. It is a sad discovery, but it is also a tender one. It shows that, even in a world of golden masks and great ceremonies, a young king and queen grieved for their children like any other parents. They kept them close. And they made sure that wherever the king was going, his little daughters would go with him.
===
TOPIC: The Beautiful Feast of Opet
BRIDGE: Inside the palace, life was private and quiet. But once a year, the whole of Thebes came out to celebrate.
===
IMG: opet-festival-crowds-avenue | a festive crowd lining an avenue of stone sphinxes in Thebes
HEAD: Once a year, the gods of Thebes went on a holiday, and the whole city came out to watch.
BODY: The Opet Festival took place during the season of the flood, when the fields were under water and there was little farm work to do. It celebrated the god Amun, who travelled from his great temple at Karnak to the temple at Luxor, a little further south. There, his strength and the strength of the king were renewed for another year. For ordinary people, it was one of the happiest times of the year. Crowds lined the processional route, dressed in their best linen. There was music, dancing, singing and food. In early times, the festival lasted about eleven days. Later, it grew to almost a month. Normally, the statues of the gods were hidden away in dark sanctuaries, where only priests could see them. During Opet, the gods came out into the light, carried among the people. For a few days, the great and the ordinary shared the same streets and the same joy.
===
IMG: sacred-barque-priests-shoulders | priests carrying a golden sacred boat with a veiled shrine on their shoulders
HEAD: The gods of Egypt travelled by boat, even when they were carried over dry land.
BODY: In Egyptian belief, the gods moved through the heavens in boats, just as people moved along the Nile. So when a god's statue left its temple for a festival, it travelled in a sacred boat. These boats were made of wood covered in gold, with a small closed shrine in the middle where the statue sat, hidden behind a veil. Priests carried the boat on long poles resting on their shoulders. Sometimes there were twenty or thirty of them, walking slowly in step. People along the route could ask the god questions, and the movement of the boat was taken as an answer. If it seemed to lean forward, the answer was yes. If it pulled back, the answer was no. This way of asking the gods was called an oracle. It was used to settle quarrels, find lost things and even choose officials. For a moment, an ordinary person could stand before a god and receive a reply.
===
IMG: temple-singers-sistrum-procession | temple singers shaking bronze rattles in a procession with drums and incense
HEAD: Egyptian temple singers used a rattle called a sistrum, which sounded like wind in the papyrus reeds.
BODY: The sistrum was a small bronze instrument with a handle and a loop of metal. Thin rods ran across the loop, with little metal discs on them. When it was shaken, the discs jingled with a soft, shimmering sound. The Egyptians said it sounded like the rustling of papyrus in the marsh. It was especially linked to the goddess Hathor, the goddess of music, love and joy. During processions, women from important families served as temple singers. They walked in rows, shaking their sistrums and singing hymns, while drummers and clapper players kept the rhythm. Incense smoke drifted through the air. People along the road knelt or raised their hands as the gods passed by. Several sistrums have survived from ancient Egypt, and some can still be played. Their sound is gentle and bright at the same time. It is easy to imagine it rising above a crowd on a warm festival morning, long ago.
===
IMG: golden-barge-towed-river | a golden ceremonial barge on the Nile towed upstream by men on the bank
HEAD: During the festival, the god's golden boat was placed on a real river barge and pulled upstream by teams of men.
BODY: For part of the Opet journey, the sacred boats travelled on the Nile itself. They were placed on great river barges, covered in gold and decorated with carved heads of rams, the sacred animal of Amun. Because the festival boats were travelling south, against the current, they could not simply drift. Teams of men walked along the bank, pulling the barges with long ropes. Soldiers marched beside them. Musicians played, dancers leapt and people cheered. Some walls at Luxor temple show these scenes in great detail: the barges, the ropes, the crowds, even food and drink being handed out. It must have been a long, hot and noisy day. Yet the pulling was also an honour. To help carry a god home was something you might remember for the rest of your life. And for a farmer whose fields lay under the flood, there were few better ways to spend a summer day.
===
IMG: luxor-temple-colonnade-torches | the tall papyrus columns of Luxor temple at dusk lit by torches
HEAD: The walls of Luxor temple were decorated under Tutankhamun, but later another king put his own name over his.
BODY: At Luxor temple, there is a long hall of enormous stone columns, shaped like open papyrus plants. It was begun by Tutankhamun's grandfather, Amenhotep the Third. During Tutankhamun's reign, artists covered its walls with scenes of the Opet Festival: boats, priests, musicians, dancers and crowds. It was a way of showing that the old festivals were back after the years of Akhenaten. But Tutankhamun's name is not easy to find there today. Some years after his death, a later king, Horemheb, had his own name carved over many of the young king's inscriptions. This was common practice. Kings often took credit for the work of others. Yet the scenes themselves were left in place. The singers, the dancers and the rowers still move along the walls, just as Tutankhamun's artists drew them. In the evening, when the stone glows in the last light, they seem almost ready to start the festival again.
===
IMG: riverbank-night-families-lamps | families sitting on the riverbank at night with lamps and boats on the water
HEAD: At festival time, the temples gave out bread and beer to the crowds.
BODY: Egyptian temples were not only places of prayer. They were also huge storehouses, with fields, herds, workshops and granaries of their own. Every day, food was offered to the gods. When the gods had finished with it, so to speak, the offerings were shared out among the priests and temple staff. During great festivals, much more was given away. Records from various periods mention thousands of loaves of bread, cakes, jars of beer, meat and fruit, handed out to the people who came to celebrate. Beer in ancient Egypt was thick, mild and nourishing, more like a sort of liquid bread than modern beer. After a long day of processions, families would sit together on the riverbank, eating and talking as the sun went down. Small oil lamps glowed on the boats and along the water's edge. Children fell asleep in their parents' laps. Above them, the stars came out over Thebes, one by one.

TOPIC: The last winter
BRIDGE: After the festival, the flood went down, and the year turned towards the growing season.
===
IMG: winter-nile-fields-green-morning | green fields of young wheat along the Nile on a mild winter morning
HEAD: The Egyptian winter was the season of growing, when the whole valley turned green.
BODY: When the flood went down in the autumn, the farmers sowed their seed in the damp black earth. Then came the season the Egyptians called Peret, which means coming forth. It ran through our winter months. The days were mild and sunny, and the nights could be surprisingly cold. Farmers sometimes wrapped themselves in woollen or heavy linen cloaks in the early morning. In the fields, the wheat and barley grew tall and green. Flax, used to make linen, came into blue flower. Vegetables such as onions, lentils, cucumbers and lettuce grew in small irrigated gardens. Water was lifted from canals with a simple device called a shaduf, a long pole with a bucket on one end and a weight on the other. For a few months, the valley was at its most beautiful. A bright green ribbon ran through the desert, from the far south all the way to the sea.
===
IMG: scribes-counting-grain-granary | scribes with papyrus rolls counting sacks of grain at a granary
HEAD: In ancient Egypt, taxes were paid not in money, but in grain.
BODY: There were no coins in Tutankhamun's Egypt. Instead, value was measured in grain, metal and goods. When the harvest came in, officials and scribes went out to the villages to measure the fields and count the crops. Each farmer had to hand over a share to the state or to the temple that owned the land. The grain was carried to great granaries, mud-brick buildings with tall domed silos, where it was stored for times of need. Scribes sat beside the doors with their palettes and papyrus rolls, writing down every sack. Being a scribe was a respected job, and boys who learned to read and write had a good chance of a comfortable life. One old Egyptian text tells young students to be scribes, because a scribe does not have to carry heavy loads or dig in the fields. It may sound a little proud. But it also shows how much the kingdom depended on careful counting.
===
IMG: young-king-walking-stick-garden | a young pharaoh walking slowly with a carved walking stick through a palace garden
HEAD: More than one hundred walking sticks and staffs were found in Tutankhamun's tomb.
BODY: Walking sticks were common among important men in ancient Egypt. They were a sign of rank and dignity, a little like a gentleman's cane in later times. Yet the number found with Tutankhamun is unusual. There were around one hundred and thirty sticks and staffs, some plain, some carved with figures, some covered in gold. A few seem to have been well used. When scientists studied the king's mummy using scans, some of them noticed signs of a problem in his left foot, which may have made walking painful. Others are not so sure, and the debate continues. Some paintings and carvings show the king seated while doing things that kings were usually shown doing standing up, like shooting arrows. Perhaps he needed a stick for support. Perhaps he simply liked them. We cannot ask him. Yet it is easy to picture a young man walking slowly through a quiet garden, leaning on a favourite stick.
===
IMG: palace-room-lamps-night-quiet | a quiet palace room at night with a bed with lion legs and a priest in prayer
HEAD: Nobody knows for certain how Tutankhamun died.
BODY: Tutankhamun died young, at about eighteen or nineteen years old, after reigning for around nine years. There is no written record that explains what happened. So, over the past century, people have suggested many possible causes. When his mummy was first examined, a hole in the skull led some to imagine a blow to the head. Later scans showed that this damage probably happened during mummification or when the mummy was studied in the 1920s. A break in his left leg, just above the knee, may have happened shortly before his death, perhaps in a fall or an accident. DNA tests found signs of malaria. Each of these, alone or together, could have been serious in a time without modern medicine. Yet none of them gives a final answer. Perhaps, in a quiet palace room, by the light of small oil lamps, doctors and priests did everything they knew how to do. And then the young king was gone.
===
TOPIC: Seventy days
BRIDGE: The palace fell silent. Now began the long, careful preparations for a royal burial.
===
IMG: mourning-women-palace-courtyard | women of the royal household in plain linen grieving in a palace courtyard
HEAD: When an Egyptian king died, the official mourning lasted around seventy days.
BODY: The death of a king was a moment of great uncertainty. The pharaoh was the one who kept order in the world, who held back chaos and pleased the gods. Now that order had to be passed on. Within the palace, people mourned openly. In paintings of funerals, women are shown with their hair loose and dishevelled, raising their arms and throwing dust over their heads as a sign of grief. They wore plain clothing, without jewellery. Men might stop shaving for a while. Meanwhile, the long process of preparing the king's body began. It usually took around seventy days, from death to burial. During this time, the new ruler had to be confirmed, the tomb made ready and the grave goods gathered. For Ankhesenamun, the young widow, these must have been strange and lonely weeks. She had lost her husband, her children and, in a way, her place in the world.
===
IMG: dusk-thebes-western-cliffs | the western cliffs above the Valley of the Kings at dusk with lamp-lit huts below
HEAD: Tutankhamun's tomb is surprisingly small for a king, and it may have been meant for someone else.
BODY: Egyptian kings usually began building their tombs early in their reigns. These royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings were long corridors, cut deep into the rock, with many rooms and painted walls. But Tutankhamun died young and unexpectedly. If a grand tomb had been started for him, it was not finished in time. So a much smaller tomb was used instead. It has only four little rooms and a short staircase. Many experts believe it was first cut for a high official, perhaps even for Ay, and then quickly adapted for the king. In the end, a larger tomb in the western part of the valley, which some think was begun for Tutankhamun, became the tomb of Ay himself. It is a curious exchange. The young king, whose name would one day be known all over the world, was buried in one of the smallest royal tombs in the whole valley.
===
IMG: embalmers-tent-west-bank | a tent of reed and linen on the west bank where priests prepare for embalming
HEAD: Egyptian embalmers began their work in a tent on the desert edge, called the tent of purification.
BODY: Making a mummy was a sacred task, carried out by specially trained priests. The work began on the west bank, near the desert, in a light structure of reed and linen sometimes called the tent of purification. There, the body was washed with water from the Nile and with natron, a natural salt, to cleanse it. Then it was taken to a workshop for the main part of the process. The embalmers removed the internal organs, which would otherwise decay. These were dried and stored in special containers, called canopic jars. In Tutankhamun's case, they were placed in four small golden coffins, each one a miniature copy of his own coffin. The heart was usually left in the body, because the Egyptians believed it was the seat of thought and memory. One priest wore a mask of Anubis, the jackal-headed god of embalming. It was a slow, careful and quiet process, full of prayers.
===
IMG: natron-salt-linen-rolls | rolls of white linen bandages, jars of oil and baskets of natron salt in a workshop
HEAD: More than one hundred and forty precious objects were hidden inside Tutankhamun's linen wrappings.
BODY: After the body had been cleaned, it was covered with natron salt and left to dry for around forty days. The natron drew out the moisture and stopped decay. Then the body was cleaned again, rubbed with oils and resins, and wrapped in long strips of fine linen. This could take many days, with prayers spoken as each layer was added. Between the layers, the embalmers placed amulets and jewels to protect the king on his journey. When Tutankhamun's mummy was unwrapped in 1925, archaeologists found more than one hundred and forty objects tucked inside: golden collars, rings, bracelets, amulets in the shape of gods and sacred symbols, and two daggers, one of them made of iron from a meteorite. Each item had a place and a purpose. Some protected the throat, some the heart, some the hands. Together, they formed a kind of invisible armour, made of gold, stone and magic, wrapped quietly around a young man.
===
IMG: goldsmiths-workshop-mask-making | goldsmiths hammering sheets of gold in a royal workshop with a coffin lid being polished
HEAD: Tutankhamun's innermost coffin is made of solid gold and weighs about as much as a grown man.
BODY: The king was laid to rest inside three coffins, one inside the other, like a set of nesting dolls. The two outer coffins are made of wood, covered in gold. The innermost one is made of solid gold, about two and a half to three and a half millimetres thick. It weighs around one hundred and ten kilograms. Over the king's head and shoulders lay the famous golden mask, made from two sheets of gold joined together, and weighing about ten kilograms. It is inlaid with blue glass and stones, including lapis lazuli, which came all the way from the mountains of Afghanistan. Making these objects took the work of skilled goldsmiths, who hammered, shaped, polished and inlaid the metal with simple tools and great patience. Gold was precious to the Egyptians for a special reason. It never tarnishes. They called it the flesh of the gods. To be covered in gold was to share, in a small way, in their eternal life.
===
IMG: valley-of-kings-workers-small-tomb | workers carrying baskets of stone chips up a short staircase in a desert valley
HEAD: Only one room in Tutankhamun's tomb has painted walls.
BODY: Most royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings are richly decorated. Their long corridors are covered with scenes and texts from sacred books, guiding the king through the twelve hours of the night. Tutankhamun's tomb is different. The staircase, the entrance corridor and three of the four rooms have plain, rough walls. Only the burial chamber, where the king's coffins were placed, was painted. Its walls show the funeral procession, the king's successor performing a ritual, the king meeting the gods, and twelve baboons that stand for the twelve hours of the night. The painting seems to have been done quickly. Small spots of mould appeared on the walls not long after the tomb was closed, which suggests the plaster was still damp. Everything points to a rushed burial. Yet within those few months, the craftsmen still managed to make one small room glow with golden-yellow walls, gods and quiet promises of a new dawn.
===
IMG: storeroom-chests-chariots-packed | servants carefully packing chests, chairs, beds and a chariot in a royal storeroom
HEAD: Some of the treasures in Tutankhamun's tomb were originally made for other members of his family.
BODY: When archaeologists studied the objects more closely, they noticed something unexpected. On a few items, the names had been changed. Under Tutankhamun's name, faint traces of another name could sometimes be seen. Some of the small golden coffins for his organs, for example, seem first to have been made for an earlier ruler, possibly a mysterious king or queen who reigned briefly before him. Other objects, such as boxes and pieces of jewellery, seem to have come from the family's stores. Because the king's death was sudden, his officials had to gather everything needed for a royal burial in only a few months. So they took what was available, changed the names where necessary, and packed it all into the tomb. More than five thousand objects went in, from golden shrines to boxes of food. To us, it looks like unimaginable wealth. Yet for a pharaoh, this may have been a rather modest, hurried burial.
===
IMG: cornflower-wreath-palace-window | a young queen weaving a small wreath of blue cornflowers by a palace window
HEAD: The flowers buried with Tutankhamun tell us that his funeral took place in the spring.
BODY: Many flowers and plants were found in the tomb. There were wreaths, collars and bouquets made of leaves, berries, petals and fruits. Botanists who studied them noticed something useful. Among them were blue cornflowers and the yellow fruits of the mandrake plant. In Egypt, these ripen in March and April. So the funeral most probably took place in the early spring. That fits well with around seventy days of embalming after a death in winter. The flowers had been carefully picked, sorted and threaded together by hand. Some of them were woven into collars that were worn by mourners at the funeral feast. Others were placed on the coffins. Pollen and plant remains like these are fragile, yet in the dry darkness of the tomb, they survived for more than three thousand years. It is a small and gentle detail. Someone, one spring morning, went out to gather flowers for a young king.
