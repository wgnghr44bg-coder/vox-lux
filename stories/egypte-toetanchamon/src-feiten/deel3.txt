HEAD: An Egyptian funeral began with a journey across the river, from the land of the living to the land of the dead.
BODY: The cities and palaces of Thebes lay mostly on the east bank of the Nile, where the sun rises. The tombs lay on the west bank, where it sets. So a funeral began with a crossing. The coffin was carried down to the water and placed on a boat, often decorated with flowers and linen. Priests, family members and mourners followed in other boats. The crossing was short, but it carried deep meaning. It echoed the journey of the sun, and the journey every soul would make from this life into the next. Scenes in Egyptian tombs show these funeral boats in great detail, with oarsmen rowing steadily and mourners kneeling on the decks. Herons and egrets rose from the reeds as the boats passed. On the far side, the golden cliffs of the west bank waited in the morning light. For the young king, this was the last time he would cross the river that had shaped his whole kingdom.

HEAD: Some Egyptian families hired professional mourners to weep at their funerals.
BODY: In ancient Egypt, a funeral was not meant to be quiet. Grief was shown openly, with loud cries, raised arms and tears. Women from the family led the mourning. They walked beside the coffin, wailing, beating their chests and throwing dust over their heads. In tomb paintings, they are often shown in long dresses of pale blue or grey linen, with their hair loose and their arms raised high. Some of the paintings even include the words of their songs. They call out to the dead person, asking where they are going, and telling them how much they will be missed. To us, paid mourners may sound strange. Yet for the Egyptians, a loud and crowded funeral was a sign of love and respect. The louder the mourning, the more the dead person had been valued by those left behind.

BODY: The Valley of the Kings lies in the desert hills on the west bank at Thebes. It is a dry, narrow valley, with steep golden walls of limestone. It protects towns and villages from floods that are too high, and from years when the water is too low. But it also means that the river no longer spreads across the fields each summer, and no longer leaves behind its fresh layer of black mud. Farmers now use fertiliser instead. Yet some things have stayed the same. Small wooden sailing boats called feluccas still drift on the river, their white sails catching the evening breeze, much like the boats painted in ancient tombs. Date palms still line the banks. And the sun still sets behind the western hills, exactly as it did for the young king.

HEAD: The ancient Egyptians believed that as long as your name was spoken, you would live forever.
BODY: For the Egyptians, a person's name was part of their soul. If people remembered your name and said it aloud, you would go on living in the afterlife. If your name was forgotten or erased, you might fade away completely. That is why tombs were covered with names and prayers, and why later kings chiselled out the names of rulers they wanted to forget. For many centuries, Tutankhamun was one of those forgotten kings. His name was left off the lists, and his monuments were given to others. Then, in 1922, a staircase was found beneath some old stone huts. Today, Tutankhamun's name is known all over the world. It is spoken in schools, in museums and in quiet rooms at night, perhaps more than the name of any other pharaoh. If the Egyptians were right, then the young king has been given back what he was once denied. Somewhere beyond the dark cliffs, under all these stars, his name is still being spoken.
