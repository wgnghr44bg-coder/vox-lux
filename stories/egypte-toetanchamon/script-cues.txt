{img:nile-dawn-reeds-boat}
Tonight we are going more than three thousand three hundred years back, to the banks of the River Nile, in the land of Egypt.

[long-pause]

It is early morning, and a thin mist lies over the water.

[pause]

On the eastern bank, the first light is coming up behind the low hills, turning the sky from grey to pale gold. Along the edge of the river, tall papyrus reeds stand perfectly still, and a little boat made of bundled reeds drifts slowly past, with a fisherman standing in it, holding his net.

[pause]

If calm journeys through history help you rest, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel. Now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

The river is wide here, and brown, and very slow.

[pause]

It comes from somewhere far to the south, from lands that no Egyptian has ever seen, and it flows north through the desert for hundreds and hundreds of miles, until at last it spreads out into a great green delta and runs into the sea.

[pause]

On either side of the river there is a narrow strip of green: fields of wheat and barley and flax, date palms, gardens, little villages of mud-brick houses. And beyond the green, quite suddenly, as if someone had drawn a line with a ruler, the desert begins.

[long-pause]

{img:green-strip-desert-edge}
The Egyptians call their country Kemet, the black land, because of the dark, rich soil along the river.

[pause]

The desert they call Deshret, the red land. It is the land of sand and stone and silence, of the setting sun, of the dead.

[pause]

Every Egyptian knows the difference between the two. The black land is where people live, and plant, and raise their children. The red land is where the sun goes down each evening, and where, in the end, they will all be buried, in tombs cut into the rock, looking back toward the river.

[long-pause]

Everything in Egypt depends on the Nile.

[pause]

Almost no rain falls here. Some years, there is no rain at all. Without the river, the whole country would be desert, as empty as the sand hills on either side.

[pause]

But every year, in the heat of the summer, something wonderful happens. Far away in the south, beyond the edge of the known world, heavy rains fall on distant mountains. And a few weeks later, the river begins to rise.

[long-pause]

{img:nile-flood-fields-water}
The Egyptians call this time of year Akhet, the season of the flood.

[pause]

Slowly, day after day, the river swells and spreads out over its banks, until the fields on both sides are covered with shallow brown water. The villages stand up out of the flood on their little mounds, like islands, and people go from one to another by boat.

[pause]

For two or three months, the land lies under the water. Then, slowly, the river sinks back into its bed, and it leaves behind a fresh layer of dark, rich mud, full of everything the crops will need.

[long-pause]

After the flood comes Peret, the season of growing, when the farmers plant their seeds in the soft wet earth, and the fields turn green.

[pause]

And after that comes Shemu, the season of harvest, when the wheat and barley turn golden under the sun, and everyone goes out into the fields with their sickles to cut it.

[pause]

Then the heat comes back, and the river begins to rise again, and the whole circle starts over.

[pause]

The Egyptians have lived like this for more than two thousand years already. To them, it seems that it has always been so, and always will be.

[long-pause]

{img:thebes-river-view-temples}
About halfway along the river, in the south of the country, there is a great city.

[pause]

The Egyptians call it Waset. Much later, travellers from Greece will call it Thebes, and that is the name we will use.

[pause]

From the river, Thebes is a beautiful sight. On the eastern bank, the city spreads out along the water: a mass of white and pale brown houses, gardens and palm trees, and above them, the great stone gateways and walls of the temples, painted in bright colours and hung with long flags on tall wooden poles.

[long-pause]

On the western bank, across the water, the land is different.

[pause]

There, beyond a strip of green fields, the cliffs of the desert rise up steep and golden, and along the foot of them stand the great mortuary temples of the kings, white and silent, each one built to keep the memory of a pharaoh alive forever.

[pause]

And behind those cliffs, hidden in a dry and winding valley where no one is allowed to go without permission, the kings of Egypt are buried.

[long-pause]

{img:western-cliffs-golden-light}
The year, as we would count it, is around 1323 before Christ.

[pause]

The Egyptians do not count the years like this. They count them by the reigns of their kings: the first year of a king, the second year, the third, and so on, starting again whenever a new king takes the throne.

[pause]

And on the throne of Egypt now, at the time when our story begins, sits a young man of about eighteen years old.

[pause]

His name is Tutankhamun.

[long-pause]

He has been king for nearly ten years, since he was a small boy. He lives in palaces at Memphis, in the north, and here at Thebes, in the south. He has a wife, a great court of officials and priests and servants, and an army.

[pause]

The people of Egypt call him the Lord of the Two Lands, and many of them believe that he is a living god, the son of the sun.

[pause]

But for most of the people who live along the Nile, the king is very far away. They will never see his face. For them, life is about other things: the river, the fields, the weather, their families, their work.

[pause]

So before we come to the king, let us spend a little time with them.

[long-pause]

{img:farm-village-mud-houses-palms}
In a small village on the western bank, not far from Thebes, a farming family is waking up.

[pause]

Their house is built of mud bricks, made from the river mud mixed with chopped straw and dried in the sun. It has a few small rooms, with thick walls and tiny windows high up near the ceiling, to keep out the heat and the dust.

[pause]

The floor is beaten earth. There is a low bench along one wall, a few reed mats, some clay jars and baskets, and a little shrine in a niche in the wall, with a small figure of a household god.

[long-pause]

On the hottest nights, the family sleeps up on the flat roof, where there is a little breeze.

[pause]

They climb up a ladder or a mud-brick staircase in the evening, unroll their mats, and lie under the stars, listening to the frogs in the canals and the dogs barking in the next village. In the morning, the sun wakes them early, and they come down again to begin the day.

[long-pause]

{img:woman-grinding-grain-courtyard}
The first work of the day is making bread.

[pause]

In the little courtyard behind the house, the mother kneels on the ground in front of a flat stone, and grinds the grain into flour, pushing a smaller stone back and forth over it, again and again. It is slow, hard work, and it takes a long time to grind enough flour for the whole family.

[pause]

Then she mixes the flour with water and a little of yesterday's dough, shapes it into round loaves, and bakes them in a small clay oven, shaped like a beehive, with a fire burning inside.

[pause]

Egyptian bread is good and filling, but it is a little gritty, because tiny pieces of the grinding stone always end up in the flour.

[long-pause]

Alongside the bread, there is beer.

[pause]

Beer in Egypt is thick and mild, more like a kind of porridge than the beer of later times. It is made from barley bread, soaked in water and left to ferment in big clay jars. People drink it through a reed straw, to filter out the husks.

[pause]

Everyone drinks it, the adults and the children, morning, noon and night. It is safer than the river water, and it is food as much as drink.

[long-pause]

{img:farmers-ploughing-oxen-field}
After breakfast, the father and the older sons go out to the fields.

[pause]

It is the season of growing now, and the fields have just come out from under the flood. The earth is still soft and dark and damp.

[pause]

The father walks behind a simple wooden plough, pulled by a pair of oxen, cutting shallow lines in the mud. Behind him, his son scatters seed from a basket, throwing it in wide, even sweeps. And behind the son comes a little flock of sheep or goats, driven across the field, to tread the seeds down into the soft earth.

[long-pause]

The fields belong, most often, not to the farmer himself, but to a temple, or to a great official, or to the king.

[pause]

The farmer works the land and keeps part of the harvest for his family, and the rest goes to the owner. Each year, the scribes come out to measure the fields with knotted ropes, and to work out how much grain must be paid.

[pause]

It is not an easy life. But in a good year, when the flood has been just the right height, there is enough for everyone.

[long-pause]

{img:shaduf-irrigation-canal}
Along the edge of the fields, there are small canals that carry water from the river.

[pause]

To lift the water up from the canal into the higher fields and gardens, the farmers use a shaduf. It is a long wooden pole, balanced on a frame, with a bucket hanging from one end and a heavy lump of mud tied to the other as a weight.

[pause]

A man pulls the bucket down into the canal, lets it fill, and then lets the weight swing it gently back up, so that he can tip the water out into a little channel. Up and down, up and down, all day long, with a slow, creaking rhythm.

[long-pause]

Around the house, there is a small garden.

[pause]

The family grows onions and leeks, lentils and beans, cucumbers and lettuce and melons. There are a few date palms and a fig tree, and perhaps a vine, trained over a wooden frame to give shade.

[pause]

The children are sent out to scare the birds away from the crops, and to fetch water, and to look after the goats. The younger ones run about the courtyard with the dogs and the ducks in the warm sunshine, with their hair shaved except for a single long lock on one side of the head.

[long-pause]

{img:harvest-golden-wheat-sickles}
A few months later, in the season of harvest, the whole village goes out into the fields together.

[pause]

The men walk along the rows of golden wheat and barley, cutting the stalks with sickles of wood set with little sharp teeth of flint. Women and children follow behind them, gathering up the fallen ears into baskets. Someone plays a flute. The workers sing as they go, old songs about the harvest, about the oxen, about the heat of the sun.

[pause]

At midday, they sit in the shade of a tree at the edge of the field and eat bread and onions and drink beer from a shared jar.

[long-pause]

The grain is carried to a threshing floor, a flat, hard circle of beaten earth, and spread out over it.

[pause]

Then the oxen are driven round and round over the grain, treading it with their hooves, to separate the seeds from the stalks. Afterwards, the farmers toss the grain up into the air with wooden scoops, and the wind blows the light chaff away, while the heavy seeds fall back down to the ground.

[pause]

Then the scribes come again, to count the sacks, and write it all down on their papyrus rolls.

[long-pause]

In the evening, when the work is done, the family comes home tired and dusty.

[pause]

The mother lights the fire. There is bread again, and perhaps some lentil stew, onions and cucumber, and on special days, a little fish from the river, or a duck. Meat is a luxury, eaten mostly at festivals.

[pause]

They sit together in the courtyard as the light fades, and the swallows dart over the roof, and across the river, the far-off walls of the temples of Thebes glow pink and gold in the last of the sun.

[pause]

Then the stars come out, and the family climbs up onto the roof to sleep.

[long-pause]

{img:thebes-street-market-morning}
Across the river, in the city of Thebes, the morning is busy.

[pause]

The streets are narrow and winding, and the houses stand close together, two or three storeys high, built of mud brick and painted white or pale yellow. Here and there, a wooden door stands open onto a cool, shady courtyard, and the smell of baking bread and cooking onions drifts out into the lane.

[long-pause]

Down by the river, the harbour is crowded with boats.

[pause]

There are small fishing boats and ferry boats, carrying people back and forth across the water. There are big cargo boats with tall square sails, loaded with grain and stone and jars of wine and oil, coming up the river from the north, or down from the south with ivory and ebony and gold.

[pause]

{img:nile-harbour-sailing-boats}
On the Nile, travelling is easy. To go north, a boat simply drifts with the current. To go south, it puts up its sail, because the wind in Egypt almost always blows from the north. In the Egyptian way of writing, the sign for travelling north is a boat with its sail down, and the sign for travelling south is a boat with its sail up.

[long-pause]

Near the harbour, there is a market.

[pause]

There are no coins in Egypt. People trade one thing for another. A woman sits on the ground beside a basket of fresh fish, and a man stops to offer her a pair of sandals for them. Another woman sells bread and cakes, another sells onions and garlic and herbs, and an old man sells little clay figures of the gods.

[pause]

To work out whether a trade is fair, people think of the value of things in weights of copper or silver, or in sacks of grain. A goat might be worth so much. A linen tunic might be worth so much. And then the bargaining begins, slowly and good-naturedly, with a great deal of talk.

[long-pause]

{img:craftsmen-workshop-jewellery}
In the streets behind the market, there are workshops of every kind.

[pause]

Potters sit at their wheels, shaping jars and bowls from the river clay. Carpenters work with bronze saws and chisels, making chairs and boxes and beds. Weavers sit at their looms, making the fine white linen that almost everyone in Egypt wears.

[pause]

In the workshops of the jewellers, men bend over little tables, setting tiny pieces of blue and red and green stone into gold, making collars and bracelets and rings for the rich.

[long-pause]

Most people in Thebes dress simply.

[pause]

The men wear a short linen kilt, wrapped around the waist and tied in front. The women wear a long, straight linen dress, sometimes with a shawl over the shoulders. When it is hot, which is almost always, people wear very little, and go barefoot or in sandals made of woven papyrus.

[pause]

The rich wear finer linen, so thin that it is almost see-through, pleated in delicate folds. They wear wigs of real hair, heavy collars of beads, and dark lines of kohl around their eyes, which look beautiful and also protect the eyes from the bright sun and the flies.

[long-pause]

{img:karnak-temple-gateway-flags}
Above the houses of the city rises the greatest building in all of Egypt: the temple of Karnak.

[pause]

It is not really one temple, but a whole city of temples, built and rebuilt by one king after another for hundreds of years. There are great gateways, called pylons, as tall as cliffs, with flags fluttering on cedar poles in front of them. There are courtyards and halls, obelisks of pink granite topped with gold, sacred lakes, gardens, storerooms and workshops.

[pause]

And in the heart of it, in a small dark room deep inside, lives the god Amun, the king of all the gods, in the form of a statue of gold.

[long-pause]

Ordinary people are not allowed inside the temple.

[pause]

Only the priests can go in, and only the highest of them can enter the innermost room where the god lives. Every morning, at sunrise, they open the doors of his shrine, wash the statue, dress it in fresh linen, anoint it with perfumed oil, and set before it a meal of bread and meat and fruit and beer.

[pause]

{img:priests-temple-hall-columns}
Then they close the doors again, and the god rests, in the cool and the dark, until the evening.

[long-pause]

The temple is very rich.

[pause]

It owns fields and farms all over Egypt, herds of cattle, boats, workshops and thousands of workers. Its storerooms are full of grain and wine and oil and linen. Its priests are some of the most powerful men in the land.

[pause]

For the people of Thebes, the temple is the centre of the world. They cannot go inside, but they can stand at the outer gates and pray, and leave little offerings: a loaf of bread, a bunch of flowers, a small clay figure. And on festival days, the god comes out, and everyone can see him.

[long-pause]

As the afternoon goes on, the heat grows heavy over the city.

[pause]

The streets grow quieter. The shopkeepers sit in the shade of their awnings. The donkeys stand with their heads down, half asleep. The dogs lie flat in the dust of the doorways.

[pause]

In the courtyards of the houses, families sit under the palm trees or on the roofs under little shelters of reeds, drinking water from porous clay jars that keep it cool, and waiting for the sun to go down.

[long-pause]

In the evening, Thebes comes back to life.

[pause]

A cool breeze blows in from the north, over the river. The lamps are lit, small clay bowls filled with oil, with a linen wick floating in each one. The smell of cooking fills the lanes. Friends visit each other, and sit talking in the courtyards. Somewhere, someone is playing a harp, and someone else is singing.

[pause]

Down at the harbour, the boats rock gently on the dark water, and on the far bank, the western cliffs stand black against a sky full of stars.

[pause]

And there, hidden at the foot of those cliffs, is a little village where the people who make the tombs of the kings live.

[long-pause]

{img:deir-el-medina-village-valley}
In a narrow, rocky valley on the western bank, between the desert hills, there is a small walled village.

[pause]

It has about seventy houses, built close together on either side of a single long street, with a few more scattered outside the walls. The houses are made of mud brick on stone foundations, whitewashed, with little doors painted red. Around the village, there are no fields at all, only sand and stones and the steep golden hills.

[pause]

Today, we call this place Deir el-Medina. The people who live there call it Set Maat, the Place of Truth.

[long-pause]

This is the village of the tomb-makers.

[pause]

The men who live here are stonecutters and plasterers, draughtsmen, sculptors and painters. Their work, their whole life's work, is to cut and decorate the tombs of the kings and queens of Egypt, in the hidden valleys behind the cliffs.

[pause]

It is skilled work, and it is respected. The tomb-makers are paid well, in grain and fish and vegetables and oil, delivered to the village by the government. They can read and write, many of them. They are proud of what they do.

[long-pause]

A house in the village has three or four rooms, one behind the other.

[pause]

At the front, there is a room with a raised platform of mud brick, where the family can sit, or where an altar to the household gods may stand. Then there is a main room, with a wooden column holding up the roof, and a little niche in the wall for the family's ancestors.

[pause]

Behind that are smaller rooms for sleeping and storing things, and at the very back, open to the sky, there is the kitchen, with an oven and a grinding stone, and a staircase leading up to the roof.

[long-pause]

The village has no water of its own.

[pause]

Every day, donkeys come up the valley from the river, carrying water in big jars, and the water carriers fill the great cistern near the village gate. The women go there with their jugs, and stand and talk while they wait their turn.

[pause]

The laundry is taken down to the river by washermen, and fish and vegetables and firewood are brought up by porters. The tomb-makers are so important that other people are employed just to look after them.

[long-pause]

{img:workers-walking-path-cliffs-dawn}
The work itself goes on in a ten-day week.

[pause]

On the first morning of the week, before dawn, the men of the work crew leave the village together and walk up the steep path over the hills, toward the Valley of the Kings. It is a hard climb, up rocky slopes and along narrow ledges, but from the top of the ridge, there is a wonderful view: the whole green valley of the Nile below them, and the city of Thebes on the far bank, small and white in the morning sun.

[pause]

They spend the working days near the tomb, sleeping in little stone huts on the hillside, and come home to the village for their days off.

[long-pause]

The work crew is divided into two groups, the right side and the left side, like the crew of a ship.

[pause]

Each side has a foreman, and there is a scribe who keeps a careful record of everything: who came to work and who did not, how much was cut, how many lamp wicks were used, how many copper chisels were handed out and given back.

[pause]

When a man stays home, the scribe writes down the reason. And because these records were kept on pieces of broken pottery and flakes of white limestone, many of them have survived until today.

[pause]

{img:ostracon-scribe-limestone-flake}
We can read them still. Someone was ill. Someone had to brew beer. Someone had been stung by a scorpion. Someone's daughter was having a baby. Someone's wife had argued with him, and he did not feel like working.

[long-pause]

Inside the tomb, the work goes on by lamplight.

[pause]

The stonecutters go first, cutting the corridors and rooms out of the solid rock with copper chisels and wooden mallets. The chips of stone are carried out in baskets. Behind them come the plasterers, smoothing the walls.

[pause]

{img:painters-tomb-wall-lamplight}
Then come the draughtsmen, who draw the outlines of the pictures in red paint, and the master draughtsman, who corrects them in black. Then come the sculptors, carving the figures in low relief, and last of all the painters, filling them in with bright colours: blue and green, red and yellow, black and white.

[pause]

The lamps are little bowls of oil with wicks of twisted linen, and some say the workers mixed a little salt into the oil, so that the lamps would not smoke and blacken the walls.

[long-pause]

On their days off, the tomb-makers work for themselves.

[pause]

They make coffins and statues and furniture to sell to rich people in Thebes. They decorate their own tombs, which they cut into the hillside above the village, and some of these are as beautiful as the tombs of nobles.

[pause]

They also have time to rest, and to enjoy themselves. They visit their neighbours, and drink beer, and play board games. There are festivals for the gods, when the whole village gathers to eat and drink and sing together.

[long-pause]

{img:village-evening-families-roofs}
Many of the people in the village can write, and they write all sorts of things.

[pause]

Letters to friends and relatives. Lists of things to buy. Little love poems. Prayers. Accounts of a dream. Complaints about a neighbour's donkey. Drawings on bits of limestone, made just for fun: a cat herding geese, a mouse dressed as a lady, a king driving his chariot, a quick sketch of a friend.

[pause]

Through these little scraps, the people of the Place of Truth seem very close to us, even after all these centuries.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, the village is quiet.

[pause]

The heat goes out of the stones, and the hills turn red, then purple, then grey. The smoke from the cooking fires rises straight up into the still air. Children play in the street, and old men sit in the doorways, talking. On the roofs, families settle down to sleep under the stars.

[pause]

Somewhere over the hills, in the Valley of the Kings, the tomb of the young king Tutankhamun is being cut and planned. It is meant to be large and splendid, worthy of a king.

[pause]

But no one in the village knows that it will not be finished in time.

[long-pause]

{img:scribe-seated-papyrus-palette}
Of all the jobs in Egypt, there is one that every parent hopes their son will have.

[pause]

It is the job of a scribe.

[pause]

A scribe sits cross-legged in the shade, with a roll of papyrus stretched across his knees and a little wooden palette beside him, with two cakes of ink, one black and one red, and a few thin reed pens. He writes the letters of officials and the accounts of temples. He counts the grain and the cattle, measures the fields and records the taxes.

[pause]

He does not have to plough or dig or carry heavy loads. His hands stay soft and clean.

[long-pause]

There is an old piece of writing, copied out again and again by schoolboys, called the Satire of the Trades.

[pause]

In it, a father takes his son to school, and on the way, he describes all the other jobs in Egypt, and how terrible they are. The potter is covered in mud. The fisherman is afraid of crocodiles. The washerman smells of other people's laundry. The farmer is always tired, and the reed-cutter is bitten by mosquitoes.

[pause]

But the scribe, says the father, is his own master. Be a scribe, my son. There is nothing better.

[long-pause]

{img:school-boys-writing-boards}
So the boys go to school, most often at a temple or in the house of a scribe.

[pause]

They start young, perhaps at five or six. They sit together on mats on the floor, and copy out signs and sentences, over and over, on wooden boards covered with white plaster, which can be wiped clean and used again, or on flakes of limestone and pieces of broken pots.

[pause]

There are hundreds of different signs to learn. Little pictures of birds and snakes and feet and eyes and reeds and water, which stand for sounds and words and ideas. It takes years to learn them all.

[long-pause]

The teachers are strict.

[pause]

The boys learn by heart long passages of old wisdom, and recite them aloud together. When they are lazy or talk in class, they are punished. One old saying goes that a boy's ear is on his back: he listens when he is beaten.

[pause]

But there are rewards, too. A boy who learns well will have a good life, and a good position, and perhaps one day, if he is very clever and very lucky, he might even rise to become a great official at the court of the king.

[long-pause]

{img:girls-weaving-mother-courtyard}
Girls do not usually go to school.

[pause]

Most of them learn at home, from their mothers and grandmothers: how to grind grain and bake bread, how to brew beer, how to spin flax into thread and weave linen, how to look after the younger children and the animals.

[pause]

Some girls learn to read and write too, especially in the families of scribes and priests. And women in Egypt have more freedom than in many other lands of this time. They can own property, buy and sell land, make a will, and go to court.

[long-pause]

Egyptian children love to play.

[pause]

They have balls made of leather or linen, stuffed with straw or barley husks. They have spinning tops, and little wooden animals on wheels, pulled along by a string. There is a wooden cat, found in later times, whose jaw opens and shuts when you pull a cord.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-ball-courtyard}
They play games of leapfrog and tug-of-war, and a game in which two children sit on the ground and the others try to jump over them as they raise their hands higher and higher. They swim in the canals, and fly little birds made of clay, and make dolls out of wood and linen.

[long-pause]

The grown-ups play games too.

[pause]

The most popular is a board game called senet. It is played on a long board of thirty squares, three rows of ten, with little pieces shaped like cones and spools. Instead of dice, the players throw flat sticks, and count how many land with the white side up.

[pause]

People of every kind play senet, farmers in the evening on a board scratched into the ground, and kings on boards of ebony and ivory. Over time, it comes to have a deeper meaning, as if the game were a journey through the world beyond death.

[long-pause]

{img:cat-sleeping-house-niche}
In almost every Egyptian house, there is a cat.

[pause]

The Egyptians love their cats. They keep them to catch the mice and the snakes that come into the granaries, but they also simply like them, and let them sleep on the cushions and eat from the table. Cats appear in their paintings, sitting under chairs, playing with balls of thread, or out hunting birds in the marshes.

[pause]

There are dogs too, slim hunting dogs with curly tails and long legs, who wear collars and have names. One dog is called Brave One. Another is called Good Herdsman. Another is called simply North Wind.

[long-pause]

There are pets of other kinds as well: monkeys, which climb about in the trees of the gardens, and geese, which waddle about the courtyards, and in rich houses, beautiful birds in cages.

[pause]

The Egyptians are fond of animals, and they see the gods in them. The god Thoth, the god of writing and wisdom, appears as an ibis, or as a baboon. The goddess Bastet, the goddess of the home, appears as a cat. The god Horus, the protector of the king, appears as a falcon.

[pause]

In the marshes beside the river, the ibises stand on one leg among the papyrus in the evening, white against the green, and the people who pass by greet them quietly, as one might greet a priest.

[long-pause]

When the evening comes, the scribes put away their pens and their palettes.

[pause]

They rub out the day's lessons from the writing boards, roll up the papyrus, and walk home through the streets in the cooling air. The schoolboys run ahead, shouting, glad to be free.

[pause]

At home, there is bread and beer and lentils, and a cat waiting on the doorstep. And after supper, perhaps, a game of senet in the courtyard by the light of a little oil lamp, the sticks rattling softly on the board, as the stars come out one by one above the roof.

[long-pause]

{img:amarna-city-desert-river}
To understand the story of Tutankhamun, we must go back a little in time, to a strange and unsettled period, just before he was born.

[pause]

About twenty years earlier, a new king had come to the throne of Egypt. His name, at first, was Amenhotep, like his father's. But he was not like the kings who had come before him.

[long-pause]

This king had a new idea about the gods.

[pause]

For as long as anyone could remember, the Egyptians had worshipped many gods: Amun, the hidden king of the gods, and Ra the sun, Osiris the lord of the dead, Isis his wife, Horus, Hathor, Thoth, Ptah, and hundreds more, each with their own temples, their own priests, their own festivals.

[pause]

But the new king believed that there was one god who mattered above all the others. That god was the Aten, the shining disk of the sun itself, whose rays gave life to everything on earth.

[long-pause]

{img:sun-disk-rays-relief}
So the king changed his name.

[pause]

He stopped calling himself Amenhotep, which means Amun is content, and called himself Akhenaten, which means something like Useful to the Aten, or the Living Spirit of the Aten.

[pause]

He closed many of the old temples, and stopped the great festivals of the other gods. And in time, he sent his workers all over Egypt to cut the name of Amun out of the walls of the temples and the tombs, so that the old king of the gods would be forgotten.

[long-pause]

Then he did something even more surprising.

[pause]

He left Thebes, the great old city of Amun, and built a brand new capital in the middle of Egypt, on an empty stretch of desert beside the river, where no one had lived before.

[pause]

He called it Akhetaten, the Horizon of the Aten. Today, we know it as Amarna.

[pause]

{img:amarna-palace-garden-pool}
The new city was built very quickly, of mud brick and white stone. It had long straight streets, grand palaces with painted floors and gardens and pools, houses for the officials and workers, and great temples to the Aten.

[pause]

But the temples of the Aten were not like the old temples. They had no roof, and no dark room at the centre. They were open to the sky, so that the sun could shine down on hundreds of altars piled high with offerings of bread and flowers and fruit.

[long-pause]

At Akhenaten's side was his queen, Nefertiti.

[pause]

Her name means the beautiful one has come, and she was famous in her own time, as she is in ours, for her beauty. She appears again and again in the art of Amarna, standing beside her husband, making offerings with him, riding with him in his chariot.

[pause]

{img:royal-family-sun-rays-art}
The royal couple had six daughters, and the artists of Amarna showed them in a new, gentle way: the king and queen sitting together under the rays of the sun, holding their little girls on their laps, kissing them, playing with them. No Egyptian king had ever been shown like this before.

[long-pause]

Life in the new city was bright and busy.

[pause]

Every morning, the king and queen rode out in their golden chariot along the great royal road, from their palace to the temple, while the people of the city lined the way to watch them pass. Soldiers ran alongside. Priests waited at the temple gates with flowers and incense.

[pause]

The artists of Amarna painted and carved in a new, free style. They showed birds flying up out of the marshes, calves leaping in the fields, ducks among the papyrus, and the king himself with a long face and a soft, rounded body, quite unlike the stiff, perfect kings of the old pictures. One of the sculptors, a man named Thutmose, had his workshop in the city, and it was there, more than three thousand years later, that a famous painted head of Queen Nefertiti would be found, still lying where he had left it.

[long-pause]

Among the daughters was a little girl called Ankhesenpaaten.

[pause]

She was the third daughter, born in the early years at Amarna. She grew up in the palaces of the new city, among the gardens and the pools and the painted halls, with her sisters, her nurses, and her pet birds and monkeys.

[pause]

One day, many years later, she would become queen of Egypt.

[long-pause]

{img:palace-nursery-baby-nurse}
And somewhere in the palace at Amarna, around the year 1341 before Christ, a little boy was born.

[pause]

He was a son of the king, Akhenaten. His mother was probably one of the king's other wives, a royal lady whose name we still do not know for certain.

[pause]

The boy was given the name Tutankhaten, which means the Living Image of the Aten.

[long-pause]

He was cared for by a nurse named Maia.

[pause]

We know about Maia because, a little under thirty years ago, her tomb was discovered at Saqqara, near the old city of Memphis. On its walls, she is shown seated, holding the young king on her lap, with his feet resting on a little stool. She was evidently very proud of him, and he, it seems, did not forget her.

[pause]

The little prince grew up in the sunny palaces of Amarna, among the gardens and the courtyards, playing with his sisters and his cousins, learning to read and write, to ride, to shoot with a bow.

[long-pause]

But outside the palace walls, Egypt was not happy.

[pause]

Many people missed the old gods. The great temples of Amun were empty, their priests sent away, their festivals forgotten. The new city in the desert had cost a great deal, and many old families had lost their power and their wealth.

[pause]

{img:amarna-workers-village-evening}
In the villages, ordinary people still kept little statues of the old gods in their houses, and prayed to them quietly at night, as their grandparents had done.

[long-pause]

Then, when the prince was still a small child, Akhenaten died.

[pause]

What happened next is one of the great puzzles of Egyptian history. For a short time, there seem to have been one or two other rulers, whose names and stories are still argued about by scholars today.

[pause]

But after a few years, only one member of the royal family was left who could become king.

[pause]

He was a little boy of about eight or nine years old. And his name was Tutankhaten.

[long-pause]

{img:boy-king-coronation-temple}
The little boy was crowned king of Egypt in a great ceremony, with priests and officials and soldiers gathered around him.

[pause]

He was dressed in fine white linen, with a golden collar around his neck. On his head, the priests placed the crowns of the Two Lands: the white crown of the south and the red crown of the north. In his small hands, he held the crook and the flail, the symbols of a king who is the shepherd and protector of his people.

[pause]

He was perhaps eight or nine years old.

[long-pause]

Of course, a child cannot rule a whole country by himself.

[pause]

Around the young king stood a group of powerful older men, who had served his family for many years, and who now made most of the decisions in his name.

[pause]

{img:ay-elderly-official-court}
The most important was Ay. He was an elderly official, perhaps in his fifties or sixties, who had been close to Akhenaten and Nefertiti at Amarna. He held the title God's Father, and he may even have been related to the royal family. He was calm, experienced and very clever, and the young king trusted him.

[long-pause]

The other was Horemheb.

[pause]

Horemheb was the commander of the army, a strong, ambitious man from a town in Middle Egypt, who had risen through the ranks by his own ability. He held the title Deputy of the King, and he spent much of his time in the north, at Memphis, managing the affairs of the country and its armies.

[pause]

Ay and Horemheb did not always agree. But for now, they worked together, around the young king, to steer Egypt through a difficult time.

[long-pause]

{img:treasurer-maya-scribe-office}
There was a third man, too, whom we know a good deal about.

[pause]

His name was Maya, and he was the treasurer of Egypt, in charge of the wealth of the whole country: the gold and silver, the grain and cattle, the taxes, and the great royal building works. He was a capable, loyal man, and he would serve the young king faithfully to the very end of his life.

[long-pause]

Within a few years, the new government made a great decision.

[pause]

The court would leave Amarna. The Aten would no longer be the only god. The old gods would come back.

[pause]

So the young king, too, changed his name. He stopped being Tutankhaten, the Living Image of the Aten, and became Tutankhamun, the Living Image of Amun. His young wife, the princess Ankhesenpaaten, became Ankhesenamun.

[long-pause]

{img:abandoned-city-sand-drifting}
The city of Akhetaten was slowly abandoned.

[pause]

The officials and their families packed up their belongings and moved away, back to Memphis and to Thebes. The workshops closed. The palaces stood empty. Little by little, the sand blew in through the open doorways and drifted against the walls, and the city in the desert, built so quickly with such hope, was left to the silence.

[long-pause]

At Karnak, the young king set up a tall stone slab, carved with a long inscription.

[pause]

We call it the Restoration Stela. It describes how, when the king came to the throne, the temples of the gods were in ruins, their shrines fallen down, their courtyards overgrown with weeds, as if they had never been. The gods had turned their backs on Egypt, it says, and when people prayed to them, they did not answer.

[pause]

But now, it goes on, the king has restored everything. He has made new statues of the gods, of gold and precious stones. He has given the temples back their lands and their priests, their singers and their dancers. And Egypt is happy again.

[long-pause]

All over the country, the workers set about repairing the damage.

[pause]

The name of Amun was carved back into the walls where it had been chiselled out. Statues were remade. Temples were cleaned and painted and opened once more. The great festivals came back, with their processions and music and feasting.

[pause]

And the people, it seems, were glad.

[long-pause]

The king lived mostly at Memphis, the old capital in the north, near the great pyramids, and came south to Thebes for the festivals.

[pause]

{img:memphis-palace-river-pyramids}
At Memphis, the palace stood among gardens beside the river, with the white walls of the city around it, and far off on the edge of the desert, the pyramids of Giza and Saqqara, which were already more than a thousand years old, stood pale and enormous against the evening sky.

[pause]

To the young king, they were as ancient as the ruins of Rome are to us.

[long-pause]

Tutankhamun grew up.

[pause]

He learned to read and write, and to perform the ceremonies of the temples. He learned the history of Egypt and the names of the old kings. He sat on his throne to receive foreign ambassadors, who came bringing gifts from Nubia and Syria, from the islands of the sea and the kingdoms of the north.

[pause]

He was not a big, strong boy. He was slender and of medium height, and he seems to have had some trouble with one of his feet, because among his belongings there were a great many walking sticks.

[pause]

But he liked to be outdoors, and he liked to ride.

[long-pause]

{img:young-king-chariot-desert-hunt}
Out on the edge of the desert, he went hunting, in a light chariot of wood and leather, pulled by two horses, with a driver beside him.

[pause]

On a fan that was buried with him, there is a picture of him in his chariot, chasing ostriches across the sand, his bow drawn back, his hunting dog running beside the wheels. On the other side of the fan, he is shown coming home, with his servants carrying the ostriches behind him, and the feathers of the fan itself, it says, came from the birds he caught that day.

[long-pause]

And so the years went by.

[pause]

The river rose and fell. The harvests came in. The temples were full again, and the priests sang in the morning, and the festivals came round in their seasons.

[pause]

In the Place of Truth, the tomb-makers worked on the king's tomb, and in the workshops of Thebes and Memphis, the craftsmen made beautiful things for his palaces. Nobody imagined that there was any need to hurry. The king was young, and he would surely reign for many, many years.

[long-pause]

{img:queen-ankhesenamun-palace-garden}
At the young king's side, all through these years, was his queen, Ankhesenamun.

[pause]

She was a little older than he was, perhaps by a year or two or three. They had known each other since they were small children, in the palace at Amarna. She was a daughter of Akhenaten and Nefertiti, and he was a son of Akhenaten, and so they were half brother and sister. Among the kings of Egypt, such marriages were not unusual.

[long-pause]

We do not know very much about what they felt for each other.

[pause]

But among the treasures buried with the king, there are many pictures of the two of them together, and they are some of the most tender pictures in all of Egyptian art.

[pause]

{img:golden-throne-queen-anointing}
On the back of his golden throne, the king sits relaxed in a cushioned chair, with one arm thrown over the back of it. The queen stands in front of him, in a long pleated dress, gently touching his shoulder with one hand. In her other hand, she holds a little cup of perfumed oil. Above them, the sun shines down, and its rays end in little hands, holding out the sign of life.

[long-pause]

On a small golden shrine, there are more scenes like this.

[pause]

The queen hands the king a bunch of flowers. She ties a collar around his neck. She kneels at his feet as he shoots at ducks with his bow in the marshes, and hands him another arrow. He pours water into her cupped hand.

[pause]

They seem like two young people who are fond of each other, and who spend their days together.

[long-pause]

{img:palace-hall-painted-floor}
The palace where they lived was a beautiful place.

[pause]

Its halls had tall wooden columns, painted to look like bundles of papyrus or palm trees. The floors were painted too, with pictures of pools full of fish and lotus flowers, and birds flying up out of the reeds, so that walking across them felt like walking beside a river.

[pause]

There were windows high up in the walls, with stone grilles to let in the light and the breeze, and in the hottest part of the day, servants stood by with great fans of ostrich feathers, waving them slowly to keep the air moving.

[long-pause]

The furniture was light and elegant.

[pause]

There were chairs of ebony and cedar, inlaid with ivory and gold. There were beds with legs carved like the legs of lions, and wooden headrests instead of pillows. There were chests and boxes of every size, painted and inlaid, to hold clothes and jewellery and linen and games.

[pause]

The king and queen slept on beds strung with woven linen cord, under light sheets, with the windows open to the night air, and in the morning, they washed in stone basins and were dressed by their servants.

[long-pause]

Their meals were simple and good.

[pause]

There was bread, of course, of many different kinds, some sweetened with honey or dates. There was roast beef and duck and goose, fish from the river, and all kinds of fruit: figs and grapes, dates and pomegranates, watermelons and the little round fruits of the sycamore fig. There was wine from the vineyards of the delta, kept in tall clay jars, each one carefully labelled with the year and the vineyard and the name of the winemaker.

[pause]

Some of those jars were later buried with the king, and we can still read their labels.

[long-pause]

{img:musicians-harp-lute-banquet}
In the evenings, there might be music.

[pause]

Women played harps and lutes and double flutes, and sang, and girls danced, while the guests sat on low chairs and cushions, wearing garlands of flowers. There was a strange custom at these feasts. The guests sometimes wore little cones of scented fat on top of their wigs, which slowly melted in the warmth of the evening and filled the room with perfume.

[long-pause]

The king and queen also played games together.

[pause]

Among the treasures from the tomb, there are several beautiful game boxes, for senet and other games, made of ebony and ivory, with little drawers to hold the playing pieces. One of them is small enough to carry while travelling. It seems they liked to play wherever they went.

[pause]

There were toys too, kept from the king's childhood: a little box with his name on it, slings, a fire-lighting kit, and a small chair that he must have sat on as a very young boy.

[long-pause]

{img:royal-couple-boat-marsh-lotus}
On calm days, they went out onto the river in a light boat, among the papyrus and the lotus flowers.

[pause]

The marshes were full of life. Herons and ducks and geese, kingfishers darting over the water, butterflies, frogs, dragonflies. The king hunted birds with a throwing stick, and fished with a spear, and the queen sat in the boat beside him, holding a lotus flower, or pointing out the birds.

[pause]

It was a peaceful world, of sunlight and water and green reeds.

[long-pause]

There was sorrow in their lives too.

[pause]

The king and queen had no children who lived. This was a great sadness for them, and also a worry for Egypt, because a king needs an heir to take the throne after him.

[pause]

But they were young. There was still plenty of time.

[pause]

{img:palace-terrace-sunset-couple}
In the evenings, they could stand together on the roof terrace of the palace, looking out over the river as the sun went down behind the western desert, turning the water to gold. Boats with white sails drifted home. The swallows wheeled over the gardens.

[pause]

And far away in the west, beyond the cliffs, the sun went down into the land of the dead, as it did every evening, to rise again in the morning.

[long-pause]

{img:opet-festival-crowds-avenue}
Once a year, in the season of the flood, when the fields are under water and there is little work to do, Thebes holds its greatest festival.

[pause]

It is called the Beautiful Feast of Opet, and it lasts for many days, sometimes for nearly a month. The whole city takes part, and people come from all the villages around, by boat and on foot, to see it.

[long-pause]

At the heart of the festival is a journey.

[pause]

The god Amun leaves his home in the great temple of Karnak, and travels south, about a mile and a half, to visit another temple, beside the river. That temple is the one we call Luxor. There, the god will stay for a while, and through his visit, the power of the king will be renewed for another year.

[pause]

The young king Tutankhamun has a special love for this festival. On the walls of the temple of Luxor, he has had the whole procession carved in long, beautiful pictures, so that it will be remembered forever.

[long-pause]

{img:sacred-barque-priests-shoulders}
On the first morning of the festival, the doors of Karnak open.

[pause]

Out of the darkness of the temple comes a procession of priests, in white linen, with their heads shaved, walking slowly and carefully. On their shoulders, on long wooden poles, they carry a boat.

[pause]

It is not a real boat for sailing, but a sacred boat, made of cedar wood covered with gold. In the middle of it stands a little shrine, hidden behind a veil, and inside the shrine is the statue of the god. The god travels in his boat, as the sun travels in its boat across the sky.

[long-pause]

Behind the boat of Amun come two more, carrying the goddess Mut, his wife, and the young god Khonsu, their son.

[pause]

Then come more priests, burning incense in long bronze holders, so that a sweet blue smoke drifts over the procession. Then singers, and musicians with drums and rattles and clappers, and women shaking the sistrum, a little rattle of bronze that makes a soft, shimmering sound, like wind in the papyrus reeds.

[pause]

{img:temple-singers-sistrum-procession}
The crowd along the way bows down as the god passes. Some people kneel in the dust. Some people call out questions to the god, hoping for an answer: should I marry this person, will my son get well, did my neighbour steal my goat? And sometimes, it is said, the boat seems to dip or move toward a person, as if the god were answering.

[long-pause]

Part of the journey goes by land, along a great avenue lined with stone sphinxes, and part of it goes by water.

[pause]

At the riverbank, the sacred boats are loaded onto real ships, great barges painted and covered with gold, and these are towed slowly up the river by teams of men walking along the bank, pulling on long ropes and singing as they go.

[pause]

{img:golden-barge-towed-river}
Along the bank, the people of Thebes walk beside the ships, cheering and singing. Soldiers march in step, with their shields and spears. Nubian drummers beat out a rhythm. Acrobats turn somersaults. Dancers leap and spin.

[long-pause]

And all along the way, there are stalls and booths, where the temple gives out food and drink to the people.

[pause]

There is bread and beer, of course, enormous amounts of it. There is roast meat and cakes and fruit. During the Opet festival, it is said, the temple gives out more than eleven thousand loaves of bread, and hundreds of jars of beer, and that is only on a single day.

[pause]

For ordinary people, this is the best time of the whole year. There is no work, the fields are under water, and there is food and music and dancing everywhere.

[long-pause]

{img:luxor-temple-colonnade-torches}
At last, the procession arrives at the temple of Luxor.

[pause]

The boats are carried in through the great gateway, past the tall columns of the colonnade, and into the dark inner rooms of the temple. There, the king goes in alone with the highest priests, and performs secret ceremonies before the god, ceremonies that ordinary people never see.

[pause]

When he comes out again, he is renewed. He is the king, the son of Amun, for another year.

[long-pause]

For days, the festival continues.

[pause]

In the evenings, torches are lit all along the avenue and the riverbank, and the light flickers on the stone sphinxes and the painted walls. Families sit on the banks of the river, eating and talking. Musicians play late into the night. Children fall asleep in their parents' laps.

[pause]

{img:riverbank-night-families-lamps}
Across the dark water, the lamps of the boats bob gently up and down, and above the city, the stars of the flood season shine down, very bright, on a land half covered with water.

[long-pause]

Then, at the end of the festival, the whole procession goes back again, the way it came.

[pause]

The boats are carried back to Karnak, and the god returns to his dark room, and the doors are closed. The crowds go home. The stalls are taken down, and the streets of Thebes are swept clean.

[pause]

Slowly, the floodwater begins to go down. The farmers go back out into their fields, now covered with fresh black mud, to begin ploughing and sowing again.

[long-pause]

This was the Egypt that Tutankhamun had given back to his people: the Egypt of the old gods and the old festivals, with its music and its processions, its bread and beer and dancing.

[pause]

On the walls of Luxor, his pictures of the Opet festival are still there today. We can still see the priests carrying the boats, the musicians and the dancers, the soldiers marching, the men pulling the ropes along the riverbank. We can still see the little stalls where the food was given out, and the people bowing down as the god went by.

[pause]

It is as if, three thousand years later, we could still stand in the crowd and watch the procession pass.

[long-pause]

{img:winter-nile-fields-green-morning}
It is the winter of the year that we call 1323 before Christ.

[pause]

In Egypt, winter is the gentlest season. The floodwaters have gone down, and the fields along the river are green with young wheat and barley. The days are sunny and mild, and the nights are cool, sometimes even cold, so that people wrap themselves in woollen cloaks and sit close to the fire in the evenings.

[long-pause]

The king is about eighteen or nineteen years old now. He has been on the throne for nearly ten years.

[pause]

He has grown into a young man, slim and fine-featured, with dark eyes and full lips and a gentle face. He has become more confident, and he is beginning, perhaps, to make more of his own decisions, and to rely a little less on the old men who have guided him since he was a boy.

[pause]

His reign has been a good one, so far, for Egypt. The temples are open. The festivals are back. The harvests have been good.

[long-pause]

{img:scribes-counting-grain-granary}
All over the country, the ordinary work of the season goes on.

[pause]

In the fields, the young crops are growing tall. In the granaries, the scribes are counting the grain from last year's harvest. In the temples, the priests are preparing the offerings. In the workshops, the craftsmen are making furniture and jewellery and linen for the palaces.

[pause]

In the Place of Truth, the tomb-makers walk over the hill to the valley each week, as they always do, and work by lamplight in the tombs.

[long-pause]

In the Place of Truth, the evenings are cold now, and the families light small fires of dried brushwood in their courtyards and sit close around them, wrapped in shawls of wool.

[pause]

The children are sent to bed early, under thick blankets, and the old people tell stories by the firelight: stories about clever peasants and foolish officials, about a sailor shipwrecked on a magic island where a great kind serpent lived, about a prince who was fated to meet his end through a crocodile, a snake or a dog, and who escaped them all.

[pause]

Outside, the stars over the desert are bright and very cold. Jackals call from the hills. And in the morning, there is a thin white frost on the stones, which melts away as soon as the sun comes up.

[long-pause]

In the cities, too, winter is a time of comfort.

[pause]

In the markets of Thebes, there are piles of oranges and pomegranates, dates and dried figs, and baskets of fresh green onions and leeks. The bakers make little cakes sweetened with honey and dates. In the houses of the rich, there are braziers of glowing charcoal in the rooms, and thick woollen blankets on the beds.

[pause]

It is the season of weddings and visits, of long evenings with friends and family, of music and storytelling. The work in the fields is light. The weather is kind.

[long-pause]

There are a few small things that we know about the king's health.

[pause]

His left foot was not quite straight, and part of it seems to have been painful, so that he walked with a stick. He was not a strong young man. And modern scientists, who have studied him very carefully, believe that he had suffered more than once from malaria, the fever that comes from the bite of mosquitoes in the marshes.

[pause]

{img:young-king-walking-stick-garden}
None of this was unusual in ancient Egypt. Many people lived with an aching foot, or fell ill with a fever now and then, and got better again.

[pause]

So when the king was unwell that winter, it seems, nobody thought very much of it at first.

[long-pause]

Perhaps it was a fever. Perhaps it was a fall, from a chariot or simply on a rough piece of ground. Perhaps it was both together. The scientists who have studied his remains have found that one of his legs was broken, just above the knee, not long before he died, and that it had not had time to heal.

[pause]

We do not know exactly what happened. And perhaps we never will.

[long-pause]

What we do know is this. Sometime in the winter, probably in January or February, the young king Tutankhamun became seriously ill. The royal doctors came, with their medicines and their prayers. Priests came, with their spells and their amulets.

[pause]

{img:palace-room-lamps-night-quiet}
In the palace, the servants moved about quietly, and the lamps burned through the night.

[pause]

And then, one day, the king died. He was about eighteen or nineteen years old.

[long-pause]

The news spread slowly through the palace, and then through the city, and then up and down the river.

[pause]

In ancient Egypt, there was a special way to say that a king had died. People said that the falcon had flown up to the horizon. The king, they believed, had gone to join the sun in the sky, and to become one with Osiris, the lord of the world beyond.

[pause]

{img:mourning-women-palace-courtyard}
In the palace, the women of the royal household let down their hair and threw dust on their heads, as was the custom when someone died. The queen, Ankhesenamun, was a widow now, at the age of perhaps twenty or twenty-one, with no children.

[long-pause]

All over Egypt, in the villages and the towns, people heard the news.

[pause]

For most of them, life went on much as before. The fields still needed watering. The bread still needed baking. But they stopped for a moment, and spoke quietly about the young king who had given them back their old gods and their festivals, and who had died so young.

[long-pause]

{img:dusk-thebes-western-cliffs}
And in the palace at Thebes, the old men of the court gathered to decide what must be done.

[pause]

There was a great deal to do, and not very much time. According to the old customs, the burial of a king must take place about seventy days after his death. In those seventy days, his body must be prepared, his coffins and his golden mask and all his belongings must be made ready, and his tomb must be finished.

[pause]

But the tomb that the workers had been cutting for him, in the Valley of the Kings, was nowhere near ready.

[long-pause]

The young king had died much too soon.

[pause]

Out in the western hills, as the sun went down that evening, the cliffs above the Valley of the Kings turned red and gold, and then dark. In the Place of Truth, the tomb-makers sat in their doorways and talked about the news in low voices.

[pause]

They knew that the next seventy days would be very busy indeed.

[long-pause]

{img:embalmers-tent-west-bank}
On the western bank of the Nile, near the edge of the desert, the embalmers set up their workshop.

[pause]

The Egyptians called it the Beautiful House, or the Pure Place. It was a tent or a light building of reed and linen, open to the breeze from the river, with clean water close at hand. Here, the body of the king was carried, quietly, at the start of the seventy days.

[long-pause]

The embalmers were priests, and their work was sacred.

[pause]

The chief embalmer wore a mask in the shape of a jackal's head, the face of Anubis, the god who watched over the dead. The others moved slowly and carefully, reciting prayers and spells as they worked.

[pause]

{img:natron-salt-linen-rolls}
For many days, the body of the king lay covered in natron, a kind of natural salt gathered from dry lakes in the desert, which slowly took away all the moisture and kept it from decay. Then it was washed, anointed with oils and resins that smelled of cedar and frankincense, and wrapped in hundreds of yards of fine linen, layer upon layer.

[long-pause]

Between the layers of linen, the embalmers placed amulets.

[pause]

Little figures of gold and precious stones, each one with its own meaning and its own protection. A scarab beetle over the heart. An eye of Horus, for health. A djed pillar, for stability. Little golden sandals on the feet, and golden caps on each finger and toe. A dagger of iron, a metal so rare in Egypt that it was more precious than gold, which scientists now believe was made from a meteorite that fell from the sky.

[pause]

All the while, the priests read out the spells, so that each amulet would wake and do its work.

[long-pause]

{img:goldsmiths-workshop-mask-making}
Meanwhile, in the royal workshops, the craftsmen were working day and night.

[pause]

There was so much to make. The king needed coffins, one inside another, the innermost of solid gold. He needed a mask of gold for his face. He needed a great stone sarcophagus to hold the coffins, and four golden shrines, one inside another, to enclose the sarcophagus. He needed jars and boxes for his belongings, and hundreds of little figures, called shabtis, to work for him in the next world.

[pause]

There was not time to make everything new. So the craftsmen did what they could. Some things were made fresh, with great care and skill. Other things were taken from the royal storerooms, objects that had been made for other members of the family, and the names on them were carefully changed.

[long-pause]

The golden mask is one of the things that was made with the greatest care of all.

[pause]

It was beaten out of two thick sheets of gold, joined together, and shaped into the face of the young king, calm and beautiful, with wide dark eyes of quartz and obsidian. On his head was the striped royal headcloth, in gold and deep blue glass. On his brow were the vulture and the cobra, the protectors of the south and the north. Around his neck was a broad collar of gold and blue and red stones.

[pause]

It was made to cover the face of the wrapped king, so that his spirit would always know him, and find its way back to him.

[long-pause]

Meanwhile, the officials had to solve the problem of the tomb.

[pause]

The great tomb that had been begun for the king was far from finished, and there was no hope of finishing it in seventy days. So they chose another tomb instead, a much smaller one, cut into the floor of the Valley of the Kings, which had perhaps been meant for a nobleman.

[pause]

It had a short staircase leading down into the rock, a sloping corridor, and four small rooms. It was not the tomb of a great king. But it would have to do.

[long-pause]

{img:valley-of-kings-workers-small-tomb}
The tomb-makers from the Place of Truth were sent to make it ready.

[pause]

They widened the rooms a little and smoothed the walls. In the burial chamber, the painters set to work, very quickly, covering the walls with a warm golden-yellow background and painting the pictures that a king's tomb needed.

[pause]

On one wall, they painted the funeral procession. On another, the old man Ay, dressed as a priest in a leopard skin, performing a sacred ceremony for the king. On another, the king being welcomed by the gods. And on the far wall, twelve baboons, sitting in rows, representing the twelve hours of the night through which the sun must pass before it rises again.

[pause]

There was no time to paint the other rooms at all. They were left as bare, rough rock.

[long-pause]

All the while, the king's belongings were being gathered together.

[pause]

His chariots were taken apart, so that they would fit through the narrow corridor. His beds and chairs and stools, his clothes, his sandals, his walking sticks, his bows and arrows, his game boxes, his jewellery, his musical instruments, his lamps. Jars of wine and oil, baskets of fruit and bread, joints of meat wrapped in linen. Boxes full of linen garments, some of them clothes he had worn as a little boy.

[pause]

{img:storeroom-chests-chariots-packed}
Everything that a king might need in the next world, where life would go on much as it had in this one, only forever.

[long-pause]

In the palace, the queen, Ankhesenamun, was making her own preparations.

[pause]

She gathered flowers from the palace gardens and the fields along the river: blue cornflowers, olive leaves, the yellow fruits of the mandrake, the petals of the blue water lily. She wove them, it seems, into a small wreath and a collar.

[pause]

These flowers tell us something important. Cornflowers bloom in Egypt in March and April, and mandrake fruits ripen in the spring. So we know that the king was buried in the springtime, when the fields were green and the air smelled of blossoms.

[long-pause]

{img:cornflower-wreath-palace-window}
And so, as the seventy days drew to a close, everything was ready.

[pause]

The coffins were finished and polished. The mask shone in its workshop. The tomb was swept and waiting. The belongings were packed into boxes and baskets, ready to be carried across the river.

[pause]

On the evening before the funeral, the sun went down behind the western hills as it always did, and the whole of Thebes was quiet. Tomorrow, the king would go on his last journey, across the river, into the west.

[long-pause]

{img:funeral-boats-crossing-nile-dawn}
The funeral of the king began early in the morning, on a spring day, when the air was still cool and the river was calm.

[pause]

At the edge of the water on the eastern bank, the funeral boats were waiting. They were long and low, painted and decorated with flowers, and on the largest of them stood a shrine, in which the coffin of the king would cross the river.

[long-pause]

The procession came down to the water slowly.

[pause]

First the priests, in white linen, carrying incense and holy water. Then the coffin, on a wooden sledge, covered with a canopy, and surrounded by flowers. Then the officials of the court, in their finest clothes. Then the servants, carrying the king's belongings, in a long line, two by two: chests and chairs, jars and baskets, fans and walking sticks, all carried carefully on their shoulders.

[pause]

And among them, walking near the coffin, was the queen, Ankhesenamun, dressed in plain white, with her hair loose about her shoulders.

[long-pause]

{img:mourners-white-linen-riverbank}
On the riverbank, the mourning women were waiting.

[pause]

They were dressed in pale blue linen, and as the coffin passed, they raised their arms above their heads and sang, a long, rising and falling lament, the old song of grief that had been sung at funerals in Egypt for a thousand years. Some of them threw dust in the air, and it drifted slowly away on the morning breeze.

[pause]

Then the coffin was lifted onto the boat, and the boats pushed off from the bank, and began to cross the wide brown river toward the west.

[long-pause]

The crossing was slow and quiet.

[pause]

The oars dipped and rose. The water rippled gently past. A flock of white egrets lifted from the reeds and flew away over the fields. Ahead of the boats, the western cliffs rose up, golden in the morning sun, and the sky above them was clear and pale blue.

[pause]

For the Egyptians, crossing the river to the west was like the journey of the soul itself, from the land of the living to the land of the dead, following the path of the setting sun.

[long-pause]

{img:sledge-pulled-by-officials-desert}
On the western bank, the coffin was placed once more on its sledge.

[pause]

Then the most important officials of Egypt took hold of the ropes and pulled it themselves, across the fields and up toward the desert. On the wall of the burial chamber, the painters showed this moment: twelve men, dressed in white, with white bands tied around their foreheads as a sign of mourning, pulling the sledge with the king's coffin. Among them were the two viziers of Egypt, the highest officials in the land.

[pause]

It was a long, slow walk, along the road into the hills, and into the winding, silent Valley of the Kings.

[long-pause]

The valley is a strange and beautiful place.

[pause]

It is a dry, narrow canyon in the desert hills, with steep walls of pale golden rock, and above it all rises a peak shaped almost like a pyramid, which the Egyptians called the Peak, the home of a goddess who loved silence.

[pause]

{img:valley-of-kings-procession-sunlight}
There are no trees here, no water, no birds singing. Only the rock and the sand and the bright sky, and the doorways of the old tombs of the kings, cut into the foot of the cliffs.

[pause]

The procession wound slowly up the valley, the sledge creaking, the mourners singing softly, until it came to the small tomb cut into the floor of the valley, with its short staircase leading down into the rock.

[long-pause]

At the top of the steps, the last ceremony took place.

[pause]

The coffin was stood upright, facing the south, and the old man Ay came forward. He was dressed as a priest, in a leopard skin, and he held in his hands a sacred tool, shaped a little like a carpenter's adze.

[pause]

{img:ay-leopard-skin-ceremony}
Ay touched the mouth and the eyes of the coffin with the tool, and spoke the ancient words. This was the Opening of the Mouth. Through this ceremony, the Egyptians believed, the king would be able to breathe again, and to see, and to eat and drink, and to speak, in the world beyond.

[pause]

By performing it, Ay was also showing everyone that he was the one who would follow the young king on the throne.

[long-pause]

Then the coffin was carried down the steps, and along the sloping corridor, into the burial chamber.

[pause]

There, it was placed inside the others, one coffin inside another, and the golden mask was laid upon the face of the king. Around it, the great stone sarcophagus was closed, and around that, the workers assembled the four golden shrines, one inside another, filling almost the whole of the little room, so that there was barely space to move.

[pause]

And on the brow of one of the coffins, someone placed the little wreath of flowers: the cornflowers and the olive leaves and the blue lily petals.

[long-pause]

Then the other rooms were filled.

[pause]

The chariots, the beds, the chairs, the boxes, the jars, the baskets of food, the statues, the walking sticks and the bows and the game boxes, all were carried in and stacked carefully, one on top of another, in the dim light of the oil lamps, until the rooms were full.

[pause]

At last, the workers came out, sweeping away their footprints behind them, and the doorway to the burial chamber was walled up and plastered over, and stamped with the seals of the royal cemetery: the jackal of Anubis, lying above nine captives.

[long-pause]

{img:funeral-feast-desert-evening}
Outside, in the sunshine, the mourners shared a last meal together.

[pause]

It was a funeral feast, as was the custom. There was beef and duck and goose, bread and wine and beer, and the guests wore collars made of flowers and leaves. When it was over, they broke the dishes, and gathered up everything that had been used, the plates and cups, the collars of flowers, the brooms they had swept with, and packed it all into jars, which were buried nearby in a little pit in the valley.

[pause]

Those jars were found more than three thousand years later, with the flowers still inside them, dried and brown and fragile.

[long-pause]

Then the staircase was filled with stones and rubble, and the entrance was sealed.

[pause]

The mourners walked slowly back down the valley, and across the fields, to the river, where the boats were waiting. Behind them, the sun was going down, and the cliffs of the valley glowed red, and the shadows filled the canyon.

[pause]

The young king was alone, in the dark, in his small tomb, surrounded by his treasures and his flowers, at the beginning of his journey through the night.

[long-pause]

{img:widowed-queen-palace-window}
After the funeral, the queen Ankhesenamun was alone in the palace.

[pause]

She was young, perhaps twenty or twenty-one. Her parents were gone, her sisters were gone, and now her husband was gone too. She was the last of her family, the last daughter of Akhenaten and Nefertiti, and whoever married her might have a claim to the throne of Egypt.

[long-pause]

At about this time, something unusual happened, which we know about not from Egyptian records, but from the records of a foreign kingdom far to the north.

[pause]

In the land of Hatti, in the mountains of what is now Turkey, there lived a powerful king called Suppiluliuma, the king of the Hittites. He and his people were old rivals of Egypt.

[pause]

{img:hittite-king-mountain-palace}
One day, a messenger arrived at his court from Egypt, with a letter from the queen. The Hittites wrote down her name as Dahamunzu, which in Egyptian simply means the wife of the king.

[pause]

The letter said, as the Hittites recorded it: My husband has died. I have no son. They say that you have many sons. If you would give me one of your sons, he would become my husband. I do not wish to take one of my servants as a husband.

[long-pause]

The Hittite king could hardly believe it. No queen of Egypt had ever asked for such a thing before. He suspected a trick, and he sent a messenger of his own to Egypt, to find out whether it was true.

[pause]

The messenger came back with another letter from the queen, who seemed hurt that she had not been believed. She had written to no other country, she said. She was asking only him.

[pause]

So, at last, the Hittite king agreed. He chose one of his sons, a prince named Zannanza, and sent him south toward Egypt, with a company of men.

[long-pause]

{img:desert-road-travellers-caravan}
But the prince never arrived.

[pause]

Somewhere on the long road through Syria, he died, and the Hittite records do not say exactly how. His father believed that the Egyptians were to blame, and was very angry, and there was fighting between the two kingdoms for some time afterwards.

[pause]

Whether the letters really came from Ankhesenamun, or from another queen of that time, scholars still argue. But many believe that they did.

[long-pause]

Whatever happened, it was the old man Ay who became the next king of Egypt.

[pause]

He had performed the Opening of the Mouth at Tutankhamun's tomb, which was a sign that he was the rightful heir. And it seems that he may have married Ankhesenamun, because there is a little ring, found many years later, with both their names on it side by side.

[pause]

After that, Ankhesenamun disappears from history. We do not know what became of her, or where she was buried. Her name is simply never heard again.

[long-pause]

Ay was already an old man, and he reigned for only about four years.

[pause]

When he died, he was buried in the great tomb in the western valley that had perhaps first been meant for Tutankhamun. And the next king of Egypt was Horemheb, the general.

[pause]

Horemheb ruled for many years, and he was a strong and capable king. But he wanted people to forget the whole troubled time of Akhenaten and the Aten, and everyone connected with it.

[long-pause]

So Horemheb's workers went all over Egypt and cut the names of Akhenaten, Tutankhamun and Ay out of the walls of the temples, and carved Horemheb's own name in their place. On the beautiful walls at Luxor, where Tutankhamun had shown the Opet festival, his name was replaced by Horemheb's.

[pause]

{img:stonemason-chiselling-name-temple}
In later times, when the Egyptians wrote down the lists of their kings, they left out Akhenaten and Tutankhamun and Ay altogether, as if they had never reigned at all.

[pause]

The young king was forgotten.

[long-pause]

His tomb, however, was not quite forgotten, at least not at first.

[pause]

Not long after the funeral, perhaps within a few years, thieves broke into it, not once but twice. They tunnelled through the rubble in the staircase and the corridor, and crept into the rooms with lamps, looking for things that were small and easy to carry: gold and jewellery, linen, perfumed oils.

[pause]

They did not get very far. They were disturbed, or perhaps caught, and they left in a hurry, leaving things scattered all over the floors.

[long-pause]

{img:officials-resealing-tomb-torch}
Afterwards, officials from the royal cemetery came and tidied up the tomb, a little hastily.

[pause]

They pushed things back into boxes, without much care for which box they had come from. They tied up a handful of gold rings, dropped by one of the thieves, in a little linen scarf and put it back in a chest. They filled the corridor with rubble again, closed up the holes, and stamped the doorways once more with the seal of the jackal.

[pause]

Then they went away, and nobody disturbed the tomb again.

[long-pause]

About two hundred years later, workers came to the valley to cut a great tomb for another king, Ramesses the Sixth, just above the little tomb of Tutankhamun.

[pause]

They built little stone huts to live in while they worked, right on top of the hidden entrance, and they piled their stone chips all around. By the time they had finished, the staircase of the young king's tomb was buried deep beneath the huts and the rubble, and nobody remembered that it was there.

[pause]

{img:workers-huts-valley-stone-chips}
Then the floods that sometimes rush through the valley after a rare desert storm washed more sand and stones over everything.

[long-pause]

The centuries passed.

[pause]

The kings of Egypt came and went. Persians came, and Greeks, and Romans. The old temples fell into ruin, and the old gods were forgotten, and the old writing could no longer be read by anyone.

[pause]

The other tombs in the valley were opened and robbed, one by one, and their treasures scattered. Travellers came to look at their empty corridors and painted walls, and wrote their names on the stone.

[pause]

But under the rubble and the old workmen's huts, in the floor of the valley, one small tomb lay sealed and silent, with its treasures and its little wreath of flowers, for more than three thousand years.

[long-pause]

{img:howard-carter-valley-1920s}
In the autumn of 1922, a quiet, stubborn Englishman named Howard Carter was digging in the Valley of the Kings.

[pause]

He was in his late forties, with a neat moustache and a bowler hat, and he had been working in Egypt since he was seventeen years old, first as an artist, copying the paintings on tomb walls, and later as an archaeologist.

[pause]

He was not an easy man. He was proud and impatient, and he did not suffer fools. But he knew the Valley of the Kings better than almost anyone alive.

[long-pause]

For years, Carter had been convinced of one thing.

[pause]

Somewhere in the valley, he believed, there was a tomb that no one had found: the tomb of a little-known king called Tutankhamun. A few small objects with that name on them had turned up in the valley over the years, among them a little cup and some pieces of gold leaf, and the jars from the funeral feast. But no one had ever found the king himself.

[pause]

Most experts thought that the valley was exhausted, and that every tomb in it had already been discovered. Carter did not agree.

[long-pause]

{img:lord-carnarvon-country-house}
His work was paid for by an English nobleman, Lord Carnarvon, who had come to Egypt for his health after a bad motor car accident, and had fallen in love with its history.

[pause]

Carnarvon had been paying for Carter's digging for several years now, and so far they had found very little. The money was running out. In the summer of 1922, Carnarvon told Carter that this would have to be the last season.

[pause]

Carter persuaded him to allow one more.

[long-pause]

So, at the beginning of November 1922, Carter and his workmen began to dig in the one small corner of the valley that had not yet been cleared: the area beneath the old stone huts of the workers who had cut the tomb of Ramesses the Sixth.

[pause]

{img:workmen-baskets-rubble-valley}
The Egyptian workmen dug through the rubble with their picks, and the boys carried the stones away in baskets on their heads, in long lines, singing as they went, just as workers in this valley had done three thousand years before.

[long-pause]

On the morning of the fourth of November, Carter arrived at the site to find an unusual silence.

[pause]

The workmen had stopped digging. Under the very first hut, they had uncovered a step cut into the rock.

[pause]

There is a story, often told, that it was a young water boy who found it, scraping away the sand to set down his water jars. Whether that is quite true or not, nobody knows for sure. But the step was there.

[long-pause]

Carter's men worked carefully all through that day and the next.

[pause]

One step led to another, and then another, going down into the rock. By the afternoon of the fifth, they had uncovered twelve steps, and at the bottom of them, the top of a doorway, blocked with stones and covered with plaster.

[pause]

{img:sealed-doorway-staircase-sunlight}
And pressed into the plaster were the old seals of the royal cemetery: the jackal of Anubis, lying above nine captives.

[long-pause]

Carter could hardly contain his excitement. But he did a remarkable thing. He filled the staircase in again.

[pause]

He wanted Lord Carnarvon to be there when the tomb was opened, and Carnarvon was in England. So Carter sent him a telegram, which said: At last have made wonderful discovery in Valley; a magnificent tomb with seals intact; recovered same for your arrival; congratulations.

[pause]

Then he waited.

[long-pause]

Lord Carnarvon came as quickly as he could, by train and ship, with his daughter, Lady Evelyn Herbert. They arrived in Luxor about two weeks later.

[pause]

The staircase was cleared again, and the doorway was opened. Behind it was a sloping corridor, filled with rubble, which took several more days to clear. And at the end of the corridor, there was a second sealed doorway.

[long-pause]

On the afternoon of the twenty-sixth of November 1922, Carter stood in front of this second doorway, with Carnarvon and Lady Evelyn and his assistant, Arthur Callender, just behind him.

[pause]

With trembling hands, he made a small hole in the top left corner of the door. He pushed an iron rod through it, and felt nothing but empty space beyond. Then he widened the hole a little, lit a candle, and held it up to look inside.

[pause]

{img:candlelight-glimpse-gold-dark}
At first, he could see nothing. The hot air coming out of the room made the candle flame flicker.

[pause]

Then, as his eyes grew used to the dim light, he began to see shapes in the darkness. Strange animals, statues, and gold. Everywhere, the glint of gold.

[long-pause]

For a moment, Carter was too amazed to speak.

[pause]

Behind him, Lord Carnarvon could not bear the suspense. Can you see anything? he asked.

[pause]

And Carter, still staring into the darkness, answered: Yes. Wonderful things.

[long-pause]

{img:antechamber-golden-couches-statues}
What he had seen was the first room of the tomb, which he called the Antechamber.

[pause]

It was crowded with objects, piled one on top of another: golden couches with the heads of animals, a golden throne, painted chests and boxes, alabaster vases, the gilded pieces of chariots, stacked against the wall. And on either side of a sealed doorway at the far end, two life-size statues of the king, black and gold, standing guard, as they had stood in the silence for more than three thousand years.

[pause]

No one had seen anything like it, ever before.

[long-pause]

{img:carter-team-cataloguing-objects}
The news of the discovery flew around the world.

[pause]

Within days, newspapers in London and New York and Paris were full of it. Reporters and photographers came to Luxor by the hundred, and crowds of visitors gathered every day at the edge of the tomb, hoping for a glimpse of the treasures as they were carried out into the sunlight.

[pause]

Suddenly, the forgotten young king was the most famous pharaoh in the world.

[long-pause]

But Carter did not hurry.

[pause]

He knew that the objects in the tomb were very old and very fragile. Linen that had lain still for three thousand years could crumble at a touch. Wood had dried and shrunk. Beads had come loose from their threads.

[pause]

So, before anything was moved, every object was numbered and photographed exactly where it lay. Then it was drawn, measured and described on a card, and only then was it lifted, very gently, and carried out of the tomb on a padded tray.

[long-pause]

Carter's team turned a nearby empty tomb into a laboratory.

[pause]

There, the conservators worked on each object in turn, cleaning it, strengthening it with wax, and packing it carefully into a wooden crate with cotton wool. Then the crates were carried down to the river on a little railway, pushed by hand, and sent down the Nile by steamer to the museum in Cairo.

[pause]

It was slow, patient work, and it went on for ten years.

[long-pause]

In February 1923, they opened the sealed doorway between the two statues of the king.

[pause]

Behind it, filling almost the whole of the room, was an enormous shrine of wood, covered in gold, with doors of gold and blue faience. It was the first of the four shrines that the ancient workers had built around the coffin. Carter opened its doors, and found another shrine inside, and then another, and another.

[pause]

{img:burial-chamber-golden-shrine}
Lord Carnarvon did not live to see what lay inside them. He fell ill that spring, and died in Cairo in April 1923, only a few months after the discovery.

[long-pause]

It took nearly three more years of careful work to reach the king himself.

[pause]

Inside the four shrines was the great stone sarcophagus, carved from a single block of yellow quartzite, with goddesses spreading their winged arms around its corners. Inside the sarcophagus was the first coffin, of wood covered in gold, in the shape of the king lying with his arms crossed, holding the crook and the flail.

[pause]

And on its brow, still lying where it had been placed on the day of the funeral, was the little wreath of flowers.

[long-pause]

{img:dried-flower-wreath-coffin-brow}
Carter was very moved by the flowers.

[pause]

He wrote later that, of all the splendid things in the tomb, nothing was so beautiful as those few withered flowers, still keeping a little of their colour. They seemed to tell him, he wrote, how short a time three thousand years really was: only yesterday and tomorrow.

[pause]

Perhaps, he thought, they had been the last farewell of the young widowed queen to her husband.

[long-pause]

Inside the first coffin was a second, also of gold-covered wood, inlaid with coloured glass.

[pause]

And inside the second, there was a third coffin, so heavy that the men could barely lift it. When they cleaned it, they found that it was made of solid gold, more than a hundred kilos of it, and beautifully engraved with the feathered wings of goddesses wrapped protectively around the king.

[pause]

{img:golden-mask-lamplight-tomb}
And inside the third coffin, at last, lay the king, wrapped in his linen, with the golden mask covering his face and shoulders, shining in the light of the electric lamps.

[long-pause]

The golden mask became the most famous object in the world of archaeology.

[pause]

It is beautiful, and it is calm. The face of the young king is smooth and gentle, with large, dark, outlined eyes looking straight ahead, and a little half-smile on the lips. People who see it often say that it seems alive.

[pause]

Today, it is perhaps the best-known image of ancient Egypt, recognised all over the world.

[long-pause]

There were more than five thousand objects in the tomb altogether.

[pause]

{img:treasury-room-anubis-shrine}
In the last room, which Carter called the Treasury, a great statue of the god Anubis, in the form of a black jackal, lay on top of a golden shrine, guarding the entrance, with a linen shawl draped over his back. Behind him stood the gilded chest that held the king's inner organs, in four little golden coffins, protected by four golden goddesses with outstretched arms.

[pause]

There were model boats, to carry the king along the rivers of the next world. There were little figures of the king hunting, and standing on the back of a panther. There were chests of jewellery, and the king's fan with the ostrich feathers.

[long-pause]

There were simpler things too, and these, perhaps, are the most touching.

[pause]

There were the king's sandals, and his gloves, and his linen shirts. There were his walking sticks, more than a hundred of them, some of them carved and painted, and one of them, it is said, labelled as a reed that the king had cut with his own hand.

[pause]

{img:simple-objects-sandals-boxes-linen}
There was a little box with a lock of hair inside, wrapped in linen, belonging to his grandmother, Queen Tiye. There were baskets of fruit and bread, and jars of honey, and seeds for planting in the next world.

[long-pause]

Carter finished his work in 1932, ten years after the first step was found.

[pause]

He was tired, and not well, and he never wrote the full scientific account of the tomb that he had planned. He went back to England and lived quietly in London, and died there in 1939.

[pause]

On his grave, there is a line taken from one of the treasures of the tomb, a little cup of white alabaster shaped like a lotus flower, which was found lying just inside the entrance:

[pause]

May your spirit live, may you spend millions of years, you who love Thebes, sitting with your face to the north wind, your eyes beholding happiness.

[long-pause]

{img:valley-of-kings-today-morning}
The Valley of the Kings is still there today, behind the cliffs on the western bank of the Nile, opposite the modern city of Luxor.

[pause]

Every morning, early, before the heat of the day, visitors walk slowly up the dusty path into the valley, where the tomb-makers from the Place of Truth once climbed over the hill with their tools. The rocky walls of the canyon are just as they were. The Peak still rises above it all, shaped like a pyramid.

[long-pause]

The little tomb of Tutankhamun is still there too, cut into the floor of the valley, with its short staircase going down into the rock.

[pause]

Visitors walk down the steps, along the sloping corridor, and into the burial chamber, where the golden-yellow walls are still painted with the pictures that the painters made in such a hurry, so long ago. The twelve baboons still sit in their rows. The old man Ay still stands in his leopard skin, performing the Opening of the Mouth.

[pause]

{img:burial-chamber-painted-walls-quiet}
And there, in a glass case in the outer room, the young king himself still rests in his tomb, where he was laid more than three thousand years ago, under the soft, careful light.

[long-pause]

Not far away, at the foot of the hills, Howard Carter's house still stands.

[pause]

It is a small, low house with a dome, built of mud brick, where Carter lived during the long years of his work. Today it is a little museum, with his desk and his camera and his bed, and a copy of the tomb has been built nearby, so that people can see the paintings without harming the real ones.

[long-pause]

{img:deir-el-medina-ruins-today}
The village of the tomb-makers, the Place of Truth, is still there as well, in its narrow valley.

[pause]

The walls of the houses are only knee-high now, but the long street is still there, and the doorways, and the little rooms one behind another. Above the village, on the hillside, the painted tombs of the workers can still be visited, with their bright colours glowing in the lamplight: a family picking fruit in a garden, a farmer ploughing with his oxen in the fields of the next world.

[long-pause]

Across the river, in Luxor, the great temples still stand.

[pause]

At Karnak, the forest of tall stone columns still rises in the great hall, and the obelisks still point up at the sky. The sacred lake still holds its water, and in the evening, the swallows still skim low across its surface.

[pause]

{img:luxor-temple-evening-lights}
At the temple of Luxor, the walls that Tutankhamun had carved with the Opet festival are still there, with their long pictures of the priests and the boats, the musicians and the dancers. And the avenue of stone sphinxes, which once ran all the way from Luxor to Karnak, has been cleared again in our own time, so that people can walk along it, as the procession once did.

[long-pause]

The treasures of the tomb have travelled far.

[pause]

For many years, they were shown in the old Egyptian Museum in Cairo, and sometimes they went on journeys around the world, to London and Paris and New York and Tokyo, where millions of people queued for hours to see them.

[pause]

{img:museum-hall-golden-treasures}
Today, most of them have a new home, in the Grand Egyptian Museum, a vast building of stone and glass on the edge of the desert near the pyramids of Giza. There, for the first time, all of the treasures of the young king can be seen together, in great quiet halls: the golden coffins, the chariots, the throne with its picture of the queen, the little chair from his childhood, and the walking sticks, and the sandals.

[pause]

And in a room of its own, the golden mask, gazing calmly out at the world.

[long-pause]

On the Nile itself, life goes on.

[pause]

The river no longer floods each year, because a great dam has been built far to the south, at Aswan. But it still flows north through the desert, wide and brown and slow, as it did in the days of the pharaohs.

[pause]

{img:felucca-nile-sunset-modern-timeless}
Small boats with white triangular sails still drift up and down between the banks. Farmers still grow wheat and clover and sugar cane in the green strip along the water, and the date palms still stand tall against the sky. And at the edge of the fields, quite suddenly, the desert still begins.

[long-pause]

Every evening, the sun still goes down behind the western cliffs, as it did on the evening of the king's funeral.

[pause]

The cliffs above the Valley of the Kings turn gold, then red, then purple. The river catches the last of the light. On the eastern bank, the lamps come on in the windows of the town, and the call of the evening drifts across the water.

[pause]

{img:western-cliffs-night-stars-nile}
And then the stars come out over Egypt, very bright and very many, as they did three thousand years ago, when the young king Tutankhamun stood on the roof of his palace beside his queen, and watched the sun go down into the west.

[long-pause]

There we will leave the Nile for tonight, flowing quietly through the dark, past the sleeping fields and the silent temples, and the hidden valley in the western hills.
