In November 1922, an Englishman stood in a narrow rock-cut passage in Egypt and held a candle up to a small hole in an ancient wall. Warm air flowed out from the darkness and made the flame flicker. Then, slowly, shapes began to appear. Strange animals, statues, and everywhere, the glint of gold.

The tomb belonged to a young king called Tutankhamun. Today, his golden mask is one of the most famous faces in the world. Yet the king himself was almost forgotten for three thousand years. And the people of his time, the farmers, the scribes, the tomb-makers and the children, are hardly remembered at all. So what was life really like in Egypt, when Tutankhamun was king? [long-pause]

Ancient Egyptians built small boats out of the same reeds they used to make paper.

[pause]

Along the edges of the Nile grew thick beds of papyrus, a tall reed with a feathery head. The Egyptians used it for almost everything. They wove it into mats, baskets, sandals and ropes. They pressed thin strips of it together to make sheets for writing. And they tied great bundles of it together to make light little boats. A papyrus boat was easy to build and easy to carry, and it floated well in the calm water near the banks. Fishermen stood in them at dawn, casting their nets while the mist still lay on the river. Hunters used them to glide quietly through the marshes, looking for ducks and geese. Larger wooden boats carried heavy cargo up and down the river, but for everyday work, the simple reed boat was perfect. Thousands of years later, people in some parts of Africa still build boats in almost the same way. The idea was simple, and it lasted.

[long-pause]

{img:02-green-strip-desert-edge}

In ancient Egypt, you could stand with one foot in green fields and the other in empty desert.

[pause]

Egypt is one of the driest countries in the world. Almost no rain falls there. Yet along the Nile, there is a narrow strip of dark, fertile land, only a few miles wide in many places. On this strip, the ancient Egyptians grew wheat, barley, flax, vegetables and fruit, and planted tall date palms. Where the water could not reach, the green stopped suddenly, and the golden desert began. The Egyptians called their fertile land the Black Land, because of its dark soil, and the desert the Red Land. The Black Land was the world of the living, full of villages, fields and noise. The Red Land was quiet, empty and dangerous, and it was where the dead were buried, safe from the yearly flood. This simple geography shaped everything about Egyptian life. Almost every person in the kingdom lived within a short walk of the river, and almost all of them could see the desert from their doorstep.

[long-pause]

{img:03-nile-flood-fields-water}

Every summer, the Nile rose and covered the fields, and the Egyptians were glad of it.

[pause]

Far to the south, in the mountains of Ethiopia, heavy summer rains swelled the rivers that fed the Nile. Weeks later, the water reached Egypt, and the river began to rise. By late summer, it spread out across the fields, turning the valley into a wide shallow lake. The villages stood on slightly higher ground, like little islands. When the water went down again in the autumn, it left behind a fresh layer of rich black mud, perfect for planting. This yearly flood was the heartbeat of Egypt. A good flood meant a good harvest. A flood that was too low meant hunger, and one that was too high could wash away houses. The Egyptians measured the river carefully with marked stone stairways called nilometers. They even divided their year into three seasons based on the river: the flood, the growing time and the harvest. The whole kingdom lived by its gentle rhythm.

[long-pause]

{img:04-thebes-river-view-temples}

In Tutankhamun's time, the great city of Thebes was the religious heart of Egypt.

[pause]

Thebes stood on the east bank of the Nile, about four hundred miles south of the sea. Today, the modern city of Luxor stands in the same place. In ancient times, it was a large and busy city of mud-brick houses, gardens, workshops, markets and harbours. Above the low roofs rose the enormous stone gateways of its temples, painted in bright colours and decorated with tall flagpoles. The most important god of Thebes was Amun, the hidden one, and his temple at Karnak was one of the largest religious buildings ever made. For centuries, the kings of Egypt honoured Amun and gave rich gifts to his temples. Across the river, on the west bank, lay the tombs of the kings and the temples built in their memory. Thebes was a city of the living on one side of the river and a city of the dead on the other. Each day, the sun seemed to travel from one to the other.

[long-pause]

{img:05-western-cliffs-golden-light}

The Egyptians buried their dead on the west bank of the Nile, where the sun goes down.

[pause]

Every evening, the sun sank behind the cliffs on the western side of the river. To the Egyptians, this was a daily image of death. They believed that the sun travelled through the underworld at night and was born again each morning in the east. So it seemed natural to bury the dead in the west, in the dry desert hills, where they could join the sun on its nightly journey and rise again. At Thebes, the western cliffs glowed golden and red in the evening light. Hidden among them were the tombs of kings, queens, nobles and ordinary workers. The dry desert air preserved everything placed in the tombs, from linen and wood to bread and flowers. That is why so much of what we know about ancient Egypt comes from burials. It is a slightly strange thought. Our best picture of how the Egyptians lived comes from the places they prepared for the afterlife.

[long-pause]

{img:06-farm-village-mud-houses-palms}

Most ancient Egyptians lived in small villages and worked as farmers.

[pause]

We usually think of pharaohs, pyramids and golden treasures. But most people in ancient Egypt were farmers who lived in small villages along the river. Their houses were built of mud bricks, made from Nile mud mixed with straw and dried in the sun. The walls were thick, keeping the rooms cool in the heat. Roofs were flat, made of wooden beams, palm leaves and mud, and families often slept up there on hot nights. A typical house had a few rooms, a courtyard, a small kitchen with a clay oven and a storage space for grain. Goats, ducks and a donkey might live in the yard. Date palms grew between the houses, giving shade and sweet fruit. Life followed the seasons, the river and the needs of the fields. Most villagers never travelled far from home. Yet their hard work fed the whole kingdom, including the priests, the scribes, the soldiers and the king himself.

[long-pause]

{img:07-woman-grinding-grain-courtyard}

Every loaf of bread in ancient Egypt began with someone kneeling on the ground, grinding grain by hand.

[pause]

Bread was the most important food in Egypt, eaten by everyone from farmers to kings. But making it took a great deal of work. First, the grain had to be ground into flour. Women knelt behind a flat, sloping stone called a quern and pushed a smaller stone back and forth over the grain, again and again. It was slow, tiring work, done every day. Tiny bits of sand and stone often ended up in the flour. Over a lifetime, this gritty bread wore down people's teeth, and many ancient Egyptian skulls show badly worn teeth because of it. The flour was mixed with water and sometimes with honey, dates or herbs, and baked in clay pots or on the walls of small ovens. The Egyptians had many kinds of bread, in different shapes and sizes. Bread was so central to life that workers were often paid partly in loaves, along with jars of beer.

[long-pause]

{img:08-farmers-ploughing-oxen-field}

When the floodwater went down, the Egyptian farmers had only a short time to plough and sow.

[pause]

As soon as the Nile flood withdrew in the autumn, the fields were left soft, damp and covered with fresh black mud. The farmers had to work quickly, before the earth dried and hardened in the sun. They ploughed with simple wooden ploughs pulled by pairs of oxen, guiding them by hand. Behind them came the sowers, scattering seed from baskets. Then flocks of sheep or goats were sometimes driven across the fields to press the seeds into the soft ground. The main crops were emmer wheat and barley, used for bread and beer, and flax, used to make linen. Farmers also grew onions, lentils, beans, lettuce, cucumbers and melons. Tomb paintings often show these scenes of ploughing and sowing in great detail, with farmers chatting, singing and sometimes complaining. The Egyptians clearly hoped that the fields of the afterlife would be just like the fields they knew, only always fertile and never too hot.

[long-pause]

{img:09-shaduf-irrigation-canal}

A simple wooden lever called a shaduf helped Egyptian gardeners lift water from the river.

[pause]

During the dry months, gardens and orchards still needed water. To lift it from the river or the canals, the Egyptians used a clever device called a shaduf. It was a long wooden pole balanced on a frame, with a bucket hanging from one end and a heavy weight, often a lump of clay, on the other. The gardener pulled the bucket down into the water, and the counterweight helped him lift it up again. Then he swung it around and emptied it into a channel that carried the water to the plants. With a shaduf, one person could lift a surprising amount of water in a day. The device appears in tomb paintings from around the time of Tutankhamun, and it worked so well that farmers along the Nile still used it in the twentieth century. Sometimes the best inventions are the simple ones that nobody ever needs to improve.

[long-pause]

{img:10-harvest-golden-wheat-sickles}

At harvest time, the scribes arrived in the fields to count the grain for taxes.

[pause]

In spring, the wheat and barley turned golden, and the whole village went out to bring in the harvest. The men cut the stalks with curved sickles, made of wood with sharp flint teeth. Women and children followed behind, gathering the fallen ears in baskets. The grain was carried to a threshing floor, where cattle or donkeys walked over it to separate the seeds from the husks. Then the grain was tossed into the air so that the wind could blow the chaff away. But the farmers did not keep all of it. Scribes from the king or the temples came to measure the fields and count the harvest, and a share was taken as tax. The grain was stored in large granaries, which helped feed the kingdom in years of poor floods. Farmers who could not pay were sometimes punished. Even so, harvest time was also a time of songs, festivals and thanks to the gods.

[long-pause]

{img:11-thebes-street-market-morning}

The harvest was carried in, and the fields grew quiet. A little way along the river, a great city was waking up.

[pause]

There were no coins in Tutankhamun's Egypt, so people traded goods for goods.

[pause]

In the streets and markets of Thebes, people bought and sold all sorts of things, from bread, fish and vegetables to sandals, pots, linen and jewellery. But money as we know it did not yet exist. Instead, people bartered, exchanging one item for another. To make this easier, the value of goods was often measured in units of copper or silver, or in sacks of grain. A pair of sandals might be worth a certain weight of copper, and a goat a little more. Nobody actually had to hand over metal. They simply agreed on a fair exchange, perhaps a jar of oil and some fish for a basket. Records from the time show people carefully noting what they owed and what was owed to them. It sounds complicated, but it worked well for centuries. A morning market in Thebes must have been full of friendly bargaining, as people weighed, compared and argued in the shade of the narrow streets.

[long-pause]

{img:12-nile-harbour-sailing-boats}

Travelling on the Nile was easy: the current carried you north, and the wind carried you south.

[pause]

The Nile flows from south to north, toward the Mediterranean Sea. And in Egypt, the wind usually blows from the north. This happy accident made the river a perfect highway. Boats travelling north simply drifted with the current, and boats travelling south raised their sails and let the wind push them upstream. The Egyptian word for travelling north was written with a picture of a boat without a sail, and the word for travelling south with a boat with its sail raised. Large wooden boats carried grain, stone, cattle, wine and people up and down the kingdom. The harbours of Thebes were busy with sailors, porters and merchants unloading cargo onto the banks. There were few roads in Egypt, because there was no need for them. Almost everything and everyone travelled by water. For the Egyptians, the river was not only a source of life and food, but also the road that held the whole country together.

[long-pause]

{img:13-craftsmen-workshop-jewellery}

Egyptian craftsmen worked in busy workshops, often attached to the temples or the palace.

[pause]

Ancient Egypt was full of skilled craftspeople. There were potters, carpenters, stone carvers, metalworkers, jewellers, weavers, leatherworkers and many more. Many of them worked in workshops belonging to the king or to the great temples, which supplied the materials and fed the workers. Tomb paintings show these workshops in lively detail. A potter spins his wheel, a carpenter shapes a chair with a copper adze, and a jeweller threads tiny beads of gold, carnelian and turquoise. Glass was still quite new and precious in Tutankhamun's time, and Egyptian glassmakers made beautiful blue and yellow vessels. Craftsmen usually learned their trade from their fathers, and skills were passed down through families for generations. Their work was often anonymous. Few of them signed their names. Yet when we look at the objects from Tutankhamun's tomb, with their perfect inlays and delicate carvings, we are looking at the patient work of hundreds of unknown hands.

[long-pause]

{img:14-karnak-temple-gateway-flags}

The temple of Karnak was so large that it took around two thousand years to build.

[pause]

Karnak was the great temple of the god Amun at Thebes. It was not built all at once. Instead, king after king added new halls, gateways, courtyards, statues and obelisks, over a period of about two thousand years. By Tutankhamun's time, it was already enormous, with massive stone gateways called pylons, covered in carved and brightly painted scenes. In front of the gateways stood tall wooden flagpoles, with coloured cloth banners fluttering at the top. Inside lay courtyards and dim halls filled with huge columns. Ordinary people were not allowed into the inner parts of the temple. Only priests could enter the sacred rooms where the statue of the god was kept. Yet the temple was also a busy place, owning land, cattle, workshops and boats, and employing thousands of people. Today, the ruins of Karnak still cover a vast area, and walking among its columns, people often feel very small indeed.

[long-pause]

{img:15-priests-temple-hall-columns}

Egyptian priests shaved their heads and bodies, and washed several times a day, to stay pure for the gods.

[pause]

Priests served the gods in the temples, and they were expected to be very clean. They shaved off all their hair, washed in sacred pools several times a day and wore only fine white linen and simple sandals. Every morning, the most important priests entered the inner shrine, where the statue of the god stood in darkness. They woke the god with hymns, washed and dressed the statue, offered it food and drink, burned incense and sealed the shrine again. Many priests did not serve full time. They worked in shifts, a month at a time, and spent the rest of the year at home with their families and ordinary jobs. There were also priestesses, singers and musicians who served in the temples. The great temple halls were dim and cool, lit only by small windows high in the walls. In the soft light, the painted columns and the smell of incense must have made the place feel truly sacred.

[long-pause]

{img:16-deir-el-medina-village-valley}

The temples belonged to the gods and the priests. But across the river lived the people who made the tombs of kings.

[pause]

The men who built the royal tombs lived in their own small village, hidden in the desert hills.

[pause]

On the west bank at Thebes, in a narrow valley behind the hills, lay a small walled village. Today it is known as Deir el-Medina. In Tutankhamun's time, it was home to the skilled workmen who cut and decorated the royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings. There were stonecutters, plasterers, sculptors, painters and scribes, along with their wives and children. The village had about seventy houses, packed closely along a single main street. It was surrounded by a wall, and the families lived somewhat apart from the rest of Thebes, perhaps to help keep the location of the royal tombs secret. Water, food and supplies were brought to the village by porters and donkeys. The workmen were paid by the state in grain, fish, vegetables, oil and clothing. Because they were well educated, many of the villagers could read and write. This makes Deir el-Medina one of the best documented communities in the whole ancient world.

[long-pause]

{img:17-workers-walking-path-cliffs-dawn}

The tomb builders worked a ten-day week, and they often slept near their work during the week.

[pause]

The workmen of Deir el-Medina had a regular routine. The Egyptian week lasted ten days, and the men usually worked for eight of them, with days off in between and extra holidays for festivals. Each working day, they walked over the hills on a steep path to the Valley of the Kings, carrying their tools and baskets. The path is still there today. The work was divided into two shifts, the left side and the right side of the tomb, each led by a foreman. During the working week, the men often slept in small stone huts near the valley, rather than walking home every night. Records from the village tell us when men were absent and why. Some were ill, some were stung by scorpions, and some had to help with a family event, like a birthday or a funeral. One man was even excused because he was brewing beer. It all sounds surprisingly familiar.

[long-pause]

{img:18-ostracon-scribe-limestone-flake}

Instead of expensive papyrus, the villagers often wrote on smooth flakes of white limestone.

[pause]

Papyrus was costly, so for everyday notes, the people of Deir el-Medina used something they had plenty of. When the workmen cut tombs into the rock, they produced countless smooth, white flakes of limestone. These made excellent writing surfaces. Archaeologists call such pieces ostraca. Thousands of them have been found around the village. They contain all sorts of writing: lists of tools, work records, letters, laundry lists, receipts, legal disputes, prayers, poems, school exercises and even little sketches and jokes. One note asks a neighbour to return a borrowed item. Another records a complaint about a donkey. There are drawings of cats, monkeys and kings, made by artists practising their skills. These small stones are wonderfully personal. Unlike the grand inscriptions on temple walls, they show us ordinary people dealing with ordinary life, writing quick notes to each other, and sometimes grumbling, just as people do today.

[long-pause]

{img:19-painters-tomb-wall-lamplight}

Egyptian tomb painters worked deep underground, by the light of small oil lamps.

[pause]

Decorating a royal tomb was a long and careful process. First, the walls were smoothed and covered with plaster. Then an artist drew the outlines of the scenes in red paint, often using a grid of lines to keep the proportions right. A master artist checked the drawing and corrected mistakes in black. Then the sculptors carved the outlines, and finally the painters filled in the colours. Their paints were made from natural minerals: red and yellow from ochre, white from chalk, black from soot and a brilliant blue from a special pigment made by heating sand, copper and other ingredients. Deep inside the tomb, there was no daylight at all. The painters worked by the light of small lamps burning oil, and it is thought that they added a little salt to the oil so that it would make less smoke. In the soft flickering light, the gods and kings slowly appeared on the walls, in colours that still glow today.

[long-pause]

{img:20-village-evening-families-roofs}

On warm evenings, Egyptian families went up onto their flat roofs to enjoy the cool air.

[pause]

The houses of ancient Egypt had flat roofs, and these were useful spaces. During the day, people dried fish, fruit and grain up there, or stored firewood and fodder. In the evening, when the heat of the day faded, families climbed the stairs or ladders to sit on the roof. The air was cooler, and a breeze often came across the river. Neighbours could call to one another from roof to roof. Children played, and the adults talked about the day. Smoke from the evening cooking fires drifted across the village. On the hottest nights, many families slept on their roofs, on mats under light linen covers, beneath the enormous starry sky. In the village of the tomb builders, the sounds of the evening would have been gentle: a donkey braying, a dog barking, a baby crying, and somewhere, perhaps, a harp or a song. It was a simple pleasure, and it is still enjoyed in many hot countries today.

[long-pause]

{img:21-scribe-seated-papyrus-palette}

Only a small number of ancient Egyptians could read and write, and being a scribe was a respected career.

[pause]

Writing in Egypt used hieroglyphs, small pictures that stood for sounds or ideas, and a faster, flowing script called hieratic for everyday use. Learning them took years. Perhaps only a few people in every hundred could read and write. Those who could became scribes, and they were highly valued. Scribes kept the records of the kingdom. They counted harvests, recorded taxes, wrote letters, copied religious texts and managed the accounts of temples and estates. A scribe sat cross-legged, with a roll of papyrus stretched across his knees. He carried a palette with two little hollows for red and black ink, and reed pens chewed at the end to make a brush. Statues often show scribes in this pose, alert and calm. Egyptian texts praise the scribe's life, saying that he never has to carry heavy loads or work in the mud. It was clean work, it paid well, and it could lead to great positions.

[long-pause]

{img:22-school-boys-writing-boards}

Egyptian schoolboys learned to write by copying old texts again and again.

[pause]

Boys who were training to become scribes usually began their lessons at around the age of five or ten. They studied at schools attached to temples or government offices, or with a scribe who taught a few students. They practised on boards covered with white plaster, which could be wiped clean, and on flakes of limestone. Their main method was copying. They copied lists of words, letters, prayers and famous old texts, again and again, until they knew them by heart. Many of these school exercises survive, sometimes with the teacher's corrections in red ink. Some of the texts the boys copied were written to encourage them. One famous text describes all the hard and dirty jobs in Egypt, from the fisherman to the brick maker, and then says how much better it is to be a scribe. Teachers could be strict. One old text says that a boy's ear is on his back, meaning that he listens when he is beaten.

[long-pause]

{img:23-girls-weaving-mother-courtyard}

Almost all clothing in ancient Egypt was made of linen, spun and woven from the flax plant.

[pause]

Flax grew well in the fields along the Nile, and from its long fibres, the Egyptians made linen. The stalks were soaked, beaten and combed, and then the fibres were spun into thread. Spinning and weaving were often done by women at home, and girls learned from their mothers. In Tutankhamun's time, many households wove cloth on a horizontal loom laid on the ground, while larger workshops used new upright looms. The finest Egyptian linen was so thin that you could almost see through it, and it was highly valued, even used as a form of payment. Most clothes were simple. Men wore a linen kilt wrapped around the waist, and women wore long straight dresses. Wealthy people wore finely pleated garments and fringed shawls. Linen was cool in the heat, and it was usually left white or pale. When a pharaoh was buried, he was wrapped in hundreds of yards of the finest linen his kingdom could make.

[long-pause]

{img:24-children-playing-ball-courtyard}

Egyptian children played with balls, dolls, spinning tops and wooden animals with moving parts.

[pause]

Children in ancient Egypt had toys much like children today. Archaeologists have found leather balls stuffed with straw or reeds, spinning tops, rattles, dolls of wood and cloth and little animals made of clay. Some toys were remarkably clever. One wooden cat has a jaw that opens and closes when a string is pulled, and there are toy animals on little wheels. Paintings show children playing leapfrog, tug of war, ball games and juggling. Girls and boys played together when they were small. Children did not usually wear many clothes in the heat, and young boys and girls often had their heads shaved except for one long lock of hair at the side, called the sidelock of youth. By the age of about ten or twelve, though, most children were already helping their families, working in the fields, the kitchen or the workshop. Childhood was short, but the toys they left behind show that it was also playful.

[long-pause]

{img:25-cat-sleeping-house-niche}

The ancient Egyptians were among the first people in the world to keep cats in their homes.

[pause]

Cats were very welcome in ancient Egypt. They kept mice and rats away from the grain stores, and they hunted snakes and scorpions. But Egyptians also simply liked them. Paintings show cats sitting under their owners' chairs, wearing collars, playing and even helping with bird hunting in the marshes. The Egyptian word for cat was miu, which sounds much like a cat's own voice. Cats were connected with the goddess Bastet, a gentle goddess of the home, of mothers and of joy. In later centuries, millions of cats were mummified and offered to her at her temple. In Tutankhamun's time, a much-loved cat belonging to a prince was even given her own carved stone coffin, with an inscription naming her as the Osiris Tamit, which means she cat. When a family cat died, it is said that the people of the house shaved their eyebrows as a sign of mourning. Few animals have ever been so honoured.

[long-pause]

{img:26-amarna-city-desert-river}

Those children grew up in a world that seemed settled and old. Yet just before Tutankhamun's time, one king turned it upside down.

[pause]

Just before Tutankhamun's time, a pharaoh built a completely new capital city in the empty desert.

[pause]

The pharaoh Akhenaten, who was probably Tutankhamun's father, made one of the most surprising decisions in Egyptian history. He decided to worship one god above all others: the Aten, the shining disc of the sun. To honour the Aten, he built a brand new capital city on an empty stretch of desert beside the Nile, halfway between Thebes and Memphis. He called it Akhetaten, the Horizon of the Aten. Today, the place is known as Amarna. The city was built very quickly, within a few years. It had wide straight streets, palaces, temples, government offices, workshops and neighbourhoods of houses with gardens. Tens of thousands of people moved there from the old cities. It must have been an extraordinary experience, to arrive in a brand new city on a bare desert plain, with fresh white walls and gardens just beginning to grow. Tutankhamun was most likely born there, around 1341 BC.

[long-pause]

{img:27-sun-disk-rays-relief}

The temples of the sun god at Amarna had no roofs, so that the sunlight could fall directly on the altars.

[pause]

Traditional Egyptian temples were dark and mysterious inside, with the god's statue hidden in a shrine at the back. Akhenaten's temples to the Aten were very different. They were open to the sky. The sun itself was the god, so there was no need for a statue. Instead, the temples had wide courtyards filled with hundreds of small stone altars, where offerings of bread, meat, fruit, flowers and incense were laid out in the bright sunlight. Carvings from this time show the Aten as a sun disc with long rays reaching down, each ray ending in a small human hand. Some of the hands hold the sign of life toward the king and queen. Akhenaten wrote a beautiful hymn to the Aten, praising the sun for waking the world each morning and caring for every living thing. Some lines of it are remarkably similar to a psalm in the Bible, written many centuries later. Its tone is gentle and full of wonder.

[long-pause]

{img:28-amarna-palace-garden-pool}

The palaces of Amarna had gardens with pools, flowers and painted floors showing birds and marshes.

[pause]

Archaeologists who excavated Amarna found the remains of lovely palaces and villas. Many had gardens with rectangular pools, surrounded by trees, flowers and shady walkways. Blue lotus flowers floated on the water, and fish swam beneath them. Tall papyrus plants and date palms grew around the edges. Inside the palaces, the walls and even the floors were painted with scenes of nature. One famous painted floor shows a marsh full of reeds, flowers, flying ducks, fish and calves leaping in the fields. It was meant to bring the beauty of the living world indoors. Art from Amarna is more relaxed and natural than older Egyptian art. Plants seem to move in the breeze, and animals look lively and real. For the young prince Tutankhamun, growing up in these surroundings must have been very pleasant. He would have played in sunlit courtyards and shady gardens, with the sounds of water, birds and music all around him.

[long-pause]

{img:29-royal-family-sun-rays-art}

Art from Amarna shows the royal family in tender, everyday moments, something never seen before in Egypt.

[pause]

For many centuries, Egyptian kings had been shown as stiff, powerful and godlike. But at Amarna, the artists did something new. They showed Akhenaten and his queen, Nefertiti, with their daughters in warm, private moments. In one carving, the king kisses one of his little girls. In another, the queen holds a daughter on her lap, while another child climbs on her shoulder. The family sits together under the rays of the sun disc, relaxed and affectionate. Even the bodies of the king and queen were shown in a new way, with long faces, slender necks and rounded bellies. Nobody knows exactly why. It may have been a new religious style. Nefertiti herself became famous for her beauty, and a painted bust of her, found at Amarna, is one of the best-known works of art in the world. Tutankhamun's future wife, Ankhesenpaaten, was one of the daughters in these tender family scenes.

[long-pause]

{img:30-palace-nursery-baby-nurse}

We know the name of Tutankhamun's wet nurse, because her tomb was discovered in 1997.

[pause]

Royal children in ancient Egypt were often cared for by nurses, who fed and looked after them in the palace. Being the nurse of a future king was a great honour, and it brought respect and rewards for life. In 1997, archaeologists working at Saqqara, near Memphis, found the tomb of a woman named Maia. Inscriptions in her tomb call her the wet nurse of the king, the one who fed the body of the god. And one carving shows her holding a young king on her lap, almost certainly Tutankhamun himself. Maia was clearly a woman of importance, with a beautifully decorated tomb. We know nothing about her personality, of course. Yet it is touching to think of her holding a small boy in a cool palace room, long before he became king. It reminds us that even pharaohs began life as babies, who needed to be fed, comforted and rocked to sleep by someone who cared for them.

[long-pause]

{img:31-amarna-workers-village-evening}

Even when the king worshipped only the sun disc, many ordinary Egyptians quietly kept their old household gods.

[pause]

Akhenaten's new religion was centred on the royal family and the Aten. But for ordinary people, especially those living outside the court, the change may not have gone very deep. Archaeologists working in the workmen's village at Amarna found small amulets and figures of traditional household gods. There was Bes, a cheerful dwarf god who protected mothers and children, and Taweret, a goddess shaped like a hippopotamus, who watched over childbirth. People kept small shrines in their homes and prayed for health, safe births and good luck, much as their parents and grandparents had done. Official religion might change by royal command, but the habits of families changed more slowly. It is a gentle reminder that in every age, most people's faith has been personal and practical. They prayed for the things that mattered most to them: their children, their health and a good night's sleep in a safe home.

[long-pause]

{img:32-boy-king-coronation-temple}

The city of the sun disc was emptying. And on the throne of Egypt sat a child.

[pause]

Tutankhamun became king of Egypt when he was only about nine years old.

[pause]

Around the year 1332 before Christ, a young boy was crowned king of the Two Lands. At first, his name was Tutankhaten, which means the living image of the Aten, the sun disc his family had worshipped. He was probably a son of Akhenaten, the king who had turned Egypt's religion upside down. Now that king was gone, and the throne passed to a child. At the coronation, the boy would have worn fine white linen and a broad golden collar. Priests placed crowns on his head and put the crook and the flail in his small hands, the old signs of a king who guides and protects his people. Of course, a nine-year-old could not rule a great kingdom alone. Experienced officials stood close beside him and made most of the decisions. Yet in the eyes of the Egyptians, he was now something more than a boy. He was the living link between the gods and the people, and the whole land depended on him.

[long-pause]

{img:33-ay-elderly-official-court}

One of the most powerful men at the young king's side was an old courtier called Ay.

[pause]

Ay had served at the royal court for many years, perhaps since the days of Akhenaten's father. He carried a curious title: God's Father. Nobody is completely sure what it meant, but it showed that he was very close to the royal family. Some historians think he may even have been a relative of Queen Nefertiti. When the boy king came to the throne, Ay was already an old man by the standards of his time. He knew how the palace worked, which officials could be trusted, and how the great temples were run. Along with the army commander Horemheb, he helped to guide the kingdom while the king was young. We cannot hear their conversations, and we do not know how much the boy listened to them. Yet the decisions made in those years carry their fingerprints. Ay would stay close to the throne until the very end of Tutankhamun's life, and, as we will see, a little beyond it.

[long-pause]

{img:34-treasurer-maya-scribe-office}

The man in charge of Tutankhamun's treasury left him a gift that was found in the tomb.

[pause]

His name was Maya, and he was the overseer of the treasury. That meant he looked after the king's wealth: the gold, the linen, the grain, the oil and the many goods that flowed into the royal storerooms. It was a job that needed patience and careful accounts, kept by teams of scribes on papyrus rolls. Maya also organised building work, including repairs in the royal cemeteries. When the young king died, Maya did something personal. He gave a small wooden figure of the king lying on a funeral bed, and inscribed it with his own name as a gift. Another of his gifts was a lifelike figure of the king to serve him in the afterlife. Both were placed in the tomb. More than three thousand years later, Maya's own beautiful tomb was rediscovered at Saqqara, near Memphis, in 1986. It is a quiet thought. A careful official, who spent his life counting treasures, chose to leave a small one of his own.

[long-pause]

{img:35-abandoned-city-sand-drifting}

Within a few years of the young king's reign, a whole royal city was left empty.

[pause]

Akhenaten had built a brand new capital in the middle of Egypt, on a wide plain beside the Nile. Today we call it Amarna. It had palaces, temples open to the sky, wide roads, gardens and thousands of houses. But it had been built quickly, for one king and one religion. When that king died, the city lost its purpose. Under Tutankhamun, the court moved away, and the people slowly followed. Craftsmen packed their tools. Families loaded their belongings onto donkeys. Officials left their fine villas with their painted walls. In time, the empty rooms filled with sand, blown in by the desert wind. Because nobody built a new town on top of it, Amarna stayed almost untouched for more than three thousand years. For archaeologists, this is a rare gift. They can walk along its streets and see the outline of a whole city, frozen at the moment its people walked away.

[long-pause]

{img:36-memphis-palace-river-pyramids}

The young king's court moved to Memphis, an ancient city that already had pyramids on its horizon.

[pause]

Memphis lay near the point where the Nile valley opens out into the wide green delta. It was one of the oldest cities in Egypt, already more than a thousand years old in Tutankhamun's time. From its palaces and gardens, you could see the great pyramids of Giza and Saqqara standing on the desert edge. To the young king, those pyramids would have looked as ancient as the castles of the Middle Ages look to us. Memphis was a busy city of harbours, workshops, temples and government offices. Ships arrived with timber, copper, wine and oil from distant lands. Many of the kingdom's most important officials lived there. Moving the court to Memphis was a sign that Egypt was turning back to its old ways and its old centres of power. Thebes in the south stayed the religious heart of the land. But much of the day-to-day running of the kingdom now happened here, in the shadow of the oldest monuments on Earth.

[long-pause]

{img:37-young-king-chariot-desert-hunt}

Six chariots were found in Tutankhamun's tomb, taken apart so they would fit through the doors.

[pause]

Chariots were the sports cars of the ancient world. They were light, fast and beautifully made, with two wheels and a small platform for the driver. Tutankhamun owned several of them. Some were covered in gold and decorated for ceremonies. Others were simpler, made for hunting or for driving across the desert. Hunting was a favourite pastime of Egyptian kings. They chased gazelles, hares, wild bulls and ostriches, often with dogs running beside the wheels. One fan from the tomb once held ostrich feathers, and its handle tells us that the king caught the birds himself in the desert east of the city of Heliopolis. The feathers have mostly crumbled away. Yet the little inscription survives, like a note left behind after a good day out. When the king was buried, his chariots were carefully taken apart and placed in the tomb in pieces, ready for him to drive again in the fields of the afterlife.

[long-pause]

{img:38-queen-ankhesenamun-palace-garden}

Tutankhamun's queen was a daughter of Akhenaten and Nefertiti.

[pause]

Her name was Ankhesenamun. Like her husband, she had been given a different name at birth: Ankhesenpaaten, which honoured the sun disc. When the court turned back to the old gods, both of them changed their names to honour Amun instead. She was probably a few years older than the king, and she had grown up in the palaces of Amarna, among her many sisters. In ancient Egypt, it was not unusual for royal brothers and sisters, or half-brothers and half-sisters, to marry. It kept the royal line close and protected. To us, this seems very strange, but at the time it was seen as fitting for a family that was believed to be close to the gods. Ankhesenamun appears beside the king in several of the treasures from his tomb. In these scenes, she is always graceful, calm and close to him. It is one of the few royal marriages from ancient Egypt that still feels quietly personal to us.

[long-pause]

{img:39-golden-throne-queen-anointing}

On the back of Tutankhamun's golden throne, his queen is shown gently rubbing perfume onto his shoulder.

[pause]

The golden throne is one of the most famous objects from the tomb. It is made of wood covered in gold and silver, inlaid with coloured glass, stone and faience. But the most remarkable thing about it is a small scene on the backrest. The young king sits relaxed on a cushioned chair. His queen stands in front of him. In one hand she holds a little cup, and with the other she gently touches his shoulder, as if smoothing perfumed oil onto his skin. Above them, the sun disc shines down, its rays ending in tiny hands. It is a soft, private moment, quite unlike the usual images of kings striding into battle. The scene is in the relaxed style of Amarna, where the royal family was often shown in tender, everyday poses. Most royal art was meant to impress. This picture seems meant to remember a feeling.

[long-pause]

{img:40-palace-hall-painted-floor}

In Egyptian palaces of this time, even the floors were painted like a garden pond.

[pause]

Archaeologists working at royal palaces of this period found the remains of painted plaster floors. Instead of plain stone or tiles, the floors showed a pool of water, full of fish and lotus flowers, surrounded by reeds where ducks and birds flew up. Walking across such a floor must have felt like stepping into a cool marsh on a hot day. The walls were painted too, with plants, birds and patterns in bright blue, green, red and yellow. Wooden columns were carved and painted to look like bundles of papyrus. High windows with stone grilles let in light and air, while keeping out the worst of the sun. Servants stood with long fans made of ostrich feathers to move the warm air around. For the Egyptians, the marsh was a place of new life, where the world had first risen out of the water. To live inside a painted marsh was to live inside a calm, endless beginning.

[long-pause]

{img:41-musicians-harp-lute-banquet}

Two trumpets from Tutankhamun's tomb were played on the radio in 1939, and millions of people heard them.

[pause]

Music filled Egyptian life. At banquets, women played harps, lutes and double flutes, while singers performed songs about love, wine and the shortness of life. Guests wore garlands of flowers and sat on low chairs, listening in the soft light of oil lamps. Among the treasures in Tutankhamun's tomb were two trumpets, one made of silver and one of bronze, each with a wooden core to protect its shape. They were probably used in ceremonies or for the army. In 1939, more than three thousand years after they were last heard, a musician played them in a broadcast from the Cairo Museum. Their sound was loud, rough and strange, very different from modern brass instruments. Some people later claimed that the trumpets brought bad luck, but that is only a story. What remains is a recording of a sound from the time of the pharaohs, crackling through the air into living rooms around the world.

[long-pause]

{img:42-royal-couple-boat-marsh-lotus}

A small golden shrine from the tomb shows the king and queen together in a boat, hunting birds in the marshes.

[pause]

This shrine is only about half a metre tall, made of wood and covered in thin gold. Every side is decorated with little scenes of the royal couple. In one, the king sits on a chair with his bow, shooting at birds, while the queen sits at his feet and hands him an arrow. In another, she ties a necklace around his neck. In others, they glide through the marshes in a light boat. The king holds a curved throwing stick, the Egyptian way of hunting ducks, and the queen holds a lotus flower. Real throwing sticks were found in the tomb too, some of them gilded. The scenes on the shrine are full of small, warm gestures, a hand on an arm, a flower offered, a look exchanged. They were probably meant to show the queen's role in keeping the king alive and well. Yet to us, they look like pages from a family album, left in gold.

[long-pause]

{img:43-palace-terrace-sunset-couple}

In Tutankhamun's tomb lay two tiny coffins, holding the daughters he and his queen had lost.

[pause]

The young royal couple did not have any children who survived. When archaeologists explored the tomb, they found a plain wooden box in the treasury. Inside were two small coffins, each holding the mummy of a baby girl. One was born far too early. The other was born closer to term, but did not live. DNA studies suggest that they were most likely the daughters of Tutankhamun himself. The babies had been carefully preserved and wrapped, just like adults, and placed beside their father for the journey into the afterlife. They had no names, as far as we know. It is a sad discovery, but it is also a tender one. It shows that, even in a world of golden masks and great ceremonies, a young king and queen grieved for their children like any other parents. They kept them close. And they made sure that wherever the king was going, his little daughters would go with him.

[long-pause]

{img:44-opet-festival-crowds-avenue}

Inside the palace, life was private and quiet. But once a year, the whole of Thebes came out to celebrate.

[pause]

Once a year, the gods of Thebes went on a holiday, and the whole city came out to watch.

[pause]

The Opet Festival took place during the season of the flood, when the fields were under water and there was little farm work to do. It celebrated the god Amun, who travelled from his great temple at Karnak to the temple at Luxor, a little further south. There, his strength and the strength of the king were renewed for another year. For ordinary people, it was one of the happiest times of the year. Crowds lined the processional route, dressed in their best linen. There was music, dancing, singing and food. In early times, the festival lasted about eleven days. Later, it grew to almost a month. Normally, the statues of the gods were hidden away in dark sanctuaries, where only priests could see them. During Opet, the gods came out into the light, carried among the people. For a few days, the great and the ordinary shared the same streets and the same joy.

[long-pause]

{img:45-sacred-barque-priests-shoulders}

The gods of Egypt travelled by boat, even when they were carried over dry land.

[pause]

In Egyptian belief, the gods moved through the heavens in boats, just as people moved along the Nile. So when a god's statue left its temple for a festival, it travelled in a sacred boat. These boats were made of wood covered in gold, with a small closed shrine in the middle where the statue sat, hidden behind a veil. Priests carried the boat on long poles resting on their shoulders. Sometimes there were twenty or thirty of them, walking slowly in step. People along the route could ask the god questions, and the movement of the boat was taken as an answer. If it seemed to lean forward, the answer was yes. If it pulled back, the answer was no. This way of asking the gods was called an oracle. It was used to settle quarrels, find lost things and even choose officials. For a moment, an ordinary person could stand before a god and receive a reply.

[long-pause]

{img:46-temple-singers-sistrum-procession}

Egyptian temple singers used a rattle called a sistrum, which sounded like wind in the papyrus reeds.

[pause]

The sistrum was a small bronze instrument with a handle and a loop of metal. Thin rods ran across the loop, with little metal discs on them. When it was shaken, the discs jingled with a soft, shimmering sound. The Egyptians said it sounded like the rustling of papyrus in the marsh. It was especially linked to the goddess Hathor, the goddess of music, love and joy. During processions, women from important families served as temple singers. They walked in rows, shaking their sistrums and singing hymns, while drummers and clapper players kept the rhythm. Incense smoke drifted through the air. People along the road knelt or raised their hands as the gods passed by. Several sistrums have survived from ancient Egypt, and some can still be played. Their sound is gentle and bright at the same time. It is easy to imagine it rising above a crowd on a warm festival morning, long ago.

[long-pause]

{img:47-golden-barge-towed-river}

During the festival, the god's golden boat was placed on a real river barge and pulled upstream by teams of men.

[pause]

For part of the Opet journey, the sacred boats travelled on the Nile itself. They were placed on great river barges, covered in gold and decorated with carved heads of rams, the sacred animal of Amun. Because the festival boats were travelling south, against the current, they could not simply drift. Teams of men walked along the bank, pulling the barges with long ropes. Soldiers marched beside them. Musicians played, dancers leapt and people cheered. Some walls at Luxor temple show these scenes in great detail: the barges, the ropes, the crowds, even food and drink being handed out. It must have been a long, hot and noisy day. Yet the pulling was also an honour. To help carry a god home was something you might remember for the rest of your life. And for a farmer whose fields lay under the flood, there were few better ways to spend a summer day.

[long-pause]

{img:48-luxor-temple-colonnade-torches}

The walls of Luxor temple were decorated under Tutankhamun, but later another king put his own name over his.

[pause]

At Luxor temple, there is a long hall of enormous stone columns, shaped like open papyrus plants. It was begun by Tutankhamun's grandfather, Amenhotep the Third. During Tutankhamun's reign, artists covered its walls with scenes of the Opet Festival: boats, priests, musicians, dancers and crowds. It was a way of showing that the old festivals were back after the years of Akhenaten. But Tutankhamun's name is not easy to find there today. Some years after his death, a later king, Horemheb, had his own name carved over many of the young king's inscriptions. This was common practice. Kings often took credit for the work of others. Yet the scenes themselves were left in place. The singers, the dancers and the rowers still move along the walls, just as Tutankhamun's artists drew them. In the evening, when the stone glows in the last light, they seem almost ready to start the festival again.

[long-pause]

{img:49-riverbank-night-families-lamps}

At festival time, the temples gave out bread and beer to the crowds.

[pause]

Egyptian temples were not only places of prayer. They were also huge storehouses, with fields, herds, workshops and granaries of their own. Every day, food was offered to the gods. When the gods had finished with it, so to speak, the offerings were shared out among the priests and temple staff. During great festivals, much more was given away. Records from various periods mention thousands of loaves of bread, cakes, jars of beer, meat and fruit, handed out to the people who came to celebrate. Beer in ancient Egypt was thick, mild and nourishing, more like a sort of liquid bread than modern beer. After a long day of processions, families would sit together on the riverbank, eating and talking as the sun went down. Small oil lamps glowed on the boats and along the water's edge. Children fell asleep in their parents' laps. Above them, the stars came out over Thebes, one by one.

[long-pause]

{img:50-winter-nile-fields-green-morning}

After the festival, the flood went down, and the year turned towards the growing season.

[pause]

The Egyptian winter was the season of growing, when the whole valley turned green.

[pause]

When the flood went down in the autumn, the farmers sowed their seed in the damp black earth. Then came the season the Egyptians called Peret, which means coming forth. It ran through our winter months. The days were mild and sunny, and the nights could be surprisingly cold. Farmers sometimes wrapped themselves in woollen or heavy linen cloaks in the early morning. In the fields, the wheat and barley grew tall and green. Flax, used to make linen, came into blue flower. Vegetables such as onions, lentils, cucumbers and lettuce grew in small irrigated gardens. Water was lifted from canals with a simple device called a shaduf, a long pole with a bucket on one end and a weight on the other. For a few months, the valley was at its most beautiful. A bright green ribbon ran through the desert, from the far south all the way to the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:51-scribes-counting-grain-granary}

In ancient Egypt, taxes were paid not in money, but in grain.

[pause]

There were no coins in Tutankhamun's Egypt. Instead, value was measured in grain, metal and goods. When the harvest came in, officials and scribes went out to the villages to measure the fields and count the crops. Each farmer had to hand over a share to the state or to the temple that owned the land. The grain was carried to great granaries, mud-brick buildings with tall domed silos, where it was stored for times of need. Scribes sat beside the doors with their palettes and papyrus rolls, writing down every sack. Being a scribe was a respected job, and boys who learned to read and write had a good chance of a comfortable life. One old Egyptian text tells young students to be scribes, because a scribe does not have to carry heavy loads or dig in the fields. It may sound a little proud. But it also shows how much the kingdom depended on careful counting.

[long-pause]

{img:52-young-king-walking-stick-garden}

More than one hundred walking sticks and staffs were found in Tutankhamun's tomb.

[pause]

Walking sticks were common among important men in ancient Egypt. They were a sign of rank and dignity, a little like a gentleman's cane in later times. Yet the number found with Tutankhamun is unusual. There were around one hundred and thirty sticks and staffs, some plain, some carved with figures, some covered in gold. A few seem to have been well used. When scientists studied the king's mummy using scans, some of them noticed signs of a problem in his left foot, which may have made walking painful. Others are not so sure, and the debate continues. Some paintings and carvings show the king seated while doing things that kings were usually shown doing standing up, like shooting arrows. Perhaps he needed a stick for support. Perhaps he simply liked them. We cannot ask him. Yet it is easy to picture a young man walking slowly through a quiet garden, leaning on a favourite stick.

[long-pause]

{img:53-palace-room-lamps-night-quiet}

Nobody knows for certain how Tutankhamun died.

[pause]

Tutankhamun died young, at about eighteen or nineteen years old, after reigning for around nine years. There is no written record that explains what happened. So, over the past century, people have suggested many possible causes. When his mummy was first examined, a hole in the skull led some to imagine a blow to the head. Later scans showed that this damage probably happened during mummification or when the mummy was studied in the 1920s. A break in his left leg, just above the knee, may have happened shortly before his death, perhaps in a fall or an accident. DNA tests found signs of malaria. Each of these, alone or together, could have been serious in a time without modern medicine. Yet none of them gives a final answer. Perhaps, in a quiet palace room, by the light of small oil lamps, doctors and priests did everything they knew how to do. And then the young king was gone.

[long-pause]

{img:54-mourning-women-palace-courtyard}

The palace fell silent. Now began the long, careful preparations for a royal burial.

[pause]

When an Egyptian king died, the official mourning lasted around seventy days.

[pause]

The death of a king was a moment of great uncertainty. The pharaoh was the one who kept order in the world, who held back chaos and pleased the gods. Now that order had to be passed on. Within the palace, people mourned openly. In paintings of funerals, women are shown with their hair loose and dishevelled, raising their arms and throwing dust over their heads as a sign of grief. They wore plain clothing, without jewellery. Men might stop shaving for a while. Meanwhile, the long process of preparing the king's body began. It usually took around seventy days, from death to burial. During this time, the new ruler had to be confirmed, the tomb made ready and the grave goods gathered. For Ankhesenamun, the young widow, these must have been strange and lonely weeks. She had lost her husband, her children and, in a way, her place in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:55-dusk-thebes-western-cliffs}

Tutankhamun's tomb is surprisingly small for a king, and it may have been meant for someone else.

[pause]

Egyptian kings usually began building their tombs early in their reigns. These royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings were long corridors, cut deep into the rock, with many rooms and painted walls. But Tutankhamun died young and unexpectedly. If a grand tomb had been started for him, it was not finished in time. So a much smaller tomb was used instead. It has only four little rooms and a short staircase. Many experts believe it was first cut for a high official, perhaps even for Ay, and then quickly adapted for the king. In the end, a larger tomb in the western part of the valley, which some think was begun for Tutankhamun, became the tomb of Ay himself. It is a curious exchange. The young king, whose name would one day be known all over the world, was buried in one of the smallest royal tombs in the whole valley.

[long-pause]

{img:56-embalmers-tent-west-bank}

Egyptian embalmers began their work in a tent on the desert edge, called the tent of purification.

[pause]

Making a mummy was a sacred task, carried out by specially trained priests. The work began on the west bank, near the desert, in a light structure of reed and linen sometimes called the tent of purification. There, the body was washed with water from the Nile and with natron, a natural salt, to cleanse it. Then it was taken to a workshop for the main part of the process. The embalmers removed the internal organs, which would otherwise decay. These were dried and stored in special containers, called canopic jars. In Tutankhamun's case, they were placed in four small golden coffins, each one a miniature copy of his own coffin. The heart was usually left in the body, because the Egyptians believed it was the seat of thought and memory. One priest wore a mask of Anubis, the jackal-headed god of embalming. It was a slow, careful and quiet process, full of prayers.

[long-pause]

{img:57-natron-salt-linen-rolls}

More than one hundred and forty precious objects were hidden inside Tutankhamun's linen wrappings.

[pause]

After the body had been cleaned, it was covered with natron salt and left to dry for around forty days. The natron drew out the moisture and stopped decay. Then the body was cleaned again, rubbed with oils and resins, and wrapped in long strips of fine linen. This could take many days, with prayers spoken as each layer was added. Between the layers, the embalmers placed amulets and jewels to protect the king on his journey. When Tutankhamun's mummy was unwrapped in 1925, archaeologists found more than one hundred and forty objects tucked inside: golden collars, rings, bracelets, amulets in the shape of gods and sacred symbols, and two daggers, one of them made of iron from a meteorite. Each item had a place and a purpose. Some protected the throat, some the heart, some the hands. Together, they formed a kind of invisible armour, made of gold, stone and magic, wrapped quietly around a young man.

[long-pause]

{img:58-goldsmiths-workshop-mask-making}

Tutankhamun's innermost coffin is made of solid gold and weighs about as much as a grown man.

[pause]

The king was laid to rest inside three coffins, one inside the other, like a set of nesting dolls. The two outer coffins are made of wood, covered in gold. The innermost one is made of solid gold, about two and a half to three and a half millimetres thick. It weighs around one hundred and ten kilograms. Over the king's head and shoulders lay the famous golden mask, made from two sheets of gold joined together, and weighing about ten kilograms. It is inlaid with blue glass and stones, including lapis lazuli, which came all the way from the mountains of Afghanistan. Making these objects took the work of skilled goldsmiths, who hammered, shaped, polished and inlaid the metal with simple tools and great patience. Gold was precious to the Egyptians for a special reason. It never tarnishes. They called it the flesh of the gods. To be covered in gold was to share, in a small way, in their eternal life.

[long-pause]

{img:59-valley-of-kings-workers-small-tomb}

Only one room in Tutankhamun's tomb has painted walls.

[pause]

Most royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings are richly decorated. Their long corridors are covered with scenes and texts from sacred books, guiding the king through the twelve hours of the night. Tutankhamun's tomb is different. The staircase, the entrance corridor and three of the four rooms have plain, rough walls. Only the burial chamber, where the king's coffins were placed, was painted. Its walls show the funeral procession, the king's successor performing a ritual, the king meeting the gods, and twelve baboons that stand for the twelve hours of the night. The painting seems to have been done quickly. Small spots of mould appeared on the walls not long after the tomb was closed, which suggests the plaster was still damp. Everything points to a rushed burial. Yet within those few months, the craftsmen still managed to make one small room glow with golden-yellow walls, gods and quiet promises of a new dawn.

[long-pause]

{img:60-storeroom-chests-chariots-packed}

Some of the treasures in Tutankhamun's tomb were originally made for other members of his family.

[pause]

When archaeologists studied the objects more closely, they noticed something unexpected. On a few items, the names had been changed. Under Tutankhamun's name, faint traces of another name could sometimes be seen. Some of the small golden coffins for his organs, for example, seem first to have been made for an earlier ruler, possibly a mysterious king or queen who reigned briefly before him. Other objects, such as boxes and pieces of jewellery, seem to have come from the family's stores. Because the king's death was sudden, his officials had to gather everything needed for a royal burial in only a few months. So they took what was available, changed the names where necessary, and packed it all into the tomb. More than five thousand objects went in, from golden shrines to boxes of food. To us, it looks like unimaginable wealth. Yet for a pharaoh, this may have been a rather modest, hurried burial.

[long-pause]

{img:61-cornflower-wreath-palace-window}

The flowers buried with Tutankhamun tell us that his funeral took place in the spring.

[pause]

Many flowers and plants were found in the tomb. There were wreaths, collars and bouquets made of leaves, berries, petals and fruits. Botanists who studied them noticed something useful. Among them were blue cornflowers and the yellow fruits of the mandrake plant. In Egypt, these ripen in March and April. So the funeral most probably took place in the early spring. That fits well with around seventy days of embalming after a death in winter. The flowers had been carefully picked, sorted and threaded together by hand. Some of them were woven into collars that were worn by mourners at the funeral feast. Others were placed on the coffins. Pollen and plant remains like these are fragile, yet in the dry darkness of the tomb, they survived for more than three thousand years. It is a small and gentle detail. Someone, one spring morning, went out to gather flowers for a young king.

[long-pause]

{img:62-funeral-boats-crossing-nile-dawn}

When the seventy days were over, everything was ready. On a spring morning, the young king began his last journey.

[pause]

An Egyptian funeral began with a journey across the river, from the land of the living to the land of the dead.

[pause]

The cities and palaces of Thebes lay mostly on the east bank of the Nile, where the sun rises. The tombs lay on the west bank, where it sets. So a funeral began with a crossing. The coffin was carried down to the water and placed on a boat, often decorated with flowers and linen. Priests, family members and mourners followed in other boats. The crossing was short, but it carried deep meaning. It echoed the journey of the sun, and the journey every soul would make from this life into the next. Scenes in Egyptian tombs show these funeral boats in great detail, with oarsmen rowing steadily and mourners kneeling on the decks. Herons and egrets rose from the reeds as the boats passed. On the far side, the golden cliffs of the west bank waited in the morning light. For the young king, this was the last time he would cross the river that had shaped his whole kingdom.

[long-pause]

{img:63-mourners-white-linen-riverbank}

Some Egyptian families hired professional mourners to weep at their funerals.

[pause]

In ancient Egypt, a funeral was not meant to be quiet. Grief was shown openly, with loud cries, raised arms and tears. Women from the family led the mourning. But wealthy families often hired professional mourners as well, women who were skilled at weeping and lamenting. They walked beside the coffin, wailing, beating their chests and throwing dust over their heads. In tomb paintings, they are often shown in long dresses of pale blue or grey linen, with their hair loose and their arms raised high. Some of the paintings even include the words of their songs. They call out to the dead person, asking where they are going, and telling them how much they will be missed. To us, paid mourners may sound strange. Yet for the Egyptians, a loud and crowded funeral was a sign of love and respect. The louder the mourning, the more the dead person had been valued by those left behind.

[long-pause]

{img:64-sledge-pulled-by-officials-desert}

A painting in Tutankhamun's tomb shows twelve of his highest officials pulling his coffin on a sledge.

[pause]

On the east wall of the burial chamber, there is a scene of the funeral procession. The king's mummy lies inside a shrine on a wooden sledge, shaped like a boat. In front of it walk twelve men, pulling the sledge with ropes. They are not servants. They are the most important officials in the kingdom, dressed in white linen, with white bands tied around their foreheads as a sign of mourning. Two of them have shaved heads and wear the special clothing of viziers, the highest officials below the king. An inscription above them records the words they spoke as they pulled. They call out to the king, wishing him a safe journey in peace. It is one of the very few scenes of a royal funeral that has survived from ancient Egypt. And it shows something touching. In the end, it was the king's closest servants who brought him, step by step, across the sand to his resting place.

[long-pause]

{img:65-valley-of-kings-procession-sunlight}

Above the Valley of the Kings rises a mountain peak shaped like a natural pyramid.

[pause]

The Valley of the Kings lies in the desert hills on the west bank at Thebes. It is a dry, narrow valley, with steep golden walls of limestone. Above it rises a peak which the Egyptians called the Summit. From many places in the valley, it looks almost exactly like a pyramid. The great pyramids of earlier kings had been easy to see, and easy to rob. So, around five hundred years before Tutankhamun, Egyptian kings began to be buried in hidden tombs, cut into the rock of this quiet valley instead. The mountain above may have been part of the reason they chose it. In a way, every king buried here still lay beneath a pyramid, only this one had been made by nature. The valley was guarded, and only certain people were allowed to enter. Funeral processions climbed slowly along its stony paths, carrying furniture, chests and offerings, on their way to a door in the rock.

[long-pause]

{img:66-ay-leopard-skin-ceremony}

On the wall of Tutankhamun's burial chamber, the next king is painted performing his funeral ritual.

[pause]

One of the most important ceremonies of an Egyptian funeral was called the Opening of the Mouth. A priest touched the face of the mummy, or of a statue, with special tools. This was believed to restore the senses, so that the dead person could eat, drink, see and speak again in the afterlife. Traditionally, the ritual was performed by the heir, the person who would take over from the one who had died. On the north wall of Tutankhamun's burial chamber, this ceremony is shown. The priest wears a leopard skin over his linen, and on his head is the crown of a king. He is Ay, the old courtier, now pharaoh himself. It is very unusual for a successor to be shown like this in a royal tomb. By painting the scene, Ay made his claim to the throne clear for all eternity. Yet it also shows something simpler. An old man who had known the boy for years was there at the very end.

[long-pause]

{img:67-funeral-feast-desert-evening}

Leftovers from Tutankhamun's funeral feast were found in 1907, years before his tomb was discovered.

[pause]

In 1907, an American explorer named Theodore Davis dug in the Valley of the Kings and found a small pit filled with large clay jars. Inside, there was a strange collection of things. There were bags of natron salt, scraps of linen, broken pottery, bones from ducks and geese, wine jars, and collars made of leaves and flowers. Some items carried the name of Tutankhamun. At the time, nobody quite understood what they were. Later, experts realised that these were the remains of the embalming materials and the funeral meal. After the burial, the mourners had shared food and drink near the tomb, wearing flower collars. When they had finished, everything they used was carefully gathered up and buried, because it had touched something sacred. A little later, Davis found a small pit with some gold foil nearby, decided that this had been the king's tomb, and announced that the valley had nothing more to give. In fact, the real tomb lay only a short walk away, waiting under the sand.

[long-pause]

{img:68-widowed-queen-palace-window}

The tomb was sealed, and the mourners went home. But for those left behind, the story was not quite over.

[pause]

After Tutankhamun died, his young widow seems almost to disappear from history.

[pause]

Ankhesenamun was probably in her early twenties when her husband died. She had been born a princess of Amarna, married a king, lost two children and now lost her husband too. What happened to her next is one of the quiet mysteries of ancient Egypt. A ring has been found that shows her name next to the name of Ay, the old courtier who became king. Some historians think this means she married him, perhaps to give him a stronger claim to the throne. Others think the ring only shows that they ruled at the same time. After that, her name fades from the records. We do not know when she died or where she was buried. Some have searched for her tomb in the Valley of the Kings, and the search goes on. It is a strange thing. We know her face from golden shrines and painted thrones. Yet the rest of her life is hidden from us, like a page that has been torn out.

[long-pause]

{img:69-hittite-king-mountain-palace}

An Egyptian queen once wrote to a foreign king, asking him to send one of his sons to be her husband.

[pause]

Far to the north, in the mountains of what is now Turkey, lay the kingdom of the Hittites. They were one of the great powers of the age, and often rivals of Egypt. Their records mention an extraordinary letter that arrived around this time. It came from the widow of an Egyptian king. The Hittite texts call her Dahamunzu, which is probably not a name at all, but a Hittite way of writing the Egyptian words for the king's wife. She wrote that her husband had died, that she had no son, and that she did not wish to marry one of her servants. She asked the Hittite king to send one of his sons, so that he could become her husband and king of Egypt. The Hittite king was astonished. Nothing like this had ever happened before. Many historians believe the letter came from Ankhesenamun. If so, it shows a young woman, alone in a palace, trying to find her own way forward.

[long-pause]

{img:70-desert-road-travellers-caravan}

A Hittite prince set out for Egypt to marry the widowed queen, but he never arrived.

[pause]

The Hittite king did not believe the letter at first. He sent a messenger to Egypt to find out whether it was true. The queen replied, a little hurt, asking why he doubted her. In the end, he agreed. He chose one of his sons, a prince named Zannanza, and sent him south with a small company of travellers, horses and servants. The journey took them across mountains, through the dry hills of Syria and along the long roads towards Egypt. But somewhere on the way, the prince died. The Hittite records say that he was killed, and his father blamed the Egyptians. We do not know exactly what happened. Perhaps he fell ill. Perhaps someone at the Egyptian court did not want a foreign prince on the throne. The Hittite king was furious, and war followed. As for the queen, she was left waiting. Her plan had failed, somewhere on a dusty road far from home.

[long-pause]

{img:71-stonemason-chiselling-name-temple}

Later kings tried to erase Tutankhamun's name from history, and they almost succeeded.

[pause]

After Ay's short reign, the army commander Horemheb became king. He and the kings who followed him wanted to forget the whole Amarna period. Akhenaten was seen as a heretic, and the rulers connected with him were treated as if they had never existed. Stonemasons went from temple to temple, chiselling out names and carving new ones in their place. On some monuments, Tutankhamun's name was replaced with Horemheb's. Later, when official lists of kings were carved on temple walls, Tutankhamun was simply left out, along with Akhenaten, Ay and the others of that time. For many centuries, he was almost forgotten. Even the ancient Egyptians who came later hardly knew his name. Yet this forgetting turned out to be a kind of protection. A king nobody remembered was a king whose tomb nobody went looking for. And so, quietly, under the sand of the valley, his treasures waited.

[long-pause]

{img:72-officials-resealing-tomb-torch}

Tutankhamun's tomb was broken into by robbers at least once, not long after the funeral.

[pause]

When archaeologists entered the tomb in 1922, they found signs that someone had been there before them. A tunnel had been dug through the rubble of the entrance corridor and later filled in. Doorways had been broken open and sealed again. Inside, boxes were in disorder, and some objects lay where they had been dropped. The robbers seem to have been after things that were easy to carry and sell: precious oils and perfumes, linen, and small objects of metal. One of them apparently wrapped a handful of gold rings in a scarf and left them behind, perhaps because they were disturbed. Afterwards, officials came to tidy up. They pushed things back into boxes, sometimes the wrong ones, and resealed the doors with the seal of the royal cemetery. The robbers probably did not get far. It was one of the luckiest escapes in history. Most of the treasures stayed exactly where they were, for more than three thousand years.

[long-pause]

{img:73-workers-huts-valley-stone-chips}

The huts of the workmen who built a later tomb helped to hide Tutankhamun's tomb from the world.

[pause]

About two hundred years after Tutankhamun's burial, workmen began to cut a large new tomb for a king named Ramesses the Sixth. Its entrance lay just above the place where the young king had been buried. The workmen built simple huts of rough stone near their work, where they could rest, eat and store their tools. They also dumped great heaps of white stone chips from their digging across the valley floor. Without knowing it, they covered the entrance to Tutankhamun's tomb even more deeply. Over the centuries, flash floods carried more rubble and sand down into the valley. Ancient tomb robbers and later explorers searched the valley again and again, and almost every royal tomb there was emptied. But nobody thought to dig beneath the old workmen's huts. They looked like nothing at all. And so, for more than three thousand years, a few humble stone walls stood guard over one of the greatest treasures ever found.

[long-pause]

{img:74-howard-carter-valley-1920s}

For more than three thousand years, the valley kept its secret. Then a stubborn Englishman came looking.

[pause]

Howard Carter first came to Egypt as a teenager, to copy paintings in ancient tombs.

[pause]

Howard Carter was born in London in 1874, the son of an artist. He did not go to university. Instead, at the age of seventeen, he travelled to Egypt to help copy the paintings and inscriptions in ancient tombs. He was good at it, and he learned quickly. Over the years, he worked on many excavations and became an inspector of antiquities. He was careful, stubborn and hard-working, though not always easy to get along with. By the early twentieth century, many experts believed the Valley of the Kings had been fully explored. Carter disagreed. He had noticed that no tomb of Tutankhamun had ever been found, though objects with his name had turned up nearby. He was convinced the tomb was still there. From 1917 onwards, he searched the valley season after season, clearing rubble down to the bedrock. Year after year, he found almost nothing. Yet he kept believing the young king was waiting somewhere close.

[long-pause]

{img:75-lord-carnarvon-country-house}

The search for Tutankhamun's tomb was paid for by an English lord who first went to Egypt for his health.

[pause]

George Herbert, the fifth Earl of Carnarvon, lived at Highclere Castle in the south of England. He loved fast cars, horses and collecting beautiful things. In 1901, he was badly injured in a motor car accident in Germany. Afterwards, his health was weak, and his doctors advised him to spend the damp English winters somewhere warm. So he went to Egypt. There, he became fascinated by archaeology, and he hired Howard Carter to dig on his behalf. For years, he paid for the search in the Valley of the Kings, but the results were disappointing. By 1922, he had spent a great deal of money and was ready to give up. Carter visited him at Highclere and persuaded him to pay for one last season. Lord Carnarvon agreed. It is one of history's quiet turning points. A tired man in an English library, reading through the figures, decided to give the search just one more try.

[long-pause]

{img:76-workmen-baskets-rubble-valley}

The first step of Tutankhamun's tomb was found on the fourth of November, 1922.

[pause]

Carter's last season began at the end of October 1922. He decided to dig in a small area he had not yet cleared, beneath the old workmen's huts near the tomb of Ramesses the Sixth. Egyptian workmen dug through the rubble with picks, while boys carried away the stones and sand in baskets on their heads, walking in long lines. On the morning of the fourth of November, the work went strangely quiet. Under the first hut, the workmen had uncovered a step cut into the rock. A popular story says that a young water boy found it, while digging a hole to rest his water jars in. Whether that is exactly true, we may never know. What is certain is that the Egyptian team uncovered the step and called Carter over. They kept digging carefully. One step became two, then four, then more. A staircase was leading down into the floor of the valley.

[long-pause]

{img:77-sealed-doorway-staircase-sunlight}

When Carter reached the bottom of the staircase, he found a door that had been sealed for more than three thousand years.

[pause]

By the evening of the fifth of November, the workmen had cleared twelve steps. At the bottom stood the upper part of a doorway, blocked with stones and covered in plaster. Pressed into the plaster were oval seals, showing a jackal lying above nine bound captives. This was the seal of the royal cemetery. It meant that someone important was buried behind the door. Carter was thrilled, but he did something remarkable. He had the staircase filled in again, and sent a telegram to Lord Carnarvon in England, so that he could be there when the tomb was opened. It took more than two weeks for Carnarvon and his daughter, Lady Evelyn, to travel to Luxor. When they arrived, the staircase was cleared again. Now, at the bottom of the door, Carter could see seals with the name of Tutankhamun. After all those years of searching, the name he had hoped for was right there in front of him.

[long-pause]

{img:78-candlelight-glimpse-gold-dark}

When Carter first looked into the tomb by candlelight, he could hardly speak.

[pause]

On the twenty-sixth of November, 1922, after clearing the first door and a corridor full of rubble, Carter reached a second sealed doorway. He made a small hole in the top corner and held up a candle. Warm air from inside the tomb made the flame flicker. At first, he could see nothing. Then, as his eyes got used to the dim light, shapes began to appear out of the darkness: strange animals, statues and gold, everywhere the glint of gold. Behind him, Lord Carnarvon asked whether he could see anything. Carter later wrote that he answered, yes, wonderful things. His own notes from that night record the words slightly differently. But the feeling is the same. For a moment, a man stood at a small hole in a wall, holding a candle, looking at a room that had not seen light for more than three thousand years. And for that moment, he was the only person in the world who knew.

[long-pause]

{img:79-antechamber-golden-couches-statues}

As the candlelight steadied, the room beyond the wall slowly came into view.

[pause]

The first room of the tomb was crammed with treasures, piled up like furniture in an attic.

[pause]

The first room, which Carter called the Antechamber, was full almost to the ceiling. Along one wall stood three long ceremonial beds, covered in gold. Their sides were shaped like strange animals: one like a lion, one like a cow, and one like a hippopotamus with a crocodile's tail. Underneath and around them were piles of boxes, chairs, stools, baskets of food, alabaster vases and the dismantled parts of golden chariots. At one end, two life-size statues of the king stood facing each other on either side of a sealed doorway. Their skin was painted black, the colour of the fertile Nile mud and of rebirth, and they wore golden kilts and headdresses. They were guardians, watching over whatever lay beyond the wall. Everything was crowded together, as if packed in a hurry. For the first visitors, it must have felt like stepping into a dream. Every object was a treasure, and there were hundreds of them.

[long-pause]

{img:80-carter-team-cataloguing-objects}

Emptying Tutankhamun's tomb took Carter and his team about ten years.

[pause]

Carter knew that the discovery was too important to rush. Many of the objects were extremely fragile. Wood had dried and cracked, linen fell apart at a touch, and beads were held together only by threads that had rotted away. So every object was numbered, photographed, drawn and described before it was moved. A photographer named Harry Burton took thousands of photographs, many of which are still used by researchers today. A nearby empty tomb was turned into a laboratory, where conservators carefully strengthened the objects with wax and other materials. Then they were packed and carried to the river, to be taken by boat to the museum in Cairo. Crowds of visitors and journalists gathered every day at the edge of the tomb, hoping to see something golden being carried out. In total, more than five thousand objects were recorded. The work was slow and patient. Yet because of that patience, we still know exactly where each object lay.

[long-pause]

{img:81-burial-chamber-golden-shrine}

The king's coffins lay inside four golden shrines, nested one inside the other.

[pause]

In February 1923, Carter opened the sealed doorway between the two guardian statues. Behind it was the burial chamber. It was almost completely filled by a huge wooden shrine covered in gold and inlaid with blue faience. There was barely enough room to squeeze around it. Inside the first shrine was a second, inside that a third, and inside that a fourth, like a set of golden boxes. Taking them apart in that tiny room took a very long time, and the work was often hot and frustrating. Inside the last shrine stood a great stone sarcophagus, made of yellow quartzite, with goddesses carved on its corners, spreading their wings protectively. Its heavy lid was lifted in February 1924. Inside, wrapped in linen shrouds, lay the first of the three coffins. Layer by layer, the archaeologists were moving closer to the king himself. It was like slowly opening a gift that had been wrapped with great love.

[long-pause]

{img:82-dried-flower-wreath-coffin-brow}

Of all the treasures in the tomb, the one that moved Howard Carter most was a small wreath of dried flowers.

[pause]

When the lid of the outermost coffin was lifted, the archaeologists saw a small wreath resting on the forehead of the golden face. It was made of olive leaves, blue cornflowers and lotus petals, threaded together. It had dried and faded, but it still kept its shape. Carter wrote that, among all the royal splendour, nothing was so beautiful as those few withered flowers, still showing a tinge of colour. He imagined that they had been the last farewell of the young widowed queen to her husband. Of course, we cannot know for certain who placed the wreath there. It might have been a priest, following custom. Yet the thought has stayed with many people who have seen it. In a tomb full of gold, the most touching object was something anyone could have picked from a garden on a spring morning. It reminds us that behind every great ceremony, there are ordinary feelings of loss and love.

[long-pause]

{img:83-golden-mask-lamplight-tomb}

Tutankhamun's golden mask has tiny holes in its ears, for earrings he no longer wore.

[pause]

The golden mask was found in late 1925, resting over the head and shoulders of the king's mummy, inside the innermost coffin. It is perhaps the most famous object from the ancient world. The face is calm and young, with large dark-outlined eyes made of quartz and obsidian. The headcloth is striped in gold and blue glass, and a vulture and a cobra rise from the brow, protecting the king. Around the shoulders lies a broad collar of inlaid stones and glass. Look closely at the ears, and you can see that they are pierced. In Egypt at this time, both children and adults wore earrings. Kings, however, were not usually shown wearing them. So the holes were left empty. On the back and shoulders, a long sacred text is engraved, a spell to protect each part of the king's face and body. The mask was not meant to be seen by anyone. It was meant to give the king a perfect face for eternity.

[long-pause]

{img:84-treasury-room-anubis-shrine}

A statue of a black jackal guarded the treasury, wrapped in a linen shawl.

[pause]

Next to the burial chamber was a small room that Carter called the Treasury. At its entrance lay a beautiful statue of Anubis, the jackal god of embalming and protector of the dead. He lies on top of a golden shrine, with his front paws stretched out and his ears pricked, alert and watchful. His body is painted black, his eyes are inlaid with gold, calcite and obsidian, and his claws are made of silver. When the tomb was found, he was wrapped in a linen shawl, as if to keep him warm. One piece of linen carries a date from the seventh year of Akhenaten's reign. Behind him stood a great gilded chest, guarded by four golden goddesses with outstretched arms. Inside it were the king's organs, in their little golden coffins. All around lay model boats, boxes and small shrines. Anubis did his job well. For more than three thousand years, he kept watch in the dark.

[long-pause]

{img:85-simple-objects-sandals-boxes-linen}

Along with gold, Tutankhamun was buried with his underwear, his sandals and a lock of his grandmother's hair.

[pause]

Not everything in the tomb was golden. Many of the objects were ordinary things from daily life. There were woven sandals of reeds and papyrus, gloves, folded shirts and around one hundred and fifty linen loincloths, the underwear of the time. There were board games, writing palettes, boxes of clothes, baskets of bread, dates and grapes, and jars of wine labelled with the year and the vineyard. There was even a small chest with a child's clothes in it, perhaps from the king's own childhood. One small set of nested coffins held a lock of auburn hair, labelled with the name of Queen Tiye, the king's grandmother. Perhaps it was a keepsake. These everyday things show us that the afterlife was imagined as a continuation of this life, with meals, games, clean linen and comfortable shoes. It is strangely comforting. Even a pharaoh, it seems, wanted his favourite slippers with him on the longest journey.

[long-pause]

{img:86-valley-of-kings-today-morning}

The treasures have left the tomb now. But the valley, the river and the young king's name are still there.

[pause]

There are more than sixty tombs in the Valley of the Kings, and Tutankhamun's is number sixty-two.

[pause]

Archaeologists give each tomb in the Valley of the Kings a number, in the order in which they were discovered or recorded. The letters K V stand for Kings' Valley. When Carter found Tutankhamun's tomb in 1922, it became KV62. Since then, a few more have been found. KV63, discovered in 2006, turned out to be a small storage room holding coffins and embalming materials, perhaps left over from a funeral. Today, the valley is visited by many thousands of people each year. They walk along the same dusty paths that funeral processions once climbed, under the same pyramid-shaped peak, in the same clear light. Most of the tombs are long and grand, with corridors covered in sacred texts. Tutankhamun's is one of the smallest. And yet, of all the tombs in the valley, it is the one that almost every visitor wants to see. Its fame comes not from its size, but from the fact that it survived.

[long-pause]

{img:87-burial-chamber-painted-walls-quiet}

Tutankhamun's mummy still lies in his tomb in the Valley of the Kings.

[pause]

Almost all the treasures from the tomb were taken to Cairo, to be studied and displayed. But the king himself was returned to his tomb. After Carter's team had examined the mummy in 1925, it was placed back in the outermost wooden coffin, inside the stone sarcophagus in the burial chamber. For many years, it rested there, hidden from view. In 2007, his remains were moved into a climate-controlled glass case in the tomb, to protect them from the heat and humidity brought in by visitors. Today, people can stand quietly for a moment and see the face of the young king himself, small and dark, wrapped in linen up to his neck. It is a very different sight from the golden mask. More human, and more fragile. In a valley where almost every other king was moved, robbed or lost long ago, Tutankhamun is one of the very few who still lies in the place where he was laid to rest.

[long-pause]

{img:88-deir-el-medina-ruins-today}

The workmen who built the royal tombs left behind notes about why they missed work, including a scorpion sting and brewing beer.

[pause]

In a small valley near the Valley of the Kings lie the ruins of a village called Deir el-Medina. This is where the craftsmen who cut and decorated the royal tombs lived with their families, for generations. Their houses were small and close together, along a single main street. Because many of the workers could read and write, they left behind thousands of notes, written on flakes of limestone and broken pottery. These notes tell us about their daily lives in surprising detail. There are shopping lists, letters, love poems, complaints about neighbours and records of who borrowed a donkey. Some notes list why men were absent from work. One was ill. One had an eye problem. One was stung by a scorpion. Another was brewing beer, and another was helping his wife after the birth of a child. It all sounds very familiar. The people who made the tombs of kings had ordinary worries, ordinary jokes and ordinary days.

[long-pause]

{img:89-luxor-temple-evening-lights}

Inside Luxor temple, there is a mosque that has been in use for more than seven hundred years.

[pause]

Luxor temple has stood beside the Nile for more than three thousand years. Over all those centuries, it has been used in many different ways. In Roman times, part of it was turned into a shrine for the emperor. Later, Christians built churches inside its courtyards. Over time, sand and houses filled the temple, until only the tops of the columns could be seen. In the thirteenth century, a mosque was built on top of the buried courtyard, dedicated to a local holy man called Abu al-Haggag. When archaeologists cleared the temple in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the mosque was kept. Today, it still stands high above the old temple floor, its door seeming to float in mid-air above the ancient columns. In the evening, when the lights come on, the stones of the pharaohs and the minaret of the mosque glow together against the deep blue sky. It is a quiet picture of many centuries of prayer, layered on one place.

[long-pause]

{img:90-museum-hall-golden-treasures}

For the first time since 1922, almost all of Tutankhamun's treasures can now be seen together in one place.

[pause]

For about a century, the treasures from the tomb were kept in the old Egyptian Museum in Cairo, a grand pink building from 1902. But there was never enough room to show everything. Many objects stayed in storage, and others travelled abroad in famous exhibitions that drew millions of visitors. In the early twenty-first century, Egypt began building a vast new museum near the pyramids of Giza, called the Grand Egyptian Museum. After many years of work, it opened fully in 2025. There, the complete collection from Tutankhamun's tomb is displayed together for the first time: the golden coffins, the throne, the beds, the chariots, the shrines, and thousands of smaller objects, including sandals, gloves and boxes of food. Visitors can walk through the halls and see the king's world laid out around them. In a way, everything that was packed together in a small dark tomb is once more gathered in one place. Only now, it is full of light.

[long-pause]

{img:91-felucca-nile-sunset-modern-timeless}

The Nile no longer floods every summer, as it did in Tutankhamun's time.

[pause]

For thousands of years, the yearly flood shaped every part of Egyptian life. Then, in the twentieth century, Egypt built a great dam at Aswan, in the far south. It was finished in 1970. Behind it lies an enormous lake. The dam controls the flow of the river, providing water all year round and making electricity. It protects towns and villages from floods that are too high, and from years when the water is too low. But it also means that the river no longer spreads across the fields each summer, and no longer leaves behind its fresh layer of black mud. Farmers now use fertiliser instead. Yet some things have stayed the same. Small wooden sailing boats called feluccas still drift on the river, their white sails catching the evening breeze, much like the boats painted in ancient tombs. Date palms still line the banks. And the sun still sets behind the western hills, exactly as it did for the young king.

The ancient Egyptians believed that as long as your name was spoken, you would live forever.

[pause]

For the Egyptians, a person's name was part of their soul. If people remembered your name and said it aloud, you would go on living in the afterlife. If your name was forgotten or erased, you might fade away completely. That is why tombs were covered with names and prayers, and why later kings chiselled out the names of rulers they wanted to forget. For many centuries, Tutankhamun was one of those forgotten kings. His name was left off the lists, and his monuments were given to others. Then, in 1922, a staircase was found beneath some old stone huts. Today, Tutankhamun's name is known all over the world. It is spoken in schools, in museums and in quiet rooms at night, perhaps more than the name of any other pharaoh. If the Egyptians were right, then the young king has been given back what he was once denied. Somewhere beyond the dark cliffs, under all these stars, his name is still being spoken.
