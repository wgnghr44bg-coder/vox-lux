Tonight we are going nearly two thousand years back, to the city of Jerusalem, in the spring of the year 33 after Christ, in the days before the festival of Passover.

[long-pause]

On the hills of Judea, a city of pale gold stone is filling with pilgrims. Above it rises the great Temple, white and gold, shining in the spring sunshine. The roads are crowded with families singing psalms, the hillsides are covered with tents, and the smoke of the evening fires drifts over the valleys.

[pause]

And in this week, a teacher from Galilee is coming to the city, with his friends, riding on a young donkey. What happens in the next few days will be remembered, every spring, by billions of people, for two thousand years. We call it Easter.

[pause]

Tonight, we will walk through Jerusalem slowly, in those days, and see the city as the pilgrims saw it.

[pause]

If you like these quiet journeys into the past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, and to come back for the next one.

[long-pause]

Jerusalem stands high in the hills of Judea, about eight hundred metres above the sea, on the edge of the desert.

[pause]

To the west, the hills slope down, through terraces of olive trees, and vineyards, and little stone villages, toward the coast, and the Mediterranean Sea. To the east, the land falls away steeply, into the wilderness of Judea: bare, brown, empty hills, cut by deep ravines, going down, and down, to the Jordan valley, and the Dead Sea, the lowest place on earth, which, on a clear day, can be seen from the Mount of Olives, shining far below, with the mountains of Moab, beyond, turning pink and purple in the evening.

[long-pause]

In the spring, the hills are green. After the winter rains, there are wild flowers everywhere: red anemones, and poppies, and white daisies, and yellow chrysanthemums, and blue lupins, and cyclamen, among the rocks. The almond trees have blossomed, and the fig trees are putting out their first leaves. The barley is ripening in the fields, and the first sheaves of the harvest will be offered in the Temple at Passover.

[pause]

{img:spring-wildflowers-red-anemones-hillside-jerusalem-distance}
The air is fresh and clear. The days are warm, and the nights are still cool. And the city is waiting for the great festival.

[long-pause]

Jerusalem, in the year 33, is the holy city of the Jewish people, and one of the great cities of the eastern Roman world. Perhaps forty thousand people live there, or more. But at Passover, the population grows enormously, as pilgrims come from all over the land of Israel, and from all over the world, from Egypt, and Babylon, and Syria, and Asia Minor, and Greece, and Rome, to celebrate the festival in the holy city, as the Law of Moses commands.

[pause]

For a week, Jerusalem is perhaps the most crowded city on earth.

[pause]

{img:pilgrims-roads-to-jerusalem-families-donkeys-singing-psalms}
It is a city with a long history: the city of King David, who made it his capital, a thousand years before. The city of Solomon, who built the first Temple. A city destroyed by the Babylonians, and rebuilt, and fought over, by Persians, and Greeks, and Egyptians, and Syrians, and Romans. And now, in the year 33, it is ruled by Rome.

[long-pause]

{img:ship-arriving-joppa-harbour-pilgrims-disembarking-spring-sea}
At Passover, Jerusalem is full of Jews from all over the world.

[pause]

By the year 33, there are more Jews living outside the land of Israel than inside it: in Egypt, especially in Alexandria, where perhaps a hundred thousand Jews live. In Babylon, and Mesopotamia, where their ancestors were taken, six hundred years before, and where many stayed. In Syria, and Asia Minor, and Greece, and Cyprus, and Crete, and North Africa, and Rome, and even further.

[long-pause]

They speak Greek, many of them, or Aramaic, or Latin, or the languages of the lands where they live. But they read the scriptures, and keep the Sabbath, and the festivals, and send money every year, the Temple tax, half a shekel, to the Temple in Jerusalem. And many of them, at least once in their lives, make the long journey to Jerusalem, for one of the great festivals.

[pause]

They come by sea, in ships, to the port of Joppa, which is now Jaffa, or Caesarea, and then up through the hills, on foot, or on donkeys, to Jerusalem. Or by land, in caravans, across the deserts, from Babylon, and Arabia, and Egypt, for weeks.

[pause]

The Acts of the Apostles lists the peoples who were in Jerusalem, at the festival, fifty days after Easter: Parthians, and Medes, and Elamites, and people from Mesopotamia, Judea and Cappadocia, Pontus and Asia, Phrygia and Pamphylia, Egypt and the parts of Libya near Cyrene, and visitors from Rome, Cretans and Arabs.

[long-pause]

In Jerusalem, there are synagogues for the Jews from different lands, where they can pray, and meet, and stay. In 1913, archaeologists found, in the City of David, a stone inscription, in Greek, from the time of Jesus, which says that a priest, called Theodotus, built a synagogue, for the reading of the Law, and the teaching of the commandments, and a guest house, and rooms, and water installations, for visitors from abroad who need them.

[pause]

{img:greek-inscription-stone-synagogue-theodotus-museum-close-light}
So the pilgrims from abroad had places to stay, and to wash, and to pray, in Jerusalem, at the festivals, among people who spoke their language. And when they went home, after the festival, they took with them the news of everything that had happened in Jerusalem, that spring, to every corner of the world.

[long-pause]

{img:herods-temple-gleaming-white-gold-facade-courts-colonnades-mount}
Above everything in Jerusalem rises the Temple.

[pause]

It stands on a great flat platform, on the top of Mount Moriah, the Temple Mount, on the east side of the city, the largest sacred enclosure in the ancient world: almost five hundred metres long, and three hundred metres wide, surrounded by enormous walls, of huge, carefully cut stones, some of them weighing hundreds of tons.

[long-pause]

The Temple was rebuilt, and enlarged, by King Herod the Great, who began the work about fifty years before, in the year 20 before Christ. Herod was a cruel and suspicious king, but a great builder. And he made the Temple one of the wonders of the world. The rabbis later said: whoever has not seen the Temple of Herod has never seen a beautiful building.

[pause]

The work is still going on, in the year 33. It will not be finished until the year 63, only seven years before it is destroyed.

[pause]

{img:royal-stoa-colonnade-temple-mount-tall-columns-crowds-shade}
Around the edges of the great platform, there are colonnades, covered walkways, with tall columns of marble, and roofs of carved cedar wood. On the south side, there is the greatest of them, the Royal Stoa, a huge hall, like a basilica, with four rows of columns, where the money changers, and the sellers of animals for the sacrifices, have their tables, and where the teachers of the Law sit, and teach, and argue.

[long-pause]

The great open court, the Court of the Gentiles, is open to everyone, Jews and foreigners alike. And in the middle of it, there is a raised area, surrounded by a low stone wall, with warning notices, in Greek and Latin, telling foreigners that they may go no further. Two of these stones have been found by archaeologists.

[pause]

Beyond the wall, there is the Court of the Women, where all Jews, men and women, may come, with great treasury chests, shaped like trumpets, for offerings. Then the Court of Israel, for Jewish men. Then the Court of the Priests, with the great altar, of unhewn stones, where the sacrifices are offered, and the great bronze basin, for washing.

[pause]

{img:temple-sanctuary-facade-golden-vine-veil-steps-priests-white}
And then, at the top of twelve steps, there is the Sanctuary itself: a tall building, of white stone, and gold, about fifty metres high. The front is covered with plates of gold, which shine so brightly in the morning sun, the historian Josephus says, that people had to turn their eyes away, as from the rays of the sun. Over the great doorway, there is a golden vine, with bunches of golden grapes, as tall as a man.

[long-pause]

Inside the Sanctuary, there is the Holy Place, with the golden lampstand, the menorah, with its seven branches, and the golden table, for the bread of the Presence, and the golden altar of incense. And behind a great curtain, a veil, of blue, and purple, and scarlet, embroidered with the stars of heaven, there is the Holy of Holies: a small, empty, dark room, which only the High Priest may enter, once a year, on the Day of Atonement.

[pause]

From far away, Josephus says, the Temple looked like a snow-covered mountain, because where it was not covered with gold, it was of the purest white.

[long-pause]

{img:herodium-cone-shaped-hill-palace-fortress-desert-bethlehem-view}
The man who made Jerusalem so splendid was King Herod the Great, who passed away about thirty-seven years before our story.

[pause]

Herod was not of a royal family. His father was an official, from Idumea, in the south, and his mother was an Arab princess, from Petra. But he was clever, and ambitious, and a friend of the Romans, of Mark Antony, and later of Augustus. And the Romans made him king of Judea, in the year 37 before Christ. He ruled for thirty-three years.

[long-pause]

Herod was a cruel, suspicious, unhappy man. He was always afraid of plots, and put to death many people, including his own wife, Mariamne, whom he loved, and three of his own sons. The emperor Augustus is said to have joked, in Greek, that it was better to be Herod's pig than Herod's son, because Herod, following Jewish law, did not eat pork.

[pause]

But he was also one of the greatest builders of the ancient world.

[pause]

{img:masada-mesa-fortress-dead-sea-desert-cliffs-dawn-palace-terraces}
He rebuilt the Temple of Jerusalem, on an enormous scale. He built the great city of Caesarea, on the coast, with its harbour, made with Roman concrete, which set under water. He built fortresses, and palaces, all over the land: at Jericho, with gardens, and pools, in the warm winter oasis. At Masada, on top of a great flat rock, high above the Dead Sea, with a palace hanging on three terraces, down the cliff, with baths, and storerooms, and huge cisterns, for rainwater. And at the Herodium, near Bethlehem, a great palace fortress, on the top of a hill, which he made higher, and shaped like a cone, like a volcano, so that it could be seen from Jerusalem.

[long-pause]

He was buried at the Herodium. And in 2007, after searching for thirty-five years, an Israeli archaeologist, Ehud Netzer, found the remains of his tomb, on the slope of the hill.

[pause]

When Herod passed away, in the year 4 before Christ, his kingdom was divided among three of his sons: Archelaus, who ruled Judea, so badly that the Romans removed him, after ten years, and took Judea under direct Roman rule, with a governor. Herod Antipas, who ruled Galilee. And Philip, who ruled the lands to the north east.

[pause]

{img:caesarea-harbour-herod-breakwater-ships-theatre-sea-sunset}
So in the year 33, Herod's buildings are everywhere, in Jerusalem, and in the land: the Temple, and the palace, and the towers, and the fortresses, and the cities. And the people still remember the old king, and his cruelty, and his splendour.

[long-pause]

{img:jerusalem-upper-city-mansions-herods-palace-towers-west-evening}
Let us walk through Jerusalem, in the spring of the year 33.

[pause]

The city is built on two hills, with a valley between them, and surrounded by deep valleys on three sides: the Kidron valley, to the east, between the city and the Mount of Olives, and the Hinnom valley, to the south and west. Around it, there are strong walls, with towers, and gates.

[long-pause]

On the western hill, there is the Upper City, where the rich and the powerful live: the families of the high priests, and the nobles, and the wealthy merchants, in fine houses of stone, with courtyards, and gardens, and painted walls, and mosaic floors, and their own ritual baths, cut into the rock, for purification.

[pause]

At the north western corner of the Upper City, there is the palace of King Herod the Great, now used by the Roman governor when he comes to Jerusalem: a great fortress-palace, with three tall towers, named after Herod's brother, his friend, and his wife, and gardens, and pools, and fountains, and colonnades, and banqueting halls, for hundreds of guests.

[pause]

In the valley between the hills, and on the slope of the eastern hill, below the Temple, there is the Lower City, the old City of David, where most ordinary people live, in small stone houses, crowded together, along narrow, winding, stepped streets, going up and down the hills.

[long-pause]

There are markets, and bazaars, and workshops, everywhere. There is a street of the bakers, and a street of the cheesemakers, and a market for sheep, and a market for wood, and a market for wool, and a market for clothes. There are potters, and weavers, and dyers, and tanners, outside the walls, because of the smell, and stone cutters, and goldsmiths, and perfume makers.

[pause]

At the south end of the Lower City, there is the Pool of Siloam, a great stepped pool, fed by a spring, through a tunnel, cut through the rock, seven hundred years before, by King Hezekiah, where people come to wash, and purify themselves, before going up to the Temple. And a great stepped street runs from the pool, up the valley, to the Temple Mount. Archaeologists have found both of them, in recent years.

[pause]

{img:pool-of-siloam-stepped-stone-pool-pilgrims-washing-morning}
North of the Temple, there is the Antonia fortress, a great square castle, with four towers, built by Herod, and named after his friend, Mark Antony, where the Roman soldiers are stationed, looking down over the courts of the Temple, to keep order, especially at the festivals, when the crowds are huge, and riots can easily break out.

[long-pause]

And there is water: the Pool of Bethesda, north of the Temple, with five porches, where the sick lie, waiting for the water to stir, hoping to be healed. Aqueducts, bringing water from springs, near Bethlehem, to the city, and to the Temple. Great cisterns, cut into the rock, under the Temple Mount, and under the houses, to store the winter rains, for the long dry summer.

[pause]

{img:pool-of-bethesda-porches-colonnade-sick-waiting-water-light}
And outside the walls, on every side, there are gardens, and orchards, and olive groves, and vineyards, and tombs, cut into the rock of the hillsides, and on the Mount of Olives, to the east, the olive trees, silver-green, covering the slopes, and the little village of Bethany, on the far side of the hill, on the road to Jericho.

[long-pause]

{img:mikveh-ritual-bath-rock-cut-steps-water-jerusalem-house-quiet}
Water is precious in Jerusalem, which stands on a dry hill, at the edge of the desert, with only one spring, the Gihon spring, in the valley below the City of David.

[pause]

The people of Jerusalem collect the rain, in winter, from their roofs, and their courtyards, in cisterns, great tanks, cut into the rock, under their houses, plastered, to keep the water in, for the long, dry summer. There are hundreds of them, under the city, and under the Temple Mount.

[long-pause]

And water is also important for the Jewish religion: for purity.

[pause]

The Law of Moses says that people must be pure, ritually clean, before they enter the Temple, or touch holy things, or eat holy food. And they become impure by touching certain things, or after certain events in life. To become pure again, they must immerse themselves completely in water: in a natural spring, or river, or in a ritual bath, called a mikveh.

[pause]

A mikveh is a pool, cut into the rock, with steps going down into the water, often divided into two halves, by a low wall, or a line, so that people go down on one side, impure, and come up on the other side, pure. The water must be living water: rain water, or spring water, which has flowed in naturally, not carried in buckets.

[long-pause]

Archaeologists have found hundreds of mikvaot, ritual baths, in Jerusalem, from the time of Jesus: in the houses of the rich, in the Upper City, and in the Lower City, and especially around the Temple Mount, near the great staircases, leading up to the Temple, where the pilgrims, thousands of them, washed themselves, before going up to worship.

[pause]

At Passover, with hundreds of thousands of pilgrims, all needing to purify themselves before the festival, the pools, and the mikvaot, and the Pool of Siloam, and the Pool of Bethesda, were crowded with people, from dawn to dusk.

[pause]

{img:stone-water-jars-limestone-house-courtyard-purity-vessels}
The people of Jerusalem also used vessels made of stone, especially soft limestone, for water, and food, because stone, the rabbis taught, could not become impure, like pottery could. Archaeologists find these stone cups, and jars, and bowls, everywhere, in the houses of Jerusalem of that time. The Gospel of John tells of six great stone water jars, for purification, at a wedding, at Cana, in Galilee, where Jesus turned the water into wine.

[long-pause]

{img:passover-pilgrims-camping-mount-of-olives-tents-fires-evening}
Passover, Pesach, is the greatest festival of the Jewish year.

[pause]

It remembers the night, more than a thousand years before, when, the Book of Exodus tells, God brought the people of Israel out of slavery, in Egypt, under Moses. On that night, each family killed a lamb, and put its blood on the doorposts of their house, so that the angel of death would pass over it. And they ate the lamb, roasted, with unleavened bread, made in haste, without time for the dough to rise, and bitter herbs, standing, dressed for a journey, ready to leave.

[long-pause]

Every year since, the Jewish people have remembered that night, with a special meal, the Passover meal, eaten together, in families, on the evening of the fourteenth day of the month of Nisan, the first full moon of spring.

[pause]

And in the time of the Temple, everyone who can, comes to Jerusalem for Passover, to sacrifice the Passover lamb, in the Temple, and eat the Passover meal, inside the walls of the holy city.

[pause]

{img:families-walking-road-galilee-to-jerusalem-donkey-children-spring}
So, in the weeks before Passover, the roads to Jerusalem are full of pilgrims: families, from Galilee, in the north, and from all over Judea, and from the cities of the Jewish communities abroad, walking, for days, along the roads, with their donkeys, and their children, singing the Psalms of Ascent, the special psalms, for the journey up to Jerusalem: I was glad, when they said unto me, let us go into the house of the Lord. Our feet shall stand within thy gates, O Jerusalem.

[long-pause]

When they come over the top of the Mount of Olives, and see the city, and the Temple, shining gold and white, across the valley, they stop, and many of them weep, for joy.

[pause]

The city cannot hold them all. So they camp outside the walls, on the hills around, on the Mount of Olives, and in the villages nearby, in tents, and shelters of branches, or stay with relatives, and friends, in the city, and the villages, like Bethany. The historian Josephus says, perhaps exaggerating, that once, more than two and a half million people came to Jerusalem for Passover. Modern historians think perhaps several hundred thousand.

[pause]

{img:temple-court-crowds-passover-lambs-priests-levites-singing}
In the days before Passover, everyone must purify themselves, in the ritual baths, and the pools, and the families clean their houses, and search, by the light of a lamp, in every corner, for any crumb of leavened bread, which must be removed, and burned, before the festival.

[pause]

And on the afternoon of the day before Passover, in the Temple, the Passover lambs are sacrificed, in great crowds, in turns, while the Levites, the Temple singers, sing the Hallel, the psalms of praise, with trumpets, and harps, and cymbals. And then each family carries its lamb home, to roast it, for the Passover meal, that evening.

[long-pause]

{img:passover-family-table-unleavened-bread-bitter-herbs-wine-cups-lamps}
What was the Passover meal like, in the time of Jesus?

[pause]

It was eaten in the evening, after sunset, in Jerusalem, by families, and groups of friends, together, at least ten people, to share a whole lamb, roasted, which had been sacrificed that afternoon, in the Temple. Nothing of the lamb could be left until the morning.

[long-pause]

They reclined, on couches, or cushions, around low tables, like free people, at a feast, not standing, as their ancestors had done in Egypt, in a hurry, because now they were free.

[pause]

On the table, there was the roasted lamb. And matzah, the flat, unleavened bread, the bread of affliction, made only of flour and water, baked quickly, without yeast, to remember how their ancestors left Egypt in haste, without time for the bread to rise. And bitter herbs, lettuce, or endive, or horseradish, to remember the bitterness of slavery. And a sweet paste, of fruit, and nuts, and wine, called charoset, which looks like the clay that the slaves used to make bricks, in Egypt. And salt water, for the tears. And wine.

[pause]

{img:child-asking-question-father-passover-table-lamplight-family}
During the meal, they drank four cups of wine, at different moments, each with its own blessing. And the youngest child asked questions: why is this night different from all other nights? On all other nights, we eat bread with yeast. Why, tonight, only unleavened bread?

[pause]

And the father, or the head of the family, answered, by telling the story of the Exodus: how the people of Israel were slaves in Egypt, and how God heard their cry, and sent Moses, and brought them out, with a mighty hand, and an outstretched arm, through the Red Sea, to freedom.

[long-pause]

They sang the psalms of the Hallel, praising God, part before the meal, and part after it. And they ate, and drank, and talked, late into the night, and remembered.

[pause]

The Passover meal is still celebrated today, by Jewish families, all over the world, every spring, and is called the Seder, which means order, because it follows a fixed order. Without the Temple, there is no sacrificed lamb any more. Instead, there is a roasted bone, on the Seder plate, as a reminder. But the matzah, and the bitter herbs, and the charoset, and the salt water, and the four cups of wine, and the questions of the children, and the story, are the same.

[pause]

{img:modern-seder-plate-matzah-family-table-candles-warm-later}
And at the end, the door is opened, for the prophet Elijah, who, the tradition says, will come one day, to announce the Messiah. And a cup of wine is poured for him, and left on the table, waiting.

[long-pause]

{img:dawn-jerusalem-temple-trumpet-priest-silver-shofar-morning-light}
Let us spend an ordinary day in Jerusalem, a few days before Passover, in the year 33.

[pause]

The day begins before dawn, in the Temple. A priest climbs to the top of the Temple, and looks east, over the Mount of Olives, and watches for the first light of the sun, on the hills of Hebron, to the south. And when he sees it, he calls out, and the morning sacrifice begins. And the silver trumpets, and the shofar, the ram's horn, are sounded, and the city wakes.

[long-pause]

In the houses of the Lower City, the women are already grinding wheat, or barley, with stone hand mills, for the day's bread, and lighting the clay ovens, in the courtyards. The smell of baking bread, and wood smoke, drifts through the narrow streets.

[pause]

The family is a family of craftsmen, perhaps: a potter, or a stone mason, working on the Temple, which still employs thousands of workers. The father says his morning prayer, the Shema: Hear, O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is One. And he washes his hands, and puts on his tunic, and his cloak, with the blue tassels at the corners, as the Law commands, and goes out to work.

[pause]

Breakfast is simple: bread, and olives, and a little cheese, or a few dates, or figs.

[long-pause]

The streets are crowded, and noisy, and colourful. There are pilgrims everywhere, from all over the world, speaking Aramaic, and Hebrew, and Greek, and Latin, and many other languages: Jews from Egypt, and Babylon, and Asia, and Rome. There are Roman soldiers, in their helmets, and armour, and red cloaks, on patrol. There are priests, and Levites, in their white linen. There are Pharisees, teachers of the Law, with their wide tassels. There are beggars, sitting by the gates. There are sellers of everything: bread, and fruit, and oil, and wine, and fish, from the Sea of Galilee, dried, and salted, and pigeons, and lambs, for sacrifice.

[pause]

The family goes up to the Temple, in the morning, to pray, and to hear the teachers, in the colonnades, and to see the friends, and relatives, they meet there, from other towns.

[pause]

{img:teacher-colonnade-temple-students-sitting-listening-scrolls}
At midday, there is the main meal: bread, and lentils, or beans, cooked with onions, and garlic, and herbs, and olive oil, and perhaps a little fish, or meat, on a festival, and vegetables, and fruit, and wine, mixed with water.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, the work goes on. Then, at about three o'clock, the evening sacrifice is offered in the Temple, and the trumpets sound again, and people stop, wherever they are, and pray.

[pause]

And as the sun goes down, behind the western hills, and the sky turns gold, and then red, and the walls of the city glow, the family gathers on the flat roof of the house, to eat supper, and talk, and look out over the city, and the Temple, and the Mount of Olives, and the thousands of little fires of the pilgrims, burning on the hillsides, all around, under the stars.

[pause]

{img:family-flat-roof-evening-supper-view-temple-fires-on-hills}
And they say the evening prayer, and go to sleep, on mats, on the roof, in the cool night air, in the holy city, with the full moon of Passover rising, slowly, over the Mount of Olives.

[long-pause]

{img:galilean-man-tunic-mantle-tassels-sandals-woman-veil-headscarf}
What do the people of Jerusalem wear, in the year 33?

[pause]

Men wear a tunic, a long shirt, of wool, or linen, reaching to the knees, or the ankles, with a belt, and over it, a cloak, a mantle, a large rectangle of wool, wrapped around the body. At the four corners of the mantle, there are tassels, called tzitzit, with a thread of blue, as the Law of Moses commands, to remind them of the commandments. On their feet, they wear sandals, of leather.

[long-pause]

Women wear a longer tunic, to the ankles, and a mantle, and a veil, or a scarf, over their hair, when they go out. The clothes are mostly of natural colours: white, and cream, and brown, and grey, from the wool of the sheep, and the linen of the flax. The rich wear finer cloth, dyed in colours: blue, and red, and purple, which is very expensive.

[pause]

Archaeologists have found pieces of cloth, from the time of Jesus, in the dry caves of the Judean desert, near the Dead Sea, perfectly preserved: tunics, and mantles, with coloured stripes, woven in, and the remains of tassels, and sandals, and baskets, and mats.

[pause]

What do they eat?

[long-pause]

Bread is the most important food: wheat bread, for those who can afford it, and barley bread, for the poor. Bread is so important that the word for a meal is breaking bread.

[pause]

With the bread, they eat olive oil, and olives, and cheese, from goats, and sheep, and lentils, and beans, and chickpeas, and onions, and garlic, and leeks, and cucumbers, and vegetables. And fruit: figs, and grapes, and dates, and pomegranates, and melons, and almonds. And honey. And fish, from the Sea of Galilee, dried, and salted, and pickled, brought to Jerusalem in great quantities. And eggs, and chickens, and pigeons. Meat, lamb, and goat, and beef, is eaten mostly at festivals, after a sacrifice in the Temple.

[pause]

{img:olive-press-stone-wheel-donkey-oil-jars-grove-autumn}
They drink water, and milk, and wine, mixed with water. And the olive oil is used for everything: for cooking, and eating, and for the lamps, which give light at night, and for the skin, and the hair, and for medicine, and for anointing kings, and priests. The word Messiah, in Hebrew, Mashiach, and Christ, in Greek, Christos, both mean the anointed one: the one anointed with olive oil.

[long-pause]

{img:pontius-pilate-roman-governor-toga-caesarea-palace-sea-view}
In the year 33, the land of the Jews is ruled by Rome.

[pause]

Judea, and Samaria, and Idumea, the southern part of the land, are a Roman province, ruled by a Roman governor, a prefect, who lives, most of the year, in the city of Caesarea, a beautiful new Roman city, on the coast of the Mediterranean, with a great harbour, and a theatre, and a palace by the sea, built by Herod.

[long-pause]

The prefect, since the year 26, is a man called Pontius Pilatus: Pontius Pilate. He is a Roman of the class of knights, a soldier, and an official. We know very little about him, except what the Jewish writers, Philo and Josephus, and the Gospels, tell us. Philo, who did not like him, says that he was stubborn, and harsh, and quick to anger.

[pause]

In 1961, Italian archaeologists, digging in the old theatre of Caesarea, found a stone, which had been reused as a step, with an inscription, in Latin, with his name: Pontius Pilatus, prefect of Judea. It is the only inscription ever found with his name, from his own time. It is now in the Israel Museum, in Jerusalem.

[pause]

{img:pilate-stone-latin-inscription-limestone-museum-soft-light}
Pilate has had many quarrels with the Jews. Once, his soldiers brought images of the emperor, on their standards, into Jerusalem, at night, which the Jews believed was forbidden, because their law forbids images. Crowds of Jews went to Caesarea, and lay down, outside his palace, for five days, and refused to move, and said that they would rather die than allow it. And in the end, Pilate gave in, and took the images away.

[long-pause]

At the great festivals, like Passover, when hundreds of thousands of pilgrims fill Jerusalem, Pilate comes up from Caesarea, with extra soldiers, to keep order. Because the festivals, with their huge crowds, and their memories of freedom from Egypt, are times when riots, and rebellions, can easily break out.

[pause]

He stays in Herod's palace, in the Upper City, with its towers, and its gardens. And from there, and from the Antonia fortress, overlooking the Temple, his soldiers watch the crowds.

[pause]

{img:roman-soldiers-antonia-fortress-roof-watching-temple-courts-crowds}
Most of the soldiers are not Romans from Italy, but auxiliaries, recruited from the non-Jewish cities of the region, like Caesarea and Sebaste, who often do not like the Jews, and are not liked by them.

[long-pause]

Above Pilate, there is the Roman governor of Syria, and above him, the emperor, Tiberius, in Rome, an old, suspicious, gloomy man, who lives on the island of Capri, and rarely comes to Rome.

[pause]

And in Galilee, in the north, there is a Jewish king, Herod Antipas, a son of Herod the Great, who rules Galilee, and Perea, across the Jordan, under the Romans. He too comes to Jerusalem, for Passover.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-auxiliary-soldiers-patrol-jerusalem-street-helmets-shields}
The Roman soldiers in Jerusalem, in the year 33, are not legionaries, from Italy, but auxiliaries: soldiers recruited from the non-Jewish peoples of the region, from the Greek-speaking cities of Caesarea, and Sebaste, in Samaria, and other places. There are perhaps a few thousand of them, in the whole province, and a cohort, about five hundred, or a thousand men, in Jerusalem.

[pause]

They live in the Antonia fortress, beside the Temple, and in Herod's palace, in the Upper City.

[long-pause]

They wear helmets of iron, or bronze, and tunics, and armour of mail, or scales, and carry oval shields, and swords, and spears. Their commander is a tribune. Under him, there are centurions, officers, each in charge of about eighty men, who carry a vine stick, as a sign of their rank.

[pause]

Their life is mostly boring: guard duty, on the walls, and the gates, and the towers. Patrols, in the streets. Training, and drill, and marching. Cleaning their weapons, and their armour. And at night, in the barracks, playing dice, and board games, scratched on the stone pavements, which can still be seen, in some places, in Jerusalem. And waiting for their pay, and their leave.

[pause]

Many of them do not like the Jews, and the Jews do not like them. They are foreign soldiers, of a foreign power, in the holy city. And there are often tensions, and quarrels, especially at the festivals, when the crowds are enormous, and excited. Josephus tells of a soldier, on the roof of the colonnade of the Temple, at Passover, a few years later, who made a rude gesture to the crowd, and caused a riot.

[long-pause]

But the Gospels also tell of a Roman centurion, in Capernaum, in Galilee, who loved the Jewish people, and built them a synagogue, and whose servant Jesus healed. And Jesus said of him: I have not found such great faith, even in Israel.

[pause]

And of the centurion at the cross, who said: truly, this man was the Son of God.

[pause]

{img:centurion-standing-quiet-evening-light-helmet-under-arm-thoughtful}
And in the Acts of the Apostles, the first non-Jew to become a Christian is also a Roman centurion: Cornelius, of Caesarea, a good man, who prayed, and gave alms to the poor, and was baptized by Peter, with all his household.

[long-pause]

{img:high-priest-caiaphas-robes-breastplate-turban-temple-courtyard}
The most powerful Jewish leader in Jerusalem, in the year 33, is the High Priest, Joseph, called Caiaphas.

[pause]

He has been High Priest for fifteen years, since the year 18, which is a very long time. The Romans appoint the High Priest, and remove him, when they like. That Caiaphas has lasted so long shows that he is a clever, careful politician, who knows how to work with the Romans, and keep the peace.

[long-pause]

The High Priest is the head of the Temple, and of the Jewish religion, and the president of the Sanhedrin, the great council of seventy elders, priests, and scholars, which governs the Jewish people, in religious matters, and many others, under the Romans.

[pause]

He belongs to a small group of rich, powerful, priestly families, the Sadducees, who control the Temple, and its enormous wealth. His father-in-law, Annas, was High Priest before him, and five of Annas's sons will be High Priest after him. They live in fine houses, in the Upper City.

[pause]

{img:upper-city-priestly-mansion-courtyard-mosaic-ritual-bath-stone}
Archaeologists have found some of these houses, in the Jewish Quarter of the old city, buried under the ruins of the year 70: large, elegant houses, with mosaic floors, and painted walls, and fine glass, and pottery, and stone vessels, and their own ritual baths, deep in the rock. One of them is so large, and so rich, that it is called the Palatial Mansion.

[long-pause]

And in 1990, workers building a road, south of Jerusalem, found an old family tomb, cut into the rock. Inside, there were ossuaries, little stone boxes, in which the bones of the dead were placed, a year after burial, as was the custom. One of them, beautifully carved, had a name scratched on it, in Aramaic: Joseph, son of Caiaphas. Many scholars think that it is the ossuary of the High Priest himself.

[pause]

The High Priest's greatest fear is that the crowds at Passover will riot, or rebel, against the Romans, and that the Romans will come, in force, and destroy the Temple, and the nation.

[pause]

{img:carved-limestone-ossuary-rosettes-aramaic-name-museum-light}
The Gospel of John says that Caiaphas said to the council: it is better for you that one man should die for the people, than that the whole nation should perish. Because, in those days, any man who drew great crowds, and was hailed as a king, or a messiah, could start a rebellion. And a rebellion would bring the Roman legions.

[long-pause]

In fact, that is exactly what will happen, thirty-three years later, in the year 66, when the Jews rebel against Rome. And in the year 70, the Romans will destroy Jerusalem, and the Temple.

[pause]

But in the spring of the year 33, the Temple stands, shining, above the crowded city, and the High Priest prepares for Passover.

[long-pause]

{img:sea-of-galilee-fishing-boats-hills-capernaum-shore-morning}
In the spring of the year 33, among the pilgrims coming to Jerusalem for Passover, there is a group from Galilee, in the north: a teacher, and his friends, and some women, who travel with them.

[pause]

The teacher's name is Yeshua, Joshua, which in Greek is Iesous, and in English, Jesus. He comes from Nazareth, a small village, in the hills of Galilee. He is in his thirties. He was a builder, a craftsman, like his father, Joseph. And for the last two or three years, he has been travelling around Galilee, teaching, and healing, and drawing great crowds.

[long-pause]

Galilee is very different from Judea. It is green, and fertile, with hills, and valleys, and fields of wheat, and olive groves, and vineyards, and a great lake, the Sea of Galilee, a beautiful freshwater lake, full of fish, surrounded by hills, with little towns, and fishing villages, along its shores: Capernaum, and Bethsaida, and Magdala.

[pause]

Many of Jesus's closest friends are fishermen from the lake: Simon, whom he called Peter, the rock, and his brother Andrew, and James and John, the sons of Zebedee. Others are ordinary people: a tax collector, called Matthew, and others. Twelve of them are his closest followers, the apostles. And there are women too, who follow him, and support him: Mary of Magdala, and Joanna, the wife of an official of King Herod Antipas, and Susanna, and many others.

[pause]

{img:teacher-sitting-hillside-crowd-listening-lake-galilee-below-spring}
He teaches in the synagogues, and in the open air, by the lake, and on the hills, in the villages, to great crowds of ordinary people: farmers, and fishermen, and shepherds, and women, and children, and the sick, and the poor.

[long-pause]

He teaches in stories, called parables, about ordinary things: a farmer sowing seed, a woman who has lost a coin, a shepherd who goes to look for one lost sheep, a father who welcomes home a son who had gone away, a Samaritan, a foreigner, who stops to help a wounded man by the road, when the priest, and the Levite, have passed by.

[pause]

He teaches about the kingdom of God, and about love: love God, and love your neighbour as yourself. Love even your enemies. Blessed are the poor, and the meek, and the peacemakers, and those who mourn, for they shall be comforted.

[pause]

{img:children-gathered-around-teacher-village-square-smiling-mothers}
And he heals the sick, the Gospels say, and the blind, and the lame, and eats with tax collectors, and sinners, and outcasts, and welcomes children, and women, and the poor. Many people believe that he is a prophet. Some believe that he is the Messiah: the anointed king, promised by the prophets, who will come to save Israel.

[long-pause]

And now, in the spring of the year 33, he is coming to Jerusalem, for Passover, with his friends, along the road from Jericho, up through the desert hills, to the Mount of Olives.

[pause]

Most historians, of every belief, agree that Jesus of Nazareth was a real person, a Jewish teacher from Galilee, who was crucified in Jerusalem, under Pontius Pilate, around the year 30 or 33. What happened next, his followers have told, for two thousand years.

[long-pause]

{img:nazareth-small-village-hillside-stone-houses-terraces-galilee-hills}
Nazareth, in the time of Jesus, is a very small village, in the hills of Lower Galilee, perhaps two or three hundred people, or fewer, living in little houses, of stone, and mud, on a hillside, above a small valley, with a spring, at the bottom.

[pause]

It is so small, and so unimportant, that it is never mentioned in the Jewish scriptures, or in the writings of Josephus. In the Gospel of John, when someone hears that Jesus comes from Nazareth, he says: can anything good come out of Nazareth?

[long-pause]

The houses are simple, often built against the hillside, with caves, cut into the soft limestone rock, behind them, used as storerooms, and stables, and sometimes as rooms. Each family has a little courtyard, with an oven, and a cistern, for water, and a millstone, for grinding grain, and an olive press, and a wine press, shared with the neighbours.

[pause]

The villagers are farmers: they grow wheat, and barley, and olives, and grapes, and figs, on the terraces, on the hillsides around the village, and keep a few sheep, and goats.

[pause]

{img:village-courtyard-galilee-mother-grinding-children-playing-oven}
Jesus, the Gospels say, was a tekton, like his father, Joseph: a word that means a craftsman, a builder, who works with wood, and stone. He would have built houses, and made doors, and roof beams, and ploughs, and yokes, for oxen, and tables, and benches, and boxes.

[long-pause]

Only six kilometres away, across the valley, there is a city: Sepphoris, the capital of Galilee, which Herod Antipas was rebuilding, in the years when Jesus was a boy, and a young man, as a fine Roman city, with a theatre, and colonnaded streets, and fine houses, with mosaic floors. Some historians think that Joseph, and the young Jesus, may have worked there, as builders. Archaeologists have found, in Sepphoris, a beautiful mosaic, from a later time, of a woman's face, called the Mona Lisa of Galilee.

[pause]

In 2009, archaeologists found, in Nazareth, the remains of a little house, from the time of Jesus: a few small rooms, and a courtyard, and a cistern, and fragments of pottery, and stone vessels. The kind of house where a family of builders and farmers might have lived.

[pause]

{img:galilee-hills-spring-flowers-wheat-fields-view-from-nazareth}
Today, Nazareth is a large town, in the north of Israel, and in the middle of it stands the great Basilica of the Annunciation, built over the cave where, tradition says, the angel Gabriel appeared to Mary.

[long-pause]

{img:jericho-oasis-palm-groves-green-desert-mountains-winter-sun}
Pilgrims coming from Galilee to Jerusalem, for Passover, often take the road along the Jordan valley, to avoid passing through Samaria, whose people do not get on with the Jews. And so they come to Jericho.

[pause]

Jericho is one of the oldest cities in the world: people have lived there for ten thousand years. It is an oasis, in the deep, hot Jordan valley, near the Dead Sea, nearly three hundred metres below the level of the sea, with a great spring, and palm groves, and gardens, and fields, green and lush, in the middle of the desert. It is called the city of palms.

[long-pause]

In the winter, it is warm, when Jerusalem is cold, and Herod built a winter palace there, with pools, and gardens, and baths. The balsam trees of Jericho produce a precious, fragrant oil, used for perfume, and medicine, which is worth more than gold.

[pause]

From Jericho, the road goes up to Jerusalem: about twenty-five kilometres, and more than a thousand metres up, through the wilderness of Judea, along steep, winding paths, between bare, brown, rocky hills, with deep ravines, and no water, and no shade, and caves, where robbers sometimes hide.

[pause]

{img:winding-desert-road-jericho-to-jerusalem-bare-hills-travellers}
It is on this road that Jesus set his famous parable, of the Good Samaritan: a man going down from Jerusalem to Jericho, who fell among robbers, who stripped him, and beat him, and left him, half dead, by the road. A priest came by, and passed by on the other side. And a Levite. But a Samaritan, a foreigner, from the people the Jews despised, came by, and saw him, and had compassion on him, and bound up his wounds, pouring on oil and wine, and put him on his own donkey, and took him to an inn, and looked after him, and paid the innkeeper to care for him.

[long-pause]

And Jesus asked: which of these three was a neighbour to the man who fell among the robbers? And the answer was: the one who showed him mercy. And Jesus said: go, and do likewise.

[pause]

The Gospels say that, on the way to Jerusalem, at Jericho, a blind beggar, Bartimaeus, sitting by the road, called out to Jesus, and was healed. And that a rich tax collector, a small man, called Zacchaeus, climbed a sycamore tree, to see Jesus over the crowd, and Jesus stopped, and looked up, and said: Zacchaeus, come down, for today I must stay at your house. And Zacchaeus came down, joyfully, and gave half of his goods to the poor.

[pause]

{img:sycamore-fig-tree-jericho-street-crowd-small-man-climbing-legend}
Today, there is an old inn, on the road, halfway between Jerusalem and Jericho, called the Inn of the Good Samaritan, which is a little museum, of mosaics. And in Jericho, there is an ancient sycamore tree, which, the people say, is the tree of Zacchaeus.

[long-pause]

{img:jesus-riding-young-donkey-mount-of-olives-crowd-palm-branches-cloaks}
On the Sunday before Passover, the Gospels tell, Jesus came to Jerusalem, from Bethany, over the Mount of Olives.

[pause]

He sent two of his friends into a nearby village, to bring a young donkey, which had never been ridden. And they put their cloaks on its back, and he sat on it, and rode down the Mount of Olives, toward the city.

[long-pause]

It was a deliberate sign. Five hundred years before, the prophet Zechariah had written: rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion. Shout, O daughter of Jerusalem. Behold, your king comes to you. He is just, and having salvation, humble, and riding on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a donkey.

[pause]

Not on a war horse, like a conquering king, but on a donkey: a king of peace.

[pause]

{img:road-down-mount-of-olives-view-of-temple-across-kidron-valley}
And the crowds of pilgrims, on the road, and on the hillside, many of them from Galilee, who knew him, began to spread their cloaks on the road, in front of him, and others cut branches from the palm trees, and the olive trees, and spread them on the road, and waved them, and shouted: Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord! Blessed is the coming kingdom of our father David! Hosanna in the highest!

[long-pause]

Hosanna means: save us now. The words come from one of the psalms that the pilgrims sang, at the festivals, Psalm 118: Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord.

[pause]

The road down the Mount of Olives, from the top, goes steeply down, toward the Kidron valley, and from it, there is a magnificent view of the whole city, across the valley: the walls, and the Temple, shining in the spring sunshine. The Gospel of Luke says that, when Jesus came near, and saw the city, he wept over it.

[pause]

{img:olive-branches-spread-on-road-crowd-singing-joyful-spring-light}
Today, Christians all over the world remember this day, a week before Easter, as Palm Sunday, with processions, and palm branches, or branches of olive, or willow, or box, or whatever grows where they live.

[pause]

And in Jerusalem, every year, on Palm Sunday, thousands of Christians, from all over the world, walk in a great procession, from Bethphage, on the top of the Mount of Olives, down the steep road, singing, and waving palm branches, across the Kidron valley, and into the old city, through Saint Stephen's Gate, the Lions' Gate, as the crowds did, nearly two thousand years ago.

[long-pause]

The crowds went with Jesus into the city, the Gospels say, and the whole city was stirred, and asked: who is this? And the crowds said: this is the prophet Jesus, from Nazareth in Galilee.

[pause]

And in the evening, he went back out, with his friends, over the Mount of Olives, to Bethany, to spend the night.

[long-pause]

{img:mount-of-olives-slopes-olive-groves-tombs-view-temple-golden-morning}
To the east of Jerusalem, across the Kidron valley, there is a long hill, higher than the city: the Mount of Olives.

[pause]

In the time of Jesus, its slopes are covered with olive groves, silver-green, and with vineyards, and fig trees, and almond trees, and little villages, Bethphage, and Bethany, and with tombs, cut into the rock, because the Jews believe that the Messiah will come to Jerusalem by the Mount of Olives, and that the dead buried there will be the first to rise.

[long-pause]

From the top of the Mount of Olives, there is the most beautiful view of Jerusalem: the whole city, spread out, across the valley, with its walls, and its houses, and its palaces, and the Temple, shining, gold and white, on its great platform, directly opposite. Pilgrims coming from the east, from Jericho, see the city first from here.

[pause]

And to the east, on the other side, the view goes down, over the wilderness of Judea, to the Dead Sea, and the mountains of Moab, beyond, blue, and purple, and pink, at sunset.

[pause]

{img:olive-harvest-mount-of-olives-families-ladders-nets-autumn-sun}
The olive trees of the Mount of Olives are very important to Jerusalem. Their oil is used in the Temple, for the great lampstand, the menorah, and for the offerings, and in every house of the city, for cooking, and lamps, and the skin. In the autumn, families come out, to harvest the olives, beating the branches with long sticks, and gathering the olives from the ground, and taking them to the presses, like the press at Gethsemane, where great stone wheels crush them, and the oil runs out, golden green.

[long-pause]

At Passover, the Mount of Olives is covered with the tents, and shelters, and fires, of thousands of pilgrims, who cannot find room in the city. And at night, from the city walls, you can see their fires, all over the hill, like stars.

[pause]

Jesus, the Gospels say, often went to the Mount of Olives, with his friends, to rest, and pray, and teach. It was on the Mount of Olives, the Gospels say, that he sat with his disciples, looking at the Temple, across the valley, and told them that one day, not one stone would be left upon another.

[pause]

{img:pilgrim-fires-hillside-night-mount-of-olives-stars-city-walls}
And it was from the Mount of Olives, the Acts of the Apostles says, forty days after Easter, that he was taken up into heaven, while his disciples watched.

[pause]

Today, the Mount of Olives is covered with churches, and with a great Jewish cemetery, one of the oldest and largest in the world, with tens of thousands of graves, stretching down the hill, facing the Temple Mount. And from the top, the view of Jerusalem is still the most beautiful in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:temple-court-money-changers-tables-doves-cages-crowd-colonnade}
The next day, the Gospels tell, Jesus went into the Temple.

[pause]

In the great outer court, under the colonnades, there were the money changers, at their tables, and the sellers of doves, and sheep, and cattle, for the sacrifices. They were needed: pilgrims from abroad had to change their foreign money into the special coins used for the Temple tax, the silver shekels of Tyre, and buy animals for the sacrifices, which they could not bring with them, on the long journey.

[long-pause]

But Jesus, the Gospels say, was angry that the house of prayer had become a market. And he overturned the tables of the money changers, and the seats of those who sold doves, and drove out those who were buying and selling, and said: it is written, my house shall be called a house of prayer for all nations. But you have made it a den of thieves.

[pause]

It was a dramatic act, in the heart of the Temple, at the busiest time of the year, in front of thousands of people. And it alarmed the priests, and the Temple authorities.

[pause]

{img:teaching-temple-colonnade-crowd-listening-questions-afternoon-light}
For the next few days, the Gospels tell, Jesus came into the Temple every day, and taught, in the colonnades, and the crowds came to listen to him. The priests, and the scribes, and the Pharisees, and the Sadducees, came, and asked him difficult questions, to test him.

[long-pause]

They asked him: is it lawful to pay taxes to Caesar, or not? It was a trap. If he said yes, the people would turn against him, because they hated the Roman taxes. If he said no, the Romans could arrest him, as a rebel.

[pause]

And he asked them to show him a coin, a Roman silver denarius. And he asked: whose image is this, and whose inscription? And they said: Caesar's. And he said: then give to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's.

[pause]

{img:silver-denarius-tiberius-coin-palm-hand-close-soft-light}
And they marvelled at his answer.

[pause]

A scribe asked him: which is the greatest commandment? And he answered, with the words of the Law: you shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your mind, and with all your strength. And the second is this: you shall love your neighbour as yourself. There is no other commandment greater than these.

[long-pause]

Sitting near the treasury, in the Court of the Women, the Gospels say, he watched the people putting their offerings into the treasury chests. Many rich people put in large sums. And then a poor widow came, and put in two tiny copper coins, the smallest coins there were.

[pause]

And he called his friends, and said: this poor widow has put in more than all of them. Because they all gave out of their abundance. But she, out of her poverty, has put in everything she had, all she had to live on.

[long-pause]

{img:levites-singing-temple-steps-harps-lyres-cymbals-trumpets-priests}
The Temple of Jerusalem is full of music.

[pause]

Every day, at the morning and evening sacrifices, the Levites, the Temple singers and musicians, a special tribe of men, who serve in the Temple, stand on the fifteen great semicircular steps, between the Court of the Women and the Court of Israel, and sing the psalms, with harps, and lyres, and cymbals, and flutes, while the priests blow the silver trumpets.

[long-pause]

There is a choir of at least twelve men, the rabbis later remembered, and boys, the sons of the Levites, who stand beside them, to add their sweet, high voices to the singing.

[pause]

Each day of the week has its own psalm. On the first day of the week: the earth is the Lord's, and the fullness thereof. On the Sabbath: it is a good thing to give thanks unto the Lord, and to sing praises unto thy name, O most High.

[pause]

{img:silver-trumpets-priests-temple-court-dawn-light-crowd-bowing}
When the priests blow the trumpets, at the end of each section of the psalm, all the people in the Temple courts bow down, and worship, and then stand up again, and the singing goes on.

[long-pause]

At the great festivals, there is more music, and more singing. At Passover, while the lambs are being sacrificed, the Levites sing the Hallel, the psalms of praise, Psalms 113 to 118, again, and again, all afternoon. And the crowds join in, with the refrain: Hallelujah. Praise the Lord.

[pause]

At the Feast of Tabernacles, in the autumn, there is a great celebration, at night, in the Court of the Women, with huge golden lamps, on tall stands, lit, so bright that, the rabbis said, there was not a courtyard in Jerusalem that was not lit up by them. And the most respected men of Jerusalem danced, with torches in their hands, and sang, and the Levites played music, on the fifteen steps, all night long. The rabbis said: whoever has not seen the rejoicing of that night has never seen rejoicing in his life.

[pause]

{img:golden-lamps-night-court-temple-dancing-torches-festival-joy}
The psalms that the Levites sang in the Temple, two thousand years ago, are still sung, and read, every day, all over the world, by Jews, and Christians, in synagogues, and churches, and monasteries, and homes: The Lord is my shepherd, I shall not want. He maketh me to lie down in green pastures. He leadeth me beside the still waters. He restoreth my soul.

[long-pause]

{img:bethany-village-east-slope-mount-of-olives-stone-houses-evening}
Every evening, that week, the Gospels say, Jesus and his friends left the crowded city, and walked back over the Mount of Olives, to the little village of Bethany, about three kilometres away, on the eastern slope of the hill, on the road to Jericho, at the edge of the desert.

[pause]

There, they stayed with friends: Lazarus, and his sisters, Martha and Mary. A family Jesus loved, the Gospel of John says.

[long-pause]

Bethany is a quiet little village, of stone houses, and courtyards, and olive trees, and fig trees, and almond trees, looking out east, over the wilderness of Judea, and the Jordan valley, and the mountains of Moab, beyond.

[pause]

In the evenings, after the noise and the crowds of the city, and the Temple, it must have been a peaceful place to come back to: the quiet of the village, the friendly faces, the meal, the lamps, the stars over the desert.

[pause]

The Gospels tell of a supper, at Bethany, in the house of Simon, a man who had been cured of leprosy, a few days before Passover. While they were eating, a woman came in, with an alabaster jar of very expensive perfume, pure nard, from the Himalayas, worth almost a year's wages. And she broke the jar, and poured the perfume on Jesus's head, and on his feet, and wiped his feet with her hair. And the whole house was filled with the scent of the perfume.

[long-pause]

Some of the disciples were indignant, and said that the perfume could have been sold, and the money given to the poor. But Jesus said: leave her alone. She has done a beautiful thing for me. The poor you will always have with you, but you will not always have me. She has anointed my body beforehand for burial. And wherever the gospel is preached, in all the world, what she has done will be told, in memory of her.

[pause]

Today, in Bethany, which is now an Arab town, called al-Eizariya, the town of Lazarus, there is a church, and a mosque, and an old tomb, cut into the rock, which, since ancient times, has been shown to pilgrims as the tomb of Lazarus.

[pause]

{img:alabaster-perfume-jar-broken-scent-oil-table-lamplight-close}
And in the evenings, in Holy Week, there were the conversations, by the lamps, with his friends, in the house, about what was going to happen. Because the Gospels say that Jesus knew.

[long-pause]

{img:upper-room-passover-meal-low-table-lamps-bread-wine-friends-evening}
On the Thursday evening, the Gospels tell, Jesus ate a last meal with his friends, in Jerusalem, in a large upper room, furnished, and ready, in a house in the city.

[pause]

Two of his disciples had gone ahead, that morning, into the city, to prepare it. Jesus had told them: you will meet a man carrying a jar of water. Follow him. Which was a sign, because carrying water was usually the work of women. And he led them to the house, and the upper room.

[long-pause]

The meal was eaten in the evening, by the light of oil lamps, reclining on couches, or cushions, around a low table, as people did at festive meals, leaning on their left elbows, and eating with their right hands.

[pause]

On the table, there was bread, and wine, and perhaps the bitter herbs, and the other foods of the Passover meal. The first three Gospels say that it was the Passover meal itself. The Gospel of John says that it was the evening before.

[pause]

The Gospel of John tells that, during the meal, Jesus got up, and took off his outer cloak, and tied a towel around his waist, and poured water into a basin, and began to wash his disciples' feet, and to dry them with the towel, as a servant would do. Peter protested: Lord, you will never wash my feet. But Jesus said: if I, your Lord, and teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's feet. I have given you an example.

[pause]

And he said: a new commandment I give you: love one another, as I have loved you.

[long-pause]

And then, during the meal, the Gospels say, he took bread, and gave thanks, and broke it, and gave it to them, and said: take, eat, this is my body, which is given for you. Do this in remembrance of me.

[pause]

And after supper, he took the cup of wine, and gave thanks, and gave it to them, and said: drink from it, all of you. This is my blood of the covenant, poured out for many. And they all drank from it.

[pause]

{img:bread-broken-wine-cup-table-lamplight-quiet-hands-close}
This meal is remembered, by Christians, all over the world, every week, or every day, for two thousand years: the Lord's Supper, the Eucharist, the Mass, Holy Communion, with bread, and wine, in churches, and chapels, and homes, in every language, and every land.

[long-pause]

The Gospels also say that, at the meal, Jesus said that one of them would betray him. And they were very sad, and asked him, one after another: surely not I, Lord? And one of the twelve, Judas Iscariot, went out, into the night.

[pause]

At the end of the meal, they sang a hymn, the Hallel, the psalms of praise, which are sung at the end of the Passover meal. And then, late in the evening, they went out of the city, across the Kidron valley, to the Mount of Olives, under the full moon of Passover.

[pause]

{img:full-moon-over-kidron-valley-mount-of-olives-figures-walking-night}
Today, on Mount Zion, in Jerusalem, there is a medieval building, an upper room, with Gothic arches, built by the Crusaders, called the Cenacle, which, according to tradition, stands on the site of the room of the Last Supper. Pilgrims from all over the world come to visit it.

[long-pause]

{img:qumran-caves-cliffs-dead-sea-desert-shepherd-boy-goats-1947}
In the winter of 1946 to 1947, a young Bedouin shepherd, Muhammed edh-Dhib, was looking after his goats, in the cliffs, above the north western shore of the Dead Sea, near a place called Qumran, when one of his goats wandered off.

[pause]

Looking for it, he threw a stone into a cave, in the cliff, and heard something break. And when he climbed into the cave, he found tall clay jars, with lids. And inside some of them, there were old scrolls, of leather, wrapped in linen.

[long-pause]

They were the oldest Biblical manuscripts ever found: the Dead Sea Scrolls.

[pause]

Over the next ten years, archaeologists, and Bedouin, searched the caves around Qumran, and found eleven caves with scrolls, and fragments of scrolls: about nine hundred different documents, in Hebrew, and Aramaic, and Greek, written between about 250 before Christ, and the year 68 after Christ: in the time of Jesus.

[pause]

{img:ancient-scroll-unrolled-hebrew-columns-museum-glass-soft-light}
They include copies of every book of the Jewish Bible, except one, the book of Esther, a thousand years older than any copies known before. A great scroll of the prophet Isaiah, almost complete, seven metres long. And many other writings: hymns, and prayers, and commentaries, and rules for a community, and calendars, and a strange scroll of copper, which lists hidden treasures, which have never been found.

[long-pause]

Many scholars think that the scrolls belonged to a Jewish community, called the Essenes, who lived at Qumran, in the desert, by the Dead Sea, in the time of Jesus, apart from the world, in strict purity, and shared everything they had, and prayed, and studied the scriptures, and copied them, and waited for the end of the world, and the coming of the Messiah. The ruins of their settlement can still be seen, with its ritual baths, and its dining room, and its scriptorium, where the scrolls may have been written.

[pause]

When the Romans came, in the year 68, during the great rebellion, the people of Qumran, it seems, hid their scrolls in the caves, in jars, hoping to come back for them. They never did.

[pause]

{img:shrine-of-the-book-white-dome-museum-jerusalem-later-garden}
Today, the scrolls are kept in the Israel Museum, in Jerusalem, in a special building, called the Shrine of the Book, with a white dome, shaped like the lid of one of the jars in which they were found. And through them, we can hear the voices of the people who lived, and prayed, and hoped, in the land of Israel, in the time of Jesus.

[long-pause]

{img:garden-gethsemane-ancient-olive-trees-night-moonlight-quiet}
At the foot of the Mount of Olives, across the Kidron valley, from the city, and the Temple, there is an olive grove, with an olive press. In Aramaic, it is called Gat Shemanim: Gethsemane, the oil press.

[pause]

Jesus came there often, the Gospels say, with his friends, to rest, and pray, away from the crowds.

[long-pause]

That night, the night of Passover, under the full moon, he went there with eleven of his friends. And he said to them: sit here, while I go and pray. And he took Peter, and James, and John, a little further, and said to them: my soul is very sorrowful, even to death. Stay here, and keep watch with me.

[pause]

And he went a little further, about a stone's throw, and knelt down, alone, among the olive trees, and prayed: Father, if you are willing, take this cup from me. Yet not my will, but yours, be done.

[pause]

{img:figure-kneeling-praying-alone-olive-grove-moonlight-shadows}
When he came back, he found his friends asleep, exhausted, after the long day, and the meal, and the wine, and the sorrow. And he said to Peter: could you not watch with me for one hour? The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak. And he went away, and prayed again. And came back, and found them asleep again. Three times.

[long-pause]

And then, the Gospels say, there were lights, and torches, and lanterns, coming through the olive grove, and the sound of voices: a crowd, sent by the chief priests, with Temple guards, with swords and clubs, led by Judas, who came up to Jesus, and kissed him, as a sign, so that the guards would know which one to take.

[pause]

There was a moment of confusion. One of the disciples drew a sword. But Jesus told him to put it away. And the guards took Jesus, and led him away, back across the valley, to the city. And all his friends fled, into the night.

[pause]

{img:torches-lanterns-olive-grove-night-distant-crowd-quiet-tension}
Today, in Gethsemane, at the foot of the Mount of Olives, there is a beautiful church, the Church of All Nations, with a golden mosaic on its front, built in 1924, with money from many countries. And beside it, there is a little garden, with eight very old olive trees, gnarled, and twisted, and hollow, which some scientists believe are about nine hundred years old, perhaps grown from the roots of even older trees.

[pause]

And people come, from all over the world, to sit there, quietly, among the old olive trees, in the evening, and pray, and think about that night.

[long-pause]

{img:courtyard-high-priest-house-fire-servants-warming-night-peter}
That night, the Gospels say, Jesus was taken to the house of the High Priest, in the Upper City, where some of the priests, and elders, had gathered.

[pause]

Peter had followed, at a distance, and came into the courtyard of the house, and stood, with the servants, and the guards, by a fire, which they had lit, in the cold spring night, warming himself.

[long-pause]

And a servant girl looked at him, in the firelight, and said: you were with him, the man from Galilee. And Peter, afraid, said: I do not know him. And a little later, another said: you are one of them, your accent gives you away, you are from Galilee. And Peter said again: I do not know the man. And a third time.

[pause]

And then, the Gospels say, a rooster crowed, in the darkness, before dawn. And Peter remembered that Jesus had said to him, at supper: before the rooster crows, you will deny me three times. And he went out, into the night, and wept bitterly.

[pause]

{img:rooster-on-stone-wall-before-dawn-grey-light-city-quiet}
Today, on the eastern slope of Mount Zion, there is a church, called Saint Peter in Gallicantu, Saint Peter of the cock's crow, with a golden rooster on its roof, in memory of that night. And beside it, there is an old stone stairway, from the time of Jesus, going down the hill, toward the Kidron valley, which he may have walked on.

[long-pause]

In the morning, the priests took Jesus to the Roman governor, Pontius Pilate, because only the Romans could put someone to death.

[pause]

We will tell what happened next only briefly, and gently. The Gospels say that Pilate questioned him, and asked him: are you the king of the Jews? And Jesus answered: my kingdom is not of this world. And Pilate asked: what is truth? And Pilate, the Gospels say, found no fault in him, and wanted to release him, because at Passover, he would release one prisoner. But the crowd, gathered outside, asked for another prisoner, called Barabbas, instead.

[pause]

{img:roman-governor-washing-hands-basin-courtyard-morning-crowd-distant}
And in the end, Pilate gave in, and washed his hands, before the crowd, the Gospel of Matthew says, and said: I am innocent of this man's blood. And he handed Jesus over, to be crucified, which was the Roman punishment for rebels, and slaves.

[long-pause]

The soldiers, the Gospels say, mocked him, and put a purple cloak on him, and a crown of thorns, and called him the king of the Jews. And then they led him out, carrying the beam of his cross, through the streets of the city, toward a place outside the walls, called Golgotha, the place of the skull.

[pause]

And on the way, they made a man from Cyrene, in North Africa, called Simon, who was coming into the city, from the countryside, carry the cross for him. And a great crowd followed, and many women, who wept for him.

[long-pause]

{img:distant-hill-outside-city-walls-three-crosses-dark-sky-far-away}
We will not go close, to Golgotha. We will watch from very far away, as the women did, the Gospels say, who stood at a distance, and watched: Mary Magdalene, and Mary the mother of James, and Salome, and many other women, who had followed him from Galilee, and cared for him.

[pause]

The Gospel of John says that his mother, Mary, was there too, standing near the cross, with her sister, and Mary Magdalene, and John, the disciple whom Jesus loved.

[long-pause]

It was about nine o'clock in the morning, the Gospel of Mark says, when they crucified him, on a little rise of ground, a rocky hill, an old quarry, just outside the walls of the city, beside a road, where everyone passing could see. Above his head, the Romans put a sign, in Hebrew, and Latin, and Greek, saying: Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews.

[pause]

The Gospels record a few of the words that he spoke, on the cross. To the soldiers: Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing. To a man crucified beside him, who asked him to remember him: today, you will be with me in paradise. To his mother, and his friend John, standing near: woman, here is your son. And to John: here is your mother. And from that hour, the Gospel says, John took her into his own home.

[pause]

{img:women-standing-at-distance-hillside-grey-sky-silent-mourning}
At midday, the Gospels say, darkness came over the whole land, until three o'clock in the afternoon, the time of the evening sacrifice in the Temple, when the trumpets sounded, and the Passover lambs were being offered.

[pause]

And at three o'clock, Jesus cried out, with a loud voice, and said: it is finished. Father, into your hands I commit my spirit. And he passed away.

[long-pause]

The Gospels say that the curtain of the Temple, the great veil, before the Holy of Holies, was torn in two, from top to bottom. And that the Roman centurion, the officer, who stood facing him, and saw how he died, said: truly, this man was the Son of God.

[pause]

And then, in the late afternoon, before sunset, when the Sabbath, and the Passover, would begin, a rich man, a member of the council, called Joseph of Arimathea, a secret follower of Jesus, went to Pilate, bravely, and asked for his body. And Pilate gave it to him.

[pause]

{img:rock-cut-tomb-garden-olive-trees-round-stone-evening-light}
Joseph, and another member of the council, Nicodemus, took the body down, gently, and wrapped it in a clean linen cloth, with spices, myrrh, and aloes, a great quantity of them, according to the Jewish custom of burial. And they laid it in a new tomb, cut in the rock, in a garden, near the place where he was crucified, which belonged to Joseph, where no one had ever been laid. And they rolled a great stone, across the door of the tomb.

[long-pause]

The women, Mary Magdalene, and the other Mary, sat there, opposite the tomb, and watched where he was laid. And then they went home, before the sun set, to prepare spices, and ointments. And on the Sabbath, they rested, according to the commandment.

[long-pause]

{img:jerusalem-sabbath-quiet-streets-shuttered-houses-morning-pigeons}
The next day was the Sabbath, and the first day of the great festival of Passover.

[pause]

In the city, it was a holy day, a day of rest. No one worked. The shops were closed. The markets were empty. The families who had eaten the Passover meal the evening before, or that evening, rested, and went up to the Temple, to pray, and sang the psalms, and visited each other, in their best clothes, and walked, quietly, through the streets.

[long-pause]

For the friends of Jesus, it was the saddest day of their lives.

[pause]

They were hiding, in a house, in the city, with the doors locked, afraid that they would be arrested too. They had lost their teacher, their friend, the man they had followed for years, and believed in, and hoped would save Israel. Everything was over. They did not know what to do, or where to go. Perhaps they would go home, to Galilee, to their boats, and their nets, and their old lives.

[pause]

The women waited, too, for the Sabbath to end, so that they could go to the tomb, and anoint his body, with the spices, and ointments, which they had prepared.

[pause]

And Peter, perhaps, sat alone, remembering the rooster, and the fire in the courtyard, and the three times he had said: I do not know him.

[long-pause]

Outside, the city went on, with its festival. The pilgrims ate their unleavened bread, and their roasted lamb. The Temple was full of worshippers. The priests offered the festival sacrifices. The Roman soldiers watched from the Antonia fortress. Pilate stayed in Herod's palace. The full moon of Passover rose, again, over the Mount of Olives.

[pause]

{img:full-moon-rising-over-mount-of-olives-quiet-city-roofs-night}
And in the garden, outside the city wall, the tomb was quiet, with its great stone rolled across the door, and, the Gospel of Matthew says, a guard of soldiers, sent by Pilate, at the request of the chief priests, to watch it.

[pause]

It was the quietest day of the story. Christians all over the world still remember it, every year, as Holy Saturday: a day of waiting, and silence, and stillness, between the sorrow of Friday, and the joy of Easter morning.

[long-pause]

{img:rock-cut-tomb-hillside-rolling-stone-door-garden-olive-trees}
How were the dead buried, in Jerusalem, in the time of Jesus?

[pause]

The families of Jerusalem, who could afford it, had family tombs, cut into the rock, of the hillsides, around the city, outside the walls. Because, by Jewish law, the dead must be buried outside the city.

[long-pause]

A tomb is like a little cave, cut into the limestone, with a small, low doorway, which can be closed with a great round stone, like a millstone, rolled in a groove, across the entrance, or with a square stone, like a plug. Inside, there is a small room, with a bench, or a pit, in the middle, so that people can stand, and around the walls, there are niches, long, narrow shelves, cut into the rock, for the bodies, or benches, under arches.

[pause]

When a person died, the family washed the body, and anointed it with oils, and spices, and wrapped it in linen cloths, and laid it in the tomb, on a bench, or in a niche, on the day of death, before sunset, if possible. And they mourned, for seven days, at home, and came to visit the tomb.

[pause]

{img:ossuaries-stone-boxes-niche-tomb-interior-soft-lamp-light}
And a year later, when only the bones were left, the family would come back, and gather the bones, and place them in a little stone box, an ossuary, carved of limestone, often decorated, with rosettes, and patterns, and with the name of the person scratched on it, and put it in a niche, so that the space could be used again, for the next member of the family.

[long-pause]

Hundreds of these tombs have been found, around Jerusalem, and thousands of ossuaries, with names, in Hebrew, and Aramaic, and Greek: Mary, and Martha, and Joseph, and Simon, and John, and Jesus, and Judas, which were all very common names, in the time of Jesus. One of them, as we have heard, belonged to the High Priest, Caiaphas.

[pause]

In the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, behind the tomb that is honoured as the tomb of Jesus, there is a little chapel, with an old rock-cut tomb, of the first century, with niches for bodies, cut into the rock: a tomb like the ones the Gospels describe, still there, inside the great church.

[pause]

{img:quiet-garden-dawn-jerusalem-old-stones-birds-spring-calm}
And outside the walls of the Old City, north of the Damascus Gate, there is a beautiful, quiet garden, called the Garden Tomb, with an old rock-cut tomb, discovered in the nineteenth century, which some Protestant Christians believe may be the tomb of Jesus. Archaeologists think it is much older. But it is a peaceful place, with trees, and flowers, and birdsong, where pilgrims come, especially on Easter morning, at dawn, to sing, and pray, in the garden.

[long-pause]

{img:early-morning-garden-tomb-stone-rolled-away-dawn-light-women-spices}
And then, the Gospels tell, very early in the morning, on the first day of the week, the Sunday, when it was still dark, the women went to the tomb, carrying the spices, which they had prepared: Mary Magdalene, and Mary the mother of James, and Salome, and Joanna, and others.

[pause]

On the way, they were worried, and said to each other: who will roll away the stone for us, from the door of the tomb? Because it was very large.

[long-pause]

But when they came to the garden, in the first grey light of dawn, they saw that the stone had already been rolled away. And the tomb was empty.

[pause]

The Gospels tell the story in different ways, with different details, as people do, who remember something extraordinary. They tell of a young man, in a white robe, or two men, in dazzling clothes, angels, sitting in the tomb, who said to the women: do not be afraid. You are looking for Jesus of Nazareth, who was crucified. He is not here. He has risen. Why do you look for the living among the dead?

[pause]

And the women ran, the Gospels say, trembling, and astonished, with fear, and great joy, to tell the disciples. And Peter, and John, ran to the tomb, and John ran faster, and got there first, and looked in, and saw the linen cloths lying there, and the cloth that had been around his head, folded up, by itself. And they believed.

[long-pause]

The Gospel of John tells the most beautiful part of the story.

[pause]

Mary Magdalene stayed outside the tomb, weeping, after the others had gone. And she turned around, and saw a man standing there, in the garden, in the early morning light. She thought he was the gardener. And he said to her: woman, why are you weeping? Whom are you looking for? And she said: sir, if you have carried him away, tell me where you have laid him, and I will take him away.

[pause]

{img:woman-in-garden-dawn-turning-recognizing-figure-light-flowers}
And he said to her: Mary.

[pause]

And she turned, and knew him, and said: Rabbouni, which means teacher.

[long-pause]

That is the story of the first Easter morning, as the Gospels tell it.

[pause]

Historians cannot prove, or disprove, what happened, that morning, in the garden. But they agree that something changed the frightened, hiding, grieving friends of Jesus, so completely, that within a few weeks, they were standing up in the streets, and the Temple courts, of Jerusalem, telling everyone that he was alive. And that they went on telling it, all over the world, for the rest of their lives, whatever it cost them.

[pause]

{img:sunrise-over-jerusalem-hills-golden-light-garden-birds-spring}
And every year, on Easter morning, all over the world, Christians greet each other with the old words: Christ is risen. And the answer: He is risen indeed.

[long-pause]

{img:magdala-town-shore-sea-of-galilee-fish-drying-racks-boats}
Mary Magdalene is one of the most important people in the Easter story.

[pause]

She came from Magdala, a busy fishing town, on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee, famous for its salted fish, which were sent all over the Roman world. Her name means Mary of Magdala. The Gospel of Luke says that Jesus had healed her, of seven demons, perhaps a serious illness, and that she followed him, with other women, and supported him, and his disciples, with her own money.

[long-pause]

She was there, at the cross, the Gospels say, when most of the disciples had fled. She was there, at the burial. And she was the first, all four Gospels say, to come to the tomb, on Easter morning, and, in the Gospel of John, the first to see the risen Jesus, in the garden, and the first to tell the others. For that reason, the early Christians called her the apostle to the apostles.

[pause]

In later centuries, people confused her with other women in the Gospels, and said that she had been a sinful woman. But there is nothing in the Gospels to say so. In 2016, the Pope made her feast day, on the twenty-second of July, a great feast, equal to the feasts of the apostles.

[pause]

In 2009, archaeologists digging at Magdala, by the Sea of Galilee, found the remains of a synagogue, from the time of Jesus, one of the oldest ever found in Galilee, with a mosaic floor, and painted walls, and benches, and a beautiful carved stone, with a picture of the menorah, the seven-branched lampstand of the Temple. A synagogue where Jesus may have taught, and Mary Magdalene may have prayed.

[long-pause]

There is an old legend, from the Greek Orthodox Church, that, after the resurrection, Mary Magdalene went to Rome, to see the emperor Tiberius, and told him that Christ had risen. And she held out an egg, as a symbol of new life. And the emperor laughed, and said that a man could no more rise from the dead than the egg in her hand could turn red. And at that moment, the egg turned red.

[pause]

{img:red-painted-easter-eggs-basket-candles-table-orthodox-spring}
And that, the legend says, is why, in Greece, and Russia, and many other countries, people paint their Easter eggs red, and knock them together, on Easter morning, and say: Christ is risen.

[long-pause]

{img:two-travellers-road-to-emmaus-hills-afternoon-stranger-walking}
On the afternoon of that same Sunday, the Gospel of Luke tells, two of the friends of Jesus were walking from Jerusalem, to a village called Emmaus, about eleven kilometres away, in the hills, to the west.

[pause]

One of them was called Cleopas. They were talking, sadly, about everything that had happened. And as they walked, a stranger came up, and walked with them, along the road, and asked them what they were talking about.

[long-pause]

They stopped, and looked sad. And Cleopas said: are you the only visitor to Jerusalem who does not know the things that have happened there in these days? And they told him about Jesus of Nazareth, a prophet, mighty in deed and word, and how the chief priests and the rulers had handed him over, to be crucified. And they said: we had hoped that he was the one who would redeem Israel.

[pause]

And they told him, too, that some women had gone to the tomb that morning, and found it empty, and said they had seen angels, who said that he was alive.

[pause]

{img:dusty-hill-road-judean-hills-olive-terraces-evening-golden-walkers}
And the stranger walked with them, and talked with them, and explained the scriptures to them, all along the road, as the afternoon light grew golden, and the shadows grew long, across the hills.

[long-pause]

When they came near the village, he made as if to go on further. But they urged him: stay with us, for it is nearly evening, and the day is almost over. So he went in to stay with them.

[pause]

And when he was at the table with them, he took the bread, and blessed it, and broke it, and gave it to them. And their eyes were opened, and they recognized him. And then he vanished from their sight.

[pause]

{img:simple-house-table-bread-broken-oil-lamp-evening-two-amazed}
And they said to each other: did not our hearts burn within us, while he talked to us on the road, and opened the scriptures to us?

[pause]

And they got up, that same hour, and walked all the way back to Jerusalem, in the dark, eleven kilometres, to tell the others.

[long-pause]

Nobody knows exactly where Emmaus was. There are several villages, in the hills west of Jerusalem, which have been shown to pilgrims, over the centuries, as Emmaus, with old churches, and ruins.

[pause]

But the story of the two friends, walking along the road, at evening, sad, and talking, with a stranger beside them, whom they did not recognize, until he broke the bread, is one of the most loved stories in the Gospels. Painters have painted it, again and again: Caravaggio, and Rembrandt, who painted it several times, by the light of a single candle, in a dark room, with the bread, and the two friends, startled, and the quiet light around the face of the stranger.

[long-pause]

{img:pentecost-crowd-jerusalem-street-apostles-speaking-many-nations}
Fifty days after Passover, in the early summer, there was another great festival in Jerusalem: the Feast of Weeks, called in Greek Pentecost, the fiftieth day, the festival of the wheat harvest, when the first loaves of bread from the new wheat were offered in the Temple. Again, the city was full of pilgrims, from all over the world.

[pause]

And on that day, the Acts of the Apostles says, the friends of Jesus, gathered together in a house, felt the Holy Spirit come upon them, like a rushing wind, and like tongues of fire. And they went out into the streets, and began to speak to the crowds, about Jesus, and his resurrection. And the pilgrims, from many lands, heard them, each in their own language.

[long-pause]

Peter, the fisherman, who had said, on that dark night, three times, I do not know him, now stood up, in front of the crowds, and spoke boldly. And, the Acts of the Apostles says, about three thousand people were baptized that day.

[pause]

That was the beginning of the Christian church: a small community, of Jews, in Jerusalem, who believed that Jesus was the Messiah, and had risen from the dead, and who met together, in their houses, to pray, and break bread, and share everything they had, and went up to the Temple, every day, to pray.

[pause]

{img:early-christians-house-meal-sharing-bread-lamps-courtyard-evening}
From Jerusalem, in the following years, the message spread: to Samaria, and Galilee, and Damascus, and Antioch, and then, through the journeys of Paul, and others, to Asia Minor, and Greece, and Rome, and, within three hundred years, to the whole Roman Empire, and beyond.

[long-pause]

And Jerusalem itself?

[pause]

Thirty-three years later, in the year 66, the Jews of Judea rebelled against Rome. And in the year 70, after a long siege, the Roman army, under Titus, the future emperor who would open the Colosseum, captured Jerusalem, and destroyed the city, and the Temple. We will not describe it. The Temple was never rebuilt.

[pause]

{img:western-wall-great-herodian-stones-morning-light-pigeons-quiet}
Only part of the great retaining wall, of the platform of the Temple Mount, built by Herod, survived: the Western Wall, with its enormous, carefully cut stones. For nearly two thousand years, it has been the holiest place where Jews can pray, and Jews from all over the world come there, to pray, and to place little folded notes, with prayers, between the ancient stones.

[long-pause]

And in the year 135, after another rebellion, the Romans built a new Roman city, on the ruins of Jerusalem, called Aelia Capitolina, and built a temple to Venus, on the place where, the Christians remembered, Jesus had been crucified, and buried.

[pause]

Two hundred years later, the first Christian emperor, Constantine, ordered the temple of Venus to be taken down. And his workmen found, underneath, a tomb, cut into the rock, which the Christians of Jerusalem believed to be the tomb of Jesus. And Constantine built a great church there: the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, which still stands today.

[long-pause]

{img:jerusalem-old-city-today-dome-of-rock-walls-mount-of-olives-view}
Today, Jerusalem is a great city, holy to three religions, Jews, Christians, and Muslims, with nearly a million people.

[pause]

The Old City, inside its walls, built by the Ottoman Sultan Suleiman the Magnificent, in the sixteenth century, is divided into four quarters: Jewish, Christian, Muslim, and Armenian. It is a World Heritage Site, a maze of narrow, stone-paved streets, and markets, and churches, and mosques, and synagogues, and old houses, and courtyards, crowded with people, and pilgrims, and visitors, from all over the world.

[long-pause]

On the Temple Mount, where Herod's Temple once stood, there are now two of the holiest places of Islam: the golden Dome of the Rock, built in the seventh century, and the al-Aqsa Mosque. Below them, the Western Wall, where Jews pray, every day, and every night.

[pause]

And in the Christian Quarter, there is the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, a great, dark, ancient, crowded church, built and rebuilt over sixteen centuries, which contains, under one roof, both the little hill of Golgotha, and the tomb, which, since the fourth century, Christians have honoured as the places where Jesus was crucified, and buried. Six different Christian churches share it, and each has its own chapels, and its own times for worship, and the key to the great door has been kept, for centuries, by a Muslim family, who open it every morning, and close it every night.

[pause]

Through the Old City, there is the Via Dolorosa, the way of sorrows, the route that, according to tradition, Jesus walked, carrying his cross, from the place where Pilate judged him, to Golgotha, with fourteen stations, marked on the walls, and the pavements, where pilgrims stop, and pray. Every Friday, the Franciscan friars lead a procession along it.

[long-pause]

At Easter, Jerusalem is full of pilgrims again, as it was in the year 33.

[pause]

On Palm Sunday, they walk down the Mount of Olives, with palm branches, singing. On Holy Thursday, they gather in the Upper Room, and in Gethsemane, among the old olive trees. On Good Friday, thousands of them walk the Via Dolorosa, some carrying wooden crosses. And on the Saturday, the Orthodox Christians gather, in the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, for the ceremony of the Holy Fire, when, they believe, a flame appears in the tomb, and is passed, from candle to candle, through the great church, until thousands of candles are burning, and the light is carried out, into the city, and flown, on special planes, to Orthodox churches in Greece, and Russia, and other lands.

[pause]

{img:candles-held-crowd-easter-church-holy-fire-glow-faces}
And at the same time, in the Jewish Quarter, the families are celebrating Passover, with the Passover meal, the Seder, with the unleavened bread, and the bitter herbs, and the four cups of wine, and the questions of the children, and the story of the escape from Egypt, as they have done for more than three thousand years. And at the end of the meal, they say, as they have said for two thousand years, all over the world: next year, in Jerusalem.

[long-pause]

So we leave Jerusalem tonight, as the spring evening falls over the hills of Judea, and the bells of the churches ring, and the old olive trees of Gethsemane stand silver in the moonlight, at the foot of the Mount of Olives.

[pause]

The pilgrims singing on the roads, the families on their roofs at sunset, the women in the garden at dawn, and two friends walking home to Emmaus, with their hearts burning within them.
