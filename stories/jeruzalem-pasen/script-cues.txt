Tonight we go back nearly two thousand years. We go to the city of Jerusalem, in the spring of the year 33. It is the week before the great festival of Passover.

[long-pause]

The city sits on the hills of Judea. It is built of pale stone that turns gold in the sun. Above the roofs rises the great Temple, white and gold. The roads are full of families. They sing old songs as they walk. Tents cover the hillsides, and the smoke of supper fires drifts over the valleys.

[pause]

And this week, a teacher from Galilee is coming to the city. He comes with his friends, riding on a young donkey. What happens to him in the next few days will be remembered every spring, for two thousand years. We call it Easter.

[pause]

But nobody in the city knows that yet. For now, it is just a busy festival week. And under the busy streets, something is slowly getting tense.

[pause]

Tonight we will walk through Jerusalem slowly, in those days. We will see the city as the pilgrims saw it.

[pause]

If you like these quiet journeys into the past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, and to come back for the next one.

[long-pause]

Jerusalem stands high in the hills. It is about eight hundred metres above the sea, right on the edge of the desert.

[pause]

To the west, the hills slope down to the sea. There are olive trees on the slopes, and vineyards, and small stone villages. To the east, the land drops away fast. That side is the wilderness. It is bare and brown and empty. Deep dry valleys run down to the Jordan river and the Dead Sea. The Dead Sea is the lowest place on earth. On a clear day you can see it from the hill east of the city. It shines far below. And the mountains behind it turn pink in the evening.

[long-pause]

In spring, the hills are green. The winter rains have just stopped. Now there are wild flowers everywhere. There are red anemones and poppies. There are white daisies and small yellow flowers among the rocks. The almond trees have bloomed. The fig trees have their first small leaves. And the barley in the fields is almost ripe.

[pause]

{img:spring-wildflowers-red-anemones-hillside-jerusalem-distance}
The air is fresh and clear. The days are warm, but the nights are still cool. And the whole city is waiting for the festival.

[long-pause]

Jerusalem is the holy city of the Jewish people. Perhaps forty thousand people live there. But at Passover, the city grows and grows. Pilgrims come from all over the land. They also come from far away, from Egypt and Babylon and Greece and Rome. The old Law of Moses asks them to keep the festival in the holy city. So they come.

[pause]

For one week, Jerusalem may be the most crowded city on earth.

[pause]

{img:pilgrims-roads-to-jerusalem-families-donkeys-singing-psalms}
It is a very old city. King David made it his capital a thousand years before. His son Solomon built the first Temple here. Later the city was destroyed and built again. Many armies fought over it. And now, in the year 33, it is ruled by Rome. Not everyone is happy about that. And with so many people in one place, the Romans are watching closely.

[long-pause]

{img:ship-arriving-joppa-harbour-pilgrims-disembarking-spring-sea}
At Passover, Jerusalem is full of Jews from all over the world.

[pause]

By this time, more Jews live outside the land than inside it. Many live in Egypt, in the big city of Alexandria. Many live in Babylon, far to the east. Their families were taken there long ago, and many simply stayed. Others live in Syria and Greece, on the islands, in North Africa and in Rome.

[long-pause]

Many of them speak Greek at home. But they read the same holy books. They keep the same day of rest. And every year they send a small coin to the Temple, as a gift. Many of them hope to make the long trip to Jerusalem at least once in their lives.

[pause]

Some come by ship. They land at the port of Joppa, which today is called Jaffa. Then they walk up through the hills. Others come by land, in long caravans across the desert. That trip can take many weeks. Along the way there are storms at sea and robbers on the roads. Not everyone arrives safely. So when the travellers finally see the city on its hill, many of them cry with joy.

[pause]

The Bible later lists the people who were in Jerusalem in those days. There were people from Persia and from Asia Minor. There were people from Egypt and Libya, from Rome and Crete, and from Arabia. It sounds like the whole world came to one small city.

[long-pause]

In Jerusalem there are meeting houses for the Jews from different lands. These are called synagogues. People pray there, and meet friends, and find a place to sleep. In 1913, people digging in the old city found a stone with Greek writing on it. It is from the time of Jesus. It says that a priest called Theodotus built a synagogue. It also had a guest house, with rooms and water, for visitors from far away.

[pause]

{img:greek-inscription-stone-synagogue-theodotus-museum-close-light}
So the pilgrims had places to stay and wash and pray. They were among people who spoke their own language. And when they went home after the festival, they took the news with them. Whatever happened in Jerusalem that spring would travel to every corner of the world.

[long-pause]

{img:herods-temple-gleaming-white-gold-facade-courts-colonnades-mount}
Above everything in Jerusalem rises the Temple.

[pause]

It stands on a huge flat platform on top of a hill. This is the Temple Mount. The platform is almost five hundred metres long. It is the biggest holy place in the ancient world. Great walls hold it up. Some of the stones in those walls are as heavy as a hundred elephants.

[long-pause]

The Temple was made bigger by King Herod the Great. He started the work about fifty years before. Herod was a cruel man, but he was a great builder. The rabbis later said this. If you have not seen Herod's Temple, you have never seen a beautiful building.

[pause]

The work is still going on in the year 33. It will only be finished thirty years later. And just seven years after that, the Temple will be gone. But nobody walking here this spring can imagine that.

[pause]

{img:royal-stoa-colonnade-temple-mount-tall-columns-crowds-shade}
Around the edge of the platform there are long covered walkways. They have tall marble columns and roofs of carved cedar wood. On the south side is the biggest one, a great hall with four rows of columns. Here the money changers sit at their tables. Here people sell doves and lambs for the offerings. And here the teachers of the Law sit and talk and argue.

[long-pause]

The big open yard is for everyone, Jews and foreigners. In the middle, there is a low stone wall with signs on it. The signs say, in Greek and Latin, that foreigners may go no further. The warning is serious. Two of these stones have been found by archaeologists. Archaeologists are people who dig up the past.

[pause]

Past the low wall, there are more yards, one inside the other. First the yard where women and men may come. Then the yard for Jewish men. Then the yard of the priests, with the great stone altar. Each yard is a little more holy than the one before.

[pause]

{img:temple-sanctuary-facade-golden-vine-veil-steps-priests-white}
And at the top of twelve steps is the holy house itself. It is tall and white, about fifty metres high. The front is covered with plates of gold. A writer of that time, Josephus, says the gold shone so brightly in the morning that people had to look away. Over the great door hangs a golden vine. Its golden grapes are as big as a man.

[long-pause]

Inside, there is a golden lamp with seven branches. There is a golden table for holy bread. And at the back hangs a great curtain. It is blue and purple and red, and stars are sewn into it. Behind the curtain is the most holy room of all. It is small and dark and empty. Only the High Priest may go in, and only once a year.

[pause]

From far away, Josephus says, the Temple looked like a mountain covered with snow. Where it was not gold, it was pure white.

[long-pause]

{img:herodium-cone-shaped-hill-palace-fortress-desert-bethlehem-view}
The man who made Jerusalem so splendid was King Herod the Great. He passed away about thirty-seven years before our story.

[pause]

Herod was not born a prince. His father was an official from the south. His mother was an Arab princess. But Herod was clever, and he wanted power. He made friends with the Romans. And the Romans made him king of Judea. He ruled for thirty-three years.

[long-pause]

Herod was a cruel and unhappy man. He was always afraid that someone was plotting against him. Because of that fear, he had many people killed. Even his own wife and three of his own sons. The Roman emperor once made a bitter joke about it. He said it was safer to be Herod's pig than Herod's son. Herod did not eat pork, so his pigs were safe.

[pause]

But Herod was also one of the greatest builders of his time.

[pause]

{img:masada-mesa-fortress-dead-sea-desert-cliffs-dawn-palace-terraces}
He made the Temple huge. He built a new port city on the coast, called Caesarea. Its harbour walls were made of a special Roman stone mix that hardened under water. He built palaces all over the land. One stood in the warm oasis of Jericho, with gardens and pools. One stood on top of a great flat rock above the Dead Sea, called Masada. It hung down the cliff in three steps. And one stood on a hill near Bethlehem, called the Herodium. Herod made that hill higher, into the shape of a cone, so it could be seen from Jerusalem.

[long-pause]

He was buried there. For a long time, nobody knew where his grave was. An archaeologist called Ehud Netzer searched for thirty-five years. In 2007, he finally found it, on the side of the hill.

[pause]

When Herod passed away, his land was split between three of his sons. One ruled Judea so badly that the Romans sent him away. After that, Rome ruled Judea directly, with a Roman governor. Another son, Herod Antipas, ruled Galilee in the north. That is where the teacher in our story comes from.

[pause]

{img:caesarea-harbour-herod-breakwater-ships-theatre-sea-sunset}
So in the year 33, Herod's buildings are everywhere. People still remember the old king. They remember his cruelty, and they remember his splendour. And they know that power in this land can be dangerous.

[long-pause]

{img:jerusalem-upper-city-mansions-herods-palace-towers-west-evening}
Let us walk through Jerusalem, in the spring of the year 33.

[pause]

The city is built on two hills. A valley runs between them. Deep valleys also lie around the city on three sides. To the east is the Kidron valley, and behind it the Mount of Olives. Around the city there are strong walls, with towers and gates.

[long-pause]

On the western hill is the Upper City. This is where the rich live. The families of the high priests live here, and the wealthy traders. Their houses are of stone, with courtyards and gardens. The walls are painted and the floors have mosaics, little pictures made of coloured stones.

[pause]

At the corner of the Upper City stands Herod's old palace. It has three tall towers. It has gardens and pools and halls for hundreds of guests. When the Roman governor comes to Jerusalem, this is where he stays. This week, he is here.

[pause]

Below the Temple lies the Lower City. This is the oldest part, the City of David. Most ordinary people live here. Their small stone houses are packed close together. The streets are narrow and steep, with steps going up and down.

[long-pause]

There are markets everywhere. There is a street of bakers and a street of cheese makers. There is a sheep market and a wood market and a wool market. Potters and weavers work in small shops. The leather workers work outside the walls, because of the smell.

[pause]

At the bottom of the Lower City is the Pool of Siloam. It is a big pool with steps all around it. Its water comes from a spring, through a tunnel cut in the rock long ago. People wash here before they go up to the Temple. A wide stepped street leads from the pool up to the Temple. Archaeologists have found both of them in recent years.

[pause]

{img:pool-of-siloam-stepped-stone-pool-pilgrims-washing-morning}
North of the Temple stands the Antonia. It is a square fortress with four towers. Roman soldiers live there. From the top, they can look down into the Temple yards. At festivals the crowds are huge. A small fight can quickly turn into a riot. So the soldiers watch, day and night.

[long-pause]

There is water too, here and there. North of the Temple is the Pool of Bethesda, with five covered porches. Sick people lie there and wait. They hope the water will move, because they believe it can heal them. Long stone channels bring water from springs near Bethlehem. And big tanks are cut into the rock under the city, to keep the winter rain.

[pause]

{img:pool-of-bethesda-porches-colonnade-sick-waiting-water-light}
Outside the walls there are gardens and olive groves. Graves are cut into the rock of the hills. To the east, olive trees cover the Mount of Olives. Their leaves are silver green. And on the far side of that hill lies a small village called Bethany. Our teacher from Galilee has friends there.

[long-pause]

{img:mikveh-ritual-bath-rock-cut-steps-water-jerusalem-house-quiet}
Water is precious in Jerusalem. The city stands on a dry hill at the edge of the desert. It has only one spring, down in the valley.

[pause]

So the people catch the rain. In winter, the rain runs off their roofs into tanks cut in the rock under their houses. These tanks are called cisterns. They are covered with plaster to keep the water in. It has to last all through the long dry summer. If the winter rains are poor, the whole city worries.

[long-pause]

Water is also important in the Jewish faith. It is about being clean in a holy way.

[pause]

The Law says that people must be clean before they go into the Temple. This is not about dirt. It is a kind of holy cleanness. You can lose it by touching certain things, or after certain events in life. To get it back, you go fully under water. You can do this in a river or a spring. Or you can use a special bath called a mikveh.

[pause]

A mikveh is a small pool cut into the rock. Steps lead down into the water. Often a low line divides the steps in two. You go down one side, not yet clean. You come up the other side, clean again. The water must be living water. That means rain or spring water that flowed in by itself.

[long-pause]

Archaeologists have found hundreds of these baths in Jerusalem. They are in the rich houses and the poor houses. Many are near the big stairs that lead up to the Temple. Thousands of pilgrims washed there before going up to pray.

[pause]

At Passover the city is full of pilgrims. All of them need to be clean before the festival. So the baths and the pools are crowded from morning to night.

[pause]

{img:stone-water-jars-limestone-house-courtyard-purity-vessels}
People also used cups and jars made of soft white stone. The rabbis taught that stone could not become unclean, the way clay pots could. Archaeologists find these stone cups and jars everywhere in the old houses. One story in the Bible tells of six big stone water jars at a wedding in Galilee. Jesus was a guest there. And the story says he turned the water in those jars into wine.

[long-pause]

{img:passover-pilgrims-camping-mount-of-olives-tents-fires-evening}
Passover is the greatest festival of the Jewish year. In Hebrew it is called Pesach.

[pause]

It remembers a night more than a thousand years before. The old story tells how the people of Israel were slaves in Egypt. Then God led them out to freedom, with Moses. On that last night, each family killed a lamb. They put a little of its blood on their doorposts. That way, the angel of death would pass over their house. That is where the name Passover comes from. They ate the lamb with flat bread and bitter herbs. They ate it standing up, with their shoes on, ready to go.

[long-pause]

Every year since then, Jewish families have remembered that night. They eat a special meal together. It is held on the evening of the first full moon of spring.

[pause]

In the time of the Temple, everyone who can comes to Jerusalem for it. They bring a lamb to the Temple. And they eat the meal inside the walls of the holy city.

[pause]

{img:families-walking-road-galilee-to-jerusalem-donkey-children-spring}
So in the weeks before Passover, the roads are full. Families walk for days, with their donkeys and their children. As they walk, they sing the old songs for the road up to Jerusalem. One goes like this. I was glad when they said to me, let us go into the house of the Lord. Our feet shall stand within your gates, O Jerusalem.

[long-pause]

At last they climb over the Mount of Olives. Suddenly they see the city across the valley. The Temple shines gold and white. Many people stop and cry with joy.

[pause]

The city cannot hold them all. So they camp on the hills around it. They sleep in tents or shelters made of branches. Others stay with family in the city, or in nearby villages like Bethany. Josephus says that once more than two million people came. That is probably too many. Historians today think it was a few hundred thousand. Still, that is a huge crowd for one small city.

[pause]

{img:temple-court-crowds-passover-lambs-priests-levites-singing}
Before the festival, everyone must wash in the holy baths. Families clean their houses. At night they search every corner with a small lamp. They look for any crumb of bread made with yeast. Every crumb must be found and burned before the festival begins.

[pause]

And on the afternoon before Passover, the lambs are brought to the Temple. The crowds go in, group by group. The Temple singers sing songs of praise, with trumpets and harps. Then each family carries its lamb home, to roast it for the evening meal.

[long-pause]

{img:passover-family-table-unleavened-bread-bitter-herbs-wine-cups-lamps}
What was the Passover meal like, in the time of Jesus?

[pause]

It was eaten in the evening, after sunset, inside Jerusalem. Families and friends ate together, at least ten people. That way they could share a whole roasted lamb. Nothing of the lamb could be left over until morning.

[long-pause]

They did not stand, as their ancestors had done in Egypt. Now they were free people. So they lay on cushions around low tables, like guests at a feast.

[pause]

On the table lay the roasted lamb. There was flat bread, called matzah. It is made of only flour and water, with no yeast. It reminds them that their ancestors left Egypt in a hurry, with no time for the bread to rise. There were bitter herbs, like lettuce or horseradish. They stand for the bitter years of slavery. There was a sweet brown paste of fruit and nuts. It looks like the clay the slaves used to make bricks. There was salt water, for the tears. And there was wine.

[pause]

{img:child-asking-question-father-passover-table-lamplight-family}
During the meal they drank four cups of wine, each with its own blessing. And the youngest child asked a question. Why is this night different from all other nights?

[pause]

Then the father told the old story. He told how the people were slaves in Egypt. He told how God heard them cry. And he told how Moses led them out, through the sea, to freedom. The children listened, wide awake. Every year they knew how it ended. And still they waited for the part where the sea opened.

[long-pause]

They sang songs of praise before and after the meal. And they ate and talked and remembered, late into the night.

[pause]

Jewish families still hold this meal today, all over the world, every spring. It is called the Seder, which means order, because it follows a set order. There is no Temple now, so there is no lamb. Instead a roasted bone lies on the plate, as a reminder. But the flat bread is the same. So are the bitter herbs and the sweet paste. So are the four cups, the questions of the children, and the story.

[pause]

{img:modern-seder-plate-matzah-family-table-candles-warm-later}
And at the end, someone opens the door. It is opened for the prophet Elijah. The old belief is that one day he will come, to say that the promised king is near. A cup of wine is poured for him. It stays on the table, waiting.

[long-pause]

{img:dawn-jerusalem-temple-trumpet-priest-silver-shofar-morning-light}
Let us spend an ordinary day in Jerusalem, a few days before Passover.

[pause]

The day begins before dawn, in the Temple. A priest climbs to the very top. He looks east and waits. He is watching for the first light of the sun on the far hills. When he sees it, he calls out. The morning offering begins. Silver trumpets sound, and a horn made from a ram. And the city wakes up.

[long-pause]

In the houses of the Lower City, the women are already busy. They grind wheat or barley between two stones, for the day's bread. They light the clay ovens in the courtyards. Soon the smell of bread and wood smoke fills the narrow streets.

[pause]

Let us follow one family. The father is a stone cutter. He works on the Temple, like thousands of other men. He says his morning prayer. Hear, O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is One. He washes his hands and puts on his cloak. It has blue tassels at the corners, as the Law asks. Then he goes out to work.

[pause]

Breakfast is simple. Some bread and olives. A little cheese, or a few dates.

[long-pause]

The streets are crowded and noisy and full of colour. There are pilgrims everywhere, speaking many languages. Roman soldiers walk by in their helmets and red cloaks. Priests pass in white linen. Teachers of the Law walk slowly, talking. Beggars sit by the gates. And sellers shout about bread and fruit, about salted fish from the Sea of Galilee, and about doves and lambs.

[pause]

This week the streets feel different. There are more soldiers than usual. People talk in low voices on the corners. There is a rumour about a teacher from Galilee. Some say he is a prophet. Some say more than that. And everyone wonders what the priests and the Romans will think.

[pause]

{img:teacher-colonnade-temple-students-sitting-listening-scrolls}
At midday comes the main meal. Bread, and lentils or beans, cooked with onion and garlic and olive oil. Sometimes a little fish. Fruit, and wine mixed with water.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon the work goes on. At about three o'clock, the evening offering is made in the Temple. The trumpets sound again. People stop where they are, and pray.

[pause]

Then the sun goes down behind the western hills. The sky turns gold and then red, and the city walls glow. The family climbs onto the flat roof of the house. They eat supper there and look out over the city. They see the Temple and the Mount of Olives. And all over the hills, thousands of small fires are burning. Those are the camps of the pilgrims, under the stars.

[pause]

{img:family-flat-roof-evening-supper-view-temple-fires-on-hills}
They say the evening prayer. Then they lie down on mats on the roof, in the cool night air. And the full moon of Passover rises slowly over the Mount of Olives.

[long-pause]

{img:galilean-man-tunic-mantle-tassels-sandals-woman-veil-headscarf}
What do the people of Jerusalem wear in the year 33?

[pause]

Men wear a tunic. That is a long shirt of wool or linen, down to the knees, with a belt. Over it they wrap a cloak, a big square of wool. At the four corners hang tassels with a blue thread. The Law asks for these, to help people remember God's commands. On their feet they wear leather sandals.

[long-pause]

Women wear a longer tunic, down to the ankles. They also wear a cloak, and a scarf over their hair when they go out. Most clothes have natural colours. White and cream, brown and grey. Rich people wear finer cloth, dyed blue or red. Purple is the most costly colour of all.

[pause]

In dry caves near the Dead Sea, archaeologists have found real clothes from this time. The desert air kept them safe. There are tunics with coloured stripes, and pieces of tassels, and even sandals and baskets.

[pause]

And what do people eat?

[long-pause]

Bread is the most important food. Wheat bread for those who can pay for it, barley bread for the poor. Bread matters so much that a meal is simply called breaking bread.

[pause]

With the bread, people eat olive oil and olives, and cheese from goats and sheep. They eat lentils and beans and chickpeas. They eat onions, garlic and cucumbers. They eat figs and grapes and dates and pomegranates. They like honey. They eat salted fish, brought all the way from the Sea of Galilee. Meat is mostly for festivals.

[pause]

{img:olive-press-stone-wheel-donkey-oil-jars-grove-autumn}
They drink water and milk, and wine mixed with water. And olive oil is used for almost everything. For cooking and eating. For the little lamps that give light at night. For the skin and the hair, and as medicine. And for something holy too. Kings and priests were made king or priest by pouring oil on their heads. The Hebrew word Messiah means the one with oil on his head. So does the Greek word Christ. Both mean the chosen one.

[long-pause]

{img:pontius-pilate-roman-governor-toga-caesarea-palace-sea-view}
In the year 33, the land of the Jews is ruled by Rome.

[pause]

The south of the land is a Roman province. It is ruled by a Roman governor. Most of the year he lives in Caesarea, the new city by the sea. It has a harbour, a theatre and a palace on the beach, all built by Herod.

[long-pause]

Since the year 26, the governor is a man called Pontius Pilate. He is a Roman soldier and official. We do not know very much about him. A Jewish writer of the time, Philo, did not like him at all. He says Pilate was stubborn and harsh, and quick to get angry.

[pause]

In 1961, Italian archaeologists were digging in the old theatre at Caesarea. They found a stone that had been used as a step. On it, in Latin, was his name. Pontius Pilate, governor of Judea. It is the only stone with his name from his own lifetime. Today it is in a museum in Jerusalem.

[pause]

{img:pilate-stone-latin-inscription-limestone-museum-soft-light}
Pilate has already had many quarrels with the Jews. Once his soldiers carried pictures of the emperor into Jerusalem at night. The Jews believed this was forbidden in the holy city. So crowds went all the way to Caesarea. They lay down outside his palace for five days. They said they would rather die than let this happen. In the end, Pilate gave in. But he did not forget.

[long-pause]

At the great festivals, Pilate comes up to Jerusalem with extra soldiers. The crowds are huge. And Passover is a festival about freedom from a foreign ruler. It is easy to see why the Romans get nervous. One spark, and a riot could start.

[pause]

Pilate stays in Herod's old palace, with its towers and gardens. From there, and from the fortress by the Temple, his soldiers watch the crowds.

[pause]

{img:roman-soldiers-antonia-fortress-roof-watching-temple-courts-crowds}
Most of his soldiers are not from Italy. They come from the non-Jewish towns nearby. Many of them do not like the Jews, and the Jews do not like them.

[long-pause]

Above Pilate there is the Roman governor of Syria. And above him is the emperor, Tiberius. He is an old, gloomy man who trusts nobody. He lives on an island near Naples and rarely visits Rome. A governor who lets a riot happen could lose everything.

[pause]

In the north, in Galilee, there is also a Jewish ruler, Herod Antipas. He is a son of the old king Herod. He rules Galilee for the Romans. And this week he too has come to Jerusalem for Passover.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-auxiliary-soldiers-patrol-jerusalem-street-helmets-shields}
The soldiers in Jerusalem are not Romans from Italy. They are hired from the non-Jewish towns of the region. They speak Greek. In the whole province there are a few thousand of them. In Jerusalem there are perhaps five hundred to a thousand.

[pause]

They live in the fortress next to the Temple, and in Herod's palace.

[long-pause]

They wear helmets of iron and shirts of metal rings. They carry oval shields, swords and spears. Their officers are called centurions. Each one leads about eighty men. A centurion carries a short stick of vine wood, to show his rank.

[pause]

Most days are dull for them. They stand guard on the walls and at the gates. They walk through the streets. They clean their armour and practise marching. At night they play dice and board games. Some of these games are scratched into the stone floors, and you can still see them today.

[pause]

But this week is not dull. The city is full, and the soldiers are on edge. They are foreign soldiers in a holy city. A few years later, at another Passover, one soldier on the Temple roof made a rude gesture at the crowd. A riot broke out, and many people were hurt. Everyone knows how fast that can happen.

[long-pause]

Yet not all soldiers are unkind. The Bible tells of a Roman officer in Galilee who loved the Jewish people. He even paid for a synagogue. When his servant was ill, he asked Jesus for help. And Jesus said he had never seen such great faith.

[pause]

The Bible also tells of an officer standing near the cross, later in this week. He looked up and said, truly, this man was the Son of God.

[pause]

{img:centurion-standing-quiet-evening-light-helmet-under-arm-thoughtful}
And the first person who was not a Jew to join the new faith was also a Roman officer. His name was Cornelius, and he lived in Caesarea. He was a kind man who prayed and helped the poor. He was baptized by Peter, with all his family.

[long-pause]

{img:high-priest-caiaphas-robes-breastplate-turban-temple-courtyard}
The most powerful Jewish leader in Jerusalem is the High Priest. His name is Joseph, and people call him Caiaphas.

[pause]

He has been High Priest for fifteen years. That is a very long time. The Romans choose the High Priest, and they can send him away whenever they like. So Caiaphas must be a careful man. He knows how to keep both the Romans and his own people calm.

[long-pause]

The High Priest is the head of the Temple. He also leads the great council of seventy elders. This council is called the Sanhedrin. It decides many things for the Jewish people, under the Romans.

[pause]

Caiaphas comes from a small group of rich priestly families. They run the Temple and its great wealth. His father-in-law, Annas, was High Priest before him. Annas is still very powerful. These families live in fine houses in the Upper City.

[pause]

{img:upper-city-priestly-mansion-courtyard-mosaic-ritual-bath-stone}
Archaeologists have found some of these houses. They were buried under the ruins of a later war. They are big and elegant. They have mosaic floors and painted walls and fine glass. Each has its own holy bath, deep in the rock. One house is so large that people call it the Palace House.

[long-pause]

In 1990, workers building a road south of Jerusalem broke into an old family grave. Inside were small stone boxes. In those days, the bones of the dead were put in such boxes a year after burial. One box was beautifully carved. On it, a name was scratched. Joseph, son of Caiaphas. Many scholars think it held the bones of the High Priest himself.

[pause]

Caiaphas has one great fear. He fears that the Passover crowds will rise up against Rome. If that happens, the Roman army will come. And they could destroy the Temple and the whole nation.

[pause]

{img:carved-limestone-ossuary-rosettes-aramaic-name-museum-light}
The Bible says Caiaphas told the council something hard. It is better that one man dies for the people, than that the whole nation is lost. In those days, any man who drew big crowds could start a rebellion. Especially if people called him a king. And now such a man is coming up the road from Jericho.

[long-pause]

Caiaphas's fear was not foolish. Thirty-three years later, the Jews did rise up against Rome. And in the year 70, the Romans destroyed Jerusalem and its Temple.

[pause]

But in the spring of the year 33, the Temple still stands, shining over the crowded city. And the High Priest gets ready for Passover, and waits to see what will happen.

[long-pause]

{img:sea-of-galilee-fishing-boats-hills-capernaum-shore-morning}
Among the pilgrims on the road this spring, there is a group from Galilee in the north. It is a teacher and his friends. Some women travel with them too.

[pause]

The teacher's name is Yeshua. In Greek it is Iesous, and in English it is Jesus. He comes from Nazareth, a small village in the hills. He is in his thirties. He used to be a builder, like his father Joseph. But for the last two or three years he has walked around Galilee. He teaches and he heals. And big crowds follow him.

[long-pause]

Galilee is very different from Judea. It is green and rich. There are fields of wheat and olive groves and vineyards. And there is a big lake, the Sea of Galilee. It is full of fish and ringed by hills. Small fishing towns lie along its shores.

[pause]

Many of Jesus's closest friends are fishermen from the lake. There is Simon, whom he calls Peter. That name means the rock. There is his brother Andrew. There are two other brothers, James and John. There is a tax collector called Matthew. Twelve of them are his closest followers. And there are women who follow him too and help pay for the journey. Mary from the town of Magdala is one of them.

[pause]

{img:teacher-sitting-hillside-crowd-listening-lake-galilee-below-spring}
He teaches in the synagogues. But he also teaches outside, by the lake and on the hills. He talks to farmers and fishermen and shepherds. He talks to women and children, to the sick and the poor.

[long-pause]

He teaches with short stories, called parables. They are about ordinary things. A farmer sowing seed. A woman looking for a lost coin. A shepherd who goes to find one lost sheep. A father who runs to welcome home his son.

[pause]

He talks about God's kingdom, and about love. Love God, and love your neighbour as yourself. Love even your enemies. Blessed are the poor, he says, and the gentle, and the peacemakers. Blessed are those who are sad, for they will be comforted.

[pause]

{img:children-gathered-around-teacher-village-square-smiling-mothers}
The Bible says he heals the sick and the blind. He eats with people that others look down on. He welcomes children. Many people think he is a prophet. Some think he is more. They think he is the promised king who will save Israel. And that is a dangerous thing to think, with Roman soldiers in the streets.

[long-pause]

Now he is coming to Jerusalem for Passover. He walks with his friends on the road from Jericho, up through the desert hills.

[pause]

Most historians agree that Jesus was a real person. He was a Jewish teacher from Galilee. And he was put to death in Jerusalem, under Pontius Pilate, around the year 30 or 33. What happened next, his followers have told for two thousand years.

[long-pause]

{img:nazareth-small-village-hillside-stone-houses-terraces-galilee-hills}
In the time of Jesus, Nazareth is a tiny village. Perhaps two or three hundred people live there. Their small houses stand on a hillside. At the bottom of the valley there is a spring.

[pause]

It is so small that the old Jewish books never mention it. In the Bible, a man hears that Jesus comes from Nazareth. And he says, can anything good come out of Nazareth?

[long-pause]

The houses are simple. Many are built against the hill. Behind them are caves cut into the soft rock. These are used for storage, or for animals, or sometimes as rooms. Each family has a small courtyard. There is an oven, a tank for water and a stone for grinding grain. The olive press and the wine press are shared with the neighbours.

[pause]

The villagers are farmers. They grow wheat and barley, olives and grapes and figs. They grow them on narrow steps of land cut into the hills. They keep a few sheep and goats.

[pause]

{img:village-courtyard-galilee-mother-grinding-children-playing-oven}
The Bible says Jesus was a builder, like his father. He worked with wood and stone. He would have made doors and roof beams. He would have made ploughs, and the wooden yokes that oxen wear on their necks. He would have made tables and benches.

[long-pause]

Just six kilometres away, across the valley, there is a real city. It is called Sepphoris. When Jesus was young, the ruler of Galilee was rebuilding it in the Roman style. It got a theatre and fine streets with columns. Some historians think Joseph and the young Jesus may have worked there. Archaeologists have found a lovely floor mosaic there of a woman's face, from a later time. People call her the Mona Lisa of Galilee.

[pause]

In 2009, archaeologists found a small house in Nazareth from the time of Jesus. It had a few small rooms, a courtyard and a water tank. There were bits of pots and stone cups. It is the kind of house where a family of builders and farmers could have lived.

[pause]

{img:galilee-hills-spring-flowers-wheat-fields-view-from-nazareth}
Today Nazareth is a large town in the north of Israel. In the middle stands a great church. It is built over a cave where, the old story says, an angel came to Mary.

[long-pause]

{img:jericho-oasis-palm-groves-green-desert-mountains-winter-sun}
Pilgrims from Galilee often take the road down the Jordan valley. That way they do not have to pass through Samaria. The people there do not get on well with the Jews. And so the road brings them to Jericho.

[pause]

Jericho is one of the oldest towns in the world. People have lived there for ten thousand years. It is an oasis, a green place in the desert. It lies deep in the hot valley near the Dead Sea, far below the level of the sea. A big spring waters its palm trees and gardens. People call it the city of palms.

[long-pause]

In winter Jericho is warm, while Jerusalem is cold. Herod built a winter palace there, with pools and gardens. Special trees grow there too. They give a sweet-smelling oil for perfume and medicine. It is worth more than gold.

[pause]

From Jericho, the road climbs up to Jerusalem. It is about twenty-five kilometres long. And it climbs more than a thousand metres. It winds between bare brown hills, with deep dry valleys. There is no water and no shade. And there are caves where robbers sometimes hide. Travellers walk this road in groups, and they keep an eye on the rocks.

[pause]

{img:winding-desert-road-jericho-to-jerusalem-bare-hills-travellers}
Jesus set one of his most famous stories on this road. A man was walking down from Jerusalem to Jericho. Robbers attacked him. They beat him and took his clothes and left him by the road, badly hurt. A priest came by, and walked past on the other side. Another Temple helper did the same. Then a Samaritan came by. He was a foreigner, from the people the Jews did not like. But he stopped. He cleaned the man's wounds with oil and wine. He put him on his own donkey and took him to an inn. And he paid the innkeeper to look after him.

[long-pause]

Then Jesus asked a question. Which of these three was a true neighbour to the hurt man? The answer was clear. The one who was kind to him. Go, Jesus said, and do the same.

[pause]

The Bible says that at Jericho, a blind beggar called out to Jesus and was healed. It also tells of a rich tax collector called Zacchaeus. He was a small man. He could not see over the crowd. So he climbed a tree. Jesus stopped under the tree and looked up. Zacchaeus, come down, he said. Today I must stay at your house. And Zacchaeus came down happily. He gave half of what he had to the poor.

[pause]

{img:sycamore-fig-tree-jericho-street-crowd-small-man-climbing-legend}
Today, halfway along the road, there is an old inn called the Inn of the Good Samaritan. It is now a small museum. And in Jericho there is a very old tree. The people there say it is the tree of Zacchaeus.

[long-pause]

{img:jesus-riding-young-donkey-mount-of-olives-crowd-palm-branches-cloaks}
On the Sunday before Passover, the Bible says, Jesus came to Jerusalem. He came from Bethany, over the Mount of Olives.

[pause]

He sent two friends into a village nearby. They brought back a young donkey that nobody had ridden before. They laid their cloaks on its back. Jesus sat on it and rode down the hill toward the city.

[long-pause]

It was a sign, and people understood it. Five hundred years before, a prophet had written this. Rejoice, Jerusalem. Look, your king is coming to you. He is humble, and he rides on a donkey.

[pause]

Not on a war horse, like a king who conquers. On a donkey, like a king of peace.

[pause]

{img:road-down-mount-of-olives-view-of-temple-across-kidron-valley}
The crowds on the road began to cheer. Many were pilgrims from Galilee who knew him. They spread their cloaks on the road in front of him. Others cut branches from palm trees and olive trees. They waved them and laid them on the road. And they shouted. Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord!

[long-pause]

Hosanna means save us now. The words come from an old song the pilgrims sang at festivals.

[pause]

But the shouting carried far. From the fortress, Roman soldiers could see the crowd. From the Temple, the priests could hear the words. A king, people were saying. And kings were for Rome to choose.

[pause]

The road down the Mount of Olives is steep. From it you see the whole city across the valley. You see the walls, and the Temple shining in the sun. The Bible says that when Jesus saw the city, he cried over it.

[pause]

{img:olive-branches-spread-on-road-crowd-singing-joyful-spring-light}
Today Christians all over the world remember this day. It is called Palm Sunday, one week before Easter. They walk in processions and carry palm branches, or olive, or willow, or whatever grows where they live.

[pause]

And in Jerusalem, every year, thousands of Christians walk the same road. They start at the top of the Mount of Olives. They walk down singing and waving palms. And they go into the old city through the Lions' Gate, just as the crowds did long ago.

[long-pause]

The crowds followed Jesus into the city. The whole city was stirred, the Bible says. People asked, who is this? And the crowd said, this is the prophet Jesus, from Nazareth in Galilee.

[pause]

In the evening he went back out with his friends, over the hill, to sleep in Bethany. Behind them, the city was talking.

[long-pause]

{img:mount-of-olives-slopes-olive-groves-tombs-view-temple-golden-morning}
East of Jerusalem, across the Kidron valley, there is a long hill. It is higher than the city. This is the Mount of Olives.

[pause]

Its slopes are covered with olive trees, with silver green leaves. There are vineyards and fig trees and small villages. There are also many graves cut into the rock. The Jews believe the promised king will come to Jerusalem over this hill. And they believe the people buried here will be the first to rise again.

[long-pause]

From the top you have the most beautiful view of Jerusalem. The whole city lies across the valley. You see the walls and houses and palaces. And right in front of you is the Temple, gold and white on its great platform.

[pause]

Turn around, and you look the other way. The land falls down over the desert to the Dead Sea. Behind it rise the mountains, blue and purple and pink at sunset.

[pause]

{img:olive-harvest-mount-of-olives-families-ladders-nets-autumn-sun}
The olive trees here matter a lot to the city. Their oil burns in the great lamp of the Temple. It is used in every house for cooking and for light. In autumn, families come to pick the olives. They beat the branches with long sticks. They gather the olives from the ground. Then they take them to a press. A heavy stone wheel crushes them, and golden green oil runs out. One of these presses is called Gethsemane. The name means oil press.

[long-pause]

At Passover the hill is covered with tents and fires. Thousands of pilgrims sleep here because there is no room in the city. At night, from the walls, you can see their fires all over the hill, like stars.

[pause]

The Bible says Jesus often came here with his friends. He came to rest and to pray. Here he once sat looking at the Temple across the valley. And he told his friends a strange thing. One day, he said, not one stone of it will be left on another. His friends did not understand. The Temple looked like it would stand forever.

[pause]

{img:pilgrim-fires-hillside-night-mount-of-olives-stars-city-walls}
And the Bible says that from this hill, forty days after Easter, he was taken up into heaven, while his friends watched.

[pause]

Today the Mount of Olives is covered with churches. There is also a great Jewish graveyard, one of the oldest in the world. Its graves run down the hill, facing the Temple Mount. And the view from the top is still one of the most beautiful in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:temple-court-money-changers-tables-doves-cages-crowd-colonnade}
The next day, the Bible says, Jesus went into the Temple.

[pause]

In the big outer yard sat the money changers at their tables. People were selling doves and sheep for the offerings. These people were needed in a way. Pilgrims from far away had to change their money into special Temple coins. And they could not bring animals on such a long journey. So they bought them here.

[long-pause]

But Jesus was angry. To him, the house of prayer had become a market. He tipped over the tables of the money changers. Coins rolled across the stones. He pushed over the seats of the dove sellers. And he drove the traders out. He said, my house shall be called a house of prayer for all nations. But you have made it a den of thieves.

[pause]

This happened in the heart of the Temple, in the busiest week of the year. Thousands of people saw it. The priests were alarmed. Now they knew they had to do something. But what? The crowds loved him. If they arrested him in public, the crowd might riot.

[pause]

{img:teaching-temple-colonnade-crowd-listening-questions-afternoon-light}
For the next few days, Jesus came to the Temple every day. He taught under the covered walkways, and the crowds came to listen. Priests and teachers of the Law came too. They asked him hard questions. They hoped to trap him.

[long-pause]

One question was very clever. Is it right to pay taxes to the Roman emperor, or not? If he said yes, the people would turn against him. They hated the Roman taxes. If he said no, the Romans could arrest him as a rebel. Everyone waited for his answer.

[pause]

He asked them to show him a coin. It was a Roman silver coin. Whose face is this, he asked. And whose name? They said, the emperor's. Then he said, give to the emperor what belongs to the emperor. And give to God what belongs to God.

[pause]

{img:silver-denarius-tiberius-coin-palm-hand-close-soft-light}
They were amazed at his answer. The trap had not worked.

[pause]

Another teacher asked him which command was the greatest. He answered with the old words of the Law. Love the Lord your God with all your heart and all your soul and all your strength. And the second is this. Love your neighbour as yourself. There is no command greater than these.

[long-pause]

Later, the Bible says, he sat near the Temple money boxes. He watched people put in their gifts. Many rich people put in a lot. Then a poor widow came. She put in two tiny copper coins. They were the smallest coins there were.

[pause]

Jesus called his friends over. He said, this poor woman has given more than all of them. They gave what they had left over. But she gave everything she had to live on.

[long-pause]

{img:levites-singing-temple-steps-harps-lyres-cymbals-trumpets-priests}
The Temple of Jerusalem is full of music.

[pause]

Every morning and every evening, the Temple singers stand on fifteen wide curved steps. These singers come from one special family line, the Levites. They sing the old songs called psalms. They play harps and lyres and cymbals and flutes. And the priests blow silver trumpets.

[long-pause]

The choir has at least twelve men. Boys stand beside them, the sons of the singers. Their high sweet voices float over the yards.

[pause]

Each day of the week has its own song. On the first day they sing, the earth is the Lord's, and everything in it. On the day of rest they sing, it is good to give thanks to the Lord.

[pause]

{img:silver-trumpets-priests-temple-court-dawn-light-crowd-bowing}
At the end of each part of the song, the trumpets sound. Then all the people in the yards bow down to the ground. They stand up again, and the singing goes on.

[long-pause]

At the big festivals there is even more music. At Passover, while the lambs are offered, the singers sing the songs of praise all afternoon. They sing them again and again. And the crowds join in with one word. Hallelujah. It means praise the Lord.

[pause]

In autumn, at another festival, there is a great party at night. Huge golden lamps are lit on tall stands. They are so bright, the rabbis said, that every courtyard in the city was lit up. Old and respected men dance with torches in their hands. The singers play all night long. The rabbis said, whoever has not seen that night has never seen real joy.

[pause]

{img:golden-lamps-night-court-temple-dancing-torches-festival-joy}
The songs the singers sang in the Temple are still sung today. Jews and Christians sing them every day, all over the world. The Lord is my shepherd. I shall not want. He makes me lie down in green pastures. He leads me beside the still waters. He gives me new strength.

[long-pause]

{img:bethany-village-east-slope-mount-of-olives-stone-houses-evening}
Every evening that week, the Bible says, Jesus and his friends left the crowded city. They walked back over the Mount of Olives to the little village of Bethany. It is about three kilometres away, on the far side of the hill, at the edge of the desert.

[pause]

There they stayed with friends. A man called Lazarus, and his two sisters, Martha and Mary. The Bible says Jesus loved this family.

[long-pause]

Bethany is a quiet village. It has stone houses and courtyards, olive trees and fig trees. It looks out east over the desert and the Jordan valley.

[pause]

After the noise of the city, it must have felt peaceful. The quiet street. The friendly faces. A warm meal, the small lamps, and the stars over the desert.

[pause]

One evening there was a supper in a house in Bethany. While they were eating, a woman came in. She carried a jar of white stone, full of very costly perfume. It was made from a plant that grows in the far mountains of India. It was worth almost a year's wages. She broke the jar. She poured the perfume over Jesus's head and his feet. And the whole house filled with the sweet smell.

[long-pause]

Some of the friends were upset. They said the perfume could have been sold, and the money given to the poor. But Jesus said, leave her alone. She has done a beautiful thing. You will always have the poor with you. But you will not always have me. She has made my body ready for burial.

[pause]

The room went quiet. What did he mean? The Bible says that Jesus knew what was coming. His friends did not. Not yet.

[pause]

{img:alabaster-perfume-jar-broken-scent-oil-table-lamplight-close}
Today Bethany is an Arab town. Its name means the town of Lazarus. There is a church and a mosque. And there is an old grave cut into the rock. Since early times, pilgrims have been shown it as the grave of Lazarus.

[pause]

And in the evenings of that week, there were quiet talks by the lamps. The friends talked about the city, and the priests, and the crowds. And about what might happen next.

[long-pause]

{img:upper-room-passover-meal-low-table-lamps-bread-wine-friends-evening}
On the Thursday evening, the Bible says, Jesus ate a last meal with his friends. It was in a large room upstairs, in a house in the city.

[pause]

That morning, two friends had gone ahead to get it ready. Jesus had told them what to look for. You will meet a man carrying a jar of water. Follow him. That was a clear sign. In those days, carrying water was mostly done by women. The man led them to the house and the room upstairs. It all felt a little secret. And perhaps it was. The priests were watching, and nobody was to know where Jesus would be that night.

[long-pause]

The meal was eaten in the evening, by the light of oil lamps. They lay on cushions around a low table, as people did at a feast. They leaned on their left elbow and ate with their right hand.

[pause]

On the table there was bread and wine. Perhaps there were bitter herbs and the other foods of Passover. Three of the gospels say it was the Passover meal itself. The gospel of John says it was the evening before.

[pause]

John tells that during the meal, Jesus stood up. He took off his cloak and tied a towel around his waist. He poured water into a bowl. And he began to wash his friends' feet, as a servant would. Peter did not want this. Lord, he said, you will never wash my feet. But Jesus said, I am your teacher, and I have washed your feet. So you too must wash each other's feet. I have shown you the way.

[pause]

And he said, I give you a new command. Love one another, as I have loved you.

[long-pause]

Then, the Bible says, he took bread. He gave thanks and broke it and gave it to them. He said, take and eat. This is my body, given for you. Do this to remember me.

[pause]

After supper he took the cup of wine. He gave thanks and passed it to them. He said, drink from it, all of you. This is my blood, poured out for many. And they all drank from it.

[pause]

{img:bread-broken-wine-cup-table-lamplight-quiet-hands-close}
Christians have remembered this meal ever since, for two thousand years. Every week, or even every day, they share bread and wine. They do it in churches and chapels and homes, in every language and in every land.

[long-pause]

At that same meal, the Bible says, Jesus said something that made the room go still. One of you will betray me. His friends were very sad. One after another they asked, surely it is not me, Lord? And one of the twelve, Judas Iscariot, stood up and went out. Outside, it was night.

[pause]

At the end of the meal they sang a song of praise, as people do at Passover. Then, late in the evening, they left the city. They crossed the Kidron valley toward the Mount of Olives, under the full Passover moon.

[pause]

{img:full-moon-over-kidron-valley-mount-of-olives-figures-walking-night}
Today in Jerusalem, on Mount Zion, there is an old room upstairs with pointed arches. It was built by knights from Europe many centuries later. The old story says it stands on the place of the Last Supper. Pilgrims from all over the world come to see it.

[long-pause]

{img:qumran-caves-cliffs-dead-sea-desert-shepherd-boy-goats-1947}
Let us leave the story for a moment and jump forward, to the winter of 1946. A young shepherd boy is looking after his goats. He is in the cliffs above the Dead Sea, near a place called Qumran. One of his goats wanders off.

[pause]

He looks for it. He throws a stone into a cave in the cliff. And he hears something break. When he climbs inside, he finds tall clay jars with lids. In some of them there are old rolls of leather, wrapped in linen.

[long-pause]

They were the oldest Bible writings ever found. Today we call them the Dead Sea Scrolls.

[pause]

Over the next ten years, people searched all the caves around Qumran. In eleven caves they found scrolls and scraps of scrolls. There were about nine hundred different writings. Some were in Hebrew, some in Aramaic and some in Greek. They were written over a few hundred years, up to the year 68. Many were written in the time of Jesus.

[pause]

{img:ancient-scroll-unrolled-hebrew-columns-museum-glass-soft-light}
There are copies of almost every book of the Jewish Bible. They are a thousand years older than any copies known before. There is a scroll of the prophet Isaiah, nearly whole, seven metres long. There are songs and prayers and rules for living together. And there is a strange scroll made of copper. It lists hidden treasures. Nobody has ever found them.

[long-pause]

Many scholars think the scrolls belonged to a Jewish group called the Essenes. They lived in the desert by the Dead Sea, away from the world. They shared everything. They prayed and studied and copied the holy books. And they waited for the end of the world and the coming of the promised king. You can still see the ruins of their small settlement. There are baths and a dining room, and a room where the scrolls may have been written.

[pause]

In the year 68, Roman soldiers came. It seems the people of Qumran hid their scrolls in the caves, in jars. They hoped to come back for them. They never did. The scrolls waited in the dark for almost two thousand years. Until a goat wandered off.

[pause]

{img:shrine-of-the-book-white-dome-museum-jerusalem-later-garden}
Today the scrolls are kept in a museum in Jerusalem. The building has a white roof shaped like the lid of one of the jars. And through the scrolls, we can hear the voices of people who lived and prayed and hoped in the time of Jesus.

[long-pause]

{img:garden-gethsemane-ancient-olive-trees-night-moonlight-quiet}
At the foot of the Mount of Olives, across the valley from the city, there is an olive garden with an oil press. In the language of the time it is called Gethsemane. That means the oil press.

[pause]

Jesus often came here with his friends, the Bible says. He came to rest and to pray, away from the crowds.

[long-pause]

That night, under the full moon, he went there with eleven of his friends. He said to them, sit here while I go and pray. He took Peter, James and John a little further. He said to them, my heart is very heavy. Stay here and keep watch with me.

[pause]

Then he went a little further alone, about a stone's throw. He knelt down among the olive trees. And he prayed. Father, if you are willing, take this cup away from me. But let your will be done, not mine.

[pause]

{img:figure-kneeling-praying-alone-olive-grove-moonlight-shadows}
When he came back, his friends were asleep. They were worn out after the long day and the meal and the sadness. He said to Peter, could you not stay awake with me for one hour? Your spirit wants to, but your body is weak. He went away and prayed again. He came back and found them asleep again. Three times this happened.

[pause]

The night was very quiet. Only the leaves moved. And down in the valley, something was coming.

[long-pause]

Then, the Bible says, there were lights between the olive trees. Torches and lanterns came closer. There was the sound of voices and feet. It was a group sent by the chief priests, with Temple guards. And Judas walked in front. He came up to Jesus and kissed him on the cheek. That was the sign, so the guards would know which man to take.

[pause]

For a moment there was confusion. One of the friends pulled out a sword. But Jesus told him to put it away. The guards took Jesus and led him back across the valley to the city. And all his friends ran away into the night.

[pause]

{img:torches-lanterns-olive-grove-night-distant-crowd-quiet-tension}
Today, at the foot of the Mount of Olives, there is a beautiful church with a golden mosaic on its front. It is called the Church of All Nations, because many countries paid for it. Beside it there is a small garden with eight very old olive trees. They are twisted and hollow. Some scientists think they are about nine hundred years old. They may have grown from the roots of even older trees.

[pause]

People come from all over the world to sit there quietly in the evening. They sit among the old trees, and they pray, and they think about that night.

[long-pause]

{img:courtyard-high-priest-house-fire-servants-warming-night-peter}
That night, the Bible says, Jesus was taken to the house of the High Priest in the Upper City. Some priests and elders had come together there.

[pause]

Peter had followed from far behind. He slipped into the courtyard of the house. Servants and guards stood around a fire, because the spring night was cold. Peter stood with them and warmed his hands. He kept his head down. Nobody must know who he was.

[long-pause]

But a servant girl looked at him in the firelight. She said, you were with him, the man from Galilee. Peter was afraid. He said, I do not know him. A little later someone else said, you are one of them. Your accent gives you away. You are from Galilee. And Peter said again, I do not know the man. And then a third time.

[pause]

Just then, in the dark before dawn, a rooster crowed. Peter remembered what Jesus had said at supper. Before the rooster crows, you will say three times that you do not know me. And Peter went out into the night, and he cried.

[pause]

{img:rooster-on-stone-wall-before-dawn-grey-light-city-quiet}
Today on the side of Mount Zion there is a church with a golden rooster on its roof. It remembers that night. Next to it is an old stone stairway from the time of Jesus. It runs down the hill toward the valley. Perhaps he walked on it.

[long-pause]

In the morning, the priests took Jesus to the Roman governor, Pontius Pilate. Only the Romans could give the death sentence.

[pause]

We will tell what happened next only briefly and gently. The Bible says Pilate asked him, are you the king of the Jews? And Jesus answered, my kingdom is not of this world. Pilate asked, what is truth? The Bible says Pilate found nothing wrong in him. He wanted to let him go. At Passover it was the custom to free one prisoner. But the crowd outside asked for another prisoner instead. His name was Barabbas.

[pause]

{img:roman-governor-washing-hands-basin-courtyard-morning-crowd-distant}
In the end Pilate gave in. He washed his hands in a bowl, in front of the crowd. And he said, I am not to blame for this man. Then he handed Jesus over to be put to death on a cross. That was the Roman punishment for rebels and slaves.

[long-pause]

The soldiers made fun of him, the Bible says. They put a purple cloak on him and a crown of thorns. They called him the king of the Jews. Then they led him out through the streets. He carried the wooden beam of his cross. They went toward a place outside the walls called Golgotha.

[pause]

On the way, the soldiers stopped a man coming in from the fields. His name was Simon, and he came from North Africa. They made him carry the beam. A big crowd followed. Many women walked with them, and they were crying.

[long-pause]

{img:distant-hill-outside-city-walls-three-crosses-dark-sky-far-away}
We will not go close to Golgotha. We will watch from very far away, as the women did. The Bible says they stood at a distance and watched. There was Mary from Magdala. There was another Mary, and Salome. And many other women who had followed him from Galilee and cared for him.

[pause]

The gospel of John says his mother Mary was there too. She stood near the cross with her sister, and with John, his close friend.

[long-pause]

It was about nine in the morning. The place was a small rocky hill, an old quarry just outside the city walls. It was beside a road, so everyone passing by could see. Above his head the Romans put a sign in three languages. It said, Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews.

[pause]

The Bible remembers a few things he said. About the soldiers he said, Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing. To a man beside him who asked to be remembered, he said, today you will be with me in paradise. And he looked at his mother and his friend John. To his mother he said, here is your son. And to John, here is your mother. From that hour, John took her into his own home.

[pause]

{img:women-standing-at-distance-hillside-grey-sky-silent-mourning}
At midday, the Bible says, darkness came over the land. It lasted until three in the afternoon. That was the hour of the evening offering in the Temple. At that hour the trumpets sounded, and the Passover lambs were being offered.

[pause]

And at three, Jesus called out in a loud voice. It is finished. Father, into your hands I give my spirit. And he passed away.

[long-pause]

The Bible says that the great curtain in the Temple tore in two, from top to bottom. And the Roman officer standing there saw how he died. And he said, truly, this man was the Son of God.

[pause]

Then it was late afternoon. At sunset the day of rest would begin, and the festival. There was not much time. A rich man called Joseph of Arimathea went to Pilate. He was a member of the council, and a secret follower of Jesus. It was brave to go. He asked for the body. And Pilate said yes.

[pause]

{img:rock-cut-tomb-garden-olive-trees-round-stone-evening-light}
Joseph and another council member, Nicodemus, took the body down gently. They wrapped it in clean linen with sweet-smelling spices, as was the custom. They laid it in a new grave cut in the rock. It was in a garden close by, and it belonged to Joseph. Nobody had ever been laid there before. Then they rolled a big round stone across the door.

[long-pause]

Mary from Magdala and the other Mary sat across from the grave. They watched where he was laid. Then they went home before the sun went down. They began to prepare spices and oils. And on the day of rest, they rested, as the Law asks. The city was silent. And nobody knew what the next morning would bring.

[long-pause]

{img:jerusalem-sabbath-quiet-streets-shuttered-houses-morning-pigeons}
The next day was the day of rest, the Sabbath. It was also the first day of the great festival.

[pause]

In the city it was a holy day. Nobody worked. The shops were shut and the markets were empty. Families rested. They went up to the Temple to pray and sing. They visited each other in their best clothes. And they walked slowly through the quiet streets.

[long-pause]

For the friends of Jesus, it was the saddest day of their lives.

[pause]

They were hiding in a house in the city. The doors were locked. They were afraid they would be arrested too. Every step outside made them look up. They had lost their teacher and their friend. They had followed him for years. They had hoped he would save Israel. Now it all seemed over. They did not know what to do or where to go. Perhaps they would go home to Galilee, to their boats and their nets.

[pause]

The women were waiting too. They waited for the day of rest to end. Then they could go to the grave with the spices they had made ready.

[pause]

And Peter perhaps sat alone. He remembered the fire in the courtyard and the rooster. He remembered the three times he had said, I do not know him.

[long-pause]

Outside, the festival went on. Pilgrims ate their flat bread and their roasted lamb. The Temple was full of people praying. Roman soldiers watched from the fortress. Pilate stayed in Herod's palace. And the full moon rose again over the Mount of Olives.

[pause]

{img:full-moon-rising-over-mount-of-olives-quiet-city-roofs-night}
In the garden outside the city wall, the grave was quiet. The big stone lay across the door. The gospel of Matthew says soldiers were sent to guard it. The chief priests had asked Pilate for them. They wanted to be sure that nothing would happen.

[pause]

It was the quietest day of the whole story. Christians still remember it every year, as Holy Saturday. It is a day of waiting and stillness. It lies between the sorrow of Friday and the joy of Easter morning.

[long-pause]

{img:rock-cut-tomb-hillside-rolling-stone-door-garden-olive-trees}
How were people buried in Jerusalem in the time of Jesus?

[pause]

Families who had enough money had a family grave. It was cut into the rock of the hills around the city. The graves were always outside the walls. The Jewish law says the dead must rest outside the city.

[long-pause]

Such a grave is like a small cave in the soft white rock. It has a low little doorway. Often a big round stone closes it, like a millstone. The stone rolls in a groove in front of the door. Inside there is a small room. Along the walls there are long narrow shelves cut into the rock. That is where the dead were laid.

[pause]

When someone passed away, the family washed the body. They put sweet oils and spices on it, and wrapped it in linen. They laid it in the grave on the same day, before sunset if they could. Then they mourned at home for seven days.

[pause]

{img:ossuaries-stone-boxes-niche-tomb-interior-soft-lamp-light}
A year later, the family came back. By then only the bones were left. They gathered the bones into a small stone box. Such a box is called an ossuary. It was often carved with flowers and patterns. The person's name was scratched on it. That way there was room on the shelf again, for the next member of the family.

[long-pause]

Hundreds of these graves have been found around Jerusalem. Thousands of bone boxes have been found too. Many have names on them. Mary and Martha, Joseph and Simon, John and Jesus. These were all very common names then. One box, as we heard, belonged to the High Priest Caiaphas.

[pause]

Inside the great church in the old city, behind the grave that is honoured as the grave of Jesus, there is a small chapel. In it is an old grave from the time of Jesus, cut into the rock. It looks just like the graves the Bible describes.

[pause]

{img:quiet-garden-dawn-jerusalem-old-stones-birds-spring-calm}
And just outside the old city walls there is a quiet garden. It is called the Garden Tomb. It has an old grave in the rock, found about a hundred and fifty years ago. Some Christians believe it may be the grave of Jesus. Archaeologists think it is much older. But it is a peaceful place, with trees and flowers and birdsong. Pilgrims come there on Easter morning, at dawn, to sing and pray.

[long-pause]

{img:early-morning-garden-tomb-stone-rolled-away-dawn-light-women-spices}
And then, the Bible says, very early on Sunday morning, while it was still dark, the women went to the grave. They carried the spices they had made. Mary from Magdala was there, and the other Mary, and Salome, and Joanna, and others.

[pause]

On the way, they were worried. Who will roll the stone away for us, they asked. It was very big. And there were soldiers. Would they even be let in?

[long-pause]

But when they reached the garden, in the first grey light, they saw something strange. The stone had already been rolled away. And the grave was empty.

[pause]

The gospels tell this part in different ways, with different details. That is what people do when they remember something amazing. They tell of a young man in a white robe, or of two men in shining clothes. Angels. They sat in the grave and said to the women, do not be afraid. You are looking for Jesus of Nazareth. He is not here. He has risen. Why do you look for the living among the dead?

[pause]

The women ran, the Bible says, shaking, full of fear and great joy. They ran to tell the others. Peter and John ran to the grave. John ran faster and got there first. He looked in and saw the linen cloths lying there. The cloth that had been around his head was folded up, by itself. And they believed.

[long-pause]

The gospel of John tells the most beautiful part.

[pause]

Mary from Magdala stayed by the grave after the others had gone. She was crying. Then she turned around. A man was standing in the garden, in the early light. She thought he was the gardener. He asked her, why are you crying? Who are you looking for? She said, sir, if you have taken him away, tell me where you have put him.

[pause]

{img:woman-in-garden-dawn-turning-recognizing-figure-light-flowers}
And he said to her, Mary.

[pause]

And she turned and knew him. And she said, Rabbouni. That means teacher.

[long-pause]

That is the story of the first Easter morning, as the gospels tell it.

[pause]

Historians cannot prove what happened that morning in the garden. But they agree on one thing. Something changed the friends of Jesus completely. They had been frightened and hiding. Within a few weeks, they were standing in the streets of Jerusalem. They were telling everyone that he was alive. And they kept telling it all over the world, for the rest of their lives.

[pause]

{img:sunrise-over-jerusalem-hills-golden-light-garden-birds-spring}
And every year on Easter morning, Christians all over the world greet each other with the old words. Christ is risen. And the answer comes back. He is risen indeed.

[long-pause]

{img:magdala-town-shore-sea-of-galilee-fish-drying-racks-boats}
Mary from Magdala is one of the most important people in the Easter story.

[pause]

She came from Magdala, a busy fishing town on the Sea of Galilee. The town was known for its salted fish, sent all over the Roman world. Her name simply means Mary from Magdala. The Bible says Jesus had healed her of a serious illness. After that she followed him with other women. She helped pay for the journey with her own money.

[long-pause]

She was there at the cross, when most of the men had run away. She was there at the burial. And all four gospels say she was the first to come to the grave on Easter morning. In the gospel of John, she is the first to see Jesus alive in the garden. And she is the first to tell the others. So the early Christians gave her a special name. The messenger to the messengers.

[pause]

Later, people mixed her up with other women in the Bible. They said she had been a sinful woman. But the Bible does not say that anywhere. In 2016 the Pope gave her a great feast day, the same as the twelve apostles.

[pause]

In 2009, archaeologists dug at Magdala by the lake. They found a synagogue from the time of Jesus. It is one of the oldest ever found in Galilee. It has a mosaic floor and painted walls and stone benches. There is a carved stone with a picture of the seven-branched lamp of the Temple. Jesus may have taught there. And Mary may have prayed there.

[long-pause]

There is an old legend from the Greek church. After Easter, it says, Mary went all the way to Rome to see the emperor. She told him that Christ had risen. And she held up an egg, as a sign of new life. The emperor laughed. He said a man could no more rise from the dead than that egg could turn red. And at that very moment, the egg in her hand turned red.

[pause]

{img:red-painted-easter-eggs-basket-candles-table-orthodox-spring}
That, the legend says, is why people in Greece and Russia and many other lands paint their Easter eggs red. On Easter morning they tap their eggs together. And they say, Christ is risen.

[long-pause]

{img:two-travellers-road-to-emmaus-hills-afternoon-stranger-walking}
On the afternoon of that same Sunday, the gospel of Luke tells a quiet story. Two friends of Jesus were walking from Jerusalem to a village called Emmaus. It was about eleven kilometres away, in the hills to the west.

[pause]

One of them was called Cleopas. They were talking sadly about everything that had happened. As they walked, a stranger came up and walked beside them. He asked what they were talking about.

[long-pause]

They stopped. Their faces were sad. Cleopas said, are you the only visitor in Jerusalem who has not heard what happened there? And they told him about Jesus of Nazareth. A prophet, they said, strong in what he did and said. They told how he was handed over and put to death. And they said, we had hoped he was the one who would set Israel free.

[pause]

They told him one more thing. Some women had gone to the grave that morning. They found it empty. And they said they had seen angels who told them he was alive. The two friends did not know what to think.

[pause]

{img:dusty-hill-road-judean-hills-olive-terraces-evening-golden-walkers}
The stranger walked on with them. He talked with them and explained the old holy books, all along the road. The afternoon light turned golden. The shadows grew long over the hills.

[long-pause]

When they came near the village, he made as if to walk on. But they asked him to stay. Stay with us, they said. It is nearly evening, and the day is almost over. So he went in with them.

[pause]

At the table, he took the bread. He blessed it and broke it and gave it to them. And suddenly their eyes were opened. They knew him. And then he was gone.

[pause]

{img:simple-house-table-bread-broken-oil-lamp-evening-two-amazed}
They looked at each other. Did our hearts not burn inside us, they said, while he talked with us on the road?

[pause]

They got up that same hour. They walked all the way back to Jerusalem in the dark, eleven kilometres, to tell the others.

[long-pause]

Nobody knows exactly where Emmaus was. Over the centuries, several villages in the hills have been shown to pilgrims as Emmaus. Some have old churches and ruins.

[pause]

But the story is one of the most loved in the Bible. Two sad friends walk along a road at evening. A stranger walks beside them, and they do not know him. Not until he breaks the bread. Painters have painted it again and again. Rembrandt painted it several times. He painted a dark room with one candle. There is the bread, the two startled friends, and a quiet light around the stranger's face.

[long-pause]

{img:pentecost-crowd-jerusalem-street-apostles-speaking-many-nations}
Fifty days after Passover, in early summer, there was another big festival in Jerusalem. It was the feast of the wheat harvest. In Greek it was called Pentecost, which means the fiftieth day. The first loaves of new wheat were brought to the Temple. And again the city was full of pilgrims from all over the world.

[pause]

On that day, the Bible says, the friends of Jesus were together in a house. They felt the Holy Spirit come to them, like a rushing wind and like small flames of fire. They went out into the streets and spoke to the crowds about Jesus. And the pilgrims from many lands each heard them in their own language.

[long-pause]

Peter the fisherman stood up in front of the crowd. This was the same Peter who had said, I do not know him. Now he was not afraid at all. He spoke loud and clear. The Bible says about three thousand people were baptized that day.

[pause]

That was the start of the Christian church. It was a small group of Jews in Jerusalem. They believed Jesus was the promised king and that he had risen. They met in their houses to pray and to share bread. They shared everything they had. And every day they still went up to the Temple to pray.

[pause]

{img:early-christians-house-meal-sharing-bread-lamps-courtyard-evening}
From Jerusalem the message spread. First to nearby towns, then to Damascus and Antioch. Then a man called Paul travelled to Greece and to Rome. Within three hundred years, it had spread over the whole Roman Empire, and further.

[long-pause]

And Jerusalem itself?

[pause]

Thirty-three years later, the Jews of Judea rose up against Rome. Caiaphas's old fear came true. In the year 70, after a long siege, the Roman army took Jerusalem. The city and the Temple were destroyed. We will not describe it. The Temple was never built again.

[pause]

{img:western-wall-great-herodian-stones-morning-light-pigeons-quiet}
Only one part of Herod's great wall around the Temple platform survived. It is called the Western Wall. Its huge stones are still there today. For nearly two thousand years it has been the holiest place where Jews can pray. People come from all over the world. They pray there, and they push little folded notes with prayers between the old stones.

[long-pause]

Later, after another uprising, the Romans built a new Roman town on the ruins. On the place where Christians remembered the cross and the grave, the Romans built a temple to one of their goddesses.

[pause]

Two hundred years after that, the first Christian emperor, Constantine, had that temple taken down. Under it, his workers found a grave cut in the rock. The Christians of Jerusalem believed it was the grave of Jesus. So Constantine built a great church there. It is called the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, which means the holy grave. And it still stands today.

[long-pause]

{img:jerusalem-old-city-today-dome-of-rock-walls-mount-of-olives-view}
Today Jerusalem is a big city with nearly a million people. It is holy to three faiths. To Jews, to Christians and to Muslims.

[pause]

The old city lies inside walls built about five hundred years ago, by a Turkish sultan. It has four parts. A Jewish part, a Christian part, a Muslim part and an Armenian part. It is a maze of narrow stone streets. There are markets, churches, mosques and synagogues. And it is always crowded with pilgrims and visitors.

[long-pause]

On the Temple Mount, where Herod's Temple once stood, there are now two of the holiest places of Islam. One is the golden Dome of the Rock. The other is the al-Aqsa Mosque. Below them is the Western Wall, where Jews pray every day and every night.

[pause]

In the Christian part is the Church of the Holy Sepulchre. It is a big, dark, very old church, built again and again over many centuries. Under one roof it holds both the small hill of Golgotha and the grave. Six different Christian churches share it. They do not always agree. So for centuries, the key to the great door has been kept by a Muslim family. Every morning they open the door. And every night they close it again.

[pause]

A street called the Via Dolorosa runs through the old city. The name means the way of sadness. Tradition says Jesus walked this way carrying his cross. There are fourteen stops along it, marked on the walls. Pilgrims stop there and pray. Every Friday, monks in brown robes lead a walk along it.

[long-pause]

At Easter, Jerusalem is full of pilgrims again, just as it was in the year 33.

[pause]

On Palm Sunday they walk down the Mount of Olives with palm branches, singing. On Thursday they gather in the upper room and in the olive garden. On Friday thousands walk the Via Dolorosa. Some carry wooden crosses. On Saturday, the Orthodox Christians fill the great church for the Holy Fire. They believe a flame appears in the grave. It is passed from candle to candle, all through the church. Soon thousands of candles are burning. The flame is carried out into the city. It is even flown on planes to churches in Greece and Russia.

[pause]

{img:candles-held-crowd-easter-church-holy-fire-glow-faces}
And at the same time, in the Jewish part of the city, families sit down for the Passover meal. There is flat bread and bitter herbs and four cups of wine. The children ask their questions. And the old story of leaving Egypt is told again, as it has been for more than three thousand years. At the end of the meal, they say the words that Jews all over the world have said for two thousand years. Next year in Jerusalem.

[long-pause]

So we leave Jerusalem tonight. The spring evening falls over the hills. The church bells ring. And the old olive trees of Gethsemane stand silver in the moonlight.

[pause]

We leave the pilgrims singing on the roads, and the families on their roofs at sunset. We leave the women in the garden at dawn. And we leave two friends walking home to Emmaus in the dark, with their hearts burning inside them.

[pause]

{img:mount-of-olives-evening-old-city-golden-walls-quiet-dusk}
If this quiet Easter story helped you rest, you are very welcome to subscribe to the channel, and to come back for another one.

[pause]

There should be another quiet story waiting on the screen now, if you are still awake.
