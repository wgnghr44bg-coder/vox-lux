What was the Passover meal like, in the time of Jesus?

It was eaten in the evening, after sunset, inside Jerusalem. Families and friends ate together, at least ten people. That way they could share a whole roasted lamb. Nothing of the lamb could be left over until morning.

--

They did not stand, as their ancestors had done in Egypt. Now they were free people. So they lay on cushions around low tables, like guests at a feast.

On the table lay the roasted lamb. There was flat bread, called matzah. It is made of only flour and water, with no yeast. It reminds them that their ancestors left Egypt in a hurry, with no time for the bread to rise. There were bitter herbs, like lettuce or horseradish. They stand for the bitter years of slavery. There was a sweet brown paste of fruit and nuts. It looks like the clay the slaves used to make bricks. There was salt water, for the tears. And there was wine.

{img:child-asking-question-father-passover-table-lamplight-family}
During the meal they drank four cups of wine, each with its own blessing. And the youngest child asked a question. Why is this night different from all other nights?

Then the father told the old story. He told how the people were slaves in Egypt. He told how God heard them cry. And he told how Moses led them out, through the sea, to freedom. The children listened, wide awake. Every year they knew how it ended. And still they waited for the part where the sea opened.

--

They sang songs of praise before and after the meal. And they ate and talked and remembered, late into the night.

Jewish families still hold this meal today, all over the world, every spring. It is called the Seder, which means order, because it follows a set order. There is no Temple now, so there is no lamb. Instead a roasted bone lies on the plate, as a reminder. But the flat bread is the same. So are the bitter herbs and the sweet paste. So are the four cups, the questions of the children, and the story.

{img:modern-seder-plate-matzah-family-table-candles-warm-later}
And at the end, someone opens the door. It is opened for the prophet Elijah. The old belief is that one day he will come, to say that the promised king is near. A cup of wine is poured for him. It stays on the table, waiting.
