What was the Passover meal like, in the time of Jesus?

It was eaten in the evening, after sunset, in Jerusalem, by families, and groups of friends, together, at least ten people, to share a whole lamb, roasted, which had been sacrificed that afternoon, in the Temple. Nothing of the lamb could be left until the morning.

--

They reclined, on couches, or cushions, around low tables, like free people, at a feast, not standing, as their ancestors had done in Egypt, in a hurry, because now they were free.

On the table, there was the roasted lamb. And matzah, the flat, unleavened bread, the bread of affliction, made only of flour and water, baked quickly, without yeast, to remember how their ancestors left Egypt in haste, without time for the bread to rise. And bitter herbs, lettuce, or endive, or horseradish, to remember the bitterness of slavery. And a sweet paste, of fruit, and nuts, and wine, called charoset, which looks like the clay that the slaves used to make bricks, in Egypt. And salt water, for the tears. And wine.

{img:child-asking-question-father-passover-table-lamplight-family}
During the meal, they drank four cups of wine, at different moments, each with its own blessing. And the youngest child asked questions: why is this night different from all other nights? On all other nights, we eat bread with yeast. Why, tonight, only unleavened bread?

And the father, or the head of the family, answered, by telling the story of the Exodus: how the people of Israel were slaves in Egypt, and how God heard their cry, and sent Moses, and brought them out, with a mighty hand, and an outstretched arm, through the Red Sea, to freedom.

--

They sang the psalms of the Hallel, praising God, part before the meal, and part after it. And they ate, and drank, and talked, late into the night, and remembered.

The Passover meal is still celebrated today, by Jewish families, all over the world, every spring, and is called the Seder, which means order, because it follows a fixed order. Without the Temple, there is no sacrificed lamb any more. Instead, there is a roasted bone, on the Seder plate, as a reminder. But the matzah, and the bitter herbs, and the charoset, and the salt water, and the four cups of wine, and the questions of the children, and the story, are the same.

{img:modern-seder-plate-matzah-family-table-candles-warm-later}
And at the end, the door is opened, for the prophet Elijah, who, the tradition says, will come one day, to announce the Messiah. And a cup of wine is poured for him, and left on the table, waiting.
