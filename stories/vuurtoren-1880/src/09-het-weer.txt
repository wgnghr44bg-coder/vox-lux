For a lighthouse keeper, the weather is very important. It decides everything. Whether the supply boat can come. Whether the fog signal must be sounded. Whether the windows of the lantern will be covered with salt or snow. Whether there will be ships in trouble. So the keepers watch the weather constantly, and write it down, several times a day, in the log book.

--

The lighthouse has weather instruments. A barometer, which measures the pressure of the air. When the pressure falls, bad weather is coming. When it rises, good weather is coming. A thermometer, which measures the temperature. A rain gauge, a little copper pot in the garden, which collects the rain, so that the keepers can measure how much has fallen. And a wind vane on top of the lantern, which shows the direction of the wind.

Three times a day, the keepers read the instruments, and write the results in a special book. The temperature, the pressure, the wind, the rain, the clouds and the state of the sea. Once a month, they send these records to Edinburgh, where scientists study them. The lighthouses of Scotland are like a great chain of weather stations, all around the coast. Their records help the scientists to understand the weather, and to start making forecasts.

{img:dark-storm-clouds-approaching-island-sea-whitecaps-lighthouse-waiting}
The keepers also learn to read the weather from nature, like fishermen and farmers. James can look at the sky in the morning, and tell you what the weather will be in the afternoon. A red sky at night means fine weather. A red sky in the morning means rain. When the sea birds fly inland, a storm is coming. When the seals come up on the rocks, the weather will be calm. When the distant islands look very close and clear, rain is on its way.

The weather on the island changes very quickly. In the morning, it can be sunny and calm, with the sea like glass. By the afternoon, the wind can be howling, and the waves crashing against the cliffs. Then, by the evening, it can be calm again, with a beautiful sunset. Islanders say that if you do not like the weather in Scotland, you only have to wait ten minutes. There are often several kinds of weather in a single day.

--

{img:rainbow-over-sea-island-after-rain-lighthouse-sunlight-breaking-clouds}
And after the rain, there are often rainbows. Great arches of colour, over the sea, between the islands. Sometimes two rainbows at once, one above the other. Calum once tried to run to the end of a rainbow, to find the pot of gold that his grandmother had told him about. He ran all the way across the island, to the beach. But the rainbow kept moving away. When he came back, out of breath, his mother laughed, and said that the gold was not at the end of the rainbow. It was the rainbow itself.

In the log book, James also writes down the ships that pass the island. Their type, their direction, and sometimes their name, if they are close enough to read it through his telescope. Fishing boats from the nearby villages. Cargo steamers, with black smoke pouring from their funnels. Big sailing ships, with all their white sails spread. Little puffers, the small steam boats that carry coal and goods between the islands. And sometimes, a smart yacht, belonging to some rich gentleman on holiday.

{img:keeper-telescope-balcony-watching-sailing-ship-passing-island-sunny}
Isla loves to look through the telescope. Her father lets her use it, if she is careful. She watches the ships passing, far out at sea, and tries to guess where they are going. To Glasgow. To Liverpool. To Norway. To America. She writes their names in a little notebook of her own. She has more than two hundred names in it now. And she dreams that one day, she will sail on one of them, to some faraway place.

--

At the end of each day, James writes a short summary in the log book. The weather. The ships. The work done. Any problems. It is usually very short. Wind westerly, moderate. Fine and clear. Light lit at sunset, extinguished at sunrise. All well. All well. Those are the words that every keeper hopes to write, every day. And on most days, on the little island, that is exactly what happens.
