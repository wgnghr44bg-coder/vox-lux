Today, many of the old lighthouses of Scotland can be visited. Some are museums. Some are holiday cottages. Some are bird observatories. And some can be seen from the shore, or from a boat, standing white and tall on their islands and rocks, just as they did a hundred and fifty years ago.

--

In the town of Fraserburgh, in the north-east of Scotland, there is the Museum of Scottish Lighthouses. It is built next to Kinnaird Head, the very first lighthouse of the Northern Lighthouse Board, lit in 1787. Visitors can climb up the old tower, to the lantern room, and see the great glass lens, still in its brass frame, shining. And in the museum, there are dozens of lenses, lamps, fog bells, uniforms, log books and photographs, telling the story of the lighthouses and their keepers.

The greatest treasure of the museum is the collection of lenses. Great, beautiful glass lenses, from lighthouses all around Scotland, some of them taller than a person. When the sun shines through them, they break the light into rainbows, which dance on the walls of the museum, just as they danced in Isla's lantern room. Children stand inside the lenses, and look out through them, and see the world turned upside down and broken into pieces of light.

{img:visitors-looking-at-giant-glass-lens-museum-rainbows-on-walls-today}
Many of the keepers' houses, at the old lighthouses, have become holiday cottages. People can stay in them, for a week or two, on lonely headlands and islands, with the lighthouse right outside the window. They can watch the sea, and the birds, and the stars, and see the light flashing over the water at night. Some people say it is the most peaceful holiday in the world. A taste of the life of the keepers, without the work.

Some lighthouse islands have become nature reserves. On islands like the Isle of May, in the Firth of Forth, or Fair Isle, the old lighthouse buildings are now used by scientists and bird watchers, who study the sea birds. The puffins, the gannets, the guillemots and the kittiwakes still nest on the cliffs, every spring, in their thousands, just as they did when Calum lay on his stomach at the top of the cliff, watching them.

--

{img:puffins-today-cliff-top-lighthouse-behind-birdwatchers-binoculars-spring}
The Bell Rock lighthouse, built by Robert Stevenson more than two hundred years ago, still stands in the North Sea, with the waves breaking around it. It is still working, automatically. On a clear day, from the coast near Arbroath, you can see it, a thin white pencil on the horizon. And in Arbroath, there is the old signal tower, where the keepers' families lived, which is now a museum. Visitors can see how they sent signals to the keepers on the rock, with flags and balls on a mast.

Skerryvore still stands too, tall and beautiful, on its lonely rock in the Atlantic, west of the island of Tiree. On the island, there is a little museum about it, in the old signal tower. And on clear nights, from the beaches of Tiree, you can see its light, flashing out over the dark ocean, every ten seconds. It has been shining for more than a hundred and eighty years.

{img:skerryvore-lighthouse-distant-sunset-from-tiree-beach-today-calm}
And on the west coast, among the islands, little lighthouses still stand on the headlands and the rocks. Some are tall towers, some are small white huts, with a single lamp. They are painted white, or white with a red or black band. They are photographed by tourists, painted by artists, and loved by sailors and walkers. They have become one of the symbols of Scotland. Like the castles, the mountains and the lochs.

--

People still love lighthouses. All over the world. There are books, films, paintings and songs about them. Children draw them. Adults dream of living in them. Perhaps it is because they stand for something good. For watchfulness, and care, and faithfulness. For a light in the darkness, which never goes out. For people who stay awake, through the long nights, so that others can be safe.
