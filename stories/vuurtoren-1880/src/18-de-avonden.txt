In the winter, the evenings on the island are very long. The sun sets in the middle of the afternoon, and rises late in the morning. In December, in the north of Scotland, there are only about six hours of daylight. The rest is darkness. And the families must find ways to fill the long, dark evenings, by the fire, while the storms blow outside.

--

The Northern Lighthouse Board has a wonderful idea, to help the keepers. A travelling library. Every few months, a wooden box of books arrives on the relief boat. It contains about fifty books, of all kinds. Novels, histories, travel books, books about science and nature, religious books, and books for children. The families read them, and then, after a few months, send the box back, and receive a different one. So over the years, they read hundreds of books.

When the library box arrives, it is like Christmas. Everyone gathers around the kitchen table, as James opens the lid. They take the books out, one by one, and look at them, and read the titles aloud. Who wants this one? Who has read that one? Isla always grabs the books about faraway countries and natural history. Mairi likes the novels. James likes history and adventure. And Calum looks for books with pictures of ships.

{img:family-reading-by-paraffin-lamp-fireside-winter-evening-storm-shutters}
In this box, there is a new book, by a young Scottish writer called Robert Louis Stevenson. Yes, the son of the lighthouse engineer. It is about his journey through the mountains of France, on foot, with a stubborn little donkey called Modestine. It is not one of his famous novels. Those are still to come. But James is very interested, because he knows the Stevenson family. He reads the book aloud, in the evenings, to the whole family, by the fire. And he says that the young man writes very well, and that perhaps he will be famous one day.

There is also music, in the evenings. James plays the fiddle. He plays old Scottish and Gaelic tunes. Jigs and reels, quick and lively, for dancing. Slow airs, sad and beautiful, which make Mairi's eyes wet. And songs, which everyone sings together. Songs about the sea, about the islands, about love and loss, about the hills of home. Donald has a beautiful deep voice, and Effie sings like a bird. The houses ring with music.

--

{img:keeper-playing-fiddle-family-dancing-kitchen-reel-lamp-light-laughter}
Sometimes, they dance. They push back the table and the chairs, in the kitchen, and dance a reel. James plays, and the others dance, in pairs, round and round, stamping their feet, and clapping. Even little Angus, the baby, bounces up and down in his mother's arms. The kitchen is small, and they bump into each other, and laugh. And outside, the storm howls, but nobody notices.

And there are stories. In the long evenings, by the fire, James tells the old Gaelic stories that his grandmother told him, on Skye. Stories of giants and heroes, of selkies and kelpies, of the fairy folk who live in the green hills, and the blue men of the Minch, who live in the sea between the islands, and raise storms to sink ships. The children listen, wide-eyed, as the wind moans in the chimney.

{img:father-telling-gaelic-stories-fireside-children-wide-eyes-wind-outside}
Mairi knits, all evening, every evening. Sweaters for James, thick and warm, for the cold nights in the lighthouse. Socks for everyone. Hats and mittens for the children. She knits beautiful patterns, which her mother taught her, on Skye. She can knit without looking, while she listens to the stories, or the music, or the book being read aloud. Her needles click softly, all evening, like a little clock.

--

At about nine o'clock, the children go to bed. James goes up the tower, for his watch. Mairi banks down the fire. And the house becomes quiet. Outside, the wind is still blowing, and the sea is still roaring against the cliffs. But inside, it is warm and safe. And every thirty seconds, the beam of the lighthouse passes over the shutters, steady and calm, keeping watch over the island, and the sea, and the ships, through the long winter night.
