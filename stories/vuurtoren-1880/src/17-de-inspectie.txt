Once a year, in the summer, there is a very special visit. The Commissioners of the Northern Lighthouse Board come to inspect the lighthouse. They travel around the whole coast of Scotland, in their own steamship, visiting every lighthouse, one after another. The ship is called the Pharos, after the famous lighthouse of Alexandria. And when it appears on the horizon, the whole island is in a flurry.

--

For weeks before the inspection, the keepers and their families prepare. Everything must be perfect. The lens is cleaned until it sparkles. The brass is polished until it shines like gold. The tower is painted white, inside and out. The houses are scrubbed from top to bottom. The garden is weeded. The paths are swept. The store rooms are tidied. The log books are checked, and checked again. Even the cow is brushed.

When the Pharos arrives, it anchors off the island, and a small boat brings the Commissioners to the jetty. They are important men. Judges, lawyers and officials from Edinburgh, in dark suits and top hats. And with them comes the engineer, one of the Stevensons, a tall, serious man with a beard. The keepers are waiting on the jetty, in their best uniforms, with their caps on, and their brass buttons shining.

{img:commissioners-top-hats-inspecting-lighthouse-lantern-room-keeper-attention}
The Commissioners walk up to the lighthouse, and the inspection begins. They climb the tower, and look at everything. The lamp. The lens. The clockwork. The windows. They run their white gloves over the brass, to check for dust. They look at the log books, and read the records of the weather, the ships and the work. They ask the keepers questions. How much oil do you use? How often do you trim the wicks? Have there been any problems?

The engineer looks at the technical things. The lamp, the lens and the machinery. He checks that everything is working properly. He listens to the clockwork, and checks the timing of the flashes. He looks at the walls of the tower, for cracks. He looks at the fog bell. He asks James about the storm, and the fog, and the rescue of the fishermen. He writes everything down, in a little notebook.

--

{img:family-lined-up-best-clothes-commissioners-visit-cottage-door-nervous}
Then the Commissioners visit the houses. They look at the kitchens, the bedrooms, the store rooms. They want to see that the keepers and their families are living well, and that the houses are clean and in good repair. Mairi and Effie stand at their doors, in their best dresses, nervous. The children stand beside them, washed and combed, in their Sunday clothes. Calum's hair, as usual, is sticking up at the back.

The Commissioners are pleased. Everything is in perfect order. The lens sparkles. The brass shines. The log books are neat and complete. The houses are spotless. And the chief Commissioner, an old judge with white whiskers, has heard about the rescue of the fishermen. He shakes James's hand, and Donald's, and thanks them. And he says that the Board will give them each a reward, of money, for their bravery.

{img:old-commissioner-giving-girl-book-reward-lighthouse-yard-smiling}
Then he turns to Isla, and asks if it is true that she was the first to see the wrecked boat. Isla blushes, and says yes. The old judge smiles, and says that she has the eyes of a true lighthouse keeper. And he gives her a present, from his own pocket. A small book, bound in leather, about the history of the lighthouses of Scotland. Isla is so happy that she cannot speak. She just curtseys, and holds the book against her chest.

--

In the afternoon, the Commissioners return to their ship. The Pharos raises its anchor, and steams away, toward the next lighthouse, with smoke pouring from its funnel. The families stand on the jetty, waving. And then they walk back up to the lighthouse, and take off their best clothes, and sit down in the kitchen, and laugh with relief. It has been a good inspection. The best ever, James says. And Mairi makes a special supper, to celebrate.
