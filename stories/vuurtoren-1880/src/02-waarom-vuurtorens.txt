People have been building lighthouses for more than two thousand years. The most famous lighthouse of the ancient world was the Pharos of Alexandria, in Egypt. It was built about three hundred years before Christ, on an island at the entrance to the harbour. It was enormous, more than a hundred metres tall. And at the top, a great fire burned every night, to guide the ships into the harbour. It was one of the Seven Wonders of the World.

--

The Romans built lighthouses too, all over their empire. At Dover, on the cliffs above the harbour, one of their lighthouses still stands today, almost two thousand years later. In the Middle Ages, monks sometimes kept lights burning in their chapels on the coast, to warn sailors. And in the sixteen and seventeen hundreds, more and more lighthouses were built, as more and more ships sailed the seas.

The early lighthouses used fires of wood or coal, burning in an iron basket at the top of a tower. They used enormous amounts of fuel. And they were not very bright. Smoke and soot covered everything. In a strong wind, the fire could blow out, or burn too fast. Later, candles were used, dozens of them, in a lantern of glass. And then oil lamps, burning whale oil or vegetable oil, with reflectors behind them, to make the light brighter.

The coast of Scotland is one of the most dangerous coasts in the world. It is long, rocky and broken, with thousands of islands, reefs and hidden rocks. The weather is often wild, with storms, gales and fog. The tides are strong. And in the eighteenth century, there were very few lights on the Scottish coast. Every year, many ships were wrecked, and many sailors were lost. Something had to be done.

So in 1786, a group of people was formed, to build lighthouses around Scotland. It was called the Northern Lighthouse Board. They began with four lighthouses, at the most dangerous points of the coast. And then they built more and more. By 1880, there are dozens of lighthouses around Scotland, on headlands, on islands and even on rocks in the middle of the sea. Their lights shine all around the coast, every night.

--

{img:ship-captain-on-deck-night-seeing-lighthouse-flash-relieved-crew}
Every lighthouse has its own character, so that sailors can tell them apart. Some lights are fixed, shining steadily. Some flash, once, twice or three times. Some flash white, some red. Some flash every ten seconds, some every thirty seconds. The pattern of each light is printed in a book, which every ship's captain carries. So when a captain sees a light at night, he counts the flashes, looks in his book, and knows exactly which lighthouse it is, and where his ship is.

The light of our island flashes white, once every thirty seconds. It can be seen from about thirty kilometres away, on a clear night. It warns the ships of the dangerous rocks around the island, and guides them through the passage between the islands. Fishing boats, cargo ships, steamers carrying passengers, and big sailing ships, all pass by the island. And all of them watch for its light.

{img:lighthouse-keepers-uniform-1880-cap-brass-buttons-standing-proud-tower}
Each lighthouse needs keepers, to look after the light, day and night. In Scotland, the keepers work for the Northern Lighthouse Board. They wear a uniform, of dark blue cloth, with brass buttons, and a cap. They are carefully chosen. They must be reliable, sober, hardworking and careful. They must be able to read and write, to keep the log book. And they must be willing to live in lonely places, far from towns and villages, sometimes for many years.

--

The keepers are moved from one lighthouse to another, every few years. So they live in many different places, all around the coast of Scotland, during their lives. Some lighthouses are on the mainland, near a village. Others are on islands, with the keepers' families. And some are on rocks in the middle of the sea, where only the keepers can live, without their families. Our island is in between. Lonely, but big enough for families to live together, with the sea all around.
