At sunrise, the keeper on watch puts out the light. He stops the clockwork, draws the curtains, and writes the time in the log book. And then the morning work begins. Because the light must be perfect every night. And to be perfect, it must be cleaned and cared for every day.

--

First, the lamp. The keeper takes it apart, very carefully. He cleans the burners, removes the burnt wicks and fits new ones, and refills the oil. Then the lens. Every single piece of glass must be cleaned, inside and out, with a soft cloth and spirits of wine, until it is perfectly clear. Even a little bit of dust, or a fingerprint, or a trace of soot, would make the light dimmer. It takes hours. The keepers wear linen aprons, so that their uniforms do not scratch the glass.

Then the windows of the lantern. They must be cleaned too, inside and out. Outside, on the balcony, the keeper climbs a little ladder, and wipes away the salt, which the wind blows up from the sea, and the bird droppings, and the dead insects. In winter, sometimes, there is snow or ice on the glass, which must be cleared away. On windy days, this is not easy, high up on the balcony, with the wind trying to blow you off.

{img:keeper-cleaning-lantern-glass-outside-balcony-ladder-wind-sea-below}
And then the brass. The lighthouse is full of brass. The frame of the lens. The lamp. The clockwork. The handrails. The door handles. The instruments. Everything made of brass must be polished, every day, until it shines like gold. The Northern Lighthouse Board is very strict about this. When the inspectors come, they check that everything shines. A lighthouse with dull brass is a badly kept lighthouse.

Isla often helps with the brass. Her father gives her a soft cloth and a tin of polish. She rubs and rubs, the handrails and the door handles, until she can see her face in them. It is hard work, but she likes it. She likes being in the lighthouse, high above the island, with the sea all around. And she likes it when her father looks at her work, and nods, and says that it shines like the sun.

--

{img:girl-polishing-brass-handrail-spiral-staircase-lighthouse-reflection-face}
The keepers also look after the whole station. They paint the tower and the houses, every few years, inside and out. They repair the roofs and the walls, after storms. They clean the gutters. They look after the fog signal. They keep the store rooms tidy, with the oil, the coal, the wicks and the spare parts all in their places. They mend the path to the jetty. Everything must be clean, tidy and in good order, all the time.

There is a list of duties for every day of the week. On Mondays, the keepers do this. On Tuesdays, that. And so on. The Board sends instructions about everything. How to clean the lens. How to trim the wicks. How to paint the tower. How to keep the log book. Even how to wear the uniform. It is a very orderly life. Some people might find it boring. But the keepers know that every little task helps to keep the light shining.

{img:keeper-painting-tower-white-ladder-rope-sunny-day-sea-gulls}
By about midday, the morning work is usually finished. Then the keeper on day duty has his dinner, and the other keeper, who was on watch in the early morning, can rest. In the afternoon, there is time for other work. In the garden. With the animals. Fishing from the rocks. Collecting driftwood on the beach. Or simply sitting outside the house, in the sun, smoking a pipe, and looking at the sea.

--

James likes to walk around the island in the afternoon, when the weather is good. He walks along the cliffs, watching the birds. He checks the rocks and the shore, for anything washed up by the sea. He looks at the sky, and the clouds, and the colour of the sea, to guess what the weather will do. Calum often comes with him, running ahead, climbing on the rocks, and asking a hundred questions. And James answers all of them, patiently, as they walk together in the wind.
