At the end of the year, there are two celebrations. Christmas, and New Year. In Scotland in 1880, New Year is the bigger celebration. It is called Hogmanay. But on the island, the families celebrate both, as well as they can, far from the mainland, with the sea all around them.

--

Christmas in Scotland, in 1880, is a quieter feast than in England. For a long time, the Scottish church did not celebrate it much. But slowly, it is becoming more popular. On the island, the families go to no church, because there is none. But on Christmas morning, James reads the Christmas story from the Bible, by the fire, and everyone sings a hymn. Then there is a special dinner. A goose, or a chicken, with potatoes and vegetables from the garden. And a clootie dumpling, rich and sweet.

The children get presents. Small ones. A new book. A wooden toy. A pair of knitted mittens. An orange, which is a great treat, brought on the last relief boat before the winter. Calum gets a little telescope of his own, made of brass, from his father. Isla gets a box of watercolour paints, so that she can paint the birds of the island. They are both delighted.

{img:children-opening-christmas-presents-fireside-telescope-paints-orange}
But the great feast is Hogmanay, on the last night of the year. In Scotland, everyone celebrates Hogmanay. The house must be cleaned from top to bottom, before the New Year, so that the year begins fresh and clean. All debts should be paid. And on the night of Hogmanay, there is a party, with food, drink, music and dancing, until midnight, when the New Year begins.

On the island, the two families celebrate together, in James and Mairi's kitchen. Mairi has baked shortbread, and black bun, a very rich fruit cake in a pastry case, which is eaten at Hogmanay. There is cheese, and oatcakes, and a little whisky for the grown-ups. James plays the fiddle. Donald sings. Everyone dances. And the children are allowed to stay up until midnight, for the only time in the whole year.

--

{img:hogmanay-kitchen-party-fiddle-dancing-shortbread-whisky-clock-midnight}
At midnight, everyone stands together, holding hands, in a circle, and sings an old Scottish song, written by the poet Robert Burns, almost a hundred years before. It is called Auld Lang Syne. It is about old friends, and old times, and remembering them. Should auld acquaintance be forgot, and never brought to mind. Everyone sings it, with their arms crossed, holding hands. Mairi has tears in her eyes, thinking of her family on Skye.

After midnight, there is the custom of first-footing. The first person to step over the threshold of a house, in the New Year, brings luck for the whole year. The best first-foot is a tall, dark-haired man, carrying gifts. A lump of coal, for warmth. Some bread, for food. Some salt, for flavour. And a little whisky, for good cheer. On the island, Donald, who is tall and dark, goes out of the house, and knocks on the door again, as the first-foot, with his gifts. Everyone cheers.

{img:first-footing-dark-haired-man-doorway-coal-bread-salt-gifts-new-year}
But the lighthouse must still be kept. Even at Hogmanay. Even at midnight. So James slips away, before midnight, to climb the tower, for his watch. And from the lantern room, high above the island, he watches the New Year arrive. At midnight exactly, he looks out over the dark sea, and sees, far away on the mainland, little lights. Bonfires on the hills, celebrating the New Year. And ships out at sea, sounding their horns, and firing rockets.

--

James stands at the window of the lantern, with the great lens turning behind him, and the beam sweeping out over the sea. He thinks about the past year. The storm. The fog. The rescue. The inspection. The children growing. And he thinks about the new year ahead. And he says a little prayer, for his family, for the sailors at sea, and for the light. Then he trims the wicks, and winds the clockwork, and sits down at his desk, to write the first entry of the New Year in the log book. All well.
