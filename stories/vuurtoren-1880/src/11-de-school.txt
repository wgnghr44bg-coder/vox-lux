On the island, there is no school. The nearest school is on the mainland, fifteen kilometres away, across the sea. So the children of the lighthouse keepers must be taught at home. And in 1880, this is a very important matter. Because a few years before, in 1872, a new law was passed in Scotland, which says that every child between five and thirteen must go to school.

--

For children on lonely islands and in remote lighthouses, this is not easy. The Northern Lighthouse Board helps. Sometimes, it pays for a teacher to come and live at a lighthouse, for a few months, to teach the children. Sometimes, the children are sent to the mainland, to live with relatives, and go to school there. And sometimes, the mothers and fathers teach their children themselves, at home.

On our island, Mairi teaches the children. Every morning, after breakfast, they sit at the kitchen table, with their books, slates and pencils. Mairi went to school on Skye until she was fourteen, and she was a good pupil. She teaches them reading, writing and arithmetic. History and geography. A little Bible study. And she teaches them songs, in English and Gaelic.

{img:children-learning-geography-globe-map-lighthouse-kitchen-pointing}
Isla is a very good pupil. She reads everything she can find. She knows the names of all the countries of the world, and their capitals. She can do long sums in her head. She writes beautiful compositions about the sea and the birds. Mairi says that she has taught Isla everything she knows, and that the girl needs a real teacher now. Next year, perhaps, Isla will go to the mainland, to a school in the town, to continue her education.

Calum is not such a good pupil. He finds it very hard to sit still. His eyes keep wandering to the window, where the sea is sparkling and the birds are flying. He would much rather be on the rocks, looking for crabs. His spelling is terrible. But he is very good at drawing, especially boats and birds. And he knows the name of every part of a ship, which is more than his mother does.

--

{img:boy-drawing-ships-on-slate-distracted-looking-out-window-sea-birds}
James also teaches the children, in the afternoons and the evenings. He teaches them about the lighthouse, the lamp and the lens. About the stars, and how to find your way by them at sea. About knots, and boats, and the tides. About the history of Scotland. And he teaches them Gaelic, the language of his own childhood, so that they do not forget it. He tells them the old Gaelic stories, of heroes and giants and the fairy folk of the hills.

Once a year, an inspector comes from the mainland, to check that the children are being properly taught. He is a serious man, with a black coat and spectacles. He asks Isla and Calum questions, and looks at their copy books, and listens to them read. Isla answers everything perfectly. Calum gets most of his spelling wrong. But when the inspector sees his drawings of ships, he smiles, and says that the boy has talent.

{img:school-inspector-black-coat-spectacles-examining-children-copy-books-kitchen}
The children also learn many things that children in town schools do not learn. They know how the weather works, and how to read the sky. They know the names of all the birds, the fish and the plants of the island. They know how to milk a cow, catch a fish, plant potatoes and cut peat. They know how a lighthouse works. And they know the sea, in all its moods. Their father says that the island is their real school. And the sea is their real teacher.

--

In the evenings, after supper, the whole family reads together, by the light of the paraffin lamp. James reads the newspaper, which is weeks old by the time it arrives. Mairi reads a novel. Isla reads a book about faraway countries. And Calum looks at a book of pictures of ships, and asks his father a hundred questions about them. And outside, the light of the lighthouse sweeps across the windows, every thirty seconds, steady and calm.
