The heart of the lighthouse is the light. And in 1880, it is a wonderful piece of engineering. At the top of the tower, in the glass lantern room, there is a great lens, made of hundreds of pieces of glass, set in a frame of brass. It is called a Fresnel lens, after a French scientist, Augustin Fresnel, who invented it, about sixty years before. And it is the reason why the light can be seen so far away.

--

The lens is shaped like a great barrel, or a beehive, taller than a man, made of rings and panels of glass. Each piece of glass is cut very carefully, with a special shape, so that it bends the light from the lamp in the middle, and sends it out in one direction, in a strong, narrow beam. Without the lens, the lamp would light up only the room. With it, its light can be seen for thirty kilometres across the sea.

The lens is very beautiful. When the sun shines through it, during the day, it breaks the light into rainbows, which dance on the walls and the floor of the lantern room. Isla loves to sit there in the afternoons, watching the rainbows move slowly across the room, as the sun moves. But the sun can also be dangerous. The lens can focus the sun's rays, like a magnifying glass, and start a fire. So during the day, the keepers draw linen curtains around the lantern windows, to protect it.

{img:rainbows-dancing-lantern-room-walls-sunlight-through-lens-girl-watching}
In the middle of the lens, there is the lamp. In 1880, it burns paraffin oil, which is a new fuel, made from petroleum. Before, lighthouses burned whale oil, or oil made from rapeseed, called colza. Paraffin burns more brightly, and is cheaper. The lamp has several wicks, one inside the other, in circles, to make a big, bright flame. The oil is pumped up to the wicks from a tank below, by a small pump, driven by a weight, like a clock.

The lens turns around the lamp, so that the beam sweeps across the sea, and flashes as it passes. It is turned by clockwork. A heavy weight hangs on a chain, down the middle of the tower. As the weight slowly falls, it turns a set of gears, which turn the lens. Every hour or so, the keeper must wind the weight back up to the top, with a handle. If he forgets, the lens stops turning, and the light becomes a fixed light, instead of a flashing one. Then the ships might think it is a different lighthouse.

--

{img:keeper-winding-clockwork-handle-weight-chain-gears-lantern-room-night}
Every evening, at sunset, exactly, the keeper lights the lamp. The time of sunset is printed in a table, for every day of the year. The keeper must light the lamp at that exact time, not a minute late. He climbs the stairs, draws back the curtains, lights the wicks, waits for them to burn evenly, adjusts the flame, and starts the clockwork. Then he writes the time in the log book. And the light shines out over the sea.

Lighting the lamp is like a little ceremony, every evening. James does it with great care. Sometimes, Isla goes up with him, and watches. She loves the moment when the flame catches, and the lens suddenly fills with light, and the first beam sweeps out across the darkening sea. Her father says that every evening, when he lights the lamp, he says a little prayer, for all the sailors at sea.

{img:father-and-daughter-lantern-room-first-beam-sweeping-over-sea-dusk}
Every morning, at sunrise, exactly, the keeper puts out the lamp. He stops the clockwork. He draws the curtains around the windows. He writes the time in the log book. And the light rests, until the evening. In winter, when the nights are long, the light burns for sixteen hours. In summer, in the north of Scotland, when the nights are very short, it burns for only five or six.

--

The light must never go out. That is the first rule of every lighthouse. If the lamp goes out, even for a few minutes, a ship could be wrecked on the rocks. So the keepers watch it, all night long, every night, for their whole working lives. In stormy weather and calm, in winter and summer, at Christmas and New Year. Every night, without exception. James has kept that watch for twenty years. And he will keep it for twenty more.
