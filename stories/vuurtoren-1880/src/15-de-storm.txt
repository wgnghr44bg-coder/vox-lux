In the winter, the storms come. The great Atlantic storms, which blow in from the west, across thousands of kilometres of open ocean. They are some of the strongest storms in the world. And the little island, with its lighthouse, stands right in their path.

--

The first sign is the barometer. One morning in December, James sees that it is falling, fast. Lower and lower. He taps it with his finger. It falls further. He looks out of the window. The sky in the west is a strange, dark yellow-grey colour. The sea is calm, but there is a long, slow swell, rolling in from the west. And the sea birds have all disappeared. James knows what is coming.

He tells everyone to get ready. They bring in the washing. They tie down everything that might blow away. They close the shutters on the windows. They bring the cow and the hens into their sheds, and give them extra food. They fill the water buckets, and bring in extra peat and coal for the fires. They check the store of food and oil. And then they wait.

{img:keepers-tying-down-equipment-yard-wind-rising-dark-clouds-family-helping}
The storm arrives in the afternoon. First, the wind rises. It moans around the houses, and whistles in the chimneys. Then it howls. Then it roars. The rain comes, not falling down, but flying sideways, in great sheets, battering against the windows. The sea, which was calm in the morning, becomes a wild, grey, heaving mass, covered with white foam. Huge waves crash against the cliffs, and send spray flying high into the air, over the top of the island.

Inside the houses, the families sit by the fire. The walls are thick, and the houses are strong. They were built for storms like this. But the noise is tremendous. The wind roars like a great beast. The rain drums on the roof. The windows rattle in their frames. And every few seconds, there is a deep boom, as a great wave hits the cliffs, and the whole island seems to shake.

--

{img:family-by-fire-storm-outside-candles-mother-knitting-children-listening}
In the lighthouse, James is on watch. The tower sways a little in the wind. Very little, but enough to feel. The lamp flickers in the draughts. The windows of the lantern are streaming with rain and spray, even though they are thirty metres above the sea. James wipes them, again and again, from inside. Every hour, he must go out onto the balcony, holding onto the rail with all his strength, to clear the salt from the outside of the glass. The wind almost blows him off his feet.

But the light keeps burning. All night, through the storm, it sweeps around, flashing every thirty seconds, out over the wild sea. Somewhere out there, in the darkness, ships are struggling through the storm. Their captains are watching for the light. And James is determined that it will not fail them. He trims the wicks. He winds the clockwork. He wipes the glass. He does not sleep. Not tonight.

{img:keeper-on-lantern-balcony-storm-night-holding-rail-wind-rain-beam}
In the house, Mairi cannot sleep either. She sits by the fire, knitting, and listening to the storm. Every half minute, she sees the beam of the lighthouse, passing over the shutters, through the cracks. As long as she sees it, she knows that James is all right, and the light is burning. Isla comes down, in her nightgown, and sits beside her mother. They do not talk. They just sit together, by the fire, watching the beam pass, again and again.

--

By the morning, the storm has passed. The wind drops. The rain stops. The clouds break up, and the sun comes out, pale and watery. The sea is still wild, with huge waves, but they are slowly calming down. The island is wet and battered. Some slates have blown off the roof. The garden wall has fallen down in one place. The beach is covered with seaweed and driftwood. But everyone is safe. And the light did not go out. James comes down from the tower, grey with tiredness, and Mairi gives him a hug, and a cup of hot tea, and a bowl of porridge. And then he sleeps, all day long.
