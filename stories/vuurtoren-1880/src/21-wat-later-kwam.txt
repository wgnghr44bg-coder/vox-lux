The life of the lighthouse keepers went on, in much the same way, for more than a hundred years after our story. The lamps changed. The lenses changed. The fog signals changed. But the work stayed the same. Keeping the light burning, every night, without fail. And watching the sea.

--

In 1900, there was a famous mystery, at a lighthouse on the Flannan Isles, a group of lonely islands far out in the Atlantic, west of Lewis. A ship passing by noticed that the light was not shining. When the relief boat arrived, a few days later, the three keepers had disappeared. The lighthouse was empty. Some of the stories say that the table was still laid, and that a clock had stopped. Nobody ever found out exactly what happened. Most people believe that they were swept away by a giant wave, during a storm. It became one of the most famous mysteries of the sea, and people still wonder about it today.

In the early twentieth century, the lamps changed. The old paraffin wick lamps were replaced by a new kind of lamp, which burned paraffin vapour, under pressure, through a little mantle, which glowed white-hot. It was much brighter. Later still, electricity came to the lighthouses. Electric lamps, powered by generators, or by cables from the mainland. The great glass lenses stayed. But the light inside them became brighter and brighter.

{img:electric-lamp-in-old-fresnel-lens-modern-bulb-brass-frame-later}
The fog bells were replaced by foghorns. Great horns, driven by compressed air, which made a deep, loud, moaning sound, that could be heard for many kilometres. People who lived near the coast grew used to the sound of the foghorns, on foggy nights. Some people loved it. Some people hated it. Today, most of the foghorns have been switched off, because ships now have radar and satellites to find their way.

Life for the keepers became a little easier. Radios came, so that they could talk to the mainland, and hear the news, and call for help in an emergency. Helicopters came, to bring the relief keepers and supplies to the rock lighthouses, instead of boats. Electricity brought light and heat to the houses. But the keepers still had to keep watch, every night, and clean the lens, and polish the brass.

--

{img:helicopter-landing-rock-lighthouse-helipad-relief-keepers-later}
Then, in the second half of the twentieth century, the lighthouses began to be automated. Machines were invented, which could switch the light on and off automatically, at sunset and sunrise. Which could change a bulb if it burned out. Which could start the foghorn when there was fog. Which could send a message to the mainland if anything went wrong. The keepers were no longer needed.

One by one, the lighthouses of Scotland were automated, and the keepers left. The last one was at Fair Isle, a small island between Orkney and Shetland. On the thirty-first of March, 1998, the last keepers left Fair Isle South lighthouse, and the light was left to shine by itself. After two hundred and twelve years, the age of the Scottish lighthouse keepers was over.

{img:last-keepers-leaving-lighthouse-1998-boat-jetty-farewell-island-later}
Many people were sad. The lighthouse keepers had been a part of Scottish life for so long. Their families, their stories, their courage and their patience. Some of the keepers had spent their whole lives in lighthouses, and their fathers before them, and their grandfathers before that. Now, the towers stood empty. The houses were sold, or left empty. The gardens became wild. Only the lights kept shining, alone, every night, as they always had.

--

But the lights still shine. All around the coast of Scotland, every night, more than two hundred lighthouses still flash their signals out over the sea. Each with its own pattern, its own colour, its own character. The ships still watch for them. And on the islands and the headlands, in the dark, the beams still sweep across the waves, faithfully, just as they did in 1880, when James MacLeod climbed the stairs at sunset, to light the lamp.
