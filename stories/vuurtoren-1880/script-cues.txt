Tonight we travel back almost a hundred and fifty years, to the year 1880. We go to the west coast of Scotland, to a small, rocky island, far out in the Atlantic Ocean. There are no trees on the island. Only grass, heather, rocks and sea birds. And on the highest point of the island, there stands a tall white tower, with a lantern of glass at the top. It is a lighthouse.

[pause]

He opens the door of the tower, and begins to climb the spiral staircase inside. Round and round, up and up, more than a hundred steps. Life on a lighthouse island was lonely, and sometimes hard. But it was also peaceful, simple and beautiful. And the keepers knew that their work was important. Every night, ships were passing in the darkness, along the dangerous rocky coast. And the light, flashing from the tower, told them where they were, and kept them safe.

So let us begin, on a calm summer evening in 1880, on a little island off the coast of Scotland. The sun has set. The first stars are coming out. And high up in the tower, the great light is turning, slowly, flashing out over the sea, as it will all night long, until the sun rises again.

{img:ancient-pharos-of-alexandria-lighthouse-fire-at-top-harbour-ships-night}
People have been building lighthouses for more than two thousand years. The most famous lighthouse of the ancient world was the Pharos of Alexandria, in Egypt. It was built about three hundred years before Christ, on an island at the entrance to the harbour. It was enormous, more than a hundred metres tall. And at the top, a great fire burned every night, to guide the ships into the harbour. It was one of the Seven Wonders of the World.

[long-pause]

The Romans built lighthouses too, all over their empire. At Dover, on the cliffs above the harbour, one of their lighthouses still stands today, almost two thousand years later. In the Middle Ages, monks sometimes kept lights burning in their chapels on the coast, to warn sailors. And in the sixteen and seventeen hundreds, more and more lighthouses were built, as more and more ships sailed the seas.

[pause]

The early lighthouses used fires of wood or coal, burning in an iron basket at the top of a tower. They used enormous amounts of fuel. And they were not very bright. Smoke and soot covered everything. In a strong wind, the fire could blow out, or burn too fast. Later, candles were used, dozens of them, in a lantern of glass. And then oil lamps, burning whale oil or vegetable oil, with reflectors behind them, to make the light brighter.

[pause]

{img:rocky-scottish-coast-storm-shipwreck-old-days-dangerous-reefs-waves}
The coast of Scotland is one of the most dangerous coasts in the world. It is long, rocky and broken, with thousands of islands, reefs and hidden rocks. The weather is often wild, with storms, gales and fog. The tides are strong. And in the eighteenth century, there were very few lights on the Scottish coast. Every year, many ships were wrecked, and many sailors were lost. Something had to be done.

[pause]

So in 1786, a group of people was formed, to build lighthouses around Scotland. It was called the Northern Lighthouse Board. They began with four lighthouses, at the most dangerous points of the coast. And then they built more and more. By 1880, there are dozens of lighthouses around Scotland, on headlands, on islands and even on rocks in the middle of the sea. Their lights shine all around the coast, every night.

[long-pause]

{img:ship-captain-on-deck-night-seeing-lighthouse-flash-relieved-crew}
Every lighthouse has its own character, so that sailors can tell them apart. Some lights are fixed, shining steadily. Some flash, once, twice or three times. Some flash white, some red. Some flash every ten seconds, some every thirty seconds. The pattern of each light is printed in a book, which every ship's captain carries. So when a captain sees a light at night, he counts the flashes, looks in his book, and knows exactly which lighthouse it is, and where his ship is.

[pause]

The light of our island flashes white, once every thirty seconds. It can be seen from about thirty kilometres away, on a clear night. It warns the ships of the dangerous rocks around the island, and guides them through the passage between the islands. Fishing boats, cargo ships, steamers carrying passengers, and big sailing ships, all pass by the island. And all of them watch for its light.

[pause]

{img:lighthouse-keepers-uniform-1880-cap-brass-buttons-standing-proud-tower}
Each lighthouse needs keepers, to look after the light, day and night. In Scotland, the keepers work for the Northern Lighthouse Board. They wear a uniform, of dark blue cloth, with brass buttons, and a cap. They are carefully chosen. They must be reliable, sober, hardworking and careful. They must be able to read and write, to keep the log book. And they must be willing to live in lonely places, far from towns and villages, sometimes for many years.

[long-pause]

The keepers are moved from one lighthouse to another, every few years. So they live in many different places, all around the coast of Scotland, during their lives. Some lighthouses are on the mainland, near a village. Others are on islands, with the keepers' families. And some are on rocks in the middle of the sea, where only the keepers can live, without their families. Our island is in between. Lonely, but big enough for families to live together, with the sea all around.

[long-pause]

{img:bell-rock-lighthouse-tower-rising-from-sea-waves-rock-reef-stormy-sky}
Many of the lighthouses of Scotland were built by one remarkable family. The Stevensons. For more than a hundred and fifty years, four generations of Stevensons were the engineers of the Northern Lighthouse Board. They designed and built almost all the lighthouses of Scotland. And some of them are among the greatest engineering works in the world.

[long-pause]

The first was Robert Stevenson. He was born in 1772, in Glasgow. As a young man, he became the assistant of the first engineer of the Northern Lighthouse Board, Thomas Smith, and later married his daughter. And in 1807, he began his greatest work. A lighthouse on the Bell Rock. A reef of rocks in the North Sea, off the east coast of Scotland, which is covered by the sea at high tide. Many ships had been wrecked on it. And nobody had ever built a lighthouse on a rock like this.

[pause]

The work was incredibly difficult. The builders could only work on the rock for a few hours a day, at low tide, when the rock was above the water. Then the tide came in, and they had to climb back into their boats, or onto a wooden platform on stilts, and wait for the water to go down again. They lived on a ship, anchored nearby, through storms and gales. The stones were cut on the mainland, and carried out to the rock by boat, and lifted into place, one by one.

[pause]

{img:workers-building-on-rock-low-tide-stone-blocks-boats-waiting-waves}
It took four years. But in 1811, the Bell Rock lighthouse was finished. A tall, elegant tower of granite and sandstone, rising thirty-five metres out of the sea. Its stones are cut so that they lock together, like the pieces of a puzzle, so that the waves cannot move them. It still stands today, more than two hundred years later, and is the oldest working sea-rock lighthouse in the world. People call it one of the seven wonders of the industrial world.

[pause]

Robert Stevenson's sons became lighthouse engineers too. His son Alan built a lighthouse called Skerryvore, on a lonely rock in the Atlantic, off the west coast of Scotland. It is very tall, almost fifty metres, and very beautiful, curving gently outward at the bottom, like the trunk of a great tree. His sons David and Thomas built dozens more lighthouses, all around the coast. They also improved the lamps and the lenses, making the lights brighter and brighter.

[long-pause]

{img:skerryvore-lighthouse-tall-graceful-tower-atlantic-rocks-waves-sunset}
And Thomas Stevenson had a son, called Robert Louis. He was supposed to become a lighthouse engineer too, like his father, his uncles and his grandfather. As a young man, he went on trips with his father, to visit the lighthouses, and to see the work. He visited lonely islands and rocks, and watched the waves breaking on the towers. But he did not want to be an engineer. He wanted to be a writer.

[pause]

His father was disappointed. But in the end, Robert Louis became a writer. In 1880, he is thirty years old. And in the next few years, he will write some of the most famous books in the world. Treasure Island, about pirates and buried gold. Kidnapped, about adventures in the Highlands of Scotland. And the strange story of Doctor Jekyll and Mr Hyde. Many of his stories are full of the sea, islands and rocky coasts, that he saw as a young man, visiting his family's lighthouses.

[pause]

{img:young-writer-robert-louis-stevenson-on-boat-visiting-lighthouse-notebook}
Robert Louis Stevenson was always proud of his family. He wrote that whenever he smelled salt water, he knew that he was not far from one of the works of his ancestors. He said that the lighthouses of his family shone around the coast of Scotland, every night, and that he was sometimes a little ashamed to be just a writer, when they had built such things.

[long-pause]

The keepers of the lighthouses knew the Stevensons well. The engineers came every year, to inspect the lighthouses, and see that everything was in order. They were strict, and expected everything to be perfect. But they were also respected, because everyone knew how much they had done, to make the coast of Scotland safe. And on our island, the lighthouse itself was built by David and Thomas Stevenson, about twenty-five years before our story. A tall white tower, standing on the cliff, looking out over the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:small-scottish-island-cliffs-grass-heather-lighthouse-white-houses-walled-garden}
The island is small. You can walk all the way around it in about two hours. It is made of grey rock, covered with short grass and heather, and patches of wild flowers in summer. There are cliffs on the west side, facing the open Atlantic, where the waves crash and the sea birds nest. And on the east side, there is a little bay, with a sandy beach and a stone landing jetty, where the boats come in.

[long-pause]

There are no trees on the island. The wind is too strong. Only a few small bushes grow, in sheltered places, bent over by the wind. But there is grass, enough for a cow and a few sheep. And in summer, the island is covered with flowers. Pink sea thrift, on the cliffs. Yellow primroses and buttercups. Purple heather. White daisies. It is very beautiful.

[pause]

The lighthouse stands on the highest point of the island, near the western cliffs. It is a round tower, of stone, painted white, about thirty metres tall. At the top, there is the lantern, a room of glass, with a little balcony around it, and a dome on top, with a weather vane. Next to the tower, there is a long, low building, also painted white, with the houses of the keepers. Two houses, side by side, one for each keeper and his family.

[pause]

{img:keepers-cottages-white-walls-slate-roof-chimneys-smoke-walled-yard}
Around the houses, there is a high stone wall, to protect them from the wind. Inside the wall, there is a yard, with a washing line, a coal shed, a store room for the oil, and a workshop. There is also a walled garden, where the families grow vegetables. Potatoes, cabbages, carrots, turnips and onions. The walls protect the plants from the salty wind. Without them, nothing would grow.

[pause]

There is also a little building for the fog signal. In 1880, it is a big bell, which is rung by a machine, driven by clockwork, when there is fog. In later years, it will be replaced by a great foghorn, driven by compressed air, which can be heard for many kilometres. And there are two little huts, one for the cow, and one for the hens. And a peat stack, a big pile of dried peat, which the families burn in their fires.

[long-pause]

{img:island-landing-jetty-stone-steps-small-bay-sandy-beach-boat-moored}
From the lighthouse to the landing jetty, there is a path, about a kilometre long, across the island. It is the only road. Everything that comes to the island must be carried along it. Food, coal, oil, letters, furniture and people. There is a little cart, pulled by hand, or by the pony that belongs to the station. In bad weather, the path is muddy and slippery. In good weather, it is a lovely walk, with larks singing overhead.

[pause]

There are no other people on the island. Only the two keepers and their families. Long ago, there was a small farming village on the island. But the people left, more than fifty years ago, to go to the mainland, or to America. The ruins of their houses are still there, on the east side, near the beach. Little stone walls, overgrown with grass and nettles. The keepers' children like to play there, and imagine the people who lived there long ago.

[pause]

{img:ruined-crofters-houses-stone-walls-grass-island-sea-view-evening}
From the top of the lighthouse, you can see far out to sea, in every direction. To the west, the open Atlantic, stretching away to the horizon, and beyond it, America. To the east, the mountains of the mainland, blue and purple in the distance, often with clouds on their tops. And to the north and south, other islands, some big, some small, scattered across the sea. On a clear day, it is one of the most beautiful views in the world.

[long-pause]

The island is a world of its own. The keepers and their families know every rock, every pool, every bird's nest and every patch of flowers on it. It is small. But it is their home. And to them, it is the most important island in the world. Because on it stands the light. And the light must never go out.

[long-pause]

{img:principal-lighthouse-keeper-1880-beard-uniform-pipe-doorway-cottage}
The principal keeper of the lighthouse is called James MacLeod. He is about forty-five years old. He has a grey beard, kind blue eyes, and a weathered face, brown from the wind and the sun. He was born on the island of Skye, the son of a fisherman. He went to sea as a young man, as a sailor. And then, at twenty-five, he joined the Northern Lighthouse Board. He has been a keeper for twenty years, at five different lighthouses.

[long-pause]

James is a quiet, careful man. He speaks both English and Gaelic, the old Celtic language of the Highlands and Islands. He reads a lot, especially history and books about the sea. He plays the fiddle, and sings old Gaelic songs. And he takes his work very seriously. In twenty years, he has never once let the light go out. He is proud of that. The light is his responsibility, and he looks after it as if it were his own child.

[pause]

His wife is called Mairi. She is also from Skye. She is a strong, cheerful woman, with dark hair and rosy cheeks, who is never still. She runs the house, cooks, bakes, sews, knits, looks after the cow and the hens, works in the garden, and teaches the children. She also helps with the light, when it is needed. Keepers' wives often did. Mairi knows how to trim the wicks and wind the clockwork, as well as her husband.

[pause]

{img:keepers-wife-knitting-by-window-sea-view-cottage-kitchen-warm-light}
They have two children. Isla, their daughter, is twelve. She is tall and thin, with her mother's dark hair and her father's blue eyes. She is clever and curious, and she loves the sea, the birds and the stars. She wants to know everything. She reads every book she can find. She can name every bird on the island, and every star in the sky. And she helps her father in the lighthouse, polishing the brass and cleaning the windows.

[pause]

Their son, Calum, is eight. He is small and freckled, with sandy hair that never lies flat. He is full of energy, always running, climbing and exploring. He loves the rock pools on the shore, full of crabs, starfish and little fish. He loves the seals, which come to lie on the rocks near the beach. And he loves the boats. He dreams of being a sailor, like his father was, and sailing to faraway places.

[long-pause]

{img:two-children-rock-pools-island-shore-crabs-starfish-bucket-summer}
In the other house, next door, lives the assistant keeper, with his wife. His name is Donald Morrison. He is about thirty, a big, cheerful man from the island of Lewis, who laughs a lot and tells funny stories. His wife is called Effie. She is young and shy, and she and Mairi have become good friends. They have a baby, a little boy called Angus, who is just learning to walk. Isla loves to look after him.

[pause]

The two families live very close together, on their little island. They share everything. The work, the food, the cow's milk, the books, the news. They help each other. They eat together on special days. And they know each other very well. Sometimes, of course, they get on each other's nerves. On a small island, with nobody else to talk to, this is natural. But mostly, they are good friends, and good neighbours.

[pause]

{img:two-families-eating-together-kitchen-table-lamp-evening-laughter}
The keepers have a strict timetable. One of them is always on duty, day and night. During the night, they take turns to keep watch, in the lantern room, in shifts of four hours. During the day, they clean and maintain the light, and do the other work of the station. They keep a log book, where they write down everything that happens. The weather, the ships that pass, any problems with the light. And once a month, they send a report to the Board, in Edinburgh.

[long-pause]

James and Mairi have lived on this island for three years. Before that, they were at a lighthouse on the mainland, near a village, with a school and a church and a shop. Here, it is much lonelier. The nearest village is on the mainland, about fifteen kilometres away, across the sea. They only see other people when the supply boat comes. But they have come to love the island. The quiet. The sea. The birds. The light. And the children say they never want to leave.

[long-pause]

{img:fresnel-lens-giant-glass-prisms-brass-frame-lamp-inside-lantern-room}
The heart of the lighthouse is the light. And in 1880, it is a wonderful piece of engineering. At the top of the tower, in the glass lantern room, there is a great lens, made of hundreds of pieces of glass, set in a frame of brass. It is called a Fresnel lens, after a French scientist, Augustin Fresnel, who invented it, about sixty years before. And it is the reason why the light can be seen so far away.

[long-pause]

The lens is shaped like a great barrel, or a beehive, taller than a man, made of rings and panels of glass. Each piece of glass is cut very carefully, with a special shape, so that it bends the light from the lamp in the middle, and sends it out in one direction, in a strong, narrow beam. Without the lens, the lamp would light up only the room. With it, its light can be seen for thirty kilometres across the sea.

[pause]

The lens is very beautiful. When the sun shines through it, during the day, it breaks the light into rainbows, which dance on the walls and the floor of the lantern room. Isla loves to sit there in the afternoons, watching the rainbows move slowly across the room, as the sun moves. But the sun can also be dangerous. The lens can focus the sun's rays, like a magnifying glass, and start a fire. So during the day, the keepers draw linen curtains around the lantern windows, to protect it.

[pause]

{img:rainbows-dancing-lantern-room-walls-sunlight-through-lens-girl-watching}
In the middle of the lens, there is the lamp. In 1880, it burns paraffin oil, which is a new fuel, made from petroleum. Before, lighthouses burned whale oil, or oil made from rapeseed, called colza. Paraffin burns more brightly, and is cheaper. The lamp has several wicks, one inside the other, in circles, to make a big, bright flame. The oil is pumped up to the wicks from a tank below, by a small pump, driven by a weight, like a clock.

[pause]

The lens turns around the lamp, so that the beam sweeps across the sea, and flashes as it passes. It is turned by clockwork. A heavy weight hangs on a chain, down the middle of the tower. As the weight slowly falls, it turns a set of gears, which turn the lens. Every hour or so, the keeper must wind the weight back up to the top, with a handle. If he forgets, the lens stops turning, and the light becomes a fixed light, instead of a flashing one. Then the ships might think it is a different lighthouse.

[long-pause]

{img:keeper-winding-clockwork-handle-weight-chain-gears-lantern-room-night}
Every evening, at sunset, exactly, the keeper lights the lamp. The time of sunset is printed in a table, for every day of the year. The keeper must light the lamp at that exact time, not a minute late. He climbs the stairs, draws back the curtains, lights the wicks, waits for them to burn evenly, adjusts the flame, and starts the clockwork. Then he writes the time in the log book. And the light shines out over the sea.

[pause]

Lighting the lamp is like a little ceremony, every evening. James does it with great care. Sometimes, Isla goes up with him, and watches. She loves the moment when the flame catches, and the lens suddenly fills with light, and the first beam sweeps out across the darkening sea. Her father says that every evening, when he lights the lamp, he says a little prayer, for all the sailors at sea.

[pause]

{img:father-and-daughter-lantern-room-first-beam-sweeping-over-sea-dusk}
Every morning, at sunrise, exactly, the keeper puts out the lamp. He stops the clockwork. He draws the curtains around the windows. He writes the time in the log book. And the light rests, until the evening. In winter, when the nights are long, the light burns for sixteen hours. In summer, in the north of Scotland, when the nights are very short, it burns for only five or six.

[long-pause]

The light must never go out. That is the first rule of every lighthouse. If the lamp goes out, even for a few minutes, a ship could be wrecked on the rocks. So the keepers watch it, all night long, every night, for their whole working lives. In stormy weather and calm, in winter and summer, at Christmas and New Year. Every night, without exception. James has kept that watch for twenty years. And he will keep it for twenty more.

[long-pause]

{img:keeper-night-watch-small-room-below-lantern-desk-log-book-lamp-glow}
All night long, one of the keepers is on watch. The night is divided into watches, of about four hours each. The two keepers take turns. When one keeper is on watch, the other sleeps. Then they change. So the light is never left alone, not even for a minute, from sunset to sunrise.

[long-pause]

The keeper on watch sits in a small room, called the watch room, just below the lantern. It has a little window, a desk, a chair, a stove for warmth, and a clock. From here, he can hear the clockwork ticking, and see the glow of the light above. Every hour, he climbs up into the lantern, to check the lamp, trim the wicks if needed, and wind the clockwork. And then he comes back down to the watch room, and waits.

[pause]

The wicks of the lamp must be trimmed during the night. As they burn, they get charred at the top, and the flame becomes smoky and dim. So the keeper must cut off the charred part, very carefully, with special scissors, to keep the flame bright and clean. That is why lighthouse keepers were sometimes called wickies. It is a delicate job, especially with cold fingers in the middle of a winter night.

[pause]

{img:keeper-trimming-wicks-lamp-small-scissors-careful-lantern-night}
The keeper must stay awake all through his watch. It is a strict rule. A keeper who is found asleep on watch can be dismissed. So he must find ways to stay awake. Some keepers read, by the light of a small lamp. Some write letters. Some carve wood. Some make little models of ships. Some knit, or mend clothes, or make rugs. James reads. He has read hundreds of books, on his night watches, in twenty years.

[pause]

But he also looks out of the window. The keeper must watch the sea, and the sky, and the weather. He must notice if a ship is in trouble, or if fog is coming. On clear nights, the sky is full of stars, more than anyone in a town has ever seen. Sometimes, in the north, the sky is lit up with the northern lights, great curtains of green and pink light, moving slowly across the sky. James never gets tired of watching them.

[long-pause]

{img:northern-lights-green-curtains-over-lighthouse-sea-keeper-balcony}
On calm nights, the lighthouse is very peaceful. The only sounds are the ticking of the clockwork, the soft hiss of the lamp, and the waves breaking gently on the rocks below. Sometimes, a sea bird calls. Sometimes, far away, a ship passes, with its little lights, red and green, moving slowly along the horizon. The keeper watches it, and knows that the captain is watching his light. And he feels that he is not alone.

[pause]

At midnight, James's watch ends, and Donald comes up the stairs, yawning, with a mug of tea. They talk for a few minutes. James tells him about the weather, the ships he has seen, and anything else that has happened. He writes in the log book. Then he goes down the long spiral stairs, and across the yard, under the stars, to his house. And Donald takes over, until four in the morning.

[pause]

{img:keeper-walking-across-yard-night-stars-to-cottage-lantern-in-hand}
In the house, everything is dark and quiet. Mairi and the children are asleep. James takes off his boots, quietly, and climbs into bed, beside his wife. Through the window, he can see the beam of the lighthouse, sweeping across the sea, every thirty seconds. It lights up the bedroom for a moment, very faintly, and then is gone. And then again. Like a slow heartbeat. James falls asleep, counting the flashes, as he always does.

[long-pause]

Isla sometimes wakes up in the night, and sees the beam, passing across her window. It is a comforting sight. She knows that her father, or Donald, is up there, keeping watch. She knows that the ships are safe. She knows that, as long as the light is turning, everything is all right. And she turns over, and pulls the blanket up to her chin, and goes back to sleep, with the beam passing softly over her, again and again.

[long-pause]

{img:keeper-polishing-brass-lantern-room-morning-sunlight-cloth-shining}
At sunrise, the keeper on watch puts out the light. He stops the clockwork, draws the curtains, and writes the time in the log book. And then the morning work begins. Because the light must be perfect every night. And to be perfect, it must be cleaned and cared for every day.

[long-pause]

First, the lamp. The keeper takes it apart, very carefully. He cleans the burners, removes the burnt wicks and fits new ones, and refills the oil. Then the lens. Every single piece of glass must be cleaned, inside and out, with a soft cloth and spirits of wine, until it is perfectly clear. Even a little bit of dust, or a fingerprint, or a trace of soot, would make the light dimmer. It takes hours. The keepers wear linen aprons, so that their uniforms do not scratch the glass.

[pause]

Then the windows of the lantern. They must be cleaned too, inside and out. Outside, on the balcony, the keeper climbs a little ladder, and wipes away the salt, which the wind blows up from the sea, and the bird droppings, and the dead insects. In winter, sometimes, there is snow or ice on the glass, which must be cleared away. On windy days, this is not easy, high up on the balcony, with the wind trying to blow you off.

[pause]

{img:keeper-cleaning-lantern-glass-outside-balcony-ladder-wind-sea-below}
And then the brass. The lighthouse is full of brass. The frame of the lens. The lamp. The clockwork. The handrails. The door handles. The instruments. Everything made of brass must be polished, every day, until it shines like gold. The Northern Lighthouse Board is very strict about this. When the inspectors come, they check that everything shines. A lighthouse with dull brass is a badly kept lighthouse.

[pause]

Isla often helps with the brass. Her father gives her a soft cloth and a tin of polish. She rubs and rubs, the handrails and the door handles, until she can see her face in them. It is hard work, but she likes it. She likes being in the lighthouse, high above the island, with the sea all around. And she likes it when her father looks at her work, and nods, and says that it shines like the sun.

[long-pause]

{img:girl-polishing-brass-handrail-spiral-staircase-lighthouse-reflection-face}
The keepers also look after the whole station. They paint the tower and the houses, every few years, inside and out. They repair the roofs and the walls, after storms. They clean the gutters. They look after the fog signal. They keep the store rooms tidy, with the oil, the coal, the wicks and the spare parts all in their places. They mend the path to the jetty. Everything must be clean, tidy and in good order, all the time.

[pause]

There is a list of duties for every day of the week. On Mondays, the keepers do this. On Tuesdays, that. And so on. The Board sends instructions about everything. How to clean the lens. How to trim the wicks. How to paint the tower. How to keep the log book. Even how to wear the uniform. It is a very orderly life. Some people might find it boring. But the keepers know that every little task helps to keep the light shining.

[pause]

{img:keeper-painting-tower-white-ladder-rope-sunny-day-sea-gulls}
By about midday, the morning work is usually finished. Then the keeper on day duty has his dinner, and the other keeper, who was on watch in the early morning, can rest. In the afternoon, there is time for other work. In the garden. With the animals. Fishing from the rocks. Collecting driftwood on the beach. Or simply sitting outside the house, in the sun, smoking a pipe, and looking at the sea.

[long-pause]

James likes to walk around the island in the afternoon, when the weather is good. He walks along the cliffs, watching the birds. He checks the rocks and the shore, for anything washed up by the sea. He looks at the sky, and the clouds, and the colour of the sea, to guess what the weather will do. Calum often comes with him, running ahead, climbing on the rocks, and asking a hundred questions. And James answers all of them, patiently, as they walk together in the wind.

[long-pause]

{img:keeper-reading-barometer-thermometer-weather-instruments-wall-log-book}
For a lighthouse keeper, the weather is very important. It decides everything. Whether the supply boat can come. Whether the fog signal must be sounded. Whether the windows of the lantern will be covered with salt or snow. Whether there will be ships in trouble. So the keepers watch the weather constantly, and write it down, several times a day, in the log book.

[long-pause]

The lighthouse has weather instruments. A barometer, which measures the pressure of the air. When the pressure falls, bad weather is coming. When it rises, good weather is coming. A thermometer, which measures the temperature. A rain gauge, a little copper pot in the garden, which collects the rain, so that the keepers can measure how much has fallen. And a wind vane on top of the lantern, which shows the direction of the wind.

[pause]

Three times a day, the keepers read the instruments, and write the results in a special book. The temperature, the pressure, the wind, the rain, the clouds and the state of the sea. Once a month, they send these records to Edinburgh, where scientists study them. The lighthouses of Scotland are like a great chain of weather stations, all around the coast. Their records help the scientists to understand the weather, and to start making forecasts.

[pause]

{img:dark-storm-clouds-approaching-island-sea-whitecaps-lighthouse-waiting}
The keepers also learn to read the weather from nature, like fishermen and farmers. James can look at the sky in the morning, and tell you what the weather will be in the afternoon. A red sky at night means fine weather. A red sky in the morning means rain. When the sea birds fly inland, a storm is coming. When the seals come up on the rocks, the weather will be calm. When the distant islands look very close and clear, rain is on its way.

[pause]

The weather on the island changes very quickly. In the morning, it can be sunny and calm, with the sea like glass. By the afternoon, the wind can be howling, and the waves crashing against the cliffs. Then, by the evening, it can be calm again, with a beautiful sunset. Islanders say that if you do not like the weather in Scotland, you only have to wait ten minutes. There are often several kinds of weather in a single day.

[long-pause]

{img:rainbow-over-sea-island-after-rain-lighthouse-sunlight-breaking-clouds}
And after the rain, there are often rainbows. Great arches of colour, over the sea, between the islands. Sometimes two rainbows at once, one above the other. Calum once tried to run to the end of a rainbow, to find the pot of gold that his grandmother had told him about. He ran all the way across the island, to the beach. But the rainbow kept moving away. When he came back, out of breath, his mother laughed, and said that the gold was not at the end of the rainbow. It was the rainbow itself.

[pause]

In the log book, James also writes down the ships that pass the island. Their type, their direction, and sometimes their name, if they are close enough to read it through his telescope. Fishing boats from the nearby villages. Cargo steamers, with black smoke pouring from their funnels. Big sailing ships, with all their white sails spread. Little puffers, the small steam boats that carry coal and goods between the islands. And sometimes, a smart yacht, belonging to some rich gentleman on holiday.

[pause]

{img:keeper-telescope-balcony-watching-sailing-ship-passing-island-sunny}
Isla loves to look through the telescope. Her father lets her use it, if she is careful. She watches the ships passing, far out at sea, and tries to guess where they are going. To Glasgow. To Liverpool. To Norway. To America. She writes their names in a little notebook of her own. She has more than two hundred names in it now. And she dreams that one day, she will sail on one of them, to some faraway place.

[long-pause]

At the end of each day, James writes a short summary in the log book. The weather. The ships. The work done. Any problems. It is usually very short. Wind westerly, moderate. Fine and clear. Light lit at sunset, extinguished at sunrise. All well. All well. Those are the words that every keeper hopes to write, every day. And on most days, on the little island, that is exactly what happens.

[long-pause]

{img:keepers-wife-milking-cow-island-pasture-morning-sea-background-bucket}
Living on an island, far from shops and markets, the families must look after themselves. Food, water, fuel, clothes. Everything must be grown, made, collected, or brought by the supply boat, every few weeks. And it is Mairi who manages all of it, with great skill and energy.

[long-pause]

The day begins early, for Mairi. She is up at six, to light the fire in the kitchen range, and put the kettle on. Then she goes out to milk the cow, a gentle brown Highland cow called Morag, with long shaggy hair and curved horns. She feeds the hens, and collects the eggs. Then she comes back and makes breakfast. Porridge, of course, made with oatmeal, salt and water, and eaten with fresh milk. Bread and butter. Eggs. Tea.

[pause]

Water comes from a tank, which collects the rain from the roofs of the houses. On an island in the Atlantic, there is plenty of rain. But in dry summers, the water must be used carefully. Mairi uses it for drinking and cooking. For washing clothes, she uses water from a little stream that runs down the hill, behind the houses. Washing day is on Monday. And on a windy island, the clothes dry very quickly on the line.

[pause]

{img:walled-garden-island-potatoes-cabbages-woman-and-boy-digging-wind}
The garden, inside its stone wall, gives the families vegetables. Potatoes, which are the most important. Cabbages, kale, carrots, turnips, onions and peas. There are also a few rows of strawberries, and a gooseberry bush, which Calum guards jealously from the birds. Mairi and the children work in the garden every day, in the summer, digging, weeding and watering. In the autumn, the potatoes are dug up and stored in a dark shed, for the winter.

[pause]

The sea gives food too. James and Donald fish from the rocks, and from a small rowing boat. They catch mackerel, cod, haddock, pollack and lobsters, in creels, which are wicker baskets that they put out on the sea floor. Mairi smokes and salts some of the fish, to keep it for the winter. The children collect mussels and winkles from the rocks at low tide. And in early summer, they collect the eggs of some of the sea birds, which taste very good, as islanders have done for centuries.

[long-pause]

{img:peat-cutting-moorland-family-stacking-peat-bricks-summer-sky}
For fuel, the families burn coal, which comes on the supply boat, and peat. Peat is a kind of earth, made of plants that have rotted slowly, for thousands of years, in wet ground. On the island, there is a peat bog, on the north side. In late spring, the families cut the peat, into bricks, with special spades. They stack the bricks to dry in the sun and the wind, all summer. And in the autumn, they carry them home, and build a great peat stack. Peat burns slowly, with a lovely smell, warm and smoky.

[pause]

Mairi cooks on the kitchen range, an iron stove with an oven, heated by coal and peat. She bakes bread, scones and oatcakes. She makes soups and stews, of mutton, vegetables and barley. She makes fish pies, and potato cakes, and porridge every morning. On Sundays, there is a roast, of mutton or chicken, and a pudding. And for special days, a clootie dumpling, a rich pudding of flour, fruit, spices and suet, boiled for hours in a cloth.

[pause]

{img:scottish-cottage-kitchen-iron-range-scones-baking-woman-children-table}
Everything else comes on the supply boat. Flour, oatmeal, sugar, tea, salt, coffee, soap, candles, paraffin for the house lamps, clothes, boots, and anything else that is needed. Mairi writes a list, every month, and sends it with the boat to a shop on the mainland. The goods arrive on the next boat. If she forgets something, she must wait another month. So she is very careful with her lists. And she always keeps a good store of everything, in case the boat cannot come, because of bad weather.

[long-pause]

It is a busy life. Mairi works from morning until night, every day. But she likes it. She likes the freedom, the fresh air, the sea and the peace. She likes being her own master, in her own home, with nobody to tell her what to do, except the weather. And in the evenings, when the work is done, she sits by the fire, with her knitting, and her family around her, and listens to the wind outside, and the rain on the windows, and feels content.

[long-pause]

{img:mother-teaching-children-kitchen-table-slate-chalk-books-window-sea}
On the island, there is no school. The nearest school is on the mainland, fifteen kilometres away, across the sea. So the children of the lighthouse keepers must be taught at home. And in 1880, this is a very important matter. Because a few years before, in 1872, a new law was passed in Scotland, which says that every child between five and thirteen must go to school.

[long-pause]

For children on lonely islands and in remote lighthouses, this is not easy. The Northern Lighthouse Board helps. Sometimes, it pays for a teacher to come and live at a lighthouse, for a few months, to teach the children. Sometimes, the children are sent to the mainland, to live with relatives, and go to school there. And sometimes, the mothers and fathers teach their children themselves, at home.

[pause]

On our island, Mairi teaches the children. Every morning, after breakfast, they sit at the kitchen table, with their books, slates and pencils. Mairi went to school on Skye until she was fourteen, and she was a good pupil. She teaches them reading, writing and arithmetic. History and geography. A little Bible study. And she teaches them songs, in English and Gaelic.

[pause]

{img:children-learning-geography-globe-map-lighthouse-kitchen-pointing}
Isla is a very good pupil. She reads everything she can find. She knows the names of all the countries of the world, and their capitals. She can do long sums in her head. She writes beautiful compositions about the sea and the birds. Mairi says that she has taught Isla everything she knows, and that the girl needs a real teacher now. Next year, perhaps, Isla will go to the mainland, to a school in the town, to continue her education.

[pause]

Calum is not such a good pupil. He finds it very hard to sit still. His eyes keep wandering to the window, where the sea is sparkling and the birds are flying. He would much rather be on the rocks, looking for crabs. His spelling is terrible. But he is very good at drawing, especially boats and birds. And he knows the name of every part of a ship, which is more than his mother does.

[long-pause]

{img:boy-drawing-ships-on-slate-distracted-looking-out-window-sea-birds}
James also teaches the children, in the afternoons and the evenings. He teaches them about the lighthouse, the lamp and the lens. About the stars, and how to find your way by them at sea. About knots, and boats, and the tides. About the history of Scotland. And he teaches them Gaelic, the language of his own childhood, so that they do not forget it. He tells them the old Gaelic stories, of heroes and giants and the fairy folk of the hills.

[pause]

Once a year, an inspector comes from the mainland, to check that the children are being properly taught. He is a serious man, with a black coat and spectacles. He asks Isla and Calum questions, and looks at their copy books, and listens to them read. Isla answers everything perfectly. Calum gets most of his spelling wrong. But when the inspector sees his drawings of ships, he smiles, and says that the boy has talent.

[pause]

{img:school-inspector-black-coat-spectacles-examining-children-copy-books-kitchen}
The children also learn many things that children in town schools do not learn. They know how the weather works, and how to read the sky. They know the names of all the birds, the fish and the plants of the island. They know how to milk a cow, catch a fish, plant potatoes and cut peat. They know how a lighthouse works. And they know the sea, in all its moods. Their father says that the island is their real school. And the sea is their real teacher.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, after supper, the whole family reads together, by the light of the paraffin lamp. James reads the newspaper, which is weeks old by the time it arrives. Mairi reads a novel. Isla reads a book about faraway countries. And Calum looks at a book of pictures of ships, and asks his father a hundred questions about them. And outside, the light of the lighthouse sweeps across the windows, every thirty seconds, steady and calm.

[long-pause]

{img:supply-boat-sailing-towards-island-lighthouse-calm-morning-family-waiting-jetty}
Every few weeks, a boat comes to the island. It is the most exciting day of the month. The relief boat, as it is called, brings everything the families need from the mainland. Food and supplies. Oil for the light. Coal. Letters and newspapers. And sometimes, visitors. And it takes away the letters, the reports, the log books, and anything that needs to go to the mainland.

[long-pause]

The boat is a sturdy sailing boat, with two masts, rented by the Board from a boatman on the mainland. It takes about three hours to sail to the island, if the wind is good. But it can only come when the weather is calm. If there is a storm, or a heavy swell, it cannot land safely at the jetty. And so, sometimes, the families must wait for weeks, watching the sea, hoping for a calm day.

[pause]

On the morning when the boat is expected, everyone is up early, watching the sea. The children run up to the top of the lighthouse, with the telescope, to be the first to see it. And when Calum spots the white sails, far away on the horizon, he shouts so loudly that everyone on the island hears him. Boat! Boat! And everyone runs down to the jetty, to wait.

[pause]

{img:boatmen-unloading-barrels-sacks-crates-stone-jetty-island-busy}
When the boat arrives, there is a great bustle. The boatmen throw ropes to the keepers, who tie the boat to the jetty. And then the unloading begins. Barrels of paraffin oil for the light. Sacks of coal. Sacks of flour and oatmeal. Boxes of tea, sugar and soap. Crates of tinned food. Parcels and packages. All of it must be carried up the path to the lighthouse, on the little cart, or on people's backs. It takes hours.

[pause]

And then, the most important thing. The mail bag. James opens it, at the kitchen table, and hands out the letters. Letters from Mairi's sister on Skye, with all the news of the family. Letters from Donald's parents on Lewis. Official letters from the Board, in Edinburgh. Newspapers, a few weeks old. Magazines. And sometimes, a parcel, with a present inside. Everyone reads their letters at once, and shares the news.

[long-pause]

{img:family-reading-letters-kitchen-table-mail-bag-excitement-newspapers}
The boatmen stay for a cup of tea, and a bowl of soup, before they sail back to the mainland. They bring news and gossip from the villages. Who has married. Who has had a baby. Who has passed away. What the price of fish is. What is happening in Glasgow, and in London, and in the wider world. The families listen eagerly. It is their only contact with the outside world, apart from the letters.

[pause]

On this visit, the boat brings something special for Isla. A parcel, from her aunt on Skye. Inside, there is a new book. A book about birds, with beautiful coloured pictures of every bird in Britain. Isla holds it as if it were made of gold. She turns the pages slowly, looking at the pictures. Many of the birds, she has seen on the island. And she reads their names, in English and in Latin, under her breath.

[pause]

{img:girl-holding-new-bird-book-coloured-plates-joy-cottage-window}
For Calum, the boat brings a model ship, carved from wood, with sails of white cloth, sent by his uncle, who is a sailor. Calum is speechless. He carries it everywhere for weeks. He sails it in the rock pools, and in the stream, and in the washtub, until his mother tells him to stop. Then he puts it on the windowsill of his room, where he can see it from his bed, with the real sea behind it.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, the boat sails away again, back to the mainland, with the letters and the log books. The families stand on the jetty and wave, until it is just a white speck on the horizon. Then they walk back up the path, to the lighthouse, carrying the last parcels. The island is quiet again. Just the two families, the birds, the seals, and the sea. Until the next boat comes.

[long-pause]

{img:puffins-on-cliff-top-grass-burrows-sea-thrift-pink-flowers-summer}
In spring and summer, the island is full of birds. Thousands and thousands of them. They come from the open sea, to nest on the cliffs and in the grass of the island, where they are safe from foxes and other animals that live on the mainland. The air is full of their cries, and the cliffs are white with their wings. It is one of the most wonderful sights of the year.

[long-pause]

The most popular birds on the island are the puffins. Small, round, black and white birds, with bright orange feet, and big colourful beaks, striped red, yellow and grey. They look like little clowns. They nest in burrows, in the soft earth at the top of the cliffs, like rabbits. They stand at the entrances of their burrows, looking around, very seriously. And they fly out to sea, and come back with their beaks full of little silver fish, for their chicks.

[pause]

Calum loves the puffins. He lies on his stomach at the top of the cliff, very still, and watches them for hours. They are not afraid of people. They come quite close, and look at him with their funny, sad-looking eyes. He has given names to some of them. The Captain, who has a very big beak. The Fat Lady, who is very round. And Grumpy, who always seems to be in a bad mood. Isla says that all puffins look the same. Calum says that she is completely wrong.

[pause]

{img:gannets-diving-into-sea-white-wings-folded-splash-fishing-sunlight}
On the cliffs, there are other birds. Guillemots and razorbills, black and white, standing in long rows on the narrow ledges, like little soldiers. Kittiwakes, small gulls, who build their nests of seaweed on tiny ledges, and call out their own name, kitti-wake, kitti-wake. Fulmars, who glide along the cliffs on stiff wings, without ever flapping. And shags, black and green, with crests on their heads, nesting on the rocks near the water.

[pause]

Out at sea, there are the gannets. Big white birds, with black wing tips and golden heads. They fly high over the sea, looking for fish. And when they see one, they fold their wings, and dive straight down into the water, like arrows, from thirty metres high, with a great splash. Isla loves to watch them diving. She says that they are the most beautiful birds in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:grey-seals-lying-on-rocks-island-shore-pup-white-fur-autumn}
There are also seals. Grey seals, big and heavy, with long faces, who lie on the rocks near the beach, basking in the sun. And in the autumn, the mothers come to the beach to have their pups. The seal pups are covered in soft white fur, with big dark eyes. They lie on the beach, crying for their mothers, who come in from the sea to feed them. The children watch them from a distance, so as not to disturb them.

[pause]

The seals are curious. When the children walk along the shore, the seals in the water swim along beside them, with their heads up, watching them, with their big dark eyes. Sometimes they sing. A strange, sad, beautiful sound, like a moaning or a wailing, which drifts across the water on calm evenings. The old islanders say that seals are people of the sea, called selkies, who can take off their skins and become human, on the land.

[pause]

{img:whale-surfacing-near-island-spout-calm-sea-family-watching-from-cliff}
And sometimes, there are whales. Minke whales, and sometimes bigger ones, passing by the island, far out at sea. You can see their spouts, a puff of white mist, rising from the water, and their dark backs, curving through the waves. Once, a great pod of dolphins swims past the island, leaping and playing in the waves, dozens of them. The whole family runs to the cliffs to watch them, cheering, until they disappear.

[long-pause]

In the autumn, the sea birds leave. They fly away, out to sea, where they will spend the winter, far from land. The cliffs become quiet and empty. The puffins disappear, all at once, one day in August. Calum is sad. He stands at the top of the cliff, looking at the empty burrows. But his father tells him that the puffins will come back, in the spring, to the very same burrows. And they always do. Every spring, Calum is waiting for them. And every spring, the Captain, with his big beak, is the first to come home.

[long-pause]

{img:thick-fog-rolling-over-island-lighthouse-disappearing-white-grey-silence}
One summer evening, the fog comes. It comes in from the sea, very quickly, like a great grey wall, rolling over the waves. One moment, the sun is shining, and the sea is blue, and you can see the islands on the horizon. The next moment, everything is grey and white and silent. You cannot see the sea. You cannot see the beach. You can hardly see the houses, a few metres away.

[long-pause]

Fog is one of the most dangerous things for ships. In fog, the light of the lighthouse cannot be seen. Not from far away. The sailors cannot see the coast, or the rocks, or each other. They cannot see where they are going. And many ships are wrecked in fog, on rocks that the captain did not know were there. So when the fog comes, the lighthouse must use another way to warn the ships. Sound.

[pause]

In 1880, the island has a fog bell. It is a big bronze bell, hanging in a little tower, next to the lighthouse. It is rung by a machine, driven by clockwork, like the lens. A heavy hammer strikes the bell, every twenty seconds, day and night, as long as the fog lasts. The sound is deep and loud, and travels far over the water. Ships that hear it know that they are near the island, and must be careful.

[pause]

{img:fog-bell-tower-bronze-bell-clockwork-hammer-keeper-winding-mist}
James starts the fog bell as soon as the fog comes in. He winds the clockwork, and the hammer begins to strike. Dong. And twenty seconds later, dong. And again. The sound echoes across the island, muffled by the fog. The families inside the houses can hear it clearly, through the walls. It is a lonely, slightly sad sound. But it is also a comforting one. It means that the keepers are doing their job.

[pause]

Fog can last for hours, or even for days. And all that time, the bell must keep ringing. The keepers must wind the clockwork every few hours, and check that the hammer is working. At night, they must also keep the light burning, even though it can hardly be seen in the fog. Because perhaps, somewhere, the fog is thinner, and a ship can see it. It is tiring work. The keepers hardly sleep, when there is fog.

[long-pause]

{img:ship-lights-faint-in-fog-distance-sea-bell-sound-careful-sailors}
On this evening, the fog lasts all night. James and Donald take turns at the fog bell and the light. Mairi brings them hot tea and bread. The children lie in their beds, listening to the bell, and the soft sound of the sea, which they cannot see. Calum cannot sleep. He asks his mother if there are ships out there, in the fog. She says that there probably are. But that they can hear the bell, and they will be careful.

[pause]

In the middle of the night, James hears something, through the fog. A ship's horn. Far away, then closer. A steamer, out in the fog, sounding its horn, to warn other ships. It is coming closer to the island. James rings the bell harder. And then, faintly, through the grey mist, he sees the lights of the ship, red and green, very close. Too close. He holds his breath.

[pause]

{img:lighthouse-beam-blurred-in-fog-keeper-on-balcony-watching-ship-turn-away}
And then, slowly, the ship's lights begin to turn. Away from the island. Out to sea. The captain has heard the bell. He has understood. He has changed his course. The ship's horn sounds again, three times, as if to say thank you. And then it fades away into the fog, and the lights disappear. James lets out his breath. He finds that his hands are shaking. He sits down on the step of the fog tower, and says a quiet prayer of thanks.

[long-pause]

In the morning, the fog lifts, as suddenly as it came. The sun comes out. The sea is blue again, and calm. The islands appear on the horizon, as if nothing had happened. James stops the fog bell, and writes in the log book. Fog from eight in the evening until six in the morning. Fog bell sounded. Steamer passed close to the east at two in the morning, altered course. All well. And then he goes to bed, and sleeps until noon.

[long-pause]

{img:atlantic-storm-huge-waves-crashing-cliffs-lighthouse-spray-grey-sky}
In the winter, the storms come. The great Atlantic storms, which blow in from the west, across thousands of kilometres of open ocean. They are some of the strongest storms in the world. And the little island, with its lighthouse, stands right in their path.

[long-pause]

The first sign is the barometer. One morning in December, James sees that it is falling, fast. Lower and lower. He taps it with his finger. It falls further. He looks out of the window. The sky in the west is a strange, dark yellow-grey colour. The sea is calm, but there is a long, slow swell, rolling in from the west. And the sea birds have all disappeared. James knows what is coming.

[pause]

He tells everyone to get ready. They bring in the washing. They tie down everything that might blow away. They close the shutters on the windows. They bring the cow and the hens into their sheds, and give them extra food. They fill the water buckets, and bring in extra peat and coal for the fires. They check the store of food and oil. And then they wait.

[pause]

{img:keepers-tying-down-equipment-yard-wind-rising-dark-clouds-family-helping}
The storm arrives in the afternoon. First, the wind rises. It moans around the houses, and whistles in the chimneys. Then it howls. Then it roars. The rain comes, not falling down, but flying sideways, in great sheets, battering against the windows. The sea, which was calm in the morning, becomes a wild, grey, heaving mass, covered with white foam. Huge waves crash against the cliffs, and send spray flying high into the air, over the top of the island.

[pause]

Inside the houses, the families sit by the fire. The walls are thick, and the houses are strong. They were built for storms like this. But the noise is tremendous. The wind roars like a great beast. The rain drums on the roof. The windows rattle in their frames. And every few seconds, there is a deep boom, as a great wave hits the cliffs, and the whole island seems to shake.

[long-pause]

{img:family-by-fire-storm-outside-candles-mother-knitting-children-listening}
In the lighthouse, James is on watch. The tower sways a little in the wind. Very little, but enough to feel. The lamp flickers in the draughts. The windows of the lantern are streaming with rain and spray, even though they are thirty metres above the sea. James wipes them, again and again, from inside. Every hour, he must go out onto the balcony, holding onto the rail with all his strength, to clear the salt from the outside of the glass. The wind almost blows him off his feet.

[pause]

But the light keeps burning. All night, through the storm, it sweeps around, flashing every thirty seconds, out over the wild sea. Somewhere out there, in the darkness, ships are struggling through the storm. Their captains are watching for the light. And James is determined that it will not fail them. He trims the wicks. He winds the clockwork. He wipes the glass. He does not sleep. Not tonight.

[pause]

{img:keeper-on-lantern-balcony-storm-night-holding-rail-wind-rain-beam}
In the house, Mairi cannot sleep either. She sits by the fire, knitting, and listening to the storm. Every half minute, she sees the beam of the lighthouse, passing over the shutters, through the cracks. As long as she sees it, she knows that James is all right, and the light is burning. Isla comes down, in her nightgown, and sits beside her mother. They do not talk. They just sit together, by the fire, watching the beam pass, again and again.

[long-pause]

By the morning, the storm has passed. The wind drops. The rain stops. The clouds break up, and the sun comes out, pale and watery. The sea is still wild, with huge waves, but they are slowly calming down. The island is wet and battered. Some slates have blown off the roof. The garden wall has fallen down in one place. The beach is covered with seaweed and driftwood. But everyone is safe. And the light did not go out. James comes down from the tower, grey with tiredness, and Mairi gives him a hug, and a cup of hot tea, and a bowl of porridge. And then he sleeps, all day long.

[long-pause]

{img:small-fishing-boat-in-trouble-rough-sea-near-island-rocks-morning-after-storm}
On the morning after the storm, Isla goes up to the top of the lighthouse with the telescope, to look at the sea. The waves are still big, rolling in from the west. And then she sees something. On the rocks at the north end of the island, there is a small boat. A fishing boat, with a broken mast, stuck between two rocks, with the waves breaking over it. And on the boat, there are people, waving.

[long-pause]

She runs down the stairs, shouting for her father. James is asleep, but he wakes at once. He grabs the telescope, and looks. Three men, on a fishing boat, wrecked on the rocks in the night. They must have been caught in the storm, and blown onto the island in the darkness. They are holding on to the boat, which is slowly breaking up, as the waves hit it. They will not last long.

[pause]

James and Donald run to the north end of the island, with ropes, a life belt and a boat hook. Mairi and Effie follow, with blankets. Isla runs with them. The children have been told to stay at home, but Isla is twelve, and she refuses. When they reach the rocks, they see the boat, about thirty metres from the shore, with the three men clinging to it. Between them and the shore, there is a channel of rough water, with waves crashing through it.

[pause]

{img:keepers-throwing-rope-from-rocks-to-wrecked-boat-waves-crashing}
James takes the rope, and throws it, as hard as he can, toward the boat. It falls short, into the water. He pulls it back, and throws again. Short again. The wind is blowing it back. Donald tries. He is bigger and stronger. His rope reaches the boat. One of the men grabs it, and ties it around himself. And then, slowly, carefully, James and Donald pull him through the water, toward the rocks. He is battered by the waves, but he holds on. And at last, they pull him up onto the rocks, coughing and shivering, but alive.

[pause]

They do it again, for the second man. And then the third, who is the oldest, and very tired, and cannot hold on very well. The waves knock him under the water, twice. Everyone holds their breath. But James and Donald pull and pull, and at last he comes up, and they drag him onto the rocks. All three are safe. Mairi and Effie wrap them in blankets. Isla bursts into tears of relief. And the three fishermen sit on the rocks, too exhausted to speak.

[long-pause]

{img:rescued-fishermen-wrapped-blankets-by-fire-keepers-cottage-hot-tea}
They carry the fishermen back to the houses, and put them by the fire, with dry clothes, hot tea and hot soup. Slowly, the men recover. They are from a village on the mainland. Their boat was caught by the storm, the evening before, on their way home. Their mast broke. They drifted all night, in the dark and the wind. And then, in the early morning, they were thrown onto the rocks of the island. They thought they would never survive.

[pause]

The oldest fisherman, a man with a white beard, called Old Angus, takes James's hand, and holds it for a long time, without saying anything. Then he says that he saw the light, all through the night, as they drifted. And that it was the only thing that gave them hope. He says that he will never forget the light of this island. Or the people who keep it.

[pause]

{img:old-fisherman-shaking-keepers-hand-gratitude-cottage-doorway-sea}
The fishermen stay on the island for a week, until the sea is calm enough for the relief boat to come and take them home. They help with the work. They mend the garden wall. They tell stories by the fire in the evenings, and sing old songs. Calum follows them everywhere. And when they leave, Old Angus gives Calum his fishing knife, with a handle of deer horn. Calum keeps it for the rest of his life.

[long-pause]

There is a famous story, from England, about a lighthouse keeper's daughter called Grace Darling. In 1838, at the age of twenty-two, she helped her father to row a small boat through a terrible storm, to rescue nine people from a wrecked ship. She became a national heroine. Isla has read the story many times. She has always wanted to be like Grace Darling. And now, she thinks, she has helped to save three lives too. Not by rowing, but by watching. By being the first to see.

[long-pause]

{img:lighthouse-tender-steamship-pharos-anchored-off-island-flags-summer}
Once a year, in the summer, there is a very special visit. The Commissioners of the Northern Lighthouse Board come to inspect the lighthouse. They travel around the whole coast of Scotland, in their own steamship, visiting every lighthouse, one after another. The ship is called the Pharos, after the famous lighthouse of Alexandria. And when it appears on the horizon, the whole island is in a flurry.

[long-pause]

For weeks before the inspection, the keepers and their families prepare. Everything must be perfect. The lens is cleaned until it sparkles. The brass is polished until it shines like gold. The tower is painted white, inside and out. The houses are scrubbed from top to bottom. The garden is weeded. The paths are swept. The store rooms are tidied. The log books are checked, and checked again. Even the cow is brushed.

[pause]

When the Pharos arrives, it anchors off the island, and a small boat brings the Commissioners to the jetty. They are important men. Judges, lawyers and officials from Edinburgh, in dark suits and top hats. And with them comes the engineer, one of the Stevensons, a tall, serious man with a beard. The keepers are waiting on the jetty, in their best uniforms, with their caps on, and their brass buttons shining.

[pause]

{img:commissioners-top-hats-inspecting-lighthouse-lantern-room-keeper-attention}
The Commissioners walk up to the lighthouse, and the inspection begins. They climb the tower, and look at everything. The lamp. The lens. The clockwork. The windows. They run their white gloves over the brass, to check for dust. They look at the log books, and read the records of the weather, the ships and the work. They ask the keepers questions. How much oil do you use? How often do you trim the wicks? Have there been any problems?

[pause]

The engineer looks at the technical things. The lamp, the lens and the machinery. He checks that everything is working properly. He listens to the clockwork, and checks the timing of the flashes. He looks at the walls of the tower, for cracks. He looks at the fog bell. He asks James about the storm, and the fog, and the rescue of the fishermen. He writes everything down, in a little notebook.

[long-pause]

{img:family-lined-up-best-clothes-commissioners-visit-cottage-door-nervous}
Then the Commissioners visit the houses. They look at the kitchens, the bedrooms, the store rooms. They want to see that the keepers and their families are living well, and that the houses are clean and in good repair. Mairi and Effie stand at their doors, in their best dresses, nervous. The children stand beside them, washed and combed, in their Sunday clothes. Calum's hair, as usual, is sticking up at the back.

[pause]

The Commissioners are pleased. Everything is in perfect order. The lens sparkles. The brass shines. The log books are neat and complete. The houses are spotless. And the chief Commissioner, an old judge with white whiskers, has heard about the rescue of the fishermen. He shakes James's hand, and Donald's, and thanks them. And he says that the Board will give them each a reward, of money, for their bravery.

[pause]

{img:old-commissioner-giving-girl-book-reward-lighthouse-yard-smiling}
Then he turns to Isla, and asks if it is true that she was the first to see the wrecked boat. Isla blushes, and says yes. The old judge smiles, and says that she has the eyes of a true lighthouse keeper. And he gives her a present, from his own pocket. A small book, bound in leather, about the history of the lighthouses of Scotland. Isla is so happy that she cannot speak. She just curtseys, and holds the book against her chest.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, the Commissioners return to their ship. The Pharos raises its anchor, and steams away, toward the next lighthouse, with smoke pouring from its funnel. The families stand on the jetty, waving. And then they walk back up to the lighthouse, and take off their best clothes, and sit down in the kitchen, and laugh with relief. It has been a good inspection. The best ever, James says. And Mairi makes a special supper, to celebrate.

[long-pause]

{img:lighthouse-library-wooden-box-books-arriving-cottage-table-family-excited}
In the winter, the evenings on the island are very long. The sun sets in the middle of the afternoon, and rises late in the morning. In December, in the north of Scotland, there are only about six hours of daylight. The rest is darkness. And the families must find ways to fill the long, dark evenings, by the fire, while the storms blow outside.

[long-pause]

The Northern Lighthouse Board has a wonderful idea, to help the keepers. A travelling library. Every few months, a wooden box of books arrives on the relief boat. It contains about fifty books, of all kinds. Novels, histories, travel books, books about science and nature, religious books, and books for children. The families read them, and then, after a few months, send the box back, and receive a different one. So over the years, they read hundreds of books.

[pause]

When the library box arrives, it is like Christmas. Everyone gathers around the kitchen table, as James opens the lid. They take the books out, one by one, and look at them, and read the titles aloud. Who wants this one? Who has read that one? Isla always grabs the books about faraway countries and natural history. Mairi likes the novels. James likes history and adventure. And Calum looks for books with pictures of ships.

[pause]

{img:family-reading-by-paraffin-lamp-fireside-winter-evening-storm-shutters}
In this box, there is a new book, by a young Scottish writer called Robert Louis Stevenson. Yes, the son of the lighthouse engineer. It is about his journey through the mountains of France, on foot, with a stubborn little donkey called Modestine. It is not one of his famous novels. Those are still to come. But James is very interested, because he knows the Stevenson family. He reads the book aloud, in the evenings, to the whole family, by the fire. And he says that the young man writes very well, and that perhaps he will be famous one day.

[pause]

There is also music, in the evenings. James plays the fiddle. He plays old Scottish and Gaelic tunes. Jigs and reels, quick and lively, for dancing. Slow airs, sad and beautiful, which make Mairi's eyes wet. And songs, which everyone sings together. Songs about the sea, about the islands, about love and loss, about the hills of home. Donald has a beautiful deep voice, and Effie sings like a bird. The houses ring with music.

[long-pause]

{img:keeper-playing-fiddle-family-dancing-kitchen-reel-lamp-light-laughter}
Sometimes, they dance. They push back the table and the chairs, in the kitchen, and dance a reel. James plays, and the others dance, in pairs, round and round, stamping their feet, and clapping. Even little Angus, the baby, bounces up and down in his mother's arms. The kitchen is small, and they bump into each other, and laugh. And outside, the storm howls, but nobody notices.

[pause]

And there are stories. In the long evenings, by the fire, James tells the old Gaelic stories that his grandmother told him, on Skye. Stories of giants and heroes, of selkies and kelpies, of the fairy folk who live in the green hills, and the blue men of the Minch, who live in the sea between the islands, and raise storms to sink ships. The children listen, wide-eyed, as the wind moans in the chimney.

[pause]

{img:father-telling-gaelic-stories-fireside-children-wide-eyes-wind-outside}
Mairi knits, all evening, every evening. Sweaters for James, thick and warm, for the cold nights in the lighthouse. Socks for everyone. Hats and mittens for the children. She knits beautiful patterns, which her mother taught her, on Skye. She can knit without looking, while she listens to the stories, or the music, or the book being read aloud. Her needles click softly, all evening, like a little clock.

[long-pause]

At about nine o'clock, the children go to bed. James goes up the tower, for his watch. Mairi banks down the fire. And the house becomes quiet. Outside, the wind is still blowing, and the sea is still roaring against the cliffs. But inside, it is warm and safe. And every thirty seconds, the beam of the lighthouse passes over the shutters, steady and calm, keeping watch over the island, and the sea, and the ships, through the long winter night.

[long-pause]

{img:lighthouse-cottage-christmas-1880-candles-window-snow-evergreen-decorations}
At the end of the year, there are two celebrations. Christmas, and New Year. In Scotland in 1880, New Year is the bigger celebration. It is called Hogmanay. But on the island, the families celebrate both, as well as they can, far from the mainland, with the sea all around them.

[long-pause]

Christmas in Scotland, in 1880, is a quieter feast than in England. For a long time, the Scottish church did not celebrate it much. But slowly, it is becoming more popular. On the island, the families go to no church, because there is none. But on Christmas morning, James reads the Christmas story from the Bible, by the fire, and everyone sings a hymn. Then there is a special dinner. A goose, or a chicken, with potatoes and vegetables from the garden. And a clootie dumpling, rich and sweet.

[pause]

The children get presents. Small ones. A new book. A wooden toy. A pair of knitted mittens. An orange, which is a great treat, brought on the last relief boat before the winter. Calum gets a little telescope of his own, made of brass, from his father. Isla gets a box of watercolour paints, so that she can paint the birds of the island. They are both delighted.

[pause]

{img:children-opening-christmas-presents-fireside-telescope-paints-orange}
But the great feast is Hogmanay, on the last night of the year. In Scotland, everyone celebrates Hogmanay. The house must be cleaned from top to bottom, before the New Year, so that the year begins fresh and clean. All debts should be paid. And on the night of Hogmanay, there is a party, with food, drink, music and dancing, until midnight, when the New Year begins.

[pause]

On the island, the two families celebrate together, in James and Mairi's kitchen. Mairi has baked shortbread, and black bun, a very rich fruit cake in a pastry case, which is eaten at Hogmanay. There is cheese, and oatcakes, and a little whisky for the grown-ups. James plays the fiddle. Donald sings. Everyone dances. And the children are allowed to stay up until midnight, for the only time in the whole year.

[long-pause]

{img:hogmanay-kitchen-party-fiddle-dancing-shortbread-whisky-clock-midnight}
At midnight, everyone stands together, holding hands, in a circle, and sings an old Scottish song, written by the poet Robert Burns, almost a hundred years before. It is called Auld Lang Syne. It is about old friends, and old times, and remembering them. Should auld acquaintance be forgot, and never brought to mind. Everyone sings it, with their arms crossed, holding hands. Mairi has tears in her eyes, thinking of her family on Skye.

[pause]

After midnight, there is the custom of first-footing. The first person to step over the threshold of a house, in the New Year, brings luck for the whole year. The best first-foot is a tall, dark-haired man, carrying gifts. A lump of coal, for warmth. Some bread, for food. Some salt, for flavour. And a little whisky, for good cheer. On the island, Donald, who is tall and dark, goes out of the house, and knocks on the door again, as the first-foot, with his gifts. Everyone cheers.

[pause]

{img:first-footing-dark-haired-man-doorway-coal-bread-salt-gifts-new-year}
But the lighthouse must still be kept. Even at Hogmanay. Even at midnight. So James slips away, before midnight, to climb the tower, for his watch. And from the lantern room, high above the island, he watches the New Year arrive. At midnight exactly, he looks out over the dark sea, and sees, far away on the mainland, little lights. Bonfires on the hills, celebrating the New Year. And ships out at sea, sounding their horns, and firing rockets.

[long-pause]

James stands at the window of the lantern, with the great lens turning behind him, and the beam sweeping out over the sea. He thinks about the past year. The storm. The fog. The rescue. The inspection. The children growing. And he thinks about the new year ahead. And he says a little prayer, for his family, for the sailors at sea, and for the light. Then he trims the wicks, and winds the clockwork, and sits down at his desk, to write the first entry of the New Year in the log book. All well.

[long-pause]

{img:young-woman-leaving-island-boat-family-waving-jetty-lighthouse-behind}
The years go by, on the island. The seasons turn. The puffins come and go. The storms come and go. The supply boat comes and goes. And the children grow up. Slowly, life on the island changes, as it always does.

[long-pause]

The next year, Isla goes to the mainland, to the school in the town, to continue her education. It is very hard to leave the island. She cries all the way across the sea, on the relief boat, looking back at the lighthouse, getting smaller and smaller. But she does very well at school. She is the best pupil in her class. And she writes long letters home, every week, which arrive with the relief boat, in bundles of four or five.

[pause]

Isla becomes a teacher. She teaches in a small school on the island of Skye, where her parents came from. She teaches the children about the sea, the birds and the stars, as her parents taught her. And she paints. Beautiful watercolours of sea birds, which she sells, and which later hang in a museum in Edinburgh. She never forgets the island, and the lighthouse, and the light sweeping over her window at night.

[pause]

{img:grown-sailor-on-ship-deck-looking-at-lighthouse-on-island-horizon}
Calum becomes a sailor, as he always dreamed. At fifteen, he goes to sea, as a deck boy on a cargo ship. He sails all over the world. To America, to India, to Australia, to China. He sees all the places he read about, in the books from the library box. And whenever his ship passes the coast of Scotland, at night, he stands on the deck, watching for the lights of the lighthouses. And when he sees the light of his island, flashing white, once every thirty seconds, he knows that his father is there, keeping watch.

[pause]

James and Mairi stay on the island for five more years. Then they are moved to another lighthouse, on the mainland, near a village, as keepers often are. Mairi is glad to be near a church and a shop again. But she misses the island. They both do. James keeps lighthouses for twenty-five more years, at four different stations, and in all that time, the light never goes out. Not once. When he retires, the Board gives him a silver watch, with his name engraved on it.

[long-pause]

{img:old-couple-retired-cottage-skye-garden-view-of-sea-distant-lighthouse}
In their old age, James and Mairi live in a small cottage on Skye, near the sea, with a garden, and a view of a lighthouse on a distant headland. In the evenings, James sits by the window, and watches its light flashing, out over the water. And he counts the flashes, out of old habit. Mairi knits by the fire. And their grandchildren come to visit, and ask for stories about the island, the storm, the fog and the rescue of the fishermen.

[pause]

Donald and Effie stay on the island for many years more. Donald becomes the principal keeper, when James leaves. Their son Angus, the baby who was just learning to walk, grows up on the island, and becomes a lighthouse keeper himself, like his father. Many families of lighthouse keepers were like that. Generation after generation, keeping the lights of Scotland burning, on islands and headlands and rocks, all around the coast.

[pause]

{img:keepers-family-generations-photograph-style-in-front-of-lighthouse-tower}
And the light on the island goes on shining. Year after year. Decade after decade. Every night, from sunset to sunrise. Flashing white, once every thirty seconds, out over the sea. Different keepers come and go. Different families live in the white houses. Different children play in the rock pools, and watch the puffins, and polish the brass. But the light is always the same.

[long-pause]

Calum's old fishing knife, with the handle of deer horn, is still kept by his family today, more than a hundred years later. And Isla's book about the lighthouses of Scotland, the one the old judge gave her, is still on a shelf, in the house of her great-granddaughter. With a pressed sea pink flower inside the front cover, picked on the cliffs of the island, a long time ago.

[long-pause]

{img:flannan-isles-lighthouse-remote-island-cliffs-grey-sea-mist-later}
The life of the lighthouse keepers went on, in much the same way, for more than a hundred years after our story. The lamps changed. The lenses changed. The fog signals changed. But the work stayed the same. Keeping the light burning, every night, without fail. And watching the sea.

[long-pause]

In 1900, there was a famous mystery, at a lighthouse on the Flannan Isles, a group of lonely islands far out in the Atlantic, west of Lewis. A ship passing by noticed that the light was not shining. When the relief boat arrived, a few days later, the three keepers had disappeared. The lighthouse was empty. Some of the stories say that the table was still laid, and that a clock had stopped. Nobody ever found out exactly what happened. Most people believe that they were swept away by a giant wave, during a storm. It became one of the most famous mysteries of the sea, and people still wonder about it today.

[pause]

In the early twentieth century, the lamps changed. The old paraffin wick lamps were replaced by a new kind of lamp, which burned paraffin vapour, under pressure, through a little mantle, which glowed white-hot. It was much brighter. Later still, electricity came to the lighthouses. Electric lamps, powered by generators, or by cables from the mainland. The great glass lenses stayed. But the light inside them became brighter and brighter.

[pause]

{img:electric-lamp-in-old-fresnel-lens-modern-bulb-brass-frame-later}
The fog bells were replaced by foghorns. Great horns, driven by compressed air, which made a deep, loud, moaning sound, that could be heard for many kilometres. People who lived near the coast grew used to the sound of the foghorns, on foggy nights. Some people loved it. Some people hated it. Today, most of the foghorns have been switched off, because ships now have radar and satellites to find their way.

[pause]

Life for the keepers became a little easier. Radios came, so that they could talk to the mainland, and hear the news, and call for help in an emergency. Helicopters came, to bring the relief keepers and supplies to the rock lighthouses, instead of boats. Electricity brought light and heat to the houses. But the keepers still had to keep watch, every night, and clean the lens, and polish the brass.

[long-pause]

{img:helicopter-landing-rock-lighthouse-helipad-relief-keepers-later}
Then, in the second half of the twentieth century, the lighthouses began to be automated. Machines were invented, which could switch the light on and off automatically, at sunset and sunrise. Which could change a bulb if it burned out. Which could start the foghorn when there was fog. Which could send a message to the mainland if anything went wrong. The keepers were no longer needed.

[pause]

One by one, the lighthouses of Scotland were automated, and the keepers left. The last one was at Fair Isle, a small island between Orkney and Shetland. On the thirty-first of March, 1998, the last keepers left Fair Isle South lighthouse, and the light was left to shine by itself. After two hundred and twelve years, the age of the Scottish lighthouse keepers was over.

[pause]

{img:last-keepers-leaving-lighthouse-1998-boat-jetty-farewell-island-later}
Many people were sad. The lighthouse keepers had been a part of Scottish life for so long. Their families, their stories, their courage and their patience. Some of the keepers had spent their whole lives in lighthouses, and their fathers before them, and their grandfathers before that. Now, the towers stood empty. The houses were sold, or left empty. The gardens became wild. Only the lights kept shining, alone, every night, as they always had.

[long-pause]

But the lights still shine. All around the coast of Scotland, every night, more than two hundred lighthouses still flash their signals out over the sea. Each with its own pattern, its own colour, its own character. The ships still watch for them. And on the islands and the headlands, in the dark, the beams still sweep across the waves, faithfully, just as they did in 1880, when James MacLeod climbed the stairs at sunset, to light the lamp.

[long-pause]

{img:museum-of-scottish-lighthouses-kinnaird-head-fraserburgh-today-visitors}
Today, many of the old lighthouses of Scotland can be visited. Some are museums. Some are holiday cottages. Some are bird observatories. And some can be seen from the shore, or from a boat, standing white and tall on their islands and rocks, just as they did a hundred and fifty years ago.

[long-pause]

In the town of Fraserburgh, in the north-east of Scotland, there is the Museum of Scottish Lighthouses. It is built next to Kinnaird Head, the very first lighthouse of the Northern Lighthouse Board, lit in 1787. Visitors can climb up the old tower, to the lantern room, and see the great glass lens, still in its brass frame, shining. And in the museum, there are dozens of lenses, lamps, fog bells, uniforms, log books and photographs, telling the story of the lighthouses and their keepers.

[pause]

The greatest treasure of the museum is the collection of lenses. Great, beautiful glass lenses, from lighthouses all around Scotland, some of them taller than a person. When the sun shines through them, they break the light into rainbows, which dance on the walls of the museum, just as they danced in Isla's lantern room. Children stand inside the lenses, and look out through them, and see the world turned upside down and broken into pieces of light.

[pause]

{img:visitors-looking-at-giant-glass-lens-museum-rainbows-on-walls-today}
Many of the keepers' houses, at the old lighthouses, have become holiday cottages. People can stay in them, for a week or two, on lonely headlands and islands, with the lighthouse right outside the window. They can watch the sea, and the birds, and the stars, and see the light flashing over the water at night. Some people say it is the most peaceful holiday in the world. A taste of the life of the keepers, without the work.

[pause]

Some lighthouse islands have become nature reserves. On islands like the Isle of May, in the Firth of Forth, or Fair Isle, the old lighthouse buildings are now used by scientists and bird watchers, who study the sea birds. The puffins, the gannets, the guillemots and the kittiwakes still nest on the cliffs, every spring, in their thousands, just as they did when Calum lay on his stomach at the top of the cliff, watching them.

[long-pause]

{img:puffins-today-cliff-top-lighthouse-behind-birdwatchers-binoculars-spring}
The Bell Rock lighthouse, built by Robert Stevenson more than two hundred years ago, still stands in the North Sea, with the waves breaking around it. It is still working, automatically. On a clear day, from the coast near Arbroath, you can see it, a thin white pencil on the horizon. And in Arbroath, there is the old signal tower, where the keepers' families lived, which is now a museum. Visitors can see how they sent signals to the keepers on the rock, with flags and balls on a mast.

[pause]

Skerryvore still stands too, tall and beautiful, on its lonely rock in the Atlantic, west of the island of Tiree. On the island, there is a little museum about it, in the old signal tower. And on clear nights, from the beaches of Tiree, you can see its light, flashing out over the dark ocean, every ten seconds. It has been shining for more than a hundred and eighty years.

[pause]

{img:skerryvore-lighthouse-distant-sunset-from-tiree-beach-today-calm}
And on the west coast, among the islands, little lighthouses still stand on the headlands and the rocks. Some are tall towers, some are small white huts, with a single lamp. They are painted white, or white with a red or black band. They are photographed by tourists, painted by artists, and loved by sailors and walkers. They have become one of the symbols of Scotland. Like the castles, the mountains and the lochs.

[long-pause]

People still love lighthouses. All over the world. There are books, films, paintings and songs about them. Children draw them. Adults dream of living in them. Perhaps it is because they stand for something good. For watchfulness, and care, and faithfulness. For a light in the darkness, which never goes out. For people who stay awake, through the long nights, so that others can be safe.

[long-pause]

{img:lighthouse-island-sunset-1880-tower-white-sea-golden-cottages-smoke}
So we leave the lighthouse tonight, in the year 1880, as the evening falls. The sun is setting over the Atlantic, in the west. The sky is orange and pink and gold. The sea is calm, and shining like a mirror. The sea birds are flying home to the cliffs, calling softly. And on the highest point of the little island, the white tower stands, glowing in the last light.

[long-pause]

In the kitchen of the keeper's house, the paraffin lamp is lit. Mairi is making supper. Oatcakes, cheese, a pot of soup, and fresh fish, caught that afternoon. Calum is setting the table, very badly. Isla is sitting by the window, painting a puffin, in her new watercolours. And the kettle is singing on the range.

[pause]

{img:keeper-climbing-spiral-staircase-tower-sunset-light-through-small-windows}
James looks at his watch. It is almost sunset. He puts on his uniform jacket and his cap, and walks out across the yard, to the tower. He opens the door, and begins to climb the spiral stairs. Round and round, up and up. At the top, he draws back the curtains. He lights the lamp. The flame grows. The great lens fills with light. He starts the clockwork. And the first beam sweeps out across the darkening sea.

[long-pause]

Out on the sea, far away, a ship is passing. A small cargo steamer, on its way north. On the bridge, the captain sees the light, flashing white, once every thirty seconds. He checks his book. He knows where he is. He adjusts his course, a little. And he thinks, as sailors often do, of the keeper in the tower, whom he will never meet, keeping watch for him, through the night.

[pause]

{img:lighthouse-beam-night-calm-sea-stars-ship-lights-far-away-peaceful}
The night comes down over the island. The stars come out, one by one, and then in thousands. The moon rises over the mountains of the mainland, far away to the east. The seals are lying on the rocks, half asleep. The puffins are in their burrows. The cow is chewing in her shed. And in the houses, the lamps go out, one by one, as the families go to bed.

[long-pause]

Isla lies in her bed, under the warm quilt, watching the beam pass across her window. Light. Dark. Light. Dark. Every thirty seconds. Slow and steady, like a heartbeat. She thinks of her father, up in the tower, keeping watch. She thinks of the ships, passing safely in the night. She thinks of the puffins, and the seals, and the stars. And slowly, counting the flashes, she falls asleep, as the light keeps turning, all night long, over the dark and quiet sea.
