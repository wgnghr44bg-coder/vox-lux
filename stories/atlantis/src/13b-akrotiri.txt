On the island of Santorini, under the ash, archaeologists found a whole town. It is called Akrotiri.

In 1967, a Greek archaeologist called Spyridon Marinatos began to dig there. He had an idea that he wanted to test. He believed the eruption of Thera might be linked to the story of Atlantis. And he hoped to find something under the thick layers of ash. He was right, and he did.

--

Under many metres of ash and pumice, there was a whole town. There were streets and little squares, and houses two and three storeys high. There were stairs and doorways and windows, still in their places. There were storerooms full of big clay jars for oil and grain. There were even the shapes of beds and tables, kept as hollow moulds in the ash. It was like a town frozen in time. It was a little like Pompeii in Italy, but much, much older.

And on the walls of the houses, there were paintings. They were beautiful, bright paintings, full of colour. They were found broken into many small pieces. Patient workers put them carefully together again, piece by piece, over many years. They are some of the loveliest paintings of the ancient world.

{img:akrotiri-fresco-spring-swallows-lilies-red-rocks-bright-colours}
One room is painted all around with a picture of spring. There are red rocks and tall lilies, swaying in the breeze. And there are swallows, pairs of swallows flying and swooping and playing in the air. It is a happy picture, full of life and sunshine. Another painting shows two boys boxing, each wearing a single glove. Another shows a young fisherman, holding up strings of fresh fish. Others show gentle antelopes, and playful blue monkeys.

--

One long painting shows a fleet of ships. They sail slowly from one town to another, across a blue sea. People watch them from the windows and the flat roofs. Dolphins leap and play around the ships. Perhaps it shows a festival, a celebration of the sea. Some people looked at this painting and thought of the ships of Atlantis.

{img:akrotiri-ship-fresco-fleet-dolphins-towns-people-watching-ancient}
The people of Akrotiri were rich, and they traded across the sea. Their houses had toilets and drains, which was unusual in those days. Their walls were decorated with paintings. They lived comfortable and pleasant lives. We can imagine a mother grinding grain in the kitchen, while her children play on the stairs. We can imagine a father unloading jars of oil from a boat in the harbour. And then the volcano gave its warnings, and the people left. Where did they go? Nobody knows for sure. Perhaps to Crete, the big island to the south. Perhaps even further away.

--

Marinatos dug at Akrotiri for many years, and he loved the place. He passed away there, in the middle of his work, after an accident. He rests there now, in the town he found. Archaeologists still dig at Akrotiri today. They have uncovered only a small part of the town so far. There is much more still under the ash, waiting quietly.

Visitors can walk through the uncovered streets today, under a great roof. The roof protects the old houses from the sun and the rain. It is cool and quiet in there. You walk past doorways and staircases, and big storage jars still standing where they were left. And you can almost hear the voices of the people who lived there, three and a half thousand years ago. You can almost hear them calling to each other across the street, and laughing. And then packing up their things in a hurry, and sailing away.
