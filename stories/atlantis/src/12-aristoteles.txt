So was Atlantis real, or was it only a story?

Even in Plato's own school, people did not agree about it. One of Plato's students was a young man from the north of Greece. His name was Aristotle. He studied at the Academy for twenty years, among the olive trees. He became one of the greatest thinkers in all of history. And he did not believe in Atlantis at all.

--

Old writers tell us what Aristotle said, or something very like it. The man who made it up, he said, also made it sink. In other words, Plato invented Atlantis himself. And then he made it disappear under the sea, so nobody could ever go and check. That was very clever of him, Aristotle thought.

Many people today agree with Aristotle about this. They think Plato invented Atlantis to teach a lesson. Plato liked to teach with stories, and the story of Atlantis has a clear lesson. A people that becomes too rich and too proud will not last. Goodness and wisdom matter much more than gold. And a small, brave city that does what is right can stand against a great proud power.

{img:ancient-athens-navy-triremes-harbour-piraeus-proud-city}
Plato may have been thinking about his own city of Athens. Long before, Athens had been brave and good. It had stood against the great empire of Persia, and it had won. But after that, Athens became rich and powerful. It built a great navy of fast ships with three rows of oars. It ruled over many other Greek cities and took their money. It grew rather proud of itself. And then it lost a long and difficult war. Plato saw all of this happen when he was young. Maybe Atlantis is a picture of what happens to a city like that. Maybe it is a gentle warning.

--

But not everyone agreed with Aristotle, even then. A few hundred years later, another Greek thinker wrote that the story might be true. Some people even said they had travelled to Egypt and seen the old records themselves. But nobody could ever show these records to anyone else.

{img:scholars-debating-library-alexandria-scrolls-shelves-columns}
In the great library of Alexandria, in Egypt, scholars sat among the shelves of scrolls. They read Plato's books and talked about them, just like the students in Athens. Some said the story was true, and some said it was not. They argued politely, and then they went for a walk by the harbour.

And so the arguing began, in Plato's own school, between his own students. And it has never stopped since then. For two thousand four hundred years, people have taken sides. Was it real? Was it a lesson? Or was it a little bit of both? Perhaps it was a lesson built on an old, half-remembered true story.

--

The young student from the island walks home that night under the stars. He thinks about everything he has heard. He thinks that Aristotle is probably right. It is a made-up story, with a lesson in it. And yet, he is not completely sure. He thinks about the old fishermen in his village at home. They told stories of islands that rose from the sea and sank again. They told of the sea pulling back before a great wave came. Perhaps there is something true in it after all.

The next day, he goes to his teacher and asks him about it. The old man thinks quietly for a long moment. Then he says something strange. A story does not have to be true to be true, he says. The young student does not understand those words. Not yet, anyway. It will take him many years to understand. But he never forgets them.
