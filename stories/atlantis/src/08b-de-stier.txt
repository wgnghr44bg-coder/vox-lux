When the ten kings met at the temple of Poseidon, there was a special ceremony. It was about bulls.

In the grounds of the temple, there were bulls that roamed free. They were sacred to Poseidon. Nobody could harm them. Except once, at the meeting of the kings.

--

Plato describes it carefully. The ten kings went into the sacred grounds alone. No guards. No servants. No weapons of iron. Only wooden staffs and ropes. They prayed to the god. And then they tried to catch one of the bulls with their hands and ropes. It was a test. Not of strength alone. But of whether the god was willing. If the god was pleased, they would catch a bull.

When they caught one, they led it to the pillar of orichalcum, where the law was written. They offered it to the god there. We will leave the details gently aside. Then they filled a great bowl with a mix of wine and the offering. They each took a cup from the bowl. And they made a promise.

{img:ten-kings-golden-cups-oath-pillar-night-fire-solemn-promise}
They promised to judge fairly, according to the law on the pillar. To never break the law. To never obey any king who broke it. They drank from their golden cups. And they placed the cups in the temple, as a sign of their promise.

--

Then, as night fell, they put on beautiful dark blue robes. They sat on the ground by the dying fire of the altar. They put out all the other lights in the temple. And there, in the dark, they judged each other. If any king had broken the law, it was decided that night. At dawn, they wrote down their judgements on golden tablets. And they hung their robes in the temple, as a memory.

{img:kings-dark-blue-robes-sitting-dark-temple-dying-embers-night-judging}
It is a strange and solemn picture. Ten kings in dark blue robes, sitting in the dark, judging each other by the glow of embers. Many people think Plato was thinking of old ceremonies he had heard of. On the island of Crete, there were old stories about bulls and kings. We will come back to Crete later. It may be an important clue.

--

The young student from the island has heard of Crete. His grandfather once sailed there. He told stories of huge ruined palaces with red columns, older than anyone could remember. Of painted walls with leaping bulls. The student thinks about it. Maybe, he thinks, there is a little bit of truth hidden in Plato's story after all. Like a pearl in an oyster.
