{img:atlantis-legend-island-rings-water-temple-sea-dawn-mythical}
Tonight we listen to one of the oldest and most famous stories in the world. The legend of Atlantis.

[pause]

It is the story of a great island in the ocean, long, long ago. An island with rich fields and hot springs and wonderful harbours. With a city built in rings of land and water. With temples covered in silver and gold. With proud kings and many ships. And then, one day and one night, the sea came. And Atlantis was gone.

[long-pause]

Is it true? Did Atlantis really exist? People have asked that question for more than two thousand years. They have searched for it in every ocean and every sea. They have written thousands of books about it. And still nobody knows for sure.

[pause]

But we do know where the story comes from. It comes from one man. A Greek thinker called Plato. He lived in Athens, about two thousand four hundred years ago. And he wrote it down.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-athens-evening-acropolis-parthenon-lamps-olive-trees}
Tonight we will go to Athens in the time of Plato. We will sit in his garden school, where young men come to learn. We will hear how the story of Atlantis travelled from priests in Egypt to a wise man of Athens. We will walk through the city of Atlantis as Plato describes it, ring by ring. We will meet its kings and visit its temple. And then we will hear how it ended.

[pause]

{img:plato-old-teacher-beard-garden-academy-students-listening-shade}
After that, we will go looking for Atlantis ourselves. Through history and through science. To a volcano in the Aegean Sea that blew apart. To a lost palace full of painted dolphins. To a Greek city that sank in one night, in Plato's own lifetime. And to a land under the North Sea, where people once walked.

[long-pause]

{img:sea-waves-dusk-calm-horizon-ancient-ship-sail-distant-mystery}
So let us begin in Athens, on a warm evening. The sun is setting over the sea. The marble temples on the hill glow pink and gold. Olive trees whisper in the breeze. And in a quiet garden outside the city walls, an old man is about to tell a story.

[long-pause]

{img:athens-360bc-agora-market-stoa-people-talking-columns-sunny}
It is Athens, around the year 360 before Christ.

[pause]

Athens is a famous Greek city. It lies near the sea, in a dry land of hills and olive trees. On a rocky hill in the middle of the city stands the Acropolis. On top of it are white marble temples. The greatest is the Parthenon, the temple of the goddess Athena. It was built about eighty years before our story. It shines in the sun.

[long-pause]

Athens is a city of talkers. In the market square, people gather to buy and sell. But also to argue and discuss. About politics. About the gods. About what is good and what is true. Athens is a democracy. That means the citizens vote on the laws themselves. So everybody has an opinion. And everybody wants to share it.

[pause]

In this city lives Plato. He is about sixty-five years old. He comes from a rich old family. When he was young, he was a student of a famous thinker called Socrates. Socrates walked around Athens asking people questions. Simple questions that turned out to be very hard. What is justice? What is courage? What is a good life? People found him annoying. Or wonderful. Usually both.

[pause]

{img:socrates-talking-young-men-street-athens-barefoot-questions}
Socrates never wrote anything down. But Plato did. He wrote dozens of books. Most of them are written as conversations. People talking and asking questions and searching for answers together. Socrates is often the main speaker. These books are still read all over the world. Plato is one of the most important thinkers who ever lived.

[long-pause]

Plato loved stories. He used them to explain his ideas. A story about a cave and its shadows. A story about a ring that makes you invisible. A story about the soul as a chariot pulled by two horses. And in his old age, he wrote down the story of Atlantis.

[pause]

{img:plato-writing-scroll-papyrus-oil-lamp-study-night-athens}
He put it in two of his books. They are called Timaeus and Critias, after the people who speak in them. And in them, the story of Atlantis is told by a man called Critias. Who heard it from his grandfather. Who heard it from a great wise man called Solon. Who heard it from priests in Egypt. A story passed from mouth to mouth across the centuries. Let us follow it back.

[long-pause]

{img:plato-academy-olive-grove-garden-paths-students-walking-talking}
Outside the walls of Athens, a short walk from the city gate, there is a grove of olive trees. It is called the Academy. It is named after an old hero who was once honoured there. And here, Plato has started a school.

[pause]

It is not like a school today. There are no classrooms or exams. It is more like a garden where people come to think together. There are shady paths under the olive trees. There is a little shrine. There is a house where Plato lives. And there are benches where people sit and talk.

[long-pause]

Young men come here from all over Greece. Some come for a few months. Some stay for years. They study mathematics and stars. They study what is good and what is just. They ask how a city should be ruled. They walk up and down the paths, arguing. And they listen to Plato.

[pause]

Over the entrance, people say, there were words carved in stone. Let nobody who does not know geometry enter here. Geometry is the mathematics of shapes. Plato believed it trained the mind to think clearly. And clear thinking was the first step to wisdom.

[pause]

{img:students-drawing-geometry-sand-stick-circles-triangles-academy}
In the evenings, when it is cooler, people gather under the trees. Someone reads aloud from a new book. Others listen and then ask questions. They talk until the stars come out. Then they light lamps and talk some more.

[long-pause]

Let us imagine one of these evenings. A young student is sitting at the edge of the group. He has come from a small island far away. He is shy. He does not often speak. But he listens to everything. Tonight, someone is reading a new book by Plato. It is called Critias. And it is about a lost island called Atlantis.

[pause]

{img:young-student-listening-intently-evening-lamp-olive-trees-academy}
The student leans forward. He has grown up by the sea. He knows its moods and storms. He has heard old sailors' tales of islands that rose and sank. And now he listens to the most amazing island story of all. Was it real, he wonders? Or is it a story with a lesson? He does not know. But he cannot stop listening.

[long-pause]

The students at the Academy are a mixed group. Some are rich young men from Athens. Some come from far-off cities. There are even a few women, people say, who came dressed as young men so they could study. They eat simple food. They drink water mixed with a little wine. And above all, they talk. About everything. Until late into the night.

[long-pause]

{img:solon-athenian-lawgiver-older-man-robes-wise-face-agora}
The story begins with a man called Solon.

[pause]

Solon lived in Athens about two hundred and fifty years before Plato. He was one of the wisest men of his time. In those days, Athens was in trouble. The rich had too much. The poor had too little. Many poor farmers owed so much money that they became slaves. People were angry. The city could have fallen apart.

[long-pause]

So the people asked Solon to help. He was trusted by both rich and poor. He made new laws. He set free the farmers who had become slaves for debt. He made rules that were fairer for everyone. He gave ordinary people a voice in the government. His laws helped to make Athens the city it became.

[pause]

Then Solon did something unusual. He left. He said, I have made the laws. Now you must live with them for ten years without changing them. And I will go away, so you cannot ask me to change them. So he set off on a long journey. To see the world and to learn.

[pause]

{img:greek-merchant-ship-sailing-mediterranean-blue-sea-solon-aboard}
He sailed across the sea. He visited many lands. And one of the places he visited was Egypt.

[long-pause]

For the Greeks, Egypt was a land of mystery and very old wisdom. Its kings and temples were far older than anything in Greece. Its priests knew writing and stars and history going back thousands of years. Greek travellers went there with great respect. Like going to visit a very old and very wise grandparent.

[pause]

{img:egyptian-temple-sais-nile-delta-pillars-priests-white-linen}
Solon sailed to the delta of the Nile, where the great river splits into many branches before reaching the sea. There, in the city of Saïs, he visited the temple of the goddess Neith. The Egyptian priests welcomed him. They talked for many days. And there, Plato tells us, Solon heard a story he would never forget.

[long-pause]

Solon was also a poet. He wrote poems about his city and his laws and his life. Some of them have survived. In one, he writes that he grows old while always learning many things. That was the kind of man he was. Curious until the end. So when he heard something new and strange in Egypt, he listened very carefully. And he remembered.

[long-pause]

{img:sais-temple-courtyard-old-priest-greek-visitor-talking-shade}
Saïs is a busy city in the green delta of the Nile. Canals run between the fields. Palm trees line the river. And in the middle of the city stands a great temple. It is very old, with tall stone columns covered in carvings and painted signs.

[pause]

Solon sits with the priests in the shade of the temple. He wants to impress them. So he tells them the oldest stories of Greece. About the first people. About a great flood long ago. About the families who survived it. He tells them how many generations have passed since then. He is proud of this old history.

[long-pause]

But one very old priest laughs gently. Oh Solon, he says. You Greeks are always children. There is not one old man among you.

[pause]

Solon is surprised. What do you mean, he asks.

[pause]

You are young in your souls, the priest says. You have no old knowledge. No old stories. Because again and again, disasters come. Floods and fires. They wipe away cities and people and writing. Only a few survive. Usually the simple people in the mountains, who cannot read or write. And so every time, you must begin again, like children. You forget everything that came before.

[pause]

{img:egyptian-priest-reading-old-temple-records-carved-walls-lamp}
But here in Egypt, says the priest, we are protected. The Nile saves us from fire and flood. So our temples are very old. And we have written down everything. For thousands of years. And in our old records, there is a story about your own city. About Athens. A story you have forgotten.

[long-pause]

Solon leans forward. Tell me, he says.

[pause]

{img:solon-listening-wide-eyes-priest-telling-evening-temple-courtyard}
And the priest begins. Nine thousand years ago, he says, your city of Athens was great and noble. And there was another great power. Beyond the Pillars of Heracles, in the outer ocean. An island called Atlantis.

[long-pause]

The priest speaks slowly. Outside, the evening birds call over the river. Lamps are lit along the temple walls. Solon does not move. He has travelled far and seen many things. But he has never heard a story like this one. He asks the priest to tell it again, every detail. And the priest smiles, and begins once more. They sit together until long after dark.

[long-pause]

{img:pillars-of-heracles-strait-gibraltar-rocks-ocean-beyond-ancient}
The priest tells Solon about the Pillars of Heracles.

[pause]

The Pillars of Heracles are two great rocks. They stand on each side of a narrow strait of water. Today we call it the Strait of Gibraltar. It is the place where the Mediterranean Sea meets the great Atlantic Ocean. For the Greeks, this was the edge of the known world. Beyond it lay the outer ocean. Wide and wild and unknown.

[long-pause]

Beyond those pillars, says the priest, in the ocean, there was once a great island. It was bigger than Libya and Asia together. That is what the Greeks called North Africa and the lands to the east. A huge island. From it, ships could sail to other islands. And from those islands, to a great continent on the other side of the ocean.

[pause]

On this island there was a great and wonderful kingdom. Atlantis. Its kings were powerful. They ruled the whole island and many other islands. And they ruled lands inside the Pillars too. Parts of Libya as far as Egypt. Parts of Europe as far as Italy.

[pause]

{img:atlantean-fleet-many-ships-ocean-sails-island-distant-mythical}
Then, the priest says, the kings of Atlantis grew greedy. They wanted more. They gathered their armies and their ships. And they tried to conquer all the lands around the sea. Greece and Egypt too. With one great blow.

[long-pause]

But Athens stood against them. The Athenians of that ancient time were brave and good. They led the other Greeks. And when the others gave up, Athens stood alone. It faced the great power of Atlantis. And it won. It freed the peoples who had been conquered. And it protected Egypt too.

[pause]

{img:ancient-warriors-standing-on-hill-sea-dawn-mythic-courage-calm}
Then, says the priest, came a terrible time. There were great earthquakes and floods. And in one day and one night, the island of Atlantis sank into the sea. It disappeared. And the ancient army of Athens was swallowed by the earth too. That is why the sea beyond the Pillars is still hard to sail, says the priest. Because there is mud, just below the surface, where the island went down.

[long-pause]

Solon is amazed. He has never heard this story. He goes home to Athens. He plans to write it as a great poem. But he is old and busy with the troubles of his city. He never finishes it. He tells it to a friend. The friend tells it to his son. And the son tells it to his grandson, Critias. A boy of ten. Who remembers it all his life. And who tells it, at last, to Plato.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-egypt-temple-wall-hieroglyphs-carvings-sunlight-old-stones}
Why would the Greeks believe a story from Egypt?

[pause]

Because for the Greeks, Egypt was the land of the deepest past. When Plato was alive, Egypt was already incredibly old. Its pyramids had been standing for two thousand years. Its kings had ruled for three thousand years. Its priests could read writing that no Greek could understand. Their temples held records going back for ages.

[long-pause]

Many Greek wise men travelled to Egypt to learn. Thinkers and mathematicians and historians. A famous Greek traveller called Herodotus spent a long time there. He wrote that the Egyptians had kept records longer than any other people. The priests showed him statues of their high priests. One after another. Three hundred and forty-five of them, father to son, he wrote. All the way back to the beginning.

[pause]

So when a Greek heard that a story came from the Egyptian priests, he took it seriously. It sounded old and true. Like something only the oldest people in the world would know.

[pause]

{img:greek-travellers-visiting-egyptian-temple-awe-columns-priests}
Did the priests at Saïs really tell Solon about Atlantis? Nobody knows. No Egyptian writing about Atlantis has ever been found. And the Egyptians wrote down a great deal. If such a story existed, you might expect to find it somewhere. But there is nothing.

[long-pause]

Some people think Plato made up the Egyptian part. To give his story a feeling of old truth. Writers do that. Even today, stories often begin with an old manuscript found in an attic. Or a tale told by a mysterious stranger. It makes the story feel real.

[pause]

{img:old-scroll-unrolled-mysterious-text-lamp-dark-room-ancient}
Others think there might be a real old story behind it. Changed and mixed up as it passed from mouth to mouth. Over two hundred years. From Egyptian to Greek. From grandfather to grandson. Perhaps a memory of a real island and a real disaster. Grown bigger and stranger with every telling.

[pause]

We will come back to that question. But first, let us hear what Plato says Atlantis was like. Because he describes it in wonderful detail.

[long-pause]

Either way, the story worked. For more than two thousand years, people have wondered whether those old priests in Saïs really kept a secret about a lost island. Somewhere in a dusty temple archive, written on papyrus, now crumbled to dust. If it ever existed, it is gone. And so we only have Plato's words.

[long-pause]

{img:poseidon-god-sea-trident-island-shore-waves-mythic-calm}
In the beginning, Plato writes, the gods shared out the earth among themselves. Each god received a land to care for. And the god Poseidon received the island of Atlantis.

[pause]

Poseidon is the Greek god of the sea. He rules the waves and the storms. He also causes earthquakes. When the earth shakes, the Greeks say, it is Poseidon striking it with his great three-pointed spear, the trident. And he loves horses. Some old stories say he made the first horse.

[long-pause]

On the island of Atlantis, near the middle of the coast, there was a wide plain. It was the most beautiful plain in the world, Plato says. Very fertile. And at the edge of the plain, about nine kilometres from the sea, there was a low hill.

[pause]

On this hill lived a man and a woman who had been born from the earth. They had one daughter. Her name was Cleito. When she was young, her parents passed away. And she lived alone on the hill.

[pause]

{img:cleito-young-woman-hill-island-flowers-alone-gentle-light}
Poseidon saw her. And he fell in love with her. He married her. And he wanted to keep her safe. So he made the hill into a protected place. He did it with his great power. He made rings around the hill. Rings of land and rings of water, one inside the other. Two rings of land and three rings of water. Perfectly round. Like the rings on a target.

[long-pause]

No ship could reach the hill. In those days, Plato writes, there were no ships yet and no sailors. So the hill was safe from everyone. An island inside an island.

[pause]

Then Poseidon made two springs come up out of the ground on the hill. One of warm water. And one of cold water. And he made the earth grow every kind of food in plenty.

[pause]

{img:two-springs-hot-cold-water-hill-steam-garden-mythic-island}
Poseidon and Cleito had children. Five pairs of twin boys. Ten sons. When they grew up, Poseidon divided the island into ten parts. One for each son. The eldest son got the best part, around the hill with the rings. He was made king over the others. His name was Atlas. And from his name, the whole island was called Atlantis. And the ocean around it, the Atlantic.

[long-pause]

Plato tells us the names of the other sons too. Strange old names. Gadeirus, the twin of Atlas, who ruled the part of the island nearest the Pillars of Heracles. Ampheres and Euaemon. Mneseus and Autochthon. Elasippus and Mestor. Azaes and Diaprepes. Plato says he translated them into Greek from the Egyptian, which had translated them from the language of Atlantis. Names passed through three languages, across thousands of years. Like whispers down a very long line.

[long-pause]

{img:atlantis-concentric-rings-water-land-bridges-aerial-view-mythic}
The children of Atlas and his brothers ruled Atlantis for many generations. And they turned Poseidon's rings into a great city.

[pause]

The rings of water became canals. Wide enough for ships. The kings dug a great canal from the sea, straight through the land, to the outer ring of water. It was about ninety metres wide and very deep. Big ships could sail right in from the ocean. And from the outer ring, they could sail on to the inner rings.

[long-pause]

The rings of land had walls around them. Over the rings of water, the kings built bridges. And under the bridges, there were tunnels, so ships could pass from one ring to the next. Each tunnel had a roof over it. So the ships sailed under the land, as if into a cave.

[pause]

Plato gives exact sizes. The outer ring of water was about five hundred metres wide. The ring of land inside it was the same. The next ring of water was about three hundred and fifty metres. And so on, getting smaller toward the centre. The island in the middle, with Poseidon's hill, was about nine hundred metres across.

[pause]

{img:ships-sailing-through-covered-tunnel-under-land-ring-atlantis}
The walls around the rings were made of stone. Stone of three colours, Plato says. White and black and red. It was dug up from under the island itself. And the stone was used in patterns. Some walls were plain. Others were made in stripes and patterns of colour, for pleasure. Beautiful to look at.

[long-pause]

And the walls were covered in metal. The outer wall was covered with bronze. It shone like gold in the sun. The next wall was covered with tin. Silver and grey. And the wall around the central island was covered with a metal called orichalcum. Plato says it glowed like fire. Red and gold.

[pause]

{img:walls-bronze-tin-orichalcum-gleaming-sunset-rings-water-city}
Imagine sailing into the city from the sea. Down the great canal. Through the first tunnel. Past the shining bronze wall. Through the next tunnel. Past the silver wall. And then, ahead, the central island with its walls glowing red like fire. And above them, rising on the hill, the great temple of Poseidon. No wonder people have dreamed about it for two thousand years.

[long-pause]

{img:atlantis-city-central-island-palace-temple-gardens-canals-sunny}
On the central island, in the middle of all the rings, stood the palace of the kings.

[pause]

It was built where the house of Poseidon and Cleito had been. Each new king tried to make it even more beautiful than the king before. So over the generations, it grew and grew. Plato says it became a wonder to look at. So big and so beautiful.

[long-pause]

Around the palace were gardens. Thanks to the hot and cold springs, there was always fresh water. The kings built pools and baths. Some baths were open to the sky. Some were indoors, for winter. There were separate baths for the kings, for ordinary people, for women. And even baths for the horses and the cattle. The water ran from the baths into the gardens. Trees of every kind grew there. Tall and beautiful in the rich wet soil.

[pause]

On the rings of land, there were temples to many gods. There were gardens and places for exercise. Places where young men ran and wrestled. And on the larger ring, there was a racetrack. A great track for racing horses, going all the way around the island. Remember, Poseidon loves horses. So the people of Atlantis love horses too.

[pause]

{img:atlantis-horse-racetrack-ring-island-crowds-chariots-mythic}
The guards of the king lived on the rings. The most trusted ones lived on the inner ring, closest to the palace. The kings could always call on them.

[long-pause]

And beyond the rings, where the great canal reached the sea, there were the harbours. Full of ships. Big warships and trading ships. And there were docks where ships were built and repaired. Plato says the harbours were so crowded that day and night you could hear the noise of people. Shouting and talking and working and trading.

[pause]

{img:atlantis-harbours-crowded-ships-docks-workers-noise-busy-sea}
Around the whole city, there was one more wall. A great wall that went in a circle around everything, about nine kilometres from the centre. It started at the sea and came back to the sea. Inside it, there were houses. Thousands of houses, packed close together. And people from all over the world.

[long-pause]

Plato tells it all with such detail. The sizes. The colours. The baths for horses. It feels as if he had been there. Maybe that is why so many people believe it was real. A made-up city usually is not described so carefully. Or maybe Plato was simply a very good storyteller.

[pause]

{img:student-imagining-city-eyes-closed-lamp-academy-night-dreaming}
At the Academy, the young student from the island has stopped breathing for a moment. He is imagining it all. The rings. The shining walls. The ships. He can almost hear the harbour. He opens his eyes. Around him, the other students are quiet too. The reader goes on.

[long-pause]

{img:temple-of-poseidon-atlantis-silver-walls-gold-pinnacles-hill}
At the very centre of everything stood the temple of Poseidon.

[pause]

It was built on the old hill where Cleito had lived. It was a holy place. Ordinary people could not go in. There was a smaller shrine for Cleito and Poseidon too. Surrounded by a wall of gold. That was where the ten sons were born. Every year, people brought gifts there from all ten kingdoms.

[long-pause]

The great temple was huge. About one hundred and eighty metres long and ninety metres wide. And very tall. Plato says it looked a little foreign. Not quite like a Greek temple. More strange and grand.

[pause]

The outside of the temple was covered with silver. All of it. Except the pointed decorations on the roof. Those were covered with gold. So in the sun, the temple shone silver and gold on top of its hill. You could see it from far out at sea.

[pause]

{img:temple-interior-ivory-ceiling-gold-poseidon-statue-chariot-horses}
Inside, the ceiling was made of ivory. White and smooth. Decorated with gold and silver and orichalcum. The walls and the columns and the floor were covered with orichalcum. Glowing red and gold.

[long-pause]

And in the middle stood a statue of Poseidon. Made of gold. So tall that his head touched the ceiling. He stood in a chariot pulled by six winged horses. Around him, riding on dolphins, were a hundred sea nymphs. Spirits of the sea. And around the outside of the temple stood golden statues of all the kings and queens of Atlantis. Rows and rows of them.

[pause]

There was also a great altar. Fit for such a temple, Plato says. And the palace beside it was just as grand.

[pause]

{img:golden-statues-kings-queens-around-temple-silver-gleaming-dusk}
It is a dazzling picture. Silver and gold and ivory. Golden horses with wings. A god whose head touches the ceiling. It sounds like a dream. And perhaps that is exactly what it is. A dream of a perfect, wealthy, shining city. Too perfect, maybe. Because in Plato's stories, things that are too perfect and too proud often come to a bad end.

[long-pause]

The young student at the Academy tries to picture the golden Poseidon. Six winged horses. A hundred sea nymphs on dolphins. He has seen the great temple of Athena in Athens, with its tall statue of gold and ivory. He thinks Poseidon's statue must have been like that, only bigger. Much bigger. He shakes his head in wonder. Could people really have made such a thing?

[long-pause]

{img:atlantis-merchant-ships-arriving-goods-amphorae-quay-busy-morning}
Atlantis was rich. Very rich. Where did all its wealth come from?

[pause]

From the island itself, Plato says. And from trade. The island had everything. It had metals, dug from under the ground. Gold and silver and copper and tin. And orichalcum, which they dug from the earth in many places. In Plato's day, nobody knew what orichalcum was. It was only a name. A precious metal, valued more than anything except gold.

[long-pause]

There were forests full of good timber for building houses and ships. There were wild animals of every kind. Even elephants, Plato says. Lots of them. Because the island had food for all animals, big and small.

[pause]

There were sweet-smelling plants. Roots and herbs and flowers and fruits and nuts. There were fruits you could eat and fruits you could drink and fruits that gave oil. Everything grew there, in plenty. The sun shone. The springs flowed. The earth gave and gave.

[pause]

{img:elephants-in-lush-forest-island-atlantis-river-mythic-abundant}
And what the island did not have, came by sea. Ships from Atlantis sailed everywhere. To the islands around them. To the great continent on the other side of the ocean. And through the Pillars into the inner sea. They brought back goods from every land. The harbours were full of foreign ships too. Traders from everywhere came to buy and sell.

[long-pause]

Plato says the kings of Atlantis had more wealth than any kings before them. Or any kings after them. They had everything they could want. Food and gold and ships and power.

[pause]

{img:atlantis-shipyard-builders-hull-timber-workers-sun-harbour}
The young student at the Academy thinks about his own island at home. It is small and rocky. The goats eat thorny bushes. The fishermen go out every day in little boats. Nobody is rich. But everyone knows everyone. And everyone helps everyone. He wonders. Would he rather live in Atlantis? With all its gold? Or at home? He is not sure. He decides to keep listening.

[long-pause]

Plato does not say much about the ordinary people of Atlantis. But we can imagine the harbour at dawn. Fishermen hauling nets. Sailors coiling ropes. Traders arguing over prices in a dozen languages. Children running between bales and barrels. The smell of tar and salt and fresh bread. And over it all, on the hill, the silver temple catching the first light of the sun.

[long-pause]

{img:atlantis-great-plain-fields-canals-grid-mountains-behind-farms}
Behind the city of Atlantis lay the great plain.

[pause]

It stretched far inland from the sea. It was flat and smooth and very fertile. And it was shaped like a long rectangle. About five hundred kilometres long and about three hundred and fifty kilometres wide. On three sides, mountains surrounded it. High and beautiful, with many villages and rivers and lakes and meadows. The mountains protected the plain from the cold north wind. The plain faced south, toward the sun.

[long-pause]

All around the plain, the people dug a great ditch. A canal so wide and deep that it was hard to believe. Plato says himself that it sounds too big to be true. But he tells us anyway. It was about thirty metres deep and almost two hundred metres wide. And it went all the way around the plain. About two thousand kilometres long.

[pause]

Water from the mountain rivers flowed into this great canal. Then smaller canals went straight across the plain, in a grid, like the lines on a chess board. From one side to the other. The water flowed through them to all the fields. And at the end, it flowed out to the sea near the city.

[pause]

{img:farmers-atlantis-plain-harvest-two-crops-canals-boats-timber}
With all this water, the farmers could grow two harvests a year. In winter, the rain watered the fields. In summer, the canals did. And the canals were roads too. Boats carried wood from the mountains and crops from the fields down to the city.

[long-pause]

The plain was divided into thousands of plots. Each one with a leader. And each leader had to provide soldiers for the king. Chariots and horses and archers and sailors. Atlantis had a huge army and twelve hundred ships. Plato counts them all. Carefully. As if reading from a record.

[pause]

{img:atlantis-plain-evening-villages-canals-golden-light-mountains}
For the people living on the plain, life was mostly about farming. Ploughing and sowing and harvesting. Herding cattle. Tending gardens. It was a good life in a good land. In the evening, they sat outside their houses and watched the sun set over the canals. The water turned gold. Boats drifted home. And far away, on the coast, the silver temple of Poseidon caught the last of the light.

[long-pause]

In autumn, after the harvest, the farmers of the plain hold a feast. They bring the first fruits to the shrines in their villages. They thank the gods for the water and the sun. Young people dance in the squares. Old people sit and watch and remember other harvests. And the boats on the canals are decorated with flowers, as they carry the grain down to the city by the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:ten-kings-atlantis-gathered-temple-robes-crowns-council-solemn}
Atlantis was ruled by ten kings. One for each of the ten parts of the island. Each king ruled his own part. But they were all joined by one law. The law of Poseidon.

[pause]

The eldest line, the family of Atlas, ruled the central part. They were the most important. The other nine kings were like their brothers. Equal in some ways. But they all looked to the king of the centre.

[long-pause]

Poseidon's law was carved on a pillar of orichalcum. It stood in the middle of the island, in the temple of Poseidon. It told the kings how to rule. They must never make war on each other. If anyone attacked one of them, the others must all help. They must make decisions together, in council. And the law said something else. No king could put any of his own people to death without the agreement of more than half of the ten kings.

[pause]

Every five or six years, the ten kings met at the temple. They took turns. Once after five years. Once after six. Plato says this was to show respect equally to odd and even numbers. They met to talk about what each king had done. To judge whether anyone had broken the law. And to make decisions for the whole island together.

[pause]

{img:orichalcum-pillar-carved-law-temple-glowing-red-gold-lamplight}
In the early days, Plato says, the kings were good and wise. They were part god, because they were descended from Poseidon. They cared little for their gold. They did not let wealth make them drunk with pride. They were kind to each other and to their people. They knew that goodness and friendship mattered more than riches.

[long-pause]

And so for a long time, Atlantis was happy. Its people were rich and safe. Its kings were wise. Its laws were good. Everything went well.

[pause]

{img:peaceful-atlantis-people-markets-gardens-harmony-golden-afternoon}
But Plato tells us there was a danger hidden inside this happiness. The part of the kings that came from the god was slowly getting smaller. Every generation, the god in them grew a little weaker. And the human part grew a little stronger. Little by little, without anyone quite noticing.

[long-pause]

Plato is careful here. He does not say the kings became bad overnight. He says it happened slowly. So slowly that nobody noticed. A little more gold here. A little more pride there. A small unkindness, forgotten the next day. Until one day the kings looked in the mirror and did not see what had changed. That, perhaps, is the most worrying part of the story. Because it could happen to anyone.

[long-pause]

{img:sacred-bulls-grazing-temple-grounds-atlantis-evening-calm}
When the ten kings met at the temple of Poseidon, there was a special ceremony. It was about bulls.

[pause]

In the grounds of the temple, there were bulls that roamed free. They were sacred to Poseidon. Nobody could harm them. Except once, at the meeting of the kings.

[long-pause]

Plato describes it carefully. The ten kings went into the sacred grounds alone. No guards. No servants. No weapons of iron. Only wooden staffs and ropes. They prayed to the god. And then they tried to catch one of the bulls with their hands and ropes. It was a test. Not of strength alone. But of whether the god was willing. If the god was pleased, they would catch a bull.

[pause]

When they caught one, they led it to the pillar of orichalcum, where the law was written. They offered it to the god there. We will leave the details gently aside. Then they filled a great bowl with a mix of wine and the offering. They each took a cup from the bowl. And they made a promise.

[pause]

{img:ten-kings-golden-cups-oath-pillar-night-fire-solemn-promise}
They promised to judge fairly, according to the law on the pillar. To never break the law. To never obey any king who broke it. They drank from their golden cups. And they placed the cups in the temple, as a sign of their promise.

[long-pause]

Then, as night fell, they put on beautiful dark blue robes. They sat on the ground by the dying fire of the altar. They put out all the other lights in the temple. And there, in the dark, they judged each other. If any king had broken the law, it was decided that night. At dawn, they wrote down their judgements on golden tablets. And they hung their robes in the temple, as a memory.

[pause]

{img:kings-dark-blue-robes-sitting-dark-temple-dying-embers-night-judging}
It is a strange and solemn picture. Ten kings in dark blue robes, sitting in the dark, judging each other by the glow of embers. Many people think Plato was thinking of old ceremonies he had heard of. On the island of Crete, there were old stories about bulls and kings. We will come back to Crete later. It may be an important clue.

[long-pause]

The young student from the island has heard of Crete. His grandfather once sailed there. He told stories of huge ruined palaces with red columns, older than anyone could remember. Of painted walls with leaping bulls. The student thinks about it. Maybe, he thinks, there is a little bit of truth hidden in Plato's story after all. Like a pearl in an oyster.

[long-pause]

{img:atlantis-dawn-harbour-fishermen-boats-temple-silver-gleaming-hill}
Plato tells us about the kings and the temples and the canals. But he does not tell us about ordinary people. So let us imagine. What might a day in Atlantis have been like?

[pause]

It is early morning in the outer city. The sun rises over the sea. Its first light touches the silver temple on the hill at the centre. It shines like a star. Down by the harbour, fishermen are already coming in with their catch. Gulls scream and circle. The market is opening.

[long-pause]

A girl called Nerea lives in a house near the harbour. Her father is a ship's carpenter. Her mother weaves sails. Nerea is twelve. Every morning she goes to the warm spring baths with her mother. It is the best part of the day. The water is warm and soft and smells a little of minerals. Women chat and children splash. Then they walk home through the streets as the city wakes up.

[pause]

{img:girl-and-mother-public-warm-baths-steam-morning-mosaic-pool}
Her father goes to the shipyard. He is building a big new ship for trade. It will sail through the Pillars to the inner sea. Nerea sometimes brings him his lunch. She loves the shipyard. The smell of fresh wood and tar. The sound of hammers and saws. The great half-built ships like the skeletons of whales.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, there are horse races on the great track. Nerea and her friends run to watch from the wall. The horses are beautiful. Their manes fly in the wind. The crowd roars. Nerea always cheers for the grey horses. They are her favourite.

[pause]

{img:children-watching-horse-race-wall-atlantis-cheering-afternoon}
In the evening, the family eats on the roof of their house. Fish and bread and olives and fruit. From the roof, they can see the rings of the city. The walls glowing bronze and silver and red in the setting sun. The temple on its hill. The ships in the harbour. The great plain behind, turning gold.

[long-pause]

Nerea asks her father, will Atlantis always be here? He laughs. Of course, he says. It has been here for a thousand years. It will be here for a thousand more. The god protects us. And she believes him. Because how could anything so big and beautiful ever disappear?

[pause]

{img:family-rooftop-dinner-sunset-view-rings-walls-glowing-atlantis}
But Nerea is only a girl we have imagined. And Plato's story is not finished. He has one more thing to tell us. And it is not about gold or horses or ships. It is about what happens when people forget what really matters.

[long-pause]

{img:atlantis-kings-feast-excess-gold-everywhere-proud-faces-palace}
For many generations, the kings of Atlantis were good. But slowly, things changed.

[pause]

The part of them that came from the god grew smaller. Mixed with ordinary human nature, again and again, Plato says. Until the human part was strongest. And then they could no longer carry their great wealth wisely. They began to behave badly.

[long-pause]

To people who could see clearly, Plato writes, the kings now looked ugly. Because they had lost the most precious things of all. Goodness and wisdom and friendship. But to people who could not see clearly, the kings looked more wonderful than ever. Richer and more powerful and more splendid. They were filled with greed and the wish for power. And they did not even know it.

[pause]

They wanted more. More land. More gold. More people under their rule. Atlantis was not enough anymore. They looked across the sea at the lands of the inner sea. At Greece and Egypt and all the rest. And they wanted it all.

[pause]

{img:atlantis-fleet-gathering-harbour-many-warships-dark-clouds-gathering}
Then Zeus, the king of the gods, saw what was happening. He saw that a noble family had fallen into a sad state. And he wanted to help them become wise again. To teach them a lesson. So he called all the gods together. In his great home in the middle of the heavens. From where you can look down on everything that happens on earth.

[long-pause]

And when they had all gathered, Zeus spoke to them. He said...

[pause]

And there, Plato's book stops.

[pause]

{img:unfinished-scroll-ending-mid-sentence-lamp-dark-desk-mystery}
That is really how it ends. In the middle of a sentence. Zeus is about to speak. And then nothing. The book was never finished. Nobody knows why. Maybe Plato grew too old. Maybe he got busy with another book. Maybe he lost interest. Or maybe the rest was lost. We will never know what Zeus said.

[long-pause]

At the Academy, the reader stops too. The students look at each other. Is that it? Is there no more? The young student from the island feels a strange disappointment. He wanted to know. But in his other book, Plato has already told us how it ends. In one day and one night. The sea.

[pause]

People have tried to guess what Zeus said. Some have written their own endings. In some, Zeus sends the earthquake as a punishment. In others, he gives the kings one last chance, and they do not take it. In others still, he speaks gently, like a father. But Plato's own words end in silence. And perhaps that silence is the most mysterious part of all.

[long-pause]

{img:atlantis-sky-darkening-sea-rising-gentle-mythic-distant-island}
The end of Atlantis is told in just a few words. In Plato's other book. The one the Egyptian priest told Solon.

[pause]

Later, says the priest, there were great earthquakes and floods. And in one terrible day and night, the island of Atlantis sank into the sea and disappeared.

[long-pause]

That is all. One sentence. But people have imagined it ever since. We will imagine it gently. From far away.

[pause]

Perhaps it began with the earth trembling. A small shaking at first. People stopped and looked at each other. Then went on with their day. Earthquakes happened sometimes. Poseidon was restless. That was all.

[pause]

Then the sea went strange. It pulled back from the harbours. The ships were left lying on the mud. Fish flapped on the sand. People came down to look. They had never seen anything like it.

[pause]

{img:people-on-hills-watching-sea-from-distance-dusk-ships-sailing-away}
And the old people remembered the stories. When the sea pulls back, run to the hills. Some ran. Some took to their ships and sailed away. Some stayed and wondered. And then the sea came back. And it did not stop.

[long-pause]

We will leave Atlantis there. In the dark, under the waves. Its silver temple and its golden statues. Its rings and its canals. Its horses and its ships. Everything gone, in one day and one night. As if it had never been.

[pause]

{img:calm-sea-morning-after-empty-horizon-gulls-soft-light-peace}
And the next morning, the sun rose over a calm sea. Gulls flew over the water where the island had been. And there was nothing there but waves. Plato says that for a long time afterwards, the sea there was hard to cross. Because of the mud just below the surface. Where the island had sunk.

[long-pause]

Did anyone survive? Plato does not say. But people have always liked to imagine that some did. That some ships got away in time. That some people fled to the mountains of other lands. And carried with them the memory of their lost home. Stories have been told about it ever since. Of the people who escaped. And of the island under the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:aristotle-young-student-academy-arguing-plato-garden-olive}
So was Atlantis real?

[pause]

Even in Plato's own school, people did not agree. One of Plato's students was a young man from the north of Greece. His name was Aristotle. He studied at the Academy for twenty years. He became one of the greatest thinkers in history. And he did not believe in Atlantis.

[long-pause]

Old writers tell us what Aristotle said, or something like it. The man who made it up, made it sink. In other words, Plato invented Atlantis. And then he made it disappear, so nobody could ever check. Very clever.

[pause]

Many people today agree with Aristotle. They think Plato invented Atlantis as a lesson. Plato liked to teach with stories. And the story of Atlantis has a clear lesson. A people that becomes too rich and too proud will fall. Goodness and wisdom matter more than gold. A small brave city that does what is right can stand against a great proud power.

[pause]

{img:ancient-athens-navy-triremes-harbour-piraeus-proud-city}
Plato may have been thinking about his own city. Athens had once been brave and good. It had stood against the great empire of Persia and won. But then Athens became rich and powerful. It built a great navy. It ruled over other Greek cities. It grew proud. And then it lost a long terrible war. Plato saw all this happen. Maybe Atlantis is a picture of what happens to a city like that. A warning.

[long-pause]

But not everyone agreed with Aristotle. A few hundred years later, another Greek thinker wrote that the story might be true. And some people said they had travelled to Egypt and seen the old records themselves. Though nobody could ever show them.

[pause]

{img:scholars-debating-library-alexandria-scrolls-shelves-columns}
And so the arguing began. In Plato's own school. Between his students. And it has never stopped. For two thousand four hundred years, people have taken sides. Was it real? Was it a lesson? Was it a bit of both? A lesson built on an old, half-remembered true story?

[long-pause]

The young student from the island walks home that night under the stars. He thinks about it. He thinks Aristotle is probably right. It is a made-up story. With a lesson. And yet. He thinks about the old fishermen at home. Their stories of islands that rose from the sea. And sank again. Of the sea pulling back before a great wave. He is not quite sure.

[pause]

The next day, he asks his teacher about it. The old man thinks for a moment. Then he says, a story does not have to be true to be true. The student does not understand. Not yet. It will take him many years. But he never forgets those words.

[long-pause]

{img:old-plato-white-beard-walking-academy-garden-evening-alone}
Plato wrote the story of Atlantis near the end of his long life.

[pause]

He was about seventy-five then. He had seen a lot. He had seen his teacher Socrates condemned by the people of Athens. He had travelled to Egypt, some people say, and to Italy and to the island of Sicily. In Sicily, he tried to help a young ruler become a wise king. It went badly. He was even sold as a slave for a time. Friends had to buy his freedom. He came home to Athens disappointed. But not bitter.

[long-pause]

At the Academy, he went on teaching and writing. His later books are about how a good city should be run. About laws and justice. About what makes people good or bad. And the story of Atlantis is part of these thoughts. It was meant to be the start of a series of books. One about the beginning of the world. One about Atlantis and ancient Athens. And a third about something else. But only the first was finished. The second stops in the middle. The third was never written.

[pause]

Why did Plato stop? Some think he started on a much bigger book instead. His last book is very long. It is called the Laws. It is about how to make good laws for a new city. He worked on it until he passed away.

[pause]

{img:plato-desk-many-scrolls-final-years-lamp-writing-slowly}
Plato passed away when he was about eighty. The story goes that he died peacefully, at a wedding feast. Or perhaps while writing. His students buried him in the garden of the Academy. Among the olive trees where he had taught for forty years.

[long-pause]

The Academy went on after him. For hundreds of years. Teachers came and went. Students came from everywhere. It was one of the longest-lasting schools in history. And the word academy is still used for schools and places of learning all over the world. Because of that garden of olive trees outside Athens.

[pause]

{img:academy-ruins-athens-today-low-stones-olive-trees-quiet-later}
And Plato's books survived. Copied by hand, again and again, for nearly two thousand years. All of them. Every one he wrote. That is very rare for an ancient writer. Including the two books with the story of Atlantis. The story Plato never finished. The story that has puzzled the world ever since.

[long-pause]

{img:thera-santorini-bronze-age-island-round-volcano-town-sea-before}
If Atlantis was not completely made up, what could it be? People have looked for answers in many places. One of the most exciting is an island in the Aegean Sea. Today it is called Santorini. In ancient times it was called Thera.

[long-pause]

Santorini is a beautiful island today. White houses with blue doors cling to the top of high cliffs. Below them, there is a great bay of deep blue water. In the middle of the bay there are small dark islands. Visitors come from all over the world to watch the sunset there.

[pause]

But that bay was not always there. Long ago, Thera was a round island. With a volcano in the middle. And around it, people lived and farmed and fished. It was a green and pleasant place. With a busy harbour town.

[pause]

{img:volcano-smoke-rising-thera-island-people-boats-leaving-calm-sea}
Then, about three thousand six hundred years ago, the volcano woke up. First there were small earthquakes. Then smoke. The people saw the signs. And it seems they left in time. Because archaeologists have found their town, but almost no people. They took their valuable things. And they sailed away.

[long-pause]

Then the volcano erupted. It was one of the biggest eruptions in the history of humans. Ash and stone shot high into the sky. The ash spread across the whole sea. It fell on islands far away. It is found in the ground on Crete and in Turkey and even in Egypt. The sky went dark for days.

[pause]

{img:great-ash-cloud-over-aegean-sea-distant-islands-dim-sun-gentle}
And the middle of the island collapsed. The volcano emptied itself and then fell in. The sea rushed into the great hole. Where there had been a round island with a mountain, there was now a ring of cliffs around a deep bay. And waves raced out across the sea toward other islands.

[long-pause]

Doesn't that sound like Atlantis? A round island. Rings of land and water. Earthquakes. A great disaster. Part of the island sinking into the sea. Many people have thought so. And the bay of Santorini today looks a little like a ring of land around a ring of water.

[pause]

{img:santorini-caldera-today-white-houses-cliffs-blue-bay-sunset-later}
But there are problems. Plato says Atlantis was beyond the Pillars of Heracles, in the outer ocean. Thera is inside the inner sea. Plato says Atlantis was huge. Thera is small. Plato says it happened nine thousand years before Solon. Thera erupted about a thousand years before Solon. Some people think the numbers got mixed up as the story was told and retold. Perhaps nine hundred became nine thousand. Who knows?

[long-pause]

{img:akrotiri-excavation-covered-roof-houses-ash-streets-archaeologists}
On the island of Santorini, under the ash, archaeologists found a whole town. It is called Akrotiri.

[pause]

In 1967, a Greek archaeologist called Spyridon Marinatos began to dig there. He had a theory. He believed the eruption of Thera might be linked to the story of Atlantis. And he hoped to find something under the ash. He did.

[long-pause]

Under many metres of ash and pumice, there was a town. Streets and squares. Houses two and three storeys high. Stairs and doorways and windows. Store rooms full of big clay jars. Beds and tables, preserved as moulds in the ash. It was like a town frozen in time. Like Pompeii in Italy, but much older.

[pause]

And on the walls, there were paintings. Beautiful, bright paintings. They were found in pieces and carefully put together again. And they are some of the loveliest paintings of the ancient world.

[pause]

{img:akrotiri-fresco-spring-swallows-lilies-red-rocks-bright-colours}
One room is painted all around with spring. Red rocks and lilies. And swallows. Pairs of swallows flying and swooping and kissing in the air. It is so happy and full of life. Another painting shows two boys boxing, wearing just a single glove each. Another shows a fisherman holding up strings of fish. Another shows antelopes. And another shows monkeys.

[long-pause]

And one long painting shows a fleet of ships. They sail from one town to another. People watch from the windows and the roofs. Dolphins leap around the ships. It is a festival, maybe. A celebration of the sea. Some people looked at it and thought of the ships of Atlantis.

[pause]

{img:akrotiri-ship-fresco-fleet-dolphins-towns-people-watching-ancient}
The people of Akrotiri were rich. They traded across the sea. Their houses had toilets and drains. Their walls had paintings. They lived well. And then the volcano warned them. And they left. Where did they go? Nobody knows. Perhaps to Crete, the big island to the south. Perhaps further.

[long-pause]

Marinatos dug at Akrotiri for many years. He loved it. He passed away there in an accident, in the middle of his work. He is buried there, in the town he found. Archaeologists still dig at Akrotiri today. They have uncovered only a small part of it. There is much more under the ash. Waiting.

[pause]

Visitors can walk through the excavated streets today, under a great roof that protects them from the sun and rain. It is cool and quiet in there. You walk past doorways and staircases and big storage jars still standing where they were left. And you can almost hear the voices of the people who lived there, three and a half thousand years ago. Calling to each other across the street. Laughing. Packing up in a hurry, and sailing away.

[long-pause]

{img:knossos-palace-crete-red-columns-courtyards-minoan-people-sunny}
South of Santorini lies a big island. Crete. And on Crete, long ago, lived a people we now call the Minoans.

[pause]

The Minoans built great palaces. The biggest was at Knossos. It was huge and complicated. Hundreds of rooms on several floors. Courtyards and staircases and corridors and light wells. Store rooms with giant clay jars. It was so complicated that some people think it gave rise to the old Greek story of the labyrinth. A maze where nobody could find their way out.

[long-pause]

The Minoans were a people of the sea. Their ships sailed everywhere. To Egypt and to the islands and to Greece. They were rich from trade. Their towns did not have big walls. Maybe they did not need them. The sea was their wall. And their ships were their protection.

[pause]

And the Minoans loved bulls. Bulls appear everywhere in their art. There are paintings of young men and women leaping over charging bulls. Grabbing the horns and somersaulting over the bull's back. It is not clear if this really happened or was a kind of dream. But it looks amazing. Bulls' horns were carved in stone on the walls of the palaces. Cups were shaped like bulls' heads.

[pause]

{img:minoan-bull-leaping-fresco-acrobat-somersault-bull-colours-knossos}
Remember the ceremony of the kings of Atlantis? The bulls roaming free in the temple. The kings catching them without weapons. Some people think this is a faint memory of the Minoans and their bulls.

[long-pause]

The Minoans were also great painters. Their walls show dolphins and fish and lilies and birds. Ladies with curly hair and fine dresses. Princes with feathered crowns. Their art is full of joy and nature and movement.

[pause]

{img:minoan-dolphins-fresco-blue-sea-fish-queens-room-knossos-light}
And then, the Minoans faded. After the eruption of Thera, their world was damaged. Ash fell on their fields. Waves hit their coasts. Their ships may have been destroyed. They went on for a time. But they grew weaker. Later, people from mainland Greece took over their palaces. And slowly, the Minoan world disappeared. Forgotten for three thousand years.

[long-pause]

Until about a hundred and twenty years ago, when a British archaeologist called Arthur Evans dug at Knossos. He found the palace and its paintings. He gave the people their name, the Minoans, after a king in an old Greek story. And people began to wonder. Could the Minoans be the people of Atlantis? A rich sea people on an island. With bulls and palaces and ships. Who were struck by a terrible disaster from the sea. Some people believe it still.

[long-pause]

{img:helike-greek-town-gulf-corinth-temple-poseidon-coast-373bc}
There is another sunken city that Plato may have known about. Because it sank in his own lifetime.

[pause]

Its name was Helike. It was a Greek town on the north coast of the Peloponnese. On the shore of a long narrow sea called the Gulf of Corinth. It was a proud old town. And it had a famous temple. A temple of Poseidon. With a great bronze statue of the god.

[long-pause]

In the winter of the year 373 before Christ, when Plato was about fifty years old, something terrible happened. One night, there was a great earthquake. And then a huge wave came from the sea. In one night, Helike was gone. The town sank. The sea covered it. In one night.

[pause]

For hundreds of years afterwards, travellers came to see the place. Greek and Roman writers say that you could still see the ruins under the water. The tops of the walls. The bronze statue of Poseidon standing on the sea floor. Fishermen complained that their nets got caught on it.

[pause]

{img:ruins-under-shallow-water-statue-faint-sunlight-rays-fishermen-boat}
Plato must have heard about it. Everyone in Greece did. A whole town, with a temple of Poseidon, swallowed by the sea in one night. It must have made a deep impression. Some people think it gave Plato the idea for the end of Atlantis.

[long-pause]

Later, people forgot exactly where Helike was. The ruins were covered by mud and sand. The coast changed. For a long time, archaeologists searched for it. They looked in the sea. They found nothing.

[pause]

{img:archaeologists-digging-helike-olive-groves-coast-trenches-today}
Then, about twenty-five years ago, they found it. But not in the sea. On land. Under fields and olive groves near the coast. The sea had pulled back over the centuries. The lagoon where Helike sank had filled with mud. Now it was dry land. And under the mud, the archaeologists found walls and streets and coins and pots. And even an older town, from an even earlier time. Also buried.

[long-pause]

So Helike was real. A town of Poseidon that sank in one night. Plato knew of it. And perhaps, when he sat down to write about Atlantis, he was thinking of the fishermen's nets catching on a bronze god at the bottom of the sea.

[pause]

Today you can walk among the olive groves near the coast where Helike lay. It looks like ordinary farmland. Peaceful and green, with the blue gulf beyond. Only a few trenches and signs show where the town was found. Under your feet, Poseidon's lost town lies sleeping.

[long-pause]

{img:doggerland-ancient-landscape-hunters-rivers-marsh-deer-north-sea-before}
There is one more lost land. Not in the warm Mediterranean. But in the cold north. Under the North Sea. Between England and the Netherlands.

[pause]

It is called Doggerland.

[long-pause]

About ten thousand years ago, at the end of the last great ice age, the sea was much lower than it is now. A lot of the world's water was frozen in huge sheets of ice. And where the North Sea is today, there was land. Green rolling land with rivers and lakes and marshes and hills. You could walk from England to the Netherlands and Denmark. No boat needed.

[pause]

People lived there. Hunters and fishers and gatherers. They hunted deer and wild boar and aurochs, huge wild cattle. They fished in the rivers. They gathered nuts and berries. They camped by the lakes. It was a rich land. Full of animals and birds and fish.

[pause]

{img:hunter-family-camp-lake-doggerland-fire-reed-hut-evening-mist}
But the ice was melting. Slowly, year by year, the sea rose. Just a little each year. Perhaps not enough to notice in one lifetime. But over hundreds of years, the sea crept in. The marshes became lagoons. The lagoons became bays. Hills became islands. People moved to higher ground. Again and again.

[long-pause]

About eight thousand years ago, there may have been a sudden blow too. A huge underwater landslide off the coast of Norway sent a great wave across the sea. And soon after, the last of Doggerland disappeared under the waves. It was gone.

[pause]

{img:fishing-trawler-net-old-bones-tools-north-sea-grey-later-find}
For a long time, nobody knew. Then fishermen started finding strange things in their nets. Bones of mammoths and lions and woolly rhinos. Old tools made of stone and bone. A spear point carved from antler. They came from the bottom of the North Sea. From a land that had once been there.

[long-pause]

Today, scientists use ships with special equipment to map the sea floor. They can see the old river valleys and lake shores and hills under the mud. They are drawing maps of a country that drowned. Some people call Doggerland Britain's Atlantis. It was not a city of gold. It was something more ordinary. A green land where families lived. Until the sea came. Slowly. And then, completely.

[pause]

Imagine a family living there, long ago. A grandmother who remembered when the lake was a meadow. A father who had to move the camp every few years, further from the water. Children who grew up with the sound of waves where their grandparents had heard only wind in the grass. They did not write anything down. But perhaps they told stories. About the old land, now under the sea. And perhaps, somewhere, faint echoes of those stories still drift through our own.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-writer-desk-maps-books-atlantis-theory-candle-1880s}
After Plato, people kept wondering about Atlantis. But for many centuries, most of them agreed with Aristotle. It was a story. A nice one, with a good lesson. Nothing more.

[pause]

Then, about a hundred and fifty years ago, everything changed.

[long-pause]

In 1882, an American politician called Ignatius Donnelly wrote a book. It was called Atlantis, the Antediluvian World. That means the world before the flood. In it, he said Atlantis was real. It had been a great continent in the middle of the Atlantic Ocean. And it was the mother of all civilisations. From Atlantis, he said, people had sailed to Egypt and to America and everywhere else. That is why the pyramids of Egypt and the pyramids of Mexico look a bit alike, he said. They both came from Atlantis.

[pause]

The book was a huge success. Millions of people read it. And suddenly, everyone was talking about Atlantis again.

[pause]

{img:explorers-ship-searching-ocean-atlantic-sonar-map-early-modern}
After Donnelly, the floodgates opened. People found Atlantis everywhere. In the Atlantic and the Mediterranean. In the Caribbean and the Arctic. In Africa and South America. Under the ice of Antarctica. In Spain and in Ireland and in Sweden. Every few years, someone says they have found it. And every time, it turns out not to be.

[long-pause]

Scientists who study the sea floor say there was never a big continent in the middle of the Atlantic. The ocean floor there is spreading apart, very slowly. New sea floor is made in the middle. There is no room for a sunken continent. And no sign of one.

[pause]

But people keep looking. Because the idea is so wonderful. A lost world. A great civilisation forgotten under the sea. Secrets waiting to be found. It is one of the best stories ever told.

[pause]

{img:diver-underwater-ancient-stones-sea-floor-light-rays-searching}
Atlantis has appeared in countless books and films and games and cartoons. Underwater cities with domes. People with gills. Crystals and lost technology. Mermaids and sea kings. Most of it has nothing to do with Plato at all. But all of it began with his unfinished story. In a garden of olive trees outside Athens.

[long-pause]

Some searchers have been very serious. They have spent years and fortunes. They have hired ships and divers and planes. In the Bahamas, divers found a long line of flat stones under the water. Some called it the road to Atlantis. Geologists looked at it and said it was a natural rock formation. The searchers were disappointed. But they kept searching somewhere else.

[long-pause]

{img:athanasius-kircher-1665-map-atlantis-upside-down-ocean-engraving}
Long before Donnelly, people had already been dreaming about Atlantis. And some of them made beautiful things from those dreams.

[pause]

About three hundred and fifty years ago, a German priest and scholar called Athanasius Kircher drew a map of Atlantis. He was a man who wanted to know everything. Volcanoes and magnets and music and ancient languages. He had himself lowered into the crater of a smoking volcano in Italy, just to look inside.

[long-pause]

His map of Atlantis is famous. It shows a big island in the middle of the ocean, between Africa and Spain on one side, and America on the other. The strange thing is that the map is drawn upside down. South is at the top. People looking at it today often turn it around to understand it. It is a beautiful map. With little ships and waves and sea monsters. But it was all imagination.

[pause]

{img:new-atlantis-bacon-island-utopia-scholars-college-ships-engraving}
A few years before that, an English thinker called Francis Bacon wrote a short book called New Atlantis. It was not about the old Atlantis. It was about a new imaginary island in the Pacific Ocean. A perfect place where everyone was wise and good. And where scientists worked together in a great college to understand nature. To invent new things for the good of everyone.

[long-pause]

Bacon's island had amazing inventions. Machines to fly and to go under water. Ways to make the weather. Ways to talk across long distances. In the year 1627, that all sounded like magic. Today, much of it is real. Bacon dreamed of a world where science would make life better. And his little book inspired real scientists. Some say it helped to start one of the first great science societies in England.

[pause]

{img:scholars-gathered-early-science-society-instruments-candles-room}
So Atlantis did not only inspire dreams of the past. It inspired dreams of the future too. Of better worlds. Of new knowledge. Of places where people live wisely and well. Plato would have liked that. Because in the end, that was what his story was really about. Not gold and ships. But how to live well. And what happens when we forget.

[long-pause]

{img:old-storyteller-fire-children-listening-night-village-sea-behind}
Why has the story of Atlantis lasted so long?

[pause]

Many stories from Plato's time have been forgotten. Many great cities that really existed are only remembered by experts. But everyone has heard of Atlantis. A city that may never have been real. Why?

[long-pause]

Perhaps because it is a story about loss. Everyone knows that feeling. Something beautiful that is gone forever. A childhood home. A person we loved. A place that has changed. Atlantis is like all of those things together. A beautiful world that sank under the waves. And we can never go back.

[pause]

Perhaps because it is a story about pride. About what happens when people become too rich and too greedy and forget what is good. Every age has seen that happen. Every age needs that warning. Plato wrote it for Athens. But it fits every time and every place.

[pause]

{img:sunken-columns-underwater-blue-light-fish-swimming-dreamlike-calm}
And perhaps because it is a mystery. People love a mystery. A question with no answer. Was it real? Where was it? What did Zeus say? Every time we think we know, another possibility appears. Thera. Crete. Helike. Doggerland. Spain. The middle of the ocean. Or only in Plato's head.

[long-pause]

There is also something true behind it. The sea really does take land. Islands really do sink. Towns really have been swallowed by waves. Coasts really do change. Long ago, people really did live on land that is now under the sea. And they really did have to move, as the water rose.

[pause]

{img:coastline-changing-sea-rising-old-village-ruins-shoreline-gentle}
In our own time, the sea is rising again. Slowly. Some low islands and coasts are worried about the future. People are building dykes and walls. Planning and preparing. The old story of Atlantis feels a little closer than it used to. A reminder that the sea is patient and powerful. And that we should look after the world we have.

[long-pause]

But for tonight, Atlantis is a story. A beautiful old story, told by a wise man in a garden under the olive trees. About rings of land and water. About silver temples and golden horses. About kings in dark blue robes, sitting in the dark. And about one day and one night, when the sea came.

[long-pause]

{img:santorini-sunset-today-caldera-white-village-visitors-watching}
Today, people still look for Atlantis. And they still find it everywhere. In books and films and dreams.

[pause]

On Santorini, every evening, thousands of visitors gather on the cliffs to watch the sunset. The sun sinks into the sea behind the dark little islands in the bay. The sky turns gold and pink and purple. The white houses glow. Some of the visitors think about Atlantis. About a round island and a terrible eruption. About a town under the ash with painted swallows on its walls.

[long-pause]

In Athens, you can still visit the place where Plato's Academy stood. It is a quiet park now, in the middle of the busy modern city. There are olive trees again. A few old stones. Children play there. Old people sit on benches. Nobody would guess that the greatest school of the ancient world once stood here. Or that the story of Atlantis was first read aloud here.

[pause]

{img:plato-academy-park-athens-today-olive-trees-benches-evening-later}
And Plato's books are still read everywhere. In schools and universities all over the world. People still argue about what he meant. They still ask the questions he asked. What is justice? What is a good life? How should a city be ruled? And was Atlantis real?

[long-pause]

So we leave Atlantis tonight, as the evening falls.

[pause]

The sun sets over a calm sea. The water turns gold and then silver. Somewhere far out, beyond the Pillars of Heracles, the waves roll gently over the place where an island may once have been. In the garden of the Academy, the lamps are being lit. The old teacher has gone to rest. The students are walking home under the olive trees, still talking. The young student from the island stops by the gate. He looks up at the stars. He thinks about rings of land and water. About a silver temple on a hill. About a girl on a rooftop asking if her city would last forever. And then he smiles, and walks home through the quiet streets of Athens. Somewhere a dog barks. The olive leaves whisper. And the stars shine down on the sleeping city and the dark, calm, endless sea.
