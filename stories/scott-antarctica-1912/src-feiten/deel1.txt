TOPIC: An old whaling ship leaves Wales
===
IMG: terra-nova-cardiff-harbour-1910 | an old black wooden three-masted steam ship in a busy Welsh harbour, crowds on the quay waving hats, gulls overhead, warm summer evening light
HEAD: Scott's great expedition didn't leave from London, but from a coal harbour in Wales.
BODY: On a summer afternoon in June 1910, the docks of Cardiff were full of people. Picture yourself standing on a crowded quay, with coal dust in the air and flags flapping overhead. They stood along the quays and on the decks of other ships. They waved hats and handkerchiefs, and a band played on the shore. Out in the harbour, an old black ship was getting up steam. Smoke rose from her single funnel, and her three tall masts stood against the sky. Why Cardiff, and not London? The answer was coal. Cardiff was one of the busiest coal ports in the world. Its merchants had given the expedition money, and free coal for the long voyage. So the city wanted to say goodbye in style. As the ship moved out, every vessel in the harbour blew its whistle. The noise rolled across the water for a long time. On deck, the young crew climbed into the rigging to wave back. Slowly, the green hills of Wales turned blue behind them, and then faded into the evening haze.
===
IMG: old-wooden-whaling-ship-oak-bow | the thick wooden bow of an old whaling ship pushing through calm grey water, ropes and rigging above, soft evening light
HEAD: The Terra Nova wasn't new, and nobody ever called her beautiful.
BODY: The ship was called the Terra Nova, which means New Land. You'd smell old timber, tar and whale oil as soon as you stepped aboard. Yet she was already old. She had been built in Dundee, in Scotland, in 1884, to hunt whales and seals in the icy seas of the far north. Her hull was made of thick oak. At the bow, the wood was extra strong, so she could push her way through floating ice. She was about a hundred and eighty-seven feet long. That isn't very big for a voyage across half the world. And she smelled, the men said, of whale oil, tar and damp old wood. No amount of scrubbing could ever get the smell out. But Scott had chosen her for a good reason. A ship built for the ice was exactly what he needed. Sleek new ships were fine in warm water. In the frozen south, only a strong old whaler would do. The men soon grew fond of her, smell and all. I've always had a soft spot for her. Some of them would speak of her lovingly for the rest of their lives.
===
IMG: crowded-ship-deck-crates-hay-coal | the crowded deck of an old wooden ship loaded with crates, barrels, bales of hay and sacks of coal, sailors at work, soft sea light
HEAD: Every inch of the deck was crammed with crates, hay and coal.
BODY: The Terra Nova carried everything the men would need for more than two years. You'd have to climb over crates just to reach the rail. There was no shop where they were going, and no way to send for anything they forgot. So the holds were packed with food, tools, clothes and scientific instruments. When the holds were full, the rest went up on deck. Sacks of coal were piled in every corner. Bales of hay stood in rows for the animals that would come aboard later. And lashed down in big wooden boxes were three motor sledges. These were a brand new idea, a kind of small tractor for the snow. Nobody had ever tried them in the far south before. Later, in New Zealand, the crew would also take on board nineteen ponies and more than thirty dogs. Imagine trying to walk across that deck on a rolling sea. You'd step over ropes, squeeze past crates, and duck under swinging lanterns. You'd find a dog asleep on every coil of rope. Somehow, the sailors found their way through it all, day and night.
===
IMG: antarctica-white-coast-distant-mountains | a vast white coastline of ice cliffs and distant snowy mountains under a pale sky, calm dark sea in front, soft evening light
HEAD: In 1910, almost nothing was known about the land they were sailing to.
BODY: Antarctica is a whole continent of ice, sitting around the South Pole. You might think the maps were nearly complete. They were almost blank. It's bigger than the United States and Mexico put together. It's the coldest, windiest and driest place on Earth. Almost all of it lies under a great sheet of ice, in places more than two miles thick. No people live there, and no trees grow there. In winter, the sun stays below the horizon for months. In 1910, only a handful of ships had ever reached its coast. Only a few people had ever stood on its shores. The maps showed long stretches of coast, and then simply empty white space. Nobody knew what lay in the middle. And no one in all of human history had ever reached the South Pole. Can you imagine looking at a map with a blank space in it? For these men, that blank space was the whole reason to go. They wanted to fill it in, step by careful step.
===
IMG: naval-captain-ship-deck-thoughtful | a naval captain of about forty in a dark Royal Navy uniform standing on the deck of a wooden ship, thoughtful face, sea behind him, soft golden light
HEAD: The leader was a quiet, serious naval officer who had been to the ice before.
BODY: His name was Robert Falcon Scott. If you'd met him, you'd have noticed his steady, thoughtful eyes. He was forty-two years old and a captain in the Royal Navy. He was of medium height, with a strong square face and clear blue eyes. People who knew him said he was thoughtful and determined. He could also be moody, and sometimes rather shy. Yet his men respected him, and many grew very fond of him. Scott had been to Antarctica once already, between 1901 and 1904, on a ship called the Discovery. On that journey, he and two friends had marched further south than anyone before them. But they hadn't reached the Pole. They had come home tired and hungry, with the job unfinished. Now Scott was going back to try again. He wasn't a man who liked to talk about his feelings. He wrote them down instead, in a journal he kept every day. You'd follow him, I think. Those pages would later become some of the most famous words ever written about the ice.
===
IMG: sculptor-woman-studio-london-1910 | a lively young woman sculptor in an artist's smock working on a clay bust in a bright studio, a baby's cradle in the corner, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: Scott's wife was a sculptor who had studied art in Paris.
BODY: Scott left behind a wife and a baby son. You'd find her studio full of clay, plaster and lively conversation. His wife was called Kathleen. She was a lively and independent woman, quite unusual for her time. She had studied sculpture in Paris and knew many famous artists and writers. She loved adventure as much as her husband did. Kathleen travelled with the expedition as far as New Zealand. She wanted to see her husband off on the last stage of his journey. Their son, Peter, wasn't yet a year old. He stayed at home in London with a nurse. Scott thought about him often. He kept a photograph of the little boy in his cabin, and later in his corner of the hut. It's strange to think of it. A man was heading for the emptiest place on Earth, with a baby's picture beside his bed. Many of the men carried photographs like that. On long evenings, far from home, those small pictures would be looked at again and again.
===
IMG: scientists-on-ship-deck-instruments | young scientists of 1910 in jerseys and caps on the deck of a wooden ship, a brass telescope and wooden instrument boxes beside them, gentle sunshine
HEAD: The expedition wasn't only a race to the Pole, but a floating school of science.
BODY: Many people today remember only the race. You'd find microscopes and notebooks packed beside the sledges. Yet the ship was full of scientists. There were geologists, who would study the rocks and the glaciers. There was a biologist, to study the strange creatures in the frozen sea. There was a physicist, and a man who studied the weather. There was a doctor, an artist, and a photographer who would even make moving films. Together, they'd spend more than two years in the south. They'd measure the winds and the ice, the tides and the stars. They'd collect rocks, fossils, birds and fish. Back home, all of this would fill many thick books of science. Some of their weather records are still used by scientists today. So the voyage had two goals at once. One was a long walk to a single point on the map. The other was simply to understand a whole unknown land, a little better than before.

IMG: young-men-crew-deck-1910-sun | young men of a 1910 polar expedition in woollen jerseys and caps standing together on the deck of a wooden sailing ship, smiling, ropes and rigging around them, warm evening sunshine
HEAD: Out of eight thousand eager letters, only a few dozen men were chosen.
BODY: There were about sixty-five men on board the Terra Nova. You can imagine the piles of envelopes on his desk. They were sailors and scientists, officers and stokers. Most of them were young, many still in their twenties. They came from all over Britain, and also from Australia and Canada. One of them, a young ski expert, came from Norway. Some were navy men, used to the hard life on a ship. Others had never been to sea in their lives. Scott had chosen them very carefully from all those thousands of letters. He looked for men who were strong and cheerful. He also wanted men who could live together in a small space without quarrelling. That was perhaps the hardest skill of all. You could be a brilliant scientist and still be awful company in a cramped hut. These men were proud to have been picked. Many wrote home that they felt like the luckiest people alive. Would you have written one too? They were about to find out what that luck would mean.

IMG: wardroom-supper-swinging-lamps-ship | officers eating supper at a long table in a small wooden ship's cabin, oil lamps swinging on chains, round portholes showing a dusky sea, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: On their first night at sea, the officers raised a glass to the Pole.
BODY: That first evening, the officers gathered below deck in the wardroom. You'd hear the clink of glasses in the little wardroom. This was a small cabin with a table, benches and a few lamps. The ship rolled gently, and the lamps swung on their chains. Through the round portholes, the last light faded over the sea. Someone opened a bottle of wine. Someone else stood up and proposed a toast. It was to the expedition, and to the Pole. Ahead of them lay a journey across more than half the world. They'd cross the Atlantic, round the southern tip of Africa, and sail on to Australia and New Zealand. Only then would they turn south into the ice. It would take them more than six months just to get there. Would you have the patience for such a long voyage? These men didn't seem to mind. For most of them, this was the adventure they had dreamed of since they were boys.
===
IMG: sailing-ship-tropical-sea-flying-fish | an old wooden steam ship sailing across a calm blue tropical sea, flying fish skimming beside the hull, a golden sunset sky
HEAD: Before they reached the ice, the men had to sail through the heat of the tropics.
BODY: For weeks, the Terra Nova sailed south through the Atlantic. You'd sweat under a blazing sun, long before you felt the cold. The weather grew warm, and then very hot. The men took off their heavy woollen clothes. They worked on deck in their shirtsleeves, and their faces turned brown in the sun. Flying fish skimmed across the waves beside the ship. Sometimes one landed on deck with a wet slap, and a sailor would toss it back. In the evenings, the sea glowed with tiny lights in the wake of the ship. These were small sea creatures that shine when the water moves. Above, the stars were bright, and strange new ones rose in the south. Some of the men had never seen the Southern Cross before. They stood at the rail and looked up at it for a long time. You'd swap your thick jumper for a cotton shirt. It was a gentle beginning to a journey that would end in the hardest cold on Earth.
===
IMG: stokers-shovelling-coal-furnace-glow | men stripped to the waist shovelling coal into the glowing furnace of an old ship's engine room, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm orange firelight
HEAD: Scientists and officers took turns shovelling coal in the hot belly of the ship.
BODY: The Terra Nova was short of sailors. You'd come up from the stokehold black with coal dust. So everybody had to help, whatever their job on land. Scientists who had studied at great universities pulled on the ropes. Officers scrubbed the decks with brushes and buckets. And they all took turns down in the stokehold. This was the room deep in the ship where the furnaces burned. There, the men shovelled coal into the fires to keep the engine running. The heat was so great that they worked stripped to the waist. They came up black with coal dust from head to toe. Yet nobody seemed to mind. Many said later it was one of the happiest times of the whole expedition. Hard work shared in a hot, dark room is a quick way to make friends. Men who had stood side by side at the furnace would stay close for the rest of their lives. Some of those friendships would matter a great deal, much later, on the ice.

IMG: quiet-man-painting-watercolour-deck | a slim quiet man sketching with a small box of watercolours on the deck of a wooden ship, seabirds over the waves, gentle evening light
HEAD: The chief scientist was a gentle doctor whom everyone called Uncle Bill.
BODY: His real name was Edward Wilson. You'd find him sketching birds at the rail, smiling quietly. He was thirty-eight years old, slim and quiet, with a kind face and a soft voice. He was a doctor, and also a fine scientist who knew all about birds. Yet perhaps his greatest gift was painting. Wilson spent hours on deck with his sketchbook and a little box of watercolours. He painted the sea, the sky, the clouds and the birds that followed the ship. His pictures were calm and delicate, and full of light. Wilson was also Scott's closest friend. The two of them had been together on the earlier expedition. Scott trusted him more than anyone else alive. When men had a worry, they often went to Wilson first. He'd listen, say very little, and somehow make them feel better. Every group of people needs someone like that. You'd trust him with anything, I think. On the Terra Nova, that person was Uncle Bill.

IMG: short-red-haired-officer-ship-stores | a short stocky young officer with bright red hair checking crates and sacks in the hold of a wooden ship, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lantern light
HEAD: A short, red-haired officer once fell nineteen feet into the hold and climbed out smiling.
BODY: His name was Henry Bowers, and he was twenty-seven years old. He was short and stocky, with bright red hair and a very large nose. Because of the nose, everyone called him Birdie. He came from the Royal Indian Marine, and he was extraordinarily strong. He hardly ever seemed to feel the cold. Scott put him in charge of the stores. Soon, Birdie knew exactly where every box and every sack on the ship was kept. Not long after he first came aboard, he stepped through an open hatch by mistake. He fell nineteen feet, down into the hold, and landed on a pile of iron bars. The men rushed to help him. But Birdie climbed back up the ladder, quite unhurt, and said it was nothing. Then he went straight back to work. That was Birdie all over. Can you believe it? He was cheerful, tireless and almost impossible to stop.
===
IMG: tall-army-officer-pipe-reading-corner | a tall quiet army officer with a long weathered face smoking a pipe and reading a book in a wooden ship's cabin, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: One officer paid a large sum of money just to be allowed to come along.
BODY: Lawrence Oates was a tall, quiet army officer from a cavalry regiment. You might think he'd expect special treatment. He didn't. He was thirty years old, with a long weathered face. He walked with a slight limp, from an old wound he had received in the war in South Africa. Oates came from a wealthy family. He gave a large sum of money to the expedition, and asked to join it. His job would be to look after the ponies. The men called him Titus, or sometimes simply the Soldier. He didn't say very much. He was often rather gloomy about how things would turn out. Yet when he did speak, he was often very funny. He liked to sit quietly in a corner, smoking his pipe. His favourite reading was a book about Napoleon. Do you know people like that? They seem grumpy at first. Then, slowly, you discover they are some of the kindest people you have ever met.

IMG: young-man-round-spectacles-ship-rail | a young man in round spectacles and a woollen jersey leaning on the rail of a wooden ship, looking out at the sea, soft golden evening light
HEAD: The youngest officer later wrote a famous book called The Worst Journey in the World.
BODY: Apsley Cherry-Garrard was only twenty-four. You might have read it yourself, or heard of its famous title. Everyone called him Cherry. He had just finished university at Oxford. He had no special training in science, and he was very short-sighted. He wore round spectacles that were always slipping down his nose. Yet he was eager, kind and willing to help with anything. Wilson knew his family, and had recommended him to Scott. He became an assistant zoologist, and a helper wherever he was needed. Cherry wasn't the strongest man on board, or the cleverest. But he noticed things, and he remembered them. Years later, he wrote a book about the expedition. He called it The Worst Journey in the World. It became one of the most famous books of travel ever written. Much of what we know about daily life on this expedition comes from Cherry. So in a way, we're seeing it all tonight through his round spectacles.

IMG: lyttelton-harbour-green-hills-1910 | a little harbour town among green hills in New Zealand, wooden houses and a jetty, an old black wooden ship tied up at the quay, soft evening light
HEAD: In a little New Zealand harbour, the ship took on ponies, dogs and the last fresh news from home.
BODY: The Terra Nova called at South Africa, and then at Australia. You'd hear dogs barking and ponies stamping on the deck. In October, she reached New Zealand. In the small harbour town of Lyttelton, among green hills, she was unloaded completely. Her hull was cleaned and repaired. Then she was loaded again, even more heavily than before. The ponies and the dogs came aboard. They were shut into stalls and kennels built right on the deck. The townspeople were very kind to the expedition. They invited the men into their homes, and gave them dinners and dances. The men wrote their last letters, and posted them. For a long time, there would be no more post. Then, on the twenty-ninth of November 1910, the ship left New Zealand and turned south. Crowds waved again from the shore. It was the last friendly harbour any of them would see for more than a year.

IMG: men-bailing-buckets-lantern-night-ship | men in oilskins passing buckets hand to hand in a long line inside a wooden ship at night, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lantern light
HEAD: In a great storm, the men saved their ship by passing buckets from hand to hand all night, singing as they worked.
BODY: Almost at once after leaving New Zealand, a great storm struck. You'd feel the cold water sloshing around your ankles as you passed the bucket along. The ship was very heavily loaded, and she rolled and pitched in huge waves. Water poured across the decks. Then the pumps became blocked with coal dust and oil. Water began to rise in the engine room. So the men formed a long line. They passed buckets from hand to hand, all through the night, emptying the water out. Officers and sailors and scientists all worked together. And to keep their spirits up, they sang. Old sea songs and music hall tunes rang out over the noise of the storm. After two days, the wind dropped and the sea grew calm again. Everyone was exhausted and soaking wet. Two ponies and a dog had been lost. Yet the ship and every man aboard were safe. They dried their clothes, ate a hot meal, and sailed on toward the ice.

TOPIC: Through the ice to Cape Evans
BRIDGE: Weeks go by, and the air grows colder every day. Then, one morning, you'd see something white and enormous on the horizon.

IMG: first-iceberg-grey-sea-ship | a vast flat-topped white iceberg floating in a calm grey sea, an old wooden ship small in the distance, pale evening light
HEAD: The first iceberg they saw was as big as a whole town.
BODY: About a week after the storm, someone on deck called out. You'd stand at the rail and stare, hardly believing your eyes. Far ahead, there was something white on the sea. As they came closer, they saw it was an iceberg. It was a great flat-topped block of ice, as big as a town. It floated silently on the grey water, with the waves breaking softly against its foot. The men lined the rail to stare at it. Some had never seen ice bigger than a puddle in winter. Then there was another iceberg, and another. Some were flat like tables. Others had been carved by wind and sea into towers and arches. Their sides glowed a pale and lovely blue. The air grew colder each day. The sea turned dark. And then, one morning, the ship reached the edge of the pack ice. Would you have felt excited, or a little afraid? You'd feel the cold coming off it from far away. Most of the men said they felt both at once.
===
IMG: ship-in-pack-ice-midnight-sun | an old wooden steam ship stuck among broken floating ice floes, the low sun glowing near the horizon at midnight, golden light on the ice
HEAD: On Christmas Day 1910, the ship was frozen fast in the ice, and the sun shone all night.
BODY: The pack ice was a vast field of floating ice, broken into pieces of every size. You'd feel the sun on your face, at midnight. Some pieces were no bigger than a table. Others were as large as a field. They drifted and ground together on the dark water. The Terra Nova pushed her way in slowly. She rammed a floe with her strong oak bow, backed off, and rammed again. Sometimes she was stuck for days. On Christmas Day, she was held fast in the ice. So the men had their Christmas dinner there, in the wardroom. There was roast mutton and plum pudding. They sang songs until late at night. When they went up on deck, it was still broad daylight. In the far south, in summer, the sun never sets. It just circles low around the sky. Imagine finishing Christmas dinner at midnight, and stepping out into sunshine. That was their first taste of the strange days of the south.

IMG: adelie-penguins-listening-ice-ship | small black and white penguins standing on an ice floe beside an old wooden ship, heads tilted as if listening, soft golden light
HEAD: Curious penguins walked up to the ship, and the sailors sang to them.
BODY: While the ship was stuck, visitors came across the ice. You'd see them waddle closer and tilt their heads to listen. They were little Adélie penguins, black and white, about knee high. They had never seen a ship before, or a person. So they weren't afraid at all. They waddled right up to the side of the Terra Nova to see what it was. The men leaned over the rail and laughed. Then someone began to sing to them. The penguins stood still and listened, with their heads on one side. When the song was over, some of them wandered off. Others stayed for an encore. One of the men tried playing a tune on a banjo. Another tried a mouth organ. The penguins seemed to have opinions about the music. Some walked away at once, and some came closer. It was a happy, silly hour in the middle of the ice. Do you think the penguins enjoyed it? I love that little detail. Perhaps they were just very polite.

IMG: mount-erebus-smoking-volcano-sea | a great snow-covered volcano with a thin plume of steam rising from its summit, dark still sea and floating ice in front, pale blue evening sky
HEAD: The first thing the men saw of Antarctica was a volcano breathing steam.
BODY: At the beginning of January 1911, the ship broke out of the pack ice at last. You'd see a thin plume of steam rising from a white mountain. She sailed into the calm, open water of the Ross Sea. And there, ahead of them, rose a mountain. It was called Mount Erebus. It was more than twelve thousand feet high and covered in snow and ice. Yet at its very top was a dark crater. From it, a thin plume of steam drifted lazily away on the wind. It was a living volcano, in the middle of the frozen world. Below it, the land ran down in long white slopes to the sea. There were black rocks and blue-green glaciers. The air was so clear that mountains a hundred miles away looked close enough to walk to. That clear air played tricks on everyone. More than once, a man set out for a hill that seemed close by. Hours later, he'd find it was still far away.

IMG: ross-island-coast-cape-black-rocks | a low cape of black volcanic rock and sand sticking out into a frozen bay, snowy mountain slopes rising behind, soft golden evening light
HEAD: Scott chose a cape of black sand and named it after his second in command.
BODY: Scott searched the coast for a good place to build a hut. You'd see the dark beach stand out sharply against all that white. He found a low cape of black volcanic rock and sand, sticking out from the shore below Mount Erebus. It was sheltered from the worst of the winds. It was close to the sea ice, which would be the road south in spring. And there were penguins and seals nearby, for fresh meat. He named it Cape Evans, after his second in command, Lieutenant Teddy Evans. On the fourth of January, the ship tied up to the edge of the ice near the cape. The men looked at their new home. It was bare and black and white, with no plant bigger than a patch of moss. Yet it had a wild and lonely beauty. Behind the cape, the great volcano rose up into the sky. In front of it, the frozen bay stretched away to distant blue mountains. This would be their home for the next two years.

IMG: ponies-pulling-sledges-sea-ice-ship | small shaggy ponies pulling sledges loaded with boxes across flat sea ice from an old wooden ship toward a dark shore, endless summer daylight
HEAD: Unloading the ship took days and nights without a break, in endless sunshine.
BODY: There was an enormous amount to unload. You'd lose track of time in the never-ending daylight. There were materials for the hut, and food and fuel for two years. There were tons of coal, and boxes of scientific instruments. There were sledges, tents and clothes, and of course the animals. Everything had to be carried across the sea ice to the shore. That was about a mile and a half. The ponies pulled sledges piled with boxes. The dogs pulled smaller loads. The men dragged the rest themselves. They worked in shifts, around the clock. Since the sun never set, there was no real night to stop them. One of the motor sledges was lost on the very first day. As it was lowered from the ship, it broke through the ice. It sank into the dark water and was gone. The men stood and looked at the hole for a moment. Then they turned back to work. You'd sleep wherever you happened to sit down. There was too much to do to stay sad for long.
===
IMG: wooden-hut-construction-cape-snow | carpenters building a large wooden hut on a black sandy cape beside a frozen bay, numbered planks stacked in the snow, warm evening sunlight
HEAD: The hut came from England in numbered pieces, like a giant puzzle.
BODY: While the ship was being unloaded, the carpenters built the hut. You'd sort through the numbered pieces with your cold fingers. It had been made in England, then taken apart. Each piece was numbered, so it could be fitted together again, like a giant puzzle. The hut was about fifty feet long and twenty-five feet wide. It had a sloping roof and small windows. Its walls were double. Between them, the builders packed a layer of dried seaweed, sewn into quilts. That seaweed kept the warmth in and the cold out, surprisingly well. The floor had layers of felt and wood to keep out the chill of the ground. In less than two weeks, the hut was finished. Around it, the men built stables for the ponies out of boxes and bales of hay. They made kennels for the dogs, and little shelters for the instruments. By the middle of January, they had a home. You'd be proud when the last board was nailed in place. And remarkably, that same wooden hut is still standing today.

IMG: hut-cape-evans-erebus-behind-sunlight | a wooden hut on a little rise of black sand near a frozen sea, a great snowy volcano rising behind it, long golden evening light
HEAD: The only sounds at the hut on calm days were the sea, the birds and a distant dog.
BODY: The hut stood on a small rise of black sand near the edge of the sea. You'd stand still and hear almost nothing at all. To the north and west, it looked out over the frozen bay. Beyond lay open water and distant mountains, white and blue and pink in the long summer light. Behind the hut, the slopes of Erebus climbed up toward the smoking summit. It was a beautiful place, wild and lonely and very quiet. On calm days, you could hear the sea lapping softly against the ice. You could hear the cries of the big brown skuas wheeling overhead. Now and then, a dog barked, and another answered. Then the silence came back. The men often said it was the quietest place they had ever known. Some found the silence strange at first. Most came to love it. Can you hear it now, in your mind? Just the sea, a bird, and the soft creak of a wooden door.

IMG: scientist-cutting-hole-sea-ice-net | a scientist in woollen clothes lowering a net through a hole in thick sea ice, wooden boxes and a sledge beside him, snowy shore behind, soft golden light
HEAD: The scientists fished through holes in the ice and sent balloons into the sky.
BODY: The men soon settled into a daily routine. You'd sit beside a hole in the ice, waiting for a tug on your line. In the mornings, the scientists went out to work. The geologists climbed the hills to study the rocks and the glaciers. The biologist cut holes in the sea ice. Through them, he lowered nets into the dark water below. Up came strange little creatures that no one had ever studied before. The weather man read his instruments in a small shelter outside the hut. He also sent up small balloons to measure the winds high above. Each balloon carried a tiny instrument, and the men watched it rise until it was a speck. Meanwhile, the sailors looked after the stores, the animals and the hut. Everyone was busy preparing for the journeys still to come. In the evenings, they compared notes about what they had found. It felt a little like a busy village. It just happened to be a village at the bottom of the world.

IMG: photographer-heavy-camera-tripod-ice | a tall dignified man with a large wooden camera on a tripod photographing icebergs on a frozen bay, soft golden evening light
HEAD: The expedition's photographer called himself a camera artist.
BODY: His name was Herbert Ponting. He was tall and dignified, about forty years old. He had travelled all over the world with his camera. He had taken famous pictures in Japan, India and China. Ponting brought the best cameras that money could buy. He also brought a film camera for making moving pictures. In the hut, he built himself a small darkroom. Every day, in all weathers, he went out with his heavy wooden cameras and his tripod. He photographed the ice and the mountains, the penguins and the seals. He photographed the men at work and the ship in the bay. He was very patient. He'd wait for hours for exactly the right light. The other men teased him about it gently. They even made up a word for posing for his pictures. Yet his photographs became some of the most beautiful ever taken. Have you seen any of his pictures? Many of them can still be seen in museums today.

HEAD: Killer whales once tried to tip the photographer off the ice.
BODY: One day, Ponting was out on the sea ice near the edge of the water. You'd hear a great thump under the ice beneath your feet. He was setting up his camera. Suddenly, a group of killer whales appeared, swimming along the edge of the ice. They were big and black and white, and very clever. They dived under the ice where Ponting stood. Then they came up underneath it, all together, and broke it into pieces with their backs. Perhaps they thought he was a seal. Ponting jumped from one floating piece of ice to another, very fast. He reached solid ice just in time. Behind him, the whales lifted their heads out of the water. They looked at him for a moment, quite calmly. Then they swam away. Ponting was safe. And being a true photographer, he was very sorry about one thing. He hadn't had time to take their picture. The men laughed about that for weeks.

IMG: men-sitting-outside-hut-golden-midnight | men sitting on wooden boxes outside a wooden hut on black sand, smoking pipes, looking out over a calm frozen bay with pink icebergs, long golden light of the midnight sun
HEAD: Late in the evening, the men sat outside on boxes and watched the icebergs turn pink.
BODY: After the day's work, the men often sat outside the hut for a while. You'd find yourself a box, sit down and simply watch. They sat on boxes and on the black sand, smoking their pipes. They looked out over the water and said very little. The light at that time of year was wonderful. Even late at night, the sun hung low in the south. Its long golden rays fell across the ice. They turned the icebergs pink and the shadows blue. The sea was calm and dark. The mountains lay upside down in it, perfectly still. Somewhere along the shore, a penguin called. A seal came up in a pool of open water. It blew out a small cloud of breath, and slipped under again. Then everything was quiet once more. Many of the men later said that these were among the happiest hours of their lives. No telegrams, no bills, no noise. Only the light, the sea and good company.

IMG: ship-disappearing-horizon-ice-evening | an old wooden ship sailing away over a calm icy sea, a thin trail of smoke on the horizon, men standing small on the shore, soft evening light
HEAD: When the ship sailed away, twenty-five men were left alone on the edge of the continent.
BODY: In late January, the Terra Nova sailed away along the coast. You'd watch her grow smaller and smaller on the horizon. She had other work to do. She would land a smaller party of men further along the coast. Then she would go back to New Zealand for the winter, before the sea froze. The men who stayed at Cape Evans stood on the shore and watched her go. They watched until her smoke disappeared over the horizon. There were twenty-five of them now, alone on the edge of the frozen continent. They had their hut, their animals and their stores. They wouldn't see the ship again for more than a year. There was no radio, no telephone and no post. Whatever happened now, they'd have to manage by themselves. Would you have felt lonely, watching that smoke disappear? Some of them did, just for a moment. Then they turned back to the hut, and got on with making it home.
