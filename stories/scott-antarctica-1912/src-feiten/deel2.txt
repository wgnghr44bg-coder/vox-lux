And there they were, the emperor penguins. There were about a hundred of them, standing close together in a huddle. They were tall and dignified, with black backs and white fronts. Each had a golden patch on the side of its neck. Some were holding eggs on top of their feet. The eggs were tucked under a warm fold of feathered skin, safe from the ice. The penguins made a strange trumpeting sound that echoed off the cliffs. The three men stood and watched them in the dim light. For a moment, they forgot how cold they were. Wilson had waited years for this. He had come further than any bird lover had ever come, just to stand here.

BODY: The men took five eggs and carried them carefully back up through the ice. Two broke on the way, and three survived. That night, a terrible storm struck. The wind tore the canvas roof from their stone shelter. It blew their tent away into the dark. For two days, they lay in their sleeping bags with snow pouring in. They sang hymns and songs to keep their spirits up. When the storm ended, Bowers went searching. To everyone's joy, he found the tent lying among the rocks, about half a mile away, undamaged. On the first of August, after five weeks away, they reached the hut again. Their clothes were so frozen that the others had to cut them off. Then the three travellers climbed into dry sleeping bags and slept and slept. The three eggs went home to England. Today they lie in a quiet drawer at the Natural History Museum in London.
