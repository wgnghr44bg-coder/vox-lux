They read a service from the Bible and sang a hymn, standing in the cold bright air. Then they built a great cairn of snow over the tent, twelve feet high. On top, they set a cross made of skis. Later, they built a smaller cairn for Oates, calling him a very gallant gentleman.

BODY: In January 1913, the Terra Nova came back to take everyone home. If you climbed that hill, you'd find the cross still standing. Before they left, the men made a large wooden cross. It was carved from Australian jarrah wood, which is hard and lasts a long time. They carried it up to the top of Observation Hill. This was a steep little hill, from which they could see far across the Barrier. Out there, somewhere, their friends lay at rest. On the cross, they carved the names of the five men who had reached the Pole. Underneath, they carved a line from a poem by Tennyson. Cherry chose it. It reads: To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield. Then the men walked down the hill and back to the ship. The Terra Nova steamed slowly out of the sound. The men at the rail watched Antarctica grow small behind them. The cross still stands on that hill today, weathered silver-grey by the wind.

HEAD: Scott's son grew up to love nature, just as his father had asked.
BODY: On the tenth of February 1913, the Terra Nova reached a small harbour in New Zealand. You might know his name: Sir Peter Scott, who loved birds all his life. Two men rowed ashore and sent a telegram to London. Within hours, the news had travelled around the world. In Britain, people were stunned. A great memorial service was held at St Paul's Cathedral in London, and the King attended. Kathleen Scott heard the news at sea. She was on a ship, sailing out to meet her husband. The captain told her as gently as he could. She walked the deck for a long time, looking at the sea. Later, she read her husband's last letter. She remembered what he had asked about their son. Peter Scott grew up loving birds and the countryside. He became a painter of birds and a famous naturalist. In 1961, he helped to found the World Wildlife Fund, whose symbol is a giant panda. His father would surely have been very proud.

HEAD: The fossil leaves they carried helped prove that the southern continents were once joined together.
BODY: The stones from the Beardmore Glacier were studied carefully by scientists. You might think a few stones couldn't matter much. They mattered a great deal. They turned out to be very important. The fossil leaves came from Glossopteris, the same plant found in South America, Africa, India and Australia. That gave scientists an important clue. Long ago, all those lands must have been joined to Antarctica in one great southern continent. Over millions of years, it slowly broke apart. So the men who walked to the Pole brought home a small piece of the Earth's own story. The emperor eggs didn't prove what Wilson had hoped. Yet they are still kept today, in a quiet drawer in London. And Amundsen always spoke of Scott with great respect. He went on exploring for many years. In 1926, he flew over the North Pole in an airship. Today, the scientific station at the South Pole bears both their names, side by side: Amundsen and Scott.

HEAD: The hut at Cape Evans still stands, looking almost exactly as the men left it.
BODY: The hut at Cape Evans is still there today, on its little cape of black sand. If you could visit, you'd see their old jars and boots still on the shelves. For many years, it stood empty. Snow drifted in and filled some rooms with ice. Then a group of people in New Zealand decided to save it. They are called the Antarctic Heritage Trust. For years, their workers came south in the summers. They dug out the ice and repaired the walls and roof. They cleaned and mended thousands of objects, one by one. Today, the hut looks almost as it did in 1913. The long table still stands in the wardroom. Tins of food and jars of pickles sit on the shelves, with their old labels. In the kitchen is Clissold's stove. In the stables, the ponies' names are still written above the stalls. Very few people ever visit. Those who do walk through quietly, speaking in low voices, as if they were in a church.
