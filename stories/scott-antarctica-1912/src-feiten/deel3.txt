TOPIC: Spring, the plan and a rival
===
IMG: sun-returns-horizon-golden-ice | a tiny sliver of golden sun appearing above snowy mountains across a frozen sea, men in the foreground lifting their caps, soft orange sky
HEAD: In late August 1911, the sun returned to Cape Evans after four months of darkness.
BODY: After the long polar night, the sun rose above the horizon again at Cape Evans in late August 1911. For weeks before, the sky had grown lighter around midday, with colours of pink, gold and orange. Historians know from diaries that the men greeted the sun's return with great joy. Some went outside to watch it appear, and a few raised their hats to it. Scott wrote about the beauty of the returning light. Even so, the spring was still very cold, with temperatures often below minus thirty degrees Celsius. Blizzards continued to sweep across the ice. Yet each day was a little longer and a little brighter. The men began to prepare seriously for the journey to the Pole. Equipment was checked, sledges were repaired, and the ponies were trained. Short spring journeys were made to test plans and equipment. After months of waiting, the time for the great journey was approaching.
===
IMG: men-studying-map-lamplit-hut-table | men leaning over a large hand-drawn map on a wooden table in a lamplit hut, pencils and notebooks beside it, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm light
HEAD: Scott's planned route to the Pole had three parts: the ice shelf, a glacier and a high plateau.
BODY: The route to the South Pole from Cape Evans was about thirteen hundred kilometres long. It had three main parts. First, the travellers had to cross the Ross Ice Shelf, then called the Great Ice Barrier, a vast flat sheet of floating ice. Second, they had to climb the Beardmore Glacier, a huge river of ice flowing through the mountains, discovered by Shackleton in 1908. Third, they had to cross the Polar Plateau, a high, flat expanse of ice nearly three thousand metres above sea level. Historians know about Scott's plans from his journal and from his lectures to the men. He planned to use motor sledges, ponies and dogs for the first part, and then men would pull the sledges themselves. Even so, the plan was complex and depended on many things going right. Support parties would accompany the polar party part of the way and then turn back, leaving depots of food and fuel.
===
IMG: packing-ration-bags-hut-table | a red-haired officer weighing and labelling small cloth food bags on a wooden table in a hut, scales and stacks of bags around him, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: Bowers organised the food for the journey, packing it carefully into weekly bags.
BODY: Food planning was essential for the journey to the Pole. Each man needed enough food to give him energy for long days of pulling heavy sledges in extreme cold. The main food was pemmican, a mixture of dried meat and fat. The men also ate biscuits, butter, sugar, cocoa and tea. Historians note that Bowers was responsible for organising the food. He spent days weighing out rations and packing them into bags, each holding a week's supply for a team. These bags were then placed in depots along the route. Even so, historians and scientists have since shown that the rations were not enough. The men burned far more energy than they ate, and the food lacked some important vitamins. This was not fully understood at the time. Bowers's careful work was much admired by Scott and the others, who trusted him completely to keep track of every supply.
===
IMG: scott-writing-journal-cubicle-candle | a naval captain in a woollen jersey writing in a notebook by candlelight in a small wooden cubicle, photographs pinned on the wall, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: In 1910, Scott learned that the Norwegian explorer Roald Amundsen was also heading for Antarctica.
BODY: Roald Amundsen was a famous Norwegian explorer. He had been the first to sail through the Northwest Passage, the sea route around northern Canada. In 1910, he was preparing an expedition to the North Pole. But when news came that American explorers claimed to have reached the North Pole, he secretly changed his plans and decided to go south instead. When the Terra Nova reached Melbourne in October 1910, Scott received a telegram from Amundsen. It said simply that he was going south. Historians note that this message turned the expedition into a race. Even so, Scott decided not to change his plans. He said his expedition would continue as intended, with its scientific programme, and would not be hurried. In February 1911, members of Scott's expedition found Amundsen's ship, the Fram, at the Bay of Whales, on the edge of the ice shelf. The race had truly begun.
===
IMG: norwegian-hut-framheim-barrier-snow | a small wooden house half buried in snow on a flat white ice plain, tents and many sled dogs around it, soft golden evening light
HEAD: Amundsen's expedition spent the winter in a hut called Framheim, on the ice shelf.
BODY: Amundsen's base was at the Bay of Whales, on the edge of the Ross Ice Shelf, about one hundred kilometres closer to the Pole than Scott's base. His hut was called Framheim, meaning home of the Fram. It was a wooden hut that the Norwegians had brought with them. Historians note that the Norwegians dug tunnels and rooms into the snow around the hut. These included workshops, storerooms and even a sauna. The team of nine men spent the winter improving their equipment. They made lighter sledges, better boots and new tents. Even so, they also made a mistake. In September 1911, Amundsen started too early, and the extreme cold forced the team to turn back. The setback caused a disagreement within the team, and Amundsen changed the members of the party. The Norwegians set out again in October 1911, with five men, four sledges and fifty-two dogs.
===
IMG: motor-sledges-departing-sea-ice-smoke | two small boxy motor sledges pulling loaded wooden sledges across flat sea ice in a puff of smoke, men walking beside them, bright spring light
HEAD: On the twenty-fourth of October 1911, the motor sledges left Cape Evans for the south.
BODY: Scott's journey to the Pole began with the motor sledges. They were an early type of tracked vehicle, designed to pull heavy loads across snow and ice. Two remained after one had sunk during unloading. On the twenty-fourth of October 1911, they left Cape Evans with a team of four men, led by Lieutenant Edward Evans. Historians note that this was one of the first uses of motor vehicles in Antarctica. Even so, they did not last long. Their engines overheated and broke down, and within a week both motor sledges had been abandoned. The men then pulled the loads themselves. Yet the motor sledges had carried supplies a useful distance. Historians consider them important early experiments. Scott had hoped they would show the way for future polar travel. In later years, tracked vehicles became essential in Antarctica, and the ideas tested by Scott's expedition were developed further by other explorers.

TOPIC: Two roads to the Pole
===
IMG: dog-teams-crossing-barrier-sunny | several dog teams pulling sledges across a flat white ice plain, men on skis beside them, bright sunshine and a deep blue sky
HEAD: Amundsen's team travelled quickly on skis, with dogs pulling their sledges.
BODY: Amundsen's team left Framheim on the twentieth of October 1911. The five men travelled on skis, and their sledges were pulled by dogs. The Norwegians were expert skiers and dog drivers, with years of experience in cold climates. Historians often compare the two expeditions. Amundsen's plan was simpler and focused entirely on reaching the Pole. He used dogs because they were fast and could survive on very little. As the journey went on, some dogs were killed to feed the others and the men, as planned. Even so, the Norwegians faced their own dangers. They found a new route through the mountains, up a steep glacier they named the Axel Heiberg Glacier, and crossed areas of dangerous crevasses. They reached the Polar Plateau in late November. Their good planning, skill and the strength of their dogs carried them forward quickly across the high, cold plateau.
===
IMG: small-tent-flag-alone-snow-plain | a small dark tent with a Norwegian flag on top standing alone on a vast flat white snow plain, low golden sun and long shadows
HEAD: On the fourteenth of December 1911, Amundsen's team reached the South Pole.
BODY: On the fourteenth of December 1911, Amundsen and his four companions, Olav Bjaaland, Helmer Hanssen, Sverre Hassel and Oscar Wisting, reached the South Pole. They were the first people ever to stand there. They planted the Norwegian flag and named the area King Haakon the Seventh's Plateau, after the king of Norway. Historians know about their arrival from Amundsen's book and the diaries of his men. The Norwegians took careful measurements to make sure they had found the right place. Before leaving, they set up a small tent, which they called Polheim. Inside, Amundsen left a letter for the king of Norway and a short note for Scott, asking him to deliver the letter if the Norwegians did not return safely. Even so, there was no great celebration. The men were tired and eager to return. They arrived safely back at Framheim on the twenty-fifth of January 1912.
===
IMG: ponies-sledges-leaving-hut-spring | a long straggling line of small shaggy ponies, each led by a man and pulling a loaded sledge, setting out across sea ice from a wooden hut, bright spring light
HEAD: Scott's main party left Cape Evans with the ponies on the first of November 1911.
BODY: On the first of November 1911, Scott and the main party set out from Cape Evans with the ponies. Each pony pulled a sledge loaded with supplies, and each was led by a man. The dog teams, driven by Meares and Dmitri, followed later. Historians know about the departure from diaries and photographs. The ponies moved slowly, and the party travelled in groups, stretched out across the ice. Even so, the start was hopeful. The weather was good, and the men were fit after the winter. The ponies managed better than some had expected. The party moved south across the Ross Ice Shelf, passing the depots laid the year before. They also found the motor sledge team, who were now pulling their sledges by hand. Day after day, the men walked south across the flat white plain, with mountains rising on the horizon to the west.
===
IMG: night-march-midnight-sun-low-ponies | a line of ponies and men marching across a vast flat ice plain under a low midnight sun, long golden shadows stretching across the snow
HEAD: On the ice shelf, Scott's party often marched at night, when the snow surface was harder.
BODY: During the Antarctic summer, the sun shines all day and night. In the middle of the day, its warmth softens the snow surface, making it harder for ponies and sledges to move. To avoid this, Scott's party often marched during the night, when the sun was lower and the snow was firmer. They then rested during the day in their tents. Historians know about this routine from diaries. Even so, the ponies struggled. They sank into soft snow and grew weaker as the journey went on. Walls of snow were built each night to shelter them from the wind. The men took great care of the animals, but their condition worsened. As planned, the weakest ponies were killed along the way, and their meat was left in depots as food for the return journey and for the dogs. The men felt sad at losing animals they had cared for over many months.
===
IMG: tents-snowed-in-glacier-foot-mountains | a few canvas tents half covered in snow at the foot of a huge glacier between tall mountains, soft grey light after a storm
HEAD: In early December 1911, a four-day blizzard stopped Scott's party at the foot of the Beardmore Glacier.
BODY: As Scott's party approached the Beardmore Glacier, a severe blizzard struck. It lasted four days, from the fifth to the eighth of December 1911. The men were trapped in their tents while snow piled up around them. The temperature was unusually warm, and the snow was wet, soaking their clothing and sleeping bags. Historians note that this delay was costly. The party used food intended for later in the journey, and they lost valuable time. Even so, the men stayed patient, reading and talking in the tents. When the blizzard ended, the snow was deep and soft, making travel extremely difficult. The remaining ponies were too weak to continue, and they were shot at a place the men named Shambles Camp. From then on, the men would pull the sledges themselves. The dog teams turned back towards Cape Evans a few days later, after helping carry supplies up to the glacier.
===
IMG: men-hauling-sledges-beardmore-glacier | men in harness pulling heavy wooden sledges up a vast glacier between enormous brown and white mountains, blue ice and bright sunshine
HEAD: Scott's party climbed the Beardmore Glacier pulling their sledges themselves, and celebrated Christmas on the ice.
BODY: The Beardmore Glacier is one of the largest glaciers in the world, about one hundred and sixty kilometres long. Scott's party climbed it by man-hauling, which means pulling the sledges themselves with harnesses. Twelve men in three teams worked their way up. Historians know from diaries that the work was exhausting. The surface was rough, with crevasses, ice ridges and soft snow. Some men fell into crevasses and had to be pulled out by their harness ropes. Even so, the men kept up their spirits. On Christmas Day 1911, high on the glacier, they had a special meal. Bowers produced a small plum pudding he had carried secretly, and there was extra chocolate and raisins. Diaries describe the meal as a happy occasion. The men also enjoyed the magnificent mountain scenery around them. By the end of December, they reached the top of the glacier and stood on the edge of the Polar Plateau.
===
IMG: two-groups-parting-plateau-handshakes | two small groups of men in heavy canvas clothes shaking hands beside sledges on a high white snow plain, low golden sun and long shadows
HEAD: On the fourth of January 1912, the last support party turned back, leaving five men to go on to the Pole.
BODY: As planned, support parties turned back at intervals, leaving the strongest men to continue. The first group of four turned back at the top of the Beardmore Glacier. Then, on the fourth of January 1912, Scott chose the final polar party. He took Wilson, Bowers, Oates and Petty Officer Edgar Evans. The last support party, led by Lieutenant Edward Evans with Tom Crean and William Lashly, said goodbye and turned north. Historians note that Scott's decision to take five men instead of four surprised some, since food and tents had been planned for teams of four. Even so, Scott believed five men could pull faster. Diaries describe the parting as emotional. Crean was in tears as he shook hands. The last support party later had their own terrible journey home. Edward Evans fell seriously ill with scurvy, and Crean walked alone for many hours across the ice to fetch help, saving his life.

TOPIC: Five men on the high plateau
===
IMG: five-men-hauling-sledge-plateau-sun | five men in harness side by side pulling one wooden sledge across a high white plateau, one of them walking on foot without skis, low golden sun
HEAD: The five men of the polar party pulled their sledge across the high plateau towards the Pole.
BODY: From early January 1912, the five men of the polar party travelled across the Polar Plateau. They pulled one heavy sledge, carrying their tent, sleeping bags, food, fuel and instruments. Four of them used skis. Bowers did not, because his skis had been sent back with the support party before Scott decided to take him. He walked on foot through the snow, keeping pace with the others. Historians note that this made his work even harder. Even so, he never complained. The plateau was high, cold and windy. The thin air at nearly three thousand metres made breathing harder. Each day, the men marched for many hours, covering about fifteen to twenty kilometres. Scott recorded the distances carefully in his journal. The men were growing tired and hungry, but their spirits were still high. They believed they had a good chance of reaching the Pole first, since they had seen no sign of Amundsen.
===
IMG: sastrugi-snow-ridges-plateau-low-sun | long hard ridges of wind-carved snow stretching across a high empty plateau like frozen waves, low sun casting long blue shadows
HEAD: On the plateau, the wind had carved the snow into hard ridges called sastrugi.
BODY: The surface of the Polar Plateau is shaped by strong winds. They carve the snow into hard, wave-like ridges called sastrugi. Some are small, while others can be more than a metre high. Historians know from diaries that the sastrugi made sledging very difficult. The sledge caught on the ridges and had to be pulled over them, using great effort. Even so, the surface was not the only problem. Fresh snow sometimes covered the ice, creating a sandy texture that made the sledge drag heavily. The weather was also very cold. Temperatures fell to around minus thirty degrees Celsius, even in the middle of summer. The wind made it feel much colder. The men suffered from frostbite on their faces, fingers and feet. Yet they continued south, day after day. Scott's journal records his concern about the difficult surface and the slow progress, as well as his hope of reaching the Pole.
===
IMG: five-men-inside-small-tent-stove-cocoa | five tired men in woollen clothes sitting close together on sleeping bags inside a small canvas tent, a little stove glowing in the middle, mugs of cocoa, an indoor scene, enclosed by canvas walls, warm light
HEAD: Each evening, the five men shared a small tent, cooked a hot meal and wrote in their diaries.
BODY: At the end of each day's march, the five men put up their tent. It was cone-shaped, made of canvas, and designed to stand up to strong winds. Inside, they lit a small stove called a Primus, which burned paraffin. They cooked a hot meal, usually pemmican stew called hoosh, with biscuits and hot cocoa or tea. Historians know about these evenings from the diaries kept by Scott, Wilson and Bowers. The men wrote that the tent felt warm and comfortable after the hard day's work. They talked, made plans and wrote in their journals. Even so, the tent was crowded with five people, since it had been designed for four. Getting into frozen sleeping bags each night was difficult. The men slept close together for warmth. Wilson cared for the others' injuries, using his skills as a doctor. These evening hours were a small refuge from the cold and wind outside.
===
IMG: black-flag-distant-snow-plain | a small black flag tied to a wooden pole standing upright alone on a vast white snow plain, faint ski tracks around it, pale grey light
HEAD: On the sixteenth of January 1912, the polar party saw a black flag left by the Norwegians.
BODY: On the sixteenth of January 1912, the five men were about twenty-five kilometres from the South Pole. Bowers, who had sharp eyes, spotted something on the horizon. As they came closer, they saw that it was a black flag tied to a sledge runner. Around it were ski tracks, sledge tracks and the paw prints of many dogs. Historians know about this moment from Scott's journal and the other diaries. The men realised that the Norwegians had reached the Pole before them. Scott wrote that it was a terrible disappointment. Even so, the men continued to the Pole. They knew it was important to complete their journey and to record their scientific observations. Scott's diary from that day shows how deeply the men were affected. After months of planning and hardship, they knew they had come second. Yet they faced the news with dignity and kept going, as they had always done.
===
IMG: five-men-standing-pole-flag-wind | five men in heavy canvas clothes and fur mittens standing together beside a small flag on a flat white snow plain, wind blowing snow at their feet, pale light
HEAD: On the seventeenth of January 1912, Scott's party reached the South Pole, thirty-four days after Amundsen.
BODY: On the seventeenth of January 1912, Scott, Wilson, Bowers, Oates and Edgar Evans reached the South Pole. They took measurements to confirm their position and raised the British flag. The next day, they found Amundsen's tent, Polheim, with the Norwegian flag above it. Inside was the note for Scott and the letter for the king of Norway. Scott took the letter with him, as Amundsen had asked. Historians know about these days from the diaries. Scott wrote that the Pole was an awful place, cold and bleak, and that it was terrible to have worked so hard without the reward of being first. Even so, the men had achieved something extraordinary. They had walked across the ice shelf, climbed the glacier and crossed the plateau, pulling their own sledge. They spent a short time at the Pole, building a cairn and taking photographs. Then they turned north to begin the long journey home.
===
IMG: five-explorers-group-portrait-snow | five weary explorers in heavy clothes posing together for a photograph in the snow, three standing and two sitting in front, frost on their beards, soft pale light
HEAD: Bowers took a famous photograph of the five men at the South Pole, using a string to work the camera.
BODY: At the South Pole, Bowers took several photographs of the polar party. To include himself in the group, he pulled a string attached to the camera's shutter. The best known photograph shows the five men standing and sitting together, tired and weather-beaten, beside the flag. Historians consider it one of the most famous photographs in the history of exploration. It shows the men's exhaustion and disappointment, but also their pride. Even so, the photographs were not seen by the world until more than a year later. The camera and plates were found with the men's bodies in November 1912 and brought back to Britain. Ponting had trained the men in photography before the journey. Scott and Bowers had both learned to use a camera. Their photographs from the plateau and the Pole are a unique record. They show the conditions and the people of the polar party during its final months.

TOPIC: The long way home
===
IMG: men-marching-north-sail-on-sledge | five men pulling a wooden sledge with a small square sail rigged on it across a white plateau, the wind at their backs, soft grey light
HEAD: On the return journey, the polar party sometimes used a sail on their sledge to catch the wind.
BODY: The return journey from the Pole began on the nineteenth of January 1912. The five men faced more than thirteen hundred kilometres of travel back to Cape Evans. On the plateau, the wind sometimes blew from behind them. To make use of it, they rigged a sail on their sledge, made from a tent floor. The wind helped push the sledge, making their work a little easier. Historians know about this from Scott's journal and Wilson's diary. Even so, the journey was very hard. The men were growing weaker. They were short of food and suffering from the cold. Edgar Evans had a badly cut hand that would not heal. They had to find each depot they had left on the way south, following their old tracks and cairns. Finding the depots was essential, because each one held the food and fuel they needed to survive the next part of the journey.
===
IMG: fossil-rocks-glacier-moraine-sketch | a scientist kneeling among brown rocks at the edge of a glacier, holding a stone with a fossil leaf, a sledge waiting nearby, soft golden afternoon light
HEAD: On the way down the Beardmore Glacier, the men collected rocks containing fossils.
BODY: On the eighth of February 1912, while descending the Beardmore Glacier, the polar party stopped near a mountain called Mount Buckley. Wilson, the scientist, wanted to collect rock samples. The men spent the afternoon geologising, as Scott called it, gathering rocks from the moraine, the loose stones at the edge of the glacier. Some of the rocks contained fossils of ancient plants, including leaves of a tree-like plant called Glossopteris. Historians note that the men carried about sixteen kilograms of rocks with them on their sledge, despite their weakening condition. Even so, they did not abandon them. Scott and Wilson believed the samples were scientifically important. They were right. The fossils were later found with the men's bodies. Scientists studying them confirmed that Glossopteris had once grown in Antarctica. The same plant was known from South America, Africa, India and Australia. This helped show that these continents were once joined together.
===
IMG: quiet-tent-glacier-foot-evening-light | a single small canvas tent standing quietly at the foot of a great glacier, mountains glowing softly in the low evening light
HEAD: On the seventeenth of February 1912, Edgar Evans died at the foot of the Beardmore Glacier.
BODY: Petty Officer Edgar Evans was a strong, cheerful Welshman from the Gower Peninsula, near Swansea. He had been on the Discovery expedition with Scott and was known for his great physical strength. On the return journey, his condition grew worse. His cut hand did not heal, he suffered from frostbite, and he probably hit his head in a fall into a crevasse. He became confused and very weak. Historians note that he may also have suffered from a lack of food and vitamins. On the seventeenth of February 1912, near the foot of the Beardmore Glacier, he collapsed. His companions carried him to the tent, where he died that night. Even so, his companions spoke kindly of him in their diaries. Evans was the first of the polar party to die. Today, he is remembered in Wales with memorials, including one at the church in Rhossili, his home village.
===
IMG: tall-man-quiet-tent-evening-stove | a tall weary man with a long face sitting quietly in a small lamplit tent beside a little stove, three companions close by, an indoor scene, enclosed by canvas walls, warm soft light
HEAD: In March 1912, Captain Oates walked out of the tent into a blizzard, so as not to slow his companions.
BODY: As the four remaining men crossed the ice shelf, conditions became worse. The temperatures were much lower than expected for the time of year, and the surface was very hard to pull on. Oates was suffering badly from frostbite in his feet, and an old war wound had reopened. He could barely walk and knew he was slowing the others. Historians know about his final days from Scott's journal. On the sixteenth or seventeenth of March 1912, Oates got up from his sleeping bag and said, according to Scott, that he was just going outside and might be some time. He then walked out into a blizzard and was never seen again. It was his thirty-second birthday. Even so, his companions did not stop him. Scott wrote that it was the act of a brave man and an English gentleman. His body was never found. His sacrifice became one of the most famous stories of the expedition.
===
IMG: candle-tent-writing-letters-quiet | a small canvas tent lit softly by a candle, a man in woollen clothes writing a letter in a notebook with a pencil, an indoor scene, enclosed by canvas walls, warm gentle light
HEAD: In his last days, Scott wrote letters to the families of his companions and to his wife.
BODY: After Oates's death, Scott, Wilson and Bowers struggled on. On about the twentieth of March 1912, they made their last camp, about eighteen kilometres from One Ton Depot. A blizzard trapped them in their tent. They had very little food and fuel left. Historians know about these last days from Scott's journal and letters, which were found with the bodies. Scott wrote letters to the families of Wilson and Bowers, praising their courage. He wrote a message to the public, explaining the reasons for the disaster and asking that the families be looked after. He also wrote to his wife, Kathleen. In this letter, he asked her to make their son interested in natural history, rather than games. Even so, he wrote calmly and clearly. His final journal entry is dated the twenty-ninth of March 1912. It ends with the words: For God's sake look after our people.

===
IMG: two-men-dog-sledges-depot-waiting | two men with dog sledges waiting beside a snow cairn with a flag on a vast white plain, one looking south through a telescope, low pale sun
HEAD: In March 1912, Cherry-Garrard and Dmitri waited at One Ton Depot with dog teams, but the polar party did not arrive.
BODY: In late February 1912, Cherry-Garrard and the dog driver Dmitri Girev took two dog teams south from Cape Evans to One Ton Depot. They brought extra supplies and hoped to meet the polar party. They reached the depot on the fourth of March and waited there for six days. Historians know about this journey from Cherry-Garrard's book. He watched the horizon for any sign of the returning men. Even so, the weather was bad, and dog food was running short. He had been told not to risk the dogs, since they would be needed later. On the tenth of March, he decided to return to Cape Evans. At that moment, the polar party was struggling on about one hundred kilometres to the south. Historians have discussed Cherry-Garrard's decision for many years. Most agree that he followed his orders and could not have known where the polar party was. Even so, he felt the weight of that decision for the rest of his life.
===
IMG: second-winter-hut-thirteen-men-lamp | a small group of men sitting quietly around a lamplit table in a wooden hut, empty bunks with books on the shelves behind them, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm soft light
HEAD: During the second winter of 1912, thirteen men waited at Cape Evans, not knowing what had happened.
BODY: When autumn came in 1912 and the polar party had not returned, the men at Cape Evans knew that something terrible must have happened. The Terra Nova had come and gone again, taking some members home, including Ponting and Simpson. Thirteen men stayed for a second winter, led by the doctor Edward Atkinson. Historians know about this winter from diaries. It was a sad and difficult time. The empty bunks of the polar party stood in the hut, with their books and possessions still on the shelves. Even so, the men kept to their routine and continued their scientific work. They also planned a search for the polar party for the following spring. In the meantime, they did not know that a separate group, the Northern Party, was surviving a winter in an ice cave far to the north. The second winter was long and quiet, with few celebrations and much uncertainty about the future.
===
IMG: snow-cairn-ski-cross-plain-sunlight | a tall cairn of snow topped with a simple cross made of skis standing alone on a vast white plain, bright gentle sunlight and a clear blue sky
HEAD: On the twelfth of November 1912, a search party found the tent with the bodies of Scott, Wilson and Bowers.
BODY: In late October 1912, as spring returned, a search party left Cape Evans to look for the polar party. It was led by Atkinson and included Cherry-Garrard, Tom Crean, William Lashly and others. On the twelfth of November 1912, they saw a small mound of snow on the ice shelf, with the tip of a tent showing. Inside, they found the bodies of Scott, Wilson and Bowers in their sleeping bags. Historians know about this moment from several diaries. The men collected Scott's journal, letters, photographs and the geological samples. They read parts of the journal to learn what had happened. Even so, they did not move the bodies. They collapsed the tent over them and built a large cairn of snow on top. They placed a cross made from skis on the cairn. They also searched for Oates's body but did not find it, so they built a cairn near where he was believed to have died.
===
IMG: wooden-cross-observation-hill-view | a tall wooden cross standing on top of a steep snowy hill, looking out over a vast white ice shelf and the sea, soft golden evening light
HEAD: The expedition members put up a large wooden cross on Observation Hill, with a line from a famous poem.
BODY: Before leaving Antarctica, the remaining members of the expedition made a memorial to the polar party. They built a large wooden cross and carried it to the top of Observation Hill, near Hut Point, overlooking the ice shelf. On the cross, they carved the names of the five men who had died. Below the names, they carved a line from Ulysses, a poem by Alfred Tennyson: To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield. Historians note that the line was chosen by Cherry-Garrard. The memorial was put up in January 1913. Even so, the expedition was not quite over. In February 1913, the Terra Nova sailed back to New Zealand with the survivors, and news of the deaths was sent to the world by telegraph. The cross still stands today on Observation Hill, near the modern American base of McMurdo Station and the New Zealand base of Scott Base.

HEAD: Scott's son, Peter Scott, grew up to become a famous naturalist, as his father had hoped.
BODY: Kathleen Scott was on a ship on her way to New Zealand to meet her husband when she learned of his death. The news shocked Britain and the world. Scott and his companions became national heroes. Memorials were built in many towns, and money was raised for the families. Historians note that Scott's journal was published and widely read. Even so, in later decades, some writers questioned Scott's leadership and decisions, while others defended him. The debate continues among historians today. Scott's son, Peter, was only two years old when his father died. He grew up to become a famous naturalist, painter and conservationist, just as his father had hoped. He founded the Wildfowl and Wetlands Trust in Britain and helped start the World Wildlife Fund. He designed its famous panda logo. He was knighted in 1973 and died in 1989. His father's wish for him had come true.

HEAD: The fossils collected by the polar party helped prove that Antarctica was once part of a great southern continent.
BODY: The rocks collected by the polar party on the Beardmore Glacier were brought back to Britain and studied by scientists. They contained fossils of Glossopteris, an ancient plant that grew about two hundred and fifty million years ago. Historians and geologists note that these fossils were important evidence. Glossopteris fossils had already been found in South America, Africa, India and Australia. Finding them in Antarctica supported the idea that these lands were once joined together in a single great continent, now called Gondwana. Even so, at the time, many scientists did not accept that continents could move. The theory of continental drift, proposed by the German scientist Alfred Wegener in 1912, was not widely accepted until the 1960s. The fossils from Scott's expedition became part of that story. Some of them are kept today at the Natural History Museum in London, where scientists can still study them.

HEAD: The hut at Cape Evans still stands today and is carefully preserved.
BODY: More than a century after the expedition, the hut at Cape Evans still stands on Ross Island. Because of the cold, dry climate, many objects inside have survived. There are tins of food, bottles, books, clothing, equipment and scientific instruments. The bunks and the long table are still in place. Historians and conservators consider it one of the most important historic sites in Antarctica. It is cared for by the Antarctic Heritage Trust, a New Zealand charity, which has carried out major conservation work to protect the building and its contents. Even so, the hut faces challenges. Snow, wind and salt damage the wood, and conservators must work carefully to preserve it. Visitors to Antarctica can sometimes see the hut, but only in small groups and with permission. Standing inside, they can see the rooms where the men lived, worked and waited, as if they had left only a short time ago.
