The Antarctic winter is a long night.

For four months, from the end of April to the end of August, the sun does not rise at all. At midday, there is a little grey twilight along the northern horizon, a pale glow that lasts for an hour or two and then fades away. The rest of the time, it is dark.

--

But the darkness is not always black.

When the moon is up, it circles round and round the sky for days on end, never setting, and lights the whole frozen landscape in silver. The snow glows. The mountains stand out sharp and pale against the dark sky. The smoke from Erebus drifts slowly across the moon.

And on some nights, the sky is filled with the aurora australis, the southern lights: great curtains and ribbons of pale green and white and rose, rippling and folding and fading across the stars, silently, for hours.

{img:aurora-australis-green-curtains-hut}
The men come out of the hut to watch, wrapped in their heavy clothes, and stand in the snow with their heads tilted back, saying nothing, until the cold drives them back inside.

--

Inside the hut, life goes on to a steady rhythm.

At eight in the morning, the night watchman wakes the cook, and the cook lights the stove and makes the breakfast. At half past eight, everyone gets up, and there is a scramble for the washbasins. Breakfast is porridge, seal liver, bread and butter, marmalade, and tea or cocoa.

After breakfast, everyone goes to work. The scientists write up their notes and study their specimens. The sailors repair the sledges and the harness, sew tents, and pack food into bags for the spring journeys. Oates and Anton look after the ponies. Meares and Dimitri look after the dogs.

--

Every day, if the weather allows, the men go outside for exercise.

They walk across the frozen bay, or up the slopes behind the hut, or around the icebergs frozen into the sea ice, carrying lanterns. They exercise the ponies, leading them in a long line along the shore in the moonlight, with their breath steaming in the cold air.

{img:men-leading-ponies-moonlight-sea-ice}
Sometimes, a blizzard blows up, and the wind roars across the cape so loudly that the men inside the hut have to shout to hear each other. Then no one can go out, except to read the instruments, and even that is dangerous. The men tie themselves to a rope stretched from the hut to the instrument shelter, so that they will not lose their way in the whirling snow, only a few yards from the door.

--

The evenings are the best time of the day.

After dinner, the men sit around the long table, or in their bunks, and read, or write their journals, or play chess or cards, or mend their clothes. Someone puts a record on the gramophone. Someone plays the pianola.

{img:men-reading-playing-chess-hut-evening}
Three evenings a week, someone gives a lecture. Wilson talks about the birds and the animals of the Antarctic, and shows his paintings. Ponting shows his photographs of Japan, with cherry blossoms and temples and women in kimonos, so far from the ice and the dark. Simpson talks about the weather. The geologists talk about rocks and glaciers and volcanoes. Oates gives a talk about how to look after horses.

Scott talks about his plans for the journey to the Pole, and the men listen very closely.

--

There is a newspaper, too.

It is called the South Polar Times, and it is edited by Cherry-Garrard. The men write articles and stories and poems for it, sometimes funny and sometimes serious, and Wilson draws pictures for it. It is typed out, one copy only, on the expedition's typewriter, and bound in a beautiful cover made from the wood of packing cases and sealskin.

When a new issue is finished, Scott reads it aloud to everyone in the evening, while the authors sit and pretend not to be listening, and everyone tries to guess who wrote what.

--

Saturday evening is a special time in the hut.

After supper on Saturdays, the men drink a toast to sweethearts and wives, with a small glass of port or sherry, as sailors have done at sea for hundreds of years. Then someone puts on a favourite record, and the talk turns to home: to gardens and pubs and country lanes, to mothers and sisters and girls, to the meals they will eat when they get back to England.

Oates, who is not usually a talker, sometimes joins in, and tells stories about hunting in Ireland, and his horses, and his mother's house in Essex, with its gardens and its old trees.

--

On the sixth of June, it is Scott's forty-third birthday.

There is a special dinner, with a great birthday cake made by Clissold, decorated with candied fruit and chocolate and flags. The table is decorated with sledging flags, the little silk flags that each man will carry on his sledge, embroidered with his family's crest or his initials.

Scott is touched. He writes in his journal that evening that he has been very lucky in the men who have come with him.

--

{img:midwinter-feast-flags-table-celebration}
Then, on the twenty-second of June, comes the most important day of the whole Antarctic year: Midwinter Day.

It is the shortest day, the middle of the long night. From now on, the sun will begin to come back. It is the Antarctic Christmas, and the men celebrate it with a great feast.

The table is decorated with flags and candles. There is soup, and roast beef, and plum pudding, and mince pies, and champagne. There are toasts, and speeches, and songs. Bowers brings in a little Christmas tree that he has made himself, out of ski sticks and feathers and coloured paper, hung with little presents for everyone.

The party goes on until late in the night. Everyone agrees that it has been a wonderful evening.

--

{img:hut-window-glowing-dark-snow-night}
And then, a few days later, three of the men set out on a journey into the darkness that will become one of the most famous journeys in the history of exploration.

Wilson, Bowers and Cherry-Garrard are going to try to reach the emperor penguins.

They are going to walk, in the middle of the Antarctic winter, in complete darkness, at temperatures far colder than anyone has ever travelled in before, to a place called Cape Crozier, sixty miles away, to find the penguins' eggs.
