At the beginning of January 1911, the Terra Nova breaks out of the pack ice at last, and sails into the calm, open water of the Ross Sea.

And there, ahead of the ship, rising out of the sea, is Antarctica.

--

The first thing the men see is a mountain.

It is called Mount Erebus, and it is a volcano, more than twelve thousand feet high, completely covered in snow and ice, except for a dark crater at the very top, from which a thin plume of steam and smoke rises lazily into the sky and drifts away on the wind.

Below it, the land runs down in long white slopes to the sea, broken by black rocks and blue-green glaciers. The water is dark and still, and full of floating ice. The sky is pale blue. The air is so clear that mountains a hundred miles away look close enough to walk to.

--

{img:ross-island-coast-cape-black-rocks}
Scott chooses a place to build the expedition's hut, on a low cape of black volcanic rock and sand, sticking out from the coast of Ross Island, below the slopes of Erebus.

He names it Cape Evans, after his second in command, Lieutenant Teddy Evans.

On the fourth of January, the ship ties up to the ice at the edge of the cape, and the work of unloading begins.

--

There is an enormous amount to unload: the materials for the hut, the stores of food and fuel, the coal, the scientific instruments, the sledges and tents and clothes, the ponies, the dogs, and the motor sledges.

Everything must be carried from the ship across the sea ice to the shore, about a mile and a half. The ponies pull sledges loaded with boxes, and the dogs pull smaller ones, and the men drag the rest themselves, all day and all night, in the endless summer daylight.

One of the three motor sledges is lost on the first day. As it is being lowered from the ship, it breaks through the ice and sinks into the sea. The men look at the hole in the ice in dismay. But they carry on.

--

Meanwhile, on the cape, the carpenters are building the hut.

It is made of wood, brought all the way from England in numbered pieces, like a giant puzzle. It is about fifty feet long and twenty-five feet wide, with a sloping roof. Its walls are double, with a layer of seaweed packed between them to keep out the cold, and there is a thick layer of felt and wood on the floor.

{img:wooden-hut-construction-cape-snow}
In less than two weeks, the hut is finished. Around it, the men build stables for the ponies out of boxes and bales of hay, and kennels for the dogs, and little sheds and shelters for the scientific instruments.

By the middle of January, they have a home.

--

The hut stands on a little rise of black sand, near the edge of the sea.

To the north and west, it looks out over the frozen bay and the open water beyond, toward the distant mountains on the far side of the sound, white and blue and pink in the long summer light. Behind it, to the east, the slopes of Erebus climb up and up toward the smoking summit.

{img:hut-cape-evans-erebus-behind-sunlight}
It is a beautiful place, wild and lonely and very quiet. On calm days, the only sounds are the soft lapping of the sea against the ice, the cries of the skuas overhead, and the occasional bark of a dog.

--

The men soon settle into a routine.

In the mornings, the scientists go out to work. The geologists climb the hills to study the rocks. The biologist cuts holes in the sea ice and lowers nets into the dark water, to catch the strange creatures that live beneath it. The meteorologist reads his instruments in the little shelter outside, and sends up balloons to measure the winds high above.

The sailors and the other men look after the stores, and the animals, and the hut, and prepare for the journeys to come.

--

The photographer, Herbert Ponting, is busy everywhere.

He is a tall, dignified man of about forty, who has travelled all over the world with his camera, and taken famous pictures in Japan and India and China. He calls himself a camera artist. He has brought with him the best cameras that money can buy, and a film camera, for moving pictures, and he has built himself a little darkroom in the hut.

He goes out every day, in all weathers, carrying his heavy wooden cameras and his tripod, to photograph the ice and the mountains, the penguins and the seals, the men at work, the ship in the bay. His pictures will become some of the most beautiful photographs ever taken.

--

One day, Ponting has an adventure.

He is out on the sea ice near the edge of the water, with his camera, when a pod of killer whales appears, swimming along the edge of the ice. Suddenly, the whales dive under the ice where he is standing, and come up beneath it, breaking it into pieces with their backs, trying to tip him into the water.

{img:killer-whales-ice-edge-calm-water}
Ponting jumps from one piece of floating ice to another, very quickly, and reaches the solid ice just in time. The whales put their heads up out of the water and look at him for a moment, and then they swim away.

Ponting, being a true photographer, is very sorry that he did not have time to take their picture.

--

In the evenings, after the day's work, the men often sit outside the hut for a while, on boxes and on the black sand, smoking their pipes and looking out over the water.

The light at this time of year is wonderful. Even late at night, the sun hangs low in the south, and its long golden rays fall across the ice, turning the icebergs pink and the shadows blue. The sea is calm and dark, and the reflections of the mountains lie upside down in it, perfectly still.

Somewhere along the shore, a penguin calls. A seal surfaces in a pool of open water, blows out a little cloud of breath, and slips under again. Then everything is quiet once more.

--

In late January, the Terra Nova sails away along the coast, to land a smaller party of men in another place, and then to return to New Zealand for the winter.

The men who stay behind at Cape Evans stand on the shore and watch her go, until her smoke disappears over the horizon.

{img:ship-disappearing-horizon-ice-evening}
They will not see her again for more than a year. They are alone now, twenty-five men, on the edge of the frozen continent, with their hut and their animals and their stores, and the long, dark winter ahead of them.
