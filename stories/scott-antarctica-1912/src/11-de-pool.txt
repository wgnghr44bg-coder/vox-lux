The five men march south across the high plateau, pulling their sledge behind them.

They walk in a line, side by side in their harnesses, leaning forward against the weight. Scott is in the middle. On one side of him is Wilson, quiet and steady. On the other side are Edgar Evans and Oates, the big Welsh sailor and the tall cavalry officer. Bowers, who has no skis, because he was told to leave them behind with an earlier party, walks on foot beside them, taking shorter steps, keeping pace with the others.

--

The plateau is a strange, lonely world.

It is nearly two miles above the sea, and the air is thin, so that the men are short of breath, and tire easily. The snow is dry and gritty, like sand, and the sledge drags heavily over it. Sometimes, the wind blows the surface into long, hard ridges, called sastrugi, which run across their path like frozen waves.

{img:sastrugi-snow-ridges-plateau-low-sun}
There is nothing to see in any direction, except the snow, and the sky, and the sun, circling round and round above them. On clear days, the light is dazzling, and the men wear goggles to protect their eyes. On grey days, the snow and the sky melt together into a featureless whiteness, and it is hard to tell which way is up.

--

It is very cold on the plateau, even in summer.

The temperature is usually between ten and thirty degrees below zero, and the wind makes it feel much colder. The men's beards are always full of ice. Their fingers and toes and noses are often frostbitten. At night, in the tent, they rub each other's feet to warm them.

But inside the tent, in the evening, with the Primus stove roaring and a hot meal cooking, it is almost cosy. The five men sit close together on their sleeping bags, eating their pemmican and their biscuits, and drinking hot cocoa, and talking quietly about home, and about the Pole, which is coming closer every day.

Bowers takes the measurements of the sun with his instruments, and works out their position. They are going well. Each day, they come eleven or twelve miles nearer.

--

Each of the five men has his own job in the tent.

Bowers looks after the instruments and the navigation, and keeps the records of the weather. Wilson looks after the men's health, and their feet and hands, and sketches the sky and the snow whenever he can. Evans is the handyman, who mends the sledge and the tent and the harness, with his big, skilful hands. Oates looks after the stores of food. And Scott leads the way, and makes the decisions, and writes the journal of the journey every evening, in pencil, in a small notebook.

At night, they lie side by side in their sleeping bags, in a row, packed close together for warmth, and talk a little before they sleep, about home, and the people they love, and the journey that remains.

--

On the sixteenth of January, 1912, they are only about fifteen miles from the Pole.

They are in good spirits. Tomorrow, or the day after, they will be there. They will be the first people ever to stand at the South Pole.

{img:black-flag-distant-snow-plain}
Then, in the afternoon, Bowers sees something ahead of them, a small dark speck on the white plain. At first, he thinks it is a cairn of snow, or a trick of the light. As they come closer, they see that it is a black flag, tied to a sledge runner, standing upright in the snow.

All around it, there are the tracks of sledges and skis, and the paw prints of many dogs.

The Norwegians have been there first.

--

The men stand in silence for a while.

Then they put up their tent, and cook their supper, and talk quietly about it. They are very disappointed. Scott writes in his diary that night: The worst has happened. All the day dreams must go.

But they have come so far, and they will go on to the Pole.

--

On the seventeenth of January, they march the last few miles.

The day is bitterly cold, with a strong wind blowing in their faces, and the temperature is far below zero. That evening, according to Bowers' measurements, they reach the South Pole.

{img:five-men-standing-pole-flag-wind}
Scott writes in his diary: Great God! This is an awful place, and terrible enough for us to have laboured to it without the reward of priority.

They have a special meal in the tent that night, to mark the day, with a little extra food, and some of the chocolate and cigarettes they have been saving.

--

The next day, they find the Norwegians' tent, a little way off, standing alone on the snow, with the Norwegian flag flying above it.

Inside, there is some equipment, and a note from Amundsen to Scott, asking him to send on the letter to the King of Norway, which is lying beside it. Scott takes the letter, carefully, to carry home.

The five men build a small cairn of snow, and put up their own flag, the Union Jack, and take some photographs of themselves, with a camera that Bowers has carried, by pulling a long piece of string to work the shutter.

--

In the photograph, the five of them stand and sit together in the snow, in their heavy clothes, with their faces dark from the sun and the wind, and their beards white with frost.

Scott stands in the middle, looking straight at the camera. Oates stands on one side of him, tall and thin, and Evans on the other. In front of them sit Bowers, holding the string, and Wilson.

{img:five-explorers-group-portrait-snow}
They look very tired. But they look steady, too, and brave, and they look like friends.

The photograph will come home, one day, and it will become one of the most famous photographs ever taken.

--

Then, on the nineteenth of January, they turn around, and start the long march home.

They have eight hundred miles to go, back across the plateau, down the glacier, and across the Barrier, to the hut at Cape Evans.

{img:men-marching-north-wind-at-backs}
For the first days, the wind is behind them, and they rig a sail on the sledge, made from the floor cloth of the tent, and it helps to push them along across the snow. Scott writes in his diary that it is a relief to have the wind at their backs, instead of in their faces.

Behind them, the little cluster of flags and tents at the Pole grows smaller and smaller, and then disappears into the white.
