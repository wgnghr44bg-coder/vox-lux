On the first of November, 1911, Scott and the main party leave Cape Evans for the Pole.

The ponies set out across the sea ice in a long, straggling line, each one led by a man and pulling a loaded sledge behind it. Oates leads Christopher, the wildest of them, who has to be held down by four men before he can be harnessed, and then sets off at a gallop. Cherry leads Michael. Bowers leads Victor.

--

The men who stay behind at the hut, Simpson and Ponting and a few others, stand on the shore and watch them go.

Ponting films the departure with his moving picture camera, so that people at home will be able to see it. Then he goes back inside and develops his photographs. The hut seems very quiet and empty.

Behind the ponies come the dog teams, with Meares and Dimitri, who will catch up quickly, because the dogs are much faster.

--

Out on the Barrier, the travelling falls into a routine.

The men march at night, when it is a little colder and the snow is firmer, and rest during the day, when the sun is high. It is always light now. The sun circles the sky without setting, sometimes high, sometimes low, but never going down.

{img:night-march-midnight-sun-low-ponies}
Each morning, or rather each evening, they wake in their tents, cook breakfast on the little Primus stoves, pack up, harness the ponies, and set off across the snow. They march for about ten or twelve miles, then stop, put up the tents, build snow walls to shelter the ponies from the wind, feed the animals, cook their own supper, write their diaries, and sleep.

--

The Barrier is very strange, and very beautiful.

It is flat and white and empty, in every direction, as far as the eye can see. There is no sound, except the creak of the sledges and the crunch of the snow underfoot and the breathing of the ponies. The sky is enormous. Sometimes there are mirages, and distant mountains seem to float above the horizon, upside down, shimmering.

{img:mirage-floating-mountains-ice-plain}
On clear days, far away to the west, the men can see the long line of the Transantarctic Mountains, white and blue and gold, running south toward the Pole.

--

They pass the motor sledges, broken down and abandoned in the snow. The engines were not made for such cold. Teddy Evans and Lashly and their men have gone on ahead, pulling their sledges themselves.

They pass One Ton Depot, and stock up with food. They keep going south.

The ponies grow weaker as they go. The cold and the hard work and the poor food are too much for them. One by one, when they can go no further, they are put down quietly, as had always been planned, and their meat is used to feed the dogs and the men. Oates is very sad about it. He has looked after them for so long.

--

At the beginning of December, a terrible blizzard strikes, just as the party reaches the foot of the Beardmore Glacier.

For four days, the men lie in their tents while the wind roars and the snow piles up around them. The temperature is unusually warm, and the snow is wet. Everything gets soaked. They eat into the food that they had meant to save for the glacier.

{img:tents-buried-blizzard-glacier-foot}
When the blizzard is over, the last of the ponies are gone. The men call the place Shambles Camp.

--

The dogs go a little further up the glacier with them. Then, on the eleventh of December, Meares and Dimitri turn back with the dog teams, toward Cape Evans, as planned.

There are twelve men left, in three teams of four. Now they must pull the sledges themselves.

{img:men-hauling-sledges-beardmore-glacier}
They put on their harnesses, and lean forward, and pull, and the heavy sledges begin to move, slowly, up the great glacier. Around them, the mountains rise up on both sides, enormous and silent, with bare brown rock showing through the snow. Ahead, the glacier rises in long, broken steps of blue ice, toward the plateau.

--

The climb up the glacier takes about two weeks.

It is very hard work. Sometimes the snow is soft and deep, and the sledges sink into it, and the men have to pull with all their strength to move them a few feet. Sometimes the ice is hard and slippery, and full of hidden crevasses, deep cracks in the ice covered by thin bridges of snow, and now and then someone falls through, up to the length of his harness, and is pulled out again by his companions.

But the views are magnificent, and the weather is often fine and sunny. Wilson stops to sketch the mountains. The geologists in the party pick up rocks and fossils.

--

On Christmas Day, 1911, they are near the top of the glacier, almost on the plateau.

That evening, they have a feast in the tents: pemmican with pony meat, and biscuits, and a little chocolate, and raisins, and a small plum pudding that Bowers has been carrying secretly all the way from the hut. They are so full afterwards that they can hardly move.

Lashly turns forty-four that day. Everyone wishes him a happy birthday.

--

On the fourth of January, 1912, about a hundred and fifty miles from the Pole, the last support party turns back.

Scott has chosen who will go on with him to the Pole. He has chosen Wilson, and Oates, and Petty Officer Edgar Evans. And at the last moment, he decides to take a fifth man, Birdie Bowers, as well.

{img:two-groups-parting-plateau-handshakes}
The other three men, Teddy Evans, Lashly and Crean, shake hands with them, and wish them luck, and then turn north, and start the long march home. Crean is in tears. He would have liked very much to go on.

Scott and his four companions watch them grow smaller and smaller on the white plain, until they are only three little dots, and then nothing.

Then the five of them turn south, toward the Pole.
