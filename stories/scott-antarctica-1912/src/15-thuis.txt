On the tenth of February, 1913, the Terra Nova sails quietly into a little harbour on the coast of New Zealand, in the early hours of the morning.

Two of the men row ashore, and go to the telegraph office, and send a message to London.

Within a few hours, the news is all over the world.

--

In Britain, people are stunned.

The newspapers print the story on their front pages, with black borders. Crowds gather outside the newspaper offices to read the latest bulletins. In schools, teachers read Scott's last message to the children. People speak of the five men in hushed voices, on trains and in shops, in churches and in homes.

On the fourteenth of February, a great memorial service is held at St. Paul's Cathedral in London, attended by the King. Thousands of people stand outside in the cold, unable to get in. Inside, the organ plays, and the choir sings, and many people weep.

--

Kathleen Scott hears the news on board a ship, on her way to New Zealand to meet her husband.

The captain of the ship receives the news by wireless, and does not know how to tell her. For a day, he keeps it to himself. Then he goes to her cabin, and tells her, as gently as he can.

{img:woman-ship-deck-sea-calm-thoughtful}
She is very brave. She goes out on deck, and walks up and down for a long time, looking at the sea. Then she goes back to her cabin, and sits down, and begins to read a book, very slowly, because she wants to be calm when she arrives.

--

Back in England, she reads her husband's last letter to her.

And she remembers what he asked, about their son, Peter: to make the boy interested in natural history.

She does exactly that. Peter grows up loving birds and animals and the countryside. He becomes a painter of birds, and a naturalist, and one of the most famous lovers of nature in Britain. He sets up a great centre for wild ducks and geese and swans, at Slimbridge, beside the River Severn, where people can come and watch the birds.

And in 1961, he helps to start an organization to protect the wild animals of the world, whose symbol is a giant panda. Today, it is known all over the world as the WWF.

His father would have been very proud.

--

The other members of the expedition go on with their lives.

Many of them serve in the Great War, which begins only a year and a half later. Some of them do not come home from it. Others live long lives, and become scientists and explorers, writers and sailors.

{img:old-man-writing-book-country-house-study}
Apsley Cherry-Garrard goes home to his family's estate in Hertfordshire, and, after the war, writes his book about the expedition, The Worst Journey in the World. It is beautifully written, honest and sad and funny, and it becomes one of the best-loved books of its kind. He never really stops thinking about his friends who were lost.

Tom Crean goes on to sail with Ernest Shackleton to Antarctica, on another famous expedition, and then retires to Ireland, where he opens a small pub in his home village of Annascaul, and calls it the South Pole Inn.

--

Edward Wilson's sketches and watercolours come home too.

They are found in the tent beside him, carefully wrapped, and carried back to England. There are hundreds of them: paintings of the mountains and the glaciers, of the sun's halos and the southern lights, of penguins and seals and birds, of the hut at Cape Evans, and of the ship in the ice. Today, many of them hang in the museum in Cheltenham, his home town, and at the Scott Polar Research Institute in Cambridge, which was founded in memory of the five men.

People still come to look at them, and are surprised at how gentle and beautiful they are, after such a hard journey.

--

Roald Amundsen is hailed as a hero, in Norway and around the world.

He goes on exploring for many more years. In 1926, he flies over the North Pole in an airship, called the Norge. He is a quiet, private man, and he always speaks with great respect of Scott and his companions. He says that he would gladly give up the honour of reaching the Pole, if it could bring them back to life.

{img:norwegian-explorer-older-fjord-home}
In 1928, he flies north over the Arctic Ocean to look for some lost explorers, and is never seen again.

--

The emperor penguin eggs, carried home so carefully by Wilson, Bowers and Cherry-Garrard, are taken to the Natural History Museum in London.

At first, nobody at the museum seems very interested in them. When Cherry-Garrard brings them in, a clerk takes them and gives him a receipt, and does not even say thank you.

In the end, the scientists find that the eggs do not prove what Wilson had hoped. But they are kept, carefully, in a quiet drawer at the museum, where they still lie today, three large pale eggs, with a little label beside them.

--

The fossils that Wilson and Bowers collected on the Beardmore Glacier, and carried all the way to the last tent, are studied by scientists, too.

They turn out to be very important. They are leaves of the plant called Glossopteris, which also grew in South America and Africa, India and Australia. They help to show that, long ago, all those lands were joined together with Antarctica in one great southern continent, which later slowly broke apart.

{img:fossil-leaf-stone-museum-lamp}
So the five men who walked to the Pole brought home something important after all: a small piece of the great story of the Earth itself.
