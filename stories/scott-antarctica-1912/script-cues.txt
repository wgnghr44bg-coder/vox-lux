{img:terra-nova-cardiff-harbour-1910}
Tonight we are going more than a hundred years back, to the edge of a frozen continent at the very bottom of the world.

[long-pause]

But our story begins somewhere much warmer, in a busy harbour in Wales, on a summer afternoon in 1910.

[pause]

The docks of Cardiff are crowded with people. They stand along the quays and on the decks of the other ships, waving hats and handkerchiefs, while a band plays and the gulls wheel overhead. Out in the harbour, an old black wooden ship is getting up steam, with smoke rising from her single funnel, and her three tall masts standing against the sky.

[pause]

If quiet stories like this help you drift off, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

The ship is called the Terra Nova.

[pause]

She is not new, and she is not beautiful. She was built nearly thirty years ago, in Scotland, as a whaling ship, to hunt among the ice of the far north. Her hull is made of thick oak, extra strong at the bow, so that she can push her way through ice. She is about a hundred and eighty-seven feet long, and she smells, people say, of whale oil and tar and old wood, however much she is scrubbed.

[pause]

{img:old-wooden-ship-deck-crates-ponies}
Her decks are crammed with crates and barrels and sacks, and with bales of hay, and with sacks of coal piled everywhere. There are three motor sledges in big wooden boxes, lashed down on the deck. And later, in New Zealand, she will take on board nineteen ponies and thirty-three dogs.

[long-pause]

The ship is setting out on an expedition to Antarctica.

[pause]

Antarctica is a continent of ice, larger than the United States and Mexico put together, surrounding the South Pole. It is the coldest, windiest, driest place on Earth. Almost all of it is covered by a great sheet of ice, in some places more than two miles thick. No one lives there. No trees grow there. In winter, the sun does not rise for months.

[pause]

In 1910, almost nothing is known about it. Only a few ships have ever been there, and only a few people have ever set foot on its shores. And no one, in all of human history, has ever reached the South Pole.

[long-pause]

{img:captain-scott-naval-uniform-portrait}
The leader of the expedition is a naval officer named Robert Falcon Scott.

[pause]

He is forty-two years old, a captain in the Royal Navy, of medium height, with a strong, square face, a high forehead, and blue eyes. He is thoughtful and determined, sometimes moody, sometimes shy, and deeply serious about his work. His men respect him, and many of them become very fond of him.

[pause]

He has been to Antarctica before, on an expedition between 1901 and 1904, aboard a ship called the Discovery. On that journey, he and two companions marched further south than anyone had ever gone before. But they did not reach the Pole. Now he has come back to try again.

[long-pause]

He has left behind him in England his wife, Kathleen, and their baby son, Peter, who is not yet a year old.

[pause]

Kathleen Scott is a sculptor, a lively, independent, adventurous woman, who has studied art in Paris and has friends among the famous artists and writers of the day. She will travel with the ship as far as New Zealand, to see her husband off on the last stage of his journey.

[pause]

{img:sculptor-woman-studio-london-1910}
Peter has been left at home, in London, with a nurse. Scott thinks of him often, and keeps a photograph of him in his cabin.

[long-pause]

The expedition is not only about reaching the Pole.

[pause]

There are scientists on board: geologists, to study the rocks; a biologist, to study the creatures of the sea and the ice; a physicist and a meteorologist, to study the weather and the magnetism of the Earth. There is a doctor and an artist, and a photographer, who will take pictures and even moving films of everything they see.

[pause]

They will spend more than two years in Antarctica. They will measure, and record, and collect, and bring home a great treasure of knowledge about this unknown land.

[long-pause]

There are about sixty-five men on the ship, sailors and scientists, officers and stokers.

[pause]

They are mostly young. Many of them are in their twenties. They come from all over Britain, and from Australia and Canada, and one of them, a young ski expert, from Norway. Some of them are navy men, used to the hard life of a ship. Others have never been to sea at all.

[pause]

{img:young-men-crew-deck-1910-sun}
They have been chosen from more than eight thousand men who wrote to Scott asking to come. They are proud to be here, and very excited.

[long-pause]

At last, in the late afternoon, the Terra Nova casts off from the quay.

[pause]

The crowd cheers. The band plays. The ships in the harbour blow their whistles and their sirens, one after another, until the whole harbour is ringing with noise. The men on the Terra Nova stand along the rails and wave back, and some of them climb up into the rigging to wave from there.

[pause]

{img:ship-leaving-harbour-evening-sunset}
Slowly, the old black ship moves out of the harbour, past the lighthouse, and into the open water of the Bristol Channel. The crowd grows small behind her, and the green hills of Wales grow blue and faint.

[long-pause]

That evening, the men sit together in the little wardroom below deck, and eat their first supper at sea.

[pause]

The ship is rolling gently. The lamps swing on their chains. Through the round portholes, they can see the last light fading over the sea. Someone opens a bottle of wine, and someone proposes a toast: to the expedition, and to the Pole.

[pause]

Ahead of them lies a journey of more than half the length of the world, across the Atlantic and around the Cape of Good Hope, to Australia and New Zealand, and then south, and south again, into the ice.

[long-pause]

{img:sailing-ship-tropical-sea-calm}
For weeks, the Terra Nova sails south through the Atlantic.

[pause]

The weather grows warm, and then hot. The men take off their heavy woollen clothes and work on deck in their shirtsleeves, with their faces turning brown in the sun. Flying fish skim across the waves beside the ship. In the evenings, the sea glows with tiny lights, and the stars are bright and strange, and new ones appear in the south that the men have never seen before.

[long-pause]

Life on board is busy.

[pause]

The ship is short of sailors, so everyone helps. The scientists and the officers take their turn hauling on the ropes, scrubbing the decks, and shovelling coal into the furnaces deep in the hull, where the heat is so great that the men work stripped to the waist, black with coal dust from head to foot.

[pause]

{img:men-hauling-ropes-deck-sunlight}
They do not mind. It is all part of the adventure, and it brings them together. By the time they reach the far south, the men who worked side by side in the stokehold will be friends for life.

[long-pause]

There are many characters among them.

[pause]

There is Edward Wilson, the doctor and the chief of the scientists, whom everyone calls Uncle Bill. He is thirty-eight, slim and quiet, with a gentle, thoughtful face and a soft voice. He is also a wonderful artist, and he spends hours on deck with his sketchbook and his little box of watercolours, painting the sea, the sky and the birds.

[pause]

He is Scott's closest friend. The two of them were together on the earlier expedition, and Scott trusts him more than anyone.

[long-pause]

{img:short-red-haired-officer-ship-stores}
There is Henry Bowers, a short, stocky young lieutenant from the Royal Indian Marine, with bright red hair and an enormous nose. Because of the nose, everyone calls him Birdie.

[pause]

Birdie is twenty-seven. He is cheerful and tireless, extraordinarily strong, and almost never seems to feel the cold. He is in charge of the stores, and he knows exactly where every box and every sack on the ship is to be found. When he first came aboard, he fell down an open hatch, nineteen feet, into the hold, landed on the pig iron at the bottom, and climbed back up, quite unhurt, saying it was nothing.

[long-pause]

There is Lawrence Oates, a tall, quiet, rather gloomy army officer from a cavalry regiment, who has paid a large sum of money to join the expedition, and who will look after the ponies.

[pause]

The men call him Titus, or sometimes the Soldier. He is thirty, with a long, weathered face, and he walks with a slight limp, from an old wound he received in the war in South Africa. He does not say very much. But when he does, he is often very funny.

[pause]

He likes to sit in a corner, smoking his pipe, reading a book about Napoleon.

[long-pause]

And there is a young man of twenty-four named Apsley Cherry-Garrard, whom everyone calls Cherry.

[pause]

He has just finished university, at Oxford, and he has no special scientific training. He is shortsighted, and wears round spectacles. But he is eager, kind and willing, and Wilson, who knows his family, has recommended him. He is to be an assistant zoologist, and to help wherever he is needed.

[pause]

Years later, Cherry will write a book about the expedition, which he will call The Worst Journey in the World. It will become one of the most famous books of travel ever written.

[long-pause]

The Terra Nova stops in South Africa, and then in Australia, and then, in October, she arrives in New Zealand.

[pause]

{img:lyttelton-harbour-green-hills-1910}
There, in the little harbour town of Lyttelton, among green hills, the ship is unloaded, cleaned, repaired, and loaded again, even more heavily than before. The ponies and the dogs are brought on board, and shut into stalls and kennels on the deck. The last letters are written and posted. The last visits are made.

[pause]

On the twenty-ninth of November, 1910, the ship leaves New Zealand, and turns south.

[long-pause]

Almost at once, a great storm strikes.

[pause]

For two days, the overloaded ship rolls and pitches in enormous waves. Water pours over the decks. The pumps become blocked, and the men form a long line and bail out the engine room with buckets, all through the night, singing songs to keep their spirits up.

[pause]

When at last the storm passes, everyone is exhausted, soaking wet, and still alive. And the ship sails on.

[long-pause]

{img:first-iceberg-blue-sea-ship}
A little more than a week later, someone on deck sees the first iceberg.

[pause]

It is a vast, flat-topped block of white ice, as big as a town, floating silently in the grey sea, with the waves breaking softly against its foot. Then there is another, and another. The air grows cold. The sea turns dark. And then the ship comes to the pack ice.

[long-pause]

The pack ice is a great field of floating ice, broken into pieces of every size, from little chunks no bigger than a table to great flat floes as big as a field, all drifting and grinding together on the dark water.

[pause]

The Terra Nova pushes her way into it, slowly, ramming the floes with her strong oak bow, and backing off, and ramming again. Sometimes she is stuck for days. On Christmas Day, 1910, she is stuck fast in the ice.

[pause]

{img:ship-in-pack-ice-midnight-sun}
The men hold a Christmas dinner in the wardroom, with roast mutton and plum pudding, and sing songs late into the night. Outside, it is still full daylight, at midnight. In the far south, in summer, the sun never sets.

[pause]

Penguins come waddling across the ice to look at the ship, curious and unafraid. The men laugh, and sing to them, and the penguins stand and listen, with their heads on one side.

[long-pause]

{img:mount-erebus-smoking-volcano-sea}
At the beginning of January 1911, the Terra Nova breaks out of the pack ice at last, and sails into the calm, open water of the Ross Sea.

[pause]

And there, ahead of the ship, rising out of the sea, is Antarctica.

[long-pause]

The first thing the men see is a mountain.

[pause]

It is called Mount Erebus, and it is a volcano, more than twelve thousand feet high, completely covered in snow and ice, except for a dark crater at the very top, from which a thin plume of steam and smoke rises lazily into the sky and drifts away on the wind.

[pause]

Below it, the land runs down in long white slopes to the sea, broken by black rocks and blue-green glaciers. The water is dark and still, and full of floating ice. The sky is pale blue. The air is so clear that mountains a hundred miles away look close enough to walk to.

[long-pause]

{img:ross-island-coast-cape-black-rocks}
Scott chooses a place to build the expedition's hut, on a low cape of black volcanic rock and sand, sticking out from the coast of Ross Island, below the slopes of Erebus.

[pause]

He names it Cape Evans, after his second in command, Lieutenant Teddy Evans.

[pause]

On the fourth of January, the ship ties up to the ice at the edge of the cape, and the work of unloading begins.

[long-pause]

There is an enormous amount to unload: the materials for the hut, the stores of food and fuel, the coal, the scientific instruments, the sledges and tents and clothes, the ponies, the dogs, and the motor sledges.

[pause]

Everything must be carried from the ship across the sea ice to the shore, about a mile and a half. The ponies pull sledges loaded with boxes, and the dogs pull smaller ones, and the men drag the rest themselves, all day and all night, in the endless summer daylight.

[pause]

One of the three motor sledges is lost on the first day. As it is being lowered from the ship, it breaks through the ice and sinks into the sea. The men look at the hole in the ice in dismay. But they carry on.

[long-pause]

Meanwhile, on the cape, the carpenters are building the hut.

[pause]

It is made of wood, brought all the way from England in numbered pieces, like a giant puzzle. It is about fifty feet long and twenty-five feet wide, with a sloping roof. Its walls are double, with a layer of seaweed packed between them to keep out the cold, and there is a thick layer of felt and wood on the floor.

[pause]

{img:wooden-hut-construction-cape-snow}
In less than two weeks, the hut is finished. Around it, the men build stables for the ponies out of boxes and bales of hay, and kennels for the dogs, and little sheds and shelters for the scientific instruments.

[pause]

By the middle of January, they have a home.

[long-pause]

The hut stands on a little rise of black sand, near the edge of the sea.

[pause]

To the north and west, it looks out over the frozen bay and the open water beyond, toward the distant mountains on the far side of the sound, white and blue and pink in the long summer light. Behind it, to the east, the slopes of Erebus climb up and up toward the smoking summit.

[pause]

{img:hut-cape-evans-erebus-behind-sunlight}
It is a beautiful place, wild and lonely and very quiet. On calm days, the only sounds are the soft lapping of the sea against the ice, the cries of the skuas overhead, and the occasional bark of a dog.

[long-pause]

The men soon settle into a routine.

[pause]

In the mornings, the scientists go out to work. The geologists climb the hills to study the rocks. The biologist cuts holes in the sea ice and lowers nets into the dark water, to catch the strange creatures that live beneath it. The meteorologist reads his instruments in the little shelter outside, and sends up balloons to measure the winds high above.

[pause]

The sailors and the other men look after the stores, and the animals, and the hut, and prepare for the journeys to come.

[long-pause]

The photographer, Herbert Ponting, is busy everywhere.

[pause]

He is a tall, dignified man of about forty, who has travelled all over the world with his camera, and taken famous pictures in Japan and India and China. He calls himself a camera artist. He has brought with him the best cameras that money can buy, and a film camera, for moving pictures, and he has built himself a little darkroom in the hut.

[pause]

He goes out every day, in all weathers, carrying his heavy wooden cameras and his tripod, to photograph the ice and the mountains, the penguins and the seals, the men at work, the ship in the bay. His pictures will become some of the most beautiful photographs ever taken.

[long-pause]

One day, Ponting has an adventure.

[pause]

He is out on the sea ice near the edge of the water, with his camera, when a pod of killer whales appears, swimming along the edge of the ice. Suddenly, the whales dive under the ice where he is standing, and come up beneath it, breaking it into pieces with their backs, trying to tip him into the water.

[pause]

{img:killer-whales-ice-edge-calm-water}
Ponting jumps from one piece of floating ice to another, very quickly, and reaches the solid ice just in time. The whales put their heads up out of the water and look at him for a moment, and then they swim away.

[pause]

Ponting, being a true photographer, is very sorry that he did not have time to take their picture.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, after the day's work, the men often sit outside the hut for a while, on boxes and on the black sand, smoking their pipes and looking out over the water.

[pause]

The light at this time of year is wonderful. Even late at night, the sun hangs low in the south, and its long golden rays fall across the ice, turning the icebergs pink and the shadows blue. The sea is calm and dark, and the reflections of the mountains lie upside down in it, perfectly still.

[pause]

Somewhere along the shore, a penguin calls. A seal surfaces in a pool of open water, blows out a little cloud of breath, and slips under again. Then everything is quiet once more.

[long-pause]

In late January, the Terra Nova sails away along the coast, to land a smaller party of men in another place, and then to return to New Zealand for the winter.

[pause]

The men who stay behind at Cape Evans stand on the shore and watch her go, until her smoke disappears over the horizon.

[pause]

{img:ship-disappearing-horizon-ice-evening}
They will not see her again for more than a year. They are alone now, twenty-five men, on the edge of the frozen continent, with their hut and their animals and their stores, and the long, dark winter ahead of them.

[long-pause]

{img:hut-interior-wardroom-table-lamps}
Inside the hut at Cape Evans, everything has its place.

[pause]

The hut is divided into two parts by a wall made of packing cases, stacked up from the floor almost to the ceiling. On one side live the officers and the scientists. On the other side live the sailors, the petty officers and the cook. It is how things are done in the navy, and nobody questions it.

[long-pause]

The officers' side is called the wardroom, after the officers' room on a ship.

[pause]

In the middle of it, there is a long wooden table, where everyone eats their meals together. Above it hang lamps, burning acetylene gas, which give a bright, steady white light. Along the walls are bunks, shelves of books, and little cubicles, separated by curtains and more packing cases.

[pause]

{img:bunks-cubicle-books-pictures-hut}
In one corner, Scott has his own small cubicle, with a bunk, a little table, and a shelf of books. On the wall are photographs of Kathleen and Peter. He spends many hours here, writing in his journal and making plans.

[pause]

Next to him, Wilson and Teddy Evans share a cubicle, and near them, the scientists have a space that the others call the Tenements, with five bunks crammed together, where Cherry-Garrard, Bowers, Oates and two others sleep. It is very untidy. Oates keeps his saddles there, and Bowers has piled up clothes and gear to the ceiling.

[long-pause]

At the far end of the hut, there is the kitchen.

[pause]

It belongs to the cook, Thomas Clissold, a young man who was once a cook in a hotel, and who is very good at his job. He has a big iron stove, which burns coal and also seal blubber, and he bakes fresh bread every day, and makes cakes and puddings and pies.

[pause]

{img:hut-kitchen-stove-cook-bread}
The food in the hut is good, and there is plenty of it. There is tinned meat and fish, and seal meat, and penguin, which the men find tastes rather like beef that has been soaked in fish. There are tinned vegetables and fruit, jams and pickles, and great quantities of biscuits, butter, cheese, cocoa and tea.

[pause]

On Sundays, there is something special: roast mutton, perhaps, from a frozen sheep brought from New Zealand, with a jam roly-poly to follow.

[long-pause]

In one corner of the wardroom stands a pianola, a piano that can play itself, if you pump the pedals, from long rolls of paper punched with holes.

[pause]

There is also a gramophone, with a large collection of records. In the evenings, the men take turns to play their favourite music. There are songs from the music halls, and operas, and popular tunes of the day, and some of the quieter men like to listen to the old classical pieces, sitting in the lamplight with their eyes closed.

[pause]

There are books too, hundreds of them, on every subject: novels and histories, poetry and travel books and science. Many of the men read for hours in the long evenings, curled up in their bunks with a candle.

[long-pause]

Ponting has his darkroom in a little room at the side, which is also his bedroom.

[pause]

It is the warmest room in the hut, and it smells of chemicals. Ponting develops his photographs there, in trays of liquid, under a dim red light, and then hangs them up to dry. Sometimes, in the evenings, he gives lectures to the others, about his travels in Japan, and shows them his photographs, projected onto a sheet with a magic lantern.

[long-pause]

{img:scientists-laboratory-corner-microscope}
The scientists have their own corner too, with work benches and shelves full of instruments, bottles, and specimens.

[pause]

There is a microscope, and a set of scales, and boxes of rocks and fossils. There are glass jars of little sea creatures, preserved in spirit. There are charts and graphs on the walls, and piles of notebooks, filled with figures and observations.

[pause]

The meteorologist, George Simpson, whom everyone calls Sunny Jim, has wires running from his instruments outside, through the wall, into his corner, where little pens trace lines on rolls of paper, recording the wind and the temperature and the pressure of the air, day and night.

[long-pause]

On the other side of the wall of packing cases, the sailors have their own mess.

[pause]

They are a cheerful, hardworking group. There is Petty Officer Edgar Evans, a big, strong Welshman, who was with Scott on the earlier expedition, and whom Scott thinks of as one of the finest men he has ever known. There is Tom Crean, a tall, good-humoured Irishman from County Kerry, and William Lashly, a quiet, steady, skilful man who looks after the motor sledges.

[pause]

{img:sailors-mess-pipes-cards-lamplight}
In the evenings, the sailors play cards, smoke their pipes, mend their clothes, and tell stories about their voyages around the world. Sometimes they sing, and the officers on the other side of the wall stop their own talk to listen.

[long-pause]

Outside the hut, there are the stables, built of boxes and bales.

[pause]

Here, the nineteen ponies live in a long row of stalls, with blankets over their backs, eating hay and oats and chewing on the wooden walls. Next to them, in little shelters and on chains in the snow, live the dogs, curled up with their noses tucked under their tails.

[long-pause]

{img:hut-at-night-lit-window-snow-stars}
At the end of the day, when the lamps are turned low, the hut grows quiet.

[pause]

The men climb into their bunks and pull the reindeer skin sleeping bags up to their chins. Someone is still reading by candlelight. Someone is snoring. The stove ticks softly as it cools. Outside, the wind blows across the cape, and the snow hisses against the walls.

[pause]

But inside, it is warm and dry and safe, a little island of light and comfort on the edge of the frozen world.

[long-pause]

{img:ponies-in-snow-stables-blankets}
The ponies came from Manchuria, in the far north of China, and from Siberia.

[pause]

They are small, sturdy, shaggy animals, mostly white or grey, with thick winter coats and short, strong legs. They are used to cold winters and hard work. Scott hopes that they will be able to pull the heavy sledges across the snow, at least for the first part of the journey to the Pole.

[long-pause]

The ponies are looked after by Captain Oates, with the help of a young Russian groom named Anton Omelchenko.

[pause]

Oates does not think very highly of the ponies. He says that they are the worst collection of crocks he has ever seen, old and weak and bad-tempered. But he cares for them with great devotion, all the same. He feeds them, brushes them, checks their hooves, and talks to them quietly in the stables. When one of them is ill, he sits up with it through the night.

[pause]

{img:cavalry-officer-brushing-pony-stable-lamp}
The ponies have names. There is Christopher, who is wild and bad-tempered, and has to be held down by four men to be harnessed. There is Snatcher, and Jehu, and Nobby, and Victor, and Snippets. There is a gentle white pony called Jimmy Pigg, and one called Michael, who is very fond of Cherry-Garrard.

[long-pause]

The dogs came from Siberia too, chosen by a man named Cecil Meares, who had travelled widely in Russia and the East.

[pause]

There are thirty-three of them, big, thick-coated, wolf-like dogs, grey and black and white, with sharp ears and curling tails. They are looked after by Meares, and by a young Russian dog-driver called Dimitri Gerof, who is quiet and gentle and wonderfully skilful with the dogs.

[pause]

The dogs are strong and fast, and they love to run. When they are harnessed to a sledge, they leap and bark and pull at their traces with excitement, and Dimitri has to hold them back. On the snow, they can run for hours, pulling heavy loads, and they sleep curled up in the open, even in a blizzard, with the snow drifting over them.

[long-pause]

But it is not only the expedition's animals that the men come to know.

[pause]

Antarctica, so empty and lifeless on the land, is full of life at the edge of the sea.

[pause]

There are the penguins. Near the hut, there are little Adélie penguins, about knee high, black and white, with a white ring around each eye that makes them look permanently surprised. They are curious and busy and very funny, and they come waddling up to the men to see what they are doing.

[pause]

{img:adelie-penguins-curious-ice-edge}
The men love to watch them. They see the penguins walking in long lines across the ice, sliding on their bellies down the slopes, diving into the sea and shooting out of it again onto the ice like corks. They see them quarrelling over little stones for their nests, and stealing stones from each other's nests when nobody is looking.

[long-pause]

There are seals too, lying on the ice like great grey sausages, sleeping in the sun.

[pause]

The Weddell seals live under the sea ice all year round. They make breathing holes in the ice with their teeth, and come up through them to rest on the surface. When the men walk past, the seals open one large dark eye, look at them sleepily, and go back to sleep.

[pause]

{img:weddell-seal-sleeping-sea-ice-sun}
At night, sometimes, the men hear them singing under the ice: strange, beautiful, echoing calls, like whistles and bells and the sound of falling water, coming up through the floor of the frozen sea.

[long-pause]

There are the skuas, big brown birds like gulls, which fly around the hut and steal anything they can find to eat.

[pause]

There are snow petrels, pure white, flying over the ice like little ghosts. And far out at sea, there are whales, and great albatrosses, gliding on the wind with their long wings perfectly still.

[long-pause]

Edward Wilson, the doctor and artist, watches all of them, and draws them, and paints them.

[pause]

He has studied birds and animals all his life. He knows more about the penguins of the Antarctic than almost anyone in the world. He has a special interest in the largest of them all, the emperor penguin, which is nearly four feet tall, and which lives in great colonies on the sea ice, far from the hut.

[pause]

Nobody knows very much about the emperor penguins. Wilson believes that they lay their eggs in the middle of the dark winter, the coldest time of the year. And he wants to find out more.

[long-pause]

At the end of January 1911, before the winter comes, Scott leads a party south across the ice, with ponies and dogs, to lay down a line of depots, stores of food and fuel, along the route that they will follow to the Pole the next spring.

[pause]

The weather is bad. The ponies sink deep into the soft snow, and struggle, and grow thin and weak. Oates wants to take them further south, and use the weakest ones as food for the dogs. But Scott does not want to be so hard on the animals.

[pause]

{img:depot-flag-cairn-snow-plain-evening}
So they lay the last and biggest depot at a place about thirty miles short of where they had planned, and they mark it with a tall cairn of snow and a black flag, so that they can find it again in the vast white plain. They call it One Ton Depot, because there is about a ton of food and fuel there.

[pause]

Later, those thirty miles will matter a great deal.

[long-pause]

At the end of April, all the men are back at the hut.

[pause]

The sun is very low in the sky now, and each day it rises a little later and sets a little earlier. The temperature falls lower and lower. The sea freezes over, and the ice grows thick enough to walk on. The penguins have gone north, to the open sea, and the birds have gone too.

[pause]

{img:last-sunset-before-winter-ice-hut}
On the twenty-third of April, the men stand outside the hut and watch the sun set for the last time. It sinks slowly behind the mountains in a blaze of red and gold, and is gone.

[pause]

It will not rise again for four months.

[long-pause]

{img:polar-night-hut-moonlight-erebus}
The Antarctic winter is a long night.

[pause]

For four months, from the end of April to the end of August, the sun does not rise at all. At midday, there is a little grey twilight along the northern horizon, a pale glow that lasts for an hour or two and then fades away. The rest of the time, it is dark.

[long-pause]

But the darkness is not always black.

[pause]

When the moon is up, it circles round and round the sky for days on end, never setting, and lights the whole frozen landscape in silver. The snow glows. The mountains stand out sharp and pale against the dark sky. The smoke from Erebus drifts slowly across the moon.

[pause]

And on some nights, the sky is filled with the aurora australis, the southern lights: great curtains and ribbons of pale green and white and rose, rippling and folding and fading across the stars, silently, for hours.

[pause]

{img:aurora-australis-green-curtains-hut}
The men come out of the hut to watch, wrapped in their heavy clothes, and stand in the snow with their heads tilted back, saying nothing, until the cold drives them back inside.

[long-pause]

Inside the hut, life goes on to a steady rhythm.

[pause]

At eight in the morning, the night watchman wakes the cook, and the cook lights the stove and makes the breakfast. At half past eight, everyone gets up, and there is a scramble for the washbasins. Breakfast is porridge, seal liver, bread and butter, marmalade, and tea or cocoa.

[pause]

After breakfast, everyone goes to work. The scientists write up their notes and study their specimens. The sailors repair the sledges and the harness, sew tents, and pack food into bags for the spring journeys. Oates and Anton look after the ponies. Meares and Dimitri look after the dogs.

[long-pause]

Every day, if the weather allows, the men go outside for exercise.

[pause]

They walk across the frozen bay, or up the slopes behind the hut, or around the icebergs frozen into the sea ice, carrying lanterns. They exercise the ponies, leading them in a long line along the shore in the moonlight, with their breath steaming in the cold air.

[pause]

{img:men-leading-ponies-moonlight-sea-ice}
Sometimes, a blizzard blows up, and the wind roars across the cape so loudly that the men inside the hut have to shout to hear each other. Then no one can go out, except to read the instruments, and even that is dangerous. The men tie themselves to a rope stretched from the hut to the instrument shelter, so that they will not lose their way in the whirling snow, only a few yards from the door.

[long-pause]

The evenings are the best time of the day.

[pause]

After dinner, the men sit around the long table, or in their bunks, and read, or write their journals, or play chess or cards, or mend their clothes. Someone puts a record on the gramophone. Someone plays the pianola.

[pause]

{img:men-reading-playing-chess-hut-evening}
Three evenings a week, someone gives a lecture. Wilson talks about the birds and the animals of the Antarctic, and shows his paintings. Ponting shows his photographs of Japan, with cherry blossoms and temples and women in kimonos, so far from the ice and the dark. Simpson talks about the weather. The geologists talk about rocks and glaciers and volcanoes. Oates gives a talk about how to look after horses.

[pause]

Scott talks about his plans for the journey to the Pole, and the men listen very closely.

[long-pause]

There is a newspaper, too.

[pause]

It is called the South Polar Times, and it is edited by Cherry-Garrard. The men write articles and stories and poems for it, sometimes funny and sometimes serious, and Wilson draws pictures for it. It is typed out, one copy only, on the expedition's typewriter, and bound in a beautiful cover made from the wood of packing cases and sealskin.

[pause]

When a new issue is finished, Scott reads it aloud to everyone in the evening, while the authors sit and pretend not to be listening, and everyone tries to guess who wrote what.

[long-pause]

Saturday evening is a special time in the hut.

[pause]

After supper on Saturdays, the men drink a toast to sweethearts and wives, with a small glass of port or sherry, as sailors have done at sea for hundreds of years. Then someone puts on a favourite record, and the talk turns to home: to gardens and pubs and country lanes, to mothers and sisters and girls, to the meals they will eat when they get back to England.

[pause]

Oates, who is not usually a talker, sometimes joins in, and tells stories about hunting in Ireland, and his horses, and his mother's house in Essex, with its gardens and its old trees.

[long-pause]

On the sixth of June, it is Scott's forty-third birthday.

[pause]

There is a special dinner, with a great birthday cake made by Clissold, decorated with candied fruit and chocolate and flags. The table is decorated with sledging flags, the little silk flags that each man will carry on his sledge, embroidered with his family's crest or his initials.

[pause]

Scott is touched. He writes in his journal that evening that he has been very lucky in the men who have come with him.

[long-pause]

{img:midwinter-feast-flags-table-celebration}
Then, on the twenty-second of June, comes the most important day of the whole Antarctic year: Midwinter Day.

[pause]

It is the shortest day, the middle of the long night. From now on, the sun will begin to come back. It is the Antarctic Christmas, and the men celebrate it with a great feast.

[pause]

The table is decorated with flags and candles. There is soup, and roast beef, and plum pudding, and mince pies, and champagne. There are toasts, and speeches, and songs. Bowers brings in a little Christmas tree that he has made himself, out of ski sticks and feathers and coloured paper, hung with little presents for everyone.

[pause]

The party goes on until late in the night. Everyone agrees that it has been a wonderful evening.

[long-pause]

{img:hut-window-glowing-dark-snow-night}
And then, a few days later, three of the men set out on a journey into the darkness that will become one of the most famous journeys in the history of exploration.

[pause]

Wilson, Bowers and Cherry-Garrard are going to try to reach the emperor penguins.

[pause]

They are going to walk, in the middle of the Antarctic winter, in complete darkness, at temperatures far colder than anyone has ever travelled in before, to a place called Cape Crozier, sixty miles away, to find the penguins' eggs.

[long-pause]

{img:three-men-hauling-sledge-dark-lantern}
On the twenty-seventh of June, 1911, three men walk out of the hut at Cape Evans into the dark, pulling two sledges behind them.

[pause]

They are Edward Wilson, Birdie Bowers, and young Cherry-Garrard.

[long-pause]

Why are they going?

[pause]

Wilson believes that the emperor penguin might be one of the most ancient kinds of bird in the world, and that by studying its eggs, and the tiny chicks growing inside them, scientists might learn something about how birds first came to be, long ago, from creatures more like reptiles.

[pause]

And the only way to get the eggs is to go to the emperor colony in the middle of winter, when the penguins are sitting on them.

[long-pause]

The journey is slow and very hard.

[pause]

They pull the two sledges, loaded with their tent, their sleeping bags, their food and fuel and instruments, across the frozen sea ice and the snow. In the dark, they cannot see where they are going. They carry a candle lantern, and steer by the stars, and by the dim shapes of the mountains in the moonlight.

[pause]

{img:polar-night-snow-plain-stars-moonlight}
The cold is beyond anything any of them has ever known. One night, the thermometer falls to seventy-seven degrees below zero, Fahrenheit. Their breath freezes as it leaves their mouths, and falls in little crystals, tinkling, onto their clothes. Their clothes freeze stiff as boards, so that every morning they must bend them, slowly, to get into them.

[long-pause]

Some days, the snow is so soft and cold that the sledge runners will not slide on it, and they must take one sledge forward, and then go back for the other, covering every mile three times.

[pause]

Some days, they travel only a mile or two.

[pause]

But they keep going. Wilson is calm and patient. Bowers is cheerful and tireless, and never seems to feel the cold at all. Cherry, who is shortsighted and cannot wear his spectacles because they freeze over, stumbles along behind them in the dark, and never once complains.

[pause]

{img:small-tent-lantern-glow-dark-snow}
At night, they put up their tent, and light their little stove, and cook a hot meal of pemmican, which is dried meat mixed with fat, and biscuits, and drink hot tea. Then they climb into their frozen sleeping bags and lie shivering until morning, too cold to sleep for long.

[pause]

Through it all, they are always kind to one another. They say please and thank you. They never lose their tempers. Cherry will later write that they were gentlemen, and that he never once heard a cross word between them.

[long-pause]

After nineteen days, they reach Cape Crozier, at the far end of Ross Island.

[pause]

Here, the great ice cliffs of the Barrier, the vast floating shelf of ice that stretches south toward the Pole, press up against the land, and the ice is broken into huge ridges and crevasses.

[pause]

On a rocky slope above the sea, the three men build a little hut of stones and snow, with a sledge for a roof beam and a canvas roof, so that they will have a shelter while they visit the penguins.

[long-pause]

Then, in the faint twilight of the middle of the day, they climb down through the ice ridges to the sea ice below the cliffs.

[pause]

And there they are: the emperor penguins.

[pause]

{img:emperor-penguins-huddle-winter-twilight}
There are about a hundred of them, standing close together in a huddle on the ice, under the great cliffs. They are tall and dignified, with black backs and white fronts and a soft golden-yellow patch on each side of the neck. Some of them are holding eggs on top of their feet, tucked under a warm fold of feathered skin, to keep them off the ice.

[pause]

The penguins make a strange, trumpeting sound, which echoes off the cliffs. The three men stand and look at them, in the dim light, and for a moment they forget the cold.

[long-pause]

They take five eggs, and carry them carefully back up through the ice ridges, in their mittens and in their pockets. Two break on the way. Three come home safely.

[pause]

That night, a terrible storm strikes.

[pause]

The wind blows with a force such as none of them has ever imagined. In the morning, it tears the canvas roof off their little stone hut, and carries their tent away into the darkness. For two days, they lie in their sleeping bags in the roofless hut, with the snow pouring in over them, and the wind roaring above.

[pause]

{img:blizzard-snow-swirling-dark-rocks}
They sing hymns, and songs, to keep their spirits up. They are not sure that they will ever get home.

[long-pause]

When the storm at last dies down, Bowers goes out to search, and, to everyone's astonishment and joy, he finds the tent, lying folded up among the rocks, about half a mile away, undamaged.

[pause]

The three men thank God, and pack up their things, and begin the long walk home.

[pause]

{img:three-tired-men-arriving-hut-dawn}
On the first of August, after five weeks away, they stagger back into the hut at Cape Evans, so stiff with ice that the others have to cut their clothes off them. They are thin and exhausted and frostbitten. But they are alive, and they have the three emperor penguin eggs.

[long-pause]

The others welcome them home with great joy, and sit them down by the stove, and give them hot food and drink, and listen to their story in amazement.

[pause]

Then the three travellers climb into dry sleeping bags, for the first time in five weeks, and sleep, and sleep.

[pause]

Cherry-Garrard will later call it the worst journey in the world. But he will also write that, in all that darkness and cold, he had never known such good companions.

[pause]

The three eggs are taken back to England, and they are still there today, in the Natural History Museum in London.

[long-pause]

{img:sun-returns-horizon-golden-ice}
In the last days of August 1911, the sun comes back.

[pause]

For several days before, the men have watched the glow in the north growing brighter and brighter at midday, turning the sky from grey to pale yellow, and then to orange and red. Then, on a clear, cold morning, a tiny sliver of gold appears above the mountains, and shines for a few minutes across the frozen sea, and sinks again.

[pause]

The men stand outside the hut and cheer. Some of them take off their hats to it.

[long-pause]

Each day after that, the sun stays a little longer.

[pause]

The light changes everything. The snow sparkles. The ice turns blue and green and gold. The mountains glow pink in the morning and purple in the evening. The men feel their spirits rise. They go out walking and skiing on the sea ice, and laugh, and throw snowballs at each other, like schoolboys on holiday.

[pause]

{img:men-skiing-sea-ice-spring-light}
And all their thoughts now turn toward the great journey ahead: the journey to the South Pole.

[long-pause]

Scott has worked out his plan carefully, through the long winter.

[pause]

The Pole is about nine hundred miles from the hut at Cape Evans. The journey there and back will be about eighteen hundred miles, and it will take about five months. Everything the men need, food and fuel and tents and clothes, must be carried with them on sledges, or left in depots along the way.

[pause]

The journey has three parts.

[long-pause]

First, there is the Barrier: a vast, flat plain of ice floating on the sea, about four hundred miles across, as big as France. It is perfectly white and perfectly flat, with nothing on it at all, as far as the eye can see in every direction, and the wind blows across it without stopping.

[pause]

Then, there is the Beardmore Glacier: a great river of ice, more than a hundred miles long, flowing down from the high mountains to the Barrier, full of crevasses and ridges of broken ice. Scott and his companions on the earlier expedition saw it from a distance. Another explorer, Ernest Shackleton, climbed it in 1908, and found it was the way up to the high plateau.

[pause]

And last, there is the plateau itself: an enormous, high, empty dome of ice and snow, nearly two miles above the sea, where the air is thin and the cold is bitter, which stretches for hundreds of miles to the Pole.

[long-pause]

The motor sledges, the ponies and the dogs will help to carry the loads across the Barrier.

[pause]

Then the ponies will go no further. At the foot of the Beardmore Glacier, the dogs will turn back, too. And after that, the men will pull the sledges themselves, in teams of four, harnessed to the sledges like horses, up the glacier and across the plateau.

[pause]

{img:men-in-harness-hauling-sledge-practice}
Along the way, small parties will turn back one by one, so that the food will last for the last group, which will go on to the Pole. Nobody knows yet who will be in that last group. Every man hopes that it will be him.

[long-pause]

All through September and October, the hut is busy with preparations.

[pause]

The sledges are repaired and loaded. The food is weighed out and packed into bags, a week's rations at a time, for each tent. The tents are checked and mended. The sleeping bags of reindeer skin are aired. The ponies are exercised every day, to make them strong. The motor sledges are taken apart and put back together again.

[pause]

{img:packing-ration-bags-hut-table}
Bowers spends whole days weighing and counting and labelling, in his careful, tidy way, until everything is ready.

[long-pause]

There is one more thing on Scott's mind.

[pause]

Back in February, at the end of the depot journey, when the Terra Nova was sailing along the edge of the Barrier, the men on board had seen something they did not expect.

[pause]

In a bay in the ice, several hundred miles to the east, called the Bay of Whales, there was another ship.

[pause]

It was the Fram, the ship of the Norwegian explorer Roald Amundsen. And Amundsen had built a hut there, on the Barrier, with a large team of dogs. He, too, was going to try to reach the South Pole.

[long-pause]

Scott had not known about this until the expedition was already on its way.

[pause]

In Australia, he had received a short telegram from Amundsen, which said only: Beg to inform you Fram proceeding Antarctic. Amundsen. At first, nobody had quite understood what it meant. Now they knew. It was going to be a race.

[pause]

{img:scott-writing-journal-cubicle-candle}
Scott writes in his journal that he does not intend to change his plans. He will go on exactly as he had meant to, as if Amundsen were not there. He will do his best, and if the Norwegians reach the Pole first, then so be it.

[pause]

But the men talk about it often, in the evenings. And they wonder how the Norwegians are getting on, a few hundred miles away across the ice.

[long-pause]

At the end of October, everything is ready.

[pause]

The motor sledges set out first, on the twenty-fourth of October, with Teddy Evans and Lashly and two others, chugging slowly away across the sea ice in a cloud of smoke and noise, pulling their loaded sledges behind them.

[pause]

{img:motor-sledges-departing-sea-ice-smoke}
The men at the hut watch them go, and wave, and cheer. Everyone hopes that the machines will work well. If they do, it will be the first time in history that motor vehicles have been used in Antarctica.

[pause]

A week later, it will be time for the rest of them to go.

[long-pause]

{img:norwegian-hut-framheim-barrier-snow}
About four hundred miles to the east, on the Barrier at the Bay of Whales, the Norwegians have also spent the winter in a hut.

[pause]

They call it Framheim, the home of the Fram. It is a small wooden house, brought from Norway in pieces, standing alone on the flat white ice, half buried in snow, with tents and tunnels dug into the snow around it for the stores and the dogs.

[long-pause]

Roald Amundsen is thirty-nine years old in 1911.

[pause]

He is tall and lean, with a weathered, hawk-like face, a big nose, and deep-set, pale blue eyes. He has wanted to be a polar explorer since he was a boy of fifteen, in Norway, reading about the explorers of the Arctic. As a young man, he trained himself for it, sleeping with his windows open in the winter, and going on long ski journeys through the mountains.

[pause]

He has already been the first to sail through the Northwest Passage, the long-sought sea route through the ice of northern Canada, a journey that took three years. On that voyage, he lived among the Inuit, and learned from them how to live and travel in the Arctic: how to dress in furs, how to drive dogs, how to stay warm and dry in the coldest weather.

[long-pause]

Amundsen had first planned to go to the North Pole.

[pause]

But in 1909, two American explorers each claimed that they had reached it. So Amundsen, without telling almost anyone, changed his plans. He would go south instead. Even his own men did not know until the Fram was out at sea. Then he gathered them on deck and told them, and asked if they would come. Every one of them said yes.

[long-pause]

There are nine men at Framheim, and about a hundred dogs.

[pause]

{img:norwegians-skiing-dogs-sledges-plain}
They are all expert skiers, and expert dog drivers. One of them, Olav Bjaaland, is a champion skier from the mountains of Telemark. Another, Helmer Hanssen, is a famous dog driver from the far north of Norway. The cook, Adolf Lindstrøm, is a cheerful, round little man who makes the best pancakes in the Antarctic, and serves them every Sunday.

[long-pause]

The hut at Framheim is small and snug.

[pause]

There is one main room, with bunks along the walls, a long table in the middle, and the kitchen at one end, where Lindstrøm reigns over his stove. The walls are hung with skis and clothes and instruments. There is a gramophone, and a little library of books, and pictures of home pinned up above the bunks: the mountains and fjords of Norway, families and sweethearts, a farmhouse in the snow.

[pause]

Outside, under the snow, the men have dug a whole little village of rooms and tunnels: a workshop, a store room, a room for the sewing machine, a room for packing the sledges, and even a little steam bath, which they use on Saturday evenings, before scrubbing themselves clean and putting on fresh clothes.

[long-pause]

During the winter, the Norwegians work on their equipment.

[pause]

They make everything as light as possible. They shave down the wooden sledges to make them lighter. They make new boots, and new tents, and new goggles. They pack their food in boxes that fit exactly on the sledges. They wax their skis, and practise with their dogs on the ice around the hut.

[pause]

{img:workshop-snow-cave-lamplight-sledge-repair}
They work in rooms dug out of the snow, lit by lamps, and in the evenings, they gather in the hut to eat and talk and play cards, and listen to the gramophone, and read.

[pause]

Amundsen leads them quietly, without fuss. He is careful and patient and very well organized, and he leaves nothing to chance.

[long-pause]

On the nineteenth of October 1911, five days before Scott's motor sledges set out from Cape Evans, Amundsen leaves Framheim for the Pole, with four companions: Bjaaland, Hanssen, Sverre Hassel and Oscar Wisting.

[pause]

They have four sledges and fifty-two dogs. They travel on skis, quickly and lightly, with the dogs pulling the sledges, covering fifteen or twenty miles a day across the Barrier. They have laid depots of food along their route the autumn before, marked with flags and lines of snow cairns, so that they cannot miss them.

[pause]

{img:dog-teams-crossing-barrier-sunny}
They find a new way up onto the plateau, a steep glacier through the mountains, which Amundsen names after one of his supporters, the Axel Heiberg Glacier. It is very hard work, but the dogs pull the sledges all the way to the top.

[long-pause]

On the plateau, the weather is often cruel, with blizzards and fog and bitter cold. But the Norwegians keep going, steadily, day after day.

[pause]

And at about three o'clock in the afternoon, on the fourteenth of December, 1911, the drivers call out, and the sledges stop. According to their instruments and their careful calculations, they have reached the South Pole.

[pause]

There is nothing there. Only the flat, white, endless snow, stretching away in every direction to the horizon, under a pale blue sky, with a cold wind blowing.

[pause]

The five men gather round and, together, they plant the Norwegian flag in the snow. Amundsen names the high plain around the Pole after the King of Norway.

[long-pause]

They stay for three days, taking measurements, to be sure that they are in exactly the right place.

[pause]

Before they leave, they put up a small tent, with the Norwegian flag flying on top of it. Inside the tent, they leave some spare equipment, and a letter for King Haakon of Norway. And they leave a short note for Captain Scott, asking him, kindly, to send the letter on to the King, in case they do not make it home themselves.

[pause]

{img:small-tent-flag-alone-snow-plain}
Then they turn north, and set off for home, and the little tent is left standing alone on the vast white plain, with its flag fluttering in the wind.

[long-pause]

The Norwegians travel quickly and safely back across the plateau, down the glacier, and across the Barrier.

[pause]

On the twenty-fifth of January, 1912, they arrive back at Framheim, all five of them well and healthy. Lindstrøm, the cook, greets them with hot coffee and pancakes. They have been away for ninety-nine days, and travelled about sixteen hundred miles.

[pause]

A few days later, they board the Fram and sail away north, toward Tasmania, to tell the world what they have done.

[long-pause]

{img:ponies-sledges-leaving-hut-spring}
On the first of November, 1911, Scott and the main party leave Cape Evans for the Pole.

[pause]

The ponies set out across the sea ice in a long, straggling line, each one led by a man and pulling a loaded sledge behind it. Oates leads Christopher, the wildest of them, who has to be held down by four men before he can be harnessed, and then sets off at a gallop. Cherry leads Michael. Bowers leads Victor.

[long-pause]

The men who stay behind at the hut, Simpson and Ponting and a few others, stand on the shore and watch them go.

[pause]

Ponting films the departure with his moving picture camera, so that people at home will be able to see it. Then he goes back inside and develops his photographs. The hut seems very quiet and empty.

[pause]

Behind the ponies come the dog teams, with Meares and Dimitri, who will catch up quickly, because the dogs are much faster.

[long-pause]

Out on the Barrier, the travelling falls into a routine.

[pause]

The men march at night, when it is a little colder and the snow is firmer, and rest during the day, when the sun is high. It is always light now. The sun circles the sky without setting, sometimes high, sometimes low, but never going down.

[pause]

{img:night-march-midnight-sun-low-ponies}
Each morning, or rather each evening, they wake in their tents, cook breakfast on the little Primus stoves, pack up, harness the ponies, and set off across the snow. They march for about ten or twelve miles, then stop, put up the tents, build snow walls to shelter the ponies from the wind, feed the animals, cook their own supper, write their diaries, and sleep.

[long-pause]

The Barrier is very strange, and very beautiful.

[pause]

It is flat and white and empty, in every direction, as far as the eye can see. There is no sound, except the creak of the sledges and the crunch of the snow underfoot and the breathing of the ponies. The sky is enormous. Sometimes there are mirages, and distant mountains seem to float above the horizon, upside down, shimmering.

[pause]

{img:mirage-floating-mountains-ice-plain}
On clear days, far away to the west, the men can see the long line of the Transantarctic Mountains, white and blue and gold, running south toward the Pole.

[long-pause]

They pass the motor sledges, broken down and abandoned in the snow. The engines were not made for such cold. Teddy Evans and Lashly and their men have gone on ahead, pulling their sledges themselves.

[pause]

They pass One Ton Depot, and stock up with food. They keep going south.

[pause]

The ponies grow weaker as they go. The cold and the hard work and the poor food are too much for them. One by one, when they can go no further, they are put down quietly, as had always been planned, and their meat is used to feed the dogs and the men. Oates is very sad about it. He has looked after them for so long.

[long-pause]

At the beginning of December, a terrible blizzard strikes, just as the party reaches the foot of the Beardmore Glacier.

[pause]

For four days, the men lie in their tents while the wind roars and the snow piles up around them. The temperature is unusually warm, and the snow is wet. Everything gets soaked. They eat into the food that they had meant to save for the glacier.

[pause]

{img:tents-buried-blizzard-glacier-foot}
When the blizzard is over, the last of the ponies are gone. The men call the place Shambles Camp.

[long-pause]

The dogs go a little further up the glacier with them. Then, on the eleventh of December, Meares and Dimitri turn back with the dog teams, toward Cape Evans, as planned.

[pause]

There are twelve men left, in three teams of four. Now they must pull the sledges themselves.

[pause]

{img:men-hauling-sledges-beardmore-glacier}
They put on their harnesses, and lean forward, and pull, and the heavy sledges begin to move, slowly, up the great glacier. Around them, the mountains rise up on both sides, enormous and silent, with bare brown rock showing through the snow. Ahead, the glacier rises in long, broken steps of blue ice, toward the plateau.

[long-pause]

The climb up the glacier takes about two weeks.

[pause]

It is very hard work. Sometimes the snow is soft and deep, and the sledges sink into it, and the men have to pull with all their strength to move them a few feet. Sometimes the ice is hard and slippery, and full of hidden crevasses, deep cracks in the ice covered by thin bridges of snow, and now and then someone falls through, up to the length of his harness, and is pulled out again by his companions.

[pause]

But the views are magnificent, and the weather is often fine and sunny. Wilson stops to sketch the mountains. The geologists in the party pick up rocks and fossils.

[long-pause]

On Christmas Day, 1911, they are near the top of the glacier, almost on the plateau.

[pause]

That evening, they have a feast in the tents: pemmican with pony meat, and biscuits, and a little chocolate, and raisins, and a small plum pudding that Bowers has been carrying secretly all the way from the hut. They are so full afterwards that they can hardly move.

[pause]

Lashly turns forty-four that day. Everyone wishes him a happy birthday.

[long-pause]

On the fourth of January, 1912, about a hundred and fifty miles from the Pole, the last support party turns back.

[pause]

Scott has chosen who will go on with him to the Pole. He has chosen Wilson, and Oates, and Petty Officer Edgar Evans. And at the last moment, he decides to take a fifth man, Birdie Bowers, as well.

[pause]

{img:two-groups-parting-plateau-handshakes}
The other three men, Teddy Evans, Lashly and Crean, shake hands with them, and wish them luck, and then turn north, and start the long march home. Crean is in tears. He would have liked very much to go on.

[pause]

Scott and his four companions watch them grow smaller and smaller on the white plain, until they are only three little dots, and then nothing.

[pause]

Then the five of them turn south, toward the Pole.

[long-pause]

{img:five-men-hauling-sledge-plateau-sun}
The five men march south across the high plateau, pulling their sledge behind them.

[pause]

They walk in a line, side by side in their harnesses, leaning forward against the weight. Scott is in the middle. On one side of him is Wilson, quiet and steady. On the other side are Edgar Evans and Oates, the big Welsh sailor and the tall cavalry officer. Bowers, who has no skis, because he was told to leave them behind with an earlier party, walks on foot beside them, taking shorter steps, keeping pace with the others.

[long-pause]

The plateau is a strange, lonely world.

[pause]

It is nearly two miles above the sea, and the air is thin, so that the men are short of breath, and tire easily. The snow is dry and gritty, like sand, and the sledge drags heavily over it. Sometimes, the wind blows the surface into long, hard ridges, called sastrugi, which run across their path like frozen waves.

[pause]

{img:sastrugi-snow-ridges-plateau-low-sun}
There is nothing to see in any direction, except the snow, and the sky, and the sun, circling round and round above them. On clear days, the light is dazzling, and the men wear goggles to protect their eyes. On grey days, the snow and the sky melt together into a featureless whiteness, and it is hard to tell which way is up.

[long-pause]

It is very cold on the plateau, even in summer.

[pause]

The temperature is usually between ten and thirty degrees below zero, and the wind makes it feel much colder. The men's beards are always full of ice. Their fingers and toes and noses are often frostbitten. At night, in the tent, they rub each other's feet to warm them.

[pause]

But inside the tent, in the evening, with the Primus stove roaring and a hot meal cooking, it is almost cosy. The five men sit close together on their sleeping bags, eating their pemmican and their biscuits, and drinking hot cocoa, and talking quietly about home, and about the Pole, which is coming closer every day.

[pause]

Bowers takes the measurements of the sun with his instruments, and works out their position. They are going well. Each day, they come eleven or twelve miles nearer.

[long-pause]

Each of the five men has his own job in the tent.

[pause]

Bowers looks after the instruments and the navigation, and keeps the records of the weather. Wilson looks after the men's health, and their feet and hands, and sketches the sky and the snow whenever he can. Evans is the handyman, who mends the sledge and the tent and the harness, with his big, skilful hands. Oates looks after the stores of food. And Scott leads the way, and makes the decisions, and writes the journal of the journey every evening, in pencil, in a small notebook.

[pause]

At night, they lie side by side in their sleeping bags, in a row, packed close together for warmth, and talk a little before they sleep, about home, and the people they love, and the journey that remains.

[long-pause]

On the sixteenth of January, 1912, they are only about fifteen miles from the Pole.

[pause]

They are in good spirits. Tomorrow, or the day after, they will be there. They will be the first people ever to stand at the South Pole.

[pause]

{img:black-flag-distant-snow-plain}
Then, in the afternoon, Bowers sees something ahead of them, a small dark speck on the white plain. At first, he thinks it is a cairn of snow, or a trick of the light. As they come closer, they see that it is a black flag, tied to a sledge runner, standing upright in the snow.

[pause]

All around it, there are the tracks of sledges and skis, and the paw prints of many dogs.

[pause]

The Norwegians have been there first.

[long-pause]

The men stand in silence for a while.

[pause]

Then they put up their tent, and cook their supper, and talk quietly about it. They are very disappointed. Scott writes in his diary that night: The worst has happened. All the day dreams must go.

[pause]

But they have come so far, and they will go on to the Pole.

[long-pause]

On the seventeenth of January, they march the last few miles.

[pause]

The day is bitterly cold, with a strong wind blowing in their faces, and the temperature is far below zero. That evening, according to Bowers' measurements, they reach the South Pole.

[pause]

{img:five-men-standing-pole-flag-wind}
Scott writes in his diary: Great God! This is an awful place, and terrible enough for us to have laboured to it without the reward of priority.

[pause]

They have a special meal in the tent that night, to mark the day, with a little extra food, and some of the chocolate and cigarettes they have been saving.

[long-pause]

The next day, they find the Norwegians' tent, a little way off, standing alone on the snow, with the Norwegian flag flying above it.

[pause]

Inside, there is some equipment, and a note from Amundsen to Scott, asking him to send on the letter to the King of Norway, which is lying beside it. Scott takes the letter, carefully, to carry home.

[pause]

The five men build a small cairn of snow, and put up their own flag, the Union Jack, and take some photographs of themselves, with a camera that Bowers has carried, by pulling a long piece of string to work the shutter.

[long-pause]

In the photograph, the five of them stand and sit together in the snow, in their heavy clothes, with their faces dark from the sun and the wind, and their beards white with frost.

[pause]

Scott stands in the middle, looking straight at the camera. Oates stands on one side of him, tall and thin, and Evans on the other. In front of them sit Bowers, holding the string, and Wilson.

[pause]

{img:five-explorers-group-portrait-snow}
They look very tired. But they look steady, too, and brave, and they look like friends.

[pause]

The photograph will come home, one day, and it will become one of the most famous photographs ever taken.

[long-pause]

Then, on the nineteenth of January, they turn around, and start the long march home.

[pause]

They have eight hundred miles to go, back across the plateau, down the glacier, and across the Barrier, to the hut at Cape Evans.

[pause]

{img:men-marching-north-wind-at-backs}
For the first days, the wind is behind them, and they rig a sail on the sledge, made from the floor cloth of the tent, and it helps to push them along across the snow. Scott writes in his diary that it is a relief to have the wind at their backs, instead of in their faces.

[pause]

Behind them, the little cluster of flags and tents at the Pole grows smaller and smaller, and then disappears into the white.

[long-pause]

{img:plateau-return-march-grey-sky}
The journey back across the plateau is long and hard.

[pause]

The weather turns against them. There are blizzards, and days of thick, grey fog, when they can barely see the tracks they made on the way out. The snow grows soft and sticky, and the sledge drags. The cold grows sharper, as the short Antarctic summer begins to end.

[long-pause]

They find their depots, one after another, small cairns of snow with a flag on top, holding a week's food and fuel. Each time they find one, they feel a great relief, and they eat a good meal, and rest for a little while.

[pause]

But they are growing tired. They have been walking and pulling for nearly three months now, and they have been eating less food than their bodies need. They are thinner than when they started, and the cold is harder to bear.

[pause]

Edgar Evans, the strongest of them all at the start, has hurt his hand, and it will not heal. He has had a fall or two on the ice. He grows quieter and slower, day by day.

[long-pause]

In the second week of February, they reach the top of the Beardmore Glacier, and begin to go down it.

[pause]

Even now, tired as they are, they stop for an afternoon at a place where the rocks of the mountains come down to the edge of the ice. Wilson and Bowers go off among the rocks, and collect samples, as the scientists have asked them to do.

[pause]

{img:fossil-rocks-glacier-moraine-sketch}
Wilson finds pieces of coal, and stones with the prints of leaves in them, fossils of plants called Glossopteris, which grew there hundreds of millions of years ago, when Antarctica was warm and green, and joined to the other continents of the south. They wrap the stones carefully, and put them on the sledge, about thirty-five pounds of them, and carry them on.

[long-pause]

Lower down the glacier, they lose their way among the crevasses for a while, and then find it again.

[pause]

And then, on the seventeenth of February, near the foot of the glacier, Edgar Evans becomes very weak, and can no longer walk. His companions stay with him, and look after him in the tent. That night, very quietly, he passes away. He was thirty-five.

[pause]

{img:quiet-tent-glacier-foot-evening-light}
The four others are deeply sad. Evans was a big, cheerful, loyal man, who had been with Scott on the earlier expedition, and who had worked harder than anyone. They say a prayer for him, and they go on.

[long-pause]

Now they are out on the Barrier again, and there are four hundred miles still to go.

[pause]

The Barrier is colder than they expected. Much colder. Week after week, the temperature falls lower and lower, far below what anyone had known at that time of year before. The snow becomes rough and gritty, so that the sledge runners stick to it, and the men must pull with all their strength to make the sledge move at all.

[pause]

The days are growing shorter now. For the first time in months, the sun begins to set at night, and the evenings are dark and bitterly cold.

[pause]

The men march on, mile after mile, across the flat white plain.

[long-pause]

Oates is suffering most of all.

[pause]

His feet are badly frostbitten, and it is very painful for him to walk. Every morning, it takes him longer and longer to put on his boots. He does not complain. But the others can see that he is in great pain, and that it is slowing them down.

[pause]

{img:tall-man-quiet-tent-evening-stove}
Wilson, the doctor, looks after him as well as he can. In the evenings, in the tent, the four of them sit close together around the little stove, talking quietly. Oates talks about his mother, and his home in England, and his horses.

[long-pause]

On the sixteenth or seventeenth of March, nobody is quite sure which, there is a blizzard blowing outside the tent.

[pause]

That morning, Oates wakes up, and knows that he cannot go any further, and that his friends will not leave him.

[pause]

He stands up, and goes to the door of the tent, and says to the others, quietly and simply: I am just going outside and may be some time.

[pause]

Then he goes out into the blizzard, and does not come back. It is just around his thirty-second birthday.

[long-pause]

{img:blizzard-white-tent-alone-gentle}
The three who remain, Scott, Wilson and Bowers, know what he has done, and why.

[pause]

Scott writes in his diary that evening: We knew that poor Oates was walking to his death, but though we tried to dissuade him, we knew it was the act of a brave man and an English gentleman.

[pause]

They never find him. Somewhere out on the Barrier, under the snow, he lies at rest, and he is there still.

[long-pause]

The three men go on, for a few more days.

[pause]

They are now only about eleven miles from One Ton Depot, where there is plenty of food and fuel waiting for them. Eleven miles. On a good day, they could cover that distance in a single march.

[pause]

But on the twentieth of March, a great blizzard blows up, and they cannot leave the tent. They are too weak now to walk through it. They lie in their sleeping bags, and wait for the wind to drop.

[pause]

It does not drop.

[long-pause]

For days, the blizzard roars over the little tent.

[pause]

Inside, the three friends lie close together, side by side, in their sleeping bags. Their food and fuel are almost gone. They talk quietly, when they have the strength. Wilson and Bowers write letters to their families. Scott writes letters too, to his wife, to Wilson's wife, to Bowers' mother, to his friends, and to the people of England.

[pause]

{img:candle-tent-writing-letters-quiet}
In the letter to his wife, Kathleen, he asks her to make their little son Peter interested in natural history, if she can. It is better than games, he writes. They encourage it at some schools.

[pause]

He tells her not to grieve too much. He tells her that he has been with good friends, and that he does not regret the journey.

[long-pause]

{img:tent-snow-drift-soft-grey-light}
The blizzard blows on, through the last days of March 1912.

[pause]

In the little green tent, half buried now in snow, the three men grow quieter. They have written their letters. They have said what they wanted to say to each other. There is nothing left to do but rest.

[long-pause]

Wilson, the doctor and artist, writes to his wife Oriana, whom he loves very much.

[pause]

He tells her that he is not afraid. He tells her that all the things he has ever thought and hoped about the world beyond this one are still true for him, and that he is quite ready to go. He asks her to remember that they will meet again. He writes that the others are being very brave, and that Birdie is the most cheerful of them all.

[pause]

Beside him lie his sketchbooks, full of the watercolours he has painted along the journey: the mountains, the glacier, the sky at sunset, the strange halos around the sun.

[long-pause]

Bowers writes to his mother.

[pause]

He was always very close to her. He tells her that he loves her, and that he has done his best. He tells her not to be too sad, because he believes that he is going somewhere better, and that she will see him again one day.

[pause]

{img:small-red-haired-man-writing-letter-tent}
Even now, Birdie is cheerful. Scott writes in one of his letters that Bowers has been splendid all the way, hopeful to the very end.

[long-pause]

Scott writes his last letters, and a message to the public, to be read in England, explaining what has happened.

[pause]

In it, he writes about the cold, and the bad weather, and the illness of Evans and Oates, and how close they came to reaching safety. He writes that they have taken risks, and that things have come out against them, and that they have no cause for complaint.

[pause]

And he writes some words that will become famous all over the world: Had we lived, I should have had a tale to tell of the hardihood, endurance and courage of my companions which would have stirred the heart of every Englishman. These rough notes and our dead bodies must tell the tale.

[long-pause]

The last entry in Scott's journal is dated Thursday, the twenty-ninth of March, 1912.

[pause]

He writes that every day they have been ready to start for the depot eleven miles away, but that outside the door of the tent, it remains a scene of whirling drift. He writes that they shall stick it out to the end, but that they are getting weaker, of course, and the end cannot be far.

[pause]

It seems a pity, he writes, but I do not think I can write more.

[pause]

{img:pencil-on-closed-journal-snow-light}
And then, at the bottom, one more line, written larger, in a shaky hand:

[pause]

For God's sake look after our people.

[long-pause]

Some time after that, in the quiet of the tent, the three friends fall asleep, and do not wake.

[pause]

Wilson and Bowers lie side by side, with their sleeping bags closed over their heads. Scott lies between them, with his sleeping bag open, and his arm stretched out across his friend Wilson, as if to keep him company.

[pause]

Outside, the blizzard slowly dies away. The snow settles. The wind drops. And the long dark winter of 1912 comes down over the Barrier, and over the little tent, which stands alone on the white plain, under the stars and the shimmering curtains of the southern lights.

[long-pause]

Back at the hut at Cape Evans, the men who stayed behind have been waiting.

[pause]

At the end of February, as Scott had asked, Cherry-Garrard and the young Russian dog driver, Dimitri, set out with two dog teams for One Ton Depot, to meet the returning party, and help them home.

[pause]

{img:two-men-dog-sledges-depot-waiting}
They reach the depot on the third of March, and wait there, for six days, in a terrible cold, searching the horizon to the south with their telescopes every day, for any sign of the polar party.

[pause]

But they see nothing. Only the empty white plain, and the low sun, and the drifting snow.

[long-pause]

They do not know that Scott and his companions are still far away to the south, and moving more and more slowly. They have no orders to go further. Their dogs are running short of food. And so, on the tenth of March, they turn back toward the hut, very sadly.

[pause]

Cherry will always wonder, for the rest of his life, whether he might have done something more.

[pause]

Back at Cape Evans, the men wait, and watch, and hope. Every day, someone climbs the little hill behind the hut and looks south across the frozen sea, toward the Barrier.

[pause]

But no one comes.

[long-pause]

In March, the Terra Nova comes back to the bay, to take some of the men home before the winter, and to bring news from the world outside.

[pause]

{img:ship-in-bay-icy-autumn-evening}
The men who are leaving go on board with heavy hearts, without knowing what has happened. As the ship sails away, they look back at the hut on its little black cape, under the white slopes of Erebus, and wonder if they will ever see the polar party again.

[pause]

And the men who stay behind settle down to face a second winter in the dark.

[long-pause]

{img:second-winter-hut-thirteen-men-lamp}
The second winter at Cape Evans is a quiet one.

[pause]

There are only thirteen men left in the hut now. The surgeon, Edward Atkinson, is in charge. He is a calm, kind, sensible man, and he does his best to keep everyone busy and cheerful through the long dark months.

[long-pause]

They carry on with the scientific work, as Scott would have wanted.

[pause]

They read the instruments. They measure the ice. They write up their notes. They give lectures in the evenings, and play the gramophone, and read, and publish another issue of the South Polar Times. On Midwinter Day, they have a feast, as before, with flags and candles and plum pudding, and they drink a toast to absent friends.

[pause]

But the hut feels empty without the others. The bunks of Scott and Wilson, Bowers and Oates and Evans stand empty, with their books and their pictures still on the shelves. Nobody says very much about it. But everyone knows, in their hearts, that the polar party will not be coming home.

[long-pause]

As the winter goes on, Atkinson has a hard decision to make.

[pause]

When the spring comes, should they go south to search for the polar party, who are almost certainly lost? Or should they go north, to look for another group of six men, the Northern Party, under Lieutenant Victor Campbell, who were landed far up the coast a year ago, and who have not been picked up?

[pause]

{img:men-discussing-lamplit-table-maps}
He puts the question to the whole group, and they talk about it for a long time, and then they vote. Almost everyone agrees: they will go south, to find Scott and his companions, and to find out what happened to them.

[pause]

As it turns out, the Northern Party, after a winter spent in a cave of snow, eating seals and penguins, will walk safely back to Cape Evans on their own, in November.

[long-pause]

At the end of October 1912, the search party sets out from Cape Evans.

[pause]

There are eleven men, with dogs and mules, which were brought on the ship the previous summer. Atkinson leads them, and with him are Cherry-Garrard, Crean, Lashly, Dimitri, the Norwegian Tryggve Gran, and others.

[pause]

{img:search-party-mules-dogs-barrier}
They travel south across the Barrier, along the old route, past the cairns that mark the way. The weather is fine, and the sun shines on the snow, and the mules plod steadily along.

[long-pause]

On the twelfth of November, about eleven miles south of One Ton Depot, one of the men sees something on the snow ahead, a little to the right of their path.

[pause]

At first, it looks like a cairn of snow. But as they come closer, they see that it is the top of a tent, almost completely buried, with only the tip of it showing above the snow.

[pause]

The men stop. They stand very still for a moment, and nobody says anything. Then they walk slowly toward it.

[long-pause]

They dig away the snow from the door of the tent, and Atkinson goes inside.

[pause]

There, he finds the three men, Scott, Wilson and Bowers, lying in their sleeping bags, as if asleep. Everything is neat and in order. Beside them are their diaries, and their letters, and the little bag of fossil rocks they had carried from the glacier, and the letter from Amundsen to the King of Norway.

[pause]

{img:men-standing-bareheaded-snow-cairn}
Atkinson reads part of Scott's diary aloud to the others, standing outside in the snow, so that they will understand what happened. The men listen in silence, with their heads bowed.

[long-pause]

They do not move the three friends. They leave them where they lie, in their tent, together.

[pause]

Then they read a service from the Bible, and sing a hymn, standing in the snow, in the cold bright air. Gran puts on Scott's skis, which he will use on the way back to Cape Evans, so that, as he says, the skis will at least complete the journey.

[pause]

Then, over the tent, they build a great cairn of snow, twelve feet high, and on top of it they put a cross made from Gran's skis, which can be seen from far away across the white plain.

[pause]

{img:snow-cairn-ski-cross-plain-sunlight}
Beside it, they leave a note, written by Atkinson and Cherry-Garrard, saying who lies there, and what they had done.

[long-pause]

The next day, the search party goes on south, for about twenty miles, looking for Oates.

[pause]

They do not find him. Somewhere out on the Barrier, under the snow, he lies at rest. Near the place where they believe he went out into the blizzard, they build another cairn of snow, with a small cross on top, and leave a note on it, saying that near this spot died a very gallant gentleman, Captain L. E. G. Oates, of the Inniskilling Dragoons.

[pause]

Then, slowly and quietly, the search party turns, and goes back to the hut.

[long-pause]

In January 1913, the Terra Nova comes back to Cape Evans for the last time, to take everyone home.

[pause]

Before they leave, the men make a large cross of Australian jarrah wood, and carry it up to the top of Observation Hill, a steep little hill near the old hut at Hut Point, from which they can see far out across the Barrier, toward the place where their friends lie.

[pause]

{img:wooden-cross-observation-hill-view}
On the cross, they carve the names of the five men who reached the Pole. And underneath, they carve a line from a poem by Tennyson, which Cherry-Garrard has chosen:

[pause]

To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield.

[long-pause]

Then they go down the hill, and walk back to the ship, and the Terra Nova steams slowly out of the sound, past the white slopes of Erebus with its smoking summit.

[pause]

The men stand at the rail and watch Antarctica grow small behind them, until it is only a white line on the horizon, and then nothing at all.

[long-pause]

{img:ship-arriving-new-zealand-harbour-dawn}
On the tenth of February, 1913, the Terra Nova sails quietly into a little harbour on the coast of New Zealand, in the early hours of the morning.

[pause]

Two of the men row ashore, and go to the telegraph office, and send a message to London.

[pause]

Within a few hours, the news is all over the world.

[long-pause]

In Britain, people are stunned.

[pause]

The newspapers print the story on their front pages, with black borders. Crowds gather outside the newspaper offices to read the latest bulletins. In schools, teachers read Scott's last message to the children. People speak of the five men in hushed voices, on trains and in shops, in churches and in homes.

[pause]

On the fourteenth of February, a great memorial service is held at St. Paul's Cathedral in London, attended by the King. Thousands of people stand outside in the cold, unable to get in. Inside, the organ plays, and the choir sings, and many people weep.

[long-pause]

Kathleen Scott hears the news on board a ship, on her way to New Zealand to meet her husband.

[pause]

The captain of the ship receives the news by wireless, and does not know how to tell her. For a day, he keeps it to himself. Then he goes to her cabin, and tells her, as gently as he can.

[pause]

{img:woman-ship-deck-sea-calm-thoughtful}
She is very brave. She goes out on deck, and walks up and down for a long time, looking at the sea. Then she goes back to her cabin, and sits down, and begins to read a book, very slowly, because she wants to be calm when she arrives.

[long-pause]

Back in England, she reads her husband's last letter to her.

[pause]

And she remembers what he asked, about their son, Peter: to make the boy interested in natural history.

[pause]

She does exactly that. Peter grows up loving birds and animals and the countryside. He becomes a painter of birds, and a naturalist, and one of the most famous lovers of nature in Britain. He sets up a great centre for wild ducks and geese and swans, at Slimbridge, beside the River Severn, where people can come and watch the birds.

[pause]

And in 1961, he helps to start an organization to protect the wild animals of the world, whose symbol is a giant panda. Today, it is known all over the world as the WWF.

[pause]

His father would have been very proud.

[long-pause]

The other members of the expedition go on with their lives.

[pause]

Many of them serve in the Great War, which begins only a year and a half later. Some of them do not come home from it. Others live long lives, and become scientists and explorers, writers and sailors.

[pause]

{img:old-man-writing-book-country-house-study}
Apsley Cherry-Garrard goes home to his family's estate in Hertfordshire, and, after the war, writes his book about the expedition, The Worst Journey in the World. It is beautifully written, honest and sad and funny, and it becomes one of the best-loved books of its kind. He never really stops thinking about his friends who were lost.

[pause]

Tom Crean goes on to sail with Ernest Shackleton to Antarctica, on another famous expedition, and then retires to Ireland, where he opens a small pub in his home village of Annascaul, and calls it the South Pole Inn.

[long-pause]

Edward Wilson's sketches and watercolours come home too.

[pause]

They are found in the tent beside him, carefully wrapped, and carried back to England. There are hundreds of them: paintings of the mountains and the glaciers, of the sun's halos and the southern lights, of penguins and seals and birds, of the hut at Cape Evans, and of the ship in the ice. Today, many of them hang in the museum in Cheltenham, his home town, and at the Scott Polar Research Institute in Cambridge, which was founded in memory of the five men.

[pause]

People still come to look at them, and are surprised at how gentle and beautiful they are, after such a hard journey.

[long-pause]

Roald Amundsen is hailed as a hero, in Norway and around the world.

[pause]

He goes on exploring for many more years. In 1926, he flies over the North Pole in an airship, called the Norge. He is a quiet, private man, and he always speaks with great respect of Scott and his companions. He says that he would gladly give up the honour of reaching the Pole, if it could bring them back to life.

[pause]

{img:norwegian-explorer-older-fjord-home}
In 1928, he flies north over the Arctic Ocean to look for some lost explorers, and is never seen again.

[long-pause]

The emperor penguin eggs, carried home so carefully by Wilson, Bowers and Cherry-Garrard, are taken to the Natural History Museum in London.

[pause]

At first, nobody at the museum seems very interested in them. When Cherry-Garrard brings them in, a clerk takes them and gives him a receipt, and does not even say thank you.

[pause]

In the end, the scientists find that the eggs do not prove what Wilson had hoped. But they are kept, carefully, in a quiet drawer at the museum, where they still lie today, three large pale eggs, with a little label beside them.

[long-pause]

The fossils that Wilson and Bowers collected on the Beardmore Glacier, and carried all the way to the last tent, are studied by scientists, too.

[pause]

They turn out to be very important. They are leaves of the plant called Glossopteris, which also grew in South America and Africa, India and Australia. They help to show that, long ago, all those lands were joined together with Antarctica in one great southern continent, which later slowly broke apart.

[pause]

{img:fossil-leaf-stone-museum-lamp}
So the five men who walked to the Pole brought home something important after all: a small piece of the great story of the Earth itself.

[long-pause]

{img:cape-evans-hut-today-erebus-sunlight}
The hut at Cape Evans is still there today, on its little cape of black sand, beneath the slopes of Mount Erebus.

[pause]

For many years after the expedition left, it stood empty and forgotten. Snow drifted into it, and filled some of the rooms with ice. Later expeditions used it now and then, as a shelter. But mostly, it stood alone, in the cold and the silence, year after year.

[long-pause]

Then, not so long ago, a group of people in New Zealand decided that the hut should be saved.

[pause]

They are called the Antarctic Heritage Trust. For years, their workers and conservators came down to Cape Evans in the summers, and dug out the ice, and repaired the walls and the roof, and cleaned and mended and preserved, one by one, thousands of objects that had been left in the hut.

[pause]

Today, the hut looks almost exactly as it did when Scott's men left it in 1913.

[pause]

The long table still stands in the wardroom. The bunks are still there, with old reindeer skin sleeping bags on some of them. On the shelves, there are tins of food and jars of pickles and bottles of medicine, with their old labels. In the kitchen, there is Clissold's stove. In the corner, Ponting's darkroom, with its bottles of chemicals. In the stables, the names of the ponies are still written above the stalls.

[long-pause]

Very few people ever see it.

[pause]

Antarctica is still far away, and hard to reach. But each summer, a small number of visitors come, scientists and travellers, and walk quietly through the hut, speaking in low voices, as if they were in a church.

[pause]

{img:penguins-near-old-hut-summer}
Outside, the Adélie penguins still waddle along the shore, and the skuas still circle overhead, and the seals still lie on the ice in the sun. The view across the frozen sound to the distant mountains is just the same as it was.

[long-pause]

Not far away, at Hut Point, the older hut from Scott's first expedition is still standing too, a small square wooden building with a wide veranda, close to the edge of the sea.

[pause]

It was used as a shelter many times during the Terra Nova years, by men travelling to and from the Barrier, and the walls inside are still black with the smoke of the blubber stoves they cooked on. Close by, today, there is a busy scientific base, with roads and buildings and a runway on the ice. But the old hut stands quietly apart, on its little point, looking out across the water as it always has.

[long-pause]

On Observation Hill, the wooden cross still stands, weathered grey by more than a century of wind and snow, with the five names carved on it, and the line of Tennyson underneath.

[pause]

{img:weathered-wooden-cross-hilltop-ice-view}
From the top of the hill, looking south, a person can see far out across the Barrier, which today is called the Ross Ice Shelf.

[pause]

Somewhere out there, under the ice, Scott and Wilson and Bowers still lie together in their tent. Over the years, the snow has buried them deeper and deeper, and the ice shelf itself has slowly carried them toward the sea. One day, perhaps hundreds of years from now, they will reach the edge of the ice, and drift away north, quietly, in an iceberg.

[long-pause]

At the South Pole itself, there is now a scientific station, where people live and work all year round.

[pause]

It is called the Amundsen-Scott South Pole Station, named after both of the explorers who reached it in that summer long ago. In winter, about forty or fifty people stay there, through six months of darkness, studying the weather and the ice and the stars.

[pause]

{img:modern-polar-station-snow-plain-twilight}
Near the station, there is a small marker, which is moved a little every year, because the ice sheet slowly drifts. Around it stand the flags of the countries that first signed the Antarctic Treaty, an agreement which says that Antarctica belongs to no single country, and that it should be used only for peace and for science.

[long-pause]

Antarctica remains one of the last great wild places on Earth.

[pause]

The emperor penguins still gather on the sea ice at Cape Crozier in the middle of the dark winter, huddling together against the cold, with their eggs tucked on their feet, just as they did when Wilson, Bowers and Cherry-Garrard came stumbling toward them by the light of a candle lantern.

[pause]

The snow petrels still fly over the ice like little white ghosts. The Weddell seals still sing under the frozen sea. And above it all, the southern lights still shimmer across the long winter night, in curtains of green and rose and white.

[long-pause]

In the city of Christchurch, in New Zealand, and in churches and schools and town squares all over Britain, there are memorials to the five men who walked to the Pole.

[pause]

In Cheltenham, where Wilson grew up, there is a statue of him, made by Kathleen Scott. In London, in Waterloo Place, there is a statue of Scott himself, also made by Kathleen, standing in his sledging clothes, looking out over the city.

[pause]

{img:bronze-statue-explorer-london-evening}
And in Cardiff, where the Terra Nova set out on that summer afternoon in 1910, there is a little lighthouse memorial beside a lake in a park, painted white, which glows softly at night.

[long-pause]

So we leave the frozen continent for tonight: the old hut on its black cape beneath the smoking mountain, the wooden cross on the hill, and the vast white silence of the ice, stretching away under the stars toward the bottom of the world.
