In June 1910, an old wooden whaling ship called the Terra Nova sailed out of Cardiff, bound for Antarctica. On board were Captain Robert Falcon Scott and the men who hoped to be the first to reach the South Pole. This is the story of their journey.

[long-pause]

In June 1910, Captain Scott's ship, the Terra Nova, left the Welsh port of Cardiff for Antarctica. Robert Falcon Scott's second Antarctic expedition is known as the Terra Nova Expedition, after its ship. The ship left London on the first of June 1910, but its final departure from Britain was from Cardiff, in Wales, on the fifteenth of June. Cardiff was then one of the busiest coal ports in the world. Welsh businesses gave the expedition coal, money and supplies, and the people of the city gave the crew a warm farewell. Historians know about the departure from newspapers, diaries and photographs. The expedition aimed to reach the South Pole and to carry out a large programme of scientific research. Even so, it was not well funded. Scott had to raise money from businesses, schools and the public, and the government gave only part of the cost. When the ship sailed out of Cardiff, crowds cheered from the docks. Few could imagine how long the men would be away.

[long-pause]

{img:02-old-wooden-whaling-ship-oak-bow}

The Terra Nova was an old wooden whaling and sealing ship, built in Dundee in 1884. The Terra Nova was not a new ship. She had been built in Dundee, in Scotland, in 1884, for the whaling and sealing trade in the Arctic. Her strong wooden hull was designed to push through ice. Scott bought her for the expedition because she was tough and available. She was refitted to carry men, scientific equipment, animals and supplies. Historians note that the ship was cramped and leaked badly, and the crew had to pump water out regularly. She was powered by both sails and a coal-fired steam engine. Even so, she proved her strength many times in heavy seas and thick ice. Sailors described her as a hard but faithful ship. After the expedition, she returned to work in the sealing trade off Newfoundland for many years. She finally sank off the coast of Greenland in 1943. Her figurehead is kept today in the National Museum of Wales in Cardiff.

For anyone who likes to fall asleep among ice, ships and explorers, a subscription keeps the next expedition close by.

[long-pause]

{img:03-crowded-ship-deck-crates-hay-coal}

The deck of the Terra Nova was crowded with crates, coal, fodder and supplies for two years. The expedition had to carry everything it needed to live and work in Antarctica for at least two years. The Terra Nova was loaded with food, fuel, tents, sledges, scientific instruments, a prefabricated hut, clothing and tools. On deck, there were sacks of coal, bales of hay and fodder for animals, and crates of every size. There was even a motor car and three motor sledges, which were new technology at the time. Historians know about the cargo from lists kept by the expedition and from photographs of the crowded deck. The ship sat very low in the water. Even so, every item had been carefully chosen. Running out of something in Antarctica could be dangerous, since there would be no way to obtain more. The men packed and repacked the cargo many times. They later said that every space on board, from the holds to the cabins, was filled with something needed for the voyage.

[long-pause]

{img:04-antarctica-white-coast-distant-mountains}

In 1910, much of Antarctica had never been seen by any human being. Antarctica is the coldest, windiest and highest continent on Earth. It is covered by an enormous sheet of ice, in places more than four kilometres thick. In 1910, explorers had seen only parts of its coast and had travelled a short distance inland. No one had reached the South Pole. Large areas on the maps were blank. Historians note that the early twentieth century is often called the Heroic Age of Antarctic Exploration. Expeditions from Britain, Norway, Germany, Sweden, Japan and other countries explored the continent with sledges, ships and very simple equipment. Scott had already led an expedition there from 1901 to 1904, on the ship Discovery. Even so, much remained unknown. Scientists did not know what lay beneath the ice, how the weather worked or whether the continent was one land mass. Exploring Antarctica was a journey into one of the last great unknown places on the planet.

[long-pause]

{img:05-naval-captain-ship-deck-thoughtful}

Robert Falcon Scott was a Royal Navy officer who had already led an expedition to Antarctica. Robert Falcon Scott was born in 1868 in Devon, in the south of England. He joined the Royal Navy as a boy and became an officer. In 1901, he was chosen to lead the Discovery expedition to Antarctica, which explored and mapped large areas and carried out important scientific work. During that expedition, he and two companions travelled further south than anyone before them. When he returned, he became famous. Historians describe Scott as a serious, hardworking and sometimes moody man. He could be strict, but he cared deeply for his men and was devoted to science. He wrote well, and his journals are among the most famous in the history of exploration. Even so, historians have debated his leadership. Some have criticised his decisions, while others have defended them, pointing to bad weather and bad luck. In 1910, he was forty-two years old and determined to reach the Pole.

[long-pause]

{img:06-sculptor-woman-studio-london-1910}

Scott's wife, Kathleen, was a sculptor who had studied art in Paris. Kathleen Bruce was born in 1878 and grew up in a large family. She studied art in London and then in Paris, where she met the sculptor Auguste Rodin and other artists. She was independent, adventurous and lively. She married Robert Falcon Scott in 1908, and their son, Peter, was born in 1909. Kathleen supported her husband's plans and helped raise money for the expedition. She travelled with him as far as New Zealand before he sailed for Antarctica. Historians know about their relationship from their many letters and from Kathleen's diaries. Even so, she was also a successful artist in her own right. She later made many sculptures of famous people, including a statue of Scott that stands in London. In 1922, she married again, to the politician Edward Hilton Young. Her son Peter later became a famous naturalist. Kathleen's work can still be seen in museums and public spaces across Britain.

[long-pause]

{img:07-scientists-on-ship-deck-instruments}

The Terra Nova Expedition carried a large team of scientists as well as explorers. Scott wanted his expedition to be remembered for science as well as for reaching the Pole. The team included geologists, biologists, a physicist, a meteorologist and other specialists. They planned to study rocks, weather, ice, animals, plants and the magnetism of the Earth. The chief scientist was Edward Wilson, a doctor and naturalist. Historians consider the scientific work of the expedition very important. Its members collected thousands of specimens, made detailed weather records and mapped large areas. Their results were published in many volumes over the following decades. Even so, the scientific work is often forgotten in popular stories, which focus on the race to the Pole. Many scientists of the expedition later had long careers. Some, such as the geologist Griffith Taylor and the physicist Charles Wright, became well known in their fields. The expedition helped lay the foundations of Antarctic science, which continues today at research stations across the continent.

[long-pause]

{img:08-young-men-crew-deck-1910-sun}

Around eight thousand people applied to join Scott's expedition, but only a few dozen were chosen. When Scott announced his expedition, thousands of people wanted to join. Historians often repeat that around eight thousand applications arrived. Scott chose men with useful skills, good health and the right character for a long and difficult journey. The team included naval officers and sailors, scientists, a doctor, a photographer, a cook and specialists to care for the animals. Some men had been on the Discovery expedition with Scott. Others came from the army, from universities and from other walks of life. Historians know about the members from their diaries, letters and later memoirs, many of which were published. Even so, the expedition reflected the society of its time. Officers and scientists lived separately from the sailors, and the expedition was entirely made up of men. Yet the members came to know each other closely. Many of them formed friendships that lasted for the rest of their lives, and they wrote warmly about one another.

[long-pause]

{img:09-wardroom-supper-swinging-lamps-ship}

Life on board the Terra Nova was crowded, but the officers and scientists ate together in a small wardroom. On the Terra Nova, the officers and scientists shared a small mess room called the wardroom. They ate their meals there and spent evenings talking, reading and playing games. Oil lamps swung from the ceiling as the ship rolled. The sailors had their own quarters at the front of the ship. Space was very limited, and men shared small cabins and bunks. Historians know about life on board from diaries, which describe meals, jokes and the friendships that formed during the voyage. Even so, conditions could be uncomfortable. In rough seas, water leaked into cabins, and many men were seasick. Yet the long voyage helped the team get to know one another before they reached Antarctica. Men from different backgrounds learned to work and live together. Many diaries describe the cheerful atmosphere in the wardroom during the first months, with songs, discussions and plans for the work ahead.

[long-pause]

{img:10-sailing-ship-tropical-sea-flying-fish}

Before reaching the ice, the Terra Nova sailed through the tropics. The voyage from Britain to New Zealand took several months. The Terra Nova sailed south through the Atlantic, stopping at Madeira and South Trinidad, an island off the coast of Brazil, and then at Simon's Town in South Africa. In the tropics, the weather was hot, and the men slept on deck to escape the heat below. They saw flying fish, dolphins and sea birds. The scientists collected samples from the sea and studied the animals they saw. Historians know about this part of the voyage from diaries and from Scott's own journal. Even so, the long voyage was hard work. The ship's engine used large amounts of coal, and the sails had to be handled constantly. From South Africa, the ship continued to Melbourne and then to New Zealand, where final preparations were made. For many of the younger men, the voyage was an exciting adventure, full of new sights before the cold of the south.

[long-pause]

{img:11-stokers-shovelling-coal-furnace-glow}

Officers and scientists on the Terra Nova took turns helping the stokers shovel coal. The Terra Nova's steam engine needed a steady supply of coal. In the hot engine room, stokers shovelled coal into the furnaces for hours at a time. It was some of the hardest work on the ship. During the voyage, several officers and scientists volunteered to help with the stoking, working shifts alongside the regular crew. Historians note that this helped build a sense of shared effort. Men who would later lead sledge journeys or carry out scientific work learned what physical labour on the ship involved. Diaries describe the heat, the coal dust and the exhaustion. Even so, many volunteers took pride in the work. It was a way of showing they were willing to do anything for the expedition. The ship also used its sails whenever possible to save coal. Coal was precious, because the ship needed enough for the whole journey to Antarctica and back again to New Zealand.

[long-pause]

{img:12-quiet-man-painting-watercolour-deck}

Edward Wilson, the chief scientist and doctor, was respected and loved by everyone on the expedition. Edward Adrian Wilson was born in 1872 in Cheltenham. He trained as a doctor and was also a talented naturalist and artist. He had sailed with Scott on the Discovery expedition and was one of his closest friends. On the Terra Nova Expedition, he led the scientific team. The men called him Uncle Bill. Historians describe him as calm, kind and deeply religious. Many members of the expedition wrote that he was the person they trusted most. He listened to everyone and helped settle disagreements. Wilson painted beautiful watercolours of Antarctic landscapes, birds and animals. Many of these paintings survive and are kept in museums, including in Cheltenham. Even so, he was also a careful scientist. He studied penguins, seals and birds and made important observations. Wilson was one of the five men who reached the South Pole with Scott. His paintings remain among the finest images of Antarctica from that time.

[long-pause]

{img:13-short-red-haired-officer-ship-stores}

Henry Bowers, a short and energetic officer, was in charge of the expedition's stores. Henry Robertson Bowers was born in 1883 in Scotland. He served in the Royal Indian Marine, a naval service in India, before joining the expedition. He was short and strongly built, with red hair and a large nose, which earned him the nickname Birdie. He was in charge of the stores, keeping track of every item of food and equipment. Historians describe him as tireless, cheerful and extremely reliable. Early in the voyage, he fell nineteen feet through an open hatch into the hold. He landed on the pig iron ballast but climbed out almost unhurt and went straight back to work. Even so, at first some members doubted whether he was suited for the expedition, since he had no polar experience. He soon proved himself. Scott came to rely on him completely. Bowers was one of the five men who reached the South Pole, and his organisation was essential to the expedition's success.

[long-pause]

{img:14-tall-army-officer-pipe-reading-corner}

Captain Lawrence Oates, an army cavalry officer, paid a large sum to join the expedition. Lawrence Edward Grace Oates was born in 1880 into a wealthy family. He served as an officer in the Inniskilling Dragoons, a cavalry regiment, and fought in the Boer War in South Africa, where he was wounded in the leg. He joined the expedition to look after the ponies, which were to help pull sledges. He also paid a thousand pounds towards the cost of the expedition, a large sum at the time. The men called him Titus or the Soldier. Historians describe him as quiet, dry-humoured and honest. He did not always agree with Scott, especially about the choice of ponies. Even so, he worked hard to care for the animals and was respected by the men. Oates was one of the five men who reached the South Pole. His courage on the return journey later became famous across Britain. He is remembered with memorials in his home village of Gestingthorpe and elsewhere.

[long-pause]

{img:15-young-man-round-spectacles-ship-rail}

Apsley Cherry-Garrard, the youngest officer, later wrote a famous book about the expedition. Apsley Cherry-Garrard was born in 1886 and studied at Oxford. He was very short-sighted and had little practical experience, but he was eager to join. He paid towards the cost of the expedition and was taken on as an assistant zoologist. The men called him Cherry. Historians describe him as modest, sensitive and loyal. He edited the expedition's newspaper during the winter and took part in several sledge journeys. In 1922, he published a book about the expedition called The Worst Journey in the World. It describes the expedition's experiences with honesty and great feeling. Historians and readers consider it one of the finest books ever written about exploration. Even so, writing it was difficult for him. The memories of the expedition stayed with him for the rest of his life. His book remains widely read today and has been named by some as the greatest travel book ever written.

[long-pause]

{img:16-lyttelton-harbour-green-hills-1910}

In New Zealand, the Terra Nova took on the ponies and dogs for the expedition. The Terra Nova arrived in New Zealand in October 1910. There, the expedition made its final preparations in the port of Lyttelton, near Christchurch. The ship was unloaded, repaired and repacked. The ponies and dogs, which had been brought from Siberia and Manchuria by the expedition's dog expert Cecil Meares, were loaded on board. Last letters were written and posted home. Historians know about these weeks from diaries and local newspapers. The people of Christchurch welcomed the expedition warmly, and many visited the ship. Kathleen Scott and other wives said goodbye to their husbands. Even so, there was a serious problem. The ship was dangerously overloaded, carrying far more than it had been designed for. The ship finally sailed from Port Chalmers, further south, on the twenty-ninth of November 1910. The green hills of New Zealand would be the last land the men saw before Antarctica.

[long-pause]

{img:17-men-bailing-buckets-lantern-night-ship}

In December 1910, a severe storm nearly sank the Terra Nova, and the crew bailed water all night. Soon after leaving New Zealand, the Terra Nova was struck by a violent storm in the Southern Ocean. Huge waves washed over the deck, and the overloaded ship rolled heavily. Some coal and fuel were lost overboard, and two ponies and a dog died. Water poured into the ship, and the pumps became blocked. The engine fires had to be put out. The men formed a chain and bailed water out with buckets, working through the night. According to diaries, some of them sang as they worked to keep their spirits up. Eventually, a passage was cut to clear the pumps. Historians know about the storm from several detailed accounts. Even so, the ship survived. The men's courage and teamwork were praised by everyone. Scott wrote in his journal about the cheerful way the men had worked. The storm showed how dangerous the voyage was, even before reaching the ice.

[long-pause]

{img:18-first-iceberg-grey-sea-ship}

As the Terra Nova sailed south, the men saw their first icebergs, some of them enormous. After the storm, the weather improved, and the ship continued south. In December, the men saw their first icebergs. Some were huge, flat-topped blocks of ice that had broken away from the ice shelves of Antarctica. Others had been shaped by wind and waves into towers and arches. Historians note that Antarctic icebergs can be many kilometres long. The men were amazed by their size and beauty. Diaries describe the changing colours of the ice, from white to blue and green. Even so, icebergs were also dangerous. The ship had to steer carefully around them, especially in fog or at night. As the ship moved further south, the sea became colder, and floating ice became more common. The men knew they were approaching the pack ice, a belt of floating ice that surrounds Antarctica. Getting through it would be the next great challenge of the voyage.

[long-pause]

{img:19-ship-in-pack-ice-midnight-sun}

On Christmas Day 1910, the Terra Nova was stuck in the pack ice under the midnight sun. The Terra Nova entered the pack ice in December 1910. For weeks, the ship pushed slowly through the floating ice, sometimes stopping completely when the ice closed around her. The men spent Christmas Day 1910 stuck in the pack. In the southern summer, the sun did not set, and it shone all through the night. The men decorated the wardroom, ate a special dinner and sang songs. Historians know about this Christmas from several diaries, which describe the strange beauty of the scene. Even so, the delay was worrying. The ship was using precious coal while waiting, and the season for building a base was short. The men took the opportunity to observe seals and penguins on the ice and to collect scientific samples. Finally, at the end of December, the ice loosened, and the ship broke through into open water. Ahead lay the Ross Sea and the coast of Antarctica.

[long-pause]

{img:20-adelie-penguins-listening-ice-ship}

Curious penguins often approached the ship, and the sailors sang to them. While the ship was in the pack ice, many Adélie penguins came to look at it. Adélie penguins are small, black and white penguins with white rings around their eyes. They are curious and not afraid of people. According to the diaries of the expedition, the penguins would walk up to the ship, and the men noticed that they seemed interested in music. Some sailors sang to them, and the penguins appeared to listen before walking away. Historians enjoy these stories, which show the lighter side of life on the expedition. Scientists of the expedition studied the penguins' behaviour and recorded many observations. Even so, life for the penguins was hard. They had to survive harsh weather and predators such as leopard seals. The men found them very entertaining. Many diaries contain funny descriptions of the penguins' habits, comparing them to little men in evening dress, busy and full of their own importance.

[long-pause]

{img:21-mount-erebus-smoking-volcano-sea}

One of the first sights of Antarctica for the expedition was Mount Erebus, an active volcano. In early January 1911, the Terra Nova reached Ross Island. The men saw Mount Erebus, a volcano more than three thousand seven hundred metres high, with smoke rising from its summit. It is the southernmost active volcano on Earth. It was named in 1841 by the British explorer James Clark Ross, after one of his ships. Members of Ernest Shackleton's expedition had climbed it in 1908. Historians note that Erebus was a famous landmark for Antarctic expeditions. Wilson painted it many times. Even so, the volcano was not dangerous to the expedition. Its activity was gentle, mostly steam and gas. Scientists today still study it, because it has a rare lake of lava inside its crater. For the men of the Terra Nova, the smoking mountain was a striking sight, rising white and grey above the frozen sea. It became a familiar part of their view for the next two years.

[long-pause]

{img:22-ross-island-coast-cape-black-rocks}

Scott chose a cape on Ross Island as the site for his base and named it Cape Evans. Scott searched for a good place to build his base. He chose a small cape of dark volcanic rock and sand on the west coast of Ross Island. He named it Cape Evans, after his second in command, Lieutenant Edward Evans. The cape was sheltered and had easy access to the sea ice, which the men could use as a road towards the south. It was also some distance from the base of the earlier Discovery expedition at Hut Point. Historians know about the choice from Scott's journal and from maps made by the expedition. Even so, the site had a disadvantage. If the sea ice broke up, the route south would be cut off. The men had to be careful about travelling at certain times of year. Yet Cape Evans proved a good choice for a home. The hut built there still stands today, more than a century later.

[long-pause]

{img:23-ponies-pulling-sledges-sea-ice-ship}

Unloading the Terra Nova at Cape Evans took days of continuous work. As soon as the base site was chosen, the men began unloading the ship. Everything had to be carried across the sea ice from the ship to the shore. Ponies, dogs and men pulled sledges loaded with crates, coal, fodder and equipment. Because the sun did not set, they worked in shifts through the day and night. Historians know about the unloading from diaries, which describe exhausting but cheerful work. Even so, there were accidents. One of the motor sledges fell through the sea ice and sank. It was a serious loss, since the motor sledges were meant to help carry supplies south. The work continued for about a week. By the end, the beach was covered with stores, and the hut was being built. Diaries describe the satisfaction the men felt when the most important supplies were safely on land, ready for the long months ahead.

[long-pause]

{img:24-wooden-hut-construction-cape-snow}

The hut at Cape Evans was made in Britain and brought to Antarctica in pieces. The expedition's hut was designed and built in Britain, then taken apart and carried on the ship in numbered pieces. At Cape Evans, the men put it together like a giant puzzle. It was about fifteen metres long and eight metres wide. The walls were made of wooden boards with layers of insulation, including seaweed in quilted sacks, to keep out the cold. Historians know about its construction from plans, photographs and diaries. The carpenter Frank Davies and other men worked quickly, and the hut was finished in about two weeks. Even so, it was not perfect. Some parts leaked, and the men had to make adjustments. Yet it proved warm and solid enough for the winter. A stable for the ponies was built along one side. The hut would be home for twenty-five men for the next year, and for some of them, for two winters.

[long-pause]

{img:25-hut-cape-evans-erebus-behind-sunlight}

On calm days at Cape Evans, the only sounds were the sea, the birds and the dogs. In the summer, when the weather was fine, Cape Evans could be a peaceful place. The hut stood on the dark beach, with Mount Erebus rising behind it. The sea ice stretched away towards distant mountains. Penguins and seals lived nearby, and skuas, large brown seabirds, flew overhead. On calm days, the men could hear the sea lapping against the ice, the calls of birds and the occasional barking of the dogs. Historians know about these quiet moments from diaries and from Ponting's photographs, which show the base in calm weather. Even so, the weather could change very quickly. Strong winds, called blizzards, could blow for days, filling the air with snow. Yet the men treasured the calm days. Many wrote about the beauty and silence of the landscape, which seemed to them like no other place on Earth. Some said those days were among the happiest of their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:26-scientist-cutting-hole-sea-ice-net}

The scientists at Cape Evans studied the sea, the weather and the ice in many different ways. The scientists of the expedition began their work as soon as the hut was built. Biologists cut holes in the sea ice and lowered nets to catch fish and small sea creatures. Geologists studied the rocks of Ross Island and the surrounding mountains. The meteorologist George Simpson set up instruments to record temperature, wind and air pressure. He also sent balloons into the sky to study the upper atmosphere. Historians consider the expedition's weather records especially valuable. They were among the most detailed made in Antarctica at that time and are still used by scientists today. Even so, the work was difficult in the cold. Instruments froze, and handling equipment with gloves was hard. Yet the scientists persevered. Their notebooks were filled with measurements and observations. Many samples collected by the expedition are still kept in museums, including the Natural History Museum in London, and continue to be studied.

[long-pause]

{img:27-photographer-heavy-camera-tripod-ice}

Herbert Ponting, the expedition's photographer, took some of the most famous pictures of Antarctica ever made. Herbert George Ponting was born in 1870. Before joining the expedition, he had travelled widely in Asia and become famous for his photographs of Japan. He called himself a camera artist. On the expedition, he photographed the landscapes, the animals, the men and their daily life. He also made moving films, among the first ever made in Antarctica. Historians and art experts consider his work outstanding. His photographs of icebergs, the hut and the expedition members are still widely reproduced. Even so, his work was very difficult. Cameras were heavy, glass plates had to be developed in the cold, and fingers could freeze to metal. Ponting worked patiently, often waiting hours for the right light. He stayed for the first winter and returned home in 1912. Later, he gave lectures and made a film about the expedition, called The Great White Silence, which was restored and re-released in 2011.

[long-pause]

{img:28-killer-whales-ice-edge-calm-water}

Killer whales once tried to tip Ponting off the ice by breaking it from below. Killer whales, also called orcas, live in the waters around Antarctica. They are intelligent hunters that feed on fish, seals and penguins. In January 1911, while the ship was being unloaded, Ponting went out onto the sea ice to photograph a group of killer whales. Suddenly, the whales struck the ice from below, breaking it beneath him. Ponting managed to jump from floe to floe and reach safety. Scott described the event in his journal. Historians note that killer whales sometimes use this method to knock seals or penguins into the water. Even so, it is unclear whether they intended to attack Ponting or simply saw a shape on the ice. Scientists today know much more about killer whale behaviour. In Antarctica, some groups have learned to make waves that wash seals off floating ice. The incident was a reminder that the beautiful landscape also held real dangers for the men.

[long-pause]

{img:29-men-sitting-outside-hut-golden-midnight}

During the Antarctic summer, the men often sat outside late in the evening to watch the light on the ice. In the southern summer, the sun circled the sky without setting. Late in the evening, it hung low near the horizon, and the light became golden and pink. Icebergs, mountains and snow glowed in warm colours. After a day of work, the men sometimes sat outside the hut on packing cases, smoking pipes, talking and watching the changing light. Historians know about these evenings from diaries and photographs. Many men wrote about the beauty of the scene and the sense of peace it gave them. Even so, they knew that summer would not last. Each day, the sun dipped a little lower. Soon it would begin to set, and then, for four months, it would not rise at all. The men made the most of the long light, working hard and enjoying the quiet evenings. These moments of calm stayed in their memories long after the expedition was over.

[long-pause]

{img:30-ship-disappearing-horizon-ice-evening}

When the Terra Nova sailed away in February 1911, twenty-five men stayed behind at Cape Evans. After unloading and building the base, the Terra Nova had to leave before the sea froze. She sailed in late January and February 1911, carrying a small party to explore the coast elsewhere and later returning to New Zealand for the winter. At Cape Evans, the shore party of twenty-five men remained. They watched the ship sail away until it disappeared over the horizon. Historians note that from this moment, the men were completely cut off from the rest of the world. There was no radio, and no news could reach them until the ship returned the following summer. Even so, they were well prepared. The hut was stocked with food and fuel, and they had a full programme of work planned. The men settled into their new home with a mixture of excitement and seriousness. Their adventure in the Antarctic had truly begun, on the edge of a vast and silent continent.

[long-pause]

{img:31-hut-interior-wall-of-packing-cases}

Inside the hut at Cape Evans, a wall of packing cases divided the officers from the sailors. The hut at Cape Evans was one long room, but Scott divided it into two parts. The dividing wall was made of wooden packing cases filled with stores, such as bottled fruit and other supplies. On one side lived the officers and scientists, in a space called the wardroom. On the other side lived the sailors, in a space called the mess deck, close to the kitchen. This arrangement copied life on a naval ship, where officers and men lived separately. Historians note that the division reflected the society of the time, with its clear ranks and classes. Even so, the wall was not a barrier to friendship. Men from both sides worked together every day and shared the same food and dangers. The packing case wall had another advantage. As the winter went on and stores were used, the cases slowly emptied. The men joked that the wall was getting thinner every week.

[long-pause]

{img:32-wardroom-long-table-acetylene-lamps}

Scott had a small private corner in the hut, with a bunk, a desk and photographs of his family. As leader of the expedition, Scott had a small cubicle at one end of the wardroom. It held a bunk, a small desk and a shelf of books. On the wall were photographs of his wife, Kathleen, and his young son, Peter. Next to his cubicle, Wilson and the second in command, Lieutenant Edward Evans, had their own spaces. The wardroom was lit by acetylene lamps, which gave a bright white light. A long table stood in the middle, where the officers and scientists ate and worked. Historians know about the layout from plans, photographs and diaries. Scott spent many hours at his desk, writing his journal, planning the journey to the Pole and working out supplies. Even so, he was often seen walking through the hut and talking with the men. Ponting's photograph of Scott writing at his desk is one of the best known images of the expedition.

[long-pause]

{img:33-crowded-bunks-saddles-clothes-hut}

The most crowded corner of the hut was nicknamed the Tenements. Five men shared a small cubicle in the wardroom. They were Bowers, Oates, Cherry-Garrard, the doctor Edward Atkinson and the ponies' assistant Cecil Meares. Their space was so crowded and untidy that it was nicknamed the Tenements, after the crowded housing of poor city districts. Bunks were squeezed tightly together, and clothes, boots, saddles and equipment were piled everywhere. Oates kept pony gear there, and Bowers stored all kinds of useful items. Historians know about the Tenements from diaries and from Ponting's photographs, which show the men sitting on their bunks. Even so, the men were proud of their corner. They made jokes about its untidiness and enjoyed one another's company there. Cherry-Garrard later wrote warmly about the friendships formed in that small space. The Tenements became a symbol of the closeness of the men who lived and worked together through the long Antarctic winter at Cape Evans.

[long-pause]

{img:34-hut-kitchen-stove-cook-bread}

The expedition's cook, Thomas Clissold, baked fresh bread almost every day. At the sailors' end of the hut was a large iron stove, where the cook prepared meals for the whole team. The cook was Thomas Clissold, a young man who had worked in kitchens before joining the expedition. He baked fresh bread nearly every day and prepared meals from tinned food, seal meat and penguin. He was also inventive. He built small gadgets, including one that rang a bell when the dough had risen. Historians know about him from diaries, which praise his cooking. Even so, his job was hard. Cooking for twenty-five hungry men in a small space, with limited ingredients, required great skill and planning. Clissold also took part in some sledge work. Later in the expedition, he was injured in a fall while posing for one of Ponting's photographs. Afterwards, he lived for many years in New Zealand, where he died in 1963.

[long-pause]

{img:35-hut-dinner-table-tins-jam-tea}

The men's meals at Cape Evans included seal meat and penguin as well as tinned food. The expedition brought large amounts of tinned and preserved food from Britain, including meat, vegetables, fruit, jam, biscuits, butter and cocoa. To add fresh food and to prevent scurvy, a disease caused by a lack of vitamin C, the men also ate seal meat and penguin. Historians note that scurvy had been a serious problem on earlier expeditions, and fresh meat helped protect against it. According to diaries, the men had mixed feelings about penguin meat. Some found it delicious, while others described its taste as like beef that had been soaked in fish. Seal liver was considered a special treat. Meals followed a regular pattern, with breakfast, lunch and dinner at set times. Even so, the food was generally good and plentiful. On special occasions, such as birthdays, the cook prepared extra dishes. Mealtimes were among the most important social moments of each day in the hut.

[long-pause]

{img:36-pianola-gramophone-corner-hut-lamplight}

The hut at Cape Evans had a pianola, a piano that could play itself, and a gramophone. The expedition took a pianola and a gramophone to Antarctica. A pianola is a piano that can play itself, using rolls of paper with holes punched in them to control which notes are played. The gramophone played records, including popular songs and classical music. Historians know about the music from diaries, which mention the pieces the men enjoyed. Music helped the men relax, especially during the long winter darkness. Evenings often ended with music, and some men had favourite records that they asked for again and again. Even so, the choice of music sometimes caused friendly arguments. Some men preferred classical pieces, while others liked music hall songs. The pianola and gramophone were treasured possessions. In such an isolated place, they brought a feeling of home. The sound of music in the warm hut, while the wind howled outside, was something many men later remembered with great affection.

[long-pause]

{img:37-darkroom-red-light-photographs-drying}

Herbert Ponting had a small darkroom in the hut, where he developed his photographs. Ponting was given a small darkroom at one end of the hut. It was a tiny space, about two metres by two metres, where he developed glass plate negatives and printed photographs. He slept there too, on a narrow bunk. The darkroom was lit by a dim red lamp, because ordinary light would spoil the photographic plates. Historians note that the darkroom was one of the warmest places in the hut, since it had to be kept warm for the chemicals to work properly. Ponting worked there for many hours, carefully developing his pictures. Even so, the work was difficult. Water had to be made from melted ice, and chemicals had to be kept from freezing. Yet the results were remarkable. Ponting's photographs and films are among the most important records of the expedition. Many of his glass plates and prints survive today in museums and archives, including the Scott Polar Research Institute in Cambridge.

[long-pause]

{img:38-scientists-laboratory-corner-microscope}

The scientists had their own working corner in the hut, with microscopes, instruments and specimens. Part of the hut was set aside for scientific work. The scientists had benches with microscopes, instruments, books and specimens. The biologists examined small sea creatures under microscopes. The geologists studied rocks and fossils. The physicist and meteorologist kept instruments that recorded the weather and the Earth's magnetism. Some instruments had pens that drew continuous lines on paper, recording changes in wind and pressure day and night. Historians consider the scientific records of the expedition to be of great value. The weather data is still used by scientists studying the climate of Antarctica. Even so, the work was demanding. Instruments had to be checked regularly, often in freezing conditions outside. The meteorologist George Simpson was nicknamed Sunny Jim. His careful records helped scientists understand Antarctic weather for the first time. The scientific corner was a busy place, where work continued throughout the long winter at Cape Evans.

[long-pause]

{img:39-ponies-in-snow-stables-blankets}

The expedition's ponies were small, tough horses brought from Manchuria in northern China. Scott decided to use ponies to help pull sledges on the journey south. He had seen them used by Ernest Shackleton on an earlier expedition. Nineteen ponies were bought in Manchuria, a region in northern China, and brought by ship to New Zealand. They were small, strong and used to cold weather. At Cape Evans, they lived in a stable built along one side of the hut. Historians have long debated Scott's choice of ponies. Ponies are heavy and sink into soft snow, and they need large amounts of fodder, which must be carried. Norwegian explorers preferred dogs, which are lighter and can eat seal meat. Even so, Scott believed ponies could carry heavy loads across the ice shelf. The ponies were given names, and the men became fond of them. Some ponies were gentle, while others were difficult. Looking after them was hard and constant work.

[long-pause]

{img:40-cavalry-officer-brushing-pony-stable-lamp}

Captain Oates criticised the quality of the ponies but cared for them devotedly. Oates was put in charge of the ponies because of his experience with horses in the army. When he first saw them in New Zealand, he was unhappy. Many were old or in poor condition, and he felt they were not suitable for the journey. He wrote in letters home that they were the worst collection of animals he had ever seen. Even so, he worked tirelessly to keep them healthy. He spent many hours in the stable, feeding, grooming and exercising them. When a pony was ill, he stayed with it through the night. Historians note that Oates's care for the ponies earned him great respect. The men appreciated his dry humour and his honesty. He was nicknamed the Soldier and sometimes Titus. In the stable, he would sit and smoke his pipe among the animals. His devotion to the ponies is one of the most remembered parts of the expedition's story.

[long-pause]

{img:41-white-pony-gentle-young-man-snow}

Each pony had its own name and character, from wild and difficult to calm and gentle. The men gave the ponies names, and diaries describe their different personalities. Some ponies were calm and easy to handle. Others were wild and stubborn. One pony, called Christopher, was so difficult that several men were needed to hold him while he was harnessed. Another, called Jimmy Pigg, was known for being gentle. Cherry-Garrard led a pony called Michael, and Bowers led one called Victor. Scott had a favourite named Snippets. Historians know about the ponies from diaries, letters and photographs. Even so, the ponies' story is a sad one. None of them survived the expedition. Some died during the depot journey, and the rest were used on the first part of the journey to the Pole, after which they were killed for food, as planned. The men who had looked after them wrote with sadness about losing them. Diaries show how attached they had become to these hardworking animals.

[long-pause]

{img:42-siberian-sled-dogs-curled-snow}

The expedition's sled dogs came from Siberia and slept outside in the snow, even in blizzards. The expedition brought more than thirty sled dogs from Siberia, in eastern Russia. They were chosen by Cecil Meares, who had travelled widely in Asia. A young Russian dog driver named Dmitri Girev came with them to help. The dogs were thick-coated and hardy. Unlike the ponies, they lived outside, sleeping in the snow even during blizzards. They curled up tightly, covering their noses with their tails to stay warm. Historians note that Scott did not fully trust dogs for the main journey to the Pole, partly because of difficulties on his earlier Discovery expedition. Even so, the dogs proved useful in many ways. They pulled sledges on several journeys and helped carry supplies. They were fed on seal meat and dried fish. The dogs had names and personalities too. Diaries describe their noisy fights, their excitement at mealtimes and their great strength when pulling heavy loads.

[long-pause]

{img:43-adelie-penguins-curious-ice-edge}

Adélie penguins lived near Cape Evans, and the men enjoyed watching their busy lives. Adélie penguins are the most common penguins on the coast of Antarctica. They are small, black and white, with white rings around their eyes, which make them look surprised. They live in large colonies, called rookeries, where they build nests of small stones. Historians note that the expedition's scientists studied them carefully. The surgeon George Murray Levick, a member of the Northern Party, spent a whole summer observing a large colony and later wrote a book about their behaviour. Even so, some of his observations were thought too shocking to publish at the time and were only made public a century later. The men found the penguins entertaining. Diaries describe them stealing stones from each other's nests, chasing intruders and tumbling into the sea. They were not afraid of people and would often walk right up to the men. Watching them was one of the great pleasures of the summer.

[long-pause]

{img:44-weddell-seal-sleeping-sea-ice-sun}

Weddell seals lived under the sea ice near Cape Evans and could be heard calling beneath it. Weddell seals are large seals that live farther south than any other mammal. They spend much of their lives under the sea ice, keeping holes open by scraping the ice with their teeth so they can breathe. On sunny days, they lie on the ice to rest. Historians know from diaries that the men often heard the seals calling under the ice. Their calls are strange, musical sounds, with trills, whistles and chirps. Some men described them as singing. Scientists today know that Weddell seals use these calls to communicate. Even so, seals were also an important source of food for the expedition. Seal meat was eaten by the men and fed to the dogs, and seal blubber was used as fuel. The seals were not afraid of humans, since they had no natural enemies on land. The men were often impressed by how calmly the seals lay on the ice.

[long-pause]

{img:45-snow-petrels-white-birds-over-ice}

Snow petrels, small pure white seabirds, flew over the ice near Cape Evans. Snow petrels are among the most beautiful birds of Antarctica. They are completely white, except for black eyes and a dark beak. They live farther south than almost any other bird, nesting in cracks in rocks and cliffs, sometimes far inland. Historians note that the men of the expedition often saw them flying over the ice and sea. Wilson, who was an expert on birds, painted them. Diaries describe them as looking like ghosts or snowflakes as they flew past. Even so, the men saw many other birds too. Skuas, large brown seabirds, often stole food and attacked penguin nests. Giant petrels and other species visited the coast. The scientists collected and studied the birds, adding to knowledge of Antarctic wildlife. Snow petrels have a special defence. When threatened, they can spit a strong-smelling oil at an attacker. The men learned to treat them with respect.

[long-pause]

{img:46-emperor-penguin-tall-golden-neck-ice}

Edward Wilson wanted to study the eggs of the emperor penguin, the largest penguin in the world. The emperor penguin is the largest penguin in the world. It can stand more than a metre tall and has a golden patch on its neck. Unlike other birds, it breeds in the middle of the Antarctic winter. The male holds the egg on his feet, under a fold of skin, and keeps it warm for about two months in total darkness and extreme cold. Historians note that in 1911, very little was known about emperor penguins. Wilson believed that their embryos, the young birds developing inside the eggs, might help scientists understand how birds had evolved from reptiles. At that time, some scientists thought that an embryo's development repeated the history of its species. Even so, collecting emperor penguin eggs meant travelling in the middle of winter to the nearest known colony, at Cape Crozier. It would mean a journey of more than one hundred kilometres in darkness and terrible cold.

[long-pause]

{img:47-depot-flag-cairn-snow-plain-evening}

Before the winter, the men laid down a large store of supplies called One Ton Depot. In early 1911, before the winter began, Scott led a journey south across the Ross Ice Shelf to lay depots. A depot is a store of food and fuel left along a route, to be used on a later journey. The largest depot was called One Ton Depot, because it held about a ton of supplies. It was marked with a cairn of snow and a flag, so that it could be found again. Historians note that One Ton Depot was placed further north than Scott had originally planned. The ponies were struggling, and Scott decided not to push them further. This decision later became important, because the depot lay about eleven miles beyond where Scott and his companions would be trapped on their return. Even so, the depot journey was difficult. Several ponies died, and some men had a narrow escape when sea ice broke up. The men returned to Cape Evans as winter approached.

[long-pause]

{img:48-last-sunset-before-winter-ice-hut}

In April 1911, the sun set at Cape Evans, and it did not rise again for four months. Because Antarctica lies so far south, it has months of continuous daylight in summer and months of continuous darkness in winter. At Cape Evans, the sun set for the last time in late April 1911. It would not rise above the horizon again until late August. Historians note that the men marked the last sunset with a sense of occasion. Some gathered outside the hut to watch the sun disappear behind the mountains. Diaries describe the beautiful colours of the sky in the final days of light. Even so, the darkness was not complete at first. For several weeks, there was twilight around midday. Later, the darkness deepened, and only the moon, stars and southern lights brightened the sky. The men prepared carefully for the long night. They had food, fuel and plenty of work to keep them busy. The winter at Cape Evans had begun.

[long-pause]

{img:49-aurora-australis-green-curtains-hut}

During the polar night, the men often watched the southern lights, called the aurora australis. The aurora australis, or southern lights, is a natural display of light in the sky near the South Pole. It happens when particles from the sun strike the Earth's atmosphere. The lights appear as glowing curtains, arcs and rays, usually green, sometimes red, pink or purple. In the long winter darkness at Cape Evans, the men often saw the aurora. Historians know about these displays from diaries, which describe them in detail. Wilson painted them, and scientists recorded their shapes and times. Even so, the scientists did not fully understand what caused the aurora. Today, scientists know much more about the connection between the sun and the lights. The men found the aurora beautiful and moving. Some stood outside for long periods despite the cold, watching the lights ripple and change across the sky. Scott described them in his journal as a scene of indescribable beauty and wonder.

[long-pause]

{img:50-hut-breakfast-porridge-morning-lamps}

In the winter darkness, life in the hut followed a regular daily routine. To stay healthy and cheerful during the winter, the men followed a strict daily routine. They woke at the same time each morning and had breakfast at eight o'clock. Porridge, bread, butter, jam and tea were common breakfast foods. After breakfast, each man went to his work. Scientists studied their specimens and recorded data. Others repaired equipment, made sledges and clothing, and cared for the ponies and dogs. Lunch was at one o'clock, and dinner was at half past six. Historians note that Scott believed a regular routine was essential. On earlier expeditions, long periods of darkness had caused depression and illness. Even so, the routine was not too strict. Sunday was a day of rest, with a church service led by Scott. Every day, someone took turns as night watchman, keeping the stove going, checking the instruments and watching over the hut while the others slept.

[long-pause]

{img:51-men-leading-ponies-moonlight-sea-ice}

During the winter, the men exercised the ponies in moonlight and sometimes had to find their way through blizzards. Even in the darkness of winter, the ponies needed daily exercise. On clear days, the men led them across the sea ice near the hut, often by the light of the moon. Diaries describe the beauty of these walks, with the ice glowing silver and the mountains faintly visible. Historians note that the weather could change suddenly. Blizzards swept in with little warning, bringing strong winds and blowing snow. In a bad blizzard, it was impossible to see more than a few feet. The men sometimes used ropes and lines to find their way between the hut and nearby instruments. Even so, there were dangerous moments. On one occasion, Scott himself was lost for a short time while walking near the hut. Men were sent out to search for people who had not returned. The blizzards reminded everyone how dangerous the Antarctic could be, even close to home.

[long-pause]

{img:52-men-reading-playing-chess-hut-evening}

Several evenings a week, the men gave lectures to each other on their special subjects. To pass the long winter evenings, the men organised a programme of lectures. Three evenings a week, one member of the expedition gave a talk on his own area of knowledge. Wilson spoke about penguins and birds. Ponting showed photographs of his travels in Japan and India. Oates gave a talk about the care of horses. Scientists spoke about geology, physics and weather. Scott talked about his plans for the journey to the Pole. Historians know about these lectures from diaries, which describe them as interesting and lively. Even so, not every lecture was easy to follow, and some led to long discussions. On other evenings, the men read books from the hut's library, played chess and cards, wrote diaries and letters, and listened to music. The lectures helped the men learn from each other and fill the long hours of darkness with something interesting.

[long-pause]

{img:53-handmade-newspaper-sealskin-cover-table}

The men produced their own newspaper during the winter, called The South Polar Times. During the winter, the men wrote and illustrated a newspaper called The South Polar Times. It had first been made on Scott's earlier Discovery expedition. Cherry-Garrard was the editor during the Terra Nova expedition. Members wrote stories, poems, articles and jokes, and Wilson contributed paintings and drawings. Each issue was typed and bound by hand. The covers were made from materials found at the base, such as wood from packing cases and sealskin. Historians note that only one copy of each issue was made, and the men took turns reading it. Even so, it was read eagerly by everyone. The newspaper gave the men a way to share their humour and creativity. Some contributions were anonymous, and the men enjoyed guessing who had written them. Original copies survive in museums and archives. A facsimile edition was later published, so that readers could see what the men had made.

[long-pause]

{img:54-midwinter-feast-flags-table-celebration}

On the twenty-second of June 1911, the men celebrated Midwinter Day with a feast. Midwinter Day, around the twenty-second of June, is the darkest day of the year in Antarctica. After it, the days slowly begin to grow lighter, and the sun's return comes closer. For Antarctic expeditions, Midwinter Day became an important celebration, like a second Christmas. At Cape Evans in 1911, the men decorated the hut with flags, and the tables were laid with special food. There was a dinner of seal, roast beef, plum pudding, mince pies and other treats. Historians know about the celebration from diaries and photographs. Bowers made a Christmas tree from ski sticks, decorated with candles and small gifts. There were speeches, toasts and songs. Even so, the evening was also a time for reflection. Many men thought of their families at home. The celebration lifted everyone's spirits. It was a sign that the darkest part of the winter was over.

[long-pause]

{img:55-three-men-hauling-sledge-dark-lantern}

In June 1911, Wilson, Bowers and Cherry-Garrard set out in the darkness to collect emperor penguin eggs. On the twenty-seventh of June 1911, just after Midwinter Day, three men left Cape Evans on a remarkable journey. Edward Wilson, Henry Bowers and Apsley Cherry-Garrard set out to walk to Cape Crozier, about one hundred kilometres away, to collect emperor penguin eggs. They pulled two heavy sledges themselves, without ponies or dogs. They travelled in almost complete darkness, using candle lanterns and moonlight to find their way. Historians consider this one of the hardest journeys ever made in polar history. Cherry-Garrard later described it in his book, which took its title, The Worst Journey in the World, from this experience. Even so, the three men had volunteered for the journey. Wilson believed the eggs were scientifically important, and the others were willing to go with him. The journey also tested food, clothing and equipment that would later be used on the expedition's journey to the Pole.

[long-pause]

{img:56-polar-night-snow-plain-stars-moonlight}

On the winter journey, temperatures fell below minus sixty degrees Celsius. The cold during the winter journey was extreme. Temperatures fell to around minus sixty degrees Celsius, and at times even lower. Historians note that these were among the lowest temperatures ever experienced by people travelling at the time. Cherry-Garrard described how their breath froze immediately and fell around them. Their clothes froze so stiff that they could not bend them. Sleeping bags became filled with ice, and it took hours each night to work their way into them. Even so, the three men kept going. The surface of the snow was so cold that it felt like sand, and the sledges were very hard to pull. Some days, they covered only a few kilometres. The darkness made everything more difficult. They often could not see where they were going and stumbled into crevasses, deep cracks in the ice. The journey to Cape Crozier took nearly three weeks.

[long-pause]

{img:57-small-tent-lantern-glow-dark-snow}

Despite terrible conditions, the three men on the winter journey remained kind and patient with each other. Cherry-Garrard later wrote about the remarkable friendship among the three men during the winter journey. Despite the cold, the darkness and the exhaustion, they never quarrelled. They remained polite and kind to one another, even when they were suffering badly. Historians often quote Cherry-Garrard's admiration for his two companions. He described Wilson as calm and wise, and Bowers as tireless and cheerful. Every night, they set up their tent and cooked a meal of pemmican, a mixture of dried meat and fat, with biscuits and hot drinks. They used a small stove that burned paraffin. Even so, the effort of each simple task was enormous. Their fingers were frostbitten, and every movement was slow. Yet they shared the work and looked after one another. Cherry-Garrard believed that their friendship and good manners were the reason they survived. Their small tent became a home in a hostile world.

[long-pause]

{img:58-stone-hut-rocks-ice-ridges-twilight}

At Cape Crozier, the three men built a small hut of stones, and a storm blew away its roof. When the three men reached Cape Crozier, they built a small hut of rocks and snow on a hillside, with a canvas roof. They hoped it would be a base while they visited the penguin colony below. Historians note that they named the place Oriana Ridge, after Wilson's wife. Even so, disaster struck. A violent blizzard hit, and the wind tore their tent away. Then the canvas roof of the stone hut was ripped off. For more than a day, the three men lay in their sleeping bags in the open, with snow blowing over them. They believed they might die. When the storm ended, they searched the area and, by great luck, found the tent caught among rocks some distance away. Without it, they could not have survived the journey back. The ruins of their stone hut can still be seen at Cape Crozier today, more than a century later.

[long-pause]

{img:59-emperor-penguins-huddle-winter-twilight}

The three men reached the emperor penguin colony and collected five eggs. Before the storm, Wilson, Bowers and Cherry-Garrard made their way down to the emperor penguin colony. It lay below high ice cliffs, on the sea ice. Getting there in the darkness was very hard, and they had to climb through ridges of broken ice. Historians know from Cherry-Garrard's account that they found about one hundred emperor penguins, far fewer than expected. Some of the birds were holding eggs on their feet. The men collected five eggs, carrying them carefully in their mittens. Even so, two of the eggs broke on the way back, when Cherry-Garrard stumbled in the darkness. Wilson prepared the remaining three eggs to preserve the embryos. He also collected some penguin skins and blubber. For a short time at the colony, the three men forgot their suffering. Seeing the great birds in their winter home was an extraordinary experience. No one had ever seen this before.

[long-pause]

{img:60-three-tired-men-arriving-hut-dawn}

The three men returned to Cape Evans on the first of August 1911, and their three eggs are now kept in London. The journey back to Cape Evans was as hard as the journey out. The three men were exhausted, frostbitten and suffering from lack of sleep. Their sleeping bags were so frozen that they could hardly use them. Historians note that they returned to the hut on the first of August 1911, after five weeks away. The other men were shocked by their appearance. Their faces were scarred by the cold, and they could barely move. Even so, they recovered after some days of rest and warmth. Scott wrote that it was the hardest journey ever made. The three emperor penguin eggs were later taken to the Natural History Museum in London. Scientists examined the embryos, but the results did not provide the answers Wilson had hoped for. The eggs are still kept at the museum today. Cherry-Garrard's account of the journey has become one of the most famous stories of exploration.

[long-pause]

{img:61-sun-returns-horizon-golden-ice}

In late August 1911, the sun returned to Cape Evans after four months of darkness. After the long polar night, the sun rose above the horizon again at Cape Evans in late August 1911. For weeks before, the sky had grown lighter around midday, with colours of pink, gold and orange. Historians know from diaries that the men greeted the sun's return with great joy. Some went outside to watch it appear, and a few raised their hats to it. Scott wrote about the beauty of the returning light. Even so, the spring was still very cold, with temperatures often below minus thirty degrees Celsius. Blizzards continued to sweep across the ice. Yet each day was a little longer and a little brighter. The men began to prepare seriously for the journey to the Pole. Equipment was checked, sledges were repaired, and the ponies were trained. Short spring journeys were made to test plans and equipment. After months of waiting, the time for the great journey was approaching.

[long-pause]

{img:62-men-studying-map-lamplit-hut-table}

Scott's planned route to the Pole had three parts: the ice shelf, a glacier and a high plateau. The route to the South Pole from Cape Evans was about thirteen hundred kilometres long. It had three main parts. First, the travellers had to cross the Ross Ice Shelf, then called the Great Ice Barrier, a vast flat sheet of floating ice. Second, they had to climb the Beardmore Glacier, a huge river of ice flowing through the mountains, discovered by Shackleton in 1908. Third, they had to cross the Polar Plateau, a high, flat expanse of ice nearly three thousand metres above sea level. Historians know about Scott's plans from his journal and from his lectures to the men. He planned to use motor sledges, ponies and dogs for the first part, and then men would pull the sledges themselves. Even so, the plan was complex and depended on many things going right. Support parties would accompany the polar party part of the way and then turn back, leaving depots of food and fuel.

[long-pause]

{img:63-packing-ration-bags-hut-table}

Bowers organised the food for the journey, packing it carefully into weekly bags. Food planning was essential for the journey to the Pole. Each man needed enough food to give him energy for long days of pulling heavy sledges in extreme cold. The main food was pemmican, a mixture of dried meat and fat. The men also ate biscuits, butter, sugar, cocoa and tea. Historians note that Bowers was responsible for organising the food. He spent days weighing out rations and packing them into bags, each holding a week's supply for a team. These bags were then placed in depots along the route. Even so, historians and scientists have since shown that the rations were not enough. The men burned far more energy than they ate, and the food lacked some important vitamins. This was not fully understood at the time. Bowers's careful work was much admired by Scott and the others, who trusted him completely to keep track of every supply.

[long-pause]

{img:64-scott-writing-journal-cubicle-candle}

In 1910, Scott learned that the Norwegian explorer Roald Amundsen was also heading for Antarctica. Roald Amundsen was a famous Norwegian explorer. He had been the first to sail through the Northwest Passage, the sea route around northern Canada. In 1910, he was preparing an expedition to the North Pole. But when news came that American explorers claimed to have reached the North Pole, he secretly changed his plans and decided to go south instead. When the Terra Nova reached Melbourne in October 1910, Scott received a telegram from Amundsen. It said simply that he was going south. Historians note that this message turned the expedition into a race. Even so, Scott decided not to change his plans. He said his expedition would continue as intended, with its scientific programme, and would not be hurried. In February 1911, members of Scott's expedition found Amundsen's ship, the Fram, at the Bay of Whales, on the edge of the ice shelf. The race had truly begun.

[long-pause]

{img:65-norwegian-hut-framheim-barrier-snow}

Amundsen's expedition spent the winter in a hut called Framheim, on the ice shelf. Amundsen's base was at the Bay of Whales, on the edge of the Ross Ice Shelf, about one hundred kilometres closer to the Pole than Scott's base. His hut was called Framheim, meaning home of the Fram. It was a wooden hut that the Norwegians had brought with them. Historians note that the Norwegians dug tunnels and rooms into the snow around the hut. These included workshops, storerooms and even a sauna. The team of nine men spent the winter improving their equipment. They made lighter sledges, better boots and new tents. Even so, they also made a mistake. In September 1911, Amundsen started too early, and the extreme cold forced the team to turn back. The setback caused a disagreement within the team, and Amundsen changed the members of the party. The Norwegians set out again in October 1911, with five men, four sledges and fifty-two dogs.

[long-pause]

{img:66-motor-sledges-departing-sea-ice-smoke}

On the twenty-fourth of October 1911, the motor sledges left Cape Evans for the south. Scott's journey to the Pole began with the motor sledges. They were an early type of tracked vehicle, designed to pull heavy loads across snow and ice. Two remained after one had sunk during unloading. On the twenty-fourth of October 1911, they left Cape Evans with a team of four men, led by Lieutenant Edward Evans. Historians note that this was one of the first uses of motor vehicles in Antarctica. Even so, they did not last long. Their engines overheated and broke down, and within a week both motor sledges had been abandoned. The men then pulled the loads themselves. Yet the motor sledges had carried supplies a useful distance. Historians consider them important early experiments. Scott had hoped they would show the way for future polar travel. In later years, tracked vehicles became essential in Antarctica, and the ideas tested by Scott's expedition were developed further by other explorers.

[long-pause]

{img:67-dog-teams-crossing-barrier-sunny}

Amundsen's team travelled quickly on skis, with dogs pulling their sledges. Amundsen's team left Framheim on the twentieth of October 1911. The five men travelled on skis, and their sledges were pulled by dogs. The Norwegians were expert skiers and dog drivers, with years of experience in cold climates. Historians often compare the two expeditions. Amundsen's plan was simpler and focused entirely on reaching the Pole. He used dogs because they were fast and could survive on very little. As the journey went on, some dogs were killed to feed the others and the men, as planned. Even so, the Norwegians faced their own dangers. They found a new route through the mountains, up a steep glacier they named the Axel Heiberg Glacier, and crossed areas of dangerous crevasses. They reached the Polar Plateau in late November. Their good planning, skill and the strength of their dogs carried them forward quickly across the high, cold plateau.

[long-pause]

{img:68-small-tent-flag-alone-snow-plain}

On the fourteenth of December 1911, Amundsen's team reached the South Pole. On the fourteenth of December 1911, Amundsen and his four companions, Olav Bjaaland, Helmer Hanssen, Sverre Hassel and Oscar Wisting, reached the South Pole. They were the first people ever to stand there. They planted the Norwegian flag and named the area King Haakon the Seventh's Plateau, after the king of Norway. Historians know about their arrival from Amundsen's book and the diaries of his men. The Norwegians took careful measurements to make sure they had found the right place. Before leaving, they set up a small tent, which they called Polheim. Inside, Amundsen left a letter for the king of Norway and a short note for Scott, asking him to deliver the letter if the Norwegians did not return safely. Even so, there was no great celebration. The men were tired and eager to return. They arrived safely back at Framheim on the twenty-fifth of January 1912.

[long-pause]

{img:69-ponies-sledges-leaving-hut-spring}

Scott's main party left Cape Evans with the ponies on the first of November 1911. On the first of November 1911, Scott and the main party set out from Cape Evans with the ponies. Each pony pulled a sledge loaded with supplies, and each was led by a man. The dog teams, driven by Meares and Dmitri, followed later. Historians know about the departure from diaries and photographs. The ponies moved slowly, and the party travelled in groups, stretched out across the ice. Even so, the start was hopeful. The weather was good, and the men were fit after the winter. The ponies managed better than some had expected. The party moved south across the Ross Ice Shelf, passing the depots laid the year before. They also found the motor sledge team, who were now pulling their sledges by hand. Day after day, the men walked south across the flat white plain, with mountains rising on the horizon to the west.

[long-pause]

{img:70-night-march-midnight-sun-low-ponies}

On the ice shelf, Scott's party often marched at night, when the snow surface was harder. During the Antarctic summer, the sun shines all day and night. In the middle of the day, its warmth softens the snow surface, making it harder for ponies and sledges to move. To avoid this, Scott's party often marched during the night, when the sun was lower and the snow was firmer. They then rested during the day in their tents. Historians know about this routine from diaries. Even so, the ponies struggled. They sank into soft snow and grew weaker as the journey went on. Walls of snow were built each night to shelter them from the wind. The men took great care of the animals, but their condition worsened. As planned, the weakest ponies were killed along the way, and their meat was left in depots as food for the return journey and for the dogs. The men felt sad at losing animals they had cared for over many months.

[long-pause]

{img:71-tents-snowed-in-glacier-foot-mountains}

In early December 1911, a four-day blizzard stopped Scott's party at the foot of the Beardmore Glacier. As Scott's party approached the Beardmore Glacier, a severe blizzard struck. It lasted four days, from the fifth to the eighth of December 1911. The men were trapped in their tents while snow piled up around them. The temperature was unusually warm, and the snow was wet, soaking their clothing and sleeping bags. Historians note that this delay was costly. The party used food intended for later in the journey, and they lost valuable time. Even so, the men stayed patient, reading and talking in the tents. When the blizzard ended, the snow was deep and soft, making travel extremely difficult. The remaining ponies were too weak to continue, and they were shot at a place the men named Shambles Camp. From then on, the men would pull the sledges themselves. The dog teams turned back towards Cape Evans a few days later, after helping carry supplies up to the glacier.

[long-pause]

{img:72-men-hauling-sledges-beardmore-glacier}

Scott's party climbed the Beardmore Glacier pulling their sledges themselves, and celebrated Christmas on the ice. The Beardmore Glacier is one of the largest glaciers in the world, about one hundred and sixty kilometres long. Scott's party climbed it by man-hauling, which means pulling the sledges themselves with harnesses. Twelve men in three teams worked their way up. Historians know from diaries that the work was exhausting. The surface was rough, with crevasses, ice ridges and soft snow. Some men fell into crevasses and had to be pulled out by their harness ropes. Even so, the men kept up their spirits. On Christmas Day 1911, high on the glacier, they had a special meal. Bowers produced a small plum pudding he had carried secretly, and there was extra chocolate and raisins. Diaries describe the meal as a happy occasion. The men also enjoyed the magnificent mountain scenery around them. By the end of December, they reached the top of the glacier and stood on the edge of the Polar Plateau.

[long-pause]

{img:73-two-groups-parting-plateau-handshakes}

On the fourth of January 1912, the last support party turned back, leaving five men to go on to the Pole. As planned, support parties turned back at intervals, leaving the strongest men to continue. The first group of four turned back at the top of the Beardmore Glacier. Then, on the fourth of January 1912, Scott chose the final polar party. He took Wilson, Bowers, Oates and Petty Officer Edgar Evans. The last support party, led by Lieutenant Edward Evans with Tom Crean and William Lashly, said goodbye and turned north. Historians note that Scott's decision to take five men instead of four surprised some, since food and tents had been planned for teams of four. Even so, Scott believed five men could pull faster. Diaries describe the parting as emotional. Crean was in tears as he shook hands. The last support party later had their own terrible journey home. Edward Evans fell seriously ill with scurvy, and Crean walked alone for many hours across the ice to fetch help, saving his life.

[long-pause]

{img:74-five-men-hauling-sledge-plateau-sun}

The five men of the polar party pulled their sledge across the high plateau towards the Pole. From early January 1912, the five men of the polar party travelled across the Polar Plateau. They pulled one heavy sledge, carrying their tent, sleeping bags, food, fuel and instruments. Four of them used skis. Bowers did not, because his skis had been sent back with the support party before Scott decided to take him. He walked on foot through the snow, keeping pace with the others. Historians note that this made his work even harder. Even so, he never complained. The plateau was high, cold and windy. The thin air at nearly three thousand metres made breathing harder. Each day, the men marched for many hours, covering about fifteen to twenty kilometres. Scott recorded the distances carefully in his journal. The men were growing tired and hungry, but their spirits were still high. They believed they had a good chance of reaching the Pole first, since they had seen no sign of Amundsen.

[long-pause]

{img:75-sastrugi-snow-ridges-plateau-low-sun}

On the plateau, the wind had carved the snow into hard ridges called sastrugi. The surface of the Polar Plateau is shaped by strong winds. They carve the snow into hard, wave-like ridges called sastrugi. Some are small, while others can be more than a metre high. Historians know from diaries that the sastrugi made sledging very difficult. The sledge caught on the ridges and had to be pulled over them, using great effort. Even so, the surface was not the only problem. Fresh snow sometimes covered the ice, creating a sandy texture that made the sledge drag heavily. The weather was also very cold. Temperatures fell to around minus thirty degrees Celsius, even in the middle of summer. The wind made it feel much colder. The men suffered from frostbite on their faces, fingers and feet. Yet they continued south, day after day. Scott's journal records his concern about the difficult surface and the slow progress, as well as his hope of reaching the Pole.

[long-pause]

{img:76-five-men-inside-small-tent-stove-cocoa}

Each evening, the five men shared a small tent, cooked a hot meal and wrote in their diaries. At the end of each day's march, the five men put up their tent. It was cone-shaped, made of canvas, and designed to stand up to strong winds. Inside, they lit a small stove called a Primus, which burned paraffin. They cooked a hot meal, usually pemmican stew called hoosh, with biscuits and hot cocoa or tea. Historians know about these evenings from the diaries kept by Scott, Wilson and Bowers. The men wrote that the tent felt warm and comfortable after the hard day's work. They talked, made plans and wrote in their journals. Even so, the tent was crowded with five people, since it had been designed for four. Getting into frozen sleeping bags each night was difficult. The men slept close together for warmth. Wilson cared for the others' injuries, using his skills as a doctor. These evening hours were a small refuge from the cold and wind outside.

[long-pause]

{img:77-black-flag-distant-snow-plain}

On the sixteenth of January 1912, the polar party saw a black flag left by the Norwegians. On the sixteenth of January 1912, the five men were about twenty-five kilometres from the South Pole. Bowers, who had sharp eyes, spotted something on the horizon. As they came closer, they saw that it was a black flag tied to a sledge runner. Around it were ski tracks, sledge tracks and the paw prints of many dogs. Historians know about this moment from Scott's journal and the other diaries. The men realised that the Norwegians had reached the Pole before them. Scott wrote that it was a terrible disappointment. Even so, the men continued to the Pole. They knew it was important to complete their journey and to record their scientific observations. Scott's diary from that day shows how deeply the men were affected. After months of planning and hardship, they knew they had come second. Yet they faced the news with dignity and kept going, as they had always done.

[long-pause]

{img:78-five-men-standing-pole-flag-wind}

On the seventeenth of January 1912, Scott's party reached the South Pole, thirty-four days after Amundsen. On the seventeenth of January 1912, Scott, Wilson, Bowers, Oates and Edgar Evans reached the South Pole. They took measurements to confirm their position and raised the British flag. The next day, they found Amundsen's tent, Polheim, with the Norwegian flag above it. Inside was the note for Scott and the letter for the king of Norway. Scott took the letter with him, as Amundsen had asked. Historians know about these days from the diaries. Scott wrote that the Pole was an awful place, cold and bleak, and that it was terrible to have worked so hard without the reward of being first. Even so, the men had achieved something extraordinary. They had walked across the ice shelf, climbed the glacier and crossed the plateau, pulling their own sledge. They spent a short time at the Pole, building a cairn and taking photographs. Then they turned north to begin the long journey home.

[long-pause]

{img:79-five-explorers-group-portrait-snow}

Bowers took a famous photograph of the five men at the South Pole, using a string to work the camera. At the South Pole, Bowers took several photographs of the polar party. To include himself in the group, he pulled a string attached to the camera's shutter. The best known photograph shows the five men standing and sitting together, tired and weather-beaten, beside the flag. Historians consider it one of the most famous photographs in the history of exploration. It shows the men's exhaustion and disappointment, but also their pride. Even so, the photographs were not seen by the world until more than a year later. The camera and plates were found with the men's bodies in November 1912 and brought back to Britain. Ponting had trained the men in photography before the journey. Scott and Bowers had both learned to use a camera. Their photographs from the plateau and the Pole are a unique record. They show the conditions and the people of the polar party during its final months.

[long-pause]

{img:80-men-marching-north-sail-on-sledge}

On the return journey, the polar party sometimes used a sail on their sledge to catch the wind. The return journey from the Pole began on the nineteenth of January 1912. The five men faced more than thirteen hundred kilometres of travel back to Cape Evans. On the plateau, the wind sometimes blew from behind them. To make use of it, they rigged a sail on their sledge, made from a tent floor. The wind helped push the sledge, making their work a little easier. Historians know about this from Scott's journal and Wilson's diary. Even so, the journey was very hard. The men were growing weaker. They were short of food and suffering from the cold. Edgar Evans had a badly cut hand that would not heal. They had to find each depot they had left on the way south, following their old tracks and cairns. Finding the depots was essential, because each one held the food and fuel they needed to survive the next part of the journey.

[long-pause]

{img:81-fossil-rocks-glacier-moraine-sketch}

On the way down the Beardmore Glacier, the men collected rocks containing fossils. On the eighth of February 1912, while descending the Beardmore Glacier, the polar party stopped near a mountain called Mount Buckley. Wilson, the scientist, wanted to collect rock samples. The men spent the afternoon geologising, as Scott called it, gathering rocks from the moraine, the loose stones at the edge of the glacier. Some of the rocks contained fossils of ancient plants, including leaves of a tree-like plant called Glossopteris. Historians note that the men carried about sixteen kilograms of rocks with them on their sledge, despite their weakening condition. Even so, they did not abandon them. Scott and Wilson believed the samples were scientifically important. They were right. The fossils were later found with the men's bodies. Scientists studying them confirmed that Glossopteris had once grown in Antarctica. The same plant was known from South America, Africa, India and Australia. This helped show that these continents were once joined together.

[long-pause]

{img:82-quiet-tent-glacier-foot-evening-light}

On the seventeenth of February 1912, Edgar Evans died at the foot of the Beardmore Glacier. Petty Officer Edgar Evans was a strong, cheerful Welshman from the Gower Peninsula, near Swansea. He had been on the Discovery expedition with Scott and was known for his great physical strength. On the return journey, his condition grew worse. His cut hand did not heal, he suffered from frostbite, and he probably hit his head in a fall into a crevasse. He became confused and very weak. Historians note that he may also have suffered from a lack of food and vitamins. On the seventeenth of February 1912, near the foot of the Beardmore Glacier, he collapsed. His companions carried him to the tent, where he died that night. Even so, his companions spoke kindly of him in their diaries. Evans was the first of the polar party to die. Today, he is remembered in Wales with memorials, including one at the church in Rhossili, his home village.

[long-pause]

{img:83-tall-man-quiet-tent-evening-stove}

In March 1912, Captain Oates walked out of the tent into a blizzard, so as not to slow his companions. As the four remaining men crossed the ice shelf, conditions became worse. The temperatures were much lower than expected for the time of year, and the surface was very hard to pull on. Oates was suffering badly from frostbite in his feet, and an old war wound had reopened. He could barely walk and knew he was slowing the others. Historians know about his final days from Scott's journal. On the sixteenth or seventeenth of March 1912, Oates got up from his sleeping bag and said, according to Scott, that he was just going outside and might be some time. He then walked out into a blizzard and was never seen again. It was his thirty-second birthday. Even so, his companions did not stop him. Scott wrote that it was the act of a brave man and an English gentleman. His body was never found. His sacrifice became one of the most famous stories of the expedition.

[long-pause]

{img:84-candle-tent-writing-letters-quiet}

In his last days, Scott wrote letters to the families of his companions and to his wife. After Oates's death, Scott, Wilson and Bowers struggled on. On about the twentieth of March 1912, they made their last camp, about eighteen kilometres from One Ton Depot. A blizzard trapped them in their tent. They had very little food and fuel left. Historians know about these last days from Scott's journal and letters, which were found with the bodies. Scott wrote letters to the families of Wilson and Bowers, praising their courage. He wrote a message to the public, explaining the reasons for the disaster and asking that the families be looked after. He also wrote to his wife, Kathleen. In this letter, he asked her to make their son interested in natural history, rather than games. Even so, he wrote calmly and clearly. His final journal entry is dated the twenty-ninth of March 1912. It ends with the words: For God's sake look after our people.

[long-pause]

{img:85-two-men-dog-sledges-depot-waiting}

In March 1912, Cherry-Garrard and Dmitri waited at One Ton Depot with dog teams, but the polar party did not arrive. In late February 1912, Cherry-Garrard and the dog driver Dmitri Girev took two dog teams south from Cape Evans to One Ton Depot. They brought extra supplies and hoped to meet the polar party. They reached the depot on the fourth of March and waited there for six days. Historians know about this journey from Cherry-Garrard's book. He watched the horizon for any sign of the returning men. Even so, the weather was bad, and dog food was running short. He had been told not to risk the dogs, since they would be needed later. On the tenth of March, he decided to return to Cape Evans. At that moment, the polar party was struggling on about one hundred kilometres to the south. Historians have discussed Cherry-Garrard's decision for many years. Most agree that he followed his orders and could not have known where the polar party was. Even so, he felt the weight of that decision for the rest of his life.

[long-pause]

{img:86-second-winter-hut-thirteen-men-lamp}

During the second winter of 1912, thirteen men waited at Cape Evans, not knowing what had happened. When autumn came in 1912 and the polar party had not returned, the men at Cape Evans knew that something terrible must have happened. The Terra Nova had come and gone again, taking some members home, including Ponting and Simpson. Thirteen men stayed for a second winter, led by the doctor Edward Atkinson. Historians know about this winter from diaries. It was a sad and difficult time. The empty bunks of the polar party stood in the hut, with their books and possessions still on the shelves. Even so, the men kept to their routine and continued their scientific work. They also planned a search for the polar party for the following spring. In the meantime, they did not know that a separate group, the Northern Party, was surviving a winter in an ice cave far to the north. The second winter was long and quiet, with few celebrations and much uncertainty about the future.

[long-pause]

{img:87-snow-cairn-ski-cross-plain-sunlight}

On the twelfth of November 1912, a search party found the tent with the bodies of Scott, Wilson and Bowers. In late October 1912, as spring returned, a search party left Cape Evans to look for the polar party. It was led by Atkinson and included Cherry-Garrard, Tom Crean, William Lashly and others. On the twelfth of November 1912, they saw a small mound of snow on the ice shelf, with the tip of a tent showing. Inside, they found the bodies of Scott, Wilson and Bowers in their sleeping bags. Historians know about this moment from several diaries. The men collected Scott's journal, letters, photographs and the geological samples. They read parts of the journal to learn what had happened. Even so, they did not move the bodies. They collapsed the tent over them and built a large cairn of snow on top. They placed a cross made from skis on the cairn. They also searched for Oates's body but did not find it, so they built a cairn near where he was believed to have died.

[long-pause]

{img:88-wooden-cross-observation-hill-view}

The expedition members put up a large wooden cross on Observation Hill, with a line from a famous poem. Before leaving Antarctica, the remaining members of the expedition made a memorial to the polar party. They built a large wooden cross and carried it to the top of Observation Hill, near Hut Point, overlooking the ice shelf. On the cross, they carved the names of the five men who had died. Below the names, they carved a line from Ulysses, a poem by Alfred Tennyson: To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield. Historians note that the line was chosen by Cherry-Garrard. The memorial was put up in January 1913. Even so, the expedition was not quite over. In February 1913, the Terra Nova sailed back to New Zealand with the survivors, and news of the deaths was sent to the world by telegraph. The cross still stands today on Observation Hill, near the modern American base of McMurdo Station and the New Zealand base of Scott Base.

[long-pause]

{img:89-woman-ship-deck-sea-calm-thoughtful}

Scott's son, Peter Scott, grew up to become a famous naturalist, as his father had hoped. Kathleen Scott was on a ship on her way to New Zealand to meet her husband when she learned of his death. The news shocked Britain and the world. Scott and his companions became national heroes. Memorials were built in many towns, and money was raised for the families. Historians note that Scott's journal was published and widely read. Even so, in later decades, some writers questioned Scott's leadership and decisions, while others defended him. The debate continues among historians today. Scott's son, Peter, was only two years old when his father died. He grew up to become a famous naturalist, painter and conservationist, just as his father had hoped. He founded the Wildfowl and Wetlands Trust in Britain and helped start the World Wildlife Fund. He designed its famous panda logo. He was knighted in 1973 and died in 1989. His father's wish for him had come true.

[long-pause]

{img:90-fossil-leaf-stone-museum-lamp}

The fossils collected by the polar party helped prove that Antarctica was once part of a great southern continent. The rocks collected by the polar party on the Beardmore Glacier were brought back to Britain and studied by scientists. They contained fossils of Glossopteris, an ancient plant that grew about two hundred and fifty million years ago. Historians and geologists note that these fossils were important evidence. Glossopteris fossils had already been found in South America, Africa, India and Australia. Finding them in Antarctica supported the idea that these lands were once joined together in a single great continent, now called Gondwana. Even so, at the time, many scientists did not accept that continents could move. The theory of continental drift, proposed by the German scientist Alfred Wegener in 1912, was not widely accepted until the 1960s. The fossils from Scott's expedition became part of that story. Some of them are kept today at the Natural History Museum in London, where scientists can still study them.

[long-pause]

{img:91-cape-evans-hut-today-erebus-sunlight}

The hut at Cape Evans still stands today and is carefully preserved. More than a century after the expedition, the hut at Cape Evans still stands on Ross Island. Because of the cold, dry climate, many objects inside have survived. There are tins of food, bottles, books, clothing, equipment and scientific instruments. The bunks and the long table are still in place. Historians and conservators consider it one of the most important historic sites in Antarctica. It is cared for by the Antarctic Heritage Trust, a New Zealand charity, which has carried out major conservation work to protect the building and its contents. Even so, the hut faces challenges. Snow, wind and salt damage the wood, and conservators must work carefully to preserve it. Visitors to Antarctica can sometimes see the hut, but only in small groups and with permission. Standing inside, they can see the rooms where the men lived, worked and waited, as if they had left only a short time ago.
