{img:01-terra-nova-cardiff-harbour-1910}

In the spring of 1910, more than eight thousand people wrote letters to a naval captain in London. They all asked for the same thing. They wanted to go with him to the coldest place on Earth. They knew there would be months of darkness there, and winds that could knock a grown man off his feet. They knew there would be no houses, no trees and no towns. And still they begged to come.

[pause]

Only a few dozen were chosen. They sailed south in an old wooden whaling ship, and they built a little hut on a shore of black sand. Then they lived there, through a winter in which the sun did not rise at all. So how did these men survive at the bottom of the world? And how did they manage to be happy there, most of the time? Come aboard, and we'll find out together, one quiet day at a time.

[pause]

A small word before the ship casts off. Should you want to sail with us again on other evenings, you can subscribe to the channel. There is no hurry. Wait and see whether the voyage suits you first.

[pause]

Before we go, a small question for the comments. From which corner of the world are these words reaching you? Is it a city, a farmhouse, or somewhere by the sea? What does the clock beside your bed say right now? Write it below, whenever you like.

[pause]

Now pull the covers a little higher. Let your shoulders grow heavy. Breathe slowly, like the long, easy swell of a southern sea.

[long-pause]

Scott's great expedition did not leave from London, but from a coal harbour in Wales.

[pause]

On a summer afternoon in June 1910, the docks of Cardiff were full of people. They stood along the quays and on the decks of other ships. They waved hats and handkerchiefs, and a band played on the shore. Out in the harbour, an old black ship was getting up steam. Smoke rose from her single funnel, and her three tall masts stood against the sky. Why Cardiff, and not London? The answer was coal. Cardiff was one of the busiest coal ports in the world. Its merchants had given the expedition money, and free coal for the long voyage. So the city wanted to say goodbye in style. As the ship moved out, every vessel in the harbour blew its whistle. The noise rolled across the water for a long time. On deck, the young crew climbed into the rigging to wave back. Slowly, the green hills of Wales turned blue behind them, and then faded into the evening haze.

[long-pause]

{img:02-old-wooden-whaling-ship-oak-bow}

The Terra Nova was not new, and nobody ever called her beautiful.

[pause]

The ship was called the Terra Nova, which means New Land. Yet she was already old. She had been built in Dundee, in Scotland, in 1884, to hunt whales and seals in the icy seas of the far north. Her hull was made of thick oak. At the bow, the wood was extra strong, so she could push her way through floating ice. She was about a hundred and eighty-seven feet long. That is not very big for a voyage across half the world. And she smelled, the men said, of whale oil, tar and damp old wood. No amount of scrubbing could ever get the smell out. But Scott had chosen her for a good reason. A ship built for the ice was exactly what he needed. Sleek new ships were fine in warm water. In the frozen south, only a strong old whaler would do. The men soon grew fond of her, smell and all. Some of them would speak of her lovingly for the rest of their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:03-crowded-ship-deck-crates-hay-coal}

Every inch of the deck was crammed with crates, hay and coal.

[pause]

The Terra Nova carried everything the men would need for more than two years. There was no shop where they were going, and no way to send for anything they forgot. So the holds were packed with food, tools, clothes and scientific instruments. When the holds were full, the rest went up on deck. Sacks of coal were piled in every corner. Bales of hay stood in rows for the animals that would come aboard later. And lashed down in big wooden boxes were three motor sledges. These were a brand new idea, a kind of small tractor for the snow. Nobody had ever tried them in the far south before. Later, in New Zealand, the crew would also take on board nineteen ponies and more than thirty dogs. Imagine trying to walk across that deck on a rolling sea. You would step over ropes, squeeze past crates, and duck under swinging lanterns. Somehow, the sailors found their way through it all, day and night.

[long-pause]

{img:04-antarctica-white-coast-distant-mountains}

In 1910, almost nothing was known about the land they were sailing to.

[pause]

Antarctica is a whole continent of ice, sitting around the South Pole. It is bigger than the United States and Mexico put together. It is the coldest, windiest and driest place on Earth. Almost all of it lies under a great sheet of ice, in places more than two miles thick. No people live there, and no trees grow there. In winter, the sun stays below the horizon for months. In 1910, only a handful of ships had ever reached its coast. Only a few people had ever stood on its shores. The maps showed long stretches of coast, and then simply empty white space. Nobody knew what lay in the middle. And no one in all of human history had ever reached the South Pole. Can you imagine looking at a map with a blank space in it? For these men, that blank space was the whole reason to go. They wanted to fill it in, step by careful step.

[long-pause]

{img:05-naval-captain-ship-deck-thoughtful}

The leader was a quiet, serious naval officer who had been to the ice before.

[pause]

His name was Robert Falcon Scott. He was forty-two years old and a captain in the Royal Navy. He was of medium height, with a strong square face and clear blue eyes. People who knew him said he was thoughtful and determined. He could also be moody, and sometimes rather shy. Yet his men respected him, and many grew very fond of him. Scott had been to Antarctica once already, between 1901 and 1904, on a ship called the Discovery. On that journey, he and two friends had marched further south than anyone before them. But they had not reached the Pole. They had come home tired and hungry, with the job unfinished. Now Scott was going back to try again. He was not a man who liked to talk about his feelings. He wrote them down instead, in a journal he kept every day. Those pages would later become some of the most famous words ever written about the ice.

[long-pause]

{img:06-sculptor-woman-studio-london-1910}

Scott's wife was a sculptor who had studied art in Paris.

[pause]

Scott left behind a wife and a baby son. His wife was called Kathleen. She was a lively and independent woman, quite unusual for her time. She had studied sculpture in Paris and knew many famous artists and writers. She loved adventure as much as her husband did. Kathleen travelled with the expedition as far as New Zealand. She wanted to see her husband off on the last stage of his journey. Their son, Peter, was not yet a year old. He stayed at home in London with a nurse. Scott thought about him often. He kept a photograph of the little boy in his cabin, and later in his corner of the hut. It is strange to think of it. A man was heading for the emptiest place on Earth, with a baby's picture beside his bed. Many of the men carried photographs like that. On long evenings, far from home, those small pictures would be looked at again and again.

[long-pause]

{img:07-scientists-on-ship-deck-instruments}

The expedition was not only a race to the Pole, but a floating school of science.

[pause]

Many people today remember only the race. Yet the ship was full of scientists. There were geologists, who would study the rocks and the glaciers. There was a biologist, to study the strange creatures in the frozen sea. There was a physicist, and a man who studied the weather. There was a doctor, an artist, and a photographer who would even make moving films. Together, they would spend more than two years in the south. They would measure the winds and the ice, the tides and the stars. They would collect rocks, fossils, birds and fish. Back home, all of this would fill many thick books of science. Some of their weather records are still used by scientists today. So the voyage had two goals at once. One was a long walk to a single point on the map. The other was simply to understand a whole unknown land, a little better than before.

[long-pause]

{img:08-young-men-crew-deck-1910-sun}

Out of eight thousand eager letters, only a few dozen men were chosen.

[pause]

There were about sixty-five men on board the Terra Nova. They were sailors and scientists, officers and stokers. Most of them were young, many still in their twenties. They came from all over Britain, and also from Australia and Canada. One of them, a young ski expert, came from Norway. Some were navy men, used to the hard life on a ship. Others had never been to sea in their lives. Scott had chosen them very carefully from all those thousands of letters. He looked for men who were strong and cheerful. He also wanted men who could live together in a small space without quarrelling. That was perhaps the hardest skill of all. You could be a brilliant scientist and still be awful company in a cramped hut. These men were proud to have been picked. Many wrote home that they felt like the luckiest people alive. They were about to find out what that luck would mean.

[long-pause]

{img:09-wardroom-supper-swinging-lamps-ship}

The coast of Wales has slipped away now. Ahead lies the wide Atlantic, and weeks of open sea.

[pause]

On their first night at sea, the officers raised a glass to the Pole.

[pause]

That first evening, the officers gathered below deck in the wardroom. This was a small cabin with a table, benches and a few lamps. The ship rolled gently, and the lamps swung on their chains. Through the round portholes, the last light faded over the sea. Someone opened a bottle of wine. Someone else stood up and proposed a toast. It was to the expedition, and to the Pole. Ahead of them lay a journey across more than half the world. They would cross the Atlantic, round the southern tip of Africa, and sail on to Australia and New Zealand. Only then would they turn south into the ice. It would take them more than six months just to get there. Would you have the patience for such a long voyage? These men did not seem to mind. For most of them, this was the adventure they had dreamed of since they were boys.

[long-pause]

{img:10-sailing-ship-tropical-sea-flying-fish}

Before they reached the ice, the men had to sail through the heat of the tropics.

[pause]

For weeks, the Terra Nova sailed south through the Atlantic. The weather grew warm, and then very hot. The men took off their heavy woollen clothes. They worked on deck in their shirtsleeves, and their faces turned brown in the sun. Flying fish skimmed across the waves beside the ship. Sometimes one landed on deck with a wet slap, and a sailor would toss it back. In the evenings, the sea glowed with tiny lights in the wake of the ship. These were small sea creatures that shine when the water moves. Above, the stars were bright, and strange new ones rose in the south. Some of the men had never seen the Southern Cross before. They stood at the rail and looked up at it for a long time. It was a gentle beginning to a journey that would end in the hardest cold on Earth.

[long-pause]

{img:11-stokers-shovelling-coal-furnace-glow}

Scientists and officers took turns shovelling coal in the hot belly of the ship.

[pause]

The Terra Nova was short of sailors. So everybody had to help, whatever their job on land. Scientists who had studied at great universities pulled on the ropes. Officers scrubbed the decks with brushes and buckets. And they all took turns down in the stokehold. This was the room deep in the ship where the furnaces burned. There, the men shovelled coal into the fires to keep the engine running. The heat was so great that they worked stripped to the waist. They came up black with coal dust from head to toe. Yet nobody seemed to mind. Many said later it was one of the happiest times of the whole expedition. Hard work shared in a hot, dark room is a quick way to make friends. Men who had stood side by side at the furnace would stay close for the rest of their lives. Some of those friendships would matter a great deal, much later, on the ice.

[long-pause]

{img:12-quiet-man-painting-watercolour-deck}

The chief scientist was a gentle doctor whom everyone called Uncle Bill.

[pause]

His real name was Edward Wilson. He was thirty-eight years old, slim and quiet, with a kind face and a soft voice. He was a doctor, and also a fine scientist who knew all about birds. Yet perhaps his greatest gift was painting. Wilson spent hours on deck with his sketchbook and a little box of watercolours. He painted the sea, the sky, the clouds and the birds that followed the ship. His pictures were calm and delicate, and full of light. Wilson was also Scott's closest friend. The two of them had been together on the earlier expedition. Scott trusted him more than anyone else alive. When men had a worry, they often went to Wilson first. He would listen, say very little, and somehow make them feel better. Every group of people needs someone like that. On the Terra Nova, that person was Uncle Bill.

[long-pause]

{img:13-short-red-haired-officer-ship-stores}

A short, red-haired officer once fell nineteen feet into the hold and climbed out smiling.

[pause]

His name was Henry Bowers, and he was twenty-seven years old. He was short and stocky, with bright red hair and a very large nose. Because of the nose, everyone called him Birdie. He came from the Royal Indian Marine, and he was extraordinarily strong. He hardly ever seemed to feel the cold. Scott put him in charge of the stores. Soon, Birdie knew exactly where every box and every sack on the ship was kept. Not long after he first came aboard, he stepped through an open hatch by mistake. He fell nineteen feet, down into the hold, and landed on a pile of iron bars. The men rushed to help him. But Birdie climbed back up the ladder, quite unhurt, and said it was nothing. Then he went straight back to work. That was Birdie all over. He was cheerful, tireless and almost impossible to stop.

[long-pause]

{img:14-tall-army-officer-pipe-reading-corner}

One officer paid a large sum of money just to be allowed to come along.

[pause]

Lawrence Oates was a tall, quiet army officer from a cavalry regiment. He was thirty years old, with a long weathered face. He walked with a slight limp, from an old wound he had received in the war in South Africa. Oates came from a wealthy family. He gave a large sum of money to the expedition, and asked to join it. His job would be to look after the ponies. The men called him Titus, or sometimes simply the Soldier. He did not say very much. He was often rather gloomy about how things would turn out. Yet when he did speak, he was often very funny. He liked to sit quietly in a corner, smoking his pipe. His favourite reading was a book about Napoleon. Do you know people like that? They seem grumpy at first. Then, slowly, you discover they are some of the kindest people you have ever met.

[long-pause]

{img:15-young-man-round-spectacles-ship-rail}

The youngest officer later wrote a famous book called The Worst Journey in the World.

[pause]

Apsley Cherry-Garrard was only twenty-four. Everyone called him Cherry. He had just finished university at Oxford. He had no special training in science, and he was very short-sighted. He wore round spectacles that were always slipping down his nose. Yet he was eager, kind and willing to help with anything. Wilson knew his family, and had recommended him to Scott. He became an assistant zoologist, and a helper wherever he was needed. Cherry was not the strongest man on board, or the cleverest. But he noticed things, and he remembered them. Years later, he wrote a book about the expedition. He called it The Worst Journey in the World. It became one of the most famous books of travel ever written. Much of what we know about daily life on this expedition comes from Cherry. So in a way, we are seeing it all tonight through his round spectacles.

[long-pause]

{img:16-lyttelton-harbour-green-hills-1910}

In a little New Zealand harbour, the ship took on ponies, dogs and the last fresh news from home.

[pause]

The Terra Nova called at South Africa, and then at Australia. In October, she reached New Zealand. In the small harbour town of Lyttelton, among green hills, she was unloaded completely. Her hull was cleaned and repaired. Then she was loaded again, even more heavily than before. The ponies and the dogs came aboard. They were shut into stalls and kennels built right on the deck. The townspeople were very kind to the expedition. They invited the men into their homes, and gave them dinners and dances. The men wrote their last letters, and posted them. For a long time, there would be no more post. Then, on the twenty-ninth of November 1910, the ship left New Zealand and turned south. Crowds waved again from the shore. It was the last friendly harbour any of them would see for more than a year.

[long-pause]

{img:17-men-bailing-buckets-lantern-night-ship}

In a great storm, the men saved their ship by passing buckets from hand to hand all night, singing as they worked.

[pause]

Almost at once after leaving New Zealand, a great storm struck. The ship was very heavily loaded, and she rolled and pitched in huge waves. Water poured across the decks. Then the pumps became blocked with coal dust and oil. Water began to rise in the engine room. So the men formed a long line. They passed buckets from hand to hand, all through the night, emptying the water out. Officers and sailors and scientists all worked together. And to keep their spirits up, they sang. Old sea songs and music hall tunes rang out over the noise of the storm. After two days, the wind dropped and the sea grew calm again. Everyone was exhausted and soaking wet. Two ponies and a dog had been lost. Yet the ship and every man aboard were safe. They dried their clothes, ate a hot meal, and sailed on toward the ice.

[long-pause]

{img:18-first-iceberg-grey-sea-ship}

The sea is calm again, and the air is growing colder. Somewhere ahead, beyond the grey horizon, the ice is waiting.

[pause]

The first iceberg they saw was as big as a whole town.

[pause]

About a week after the storm, someone on deck called out. Far ahead, there was something white on the sea. As they came closer, they saw it was an iceberg. It was a great flat-topped block of ice, as big as a town. It floated silently on the grey water, with the waves breaking softly against its foot. The men lined the rail to stare at it. Some had never seen ice bigger than a puddle in winter. Then there was another iceberg, and another. Some were flat like tables. Others had been carved by wind and sea into towers and arches. Their sides glowed a pale and lovely blue. The air grew colder each day. The sea turned dark. And then, one morning, the ship reached the edge of the pack ice. Would you have felt excited, or a little afraid? Most of the men said they felt both at once.

[long-pause]

{img:19-ship-in-pack-ice-midnight-sun}

On Christmas Day 1910, the ship was frozen fast in the ice, and the sun shone all night.

[pause]

The pack ice was a vast field of floating ice, broken into pieces of every size. Some pieces were no bigger than a table. Others were as large as a field. They drifted and ground together on the dark water. The Terra Nova pushed her way in slowly. She rammed a floe with her strong oak bow, backed off, and rammed again. Sometimes she was stuck for days. On Christmas Day, she was held fast in the ice. So the men had their Christmas dinner there, in the wardroom. There was roast mutton and plum pudding. They sang songs until late at night. When they went up on deck, it was still broad daylight. In the far south, in summer, the sun never sets. It just circles low around the sky. Imagine finishing Christmas dinner at midnight, and stepping out into sunshine. That was their first taste of the strange days of the south.

[long-pause]

{img:20-adelie-penguins-listening-ice-ship}

Curious penguins walked up to the ship, and the sailors sang to them.

[pause]

While the ship was stuck, visitors came across the ice. They were little Adélie penguins, black and white, about knee high. They had never seen a ship before, or a person. So they were not afraid at all. They waddled right up to the side of the Terra Nova to see what it was. The men leaned over the rail and laughed. Then someone began to sing to them. The penguins stood still and listened, with their heads on one side. When the song was over, some of them wandered off. Others stayed for an encore. One of the men tried playing a tune on a banjo. Another tried a mouth organ. The penguins seemed to have opinions about the music. Some walked away at once, and some came closer. It was a happy, silly hour in the middle of the ice. Do you think the penguins enjoyed it? Perhaps they were just very polite.

[long-pause]

{img:21-mount-erebus-smoking-volcano-sea}

The first thing the men saw of Antarctica was a volcano breathing steam.

[pause]

At the beginning of January 1911, the ship broke out of the pack ice at last. She sailed into the calm, open water of the Ross Sea. And there, ahead of them, rose a mountain. It was called Mount Erebus. It was more than twelve thousand feet high and covered in snow and ice. Yet at its very top was a dark crater. From it, a thin plume of steam drifted lazily away on the wind. It was a living volcano, in the middle of the frozen world. Below it, the land ran down in long white slopes to the sea. There were black rocks and blue-green glaciers. The air was so clear that mountains a hundred miles away looked close enough to walk to. That clear air played tricks on everyone. More than once, a man set out for a hill that seemed close by. Hours later, he would find it was still far away.

[long-pause]

{img:22-ross-island-coast-cape-black-rocks}

Scott chose a cape of black sand and named it after his second in command.

[pause]

Scott searched the coast for a good place to build a hut. He found a low cape of black volcanic rock and sand, sticking out from the shore below Mount Erebus. It was sheltered from the worst of the winds. It was close to the sea ice, which would be the road south in spring. And there were penguins and seals nearby, for fresh meat. He named it Cape Evans, after his second in command, Lieutenant Teddy Evans. On the fourth of January, the ship tied up to the edge of the ice near the cape. The men looked at their new home. It was bare and black and white, with no plant bigger than a patch of moss. Yet it had a wild and lonely beauty. Behind the cape, the great volcano rose up into the sky. In front of it, the frozen bay stretched away to distant blue mountains. This would be their home for the next two years.

[long-pause]

{img:23-ponies-pulling-sledges-sea-ice-ship}

The anchor ropes are tied fast to the ice. Now the long work of making a home begins.

[pause]

Unloading the ship took days and nights without a break, in endless sunshine.

[pause]

There was an enormous amount to unload. There were materials for the hut, and food and fuel for two years. There were tons of coal, and boxes of scientific instruments. There were sledges, tents and clothes, and of course the animals. Everything had to be carried across the sea ice to the shore. That was about a mile and a half. The ponies pulled sledges piled with boxes. The dogs pulled smaller loads. The men dragged the rest themselves. They worked in shifts, around the clock. Since the sun never set, there was no real night to stop them. One of the motor sledges was lost on the very first day. As it was lowered from the ship, it broke through the ice. It sank into the dark water and was gone. The men stood and looked at the hole for a moment. Then they turned back to work. There was too much to do to stay sad for long.

[long-pause]

{img:24-wooden-hut-construction-cape-snow}

The hut came from England in numbered pieces, like a giant puzzle.

[pause]

While the ship was being unloaded, the carpenters built the hut. It had been made in England, then taken apart. Each piece was numbered, so it could be fitted together again, like a giant puzzle. The hut was about fifty feet long and twenty-five feet wide. It had a sloping roof and small windows. Its walls were double. Between them, the builders packed a layer of dried seaweed, sewn into quilts. That seaweed kept the warmth in and the cold out, surprisingly well. The floor had layers of felt and wood to keep out the chill of the ground. In less than two weeks, the hut was finished. Around it, the men built stables for the ponies out of boxes and bales of hay. They made kennels for the dogs, and little shelters for the instruments. By the middle of January, they had a home. And remarkably, that same wooden hut is still standing today.

[long-pause]

{img:25-hut-cape-evans-erebus-behind-sunlight}

The only sounds at the hut on calm days were the sea, the birds and a distant dog.

[pause]

The hut stood on a small rise of black sand near the edge of the sea. To the north and west, it looked out over the frozen bay. Beyond lay open water and distant mountains, white and blue and pink in the long summer light. Behind the hut, the slopes of Erebus climbed up toward the smoking summit. It was a beautiful place, wild and lonely and very quiet. On calm days, you could hear the sea lapping softly against the ice. You could hear the cries of the big brown skuas wheeling overhead. Now and then, a dog barked, and another answered. Then the silence came back. The men often said it was the quietest place they had ever known. Some found the silence strange at first. Most came to love it. Can you hear it now, in your mind? Just the sea, a bird, and the soft creak of a wooden door.

[long-pause]

{img:26-scientist-cutting-hole-sea-ice-net}

The scientists fished through holes in the ice and sent balloons into the sky.

[pause]

The men soon settled into a daily routine. In the mornings, the scientists went out to work. The geologists climbed the hills to study the rocks and the glaciers. The biologist cut holes in the sea ice. Through them, he lowered nets into the dark water below. Up came strange little creatures that no one had ever studied before. The weather man read his instruments in a small shelter outside the hut. He also sent up small balloons to measure the winds high above. Each balloon carried a tiny instrument, and the men watched it rise until it was a speck. Meanwhile, the sailors looked after the stores, the animals and the hut. Everyone was busy preparing for the journeys still to come. In the evenings, they compared notes about what they had found. It felt a little like a busy village. It just happened to be a village at the bottom of the world.

[long-pause]

{img:27-photographer-heavy-camera-tripod-ice}

The expedition's photographer called himself a camera artist.

[pause]

His name was Herbert Ponting. He was tall and dignified, about forty years old. He had travelled all over the world with his camera. He had taken famous pictures in Japan, India and China. Ponting brought the best cameras that money could buy. He also brought a film camera for making moving pictures. In the hut, he built himself a small darkroom. Every day, in all weathers, he went out with his heavy wooden cameras and his tripod. He photographed the ice and the mountains, the penguins and the seals. He photographed the men at work and the ship in the bay. He was very patient. He would wait for hours for exactly the right light. The other men teased him about it gently. They even made up a word for posing for his pictures. Yet his photographs became some of the most beautiful ever taken. Many of them can still be seen in museums today.

[long-pause]

{img:28-killer-whales-ice-edge-calm-water}

Killer whales once tried to tip the photographer off the ice.

[pause]

One day, Ponting was out on the sea ice near the edge of the water. He was setting up his camera. Suddenly, a group of killer whales appeared, swimming along the edge of the ice. They were big and black and white, and very clever. They dived under the ice where Ponting stood. Then they came up underneath it, all together, and broke it into pieces with their backs. Perhaps they thought he was a seal. Ponting jumped from one floating piece of ice to another, very fast. He reached solid ice just in time. Behind him, the whales lifted their heads out of the water. They looked at him for a moment, quite calmly. Then they swam away. Ponting was safe. And being a true photographer, he was very sorry about one thing. He had not had time to take their picture. The men laughed about that for weeks.

[long-pause]

{img:29-men-sitting-outside-hut-golden-midnight}

Late in the evening, the men sat outside on boxes and watched the icebergs turn pink.

[pause]

After the day's work, the men often sat outside the hut for a while. They sat on boxes and on the black sand, smoking their pipes. They looked out over the water and said very little. The light at that time of year was wonderful. Even late at night, the sun hung low in the south. Its long golden rays fell across the ice. They turned the icebergs pink and the shadows blue. The sea was calm and dark. The mountains lay upside down in it, perfectly still. Somewhere along the shore, a penguin called. A seal came up in a pool of open water. It blew out a small cloud of breath, and slipped under again. Then everything was quiet once more. Many of the men later said that these were among the happiest hours of their lives. No telegrams, no bills, no noise. Only the light, the sea and good company.

[long-pause]

{img:30-ship-disappearing-horizon-ice-evening}

When the ship sailed away, twenty-five men were left alone on the edge of the continent.

[pause]

In late January, the Terra Nova sailed away along the coast. She had other work to do. She would land a smaller party of men further along the coast. Then she would go back to New Zealand for the winter, before the sea froze. The men who stayed at Cape Evans stood on the shore and watched her go. They watched until her smoke disappeared over the horizon. There were twenty-five of them now, alone on the edge of the frozen continent. They had their hut, their animals and their stores. They would not see the ship again for more than a year. There was no radio, no telephone and no post. Whatever happened now, they would have to manage by themselves. Would you have felt lonely, watching that smoke disappear? Some of them did, just for a moment. Then they turned back to the hut, and got on with making it home.

[long-pause]

{img:31-hut-interior-wall-of-packing-cases}

The ship's smoke has gone from the sky. So let's open the wooden door, stamp the snow from our boots, and step inside.

[pause]

The hut was divided in two by a wall made of packing cases.

[pause]

Inside the hut at Cape Evans, everything had its place. Down the middle ran a wall made of wooden packing cases. They were stacked up from the floor almost to the ceiling. Many of them were still full of food. So when the cook needed a tin of jam, he might take it straight out of the wall. On one side of this wall lived the officers and the scientists. On the other side lived the sailors, the petty officers and the cook. That was how things were done in the navy at the time, and nobody questioned it. Yet the wall was not really a barrier. Sound travelled easily over the top. Jokes and songs passed back and forth all day. And when there was hard work to do outside, everyone did it together. Inside, the wall just gave each group a little bit of privacy. In a hut full of men, that was a precious thing.

[long-pause]

{img:32-wardroom-long-table-acetylene-lamps}

Scott's little corner held a bunk, a shelf of books and photographs of his wife and son.

[pause]

The officers' side was called the wardroom, after the officers' room on a ship. In the middle stood a long wooden table. Everyone ate their meals there together. Above it hung lamps that burned a gas called acetylene. They gave a bright, steady white light, which was a great comfort in the dark months. Along the walls were bunks, shelves of books and small cubicles. Curtains and more packing cases separated them. In one corner, Scott had his own small cubicle. It held a bunk, a little table and a shelf of books. On the wall were photographs of Kathleen and baby Peter. Scott spent many hours there, writing in his journal and making plans. When he wanted to think, he drew the curtain. The other men learned to keep their voices down then. Even a leader needs a small quiet place, now and then, to be alone with his thoughts.

[long-pause]

{img:33-crowded-bunks-saddles-clothes-hut}

The untidiest corner of the hut was nicknamed the Tenements.

[pause]

Next to Scott's cubicle, Wilson and Teddy Evans shared a small space. Beyond them was a corner with five bunks squeezed together. The others called it the Tenements, after the crowded houses of poor city streets. Cherry, Bowers and Oates slept there, with two others. It was by far the messiest part of the hut. Oates kept his horse saddles there. Bowers piled up clothes and equipment almost to the ceiling. Boots and socks hung everywhere to dry. And yet the men who lived there were very happy. They talked late into the night, and teased each other without mercy. Oates, so quiet in company, became quite chatty in the Tenements. He told stories about hunting and horses and his family home. Have you ever shared a small room with good friends? It is never tidy. But it is often where the best conversations happen.

[long-pause]

{img:34-hut-kitchen-stove-cook-bread}

At the end of the hut, a young cook baked fresh bread every single day.

[pause]

At the far end of the hut was the kitchen. It belonged to the cook, Thomas Clissold. He was a young man who had once cooked in a hotel, and he was very good at his job. He had a big iron stove that burned coal, and sometimes seal blubber. Every day, he baked fresh bread. He also made cakes, puddings and pies. Clissold was clever with his hands, too. He built a little machine that told him when his bread had risen enough. It rang a bell so he could put the dough in the oven at the right moment. The smell of baking bread drifted through the whole hut each morning. Imagine waking in the dark, in the coldest place on Earth, to the smell of warm bread. It must have felt like home. The men said Clissold's cooking was one of the best things about the whole expedition.

[long-pause]

{img:35-hut-dinner-table-tins-jam-tea}

The men said penguin tasted like beef that had been soaked in fish.

[pause]

The food in the hut was good, and there was plenty of it. There was tinned meat and fish, and tinned vegetables and fruit. There were jams and pickles, and great piles of biscuits. There was butter, cheese, cocoa and tea. And there was fresh meat from the animals nearby. Seal meat was eaten often, fried or stewed. Seal liver was a breakfast favourite. Penguin was eaten too, now and then. The men said it tasted rather like beef that had been soaked in fish. Not everyone liked it. On Sundays, there was something special. Perhaps it was roast mutton from a frozen sheep brought from New Zealand. After that, there might be a jam roly-poly pudding. The fresh meat was important, though the men did not fully know why. It helped keep away a sickness called scurvy, which had troubled earlier expeditions. Good food, it turned out, was one of their best defences.

[long-pause]

{img:36-pianola-gramophone-corner-hut-lamplight}

In the coldest place on Earth, there was a piano that could play itself.

[pause]

In one corner of the wardroom stood a pianola. This was a piano that could play itself if you pumped its pedals. The music came from long rolls of paper, punched with tiny holes. There was also a gramophone, with a big horn and a large collection of records. In the evenings, the men took turns choosing the music. There were songs from the music halls and popular tunes of the day. There were operas, and there were quiet classical pieces. Some of the gentler men liked to sit in the lamplight with their eyes closed, just listening. There were books too, hundreds of them. There were novels and histories, poems and travel books. Many men read for hours, curled up in their bunks with a candle. Isn't it strange to imagine? Outside, a howling white wilderness. Inside, a waltz on the gramophone, and a man turning the pages of a novel.

[long-pause]

{img:37-darkroom-red-light-photographs-drying}

The warmest room in the hut was the photographer's darkroom.

[pause]

Ponting had a small room at the side of the hut. It was his darkroom, and also his bedroom. It was the warmest room in the whole hut, and it smelled of chemicals. Ponting developed his photographs there in trays of liquid, under a dim red light. Then he hung them up to dry, like washing on a line. The other men liked to squeeze in to see the new pictures appear. Sometimes in the evenings, Ponting gave talks about his travels. He showed his photographs on a sheet, using a magic lantern. This was an early kind of projector, with a bright lamp and glass slides. There were pictures of Japan, with temples and cherry blossom. There were pictures of India and of Burma. In the middle of the polar winter, the men sat in the dark and looked at sunny gardens far away. For an hour, they were travelling somewhere warm.

[long-pause]

{img:38-scientists-laboratory-corner-microscope}

Little pens in the scientists' corner wrote down the wind, day and night.

[pause]

The scientists had their own corner of the hut. There were workbenches and shelves full of instruments, bottles and boxes. There was a microscope and a set of scales. There were rocks and fossils in trays. There were glass jars of tiny sea creatures, kept in spirit. Charts hung on the walls, and notebooks lay in piles. The weather man was George Simpson. Everyone called him Sunny Jim. He had wires running from instruments outside, through the wall, into his corner. There, little pens moved slowly across rolls of paper. They traced lines that showed the wind, the temperature and the pressure of the air. They worked day and night, even when everyone was asleep. Simpson checked them like a careful gardener checking his plants. His records from that winter were so good that scientists still study them. Even now, more than a century later, his little pens are still telling us things.

[long-pause]

{img:39-ponies-in-snow-stables-blankets}

Beyond the hut door, other voices could be heard. There were stamping hooves in the stables, and the sleepy grumble of dogs in the snow.

[pause]

The expedition's ponies came all the way from Manchuria and Siberia.

[pause]

The ponies had come from Manchuria and Siberia, in the far east of Asia. They were small and sturdy, with shaggy coats, mostly white or grey. They had short, strong legs, and they were used to hard winters and hard work. Scott hoped they could pull the heavy sledges across the snow. They would help at least for the first part of the long journey south. Out at Cape Evans, the ponies lived in stables built from boxes and bales of hay. There was a long row of stalls, joined onto the side of the hut. Each pony had a warm blanket over its back. They ate hay and oats. When they were bored, they chewed the wooden walls. On cold nights, the men could hear them shifting and snorting through the wall. It was a comforting sound. Have you ever stood in a warm stable on a winter night? It smells of hay and horses, and it feels safe.

[long-pause]

{img:40-cavalry-officer-brushing-pony-stable-lamp}

Oates grumbled about the ponies, yet sat up all night with the sick ones.

[pause]

Captain Oates looked after the ponies. A young Russian groom named Anton Omelchenko helped him. Oates did not think much of the animals. He said they were the worst collection of crocks he had ever seen. He grumbled that they were old, weak and bad-tempered. Yet he cared for them with great devotion. He fed them and brushed them every day. He checked their hooves and talked to them quietly in the stables. When one of them fell ill, he sat up with it all night. He spent so many hours in the stables that the men joked he lived there. The stables became a kind of club. Some of the men would gather there with Oates after dinner. They sat on bales of hay and smoked their pipes. They talked about horses, and the war, and home. Oates was never happier than with a horse beside him.

[long-pause]

{img:41-white-pony-gentle-young-man-snow}

One pony was so wild it took four men to hold him, and one gentle pony was called Jimmy Pigg.

[pause]

Every pony had a name, and every pony had its own character. There was Christopher, who was wild and bad-tempered. It took four men to hold him down while he was harnessed. Then he would set off at a gallop, dragging his sledge behind him. There were others called Snatcher, Jehu, Nobby, Victor and Snippets. There was a gentle white pony called Jimmy Pigg. And there was one called Michael, who grew very fond of Cherry. Each man was given a pony to lead. Over the months, the men came to know their ponies as friends. They knew which ones liked a sugar lump, and which ones would try to bite. Bowers led Victor, and was very proud of him. It is easy to forget that these men were not only explorers. Many were simply young men who had grown up around animals, and who missed them.

[long-pause]

{img:42-siberian-sled-dogs-curled-snow}

The sled dogs slept curled up in the open snow, even in a blizzard.

[pause]

The dogs had come from Siberia too. They were chosen by a man named Cecil Meares, who had travelled widely in Russia and the East. There were more than thirty of them. They were big, thick-coated dogs, grey and black and white, with sharp ears and curling tails. A young Russian named Dimitri Gerof helped to care for them. He was quiet and gentle, and wonderfully skilful with the dogs. When they were harnessed to a sledge, they leapt and barked with excitement. Dimitri had to hold them back until it was time to go. On the snow, they could run for hours, pulling heavy loads. And at night, they slept in the open. They curled up with their noses tucked under their bushy tails. Sometimes the snow drifted right over them. In the morning, a dog would shake itself out of the drift, warm and ready to run again.

[long-pause]

{img:43-adelie-penguins-curious-ice-edge}

The little Adélie penguins always looked surprised, because of the white rings around their eyes.

[pause]

Antarctica seems empty and lifeless on land. Yet at the edge of the sea, it is full of life. Near the hut lived the little Adélie penguins. They were about knee high and black and white. Each one had a white ring around its eye, which made it look permanently surprised. They were curious, busy and very funny. They walked in long lines across the ice. They slid down slopes on their bellies. They dived into the sea and shot out again onto the ice, like corks from a bottle. In spring, they built nests of small stones. And the men often saw a penguin stealing stones from its neighbour's nest when nobody was looking. The neighbour would then steal them back. Wilson watched them for hours and wrote down everything they did. Would you have been able to watch them without laughing? The men certainly could not.

[long-pause]

{img:44-weddell-seal-sleeping-sea-ice-sun}

At night, the men could hear seals singing under the frozen sea.

[pause]

There were seals too. They lay on the ice like great grey sausages, sleeping in the sun. These were Weddell seals, which live under the sea ice all year round. They make breathing holes in the ice by scraping it with their teeth. Then they come up through those holes to rest on the surface. When the men walked past, a seal would open one large dark eye. It would look at them sleepily, and then go back to sleep. Nothing seemed to worry them. And at night, sometimes, the men heard them singing. Strange calls came up through the frozen floor of the sea. They sounded like whistles, and bells, and falling water. Some men lay awake in their bunks just to listen. Can you imagine it? A deep, echoing song rising up from beneath the ice, in the stillness of the polar night. It was one of the loveliest sounds they ever heard.

[long-pause]

{img:45-snow-petrels-white-birds-over-ice}

Snow petrels flew over the ice like small white ghosts.

[pause]

Birds came and went around the hut. The most common were the skuas. They were big brown birds, rather like gulls. They flew around the hut and stole anything they could find to eat. A man who put his sandwich down for a moment might find it gone. Then there were the snow petrels. These were pure white birds, small and graceful. They flew low over the ice like little ghosts, and they were very hard to see against the snow. Far out at sea, there were albatrosses. They glided on the wind for hours without once flapping their long wings. There were whales in the open water, too, blowing tall plumes of spray. Wilson painted many of these birds and animals. His watercolours are soft and careful and full of love. You can tell from them how much he enjoyed simply looking at the living world around him.

[long-pause]

{img:46-emperor-penguin-tall-golden-neck-ice}

Wilson believed the largest penguin of all laid its eggs in the middle of winter.

[pause]

Wilson had studied birds all his life. He knew more about Antarctic penguins than almost anyone. He was especially interested in the largest penguin of all. That was the emperor penguin, nearly four feet tall, with a soft golden patch on each side of its neck. Emperor penguins live in big colonies on the sea ice, far from Cape Evans. Very little was known about them. On his earlier expedition, Wilson had found a few of their chicks. From their size, he worked out something surprising. The eggs must be laid in the middle of the dark winter, the coldest time of the whole year. No other bird was known to do anything like it. Wilson also had a theory. He thought the emperor might be one of the most ancient birds on Earth. Its eggs might tell scientists how birds first came to be. To find out, someone would have to fetch an egg in winter.

[long-pause]

{img:47-depot-flag-cairn-snow-plain-evening}

The summer is ending now. Each evening the sun dips a little lower, and the long darkness is on its way.

[pause]

Before winter came, the men hid a ton of food on the ice, under a black flag.

[pause]

At the end of January 1911, Scott led a party south across the ice. They took ponies and dogs. Their job was to lay depots along the route to the Pole. A depot was a store of food and fuel, left in the snow for later. In spring, the travellers would find these stores along the way. Then they would not have to carry everything from the start. The weather was bad. The ponies sank deep into soft snow, and grew thin and weak. Scott did not want to push them too hard. So they laid the largest depot about thirty miles short of where they had planned. They marked it with a tall cairn of snow and a black flag. That way, they could find it again on the vast white plain. They called it One Ton Depot, because about a ton of food and fuel was stored there. Later, those thirty miles would matter a great deal.

[long-pause]

{img:48-last-sunset-before-winter-ice-hut}

On the twenty-third of April, the men watched the sun set and knew it would not rise for four months.

[pause]

By the end of April, everyone was back at the hut. The sun was very low in the sky now. Each day it rose a little later and set a little earlier. The temperature fell lower and lower. The sea froze over, and the ice grew thick enough to walk on. The penguins had gone north to the open sea. Most of the birds had gone too. Then, on the twenty-third of April, the men stood outside the hut together. They watched the sun sink slowly behind the mountains, in a blaze of red and gold. And then it was gone. It would not rise again for four months. Nobody said very much. A few men took off their caps, as if saying goodbye to an old friend. Then they turned and went back inside. The stove was lit, the lamps were bright, and the long winter had begun.

[long-pause]

{img:49-aurora-australis-green-curtains-hut}

The polar night was not always black, because the southern lights danced for hours.

[pause]

For four months, from late April to late August, the sun did not rise at all. At midday, there was a little grey twilight along the northern sky. It lasted an hour or two and then faded away. The rest of the time, it was dark. Yet the darkness was not always black. When the moon was up, it circled round the sky for days on end. It lit the whole frozen land in silver. The snow glowed, and the mountains stood out sharp and pale. And on some nights, the sky filled with the southern lights. Great curtains of pale green, white and rose rippled across the stars. They folded and faded, silently, for hours. The men came out to watch, wrapped in their heaviest clothes. They stood in the snow with their heads tilted back, saying nothing. Only the cold drove them back inside. Have you ever seen the northern lights? These were their southern sisters.

[long-pause]

{img:50-hut-breakfast-porridge-morning-lamps}

Even in endless darkness, the hut ran on a steady daily clock.

[pause]

Inside the hut, life kept a steady rhythm. At eight in the morning, the night watchman woke the cook. The cook lit the stove and made breakfast. At half past eight, everyone got up. There was a scramble for the washbasins and a lot of grumbling about cold water. Breakfast was porridge, seal liver, bread and butter, marmalade, and tea or cocoa. After breakfast, everyone went to work. The scientists wrote up their notes and studied their specimens. The sailors repaired sledges and harnesses, and sewed tents. They packed food into bags for the spring journeys. Oates and Anton looked after the ponies. Meares and Dimitri looked after the dogs. Without a sunrise, it would have been easy to lose track of the days. A fixed routine kept everyone steady. It gave each day a beginning, a middle and an end, even when the sky outside never changed.

[long-pause]

{img:51-men-leading-ponies-moonlight-sea-ice}

In a blizzard, the men tied themselves to a rope just to walk a few yards from the door.

[pause]

Every day, if the weather allowed, the men went outside for exercise. They walked across the frozen bay, carrying lanterns. They climbed the slopes behind the hut. They led the ponies in a long line along the shore, in the moonlight. The ponies' breath steamed in the cold air like little clouds. But sometimes a blizzard blew up. Then the wind roared across the cape so loudly that the men inside had to shout. The snow whirled so thickly that a man could not see his own hand. No one went out then, except to read the instruments. Even that was dangerous. So the men stretched a rope from the hut to the instrument shelter. They held on to it all the way there and back. Without it, they could have lost their way only a few yards from the door. Inside again, they stamped off the snow and warmed their hands at the stove.

[long-pause]

{img:52-men-reading-playing-chess-hut-evening}

Three evenings a week, someone gave a lecture, from penguins to Japan to looking after horses.

[pause]

The evenings were the best part of the day. After dinner, the men sat around the long table or in their bunks. They read, wrote their journals, or played chess or cards. Someone put a record on the gramophone. Someone mended a sock. And three evenings a week, one of them gave a lecture. Wilson talked about the birds and animals of the Antarctic, and showed his paintings. Ponting showed his photographs of Japan. Simpson explained the weather. The geologists talked about rocks and glaciers and volcanoes. Even Oates gave a talk, about how to look after horses. Everyone agreed it was very good. And sometimes Scott talked about his plans for the journey to the Pole. On those evenings, the men listened very closely indeed. It was a little like a school, and a little like a club. Which of those talks would you have chosen to hear?

[long-pause]

{img:53-handmade-newspaper-sealskin-cover-table}

The hut had its own newspaper, with exactly one copy.

[pause]

There was even a newspaper in the hut. It was called the South Polar Times, and Cherry was its editor. The men wrote articles, stories and poems for it. Some pieces were funny and some were serious. Wilson drew pictures for it. It was typed out on the expedition's typewriter, and there was only one copy. That single copy was bound in a beautiful cover, made from packing case wood and sealskin. When a new issue was finished, Scott read it aloud to everyone in the evening. The writers sat and pretended not to be listening. Everyone else tried to guess who had written what. There was a lot of laughter, and some blushing. One copy, read aloud to everyone at once. Isn't that a lovely idea? These handmade newspapers survived the expedition. You can still see them today, in a museum, with their sealskin covers.

[long-pause]

{img:54-midwinter-feast-flags-table-celebration}

Midwinter Day was the Antarctic Christmas, with flags, candles and a tree made from ski sticks.

[pause]

Each Saturday evening, the men drank a toast to sweethearts and wives. Sailors had done the same for hundreds of years. Then the talk turned to home, to gardens and country lanes and favourite meals. In early June, Scott turned forty-three. The cook made a great birthday cake with candied fruit and chocolate. But the biggest day of all was the twenty-second of June. This was Midwinter Day, the middle of the long night. From then on, the sun would slowly start to come back. It was the Antarctic Christmas. The table was decorated with candles and the men's sledging flags. There was soup, roast beef, plum pudding and mince pies. There were toasts, speeches and songs. Then Bowers brought in a Christmas tree he had made himself. It was built from ski sticks, feathers and coloured paper. It was hung with little presents for everyone. The party went on late into the night.

[long-pause]

{img:55-three-men-hauling-sledge-dark-lantern}

The candles from the feast have burned down. Yet a few days later, three men will put on their warmest clothes and walk out into the dark.

[pause]

Three men walked out into the polar night to fetch a penguin's egg.

[pause]

On the twenty-seventh of June 1911, three men left the hut. They walked out into the dark, pulling two sledges behind them. They were Wilson, Bowers and Cherry. They were going to try to reach the emperor penguins at a place called Cape Crozier. It was about sixty miles away, across the ice. They would travel in the middle of winter, in almost complete darkness. No one had ever made a journey like it. Why go at all? Wilson hoped the eggs could help answer a great question in science. How did the first birds come to be, long ago? An emperor penguin chick growing inside its egg might hold a clue. And the only time to find those eggs was in winter, when the penguins were sitting on them. The men at the hut waved them off by lantern light. Then the three small figures disappeared into the darkness.

[long-pause]

{img:56-polar-night-snow-plain-stars-moonlight}

It was so cold that their breath froze in the air and fell tinkling onto their clothes.

[pause]

The journey was slow and very hard. They pulled the sledges across the frozen sea and the snow. In the dark, they could not see where they were going. They carried a candle lantern. They steered by the stars and the faint shapes of mountains in the moonlight. The cold was beyond anything they had ever known. One night, the thermometer fell to seventy-seven degrees below zero, Fahrenheit. That is about minus sixty Celsius. Their breath froze as it left their mouths. It fell in tiny crystals onto their clothes, with a faint tinkling sound. Their clothes froze as stiff as boards. Each morning, they had to bend them slowly, bit by bit, before they could get into them. Cherry's teeth even cracked in the cold. Yet they kept going, one slow step after another. It is hard to imagine such cold, lying here under a warm blanket. Perhaps that makes the blanket feel even warmer.

[long-pause]

{img:57-small-tent-lantern-glow-dark-snow}

Through all that cold and darkness, the three men never spoke a cross word to each other.

[pause]

Some days, the snow was so cold that the sledge runners would not slide. So the men took one sledge forward, then walked back for the other. They covered every mile three times. Some days, they moved only a mile or two. At night, they put up their tent and lit a little stove. They cooked a hot meal of pemmican, which is dried meat mixed with fat. They ate it with hard biscuits and drank hot tea. Then they climbed into frozen sleeping bags and lay shivering until morning. Cherry could not wear his spectacles, because they froze over at once. He stumbled along half-blind and never complained. And through it all, the three of them were always kind to one another. They said please and thank you. They never lost their tempers. Cherry later wrote that he never once heard a cross word between them. That, perhaps, was the real wonder of the journey.

[long-pause]

{img:58-stone-hut-rocks-ice-ridges-twilight}

At Cape Crozier, they built themselves a little house of stones and snow.

[pause]

After nineteen days, the three men reached Cape Crozier, at the far end of Ross Island. Here, the great ice shelf pressed up against the land. The ice was crumpled into huge ridges and deep cracks. On a rocky slope above the sea, the men built a little shelter. They piled up stones and packed the gaps with snow. They used a sledge as a roof beam and stretched canvas over the top. It was a tiny house, just big enough for three men lying down. But it was theirs. They lit their little stove inside and felt almost cosy. They called the place Oriana Hut, after Wilson's wife. From there, they could visit the penguins below the cliffs. Imagine building a house with your own hands, in the dark, at sixty degrees below zero. The remains of that stone shelter are still on that slope today. Visitors can see the low walls, still standing in the snow.

[long-pause]

{img:59-emperor-penguins-huddle-winter-twilight}

When they finally found the emperor penguins, the three men forgot the cold for a moment.

[pause]

In the faint twilight of midday, the three men climbed down through the ice ridges. It took a long time and was very hard work. Then, at last, they reached the sea ice below the great cliffs. And there they were, the emperor penguins. There were about a hundred of them, standing close together in a huddle. They were tall and dignified, with black backs and white fronts. Each had a golden patch on the side of its neck. Some were holding eggs on top of their feet. The eggs were tucked under a warm fold of feathered skin, safe from the ice. The penguins made a strange trumpeting sound that echoed off the cliffs. The three men stood and watched them in the dim light. For a moment, they forgot how cold they were. Wilson had waited years for this. He had come further than any bird lover had ever come, just to stand here.

[long-pause]

{img:60-three-tired-men-arriving-hut-dawn}

Three eggs came home safely, and they are still kept in a museum in London.

[pause]

The men took five eggs and carried them carefully back up through the ice. Two broke on the way, and three survived. That night, a terrible storm struck. The wind tore the canvas roof from their stone shelter. It blew their tent away into the dark. For two days, they lay in their sleeping bags with snow pouring in. They sang hymns and songs to keep their spirits up. When the storm ended, Bowers went searching. To everyone's joy, he found the tent lying among the rocks, about half a mile away, undamaged. On the first of August, after five weeks away, they reached the hut again. Their clothes were so frozen that the others had to cut them off. Then the three travellers climbed into dry sleeping bags and slept and slept. The three eggs went home to England. Today they lie in a quiet drawer at the Natural History Museum in London.

[long-pause]

{img:61-sun-returns-horizon-golden-ice}

The three travellers are asleep at last, warm and dry. And far to the north, a faint glow is growing in the sky.

[pause]

When the sun came back in August, some of the men took off their hats to it.

[pause]

In the last days of August 1911, the sun returned. For days before, the men had watched the northern sky. At midday, the glow grew brighter, from grey to pale yellow, then to orange and red. Then, on a clear cold morning, a tiny sliver of gold peeped over the mountains. It shone for a few minutes across the frozen sea, and then sank again. The men stood outside the hut and cheered. Some of them took off their hats to it, like greeting an old friend. Each day after that, the sun stayed a little longer. The light changed everything. The snow sparkled. The ice turned blue and green and gold. The mountains glowed pink in the morning and purple in the evening. The men felt their spirits rise. They went skiing on the sea ice and threw snowballs at each other, like boys on holiday. The long night was over.

[long-pause]

{img:62-men-studying-map-lamplit-hut-table}

The road to the Pole had three parts: a plain of ice, a glacier and a high frozen dome.

[pause]

All winter, Scott had worked on his plan. The Pole was about nine hundred miles from the hut. There and back would be about eighteen hundred miles, and would take about five months. Everything had to be carried on sledges, or left in depots along the way. The journey had three parts. First came the Barrier. This was a vast flat plain of ice floating on the sea, about as big as France. It was white and empty, and the wind swept across it without stopping. Next came the Beardmore Glacier. This was a huge river of ice, more than a hundred miles long. It flowed down from the mountains, full of cracks and ridges. Last came the plateau. This was a high dome of ice and snow, nearly two miles above the sea. The air there was thin and the cold bitter. It stretched for hundreds of miles, all the way to the Pole.

[long-pause]

{img:63-packing-ration-bags-hut-table}

Bowers spent whole days weighing food into little bags, one week at a time.

[pause]

The plan used the animals first. The motor sledges, the ponies and the dogs would carry the loads across the Barrier. At the foot of the glacier, the ponies would stop, and the dogs would soon turn back. After that, the men would pull the sledges themselves. They would work in teams of four, harnessed like horses. Along the way, small groups would turn back one by one. That way, the food would last for the final team, who would go on to the Pole. Nobody knew yet who would be in that team. Every man hoped it would be him. All through September and October, the hut was busy with preparations. Sledges were repaired and loaded. Tents were mended. Sleeping bags of reindeer skin were aired. Bowers weighed out the food into small cloth bags, a week's rations for each tent. He counted and labelled everything in his careful, tidy way. Nothing was left to chance.

[long-pause]

{img:64-scott-writing-journal-cubicle-candle}

A short telegram had told Scott that he was in a race.

[pause]

There was one more thing on Scott's mind. Back in Australia, on the way south, he had received a short telegram. It said simply: Beg to inform you Fram proceeding Antarctic. Amundsen. Roald Amundsen was a famous explorer from Norway. The Fram was his ship. At first, nobody quite knew what the message meant. Then, in February, the Terra Nova had sailed along the edge of the Barrier. In a bay far to the east, called the Bay of Whales, the crew saw another ship. It was the Fram. Amundsen had built a hut there on the ice, with a great many dogs. He too was going for the Pole. Scott wrote in his journal that he would not change his plans. He would carry on as if Amundsen were not there. If the Norwegians got to the Pole first, then so be it. But in the evenings, the men talked about it often. It was going to be a race.

[long-pause]

{img:65-norwegian-hut-framheim-barrier-snow}

The Norwegians spent the winter in a snug hut with a steam bath dug into the snow.

[pause]

About four hundred miles to the east, the Norwegians had also spent the winter in a hut. They called it Framheim, which means the home of the Fram. It was a small wooden house brought from Norway in pieces. It stood alone on the flat white ice, half buried in snow. There were nine men there, and more than a hundred dogs. Amundsen was thirty-nine, tall and lean, with a weathered face. As a young man in the Arctic, he had lived among the Inuit. From them, he had learned how to dress in furs, how to drive dogs and how to stay warm. Under the snow around the hut, his men dug a whole little village of rooms. There was a workshop, a store room and a room for packing sledges. There was even a little steam bath. The cook, Adolf Lindstrøm, made pancakes every Sunday. Two very different huts, on the same frozen shelf, both waiting for spring.

[long-pause]

{img:66-motor-sledges-departing-sea-ice-smoke}

The motor sledges set out first, chugging away across the ice in a cloud of smoke.

[pause]

By the end of October, everything was ready. On the twenty-fourth of October, the motor sledges set out first. Teddy Evans, William Lashly and two other men went with them. The machines chugged slowly away across the sea ice. They made a great noise and a cloud of smoke, pulling their loaded sledges behind them. The men at the hut waved and cheered. Everyone hoped the machines would work well. If they did, it would be the first time motor vehicles had ever been used in Antarctica. They were a little like the great-grandparents of every snow vehicle that came after them. Lashly was a quiet, steady sailor and a skilful engineer. He understood those engines better than anyone. If anyone could keep them running, it was Lashly. The rest of the party watched until the smoke was a small grey smudge on the white. In a week, it would be their turn to go.

[long-pause]

{img:67-dog-teams-crossing-barrier-sunny}

Across the ice, two groups of travellers are setting out toward the same point on the map. Let's walk a little way with each of them.

[pause]

The Norwegians travelled on skis, with fifty-two dogs pulling their sledges.

[pause]

On the nineteenth of October 1911, Amundsen left Framheim for the Pole. Four companions went with him. They had four sledges and fifty-two dogs. They travelled fast and light, on skis, with the dogs doing most of the pulling. Some days they covered fifteen or twenty miles. The autumn before, they had laid depots along their route. They marked them with flags and long lines of snow cairns, so they could not miss them. One of the men, Olav Bjaaland, was a champion skier. Another, Helmer Hanssen, was a famous dog driver from the far north of Norway. They found a new way up onto the plateau, a steep glacier through the mountains. Amundsen named it the Axel Heiberg Glacier, after one of his supporters. It was very hard work. Yet the dogs pulled the sledges all the way to the top. Then the Norwegians turned toward the Pole.

[long-pause]

{img:68-small-tent-flag-alone-snow-plain}

At the Pole, Amundsen left a small tent and a polite letter for Captain Scott.

[pause]

On the plateau, the weather was often cruel, with blizzards, fog and bitter cold. But the Norwegians kept going, day after day. Then, on the fourteenth of December 1911, in the afternoon, the drivers called out, and the sledges stopped. By their instruments, they had reached the South Pole. There was nothing there. Only flat white snow in every direction, under a pale blue sky. The five men planted the Norwegian flag together. They stayed three days, taking careful measurements to be sure. Before leaving, they put up a small tent with a flag on top. Inside, they left some spare equipment and a letter for the King of Norway. They also left a short note for Scott. It politely asked him to send the letter on, in case they did not get home themselves. Then they turned north. They reached Framheim safely in late January, all five of them well, to hot coffee and pancakes.

[long-pause]

{img:69-ponies-sledges-leaving-hut-spring}

Scott's party left with the ponies in a long, straggling line across the ice.

[pause]

On the first of November 1911, Scott and the main party left Cape Evans for the Pole. The ponies set out across the sea ice in a long line. Each one was led by a man and pulled a loaded sledge. Oates led Christopher, the wildest of them. It took four men to harness him. Then he set off at a gallop, with Oates hanging on. Cherry led Michael, and Bowers led Victor. Behind them came the dog teams, with Meares and Dimitri. The dogs were much faster and would soon catch up. The men who stayed behind stood on the shore and watched. Ponting filmed the departure with his moving picture camera, so people at home could see it one day. Then he went back inside to develop his photographs. The hut suddenly felt very quiet and very empty. Only a few men and the ticking stove were left.

[long-pause]

{img:70-night-march-midnight-sun-low-ponies}

Out on the Barrier, they marched at night, because the snow was firmer then.

[pause]

Out on the Barrier, the travelling fell into a routine. The men marched at night, when it was a little colder and the snow was firmer. They rested during the day, when the sun was high. It was always light now. The sun circled the sky without ever setting. Each evening, they woke in their tents and cooked breakfast on little stoves. Then they packed up, harnessed the ponies and set off. They marched ten or twelve miles. Then they stopped, put up the tents and built snow walls to shelter the ponies. They fed the animals, cooked supper, wrote their diaries and slept. The Barrier was strange and very beautiful. It was flat and white in every direction. There was no sound but the creak of sledges and the crunch of snow. Sometimes there were mirages. Distant mountains seemed to float above the horizon, upside down and shimmering, like a dream.

[long-pause]

{img:71-tents-snowed-in-glacier-foot-mountains}

A four-day blizzard trapped them at the very foot of the great glacier.

[pause]

On the way south, the party passed the motor sledges. Both had broken down and stood abandoned in the snow. Their engines were not made for such cold. Teddy Evans and Lashly had gone on ahead, pulling their sledges themselves. The main party reached One Ton Depot and stocked up with food. The ponies grew weaker as they went. The cold, the hard work and the poor food were too much for them. One by one, when they could go no further, they were put down quietly, as had always been planned. Oates was very sad. He had cared for them for so long. Then, in early December, a great blizzard struck at the foot of the Beardmore Glacier. For four days, the men lay in their tents while the wind roared outside. It was strangely warm, and the snow was wet. Everything was soaked. When it was over, they named the place Shambles Camp.

[long-pause]

{img:72-men-hauling-sledges-beardmore-glacier}

On Christmas Day, high on the glacier, Bowers pulled out a plum pudding he had carried secretly for weeks.

[pause]

On the eleventh of December, Meares and Dimitri turned back with the dogs. Twelve men were left, in three teams of four. Now they had to pull the sledges themselves. They put on their harnesses, leaned forward and pulled. Slowly, the heavy sledges began to move up the great glacier. Mountains rose on both sides, enormous and silent. Ahead, the ice climbed in long broken steps. The climb took about two weeks. Sometimes the snow was deep and soft. Sometimes the ice was hard and full of hidden cracks. Now and then, someone fell through a snow bridge, and his friends pulled him out on his harness. Yet the views were magnificent, and the sun often shone. On Christmas Day, they had a feast in the tents. There were biscuits, chocolate and raisins. And Bowers brought out a small plum pudding he had secretly carried all the way from the hut. It was Lashly's birthday too.

[long-pause]

{img:73-two-groups-parting-plateau-handshakes}

When the last support team turned home, Tom Crean was in tears.

[pause]

On the fourth of January 1912, the last support party turned back. They were about a hundred and fifty miles from the Pole. Scott had chosen who would go on with him. He chose Wilson, Oates and Petty Officer Edgar Evans. And at the last moment, he decided to take a fifth man, Birdie Bowers. The other three were Teddy Evans, Lashly and Tom Crean. They shook hands with the polar party and wished them luck. Crean was in tears. He was a tall, good-humoured Irishman, and he had dearly wanted to go on. Then the three of them turned north and began the long march home. That march would be very hard. Yet all three of them reached safety in the end. Scott and his four companions watched them grow smaller on the white plain. Soon they were three little dots, and then nothing at all. Then the five turned south, toward the Pole.

[long-pause]

{img:74-five-men-hauling-sledge-plateau-sun}

Now there are only five of them, and the sky feels even bigger. The world up here is made of snow, wind and a circling sun.

[pause]

Bowers walked the plateau on foot, keeping pace with four men on skis.

[pause]

The five men marched south across the high plateau, pulling their sledge behind them. They walked side by side in their harnesses, leaning forward against the weight. Scott was in the middle. Beside him walked Wilson, quiet and steady. On the other side were Edgar Evans and Oates. Evans was the big Welsh sailor, and Oates the tall cavalry officer. Bowers had no skis. He had left them behind with an earlier group, as he had been told. So he walked on foot beside the others. He took shorter, quicker steps to keep up. He did it for weeks without complaint. Bowers also had a job each day at noon. He measured the height of the sun with his instruments. From that, he worked out exactly where they were on the empty plateau. Each day, his figures showed them coming eleven or twelve miles nearer to the Pole.

[long-pause]

{img:75-sastrugi-snow-ridges-plateau-low-sun}

On the plateau, the wind carved the snow into hard ridges like frozen waves.

[pause]

The plateau was a strange and lonely world. It was nearly two miles above the sea. The air was thin, so the men were short of breath and tired easily. The snow was dry and gritty, like sand, and the sledge dragged over it. Sometimes the wind carved the surface into long hard ridges. These were called sastrugi. They ran across the path like frozen waves, and the sledge bumped and jolted over them. There was nothing to see in any direction. Only the snow, the sky and the sun, circling round and round above them. On clear days, the light was dazzling, and the men wore goggles to protect their eyes. On grey days, the snow and sky melted together into one soft whiteness. Then it was hard to tell where the ground ended and the sky began. It was a little like walking inside a cloud.

[long-pause]

{img:76-five-men-inside-small-tent-stove-cocoa}

Inside the tent each evening, with the stove roaring, the five men felt almost cosy.

[pause]

It was very cold on the plateau, even in summer. The temperature was often twenty or thirty degrees below zero. The men's beards were always full of ice. In the evening, they put up the tent and lit the little stove. Then it felt almost cosy. They sat close together on their sleeping bags. They ate pemmican and biscuits and drank hot cocoa. Each man had his own job. Bowers kept the weather records and the navigation. Wilson looked after everyone's health, and sketched whenever he could. Evans mended the sledge and the harness with his big, skilful hands. Oates looked after the food stores. Scott led the way and wrote the journal each evening, in pencil, in a small notebook. At night, they lay side by side in a row for warmth. They talked a little about home and the people they loved. Then, one by one, they fell asleep.

[long-pause]

{img:77-black-flag-distant-snow-plain}

Fifteen miles from the Pole, they saw a small black flag in the snow.

[pause]

On the sixteenth of January 1912, they were only about fifteen miles from the Pole. They were in good spirits. Tomorrow, or the day after, they would be there. They would be the first people ever to stand at the South Pole. Then, in the afternoon, Bowers saw something ahead. It was a small dark speck on the white plain. At first, he thought it was a cairn of snow, or a trick of the light. As they came closer, they saw what it was. A black flag was tied to a sledge runner, standing upright in the snow. All around it were the tracks of sledges and skis, and the paw prints of many dogs. The Norwegians had been there first. The five men stood in silence for a while. That night, Scott wrote in his diary: The worst has happened. Yet they had come so far, and they decided to go on.

[long-pause]

{img:78-five-men-standing-pole-flag-wind}

Scott wrote that the South Pole was an awful place.

[pause]

On the seventeenth of January, they marched the last few miles. The day was bitterly cold, with a strong wind blowing in their faces. That evening, by Bowers' measurements, they reached the South Pole. They stood in the middle of an endless white plain, just like every other part of it. Scott wrote in his diary: Great God! This is an awful place. They had a small celebration in the tent that night. There was a little extra food, and some chocolate they had saved. The next day, they found the Norwegians' tent, a little way off. Inside lay Amundsen's note, and the letter for the King of Norway. Scott took the letter with him, carefully, to carry home. It was a kind and decent thing to do, even in his disappointment. Then the five men built a small cairn of snow. They put up their own flag, the Union Jack, beside it.

[long-pause]

{img:79-five-explorers-group-portrait-snow}

Bowers took a famous photograph at the Pole by pulling on a piece of string.

[pause]

Bowers had carried a camera all the way. He set it up in the snow on its little stand. Then he tied a long piece of string to the shutter. The five men gathered in front of the camera. Three of them stood, and two sat in front. Bowers sat down and pulled the string. Click. In the photograph, their faces are dark from the sun and wind, and their beards are white with frost. Scott stands in the middle, looking straight at the camera. Oates stands tall and thin beside him. In front sit Bowers, holding the string, and Wilson. They look very tired. But they also look steady and brave, and they look like friends. That photograph would come home one day. It became one of the most famous pictures ever taken. Then, on the nineteenth of January, they turned around and began the long march home.

[long-pause]

{img:80-men-marching-north-sail-on-sledge}

The little flags at the Pole are shrinking behind them now. Ahead lies eight hundred miles of snow, and the road back to the hut.

[pause]

On the way home, they rigged a sail on their sledge to let the wind help push them.

[pause]

For the first days of the return, the wind was behind them. So the men rigged a sail on the sledge. They made it from the floor cloth of the tent, tied to a ski pole. The wind filled it and helped push them across the snow. Scott wrote that it was a relief to have the wind at their backs at last. Then the weather turned. There were blizzards, and days of thick grey fog. The men could barely see their old tracks from the way out. The snow grew soft and sticky, and the sledge dragged. They found their depots one after another, small snow cairns with a flag on top. Each one held a week's food and fuel. Each time they found one, they felt a great relief. But they were getting very tired. They had been walking for nearly three months, and they had been eating less food than their bodies needed.

[long-pause]

{img:81-fossil-rocks-glacier-moraine-sketch}

Tired as they were, they stopped to collect stones with ancient leaves pressed into them.

[pause]

In the second week of February, they reached the top of the Beardmore Glacier. They began to climb down it. Even now, they stopped for an afternoon at a place where the mountain rocks came down to the ice. Wilson and Bowers went off among the rocks. They collected samples, as the scientists back home had asked. Wilson found pieces of coal. He also found stones with the shapes of leaves pressed into them. These were fossils of a plant called Glossopteris. It had grown there many millions of years ago, when Antarctica was warm and green. The men wrapped the stones carefully and put them on the sledge. There were about thirty-five pounds of them. And they carried them all the way to their last camp. Would you have done the same, so tired and so far from home? They did, because they believed knowledge was worth carrying.

[long-pause]

{img:82-quiet-tent-glacier-foot-evening-light}

Edgar Evans, the strongest man at the start, was the first of the five to fall asleep for good.

[pause]

Edgar Evans had been the strongest of them all when they set out. But he had hurt his hand, and it would not heal. He had taken a fall or two on the ice. Day by day, he grew quieter and slower. On the seventeenth of February, near the foot of the glacier, he became too weak to walk. His companions stayed with him and looked after him in the tent. That night, very quietly, he passed away. He was thirty-five years old. The others were deeply sad. Evans had been a big, cheerful, loyal man. He had been with Scott on the earlier expedition and had worked harder than anyone. Scott had always said he was one of the finest men he knew. They said a prayer for him. Then the four of them went on, out onto the Barrier, with four hundred miles still to go.

[long-pause]

{img:83-tall-man-quiet-tent-evening-stove}

Oates said only a few quiet words before he stepped out into the snow.

[pause]

The Barrier was much colder than anyone had expected for that time of year. The surface grew rough, and the sledge stuck to it. The days were growing shorter, and for the first time in months the sun set at night. Oates was suffering most of all. His feet were badly frostbitten, and walking was very painful. He never complained. But the others could see he was in great pain, and that it was slowing them down. In the evenings, around the little stove, Oates talked about his mother and his horses. Then, around the seventeenth of March, a blizzard was blowing. That morning, Oates knew he could go no further. He also knew his friends would never leave him. So he stood up and said quietly: I am just going outside and may be some time. Then he went out into the snow. It was close to his thirty-second birthday. Scott wrote that it was the act of a brave man.

[long-pause]

{img:84-candle-tent-writing-letters-quiet}

Scott asked his wife to make their little boy interested in nature, rather than games.

[pause]

The three who remained went on for a few more days. They were now only about eleven miles from One Ton Depot, with all its food and fuel. Then a great blizzard blew up, and they could not leave the tent. It did not stop. Inside, the three friends lay close together in their sleeping bags. They wrote letters to the people they loved. Wilson wrote to his wife Oriana, and told her he was not afraid. Bowers wrote to his mother, and was cheerful to the last. Scott wrote many letters. In one, he asked Kathleen to make their son interested in natural history. It was better than games, he wrote. He wrote a message to the public too. His last journal entry is dated the twenty-ninth of March. At the bottom, he wrote: For God's sake look after our people. Some time after that, in the quiet tent, the three friends fell asleep together, and did not wake.

[long-pause]

{img:85-two-men-dog-sledges-depot-waiting}

Outside, the wind slowly drops, and winter settles over the white plain. Back at Cape Evans, a small group of friends is still waiting.

[pause]

Cherry waited six days at One Ton Depot, scanning the horizon through a telescope.

[pause]

Back at the hut, the others had been waiting. At the end of February, Cherry and Dimitri set out with two dog teams. They went to One Ton Depot to meet the returning party and help them home. They reached the depot on the third of March. For six days, they waited there in terrible cold. Every day, they searched the southern horizon with a telescope for any sign of the travellers. But they saw nothing. Only the empty white plain, the low sun and the drifting snow. They had no orders to go further, and the dogs were running short of food. So on the tenth of March, they sadly turned back. They did not know the polar party was still far away. Cherry would wonder for the rest of his life if he could have done more. Yet most people since have agreed. He did everything that he had been asked to do, and more.

[long-pause]

{img:86-second-winter-hut-thirteen-men-lamp}

In the second winter, the empty bunks stood with their owners' books still on the shelves.

[pause]

In March, the Terra Nova came back. She took some of the men home before the winter. The rest stayed to face a second winter in the dark. There were thirteen men left in the hut. The surgeon, Edward Atkinson, was in charge. He was calm, kind and sensible. He kept everyone busy and cheerful through the long months. They carried on with the scientific work, as Scott would have wanted. They gave lectures and played the gramophone. On Midwinter Day, they held a feast as before, and drank a toast to absent friends. But the hut felt empty. The bunks of the polar party stood empty, with their books and pictures still on the shelves. Nobody said much. Yet everyone knew, in their hearts, that the five would not be coming home. In spring, the men talked it over and voted. They would go south, to find their friends.

[long-pause]

{img:87-snow-cairn-ski-cross-plain-sunlight}

The search party built a tall cairn of snow over the tent and crowned it with a cross of skis.

[pause]

At the end of October 1912, the search party set out. There were eleven men, with dogs and mules. On the twelfth of November, one of them saw something on the snow ahead. It looked like a small cairn. As they came closer, they saw it was the top of a tent, almost completely buried. The men stopped and stood very still. Inside, they found Scott, Wilson and Bowers, lying in their sleeping bags as if asleep. Beside them were their diaries and letters. There were the fossil stones from the glacier, and Amundsen's letter for the King. The men did not move their friends. They read a service from the Bible and sang a hymn, standing in the cold bright air. Then they built a great cairn of snow over the tent, twelve feet high. On top, they set a cross made of skis. Later, they built a smaller cairn for Oates, calling him a very gallant gentleman.

[long-pause]

{img:88-wooden-cross-observation-hill-view}

On a hill above the ice, the men carved five names and a line of poetry into a wooden cross.

[pause]

In January 1913, the Terra Nova came back to take everyone home. Before they left, the men made a large wooden cross. It was carved from Australian jarrah wood, which is hard and lasts a long time. They carried it up to the top of Observation Hill. This was a steep little hill, from which they could see far across the Barrier. Out there, somewhere, their friends lay at rest. On the cross, they carved the names of the five men who had reached the Pole. Underneath, they carved a line from a poem by Tennyson. Cherry chose it. It reads: To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield. Then the men walked down the hill and back to the ship. The Terra Nova steamed slowly out of the sound. The men at the rail watched Antarctica grow small behind them. The cross still stands on that hill today, weathered silver-grey by the wind.

[long-pause]

{img:89-woman-ship-deck-sea-calm-thoughtful}

Scott's son grew up to love nature, just as his father had asked.

[pause]

On the tenth of February 1913, the Terra Nova reached a small harbour in New Zealand. Two men rowed ashore and sent a telegram to London. Within hours, the news had travelled around the world. In Britain, people were stunned. A great memorial service was held at St Paul's Cathedral in London, and the King attended. Kathleen Scott heard the news at sea. She was on a ship, sailing out to meet her husband. The captain told her as gently as he could. She walked the deck for a long time, looking at the sea. Later, she read her husband's last letter. She remembered what he had asked about their son. Peter Scott grew up loving birds and the countryside. He became a painter of birds and a famous naturalist. In 1961, he helped to found the World Wildlife Fund, whose symbol is a giant panda. His father would surely have been very proud.

[long-pause]

{img:90-fossil-leaf-stone-museum-lamp}

The fossil leaves they carried helped prove that the southern continents were once joined together.

[pause]

The stones from the Beardmore Glacier were studied carefully by scientists. They turned out to be very important. The fossil leaves came from Glossopteris, the same plant found in South America, Africa, India and Australia. That gave scientists an important clue. Long ago, all those lands must have been joined to Antarctica in one great southern continent. Over millions of years, it slowly broke apart. So the men who walked to the Pole brought home a small piece of the Earth's own story. The emperor eggs did not prove what Wilson had hoped. Yet they are still kept today, in a quiet drawer in London. And Amundsen always spoke of Scott with great respect. He went on exploring for many years. In 1926, he flew over the North Pole in an airship. Today, the scientific station at the South Pole bears both their names, side by side: Amundsen and Scott.

[long-pause]

{img:91-cape-evans-hut-today-erebus-sunlight}

The hut at Cape Evans still stands, looking almost exactly as the men left it.

[pause]

The hut at Cape Evans is still there today, on its little cape of black sand. For many years, it stood empty. Snow drifted in and filled some rooms with ice. Then a group of people in New Zealand decided to save it. They are called the Antarctic Heritage Trust. For years, their workers came south in the summers. They dug out the ice and repaired the walls and roof. They cleaned and mended thousands of objects, one by one. Today, the hut looks almost as it did in 1913. The long table still stands in the wardroom. Tins of food and jars of pickles sit on the shelves, with their old labels. In the kitchen is Clissold's stove. In the stables, the ponies' names are still written above the stalls. Very few people ever visit. Those who do walk through quietly, speaking in low voices, as if they were in a church.

[long-pause]

So we leave the old hut on its cape of black sand.

[pause]

The long table is still, and the stove has gone cold. Outside, the snow lies quiet across the frozen bay. High above Erebus, the southern lights begin to ripple, green and soft and slow. Down by the shore, a seal breathes once at its hole in the ice, and sinks back into the dark. The wind has dropped. Nothing moves on the white plain.

[pause]

Should your eyes still be open, there is another quiet story from long ago, waiting for you on the screen. And if this voyage brought you some rest, a subscription keeps the next one close at hand.

[pause]

Let the cold and the distance stay far away now. You are warm, and there is nowhere left to walk.

[pause]

Sleep well, and good night.
