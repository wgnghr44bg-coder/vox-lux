{img:lake-geneva-1816-grey-clouds-rain-villa-on-hillside-vineyards-boats}
Tonight, we travel back more than two hundred years, to the summer of 1816. We go to Switzerland, to the shores of Lake Geneva. A long, deep, beautiful lake, shaped like a crescent moon, lying between green hills, and vineyards, with the high white mountains of the Alps rising beyond. On a clear day, you can see the snowy peak of Mont Blanc, the highest mountain in Europe, far away, floating above the clouds.

[pause]

At the western end of the lake, there is the old city of Geneva, with its grey stone houses, and its cathedral, on the hill. And along the shores, there are villages, and farms, and vineyards, and the elegant country houses of rich families, with their gardens, running down to the water. It is one of the loveliest places in Europe. Travellers come from all over the world to see it.

[long-pause]

But the summer of 1816 is a strange summer. It is cold, and dark, and wet. It rains almost every day. Great storms sweep across the lake, with thunder, and lightning, and wind. The sun hardly shines. In June, there is snow on the hills, where there should be flowers. The crops will not ripen in the fields. People will remember it for the rest of their lives, as the year without a summer.

[pause]

{img:villa-diodati-cologny-1816-stone-house-balcony-lake-view-stormy-sky}
And in a house on the hillside above the lake, a group of young English travellers is staying. A famous poet, and another poet, and a young doctor, and two young women, and a baby. Because of the rain, they cannot go outside. So they sit by the fire, in the evenings, and talk, and read, and tell stories. And one of them, a young woman of eighteen, is about to have a dream, which will lead to one of the most famous books ever written.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:swiss-boatman-family-1816-cottage-vineyard-lake-shore-father-mother-children}
In this story, we will spend that strange summer by the lake. We will meet the English travellers. Mary Godwin, the young writer. Percy Bysshe Shelley, the poet she loves. Lord Byron, the most famous poet in Europe. His young doctor, John Polidori. And Mary's stepsister, Claire. And we will meet a local family, who live in the village below the great house. The Morets. Pierre, a boatman, and a grower of grapes. His wife Louise. Their daughter Élise, who is twelve, and helps in the kitchen of the great house. Their son Jacques, who is nine. And old Grandmother Anne.

[pause]

We will see the storms over the lake, and the evenings by the fire. We will hear the ghost stories, told in the candlelight, on a dark and stormy night. We will be there when Mary has her famous dream. We will travel to the mountains, to see the great glaciers of Mont Blanc. And at the end of the year, we will help with the grape harvest, very late, in the cold autumn, when the strange summer was finally over.

[pause]

{img:candlelit-drawing-room-1816-young-people-by-fire-books-rain-on-windows}
It is not a frightening story. It is a story about a group of young people, far from home, in a beautiful place, in bad weather, with time on their hands, and their heads full of ideas. And about a book, which was born from a summer of rain, and storms, and conversation, and which has been read, and loved, and retold, all over the world, ever since.

[pause]

So let us go to Lake Geneva, in the summer of 1816. Let us walk up through the wet vineyards, to the great house on the hill. And let us listen to the rain on the windows, and the crackle of the fire, and the voices of the young people, talking late into the night.

[long-pause]

{img:volcano-tambora-1815-eruption-distant-ash-cloud-sea-indonesia-night}
The strange weather of 1816 began more than a year before, on the other side of the world. In April 1815, on the island of Sumbawa, in what is now Indonesia, a great volcano erupted. It was called Mount Tambora. It was the biggest volcanic eruption in recorded history. The explosion was so loud that it was heard more than two thousand kilometres away. People thought it was the sound of cannons.

[pause]

The volcano threw enormous amounts of ash, and dust, and gas, high into the sky. Higher than the clouds, into the upper air. And there, the winds carried it all around the world. A thin veil of fine dust, spreading slowly across the whole sky, all around the Earth. Nobody could see it. But it blocked some of the sunlight. And so, the whole world grew a little colder.

[long-pause]

{img:snow-falling-on-green-summer-fields-1816-farmers-looking-at-sky}
In 1816, the effects were felt everywhere. In Europe, and in North America, the summer was cold, and wet, and dark. In New England, in America, it snowed in June, and there was frost in July, and August. In Europe, it rained, and rained, and rained. The rivers flooded. The harvests failed. The wheat rotted in the fields. The potatoes would not grow. The grapes stayed small, and green, and sour.

[pause]

Nobody, at that time, knew why. Nobody connected the cold summer in Europe with a volcano on the other side of the world. People thought it was a punishment from God, or a sign of the end of the world. Some people said that the sun was dying. There were strange stories, and fears. In Italy, people said that the sun had spots on it, which were growing bigger, and that the world would end.

[pause]

{img:dramatic-red-orange-sunset-over-lake-geneva-1816-volcanic-sky-glow}
But there was one beautiful thing about that strange year. The sunsets. The fine dust high in the sky made the sunsets extraordinary. Red, and orange, and purple, and gold, glowing for a long time, after the sun had gone down. Painters, like the English painter Turner, painted these glowing skies. And people, all over Europe, stood and watched them, in wonder, every evening, when the clouds parted.

[pause]

Switzerland suffered badly that year. It is a mountain country, and it was even colder there, and wetter. The snow did not melt in the mountains. The glaciers grew. The rivers and lakes rose, and flooded the fields, and the villages. Lake Geneva rose so high that the water came up into the streets of Geneva. The harvests failed, and the price of bread went up, and up, until many poor people could not afford it.

[long-pause]

The people of Geneva had seen bad years before. But none like this. The old people said that they had never known a summer like it. The farmers looked at the sky, every morning, and shook their heads. The fishermen stayed at home, because of the storms. And the travellers who came to the lake, for its famous beauty, found only rain, and cloud, and mist.

[pause]

{img:rain-on-vineyard-cologny-1816-dark-clouds-lake-below-wet-terraces}
But for the young English travellers, in the house on the hill, the bad weather turned out to be a kind of gift. It kept them indoors. It kept them together. It made them talk, and read, and think, and imagine. And in the long, dark, stormy evenings, by the fire, it led them to tell each other stories. Without the year without a summer, there might never have been a Frankenstein.

[pause]

So in a way, one of the most famous stories in the world was born from a volcano, on the other side of the world, a year before. It is a strange thought. But that is how history works, sometimes. Small things, and big things, and faraway things, all connected, in ways that nobody can see at the time.

[long-pause]

{img:swiss-boatman-1816-mending-sail-small-harbour-cologny-wooden-boat}
Pierre Moret is forty years old. He lives in the village of Cologny, on the hillside above the southern shore of the lake, about three kilometres from Geneva. He is a boatman. He owns a small sailing boat, with a single sail, and he carries people, and goods, across the lake, and along the shore. He also has a small vineyard, on the slope behind his house, where he grows grapes, for wine, like his father, and grandfather before him.

[pause]

Pierre is a calm, steady man, with a weathered face, and strong arms, and a pipe that he is always lighting, and that is always going out. He knows the lake as well as he knows his own house. Every wind, every current, every change in the weather. He knows when a storm is coming, long before anyone else. He says that he can smell it, in the air.

[long-pause]

{img:swiss-village-woman-1816-apron-bonnet-baking-bread-cottage-kitchen}
His wife, Louise, is thirty-six. She runs the house, and the garden, and the chickens, and the goats. She makes cheese, and bread, and preserves. And in the autumn, she works in the vineyard, picking grapes, with the whole family. She is a warm, practical woman, who sings while she works, and worries about everything, especially the weather, this year.

[pause]

Élise is twelve. She is quick, and curious, and talkative. Since May, she has been working in the kitchen of the great house on the hill, the Villa Diodati, where the famous English lord is staying. She helps the cook, and washes the dishes, and carries trays. She is paid a few francs a week, which helps her family, in this hard year. And she sees and hears all sorts of interesting things. She comes home every evening, full of stories.

[pause]

{img:boy-nine-1816-swiss-village-fishing-from-stone-jetty-lake-mist}
Jacques is nine. He loves the lake, and boats, and fishing. He goes out in the boat with his father, whenever he can, and helps with the ropes, and the sail. He wants to be a boatman too, when he grows up. He is also very interested in the famous English lord, who is said to be very handsome, and very wicked, and to swim across lakes, and to keep a bear. Jacques is disappointed to learn that the lord has not brought the bear to Switzerland.

[pause]

And there is Grandmother Anne, Pierre's mother. She is seventy-three. She has lived in Cologny all her life. She remembers the old days, before the French Revolution, when Geneva was a little independent republic, and the old families ruled it. She remembers when the French armies came, and took it over, and made it part of France. And she remembers when the French left, three years ago, and last year, when Geneva became part of Switzerland, at last. She says she has seen enough history for one life.

[long-pause]

{img:grandmother-by-fire-knitting-telling-stories-children-rain-window}
The Morets' house is small, and old, and built of stone, with a steep roof, and a little wooden balcony, covered with vines. It stands on the hillside, just below the Villa Diodati, with a view over the lake. From the window, they can see the boats, and the water, and on clear days, the city of Geneva, at the end of the lake, and the mountains beyond. On rainy days, which this year is most days, they can see nothing but grey.

[pause]

The family speaks French, like everyone in Geneva. They are Protestants, like most Genevans, and go to the plain, simple church in the village, every Sunday. They are hard-working, and careful with money, and they do not like waste, or show, or fuss. These are Genevan virtues, the grandmother says. The English, she says, are very different.

[pause]

This summer, the whole village is talking about the English. They watch them, and gossip about them, and some of them disapprove of them, very much. There are stories about the lord, and about the young women. The hotel owners, across the lake, even rent out telescopes, so that tourists can watch the villa, and see what the famous poet is doing. Pierre thinks this is very silly. He says that they are just young people, far from home, like any others.

[long-pause]

{img:mary-godwin-1816-young-woman-18-dark-dress-reading-by-window-thoughtful}
Mary Godwin is eighteen years old, in the summer of 1816. She is small, and slim, with pale skin, and reddish-golden hair, and large, thoughtful eyes. She is quiet, and serious, and very clever. She reads all the time. Books of history, and philosophy, and science, and poetry, and novels, in English, and French, and Latin. She has been reading, and writing, and thinking, since she was a small child.

[pause]

Mary comes from a very famous family. Her mother was Mary Wollstonecraft, one of the first great writers to argue that women should have the same rights, and the same education, as men. Her book, A Vindication of the Rights of Woman, made her famous, all over Europe. But she passed away, sadly, a few days after Mary was born. Mary never knew her mother. But she read all her books, and loved her, and wanted to be like her.

[long-pause]

{img:william-godwin-bookshop-london-1810s-shelves-young-girl-reading-corner}
Mary's father was William Godwin, a famous philosopher, and writer, in London. He believed in reason, and freedom, and justice, and education for all. Many famous writers, and thinkers, came to his house, to talk with him. When Mary was a little girl, she used to hide behind the sofa, in the evenings, and listen to them talking. Once, she heard the poet Coleridge reciting his famous poem, about an old sailor, and an albatross, and the ghostly ship. She never forgot it.

[pause]

When Mary was sixteen, a young poet came to visit her father. His name was Percy Bysshe Shelley. He was twenty-one, tall, and thin, and wild, and passionate, with bright eyes, and long hair, and a high voice. He was the son of a rich nobleman, but he had been thrown out of Oxford University, for writing a pamphlet against religion. He believed in freedom, and love, and changing the world. Mary fell in love with him. And he fell in love with her.

[pause]

{img:percy-shelley-young-poet-1816-open-collar-wild-hair-writing-outdoors}
There was a problem. Shelley was already married. He had married very young, and the marriage was unhappy, and he had left his wife. Mary and Shelley ran away together, to France, and Switzerland, in 1814, when Mary was sixteen. Mary's stepsister, Claire, went with them. It was a great scandal, in London. Mary's father was very angry. Many people would not speak to them.

[pause]

Since then, Mary and Shelley have lived together, moving from place to place, often with very little money. In 1815, Mary had a baby girl, who was born too early, and lived only a few days. It was a great sorrow. But in January 1816, Mary had a baby boy, called William, after her father. He is healthy, and strong, and Mary loves him more than anything in the world. They call him Willmouse.

[long-pause]

In the spring of 1816, Claire, Mary's stepsister, had an idea. She had met Lord Byron, the famous poet, in London, and had fallen in love with him. Byron was leaving England, to travel in Europe, and was going to Geneva. Claire persuaded Mary and Shelley to go to Geneva too, so that she could see him again. Shelley wanted to meet Byron, whose poetry he admired. So they set off, across France, and over the mountains, into Switzerland.

[pause]

{img:young-mother-holding-baby-1816-travelling-carriage-alps-snow-pass}
They crossed the Jura mountains, in May, in a carriage, through snow, and ice, and storms, with the baby, wrapped up warmly. It was cold, and frightening, and beautiful. Mary wrote letters, describing the journey, and the mountains, and the snow, and the great pine forests. She was a good writer, already. But she had not yet written a book. She did not know what she wanted to write about.

[pause]

That summer, by the lake, she would find out. And the story she wrote would be read by more people than anything her famous mother, or her famous father, or her famous lover, or her famous friend Lord Byron ever wrote.

[long-pause]

{img:hotel-secheron-geneva-1816-lakeside-carriages-arriving-english-travellers}
Mary, Shelley, Claire and the baby arrived in Geneva in the middle of May, 1816. They stayed at first in a hotel, at Sécheron, just outside the city, on the shore of the lake. It was a big, comfortable hotel, popular with English travellers. From the windows, they could see the lake, and the boats, and the mountains beyond. When the weather was fine, which was not often, it was very beautiful.

[pause]

For the first few days, there was some sunshine. Mary wrote that the weather was delightful, and that she felt as happy as a new-fledged bird. She and Shelley went out on the lake, in a little boat, in the evenings, and watched the sun set behind the mountains. They walked along the shore, and in the woods, and picked flowers. And they waited for Lord Byron.

[long-pause]

{img:lord-byron-1816-arriving-huge-black-carriage-napoleon-copy-servants}
Byron arrived about ten days later, at the end of May. He came in an enormous black travelling carriage, which was a copy of the carriage of the emperor Napoleon. It had a bed in it, and a library, and a place for eating, and everything a gentleman could need on a long journey. Byron travelled with servants, and his young doctor, and many trunks, and boxes, and books. Everyone in the hotel came out to look.

[pause]

Lord Byron was twenty-eight years old. He was the most famous poet in Europe. His long poems, about romantic heroes, and faraway lands, were read by everyone, and he was admired, and talked about, everywhere. He was very handsome, with dark curly hair, and pale skin, and a beautiful face. He had been born with a twisted foot, and walked with a slight limp. He was proud, and witty, and moody, and charming, and difficult.

[pause]

{img:two-poets-meeting-lakeside-1816-byron-shelley-shaking-hands-boats}
He had left England in a hurry, in April, after a great scandal, about his marriage, and his private life. His wife had left him, with their baby daughter. Many people in London had turned against him. He would never go back to England again. He was bitter, and sad, under his proud manner. But he was also glad to be free, and to travel, and to write.

[pause]

Byron and Shelley met for the first time, on the shore of the lake, on the twenty-seventh of May. They liked each other at once. Byron admired Shelley's mind, and his kindness. Shelley admired Byron's poetry, and his wit. They were very different. Byron was worldly, and cynical, and liked to laugh at everything. Shelley was idealistic, and serious, and believed that the world could be made better. But they became close friends.

[long-pause]

With Byron came his doctor, John Polidori. He was only twenty years old, the son of an Italian father and an English mother. He was clever, and had become a doctor very young. Byron's publisher had paid him to write a diary of the journey. Polidori was handsome, and eager, and wanted very much to be a writer too. But he was also clumsy, and touchy, and easily hurt, and Byron often teased him.

[pause]

{img:young-doctor-polidori-1816-writing-diary-hotel-room-candle-eager}
For a few weeks, they all stayed near each other, at Sécheron. They met every day, and dined together, and went out on the lake together, in a boat, in the evenings. Then they decided to move out of the hotel, and rent houses, across the lake, on the southern shore, at Cologny. Byron rented the Villa Diodati, a large and elegant house, on the hillside. And the Shelleys rented a smaller house, called Maison Chapuis, just below it, down by the water.

[pause]

Between the two houses, there was a vineyard, on the steep slope. And a path, through the vines, which they walked up and down, every day, and every evening. Élise saw them often, on that path. The young English lady, with her red-gold hair, and the baby in her arms. And the tall, thin poet, with his wild hair, talking, and waving his arms, as he walked. She thought they looked very much in love.

[pause]

In the evenings, Élise sometimes sees the lights of the two houses, from her own window. The big windows of the villa, up on the hill, glowing yellow, until very late. And the smaller windows of the little house, below, by the water. She wonders what the English are talking about, so late at night. Her grandmother says that it is probably nonsense. But Élise is not so sure.

[long-pause]

{img:villa-diodati-front-1816-columns-balcony-terrace-garden-cypresses}
The Villa Diodati stands on the hillside above the lake, at Cologny, surrounded by gardens, and vineyards, and trees. It is a large, square house, of pale stone, with tall windows, and a wide balcony, supported by columns, running all along the front, looking out over the lake. From the balcony, there is a wonderful view. The whole lake, stretching away, to the east, and the city of Geneva, to the west, and the mountains beyond.

[pause]

The house was built about a hundred years before, by a family called Diodati, who were famous in Geneva. A long time ago, the English poet John Milton, who wrote the great poem Paradise Lost, had visited a member of this family, in Geneva. Byron liked that. He felt that he was following in Milton's footsteps.

[long-pause]

{img:villa-interior-1816-drawing-room-fireplace-books-piled-candles-chairs}
Inside, the villa has high ceilings, and big rooms, with polished wooden floors, and tall windows, and fireplaces. There is a drawing room, where the friends gather in the evenings. A dining room, and a library, and bedrooms, upstairs. Byron has filled it with his books, and papers, and his things. There are books everywhere. On the tables, on the chairs, on the floor, piled up in corners.

[pause]

Élise works in the kitchen, at the back of the house, with the cook, Madame Berthe, who is from Geneva. The kitchen is warm, and busy, and smells of soup, and bread, and roasting meat. Byron eats very little, because he is always worried about getting fat. Sometimes, he lives for days on biscuits, and soda water, and potatoes with vinegar. Madame Berthe thinks this is very foolish. She says that a man needs proper food.

[pause]

{img:kitchen-girl-carrying-tray-up-servants-stairs-candle-villa-evening}
But the guests eat well. Shelley does not eat meat. He is a vegetarian, which is very unusual, in 1816. He eats bread, and vegetables, and fruit, and drinks water, or tea. Mary eats what she likes. Polidori eats a great deal. Claire eats very little, because she is in love. Élise carries the trays up and down the stairs, and listens to their talk, although she understands very little English. She is learning a few words, though. Yes, and no, and thank you, and goodnight.

[pause]

Byron keeps animals, too. He loves animals. At the villa, he has a dog, and a few other pets. In England, he had kept many more. Dogs, and cats, and birds, and a fox, and a bear, which he kept at university, because the rules said that students could not keep dogs. Jacques is very disappointed when Élise tells him there is no bear at the villa. But he is pleased to hear about the dog.

[long-pause]

The villa is often noisy, and busy. People come and go. The friends talk, and argue, and laugh, until late at night. Byron writes in the night, and sleeps in the morning. Shelley writes in the morning, and walks in the afternoon. Polidori writes his diary, and practises medicine, on anyone who will let him. Mary reads, and writes letters, and looks after the baby. Claire copies out Byron's poems, in her neat handwriting, so that they can be sent to his publisher in London.

[pause]

{img:view-from-villa-balcony-lake-geneva-storm-clouds-lightning-distance}
In the evenings, when the weather is fine, they sit on the balcony, and watch the sun set over the lake, and the mountains. But this summer, the weather is not often fine. Mostly, it rains. And then they sit inside, by the fire, in the drawing room, and talk. And outside, the rain falls on the vineyards, and the lake, and the storms roll in from the mountains.

[pause]

Élise walks home every evening, down the path through the vineyard, to her family's house. Sometimes, if it is late, Pierre comes to meet her, with a lantern. She tells her family everything she has seen, and heard, at the villa. Grandmother Anne listens, and shakes her head, and says that the English are all mad. But she always wants to hear more.

[long-pause]

{img:small-sailing-boat-lake-geneva-evening-1816-two-poets-boatman-calm-water}
When they first came to the lake, Shelley and Byron decided to buy a boat. They both loved boats, and the water. They bought a small sailing boat, together, with a single sail, and a keel, and they kept it in a little harbour, below the Shelleys' house. In the evenings, when the weather allowed, they went out on the lake, with Mary, and Claire, and sometimes Polidori.

[pause]

Neither Byron nor Shelley was a very good sailor. And the lake can be dangerous, with sudden storms, and strong winds, coming down from the mountains. So they asked Pierre Moret to go with them, sometimes, as their boatman. Pierre knows the lake better than anyone. He sits at the back of the boat, at the tiller, and steers, and handles the sail, while the English talk, and look at the view.

[long-pause]

{img:moonlit-lake-boat-drifting-poets-talking-boatman-at-tiller-mountains}
On calm evenings, the lake is very beautiful. The water is still, and clear, and reflects the sky, and the mountains, and the first stars. The boat drifts slowly, with the sail barely moving. The only sound is the water lapping against the boat, and the voices of the young people, talking quietly, about poetry, and life, and love, and the stars. Sometimes Byron sings an old song, or recites a poem. Sometimes Shelley reads aloud from a book.

[pause]

Pierre does not understand most of what they say. But he understands enough. He knows that they are talking about important things. And he likes them, the young English. They are polite to him, and they pay him well, and they ask him about the lake, and the weather, and the fish, and the villages. Shelley especially likes to talk to him, in his careful French. He asks Pierre about his life, and his family, and listens to the answers.

[pause]

{img:boatman-and-young-woman-talking-boat-lake-1816-baby-wrapped-shawl}
Mary likes Pierre too. She sometimes sits beside him, at the back of the boat, with the baby wrapped in her shawl, and asks him questions. About the lake, and the mountains, and the old stories of Geneva. Pierre tells her about the ghosts that people say walk along the shore, on stormy nights. And about the old castle of Chillon, at the other end of the lake, where prisoners were once kept in a dungeon, below the water. Mary listens, and remembers.

[pause]

One evening, a sudden wind comes down from the mountains, and the lake becomes rough, very quickly. The waves grow big, and the boat rocks, and spray flies over the side. Claire is frightened. Shelley, who cannot swim, sits very still. Byron, who is a strong swimmer, laughs, and says that if the boat sinks, he will save everyone. Pierre says nothing. He just steers, calmly, and brings the boat safely back to the harbour, in ten minutes.

[long-pause]

{img:boat-returning-safely-small-harbour-storm-clouds-lanterns-relieved}
When they are safely ashore, Byron shakes Pierre's hand, and gives him a gold coin. And Shelley thanks him, and says that he is the best sailor on the lake. Pierre is embarrassed. He says that it was only a little wind. But that night, he tells Louise that the English should be more careful. The lake is beautiful, he says, but it is not a pond. It can turn, very quickly.

[pause]

The boat trips on the lake are some of the happiest moments of that summer, for the young English travellers. Mary wrote later about the evenings on the water, and the beauty of the lake, and the mountains, in the evening light. And many years later, she remembered them, with great love, and sadness, because of what happened later. But that is a story for another time.

[pause]

For now, it is summer. Or what passes for summer, this year. And when the rain stops, for an evening, and the clouds part, and the sun sets, red, and gold, over the Jura mountains, the little boat goes out on the lake again. With Pierre at the tiller, and the young poets talking, and the stars coming out, over the dark water.

[long-pause]

{img:thunderstorm-over-lake-geneva-1816-lightning-mountains-dark-water-night}
The storms of that summer are famous. They come, again and again, in the evenings, and at night. Great black clouds pile up over the mountains, at the eastern end of the lake. The air grows heavy, and still, and strange. The birds go quiet. Then there is a rumble of thunder, far away, rolling around the mountains. And then the storm sweeps down the lake, with wind, and rain, and lightning.

[pause]

The lightning over the lake is extraordinary. Byron wrote that he had never seen anything like it. Great flashes, lighting up the whole lake, and the mountains, and the sky, for a moment, as bright as day. Then darkness. Then another flash. Sometimes, there are several storms at once, in different parts of the sky, flashing at each other, across the lake, like great giants, fighting with fire.

[long-pause]

{img:young-people-watching-storm-from-villa-balcony-wrapped-in-coats-1816}
The young people at the Villa Diodati watch the storms from the balcony, wrapped in their coats, and shawls, with the rain blowing in their faces. They are thrilled by them. They are all romantics, who love the wild, and powerful, and sublime things of nature. Mountains, and storms, and oceans, and waterfalls. Things that make you feel small, and full of wonder, and a little afraid. They call this feeling the sublime.

[pause]

Byron writes a poem about one of the storms, in his long poem, Childe Harold. He writes about the thunder leaping from peak to peak, among the mountains, and every mountain finding a voice, and the night being glorious, and the lake shining in the lightning. It is one of his most famous passages. Shelley writes poems too, and letters, about the storms, and the mountains, and the power of nature.

[pause]

{img:lightning-strike-old-oak-tree-hillside-sparks-night-rain-dramatic-calm}
In the village below, the Morets do not find the storms so thrilling. For them, the storms mean danger. Danger to the boats, and the vineyards, and the houses, and the crops. Hail can destroy a whole vineyard, in a few minutes. Lightning can set fire to a barn, or a house. The wind can tear the roofs off. And the endless rain is rotting the grapes on the vines, and flooding the fields.

[pause]

When a storm comes, Pierre goes down to the harbour, to check that the boats are tied up safely. Louise brings the goats, and the chickens, into the shed. Grandmother Anne lights a candle, and says a prayer. And the children sit by the window, and watch the lightning, and count the seconds between the flash, and the thunder, to see how far away the storm is. Jacques says that it is like the sky is fighting.

[long-pause]

One night, in June, lightning strikes an old oak tree, on the hill above the village. There is a great flash, and a crack, like a cannon, and the whole house shakes. In the morning, the family goes up to look. The great old tree has been split in two, from top to bottom, and blackened, and burned. Smoke is still rising from it. Jacques has never seen anything like it. He touches the burned wood, and it is still warm.

[pause]

{img:split-blackened-oak-tree-morning-after-storm-family-looking-mist}
Many years later, when he read Mary's book, Jacques found something in it that made him stop, and remember. In the book, the young hero, Victor Frankenstein, tells how, when he was a boy, near Geneva, he saw a great old oak tree struck by lightning, in a storm, and blasted to pieces. And how it made him wonder about the power of electricity, and of nature. Jacques wondered if Mary had seen the same tree.

[pause]

After the storm, the sky clears. The air is fresh, and clean, and smells of wet earth, and pine. The lake is calm again, and shining. The mountains are washed, and sharp, and clear. And for a few hours, the sun shines, warm, on the wet vineyards. Everyone comes out of their houses, and breathes, and looks at the sky. And then, by evening, the clouds begin to gather again, over the mountains.

[long-pause]

{img:evening-conversation-drawing-room-1816-fire-candles-poets-talking-woman-listening}
In the long, rainy evenings, the friends talk. They talk about everything. About poetry, and politics, and religion, and philosophy. About the French Revolution, which happened when they were children, and Napoleon, who has just been defeated, at Waterloo, the year before. About freedom, and tyranny, and the future of the world. About love, and marriage, and death, and the meaning of life.

[pause]

Byron and Shelley do most of the talking. They talk for hours, by the fire, often until two or three in the morning. Byron is witty, and sharp, and mocking. Shelley is passionate, and dreamy, and full of strange ideas. Polidori tries to join in, but they often tease him. Claire listens, and sometimes speaks. And Mary sits quietly, in the corner, and listens to everything. She does not say much. But she remembers.

[long-pause]

{img:galvani-experiment-frog-legs-electricity-18th-century-laboratory-sketch}
Many years later, Mary wrote about one of these conversations. She said that Byron and Shelley talked, one evening, about the nature of life. What is life? Where does it come from? Could it ever be created? Could a dead body ever be brought back to life? These were questions that the scientists of the time were asking too. And there were strange new experiments, which seemed to suggest some answers.

[pause]

Some years before, an Italian scientist, called Luigi Galvani, had discovered something remarkable. When he touched the legs of a dead frog with two different metals, the legs moved, and twitched, as if the frog were alive. He thought that there was a kind of electricity in living things, which made them move. People called this galvanism. It was very exciting, and a little frightening. Was electricity the secret of life?

[pause]

{img:old-scientist-books-electricity-machine-leyden-jars-candlelit-study}
Galvani's nephew, Giovanni Aldini, did even more dramatic experiments. He travelled around Europe, giving public demonstrations, with electricity. In London, in 1803, he used electricity to make the face of a dead body move, and the eyes open, and the hand rise. People who watched were amazed, and horrified. Some of them thought that he was about to bring the dead back to life. Of course, he could not. But the idea was in the air.

[pause]

Shelley was fascinated by science. When he was a boy, at school, and at university, he had done his own experiments, with chemicals, and electricity, and a little electrical machine. He had set fire to things, and given himself shocks, and frightened his sisters. He talked about these things, with Byron, by the fire. About electricity, and life, and whether a body could be made, and brought to life.

[long-pause]

They also talked about the English doctor, and poet, Erasmus Darwin, the grandfather of Charles Darwin, who would later become very famous. Mary wrote that they talked about some of his experiments, or the experiments people said he had done. That he had kept a piece of pasta in a glass case, until, by some strange means, it began to move by itself. It was probably a misunderstanding. But it made them laugh, and wonder.

[pause]

{img:young-woman-listening-in-corner-chair-fire-glow-shadows-thoughtful-face}
Mary listened to all of this, quietly, sitting by the fire, with her baby asleep upstairs. She thought about it, afterward, when she went to bed. About life, and death, and electricity, and the power of science. About what it would mean to create a living being. And about what that being would feel, if it were created. Would it be happy? Would it be lonely? Would its creator love it? Or would he be afraid of it?

[pause]

These were not just scientific questions, for Mary. They were personal questions too. She had lost her own mother, at birth. She had lost her own baby, the year before. She thought a great deal about life, and death, and about mothers, and children, and creators, and the things they create. And all of these thoughts were slowly mixing together, in her mind, like the ingredients of a story, waiting to be told.

[long-pause]

{img:fantasmagoriana-old-french-book-ghost-stories-candle-hands-reading}
In the middle of June, there is a week of terrible weather. It rains, and rains, and rains, for days. There are storms every night. Nobody can go outside. The friends are all at the Villa Diodati, gathered around the fire, in the drawing room, in the evenings, bored, and restless. They have read all the books they brought. They have talked about everything.

[pause]

Then somebody finds a book, in the villa. It is a collection of German ghost stories, translated into French. It is called Fantasmagoriana. They take turns reading the stories aloud, by candlelight, in the evenings, while the rain falls, and the wind howls, and the thunder rolls over the lake. The stories are about ghosts, and phantoms, and haunted castles, and strange happenings in the night.

[long-pause]

{img:group-reading-ghost-stories-aloud-candlelight-storm-window-villa-diodati}
One story is about a man who kisses all his children, one by one, as they sleep. And another is about a family portrait, and a ghostly figure, that walks at midnight. And another about a bride, who discovers that the man she has married is a ghost. They are old-fashioned, and a little silly, in places. But in the candlelight, with the storm outside, they are also quite exciting. The young people listen, wide-eyed, and shiver, a little, and laugh at themselves for shivering.

[pause]

Then, one evening, after reading the stories, Byron has an idea. He says, we will each write a ghost story. A competition. Each of us will write a story, and then we will see whose is best. The others agree, at once. It sounds like a wonderful game, for the rainy evenings. Byron, and Shelley, and Polidori, and Mary will each write one. Claire says that she will not write one. She will listen.

[pause]

{img:writers-at-desks-different-rooms-villa-night-candles-quills-rain}
So they all go off, to think about their stories. Byron begins a story about a mysterious gentleman, who travels to the East, and dies, and seems to come back to life. He writes a few pages, and then gets bored, and stops. He is not really interested in prose stories. He prefers poetry. Shelley begins a story about his own childhood, and his strange experiences. But he too soon gives up. He finds it too hard.

[pause]

Polidori begins a story about a lady with a skull for a head, who has been punished for looking through a keyhole. Even Polidori admits that it is not very good. But later, he takes Byron's unfinished story, about the mysterious gentleman, and turns it into a story of his own. It is called The Vampyre. It is published, three years later, and it is a great success. It is the first modern vampire story in English. And it inspired many of the vampire stories that came after, including, much later, the story of Dracula.

[long-pause]

And Mary? Mary tries, and tries, to think of a story. Every morning, the others ask her, have you thought of a story yet? And every morning, she has to say no. She thinks, and thinks, but nothing comes. She wants to write a story that will make people afraid to look around them. That will make their blood run cold, and their hearts beat faster. But she cannot think of one. She feels stupid, and frustrated.

[pause]

{img:young-woman-at-window-rain-night-thinking-chin-in-hand-candle-behind}
Days pass. The others have given up on their stories. But Mary keeps thinking. In the evenings, she listens to Byron and Shelley talking, by the fire, about electricity, and life, and death. And at night, she goes to bed, in the little house below the villa, and lies awake, listening to the rain, and thinking. Her baby sleeps in his cradle, beside the bed. Shelley sleeps beside her. And Mary lies awake, in the dark, waiting for a story to come.

[pause]

Élise hears about the competition from the cook, who heard it from Byron's servant. She tells her family, at supper. A ghost story competition! Jacques thinks it is a wonderful idea. That evening, by the fire, he tells a ghost story of his own, about a headless boatman, who rows across the lake, on stormy nights. It is not very frightening. But Grandmother Anne pretends to be terrified, and everyone laughs.

[long-pause]

{img:young-woman-in-bed-eyes-closed-moonlight-shutters-vision-dreamlike-1816}
Then, one night, it happens. Mary has gone to bed, late, after a long evening of conversation, by the fire, at the villa. She lies in bed, in the dark, with her eyes closed. But she cannot sleep. Her mind is full of the things she has heard, about electricity, and life, and death. And then, as she lies there, between sleeping and waking, she sees something. Not a dream, exactly. More like a vision, very clear, and very vivid, as if it were really happening, in front of her eyes.

[pause]

She sees a young student, pale, and thin, kneeling beside something he has made. It is the body of a man, lying stretched out, on a table. A body made by the student himself, out of many parts. The student has worked on it for a long time, in secret. And now, he has found a way to bring it to life. Mary sees the body begin to stir, with a kind of uneasy, half living movement. And she sees the student, terrified by what he has done.

[long-pause]

{img:shuttered-window-dawn-light-young-woman-sitting-up-in-bed-wide-eyed}
In her vision, Mary sees the student run away, from the thing he has made, in horror. He goes to his room, and lies down, and tries to sleep, and forget. He hopes that the spark of life will fade, and the creature will be still again. But then he opens his eyes. And there, beside his bed, holding open the curtains, and looking at him, with yellow, watery, questioning eyes, is the creature he has made.

[pause]

Mary opens her eyes, with a start. Her heart is beating fast. She is frightened. She looks around the dark room, to make sure that it is really empty. She sees the moonlight, coming through the shutters. The window, the curtains, the furniture, all just as they should be. And she hears the rain, falling outside, and the lake, lapping on the shore. She is safe. It was only a vision.

[pause]

{img:moonlight-on-lake-from-window-quiet-night-rain-stopped-1816-calm}
She tries to think of something else, something ordinary. Something calm. The ghost story competition. The others' stories. And then, suddenly, she realises. This is it. This is her story. What frightened her, in the night, will frighten other people too. She only needs to describe the spectre that has come to her pillow. She feels a great rush of joy, and excitement, in the dark. She has found her story.

[pause]

Many years later, in 1831, Mary wrote about that night, in an introduction to a new edition of her book. She described the vision, just as she had seen it. And she described her joy, when she realised that she had found her story. That introduction is one of the most famous descriptions of how a story was born, in the whole history of literature. People still read it today, and wonder.

[long-pause]

The next morning, when the others ask her, as usual, have you thought of a story yet? Mary says yes. She has. She tells them about her vision. They are very interested. Shelley especially. He encourages her to write it. And so, that morning, she sits down at her desk, by the window, in the little house below the villa, and begins to write. The first words she writes are, It was on a dreary night of November.

[pause]

{img:young-woman-writing-first-page-quill-desk-window-lake-morning-grey}
At first, she thinks it will be a short story. A few pages, like the others. But as she writes, the story grows. And grows. She thinks about the student, and why he made the creature. She thinks about the creature, and what it feels, and what it wants. She thinks about what happens next. The story becomes longer, and deeper, and richer, and sadder, than any ghost story. It becomes a novel.

[pause]

Élise sees her, that morning, through the window, as she walks up the path to the villa. The young English lady, with her red-gold hair, bent over her desk, writing, and writing, very fast, with her pen. She does not look up, as Élise passes. She is somewhere else, in a world of her own. Élise wonders what she is writing. She will not find out, for many years.

[long-pause]

{img:mary-writing-at-desk-baby-cradle-beside-maison-chapuis-window-rain}
For the rest of the summer, Mary writes. Every day, when she has time, she sits at her desk, in the little house by the lake, and writes her story. It is not easy. She has the baby to look after, and the house, and the visitors, and the walks, and the boat trips. And she is only eighteen. But she writes, a little every day. And slowly, the story takes shape.

[pause]

She calls her student Victor Frankenstein. He is a young man from Geneva, the son of a good family, who goes to study at a university, in Germany, at Ingolstadt. There, he becomes fascinated by science, and by the secret of life. He works day and night, in secret, in his room, gathering materials, and studying, until he discovers how to give life to dead matter. And then he makes a creature, and brings it to life.

[long-pause]

{img:geneva-landscape-1816-lake-mountains-mont-blanc-story-setting-misty}
Mary sets parts of her story in the places she knows, around the lake. Victor's family lives in Geneva, and in a country house by the lake, at a place called Belrive. Victor walks along the shore, and sails on the lake, at night, in a little boat. He sees the storms over the mountains. He climbs to the glaciers of Mont Blanc. Many of the places in the book are places that Mary saw, that summer, with her own eyes.

[pause]

The creature in Mary's book is not a monster, in the way that people think of it today. He is huge, and ugly, and frightening to look at. But inside, he is gentle, and kind, and intelligent. He learns to speak, and to read, by watching a family through a hole in the wall of their cottage. He reads books, and thinks about life. He longs for love, and friendship. But everyone who sees him is frightened of him, and runs away, or attacks him. Even his creator.

[pause]

{img:young-woman-reading-pages-aloud-to-poet-by-fire-evening-manuscript}
That is the heart of Mary's story. A creature, made by a man, and then abandoned by him. Left alone, in a world that does not want him. He asks his creator for help, and love, and a companion. And his creator refuses him. And so the creature becomes angry, and bitter, and sad. Mary's story asks a question, that people are still asking today. What do we owe to the things we create? And what happens when we create something, and then run away from it?

[pause]

Shelley reads Mary's pages, as she writes them, and encourages her, and makes suggestions. Sometimes, he changes a word, or a sentence. People have argued, ever since, about how much Shelley helped. But the story, the ideas, the characters, and most of the words, were Mary's own. She was the author. And she was proud of it.

[long-pause]

{img:baby-asleep-in-cradle-mother-writing-nearby-candle-night-quiet-room}
She writes in the mornings, while the baby sleeps. And in the afternoons, when it rains. And sometimes, late at night, by candlelight, when the house is quiet. Willmouse sleeps in his cradle, beside her desk. Sometimes he wakes, and cries, and she picks him up, and rocks him, and sings to him, until he sleeps again. And then she goes back to her writing.

[pause]

Mary does not finish the book that summer. It takes her almost a year. She writes the rest of it in England, after they go home. But it was born here, by the lake, in the summer of 1816. In the rain, and the storms, and the long evenings by the fire. In the conversations about life, and death, and electricity. And in the vision of a young woman, lying awake, in the dark, listening to the rain.

[pause]

When the book is finished, it is called Frankenstein, or, The Modern Prometheus. Prometheus was a figure in the old Greek stories, who stole fire from the gods, and gave it to people. And he was punished, terribly, for it. Mary's Victor Frankenstein is a modern Prometheus. He steals the secret of life. And he too is punished, for what he has done.

[long-pause]

{img:geneva-1816-old-town-cathedral-st-pierre-grey-houses-lake-harbour}
On Saturdays, Louise goes to Geneva, to the market, with Élise. They go by boat, with Pierre, across the end of the lake, to the city. Geneva is not a big city, in 1816. About twenty-five thousand people live there. But it is an old, proud, and famous city. It sits at the end of the lake, where the river Rhône flows out, fast, and blue, and cold, on its long journey to the Mediterranean Sea.

[pause]

The old city is built on a hill, with narrow, steep streets, and tall, grey stone houses, packed close together. At the top of the hill, there is the cathedral of Saint Pierre, with its towers, where the great preacher John Calvin preached, nearly three hundred years before. Calvin made Geneva a city of strict Protestant faith, and hard work, and plain living. People called it the Protestant Rome.

[long-pause]

{img:geneva-market-1816-stalls-vegetables-cheese-women-baskets-rain-umbrellas}
Down by the lake, there is the market. Stalls selling vegetables, and fruit, and cheese, and fish from the lake, and bread, and meat, and eggs. And everything else. Cloth, and shoes, and pots, and baskets, and tools. The market women shout their prices, and the customers argue, and bargain. But this year, there is not much to buy. The harvest has been bad, and the prices are very high. Louise looks at the price of bread, and shakes her head.

[pause]

Geneva is famous for watches. In the old town, in the upper floors of the tall houses, there are hundreds of watchmakers, working at their benches, by the windows, to get the best light. They make beautiful watches, and clocks, with tiny gears, and springs, and wheels, and enamelled faces, and gold cases. Geneva watches are sold all over the world. The watchmakers are called cabinotiers, because they work in little rooms, called cabinets, at the top of the houses.

[pause]

{img:watchmaker-at-bench-window-top-floor-geneva-1816-tiny-gears-loupe}
Élise has an uncle who is a watchmaker. Uncle Henri, her mother's brother. He works in a little room, at the top of a tall house, in the old town, with a big window, looking out over the roofs, and the lake. Élise and her mother visit him, after the market. He shows Élise the tiny gears, and springs, through his magnifying glass. They are so small, and so perfect. He says that a watch is like a living thing. Every part must work together, or it will not go.

[pause]

Geneva is also famous for its thinkers, and writers, and scientists. The great philosopher Jean-Jacques Rousseau was born here, about a hundred years before. Byron and Shelley admire Rousseau very much. They have read his books, and they visit the places he wrote about, around the lake. Shelley carries a copy of Rousseau's novel, which is set by the lake, everywhere he goes.

[long-pause]

{img:rhone-river-flowing-out-of-lake-geneva-blue-water-old-bridges-mills}
After the market, Louise and Élise walk along the river Rhône, where it flows out of the lake, through the middle of the city. The water is an extraordinary colour. A deep, clear, brilliant blue, like a jewel. It rushes past, very fast, under the old wooden bridges, and past the mills, and the island in the middle of the river. Byron wrote that the Rhône was the bluest water he had ever seen.

[pause]

Then they meet Pierre, at the harbour, and sail home, across the lake, to Cologny. The city falls away behind them, grey, and solid, and old, under the grey sky. Ahead of them, on the green hillside, they can see the Villa Diodati, with its long balcony, and the little houses of the village, and their own house, below. And the vineyards, wet, and dark, and green, climbing the hill.

[pause]

Élise thinks that in Mary's book, many years later, Victor Frankenstein makes the same journey. Across the lake, from Geneva, to his family's house by the water. And he looks at the mountains, and the lake, and the city, just as Élise is doing now. She does not know this yet, of course. But one day, when she reads the book, she will remember this afternoon. And the grey city, and the blue river, and the green hills, and her mother beside her, in the boat.

[long-pause]

{img:castle-of-chillon-1816-on-rock-in-lake-mountains-behind-boat-approaching}
At the end of June, Byron and Shelley go on a journey, together, around the lake, in their boat. They leave the women behind, and Polidori too, who has hurt his ankle. They take two boatmen, and Byron's servant. They plan to sail all the way around the lake, stopping at the villages, and towns, and places that Rousseau wrote about in his famous novel. It takes about a week.

[pause]

They sail along the southern shore, past villages, and vineyards, and mountains, coming straight down into the water. They stop at little inns, for the night, and eat fish, from the lake, and drink the local wine. They walk in the villages, and talk to the people. Shelley writes long letters, describing everything. Byron writes poetry, in the evenings, in the inns.

[long-pause]

{img:rough-lake-storm-small-boat-waves-near-cliffs-meillerie-dramatic}
Near a village called Meillerie, a sudden storm comes down from the mountains. The wind is very strong, and the waves are high, and the boat is in danger. Water comes over the side. The boatmen struggle with the sail. Shelley cannot swim, and Byron offers to help him, if the boat sinks. But Shelley refuses. He sits calmly, holding onto a locker, and says that he would rather go down with the boat. Luckily, they reach the shore safely. But it was a close call.

[pause]

At the eastern end of the lake, they visit the castle of Chillon. It is an old castle, built on a rock, in the water, close to the shore. It is very beautiful, from the outside, with its towers, and walls, rising straight out of the lake. But inside, deep down, below the level of the water, there are dungeons. Dark, cold rooms, with stone pillars, and iron rings in the walls, where prisoners were once chained.

[pause]

{img:chillon-dungeon-stone-pillars-gothic-vault-light-through-slit-window}
Long ago, a man called François Bonivard was kept prisoner in the dungeon at Chillon, for six years. He was a citizen of Geneva, who had fought for the freedom of the city, against the Duke of Savoy. He was chained to one of the pillars, in the dungeon. People said that he walked round and round the pillar, for six years, until he had worn a path in the stone floor. Visitors can still see the path, they say.

[pause]

Byron is very moved by the story, and the dungeon. He carves his name on one of the pillars. You can still see it today. And a few days later, in an inn, at a town called Ouchy, near Lausanne, during two rainy days, he writes a long poem, called The Prisoner of Chillon. It is about Bonivard, and his brothers, chained in the dungeon, and the sadness of their long imprisonment. It becomes one of his most famous poems.

[long-pause]

On the way back, they stop at Lausanne, and visit the house where the great English historian, Edward Gibbon, wrote the last part of his famous history of the fall of the Roman Empire. They walk in his garden, at night, where he finished his book. Byron picks some leaves from the acacia tree, where Gibbon used to sit, and sends them to his publisher, in London, as a souvenir.

[pause]

{img:two-men-walking-in-moonlit-garden-acacia-tree-lausanne-1816-night}
While the poets are away, Mary stays at the little house, with the baby, and Claire. It is quiet. The rain falls. And Mary writes. Every day, a few more pages. She is glad of the peace. She walks along the shore, in the evenings, with Willmouse in her arms, and looks out over the lake, and wonders where the little boat is now.

[pause]

When they come back to Cologny, at the beginning of July, they are tired, and sunburnt, and happy. Pierre goes down to the harbour, to help them with the boat. He asks them about the storm, near Meillerie. Shelley tells him about it, and says that he thought, for a moment, that it was the end. Pierre shakes his head. He says, I told you. The lake is not a pond.

[pause]

Shelley laughs, and agrees. But six years later, Shelley would drown, in another storm, in another sailing boat, on the sea, off the coast of Italy. He never did learn to swim. But that is still far in the future. In the summer of 1816, he is twenty-three, and full of life, and ideas, and poems. And the lake, for now, has let him go.

[long-pause]

{img:mer-de-glace-glacier-1816-travellers-on-ice-mont-blanc-peaks-clouds}
In the middle of July, Mary, Shelley, and Claire go on a journey to the mountains. To the valley of Chamonix, at the foot of Mont Blanc, the highest mountain in the Alps. It is about eighty kilometres from Geneva. They travel in a carriage, and then on mules, along rough mountain roads, up and up, through deep valleys, and dark forests, and past roaring rivers, and waterfalls. The baby stays at home, with his nurse.

[pause]

The journey is wild, and beautiful, and a little frightening. The road climbs along the edge of deep gorges, with the river roaring far below. The mountains rise up on every side, higher, and higher, with snow on their tops, even in July. There are great waterfalls, falling hundreds of metres, down the cliffs. And there are avalanches, which they hear, far away, like thunder, as the snow crashes down the mountainsides.

[long-pause]

{img:chamonix-valley-1816-village-wooden-chalets-glaciers-above-summer-meadows}
At last, they reach Chamonix. It is a small village, in a long, narrow valley, with wooden houses, and a church, and a few inns, for travellers. Above it, on every side, rise the great mountains. And above them all, Mont Blanc. Huge, and white, and silent, its peak hidden in the clouds. From the valley, they can see the glaciers. Great rivers of ice, coming down from the mountains, between the rocks, almost into the valley itself.

[pause]

The next day, they climb up to the most famous glacier of all, the Mer de Glace. The Sea of Ice. It is a long, wide glacier, high in the mountains, between great peaks of rock. They climb for hours, up a steep path, through pine forests, with a guide, and mules. When they reach the top, they see the glacier, spread out below them. A great frozen river of ice, cracked, and broken, into waves, and ridges, and deep blue crevasses, stretching away, between the mountains.

[pause]

{img:young-woman-standing-at-edge-of-glacier-looking-vast-ice-awe-1816}
Mary is overwhelmed. She has never seen anything so vast, and so wild, and so strange. She writes, later, that it was like a frozen sea, whose waves had been stopped, in the middle of a storm. The silence is enormous. Only the occasional crack, and groan, of the ice, moving, very slowly, down the valley. And the wind, blowing across it. She feels very small, and very far from the rest of the world.

[pause]

The weather, as usual that summer, is bad. It rains, and the clouds come down, and hide the mountains. But sometimes, the clouds part, for a moment, and they see the great white peak of Mont Blanc, shining in the sun, high above them. And then the clouds close again. Shelley writes a long poem about it, called Mont Blanc. About the power of nature, and the mystery of the mountain, and the silence of the snow.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, at the inn, in Chamonix, they sit by the fire, and write letters, and their journals, and talk about what they have seen. Mary thinks about her story. And she decides that the Mer de Glace must be part of it. In her book, Victor Frankenstein climbs up to the glacier, alone, to find peace, after a great sorrow. And there, on the ice, among the mountains, he meets the creature, for the first time since he made it.

[pause]

The creature comes toward him, across the ice, leaping over the crevasses, with superhuman speed. Victor is terrified. But the creature does not attack him. Instead, he asks Victor to listen to him. To listen to his story. And they go together into a little hut, on the mountain, and sit by a fire, and the creature tells Victor everything that has happened to him, since the night he was made. It is one of the most powerful scenes in the book.

[pause]

{img:mountain-hut-fire-two-figures-talking-glacier-outside-snow-falling}
Mary and Shelley and Claire come back to Geneva after about a week, tired, and cold, and full of wonder. They are glad to be back by the lake, in the little house, with the baby. Mary picks up Willmouse, and holds him tightly, and kisses him. And that evening, she sits down at her desk, and writes about the Mer de Glace, and the mountains, and the meeting on the ice. While it is all still fresh in her mind.

[long-pause]

{img:geneva-1816-bread-queue-poor-families-bakery-rain-shawls-hungry}
While the English travellers write their poems, and their stories, the ordinary people of Switzerland are suffering. The cold, wet summer has ruined the harvest. The wheat, and the barley, and the oats, have rotted in the fields. The potatoes are small, and few. The hay for the animals is wet, and mouldy. And everyone knows that the coming winter will be a hard one.

[pause]

The price of bread rises, and rises. By the end of the summer, it costs two or three times as much as usual. Many poor families cannot afford it. They eat less, and less. In some parts of Switzerland, in the mountains, people are so hungry that they eat wild plants, and moss, and the bark of trees. Many people leave Switzerland, and go to America, hoping for a better life.

[long-pause]

{img:village-soup-kitchen-1816-church-women-serving-poor-families-bowls}
In Geneva, the city government buys grain from other countries, and sells it cheaply to the poor, to help them. The churches, and the rich families, set up soup kitchens, where poor people can get a bowl of hot soup, and a piece of bread, every day, for free. Louise helps at the soup kitchen in the village, once a week, with the other women from the church. She peels potatoes, and chops vegetables, and stirs the great pots of soup.

[pause]

The Morets are luckier than many. They have their garden, and their goats, and their chickens. Pierre catches fish in the lake, when the weather allows. And Élise earns a little money, at the villa. So they do not go hungry. But they eat very simply. Soup, and bread, and cheese, and fish, and a few vegetables. And Louise is worried about the winter. The vineyard will give very little wine this year. And wine is the family's main source of money.

[pause]

{img:mother-sharing-bread-with-neighbour-children-doorway-grey-day-kindness}
The family next door, the Dufours, are poorer. Monsieur Dufour is a day labourer, who works in the fields, and the vineyards, for other people. This year, there is very little work, because there is very little harvest. So there is very little money. And they have six children. Louise often takes them a loaf of bread, or a jug of goat's milk, or some eggs. She does not have much to spare. But she says that you cannot let children go hungry, next door.

[pause]

Mary sees the poverty too. She sees the poor people, in the villages, and on the roads, thin, and hungry, and ragged. She sees beggars, in Geneva, and children, asking for bread. It makes her sad, and angry. She and Shelley give money, when they can. Shelley especially is very generous. He gives away money to almost anyone who asks, even when he does not have much himself.

[long-pause]

Some people think that the hunger, and the misery, of that terrible year, found their way into Mary's book. In the book, the creature lives in the woods, alone, cold, and hungry, eating berries, and roots, and nuts, searching for food, and warmth, and shelter. He watches a poor family, in a cottage, who are also hungry, and struggling. And he secretly helps them, by gathering firewood for them, at night, and leaving it at their door.

[pause]

{img:firewood-left-at-cottage-door-dawn-snow-mysterious-kindness-footprints}
It is one of the gentlest parts of the book. The creature, who is so feared, and hated, by everyone who sees him, is kind to the poor family, in secret. He sees how hard their life is, and he wants to help them. The family does not know who is helping them. They call him their good spirit. It is a little moment of kindness, in a dark story.

[pause]

Élise often thinks, later in her life, about the year without a summer. About the cold, and the rain, and the hunger, and the soup kitchen, and her mother's bread for the Dufour children. And about the young English lady, writing her book, in the little house by the lake. She thinks that the hard times made people kinder, in some ways. People helped each other, because they had to. And because it was the right thing to do.

[long-pause]

{img:carriage-leaving-cologny-1816-late-august-luggage-family-waving-vineyard}
At the end of August, the summer by the lake comes to an end. The Shelleys decide to go back to England. They have business to attend to, and money problems, and family problems. And Claire has a secret. She is expecting a baby. Byron's baby. Byron does not want to be with Claire anymore. He has made that very clear. Claire is unhappy, and must go home, with Mary and Shelley.

[pause]

On the twenty-ninth of August, they leave. Their trunks are packed, and loaded onto a carriage. Mary has her manuscript, carefully wrapped, in her bag. The baby is wrapped up warmly, in her arms. They say goodbye to Byron, and Polidori, at the villa. And then they go down the hill, to the carriage, waiting on the road below.

[long-pause]

{img:young-woman-saying-goodbye-to-girl-servant-gift-small-book-doorway}
Élise is there, to say goodbye. Mary has always been kind to her, and has learned a few words of French from her, and taught her a few words of English. Mary gives her a little present. A small book, with a blue cover, of English poems, with Mary's name written inside. Élise cannot read English. But she keeps the book, all her life, and later, she learns to read it.

[pause]

Pierre carries the trunks down to the carriage. Shelley shakes his hand, warmly, and thanks him, for everything. For the boat trips, and the storms, and the safe returns. He gives him a gift of money, and a silver pocket knife. Pierre is very touched. He says that if they ever come back to the lake, he will be their boatman again. Shelley says that he hopes they will come back one day. But they never do.

[pause]

{img:carriage-on-road-along-lake-departing-misty-morning-mountains-behind}
The carriage drives away, along the road beside the lake, toward the north, and France, and England. Élise and Pierre stand by the road, and wave, until it disappears around a bend. Then they walk back up the hill, through the wet vineyard, to their house. The little house by the water, where the Shelleys stayed, is empty now, with its shutters closed. And the path through the vineyard is quiet.

[pause]

Byron stays at the Villa Diodati for a few more weeks. Other friends come to visit him, from England. He goes on another journey, into the mountains, of the Bernese Oberland, with a friend, and writes about the glaciers, and the waterfalls, and the high peaks. And then, in October, he too leaves, and goes south, over the Alps, to Italy. He sends Polidori away, before he leaves. They have quarrelled too often.

[long-pause]

The Villa Diodati is empty again. The drawing room, where they told their ghost stories, is quiet. The fire is cold. The books are gone. The balcony, where they watched the storms, is empty, and covered with fallen leaves. Madame Berthe, the cook, goes back to Geneva. And Élise has no more work at the villa. She goes back to helping her mother, in the house, and the garden.

[pause]

But she misses the English. She misses the noise, and the visitors, and the strange conversations, and the excitement. She misses Mary, with her quiet voice, and her kind eyes, and her baby. She often walks up the path, through the vineyard, to the empty villa, and looks at it, and remembers. The summer of the storms, and the ghost stories, and the English poets.

[pause]

{img:girl-sitting-on-wall-by-vineyard-looking-at-empty-villa-autumn-dusk}
Grandmother Anne says that it is just as well that they have gone. She says that now, the village can go back to normal, and people will stop gossiping, and the tourists will stop looking through their telescopes. But even she misses them, a little. She says that life was more interesting, when the English were here. And the next year, when the tourists come, and ask about the famous poets, she tells them stories. Some of them true.

[long-pause]

{img:late-grape-harvest-cologny-1816-cold-autumn-pickers-baskets-small-grapes}
In the autumn, after the English have gone, it is time for the grape harvest. Usually, in Geneva, the grape harvest is in late September, or early October. It is the happiest time of the year, in the villages, with music, and dancing, and feasting, and the new wine. But in 1816, the grapes are not ripe. They are small, and hard, and green, and sour, because there has been so little sun, all summer.

[pause]

The farmers wait, and wait, hoping for a few weeks of sunshine, to ripen the grapes. But the sunshine does not come. October is cold, and grey, and wet, like the summer. At last, at the end of October, the farmers decide that they cannot wait any longer. The frost is coming. They must pick the grapes now, ripe or not, or lose them altogether. So the harvest begins, very late, in the cold.

[long-pause]

{img:family-picking-grapes-together-wet-terraces-cold-breath-baskets-on-backs}
The whole Moret family works in the vineyard. Pierre, and Louise, and Élise, and Jacques. Even Grandmother Anne, who sits at the end of the rows, sorting the grapes, and throwing away the rotten ones. They pick the grapes, by hand, with small curved knives, and put them in baskets. Then they carry the baskets, on their backs, down the steep slope, to the wine press, in the barn. It is cold, wet, hard work. Their fingers are numb, and sticky with grape juice.

[pause]

Usually, the neighbours help each other with the harvest. They move from vineyard to vineyard, picking each one in turn. And at the end of each day, there is a meal, and singing, and the young people dance. This year, the neighbours still help each other. But there is not much singing. Everyone is too worried, and too cold. The harvest is very small. Perhaps a third of a normal year. And the wine will be thin, and sour.

[pause]

{img:wooden-wine-press-barn-men-turning-screw-grape-juice-flowing-lantern}
In the barn, Pierre and Monsieur Dufour, from next door, press the grapes, in the big wooden wine press. They turn the great wooden screw, slowly, and the juice flows out, into a wooden tub. It is pale, and thin, and does not taste very sweet. Pierre tastes it, and makes a face. He says that this year's wine will be good for making vinegar. Monsieur Dufour laughs, and says that vinegar is better than nothing.

[pause]

But even in a bad year, the harvest brings people together. On the last evening, when the last grapes are picked, the families of the village gather in Pierre's barn. They bring what food they can. Bread, and cheese, and soup, and roasted chestnuts, and a little of last year's wine, which was good. Someone brings a fiddle. And in the end, despite everything, the young people dance, and the old people sing the old harvest songs.

[long-pause]

{img:harvest-supper-barn-lanterns-fiddler-villagers-chestnuts-warmth}
Élise dances with the Dufour boys, and with Jacques, and with her father. Grandmother Anne sings an old song, in the old dialect of Geneva, about the grape harvest, and the new wine, and a girl who loved a boatman. Everyone joins in the chorus. The barn is warm, with lanterns, and the smell of chestnuts, and wine, and wet wool. And outside, the rain falls, softly, on the bare vines.

[pause]

Late in the evening, Pierre steps outside, for a moment, into the cold, dark night. He looks down at the lake, black, and still, below him. And up at the sky. And for the first time in weeks, the clouds have parted. And there are stars. Hundreds of them, bright, and sharp, and cold, over the lake, and the mountains. He calls Louise, and the children, to come and see.

[pause]

They stand together, outside the barn, and look up at the stars. Nobody says anything. After the long, dark, strange summer, the stars feel like a promise. That the sun will come back. That next year will be better. And slowly, it was. In 1817, the summer was kinder, and the harvests began to recover. And the year without a summer became a story, that the old people told the young, for the rest of their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:frankenstein-first-edition-1818-three-small-volumes-anonymous-title-page}
Mary finished her book in England, in the spring of 1817. She had written it, and rewritten it, and copied it out, carefully, by hand. Shelley helped her to find a publisher. It was not easy. Several publishers refused it. At last, a small publisher in London agreed to publish it. On the first of January, 1818, Frankenstein was published. Mary was twenty years old.

[pause]

The book was published without the author's name on it. That was quite common, in those days. Many books were published anonymously. And there was another reason. Mary was a young woman, and a young woman writing a frightening novel, about science, and death, and the creation of life, would have shocked many people. So the book appeared, with no name. Only a dedication, to her father, William Godwin.

[long-pause]

{img:readers-in-1818-london-drawing-room-reading-novel-candlelight-intrigued}
At first, many people thought that Shelley had written it. Some reviewers praised it. They said it was original, and powerful, and full of imagination. Others hated it. They said it was horrible, and disgusting, and dangerous, and that it would give people nightmares. But people read it. And talked about it. And the book slowly became famous.

[pause]

Let us remember the story, gently, as Mary told it. It begins far in the north, among the ice, near the North Pole. A ship is trapped in the ice, and the captain, a young explorer called Walton, sees a strange sight. A huge figure, on a sledge, pulled by dogs, racing across the ice. And the next day, they find another man, on the ice, half frozen, and exhausted. They bring him on board. His name is Victor Frankenstein. And he tells them his story.

[pause]

{img:ship-trapped-in-arctic-ice-sledge-on-ice-distance-pale-sky-quiet}
He tells how he grew up in Geneva, happy, and loved. How he went to university, and became obsessed with the secret of life. How he made the creature, and brought it to life, on a dreary night of November, and then ran away from it, in horror. And how the creature, abandoned, and alone, wandered through the world, and learned to speak, and to read, and to think, and to feel. And how everyone he met was afraid of him.

[pause]

The creature asks Victor to make him a companion. Someone like him, so that he will not be alone. At first, Victor agrees. But then he changes his mind, and destroys the companion, before it is finished. And the creature, in his grief, and anger, turns against Victor, and against everyone Victor loves. It is a sad, dark story. Of loneliness, and rejection, and revenge, and regret.

[long-pause]

At the end, Victor and the creature chase each other across the world, to the frozen north. Victor passes away, on the ship, exhausted, and broken. And the creature comes on board, and sees his creator lying there. And he weeps. He says that he never wanted to be bad. He only wanted to be loved. And then he goes away, across the ice, into the darkness. And he is never seen again.

[pause]

{img:lone-figure-walking-away-across-ice-floes-twilight-arctic-sad-calm}
That is the end of Mary's story. It is not really a horror story, as many people think today. It is a story about responsibility, and loneliness, and the need for love. About what happens when we create something, and do not care for it. About science, and its power, and its dangers. And about what it means to be human. These questions are still important, today, perhaps more than ever.

[pause]

In 1823, a second edition was published, with Mary's name on it, at last. And in 1831, a third edition, revised by Mary, with her famous introduction, about the summer by the lake, and the ghost story competition, and her waking dream. From then on, everyone knew that Mary Shelley had written Frankenstein. And it has never been out of print since.

[pause]

Mary kept a copy of the first edition all her life. Three small volumes, bound in grey boards, with plain paper labels. Today, copies of that first edition are very rare, and very precious. Only a few hundred were printed. When one of them is sold, it costs more than a house. But in 1818, it cost a few shillings, and nobody knew how famous it would become.

[long-pause]

{img:mary-shelley-older-portrait-1840s-dark-dress-writing-desk-london}
In December 1816, a few months after they came back from Geneva, Mary and Shelley were married, in London. It was a quiet wedding, in a church. Mary's father was there, and he was happy, at last. The next year, Mary had another baby, a little girl, called Clara. And in 1818, the family moved to Italy, where Shelley hoped that the warm climate would be good for his health.

[pause]

Italy was beautiful. But there was great sadness there too. Both of Mary's young children, Clara and William, passed away, in Italy, of fevers, within a year of each other. Mary was heartbroken. For a long time, she could hardly bear to live. Then, in 1819, she had another son, Percy Florence. He grew up healthy, and strong, and lived a long life. He was a great comfort to her.

[long-pause]

{img:italian-coast-lerici-1822-bay-sailing-boats-villa-calm-evening}
In 1822, the Shelleys were living in a house by the sea, on the coast of Italy, near Lerici. Shelley loved sailing, as he had on Lake Geneva. He had a new boat built, and named it after one of Byron's poems. In July, he sailed along the coast, to visit Byron, and some friends. On the way back, a sudden storm came up, at sea. The boat sank, and Shelley was drowned, with his friend, and a boy who sailed with them. He was twenty-nine years old.

[pause]

Byron passed away two years later, in 1824, in Greece, where he had gone to help the Greeks, in their war for freedom against the Turks. He caught a fever, and died, at the age of thirty-six. He became a hero in Greece, and is still honoured there today. Polidori had passed away, three years earlier, in London, at the age of only twenty-five. Of all the friends at the Villa Diodati, in that summer of 1816, only Mary and Claire lived to grow old.

[pause]

{img:quiet-cemetery-bournemouth-church-gravestone-flowers-gentle-light}
Mary went back to England, with her little son. She was a widow, at twenty-four. She had very little money. So she worked, as a writer, to support herself, and her son. She wrote more novels, and stories, and travel books, and biographies. One of her novels, The Last Man, is about the end of the human race, in a great plague, far in the future. She also edited Shelley's poems, and made sure that the world remembered him, as a great poet.

[pause]

She never married again. She said that she had been married to a poet, and that was enough. She lived quietly, in London, with her son. When he grew up, he married a kind woman, who loved Mary, and looked after her. Mary passed away in 1851, at the age of fifty-three. She was buried in a churchyard, in Bournemouth, by the sea, with her mother and father, who were moved there to be with her.

[long-pause]

Claire lived the longest of them all. She had her baby, a little girl, in January 1817, and called her Allegra. Byron took the little girl to Italy, to bring her up. But Allegra passed away, of a fever, when she was five. Claire never forgave Byron. She lived a long, travelling life, as a governess, in Russia, and Germany, and Italy. She passed away in Florence, in 1879, at the age of eighty. She was the last one, of that famous summer, by the lake.

[pause]

{img:older-woman-in-florence-1870s-garden-chair-remembering-quietly-sunlit}
And the Morets, in Cologny? Pierre went on sailing his boat, on the lake, for many years. He carried many more English tourists, who came to see the Villa Diodati, and the places where the famous poets had stayed. He always told them about the storm, near the harbour, and the gold coin that Byron gave him. Jacques became a boatman too, and later, the owner of three boats, and a small hotel.

[pause]

Élise married a young watchmaker, from Geneva, who worked with her Uncle Henri. They lived in the old town, in a tall house, with a view of the lake. She learned to read English, slowly, with the little blue book that Mary gave her. And many years later, she read Frankenstein, in English, from beginning to end. When she came to the parts about Geneva, and the lake, and the storms, and the mountains, she cried a little. Because she had been there, that summer, when it all began.

[long-pause]

{img:theatre-1823-london-stage-play-presumption-audience-gaslight-creature-costume}
Frankenstein did not stay just a book. Only five years after it was published, in 1823, it was turned into a play, in London. It was called Presumption, or the Fate of Frankenstein. It was a great success. Mary went to see it, and enjoyed it very much. She was amused that the creature, on the stage, did not speak at all. In her book, he speaks beautifully. But on the stage, he only grunted, and waved his arms.

[pause]

After that, there were many more plays, all through the nineteenth century. And then, in the twentieth century, came the films. The first Frankenstein film was made in 1910, by Thomas Edison's film company. It was a short, silent film, only about twelve minutes long. But the most famous film was made in Hollywood, in 1931. It made the story famous, all over the world. And it created the image of the creature that most people know today.

[long-pause]

{img:classic-film-studio-1931-lightning-laboratory-set-gentle-black-white-style}
In that film, the creature is very tall, and green-grey, with a flat head, and heavy eyelids, and bolts in his neck, and big boots. He walks stiffly, with his arms out in front of him. He does not speak. He grunts, and groans. And he is brought to life in a laboratory, in a stone tower, during a thunderstorm, with lightning, and sparks, and strange machines. None of this is in Mary's book. But it became so famous that most people think it is.

[pause]

Many people today call the creature Frankenstein. But in the book, Frankenstein is the name of the scientist, Victor, who made the creature. The creature has no name. Victor calls him the creature, or the daemon, or the wretch. He never gives him a name. And that is part of the sadness of the story. The creature is not even given a name, by the one who made him.

[pause]

{img:bookshelf-many-editions-frankenstein-different-languages-library-warm-light}
Since then, there have been hundreds of films, and plays, and books, and cartoons, and comics, based on Frankenstein. Some of them are frightening, some are funny, some are sad, and some are silly. The creature has met Dracula, and the Wolf Man, and Abbott and Costello. He has been in cartoons for children. He appears every year, at Halloween, on decorations, and costumes, and sweets. He is one of the most famous figures in the world.

[pause]

Mary's book has been translated into dozens of languages, and read by millions of people. It is studied in schools, and universities, all over the world. Many people think that it is the first science fiction novel ever written. A story about what science might be able to do, and what might happen if it does. Some of the greatest writers of science fiction say that Mary Shelley was their great ancestor.

[long-pause]

{img:modern-laboratory-scientist-thinking-ethics-calm-light-window-gentle}
Today, when scientists talk about new inventions, that could change life itself, people often mention Frankenstein. When scientists work with genes, or with artificial intelligence, or with creating new forms of life, people ask the same questions that Mary asked, more than two hundred years ago. What are we making? What do we owe to it? Will we look after it? Or will we run away from it, as Victor did?

[pause]

Mary could not have imagined the modern world, with its computers, and its machines, and its science. But she understood something that is still true. That the power to create brings great responsibility. And that every creature, every person, whatever they look like, needs love, and care, and kindness, and a place to belong.

[pause]

That is the message of the book that was born on a stormy night, by a lake, in the year without a summer. Two hundred years later, it is still being read, and still being told, and still being argued about. Not bad, for a ghost story, written by a girl of eighteen, to win a competition, on a rainy evening in Switzerland.

[long-pause]

{img:today-lake-geneva-jet-deau-fountain-city-sunny-boats-mont-blanc}
Today, Lake Geneva is still one of the most beautiful places in Europe. The lake is still long, and deep, and blue, between the green hills, and the vineyards, and the mountains. On a clear day, you can still see Mont Blanc, white, and shining, far away, above the clouds. And the boats still sail on the lake, in the summer, white sails on the blue water, just as they did in 1816.

[pause]

Geneva is a much bigger city now, of about two hundred thousand people. It is an international city, home to many great world organisations. The Red Cross was founded here, in 1863. And the United Nations has its European headquarters here. People from every country in the world live and work in Geneva. And in the middle of the lake, there is a great fountain, the Jet d'Eau, which shoots water a hundred and forty metres into the air.

[long-pause]

{img:today-villa-diodati-cologny-private-house-hillside-trees-lake-view}
The Villa Diodati is still there, on the hillside, at Cologny. It looks much as it did in 1816, with its pale walls, and its long balcony, looking out over the lake. It is a private house now, and visitors cannot go inside. But you can walk past it, on the road, and look at it, through the gates, and imagine the young poets on the balcony, watching the storms. There is a small plaque, by the gate, which tells its story.

[pause]

The little house below the villa, where the Shelleys stayed, Maison Chapuis, is gone. It was pulled down, long ago. But the vineyard between the two houses is still there, on the steep slope. And the path through the vines, which Mary and Shelley walked up and down every day, is still there too. Cologny is now one of the most expensive and exclusive places to live, in the whole of Switzerland.

[pause]

{img:today-chillon-castle-visitors-lake-shore-autumn-mountains-reflection}
The castle of Chillon still stands on its rock, at the eastern end of the lake. It is one of the most visited castles in Switzerland. Visitors can go down into the dungeon, and see the stone pillars, and the iron rings, where Bonivard was chained. And on one of the pillars, they can see the name of Byron, carved in the stone. Or a name that people believe Byron carved. Many visitors take a photograph of it.

[pause]

In Chamonix, the glaciers are still there. But they are much smaller than they were in 1816. In the cold years of the early nineteenth century, they reached far down into the valley. Since then, as the climate has warmed, they have melted, and shrunk, and drawn back up into the mountains. The Mer de Glace is much shorter, and thinner, than when Mary saw it. Every year, it loses a little more. Visitors can take a little red mountain train up to see it, from Chamonix.

[long-pause]

In 2016, two hundred years after the famous summer, there were celebrations, and exhibitions, in Geneva, about Frankenstein, and Mary Shelley, and the Villa Diodati. A great library near Geneva held an exhibition, with rare books, and letters, and manuscripts. There were films, and talks, and plays, and readings. And people walked along the lake, to Cologny, to see the villa, and remember the summer of 1816.

[pause]

Near the old city of Geneva, there is now a statue of Frankenstein's creature. It is not a frightening statue. It shows the creature as he is in the book. A big, sad figure, standing alone, in a quiet square, looking a little lost. Children sometimes climb on it, and people take photographs with it. It seems to fit, somehow. The creature, who was born in Mary's imagination, by the lake, has come home to Geneva.

[pause]

{img:today-cologny-vineyard-autumn-grape-harvest-pickers-lake-below-sunshine}
And every autumn, in Cologny, the grapes are still harvested, on the steep slopes, above the lake. The pickers still carry the baskets down the hill, to the press. The new wine is still tasted, with bread, and cheese, and roasted chestnuts. And on a cold evening, when the storm clouds gather over the mountains, and the lightning flickers over the lake, some people in Cologny still remember the summer when Frankenstein was born.

[long-pause]

{img:lake-geneva-1816-evening-storm-clearing-last-light-villa-windows-glowing}
So we leave the lake tonight, in the summer of 1816, as the evening falls. The rain has stopped, for a while. The clouds are lifting, over the mountains, and in the west, the sky is glowing, with a strange, deep red, and orange, and gold, from the faraway volcano. The lake is still, and dark, and shining. And the first stars are coming out, between the clouds.

[pause]

Up on the hill, the windows of the Villa Diodati are glowing, with candlelight. The friends are gathered in the drawing room, by the fire. Byron is reading aloud, from a book of ghost stories. Shelley is lying on the sofa, listening, with his eyes closed. Polidori is writing in his diary. Claire is sitting by the window, looking out at the lake. And Mary is sitting in the corner, quietly, listening to everything.

[long-pause]

{img:path-through-vineyard-dusk-lantern-girl-walking-home-cottage-lights}
Down the path, through the wet vineyard, Élise is walking home, with her lantern swinging. Her day's work at the villa is finished. She is tired, but happy. She can hear the voices, from the villa, behind her, and the soft sound of the lake, in front of her. And ahead of her, she can see the warm light in the window of her own house, where her family is waiting, with supper.

[pause]

In the Morets' kitchen, the fire is burning, and the soup is bubbling, in the pot. Louise is cutting bread, and cheese. Pierre is sitting by the fire, with his pipe, which has gone out again. Grandmother Anne is knitting, in her chair. Jacques is mending a fishing line. When Élise comes in, they all look up, and smile. And she sits down, and tells them about her day.

[long-pause]

{img:cottage-window-night-family-by-fire-lake-dark-outside-distant-thunder}
After supper, they sit together by the fire, for a while. Grandmother Anne tells an old story, about a water spirit, who lived in the lake, long ago, and helped the fishermen in storms. Jacques falls asleep, with his head on the table. Élise helps her mother with the dishes. And then, one by one, they go up to bed, under the steep roof, and the house grows quiet.

[pause]

Outside, far away, over the mountains, there is a faint flicker of lightning, and a low, gentle rumble of thunder. Another storm is coming, slowly, across the lake. But it is far away, still. And the house is warm, and safe. The rain begins again, softly, on the roof. And the lake laps quietly on the shore, below the vineyard, in the dark. And somewhere up on the hill, by candlelight, a young woman of eighteen is still awake, listening to the rain, and thinking about a story.
