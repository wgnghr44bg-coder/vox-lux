While the English travellers write their poems, and their stories, the ordinary people of Switzerland are suffering. The cold, wet summer has ruined the harvest. The wheat, and the barley, and the oats, have rotted in the fields. The potatoes are small, and few. The hay for the animals is wet, and mouldy. And everyone knows that the coming winter will be a hard one.

The price of bread rises, and rises. By the end of the summer, it costs two or three times as much as usual. Many poor families cannot afford it. They eat less, and less. In some parts of Switzerland, in the mountains, people are so hungry that they eat wild plants, and moss, and the bark of trees. Many people leave Switzerland, and go to America, hoping for a better life.

--

{img:village-soup-kitchen-1816-church-women-serving-poor-families-bowls}
In Geneva, the city government buys grain from other countries, and sells it cheaply to the poor, to help them. The churches, and the rich families, set up soup kitchens, where poor people can get a bowl of hot soup, and a piece of bread, every day, for free. Louise helps at the soup kitchen in the village, once a week, with the other women from the church. She peels potatoes, and chops vegetables, and stirs the great pots of soup.

The Morets are luckier than many. They have their garden, and their goats, and their chickens. Pierre catches fish in the lake, when the weather allows. And Élise earns a little money, at the villa. So they do not go hungry. But they eat very simply. Soup, and bread, and cheese, and fish, and a few vegetables. And Louise is worried about the winter. The vineyard will give very little wine this year. And wine is the family's main source of money.

{img:mother-sharing-bread-with-neighbour-children-doorway-grey-day-kindness}
The family next door, the Dufours, are poorer. Monsieur Dufour is a day labourer, who works in the fields, and the vineyards, for other people. This year, there is very little work, because there is very little harvest. So there is very little money. And they have six children. Louise often takes them a loaf of bread, or a jug of goat's milk, or some eggs. She does not have much to spare. But she says that you cannot let children go hungry, next door.

Mary sees the poverty too. She sees the poor people, in the villages, and on the roads, thin, and hungry, and ragged. She sees beggars, in Geneva, and children, asking for bread. It makes her sad, and angry. She and Shelley give money, when they can. Shelley especially is very generous. He gives away money to almost anyone who asks, even when he does not have much himself.

--

Some people think that the hunger, and the misery, of that terrible year, found their way into Mary's book. In the book, the creature lives in the woods, alone, cold, and hungry, eating berries, and roots, and nuts, searching for food, and warmth, and shelter. He watches a poor family, in a cottage, who are also hungry, and struggling. And he secretly helps them, by gathering firewood for them, at night, and leaving it at their door.

{img:firewood-left-at-cottage-door-dawn-snow-mysterious-kindness-footprints}
It is one of the gentlest parts of the book. The creature, who is so feared, and hated, by everyone who sees him, is kind to the poor family, in secret. He sees how hard their life is, and he wants to help them. The family does not know who is helping them. They call him their good spirit. It is a little moment of kindness, in a dark story.

Élise often thinks, later in her life, about the year without a summer. About the cold, and the rain, and the hunger, and the soup kitchen, and her mother's bread for the Dufour children. And about the young English lady, writing her book, in the little house by the lake. She thinks that the hard times made people kinder, in some ways. People helped each other, because they had to. And because it was the right thing to do.
