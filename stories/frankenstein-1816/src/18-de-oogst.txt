In the autumn, after the English have gone, it is time for the grape harvest. Usually, in Geneva, the grape harvest is in late September, or early October. It is the happiest time of the year, in the villages, with music, and dancing, and feasting, and the new wine. But in 1816, the grapes are not ripe. They are small, and hard, and green, and sour, because there has been so little sun, all summer.

The farmers wait, and wait, hoping for a few weeks of sunshine, to ripen the grapes. But the sunshine does not come. October is cold, and grey, and wet, like the summer. At last, at the end of October, the farmers decide that they cannot wait any longer. The frost is coming. They must pick the grapes now, ripe or not, or lose them altogether. So the harvest begins, very late, in the cold.

--

{img:family-picking-grapes-together-wet-terraces-cold-breath-baskets-on-backs}
The whole Moret family works in the vineyard. Pierre, and Louise, and Élise, and Jacques. Even Grandmother Anne, who sits at the end of the rows, sorting the grapes, and throwing away the rotten ones. They pick the grapes, by hand, with small curved knives, and put them in baskets. Then they carry the baskets, on their backs, down the steep slope, to the wine press, in the barn. It is cold, wet, hard work. Their fingers are numb, and sticky with grape juice.

Usually, the neighbours help each other with the harvest. They move from vineyard to vineyard, picking each one in turn. And at the end of each day, there is a meal, and singing, and the young people dance. This year, the neighbours still help each other. But there is not much singing. Everyone is too worried, and too cold. The harvest is very small. Perhaps a third of a normal year. And the wine will be thin, and sour.

{img:wooden-wine-press-barn-men-turning-screw-grape-juice-flowing-lantern}
In the barn, Pierre and Monsieur Dufour, from next door, press the grapes, in the big wooden wine press. They turn the great wooden screw, slowly, and the juice flows out, into a wooden tub. It is pale, and thin, and does not taste very sweet. Pierre tastes it, and makes a face. He says that this year's wine will be good for making vinegar. Monsieur Dufour laughs, and says that vinegar is better than nothing.

But even in a bad year, the harvest brings people together. On the last evening, when the last grapes are picked, the families of the village gather in Pierre's barn. They bring what food they can. Bread, and cheese, and soup, and roasted chestnuts, and a little of last year's wine, which was good. Someone brings a fiddle. And in the end, despite everything, the young people dance, and the old people sing the old harvest songs.

--

{img:harvest-supper-barn-lanterns-fiddler-villagers-chestnuts-warmth}
Élise dances with the Dufour boys, and with Jacques, and with her father. Grandmother Anne sings an old song, in the old dialect of Geneva, about the grape harvest, and the new wine, and a girl who loved a boatman. Everyone joins in the chorus. The barn is warm, with lanterns, and the smell of chestnuts, and wine, and wet wool. And outside, the rain falls, softly, on the bare vines.

Late in the evening, Pierre steps outside, for a moment, into the cold, dark night. He looks down at the lake, black, and still, below him. And up at the sky. And for the first time in weeks, the clouds have parted. And there are stars. Hundreds of them, bright, and sharp, and cold, over the lake, and the mountains. He calls Louise, and the children, to come and see.

They stand together, outside the barn, and look up at the stars. Nobody says anything. After the long, dark, strange summer, the stars feel like a promise. That the sun will come back. That next year will be better. And slowly, it was. In 1817, the summer was kinder, and the harvests began to recover. And the year without a summer became a story, that the old people told the young, for the rest of their lives.
