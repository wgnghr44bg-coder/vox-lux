The Villa Diodati stands on the hillside above the lake, at Cologny, surrounded by gardens, and vineyards, and trees. It is a large, square house, of pale stone, with tall windows, and a wide balcony, supported by columns, running all along the front, looking out over the lake. From the balcony, there is a wonderful view. The whole lake, stretching away, to the east, and the city of Geneva, to the west, and the mountains beyond.

The house was built about a hundred years before, by a family called Diodati, who were famous in Geneva. A long time ago, the English poet John Milton, who wrote the great poem Paradise Lost, had visited a member of this family, in Geneva. Byron liked that. He felt that he was following in Milton's footsteps.

--

{img:villa-interior-1816-drawing-room-fireplace-books-piled-candles-chairs}
Inside, the villa has high ceilings, and big rooms, with polished wooden floors, and tall windows, and fireplaces. There is a drawing room, where the friends gather in the evenings. A dining room, and a library, and bedrooms, upstairs. Byron has filled it with his books, and papers, and his things. There are books everywhere. On the tables, on the chairs, on the floor, piled up in corners.

Élise works in the kitchen, at the back of the house, with the cook, Madame Berthe, who is from Geneva. The kitchen is warm, and busy, and smells of soup, and bread, and roasting meat. Byron eats very little, because he is always worried about getting fat. Sometimes, he lives for days on biscuits, and soda water, and potatoes with vinegar. Madame Berthe thinks this is very foolish. She says that a man needs proper food.

{img:kitchen-girl-carrying-tray-up-servants-stairs-candle-villa-evening}
But the guests eat well. Shelley does not eat meat. He is a vegetarian, which is very unusual, in 1816. He eats bread, and vegetables, and fruit, and drinks water, or tea. Mary eats what she likes. Polidori eats a great deal. Claire eats very little, because she is in love. Élise carries the trays up and down the stairs, and listens to their talk, although she understands very little English. She is learning a few words, though. Yes, and no, and thank you, and goodnight.

Byron keeps animals, too. He loves animals. At the villa, he has a dog, and a few other pets. In England, he had kept many more. Dogs, and cats, and birds, and a fox, and a bear, which he kept at university, because the rules said that students could not keep dogs. Jacques is very disappointed when Élise tells him there is no bear at the villa. But he is pleased to hear about the dog.

--

The villa is often noisy, and busy. People come and go. The friends talk, and argue, and laugh, until late at night. Byron writes in the night, and sleeps in the morning. Shelley writes in the morning, and walks in the afternoon. Polidori writes his diary, and practises medicine, on anyone who will let him. Mary reads, and writes letters, and looks after the baby. Claire copies out Byron's poems, in her neat handwriting, so that they can be sent to his publisher in London.

{img:view-from-villa-balcony-lake-geneva-storm-clouds-lightning-distance}
In the evenings, when the weather is fine, they sit on the balcony, and watch the sun set over the lake, and the mountains. But this summer, the weather is not often fine. Mostly, it rains. And then they sit inside, by the fire, in the drawing room, and talk. And outside, the rain falls on the vineyards, and the lake, and the storms roll in from the mountains.

Élise walks home every evening, down the path through the vineyard, to her family's house. Sometimes, if it is late, Pierre comes to meet her, with a lantern. She tells her family everything she has seen, and heard, at the villa. Grandmother Anne listens, and shakes her head, and says that the English are all mad. But she always wants to hear more.
