Mary, Shelley, Claire and the baby arrived in Geneva in the middle of May, 1816. They stayed at first in a hotel, at Sécheron, just outside the city, on the shore of the lake. It was a big, comfortable hotel, popular with English travellers. From the windows, they could see the lake, and the boats, and the mountains beyond. When the weather was fine, which was not often, it was very beautiful.

For the first few days, there was some sunshine. Mary wrote that the weather was delightful, and that she felt as happy as a new-fledged bird. She and Shelley went out on the lake, in a little boat, in the evenings, and watched the sun set behind the mountains. They walked along the shore, and in the woods, and picked flowers. And they waited for Lord Byron.

--

{img:lord-byron-1816-arriving-huge-black-carriage-napoleon-copy-servants}
Byron arrived about ten days later, at the end of May. He came in an enormous black travelling carriage, which was a copy of the carriage of the emperor Napoleon. It had a bed in it, and a library, and a place for eating, and everything a gentleman could need on a long journey. Byron travelled with servants, and his young doctor, and many trunks, and boxes, and books. Everyone in the hotel came out to look.

Lord Byron was twenty-eight years old. He was the most famous poet in Europe. His long poems, about romantic heroes, and faraway lands, were read by everyone, and he was admired, and talked about, everywhere. He was very handsome, with dark curly hair, and pale skin, and a beautiful face. He had been born with a twisted foot, and walked with a slight limp. He was proud, and witty, and moody, and charming, and difficult.

{img:two-poets-meeting-lakeside-1816-byron-shelley-shaking-hands-boats}
He had left England in a hurry, in April, after a great scandal, about his marriage, and his private life. His wife had left him, with their baby daughter. Many people in London had turned against him. He would never go back to England again. He was bitter, and sad, under his proud manner. But he was also glad to be free, and to travel, and to write.

Byron and Shelley met for the first time, on the shore of the lake, on the twenty-seventh of May. They liked each other at once. Byron admired Shelley's mind, and his kindness. Shelley admired Byron's poetry, and his wit. They were very different. Byron was worldly, and cynical, and liked to laugh at everything. Shelley was idealistic, and serious, and believed that the world could be made better. But they became close friends.

--

With Byron came his doctor, John Polidori. He was only twenty years old, the son of an Italian father and an English mother. He was clever, and had become a doctor very young. Byron's publisher had paid him to write a diary of the journey. Polidori was handsome, and eager, and wanted very much to be a writer too. But he was also clumsy, and touchy, and easily hurt, and Byron often teased him.

{img:young-doctor-polidori-1816-writing-diary-hotel-room-candle-eager}
For a few weeks, they all stayed near each other, at Sécheron. They met every day, and dined together, and went out on the lake together, in a boat, in the evenings. Then they decided to move out of the hotel, and rent houses, across the lake, on the southern shore, at Cologny. Byron rented the Villa Diodati, a large and elegant house, on the hillside. And the Shelleys rented a smaller house, called Maison Chapuis, just below it, down by the water.

Between the two houses, there was a vineyard, on the steep slope. And a path, through the vines, which they walked up and down, every day, and every evening. Élise saw them often, on that path. The young English lady, with her red-gold hair, and the baby in her arms. And the tall, thin poet, with his wild hair, talking, and waving his arms, as he walked. She thought they looked very much in love.

In the evenings, Élise sometimes sees the lights of the two houses, from her own window. The big windows of the villa, up on the hill, glowing yellow, until very late. And the smaller windows of the little house, below, by the water. She wonders what the English are talking about, so late at night. Her grandmother says that it is probably nonsense. But Élise is not so sure.
