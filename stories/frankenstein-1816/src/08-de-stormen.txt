The storms of that summer are famous. They come, again and again, in the evenings, and at night. Great black clouds pile up over the mountains, at the eastern end of the lake. The air grows heavy, and still, and strange. The birds go quiet. Then there is a rumble of thunder, far away, rolling around the mountains. And then the storm sweeps down the lake, with wind, and rain, and lightning.

The lightning over the lake is extraordinary. Byron wrote that he had never seen anything like it. Great flashes, lighting up the whole lake, and the mountains, and the sky, for a moment, as bright as day. Then darkness. Then another flash. Sometimes, there are several storms at once, in different parts of the sky, flashing at each other, across the lake, like great giants, fighting with fire.

{img:young-people-watching-storm-from-villa-balcony-wrapped-in-coats-1816}
The young people at the Villa Diodati watch the storms from the balcony, wrapped in their coats, and shawls, with the rain blowing in their faces. They are thrilled by them. They are all romantics, who love the wild, and powerful, and sublime things of nature. Mountains, and storms, and oceans, and waterfalls. Things that make you feel small, and full of wonder, and a little afraid. They call this feeling the sublime.

Byron writes a poem about one of the storms, in his long poem, Childe Harold. He writes about the thunder leaping from peak to peak, among the mountains, and every mountain finding a voice, and the night being glorious, and the lake shining in the lightning. It is one of his most famous passages. Shelley writes poems too, and letters, about the storms, and the mountains, and the power of nature.

{img:lightning-strike-old-oak-tree-hillside-sparks-night-rain-dramatic-calm}
In the village below, the Morets do not find the storms so thrilling. For them, the storms mean danger. Danger to the boats, and the vineyards, and the houses, and the crops. Hail can destroy a whole vineyard, in a few minutes. Lightning can set fire to a barn, or a house. The wind can tear the roofs off. And the endless rain is rotting the grapes on the vines, and flooding the fields.

When a storm comes, Pierre goes down to the harbour, to check that the boats are tied up safely. Louise brings the goats, and the chickens, into the shed. Grandmother Anne lights a candle, and says a prayer. And the children sit by the window, and watch the lightning, and count the seconds between the flash, and the thunder, to see how far away the storm is. Jacques says that it is like the sky is fighting.

--

One night, in June, lightning strikes an old oak tree, on the hill above the village. There is a great flash, and a crack, like a cannon, and the whole house shakes. In the morning, the family goes up to look. The great old tree has been split in two, from top to bottom, and blackened, and burned. Smoke is still rising from it. Jacques has never seen anything like it. He touches the burned wood, and it is still warm.

{img:split-blackened-oak-tree-morning-after-storm-family-looking-mist}
Many years later, when he read Mary's book, Jacques found something in it that made him stop, and remember. In the book, the young hero, Victor Frankenstein, tells how, when he was a boy, near Geneva, he saw a great old oak tree struck by lightning, in a storm, and blasted to pieces. And how it made him wonder about the power of electricity, and of nature. Jacques wondered if Mary had seen the same tree.

After the storm, the sky clears. The air is fresh, and clean, and smells of wet earth, and pine. The lake is calm again, and shining. The mountains are washed, and sharp, and clear. And for a few hours, the sun shines, warm, on the wet vineyards. Everyone comes out of their houses, and breathes, and looks at the sky. And then, by evening, the clouds begin to gather again, over the mountains.
