Frankenstein did not stay just a book. Only five years after it was published, in 1823, it was turned into a play, in London. It was called Presumption, or the Fate of Frankenstein. It was a great success. Mary went to see it, and enjoyed it very much. She was amused that the creature, on the stage, did not speak at all. In her book, he speaks beautifully. But on the stage, he only grunted, and waved his arms.

After that, there were many more plays, all through the nineteenth century. And then, in the twentieth century, came the films. The first Frankenstein film was made in 1910, by Thomas Edison's film company. It was a short, silent film, only about twelve minutes long. But the most famous film was made in Hollywood, in 1931. It made the story famous, all over the world. And it created the image of the creature that most people know today.

--

{img:classic-film-studio-1931-lightning-laboratory-set-gentle-black-white-style}
In that film, the creature is very tall, and green-grey, with a flat head, and heavy eyelids, and bolts in his neck, and big boots. He walks stiffly, with his arms out in front of him. He does not speak. He grunts, and groans. And he is brought to life in a laboratory, in a stone tower, during a thunderstorm, with lightning, and sparks, and strange machines. None of this is in Mary's book. But it became so famous that most people think it is.

Many people today call the creature Frankenstein. But in the book, Frankenstein is the name of the scientist, Victor, who made the creature. The creature has no name. Victor calls him the creature, or the daemon, or the wretch. He never gives him a name. And that is part of the sadness of the story. The creature is not even given a name, by the one who made him.

{img:bookshelf-many-editions-frankenstein-different-languages-library-warm-light}
Since then, there have been hundreds of films, and plays, and books, and cartoons, and comics, based on Frankenstein. Some of them are frightening, some are funny, some are sad, and some are silly. The creature has met Dracula, and the Wolf Man, and Abbott and Costello. He has been in cartoons for children. He appears every year, at Halloween, on decorations, and costumes, and sweets. He is one of the most famous figures in the world.

Mary's book has been translated into dozens of languages, and read by millions of people. It is studied in schools, and universities, all over the world. Many people think that it is the first science fiction novel ever written. A story about what science might be able to do, and what might happen if it does. Some of the greatest writers of science fiction say that Mary Shelley was their great ancestor.

--

{img:modern-laboratory-scientist-thinking-ethics-calm-light-window-gentle}
Today, when scientists talk about new inventions, that could change life itself, people often mention Frankenstein. When scientists work with genes, or with artificial intelligence, or with creating new forms of life, people ask the same questions that Mary asked, more than two hundred years ago. What are we making? What do we owe to it? Will we look after it? Or will we run away from it, as Victor did?

Mary could not have imagined the modern world, with its computers, and its machines, and its science. But she understood something that is still true. That the power to create brings great responsibility. And that every creature, every person, whatever they look like, needs love, and care, and kindness, and a place to belong.

That is the message of the book that was born on a stormy night, by a lake, in the year without a summer. Two hundred years later, it is still being read, and still being told, and still being argued about. Not bad, for a ghost story, written by a girl of eighteen, to win a competition, on a rainy evening in Switzerland.
