Mary Godwin is eighteen years old, in the summer of 1816. She is small, and slim, with pale skin, and reddish-golden hair, and large, thoughtful eyes. She is quiet, and serious, and very clever. She reads all the time. Books of history, and philosophy, and science, and poetry, and novels, in English, and French, and Latin. She has been reading, and writing, and thinking, since she was a small child.

Mary comes from a very famous family. Her mother was Mary Wollstonecraft, one of the first great writers to argue that women should have the same rights, and the same education, as men. Her book, A Vindication of the Rights of Woman, made her famous, all over Europe. But she passed away, sadly, a few days after Mary was born. Mary never knew her mother. But she read all her books, and loved her, and wanted to be like her.

--

{img:william-godwin-bookshop-london-1810s-shelves-young-girl-reading-corner}
Mary's father was William Godwin, a famous philosopher, and writer, in London. He believed in reason, and freedom, and justice, and education for all. Many famous writers, and thinkers, came to his house, to talk with him. When Mary was a little girl, she used to hide behind the sofa, in the evenings, and listen to them talking. Once, she heard the poet Coleridge reciting his famous poem, about an old sailor, and an albatross, and the ghostly ship. She never forgot it.

When Mary was sixteen, a young poet came to visit her father. His name was Percy Bysshe Shelley. He was twenty-one, tall, and thin, and wild, and passionate, with bright eyes, and long hair, and a high voice. He was the son of a rich nobleman, but he had been thrown out of Oxford University, for writing a pamphlet against religion. He believed in freedom, and love, and changing the world. Mary fell in love with him. And he fell in love with her.

{img:percy-shelley-young-poet-1816-open-collar-wild-hair-writing-outdoors}
There was a problem. Shelley was already married. He had married very young, and the marriage was unhappy, and he had left his wife. Mary and Shelley ran away together, to France, and Switzerland, in 1814, when Mary was sixteen. Mary's stepsister, Claire, went with them. It was a great scandal, in London. Mary's father was very angry. Many people would not speak to them.

Since then, Mary and Shelley have lived together, moving from place to place, often with very little money. In 1815, Mary had a baby girl, who was born too early, and lived only a few days. It was a great sorrow. But in January 1816, Mary had a baby boy, called William, after her father. He is healthy, and strong, and Mary loves him more than anything in the world. They call him Willmouse.

--

In the spring of 1816, Claire, Mary's stepsister, had an idea. She had met Lord Byron, the famous poet, in London, and had fallen in love with him. Byron was leaving England, to travel in Europe, and was going to Geneva. Claire persuaded Mary and Shelley to go to Geneva too, so that she could see him again. Shelley wanted to meet Byron, whose poetry he admired. So they set off, across France, and over the mountains, into Switzerland.

{img:young-mother-holding-baby-1816-travelling-carriage-alps-snow-pass}
They crossed the Jura mountains, in May, in a carriage, through snow, and ice, and storms, with the baby, wrapped up warmly. It was cold, and frightening, and beautiful. Mary wrote letters, describing the journey, and the mountains, and the snow, and the great pine forests. She was a good writer, already. But she had not yet written a book. She did not know what she wanted to write about.

That summer, by the lake, she would find out. And the story she wrote would be read by more people than anything her famous mother, or her famous father, or her famous lover, or her famous friend Lord Byron ever wrote.
