For the rest of the summer, Mary writes. Every day, when she has time, she sits at her desk, in the little house by the lake, and writes her story. It is not easy. She has the baby to look after, and the house, and the visitors, and the walks, and the boat trips. And she is only eighteen. But she writes, a little every day. And slowly, the story takes shape.

She calls her student Victor Frankenstein. He is a young man from Geneva, the son of a good family, who goes to study at a university, in Germany, at Ingolstadt. There, he becomes fascinated by science, and by the secret of life. He works day and night, in secret, in his room, gathering materials, and studying, until he discovers how to give life to dead matter. And then he makes a creature, and brings it to life.

{img:geneva-landscape-1816-lake-mountains-mont-blanc-story-setting-misty}
Mary sets parts of her story in the places she knows, around the lake. Victor's family lives in Geneva, and in a country house by the lake, at a place called Belrive. Victor walks along the shore, and sails on the lake, at night, in a little boat. He sees the storms over the mountains. He climbs to the glaciers of Mont Blanc. Many of the places in the book are places that Mary saw, that summer, with her own eyes.

The creature in Mary's book is not a monster, in the way that people think of it today. He is huge, and ugly, and frightening to look at. But inside, he is gentle, and kind, and intelligent. He learns to speak, and to read, by watching a family through a hole in the wall of their cottage. He reads books, and thinks about life. He longs for love, and friendship. But everyone who sees him is frightened of him, and runs away, or attacks him. Even his creator.

{img:young-woman-reading-pages-aloud-to-poet-by-fire-evening-manuscript}
That is the heart of Mary's story. A creature, made by a man, and then abandoned by him. Left alone, in a world that does not want him. He asks his creator for help, and love, and a companion. And his creator refuses him. And so the creature becomes angry, and bitter, and sad. Mary's story asks a question, that people are still asking today. What do we owe to the things we create? And what happens when we create something, and then run away from it?

Shelley reads Mary's pages, as she writes them, and encourages her, and makes suggestions. Sometimes, he changes a word, or a sentence. People have argued, ever since, about how much Shelley helped. But the story, the ideas, the characters, and most of the words, were Mary's own. She was the author. And she was proud of it.

She writes in the mornings, while the baby sleeps. And in the afternoons, when it rains. And sometimes, late at night, by candlelight, when the house is quiet. Willmouse sleeps in his cradle, beside her desk. Sometimes he wakes, and cries, and she picks him up, and rocks him, and sings to him, until he sleeps again. And then she goes back to her writing.

Mary does not finish the book that summer. It takes her almost a year. She writes the rest of it in England, after they go home. But it was born here, by the lake, in the summer of 1816. In the rain, and the storms, and the long evenings by the fire. In the conversations about life, and death, and electricity. And in the vision of a young woman, lying awake, in the dark, listening to the rain.

When the book is finished, it is called Frankenstein, or, The Modern Prometheus. Prometheus was a figure in the old Greek stories, who stole fire from the gods, and gave it to people. And he was punished, terribly, for it. Mary's Victor Frankenstein is a modern Prometheus. He steals the secret of life. And he too is punished, for what he has done.
