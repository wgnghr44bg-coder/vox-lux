At the end of August, the summer by the lake comes to an end. The Shelleys decide to go back to England. They have business to attend to, and money problems, and family problems. And Claire has a secret. She is expecting a baby. Byron's baby. Byron does not want to be with Claire anymore. He has made that very clear. Claire is unhappy, and must go home, with Mary and Shelley.

On the twenty-ninth of August, they leave. Their trunks are packed, and loaded onto a carriage. Mary has her manuscript, carefully wrapped, in her bag. The baby is wrapped up warmly, in her arms. They say goodbye to Byron, and Polidori, at the villa. And then they go down the hill, to the carriage, waiting on the road below.

--

{img:young-woman-saying-goodbye-to-girl-servant-gift-small-book-doorway}
Élise is there, to say goodbye. Mary has always been kind to her, and has learned a few words of French from her, and taught her a few words of English. Mary gives her a little present. A small book, with a blue cover, of English poems, with Mary's name written inside. Élise cannot read English. But she keeps the book, all her life, and later, she learns to read it.

Pierre carries the trunks down to the carriage. Shelley shakes his hand, warmly, and thanks him, for everything. For the boat trips, and the storms, and the safe returns. He gives him a gift of money, and a silver pocket knife. Pierre is very touched. He says that if they ever come back to the lake, he will be their boatman again. Shelley says that he hopes they will come back one day. But they never do.

{img:carriage-on-road-along-lake-departing-misty-morning-mountains-behind}
The carriage drives away, along the road beside the lake, toward the north, and France, and England. Élise and Pierre stand by the road, and wave, until it disappears around a bend. Then they walk back up the hill, through the wet vineyard, to their house. The little house by the water, where the Shelleys stayed, is empty now, with its shutters closed. And the path through the vineyard is quiet.

Byron stays at the Villa Diodati for a few more weeks. Other friends come to visit him, from England. He goes on another journey, into the mountains, of the Bernese Oberland, with a friend, and writes about the glaciers, and the waterfalls, and the high peaks. And then, in October, he too leaves, and goes south, over the Alps, to Italy. He sends Polidori away, before he leaves. They have quarrelled too often.

--

The Villa Diodati is empty again. The drawing room, where they told their ghost stories, is quiet. The fire is cold. The books are gone. The balcony, where they watched the storms, is empty, and covered with fallen leaves. Madame Berthe, the cook, goes back to Geneva. And Élise has no more work at the villa. She goes back to helping her mother, in the house, and the garden.

But she misses the English. She misses the noise, and the visitors, and the strange conversations, and the excitement. She misses Mary, with her quiet voice, and her kind eyes, and her baby. She often walks up the path, through the vineyard, to the empty villa, and looks at it, and remembers. The summer of the storms, and the ghost stories, and the English poets.

{img:girl-sitting-on-wall-by-vineyard-looking-at-empty-villa-autumn-dusk}
Grandmother Anne says that it is just as well that they have gone. She says that now, the village can go back to normal, and people will stop gossiping, and the tourists will stop looking through their telescopes. But even she misses them, a little. She says that life was more interesting, when the English were here. And the next year, when the tourists come, and ask about the famous poets, she tells them stories. Some of them true.
