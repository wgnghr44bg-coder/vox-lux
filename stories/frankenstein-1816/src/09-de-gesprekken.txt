In the long, rainy evenings, the friends talk. They talk about everything. About poetry, and politics, and religion, and philosophy. About the French Revolution, which happened when they were children, and Napoleon, who has just been defeated, at Waterloo, the year before. About freedom, and tyranny, and the future of the world. About love, and marriage, and death, and the meaning of life.

Byron and Shelley do most of the talking. They talk for hours, by the fire, often until two or three in the morning. Byron is witty, and sharp, and mocking. Shelley is passionate, and dreamy, and full of strange ideas. Polidori tries to join in, but they often tease him. Claire listens, and sometimes speaks. And Mary sits quietly, in the corner, and listens to everything. She does not say much. But she remembers.

--

{img:galvani-experiment-frog-legs-electricity-18th-century-laboratory-sketch}
Many years later, Mary wrote about one of these conversations. She said that Byron and Shelley talked, one evening, about the nature of life. What is life? Where does it come from? Could it ever be created? Could a dead body ever be brought back to life? These were questions that the scientists of the time were asking too. And there were strange new experiments, which seemed to suggest some answers.

Some years before, an Italian scientist, called Luigi Galvani, had discovered something remarkable. When he touched the legs of a dead frog with two different metals, the legs moved, and twitched, as if the frog were alive. He thought that there was a kind of electricity in living things, which made them move. People called this galvanism. It was very exciting, and a little frightening. Was electricity the secret of life?

{img:old-scientist-books-electricity-machine-leyden-jars-candlelit-study}
Galvani's nephew, Giovanni Aldini, did even more dramatic experiments. He travelled around Europe, giving public demonstrations, with electricity. In London, in 1803, he used electricity to make the face of a dead body move, and the eyes open, and the hand rise. People who watched were amazed, and horrified. Some of them thought that he was about to bring the dead back to life. Of course, he could not. But the idea was in the air.

Shelley was fascinated by science. When he was a boy, at school, and at university, he had done his own experiments, with chemicals, and electricity, and a little electrical machine. He had set fire to things, and given himself shocks, and frightened his sisters. He talked about these things, with Byron, by the fire. About electricity, and life, and whether a body could be made, and brought to life.

--

They also talked about the English doctor, and poet, Erasmus Darwin, the grandfather of Charles Darwin, who would later become very famous. Mary wrote that they talked about some of his experiments, or the experiments people said he had done. That he had kept a piece of pasta in a glass case, until, by some strange means, it began to move by itself. It was probably a misunderstanding. But it made them laugh, and wonder.

{img:young-woman-listening-in-corner-chair-fire-glow-shadows-thoughtful-face}
Mary listened to all of this, quietly, sitting by the fire, with her baby asleep upstairs. She thought about it, afterward, when she went to bed. About life, and death, and electricity, and the power of science. About what it would mean to create a living being. And about what that being would feel, if it were created. Would it be happy? Would it be lonely? Would its creator love it? Or would he be afraid of it?

These were not just scientific questions, for Mary. They were personal questions too. She had lost her own mother, at birth. She had lost her own baby, the year before. She thought a great deal about life, and death, and about mothers, and children, and creators, and the things they create. And all of these thoughts were slowly mixing together, in her mind, like the ingredients of a story, waiting to be told.
