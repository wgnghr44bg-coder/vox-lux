The strange weather of 1816 began more than a year before, on the other side of the world. In April 1815, on the island of Sumbawa, in what is now Indonesia, a great volcano erupted. It was called Mount Tambora. It was the biggest volcanic eruption in recorded history. The explosion was so loud that it was heard more than two thousand kilometres away. People thought it was the sound of cannons.

The volcano threw enormous amounts of ash, and dust, and gas, high into the sky. Higher than the clouds, into the upper air. And there, the winds carried it all around the world. A thin veil of fine dust, spreading slowly across the whole sky, all around the Earth. Nobody could see it. But it blocked some of the sunlight. And so, the whole world grew a little colder.

--

{img:snow-falling-on-green-summer-fields-1816-farmers-looking-at-sky}
In 1816, the effects were felt everywhere. In Europe, and in North America, the summer was cold, and wet, and dark. In New England, in America, it snowed in June, and there was frost in July, and August. In Europe, it rained, and rained, and rained. The rivers flooded. The harvests failed. The wheat rotted in the fields. The potatoes would not grow. The grapes stayed small, and green, and sour.

Nobody, at that time, knew why. Nobody connected the cold summer in Europe with a volcano on the other side of the world. People thought it was a punishment from God, or a sign of the end of the world. Some people said that the sun was dying. There were strange stories, and fears. In Italy, people said that the sun had spots on it, which were growing bigger, and that the world would end.

{img:dramatic-red-orange-sunset-over-lake-geneva-1816-volcanic-sky-glow}
But there was one beautiful thing about that strange year. The sunsets. The fine dust high in the sky made the sunsets extraordinary. Red, and orange, and purple, and gold, glowing for a long time, after the sun had gone down. Painters, like the English painter Turner, painted these glowing skies. And people, all over Europe, stood and watched them, in wonder, every evening, when the clouds parted.

Switzerland suffered badly that year. It is a mountain country, and it was even colder there, and wetter. The snow did not melt in the mountains. The glaciers grew. The rivers and lakes rose, and flooded the fields, and the villages. Lake Geneva rose so high that the water came up into the streets of Geneva. The harvests failed, and the price of bread went up, and up, until many poor people could not afford it.

--

The people of Geneva had seen bad years before. But none like this. The old people said that they had never known a summer like it. The farmers looked at the sky, every morning, and shook their heads. The fishermen stayed at home, because of the storms. And the travellers who came to the lake, for its famous beauty, found only rain, and cloud, and mist.

{img:rain-on-vineyard-cologny-1816-dark-clouds-lake-below-wet-terraces}
But for the young English travellers, in the house on the hill, the bad weather turned out to be a kind of gift. It kept them indoors. It kept them together. It made them talk, and read, and think, and imagine. And in the long, dark, stormy evenings, by the fire, it led them to tell each other stories. Without the year without a summer, there might never have been a Frankenstein.

So in a way, one of the most famous stories in the world was born from a volcano, on the other side of the world, a year before. It is a strange thought. But that is how history works, sometimes. Small things, and big things, and faraway things, all connected, in ways that nobody can see at the time.
