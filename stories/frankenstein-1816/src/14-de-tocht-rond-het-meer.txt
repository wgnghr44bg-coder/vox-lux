At the end of June, Byron and Shelley go on a journey, together, around the lake, in their boat. They leave the women behind, and Polidori too, who has hurt his ankle. They take two boatmen, and Byron's servant. They plan to sail all the way around the lake, stopping at the villages, and towns, and places that Rousseau wrote about in his famous novel. It takes about a week.

They sail along the southern shore, past villages, and vineyards, and mountains, coming straight down into the water. They stop at little inns, for the night, and eat fish, from the lake, and drink the local wine. They walk in the villages, and talk to the people. Shelley writes long letters, describing everything. Byron writes poetry, in the evenings, in the inns.

--

{img:rough-lake-storm-small-boat-waves-near-cliffs-meillerie-dramatic}
Near a village called Meillerie, a sudden storm comes down from the mountains. The wind is very strong, and the waves are high, and the boat is in danger. Water comes over the side. The boatmen struggle with the sail. Shelley cannot swim, and Byron offers to help him, if the boat sinks. But Shelley refuses. He sits calmly, holding onto a locker, and says that he would rather go down with the boat. Luckily, they reach the shore safely. But it was a close call.

At the eastern end of the lake, they visit the castle of Chillon. It is an old castle, built on a rock, in the water, close to the shore. It is very beautiful, from the outside, with its towers, and walls, rising straight out of the lake. But inside, deep down, below the level of the water, there are dungeons. Dark, cold rooms, with stone pillars, and iron rings in the walls, where prisoners were once chained.

{img:chillon-dungeon-stone-pillars-gothic-vault-light-through-slit-window}
Long ago, a man called François Bonivard was kept prisoner in the dungeon at Chillon, for six years. He was a citizen of Geneva, who had fought for the freedom of the city, against the Duke of Savoy. He was chained to one of the pillars, in the dungeon. People said that he walked round and round the pillar, for six years, until he had worn a path in the stone floor. Visitors can still see the path, they say.

Byron is very moved by the story, and the dungeon. He carves his name on one of the pillars. You can still see it today. And a few days later, in an inn, at a town called Ouchy, near Lausanne, during two rainy days, he writes a long poem, called The Prisoner of Chillon. It is about Bonivard, and his brothers, chained in the dungeon, and the sadness of their long imprisonment. It becomes one of his most famous poems.

--

On the way back, they stop at Lausanne, and visit the house where the great English historian, Edward Gibbon, wrote the last part of his famous history of the fall of the Roman Empire. They walk in his garden, at night, where he finished his book. Byron picks some leaves from the acacia tree, where Gibbon used to sit, and sends them to his publisher, in London, as a souvenir.

{img:two-men-walking-in-moonlit-garden-acacia-tree-lausanne-1816-night}
While the poets are away, Mary stays at the little house, with the baby, and Claire. It is quiet. The rain falls. And Mary writes. Every day, a few more pages. She is glad of the peace. She walks along the shore, in the evenings, with Willmouse in her arms, and looks out over the lake, and wonders where the little boat is now.

When they come back to Cologny, at the beginning of July, they are tired, and sunburnt, and happy. Pierre goes down to the harbour, to help them with the boat. He asks them about the storm, near Meillerie. Shelley tells him about it, and says that he thought, for a moment, that it was the end. Pierre shakes his head. He says, I told you. The lake is not a pond.

Shelley laughs, and agrees. But six years later, Shelley would drown, in another storm, in another sailing boat, on the sea, off the coast of Italy. He never did learn to swim. But that is still far in the future. In the summer of 1816, he is twenty-three, and full of life, and ideas, and poems. And the lake, for now, has let him go.
