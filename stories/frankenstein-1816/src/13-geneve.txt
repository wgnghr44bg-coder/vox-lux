On Saturdays, Louise goes to Geneva, to the market, with Élise. They go by boat, with Pierre, across the end of the lake, to the city. Geneva is not a big city, in 1816. About twenty-five thousand people live there. But it is an old, proud, and famous city. It sits at the end of the lake, where the river Rhône flows out, fast, and blue, and cold, on its long journey to the Mediterranean Sea.

The old city is built on a hill, with narrow, steep streets, and tall, grey stone houses, packed close together. At the top of the hill, there is the cathedral of Saint Pierre, with its towers, where the great preacher John Calvin preached, nearly three hundred years before. Calvin made Geneva a city of strict Protestant faith, and hard work, and plain living. People called it the Protestant Rome.

--

{img:geneva-market-1816-stalls-vegetables-cheese-women-baskets-rain-umbrellas}
Down by the lake, there is the market. Stalls selling vegetables, and fruit, and cheese, and fish from the lake, and bread, and meat, and eggs. And everything else. Cloth, and shoes, and pots, and baskets, and tools. The market women shout their prices, and the customers argue, and bargain. But this year, there is not much to buy. The harvest has been bad, and the prices are very high. Louise looks at the price of bread, and shakes her head.

Geneva is famous for watches. In the old town, in the upper floors of the tall houses, there are hundreds of watchmakers, working at their benches, by the windows, to get the best light. They make beautiful watches, and clocks, with tiny gears, and springs, and wheels, and enamelled faces, and gold cases. Geneva watches are sold all over the world. The watchmakers are called cabinotiers, because they work in little rooms, called cabinets, at the top of the houses.

{img:watchmaker-at-bench-window-top-floor-geneva-1816-tiny-gears-loupe}
Élise has an uncle who is a watchmaker. Uncle Henri, her mother's brother. He works in a little room, at the top of a tall house, in the old town, with a big window, looking out over the roofs, and the lake. Élise and her mother visit him, after the market. He shows Élise the tiny gears, and springs, through his magnifying glass. They are so small, and so perfect. He says that a watch is like a living thing. Every part must work together, or it will not go.

Geneva is also famous for its thinkers, and writers, and scientists. The great philosopher Jean-Jacques Rousseau was born here, about a hundred years before. Byron and Shelley admire Rousseau very much. They have read his books, and they visit the places he wrote about, around the lake. Shelley carries a copy of Rousseau's novel, which is set by the lake, everywhere he goes.

--

{img:rhone-river-flowing-out-of-lake-geneva-blue-water-old-bridges-mills}
After the market, Louise and Élise walk along the river Rhône, where it flows out of the lake, through the middle of the city. The water is an extraordinary colour. A deep, clear, brilliant blue, like a jewel. It rushes past, very fast, under the old wooden bridges, and past the mills, and the island in the middle of the river. Byron wrote that the Rhône was the bluest water he had ever seen.

Then they meet Pierre, at the harbour, and sail home, across the lake, to Cologny. The city falls away behind them, grey, and solid, and old, under the grey sky. Ahead of them, on the green hillside, they can see the Villa Diodati, with its long balcony, and the little houses of the village, and their own house, below. And the vineyards, wet, and dark, and green, climbing the hill.

Élise thinks that in Mary's book, many years later, Victor Frankenstein makes the same journey. Across the lake, from Geneva, to his family's house by the water. And he looks at the mountains, and the lake, and the city, just as Élise is doing now. She does not know this yet, of course. But one day, when she reads the book, she will remember this afternoon. And the grey city, and the blue river, and the green hills, and her mother beside her, in the boat.
