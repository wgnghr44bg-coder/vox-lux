Pierre Moret is forty years old. He lives in the village of Cologny, on the hillside above the southern shore of the lake, about three kilometres from Geneva. He is a boatman. He owns a small sailing boat, with a single sail, and he carries people, and goods, across the lake, and along the shore. He also has a small vineyard, on the slope behind his house, where he grows grapes, for wine, like his father, and grandfather before him.

Pierre is a calm, steady man, with a weathered face, and strong arms, and a pipe that he is always lighting, and that is always going out. He knows the lake as well as he knows his own house. Every wind, every current, every change in the weather. He knows when a storm is coming, long before anyone else. He says that he can smell it, in the air.

--

{img:swiss-village-woman-1816-apron-bonnet-baking-bread-cottage-kitchen}
His wife, Louise, is thirty-six. She runs the house, and the garden, and the chickens, and the goats. She makes cheese, and bread, and preserves. And in the autumn, she works in the vineyard, picking grapes, with the whole family. She is a warm, practical woman, who sings while she works, and worries about everything, especially the weather, this year.

Élise is twelve. She is quick, and curious, and talkative. Since May, she has been working in the kitchen of the great house on the hill, the Villa Diodati, where the famous English lord is staying. She helps the cook, and washes the dishes, and carries trays. She is paid a few francs a week, which helps her family, in this hard year. And she sees and hears all sorts of interesting things. She comes home every evening, full of stories.

{img:boy-nine-1816-swiss-village-fishing-from-stone-jetty-lake-mist}
Jacques is nine. He loves the lake, and boats, and fishing. He goes out in the boat with his father, whenever he can, and helps with the ropes, and the sail. He wants to be a boatman too, when he grows up. He is also very interested in the famous English lord, who is said to be very handsome, and very wicked, and to swim across lakes, and to keep a bear. Jacques is disappointed to learn that the lord has not brought the bear to Switzerland.

And there is Grandmother Anne, Pierre's mother. She is seventy-three. She has lived in Cologny all her life. She remembers the old days, before the French Revolution, when Geneva was a little independent republic, and the old families ruled it. She remembers when the French armies came, and took it over, and made it part of France. And she remembers when the French left, three years ago, and last year, when Geneva became part of Switzerland, at last. She says she has seen enough history for one life.

--

{img:grandmother-by-fire-knitting-telling-stories-children-rain-window}
The Morets' house is small, and old, and built of stone, with a steep roof, and a little wooden balcony, covered with vines. It stands on the hillside, just below the Villa Diodati, with a view over the lake. From the window, they can see the boats, and the water, and on clear days, the city of Geneva, at the end of the lake, and the mountains beyond. On rainy days, which this year is most days, they can see nothing but grey.

The family speaks French, like everyone in Geneva. They are Protestants, like most Genevans, and go to the plain, simple church in the village, every Sunday. They are hard-working, and careful with money, and they do not like waste, or show, or fuss. These are Genevan virtues, the grandmother says. The English, she says, are very different.

This summer, the whole village is talking about the English. They watch them, and gossip about them, and some of them disapprove of them, very much. There are stories about the lord, and about the young women. The hotel owners, across the lake, even rent out telescopes, so that tourists can watch the villa, and see what the famous poet is doing. Pierre thinks this is very silly. He says that they are just young people, far from home, like any others.
