Mary finished her book in England, in the spring of 1817. She had written it, and rewritten it, and copied it out, carefully, by hand. Shelley helped her to find a publisher. It was not easy. Several publishers refused it. At last, a small publisher in London agreed to publish it. On the first of January, 1818, Frankenstein was published. Mary was twenty years old.

The book was published without the author's name on it. That was quite common, in those days. Many books were published anonymously. And there was another reason. Mary was a young woman, and a young woman writing a frightening novel, about science, and death, and the creation of life, would have shocked many people. So the book appeared, with no name. Only a dedication, to her father, William Godwin.

--

At first, many people thought that Shelley had written it. Some reviewers praised it. They said it was original, and powerful, and full of imagination. Others hated it. They said it was horrible, and disgusting, and dangerous, and that it would give people nightmares. But people read it. And talked about it. And the book slowly became famous.

Let us remember the story, gently, as Mary told it. It begins far in the north, among the ice, near the North Pole. A ship is trapped in the ice, and the captain, a young explorer called Walton, sees a strange sight. A huge figure, on a sledge, pulled by dogs, racing across the ice. And the next day, they find another man, on the ice, half frozen, and exhausted. They bring him on board. His name is Victor Frankenstein. And he tells them his story.

{img:ship-trapped-in-arctic-ice-sledge-on-ice-distance-pale-sky-quiet}
He tells how he grew up in Geneva, happy, and loved. How he went to university, and became obsessed with the secret of life. How he made the creature, and brought it to life, on a dreary night of November, and then ran away from it, in horror. And how the creature, abandoned, and alone, wandered through the world, and learned to speak, and to read, and to think, and to feel. And how everyone he met was afraid of him.

The creature asks Victor to make him a companion. Someone like him, so that he will not be alone. At first, Victor agrees. But then he changes his mind, and destroys the companion, before it is finished. And the creature, in his grief, and anger, turns against Victor, and against everyone Victor loves. It is a sad, dark story. Of loneliness, and rejection, and revenge, and regret.

--

At the end, Victor and the creature chase each other across the world, to the frozen north. Victor passes away, on the ship, exhausted, and broken. And the creature comes on board, and sees his creator lying there. And he weeps. He says that he never wanted to be bad. He only wanted to be loved. And then he goes away, across the ice, into the darkness. And he is never seen again.

{img:lone-figure-walking-away-across-ice-floes-twilight-arctic-sad-calm}
That is the end of Mary's story. It is not really a horror story, as many people think today. It is a story about responsibility, and loneliness, and the need for love. About what happens when we create something, and do not care for it. About science, and its power, and its dangers. And about what it means to be human. These questions are still important, today, perhaps more than ever.

In 1823, a second edition was published, with Mary's name on it, at last. And in 1831, a third edition, revised by Mary, with her famous introduction, about the summer by the lake, and the ghost story competition, and her waking dream. From then on, everyone knew that Mary Shelley had written Frankenstein. And it has never been out of print since.

Mary kept a copy of the first edition all her life. Three small volumes, bound in grey boards, with plain paper labels. Today, copies of that first edition are very rare, and very precious. Only a few hundred were printed. When one of them is sold, it costs more than a house. But in 1818, it cost a few shillings, and nobody knew how famous it would become.
