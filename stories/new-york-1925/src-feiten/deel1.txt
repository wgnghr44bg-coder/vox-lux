TOPIC: Prohibition comes to New York
===
IMG: new-york-street-1925-evening-brownstones-lamps | a quiet New York street at dusk in 1925 with brownstone houses, iron railings, gas-style street lamps glowing, a parked black Ford Model T, a few people in coats and hats walking home, soft blue evening light
HEAD: In January 1920, it became illegal to make or sell alcohol anywhere in the United States.
BODY: On the sixteenth of January 1919, the Eighteenth Amendment to the American Constitution was ratified. It banned the making, selling and transporting of alcoholic drinks. The law gave the country one year to prepare. On the seventeenth of January 1920, it came into force. This period became known as Prohibition. Supporters believed it would make America healthier and safer. They hoped it would reduce crime, poverty and violence in the home. Churches and women's groups had campaigned for it for decades. In New York, the reaction was mixed. The city was home to millions of immigrants from Ireland, Italy, Germany and elsewhere. For many of them, wine and beer were part of daily life and religion. Even so, the law was now national. The saloons had to close. Yet very few New Yorkers stopped drinking. Instead, the drinking simply moved behind closed doors.
===
IMG: last-night-saloon-crowd-january-1920 | the inside of a crowded old New York saloon on a winter night in January 1920, men in suits and hats raising glasses at a long wooden bar, a mirror and bottles behind, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: On the last night before Prohibition, New Yorkers held mock funerals for alcohol.
BODY: On the evening of the sixteenth of January 1920, the city's bars and hotels were full. It was the last night that alcohol could be sold legally. Many New Yorkers decided to say goodbye in style. In some restaurants, guests held mock funerals. Waiters carried coffins filled with bottles through the dining rooms. Bands played sad music. People wore black and pretended to mourn. Others simply drank as much as they could. Some bought bottles to store at home. The law allowed people to keep alcohol they already owned, as long as it was for private use. Wealthy people had filled their cellars months before. Even so, many ordinary people could not afford to buy in advance. At midnight, the law took effect. The bars went quiet. Yet within days, new ways of drinking had already appeared all over the city.
===
IMG: prohibition-agents-pouring-liquor-street-drain | uniformed police and men in suits pouring barrels of liquor into a street drain in New York in the 1920s, a small crowd of onlookers watching, brick buildings behind, soft daylight
HEAD: Prohibition agents often emptied seized barrels of alcohol into the city's sewers.
BODY: Enforcing Prohibition was the job of federal agents. They raided bars, warehouses and secret breweries. When they found alcohol, they destroyed it. Often, they poured it into the street drains, so that it ran away into the sewers. Newspapers loved to photograph these scenes. Crowds gathered to watch. Some people were said to be sad to see good whisky disappear. Yet the agents faced an impossible task. There were only a few thousand of them for the whole country. New York alone had thousands of places selling alcohol. Many agents were poorly paid. Some took bribes and looked the other way. Even so, a few agents became famous for their honesty and success. Yet most New Yorkers saw the law as unfair. In 1923, New York State even repealed its own Prohibition law. After that, the city police no longer had to enforce it. Only the federal agents remained.
===
IMG: izzy-einstein-disguise-agent-hat-street | a short, round, cheerful man in a 1920s suit, bowler hat and fake moustache standing on a New York street corner, holding a small notebook, a speakeasy door behind him, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Two New York Prohibition agents, Izzy Einstein and Moe Smith, became famous for their clever disguises.
BODY: The most famous Prohibition agents in New York were Izzy Einstein and Moe Smith. They were an unlikely pair. Izzy had been a postal clerk. He was short and round. Moe was a cigar salesman, and he was also quite heavy. Neither looked like a police officer. That was their secret. They wore disguises to get into illegal bars. They dressed as fishermen, football players, grave diggers and even women. Izzy spoke several languages, which helped him blend in. Once inside, they ordered a drink and then arrested everyone. Together, they made thousands of arrests. Newspapers wrote about them constantly. Readers loved their adventures. Even so, their fame caused problems. Some of their bosses felt they were getting too much attention. In 1925, both men were dismissed from the service. Yet they remained popular. Izzy later wrote a book about his adventures, and both lived quiet lives afterwards.
===
IMG: doctor-prescription-medicinal-whiskey-pharmacy | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a 1920s pharmacy counter with glass jars and wooden drawers, a pharmacist handing a small bottle to a customer holding a paper prescription, soft warm lamplight
HEAD: During Prohibition, doctors could still prescribe whisky as medicine.
BODY: Prohibition had some unusual exceptions. One of them was medicine. Doctors were allowed to prescribe whisky to their patients. People could then buy it legally at a pharmacy. Each prescription was written on a special form. Patients could receive about a pint every ten days. Many doctors wrote prescriptions for almost any complaint. Some charged a fee for each one. Pharmacies sold large amounts of medicinal whisky during these years. Another exception was religious wine. Priests and rabbis could obtain wine for religious services. This, too, was sometimes misused. People also made their own wine at home from grape juice. Grape growers in California sold blocks of dried grapes with a warning. The warning said not to mix them with water and leave them, because they might turn into wine. Even so, these were small loopholes. Yet together, they show how hard it was to stop people from drinking.
===
IMG: rum-row-ships-offshore-small-boats-night | several small sailing ships and schooners anchored far out at sea at night off the coast of New York in the 1920s, small fast motorboats moving between them, a faint glow of the city on the horizon, calm dark water
HEAD: Ships full of alcohol waited just outside American waters, in a line known as Rum Row.
BODY: Much of the alcohol in New York came by sea. Ships loaded with whisky, rum and gin sailed from Canada, the Caribbean and Europe. They anchored just outside American waters, where United States law could not touch them. At first, this limit was three miles from the coast. Off New York and New Jersey, so many ships waited there that the line became known as Rum Row. At night, fast motorboats raced out to meet them. They loaded cases of alcohol and sped back to quiet beaches and docks. The coast guard tried to stop them, but the smugglers' boats were often faster. One famous smuggler was a sea captain named Bill McCoy. He was known for selling high-quality, undiluted alcohol. Some people say the phrase the real McCoy comes from him. Even so, that story may not be true. The phrase may be older. Yet McCoy's ships were real, and Rum Row was busy for years.
===
IMG: bathtub-gin-kitchen-apartment-bottles | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a small 1920s New York apartment kitchen with glass bottles, a funnel and a large jug on the table, a bathtub visible through a doorway, soft lamplight
HEAD: Home-made gin was so common that it became known as bathtub gin.
BODY: Real whisky and gin were expensive during Prohibition. So many people made their own. A popular method was to mix industrial alcohol with water and flavourings like juniper oil. The mixture was often made in large containers. The bottles were too tall to fill under a kitchen tap, so people used the bathtub tap instead. That is one explanation for the name bathtub gin. The drink was often harsh and unpleasant. To hide the taste, people mixed it with fruit juice, sugar and other flavours. This helped make cocktails very popular in the 1920s. Even so, home-made alcohol could be dangerous. Some industrial alcohol contained poisons. People who drank badly made liquor could become ill or even blind. Yet many people took the risk. Making a little gin at home became almost an ordinary part of life for some families. It was illegal, but very few were ever caught.

TOPIC: Speakeasies
===
IMG: speakeasy-door-peephole-alley-night | a plain wooden door with a small sliding peephole in a dim brick alley in New York at night in 1925, a single lamp glowing above it, a man in a hat knocking softly, quiet atmosphere
HEAD: Illegal bars were called speakeasies, because customers had to speak quietly about them.
BODY: When the saloons closed, new secret bars opened in their place. They were called speakeasies. The name came from the idea of speaking softly, or speaking easy, so that no one outside would hear. Speakeasies were hidden in basements, back rooms, apartments and behind ordinary shops. From the street, they often looked like nothing at all. A customer knocked on a plain door. A small window in the door slid open. A face appeared and looked at the visitor. To get in, people might need a password, a card or a friend who was known inside. Some speakeasies were small and simple, with just a few tables. Others were large and elegant, with music and dancing. Even so, all of them were illegal. They could be raided at any time. Yet the risk was part of the excitement. For many New Yorkers, the speakeasy became the most fashionable place to spend an evening.
===
IMG: elegant-speakeasy-interior-jazz-band-tables-1925 | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, an elegant 1920s New York speakeasy with small round tables, men in suits and women in beaded dresses, a small jazz band playing in a corner, soft amber light
HEAD: By the end of the 1920s, New York may have had tens of thousands of speakeasies.
BODY: No one knows exactly how many speakeasies there were in New York. They were secret, so no one counted them properly. Yet estimates were very high. In 1929, the city's police commissioner suggested there might be more than thirty thousand. That would be more than the number of legal bars before Prohibition. Some streets had a speakeasy in almost every building. West Fifty-Second Street, in Manhattan, had so many that it became famous for them. Later, it became known for its jazz clubs. Speakeasies ranged from rough cellars to fancy nightclubs. Some served good food and had live music. Others offered cheap drinks of uncertain quality. Even so, they all shared one thing. They were full of people breaking the law together. Yet most customers did not feel like criminals. They felt that the law was foolish, and they enjoyed quietly ignoring it.
===
IMG: women-friends-cocktails-speakeasy-table | three young women in 1920s cloche hats and drop-waist dresses sitting together at a small table in a dimly lit speakeasy with cocktail glasses, laughing softly, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm amber light
HEAD: Speakeasies welcomed women, who had rarely been allowed into the old saloons.
BODY: Before Prohibition, most saloons in New York were places for men only. Respectable women did not go into them. Speakeasies were different. Women came too, often in groups or with men. They drank cocktails, danced and smoked cigarettes. This was a big change in social life. It was part of a wider change in the lives of American women. In 1920, the same year Prohibition began, women gained the right to vote across the country. Many young women were working in offices and shops. They had their own money and more freedom than their mothers. Even so, not everyone approved. Older people and religious groups were shocked. Yet the change could not be stopped. By the middle of the 1920s, it was quite normal to see women drinking in speakeasies. In a strange way, a law meant to make life more proper had helped make it more relaxed.
===
IMG: texas-guinan-nightclub-hostess-stage-smile | a confident woman in a sparkling 1920s evening dress and pearls standing on a small nightclub stage, smiling and greeting a crowd at small tables, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm stage lights
HEAD: The nightclub hostess Texas Guinan greeted her guests with the words "Hello, suckers!"
BODY: One of the most famous figures of Prohibition New York was a woman called Texas Guinan. She had grown up in Texas and worked as an actress in silent films. In the 1920s, she became the hostess of several nightclubs in Manhattan. She greeted her guests with a cheerful shout. Hello, suckers, she would call as they came in. Customers loved it. Her clubs were lively and noisy. Dancers performed, and guests paid high prices for drinks. Guinan was arrested many times. Her clubs were raided and closed. Each time, she simply opened a new one. When police came, the band sometimes played the song The Prisoner's Song as she was led away. Even so, she was rarely convicted. Juries liked her. Yet her clubs were also connected to powerful criminals who supplied the alcohol. Her cheerful greeting became one of the best-known phrases of the decade.
===
IMG: secret-wine-cellar-hidden-brick-door | an indoor scene, enclosed by brick walls, a low arched brick ceiling, a hidden wine cellar behind a heavy brick door that swings open, racks of dusty wine bottles inside, soft warm lamplight
HEAD: Some speakeasies had hidden doors, secret rooms and systems to make bottles disappear in a raid.
BODY: Speakeasy owners became very creative. They needed ways to hide their alcohol quickly when the police arrived. Some bars had shelves that could tip over, sending bottles down a chute into the sewer. Others had hidden cupboards behind walls. Doors were disguised as bookcases or kitchen cabinets. Lookouts watched the street and pressed a button to warn the bar inside. Then the bartender quickly hid everything. One famous speakeasy, later known as the 21 Club, had a secret wine cellar. It was hidden behind a door made of brick, which weighed about two tonnes. It looked exactly like a solid wall. It could only be opened by pushing a thin metal rod into a small hole. Even so, many raids still succeeded. Yet these tricks have become part of the legend of the time. Some of these old hidden rooms still survive in New York today. Visitors can still see the secret doors.
===
IMG: cocktail-glasses-bar-counter-shaker-1920s | a polished wooden bar counter in the 1920s with several cocktail glasses, a silver cocktail shaker, slices of lemon and a small bowl of cherries, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft amber light
HEAD: Cocktails became popular partly because they could hide the taste of poor-quality alcohol.
BODY: Mixed drinks had existed long before the 1920s. Yet Prohibition helped make cocktails fashionable. One reason was taste. Much of the alcohol sold in speakeasies was rough and unpleasant. Mixing it with fruit juice, sugar, honey or soda made it much easier to drink. Bartenders created new recipes with clever names. The bee's knees was made with gin, lemon and honey. Sidecars and gin rickeys were also popular. Even so, many of the best American bartenders left the country during Prohibition. They went to work in Paris, London and Havana, where drinking was legal. There, they invented or popularised many famous drinks. Yet in New York, the speakeasy bartender also became a kind of artist. He mixed, shook and stirred behind closed doors. When Prohibition ended, the cocktail culture remained. Many drinks that are still popular today were first made or made famous during these years.

TOPIC: A city of six million
===
IMG: manhattan-skyline-1925-harbour-ferry-morning | the Manhattan skyline in 1925 seen from the harbour in the morning, early skyscrapers with towers and spires, small steam ferries and tugboats on the water, soft hazy golden light
HEAD: In 1925, New York was one of the largest cities in the world, with around six million people.
BODY: By the middle of the 1920s, New York City was enormous. Around six million people lived in its five boroughs. These were Manhattan, Brooklyn, the Bronx, Queens and Staten Island. They had been joined together into one city in 1898. By some measures, New York had just passed London to become the largest city on earth. It was a city of contrasts. There were great skyscrapers and crowded tenements. There were millionaires on Fifth Avenue and poor families on the Lower East Side. Ships came and went from its harbour every day. Trains carried thousands of commuters into the city each morning. Even so, New York was not one single place. It was a collection of neighbourhoods, each with its own character. Yet together they made a city that seemed full of energy. Visitors often described the noise, the speed and the excitement of New York in the twenties.
===
IMG: lower-east-side-street-pushcarts-crowds-1925 | a crowded street on the Lower East Side of New York in 1925 with rows of pushcarts selling fruit, clothes and pots, tall brick tenements with fire escapes, people shopping, warm afternoon light
HEAD: The Lower East Side of Manhattan was one of the most crowded neighbourhoods in the world.
BODY: The Lower East Side lay in the southern part of Manhattan. For decades, it had been the first home of many immigrants. Jewish families from Eastern Europe, Italians, Irish and many others lived there. The streets were lined with tall brick buildings called tenements. Families lived in small apartments, often sharing them with relatives or boarders. In the early 1900s, it was one of the most crowded places on earth. Its streets were busy all day. Pushcart sellers lined the pavements. They sold fruit, vegetables, fish, clothes, pots and pans. Customers bargained loudly in many languages. Children played in the streets, because there was no room indoors. Even so, by 1925 the area was changing. Many families who had saved some money moved to newer neighbourhoods in Brooklyn and the Bronx. Yet the Lower East Side remained full of life, with its synagogues, cafés, theatres and markets.
===
IMG: ellis-island-great-hall-immigrants-1920s | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a high vaulted tiled ceiling, the great registration hall of Ellis Island in the early 1920s with immigrants carrying bags and bundles waiting in lines on long benches, soft light through tall arched windows
HEAD: Millions of immigrants had passed through Ellis Island, but a new law in 1924 slowed their arrival.
BODY: Ellis Island is a small island in New York Harbor. From 1892, it was the main entry point for immigrants to the United States. More than twelve million people passed through its halls. They arrived on crowded ships from Europe. In the great hall, they waited in long lines. Doctors examined them for signs of illness. Officials asked them questions about their names, their jobs and where they were going. Most were allowed to enter after a few hours. Even so, the 1920s brought great change. Many Americans wanted fewer immigrants. In 1924, Congress passed a strict new law. It set low limits on the number of people allowed to come, especially from southern and eastern Europe. After that, the number of arrivals fell sharply. Yet the families who had already come stayed. In 1925, millions of New Yorkers had been born abroad, or were the children of immigrants. They gave the city its rich mix of languages and cultures.
===
IMG: neighbourhood-little-italy-mulberry-street-festival | a street in Little Italy in New York in the 1920s decorated with strings of lights and banners for a festival, families walking, food stalls and a small band, warm evening light
HEAD: Neighbourhoods like Little Italy kept the food, music and festivals of the old country alive.
BODY: Many immigrants in New York lived near people from their own country. This created neighbourhoods with strong identities. Little Italy, around Mulberry Street, was home to many Italian families. There were Italian bakeries, butchers and cafés. People spoke Italian in the shops and on the street. Each year, the neighbourhood held religious festivals in honour of saints from their home towns in Italy. The streets were decorated with arches of lights. Bands played and families walked together. Food stalls sold sausages, pastries and sweets. Other neighbourhoods had their own character too. There was Chinatown, the Jewish Lower East Side and the German area of Yorkville. Even so, these neighbourhoods were never closed off. People mixed at work, in schools and on the subway. Yet they gave immigrants a sense of home in a huge and unfamiliar city. Some of the festivals that began in those years are still held today.
===
IMG: brownstone-stoop-neighbours-summer-evening | neighbours sitting and talking on the stone front steps of brownstone houses in a New York street on a warm summer evening in the 1920s, children playing nearby, soft golden light
HEAD: On warm evenings, New Yorkers sat on the front steps of their buildings, called stoops, to talk with neighbours.
BODY: Many New York houses had a set of stone steps leading up to the front door. These were called stoops. The word came from Dutch, from the days when New York was a Dutch colony called New Amsterdam. In summer, the apartments inside were often hot and stuffy. There was no air conditioning in ordinary homes. So people came outside in the evening. They sat on the stoop to cool off and talk. Neighbours gathered to share news and gossip. Children played games on the pavement. Older people watched the street and greeted passers-by. Even so, stoop life was more than a way to keep cool. It created a sense of community. People knew their neighbours and watched out for each other's children. Yet as the century went on, life slowly moved indoors. Radios and other comforts kept people inside. In 1925, though, the stoop was still the heart of many New York streets.
===
IMG: fire-escape-sleeping-summer-night-tenement | the iron fire escapes on the front of a brick tenement building in New York on a hot summer night in the 1920s, mattresses and pillows on some of them, warm glowing windows, a starry sky
HEAD: On the hottest nights, many New Yorkers slept on fire escapes, roofs and in parks.
BODY: New York summers could be very hot and humid. In crowded tenements, the small rooms became almost unbearable. Windows were small, and the air did not move. So people found other places to sleep. Many families carried mattresses out onto the iron fire escapes on the front of their buildings. Others climbed up to the flat roofs. Some went to the parks and slept on the grass. Thousands of people slept in Central Park and at the beaches on the hottest nights. Children especially loved sleeping outside. It felt like an adventure. Even so, it could be uncomfortable and sometimes dangerous. People occasionally rolled off fire escapes in their sleep. Yet for many, a night under the stars was the only way to rest during a heat wave. These hot summer nights became a shared memory for generations of New Yorkers who grew up in the old tenements.

TOPIC: Homes and apartments
===
IMG: tenement-apartment-interior-1925-kitchen-window | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a small tenement apartment kitchen in New York in 1925 with a cast iron stove, a sink, a table with an oilcloth cover and a window to an air shaft, laundry hanging, soft daylight
HEAD: Many New York families lived in small tenement apartments of just three or four rooms.
BODY: A tenement was a type of apartment building, usually five or six storeys high. Each floor held several small apartments. A typical apartment had three or four rooms, arranged one behind the other. The kitchen was often the main room. It had a stove, a sink and a table where the family ate, talked and did homework. The other rooms were used for sleeping. Large families often shared a few beds. Older tenements had been built without enough light or air. Some inner rooms had no windows at all. A law passed in 1901 required new buildings to have proper windows, toilets and air shafts. Older buildings had to be improved too. By 1925, many apartments had running water and a toilet inside. Even so, they were still crowded. Yet families made them into homes. They hung curtains, put photographs on the walls and filled the kitchen with the smells of cooking.
===
IMG: new-apartment-building-bronx-grand-concourse-1925 | an elegant new apartment building with Art Deco details on a wide tree-lined boulevard in the Bronx in 1925, families walking on the pavement, soft morning light
HEAD: In the 1920s, many families moved from crowded Manhattan to new apartments in the Bronx and Brooklyn.
BODY: As the subway reached further out, new neighbourhoods grew quickly. The Bronx and Brooklyn were full of new apartment buildings. These were larger and more modern than the old tenements. They had bigger rooms, more windows, hot water and private bathrooms. Some had elevators and courtyards with gardens. One of the most admired streets was the Grand Concourse in the Bronx. It was a wide boulevard lined with trees and handsome buildings. Many families who had grown up on the Lower East Side moved there. They saw it as a step up in the world. Even so, the move was not always easy. People missed the busy streets and the friends they had left behind. Yet the new apartments offered something precious. There was space, light and quiet. For the children, there were parks nearby and better schools. For many immigrant families, moving to the Bronx or Brooklyn was the dream of a better life.
===
IMG: steam-radiator-hissing-apartment-winter-window | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, an old cast iron steam radiator under a frosty window in a New York apartment in winter in the 1920s, a cat sleeping on a chair beside it, warm soft lamplight
HEAD: In winter, New York apartments were heated by steam radiators that hissed and clanked.
BODY: By the 1920s, many New York apartment buildings had central heating. A large coal boiler in the basement heated water and turned it into steam. The steam travelled up through pipes to cast iron radiators in each apartment. The radiators grew very hot. They hissed, banged and clanked as the steam moved through them. For many New Yorkers, these sounds were part of winter. Each building had a superintendent who looked after the boiler. He shovelled coal into the furnace early in the morning. If he was late, the apartments were cold. Tenants sometimes banged on the pipes to let him know. Even so, the heat was often uneven. Some apartments were too hot, and people opened their windows in the middle of winter. Others stayed cold. Yet the radiators brought real comfort. On frosty mornings, families gathered near them, warming their hands and drying their wet gloves.
===
IMG: washing-lines-between-tenements-backyard-sunny | long washing lines strung between the backs of brick tenement buildings in New York in the 1920s, full of white sheets and colourful clothes drying in the sun, fire escapes and windows, bright afternoon light
HEAD: Washing was hung on long lines strung between the backs of tenement buildings.
BODY: Doing the laundry was hard work in 1925. Few families had washing machines. Most clothes were washed by hand in a tub or a sink. Women scrubbed them on a washboard with soap and hot water. Then the wet clothes had to be dried. In crowded tenements, there was no garden. So people strung washing lines from their back windows. The lines ran on pulleys across the yards between buildings. A woman could hang out the washing from her kitchen window and pull it across the yard. When it was dry, she pulled it back. Seen from above, the backyards were criss-crossed with lines of shirts, sheets and dresses. Even so, the city air was often sooty. Clothes could come back greyer than they went out. Yet on a sunny, windy day, the washing dried quickly. The colourful lines between the buildings became one of the classic images of old New York.
===
IMG: superintendent-coal-delivery-chute-basement | a coal delivery truck in front of a New York apartment building in the 1920s, coal sliding down a metal chute through a small basement window, a man in work clothes watching, winter morning light
HEAD: Coal for heating was delivered by truck and poured down a chute into the basement.
BODY: Most New York buildings were heated by coal in the 1920s. The coal had to be delivered regularly, especially in winter. Coal trucks drove through the streets. They stopped in front of buildings and set up a metal chute. The coal slid down the chute through a small window into the basement. It made a loud rumbling noise. A cloud of black dust often rose from the window. In the basement, the coal was stored in a bin near the furnace. The building's superintendent shovelled it into the boiler several times a day. He also took out the heavy ash and put it in cans on the street to be collected. Even so, coal made the city dirty. Smoke from thousands of chimneys darkened the winter sky. Yet it also kept millions of people warm. The sound of coal sliding down a chute was a familiar part of every New York winter.
