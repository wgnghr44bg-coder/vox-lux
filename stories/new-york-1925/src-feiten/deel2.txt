TOPIC: Getting around the city
===
IMG: subway-car-1925-passengers-straps-wicker-seats | an indoor scene, enclosed by the walls of a train car, a plain curved ceiling, the inside of a New York subway car in 1925 with wicker seats, ceiling fans and passengers in hats and coats holding leather straps, soft electric light
HEAD: A ride on the New York subway cost just five cents, and that price stayed the same for more than forty years.
BODY: The New York subway opened in 1904. By 1925, it was one of the busiest railway systems in the world. Millions of people rode it every day. The fare was a nickel, a five-cent coin. Passengers dropped it into a turnstile and walked onto the platform. The price did not change from 1904 until 1948. Even as wages and other prices rose, the nickel fare stayed the same. This made the subway cheap for everyone. Workers could travel from Brooklyn or the Bronx to jobs in Manhattan. In rush hour, the cars were packed. Passengers stood shoulder to shoulder, holding leather straps hanging from the ceiling. Ceiling fans turned slowly overhead. Even so, the subway was a marvel. It ran day and night. Yet it also changed the city. As new lines reached further out, people moved to new neighbourhoods, knowing they could still reach work in under an hour.
===
IMG: elevated-train-third-avenue-steel-tracks-street | an elevated railway train running on steel tracks above a busy New York avenue in 1925, sunlight falling in stripes through the tracks onto the street, shops and people below, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Elevated trains ran above the streets of New York on iron structures, casting shadows on the avenues below.
BODY: Before the subway, New York had elevated railways. They were called the els. Trains ran on iron tracks raised high above the streets on tall columns. The first one opened in 1868. By 1925, there were several el lines in Manhattan, Brooklyn and the Bronx. They ran along avenues like Second, Third, Sixth and Ninth. From the train windows, passengers could look into second-floor apartments and offices. Below, the streets were in shade. Sunlight fell through the tracks in stripes. The trains were very noisy. When they passed, conversations stopped. Dust and sometimes sparks fell onto the street. Even so, the els were useful and cheap. Yet many people disliked them. Shopkeepers and residents complained about the noise and darkness. Over the following decades, most of the els were torn down. Today, only a few elevated lines remain in the outer boroughs, still rattling above the streets.
===
IMG: yellow-taxicabs-1920s-fifth-avenue-traffic | yellow taxicabs and black cars driving along a busy Fifth Avenue in New York in 1925, a traffic policeman on a small tower directing traffic, elegant buildings and shoppers, soft afternoon light
HEAD: New York's taxis began to be painted yellow in the 1910s and 1920s, so they could be seen easily.
BODY: Taxis were a common sight in New York by 1925. Thousands of them drove through the streets of Manhattan. Many were painted yellow. A businessman named John Hertz, who ran a taxi company in Chicago, chose yellow after a study suggested it was the easiest colour to see. Soon, yellow cabs were popular in New York as well. Other taxi companies used different colours. A ride was not cheap, so taxis were mostly used by better-off people. Office workers usually took the subway or walked. Traffic was becoming a real problem. Cars, buses, taxis, trucks and horse-drawn wagons all shared the same streets. To manage it, police officers stood in small towers at busy crossings on Fifth Avenue. They used signals to tell traffic when to stop and go. Even so, the city kept growing. Yet these first traffic towers were an early step towards the traffic lights that would soon appear everywhere.
===
IMG: staten-island-ferry-deck-passengers-skyline | passengers in 1920s coats and hats standing at the rail of a ferry crossing New York Harbor, the Statue of Liberty in the distance, seagulls overhead, soft morning light
HEAD: The Staten Island Ferry carried thousands of passengers across New York Harbor every day.
BODY: New York is a city of islands. Manhattan, Staten Island and much of the rest of the city are surrounded by water. Before many bridges and tunnels were built, ferries were essential. In 1925, dozens of ferry lines crossed the rivers and the harbour. The most famous was the Staten Island Ferry. It sailed between Staten Island and the southern tip of Manhattan. The journey took about twenty-five minutes. It passed close to the Statue of Liberty. Commuters rode it every morning and evening. Some read their newspapers. Others stood at the rail and watched the boats and the skyline. In summer, families took the ferry just for the breeze. Even so, the ferries were slowly being replaced in many places. New bridges and tunnels were built across the rivers. Yet the Staten Island Ferry kept running. It still crosses the harbour today, and the ride is now free.
===
IMG: holland-tunnel-construction-workers-1925 | workers in caps and overalls inside a large round tunnel under construction beneath a river in the 1920s, curved iron rings lining the walls, electric lights, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a curved ceiling
HEAD: In 1925, workers were digging the Holland Tunnel, the first long tunnel for cars under the Hudson River.
BODY: For a long time, the only way to cross the Hudson River between New York and New Jersey was by ferry. As cars became more common, this was too slow. So engineers planned a tunnel for cars beneath the river. It was a huge challenge. Cars produce poisonous exhaust gases. In a long tunnel, these gases could build up and become dangerous. The chief engineer, Clifford Holland, designed a new system of fans and air shafts. Fresh air was blown in through ducts under the road, and dirty air was drawn out through the ceiling. Sadly, Holland died in 1924, before the tunnel was finished. Work continued, and the tunnel was named after him. Even so, building it was slow and hard. Workers dug through mud and rock under the river. Yet when it opened in 1927, it was a great success. The ventilation system worked so well that it became a model for road tunnels around the world.
===
IMG: horse-drawn-ice-wagon-delivery-street-1925 | a horse-drawn wagon delivering large blocks of ice on a New York street in 1925, the iceman carrying a block with metal tongs on his shoulder, children watching, warm morning light
HEAD: Many New York homes still had iceboxes, and the iceman delivered blocks of ice by horse and wagon.
BODY: In 1925, electric refrigerators were new and expensive. Most families kept their food cool in an icebox. It was a wooden cabinet lined with metal. A large block of ice sat in the top compartment. Cold air flowed down and kept the food below fresh. As the ice melted, the water dripped into a pan underneath. Someone had to empty it every day. The iceman delivered fresh blocks of ice several times a week. He drove a wagon, often still pulled by a horse. He carried the heavy blocks up the stairs on his shoulder, using metal tongs. Families put a card in the window to show how much ice they wanted. On hot days, children followed the wagon and begged for small chips of ice to suck. Even so, the iceman's days were numbered. Within twenty years, most homes would have electric refrigerators. Yet many older New Yorkers remembered the iceman with real affection.

TOPIC: Food in the big city
===
IMG: automat-wall-glass-doors-food-coins | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a bright 1920s automat restaurant in New York with a wall of small glass doors showing sandwiches and pies, a customer putting a coin in a slot, polished chrome and marble, soft electric light
HEAD: At the automat, customers put coins in a slot and took their food from behind a small glass door.
BODY: One of the most popular places to eat in New York was the automat. The most famous were run by a company called Horn and Hardart. An automat looked like a bright, modern café. Along one wall were rows of small glass doors. Behind each door was a dish, such as a sandwich, a slice of pie or a bowl of macaroni. Customers put coins in a slot beside the door. The door unlocked, and they took their food. Behind the wall, workers quickly refilled the empty compartments. Coffee poured from silver spouts shaped like dolphin heads. Prices were low, often just a nickel or a dime. Office workers, students, actors and families all ate there. Even so, the automat was not really automatic. It needed many workers behind the scenes. Yet it felt modern and fun. For many New Yorkers, a meal at the automat was a small daily pleasure.
===
IMG: jewish-deli-counter-pastrami-sandwiches-1925 | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a busy New York delicatessen in 1925 with a long counter, a man slicing pastrami, jars of pickles, salami hanging on hooks, customers waiting, warm light
HEAD: Jewish delicatessens served pastrami, corned beef and pickles to hungry New Yorkers.
BODY: Jewish immigrants from Eastern Europe brought their food traditions to New York. Delicatessens, or delis, became a key part of the city's life. They sold cured and smoked meats like pastrami and corned beef. Sandwiches were piled high with meat on rye bread. Barrels of pickles stood near the counter. Customers could buy smoked fish, potato salad and soups. Many delis stayed open late, serving workers and theatre-goers. Some became famous. Katz's Delicatessen on the Lower East Side had already been open for decades by 1925. It is still open today. Even so, delis were not only places to eat. They were meeting places where people talked, argued and shared news. Yet the food was what brought people back. Over time, pastrami sandwiches, bagels and cheesecake spread far beyond Jewish neighbourhoods. They became symbols of New York food for everyone.
===
IMG: hot-dog-stand-coney-island-nathans-1920s | a busy hot dog stand at Coney Island in the 1920s with a long counter, men in white jackets serving hot dogs, a crowd of beachgoers in summer clothes, bright sunshine
HEAD: Nathan's hot dogs at Coney Island cost just five cents.
BODY: In 1916, a Polish immigrant named Nathan Handwerker opened a small hot dog stand at Coney Island. He had worked at another stand that sold hot dogs for ten cents. Nathan sold his for just five cents. At first, some people were suspicious. They wondered why his hot dogs were so cheap. According to a popular story, Nathan hired men to wear white coats and eat at his stand. They looked like doctors. That made customers believe the food was good. Whether the story is true or not, the stand became hugely popular. When the subway reached Coney Island in 1920, crowds arrived from all over the city. Many of them headed straight for Nathan's. Even so, Nathan worked very hard. He and his wife ran the stand themselves for years. Yet his business grew and grew. Nathan's Famous is still at Coney Island today, more than a hundred years later.
===
IMG: pushcart-fruit-seller-street-apples-oranges | an elderly street vendor with a wooden pushcart full of apples, oranges and pears on a New York street in 1925, customers choosing fruit, tall brick buildings behind, soft morning light
HEAD: Pushcarts sold fresh fruit, vegetables and many other goods on the streets of New York.
BODY: Pushcarts were everywhere in the poorer neighbourhoods of New York. They were simple wooden carts with two wheels and long handles. Sellers pushed them through the streets or parked them along the pavements. They sold fruit, vegetables, fish, bread, clothes, shoes, hats and household goods. Many pushcart sellers were recent immigrants. A cart did not cost much to rent. It allowed people to start a small business with very little money. In some streets, hundreds of carts stood side by side, forming an outdoor market. Prices were low, and customers bargained hard. Even so, the city saw the carts as a problem. They blocked traffic and made the streets crowded and dirty. Officials began to build covered markets to replace them. The best known were built in the 1930s. Yet in 1925, the pushcarts were still at the heart of daily shopping for millions of ordinary New Yorkers.
===
IMG: family-dinner-tenement-kitchen-table-1925 | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, an immigrant family sitting down to dinner at a small kitchen table in a New York tenement apartment in 1925, a pot of soup, bread and a lamp, warm cosy light
HEAD: Many immigrant families kept cooking the dishes of their home countries in their small New York kitchens.
BODY: Food was one of the strongest links between immigrants and their homelands. In the small kitchens of New York tenements, mothers and grandmothers cooked the meals they had learned as children. Italian families made pasta and tomato sauce. Jewish families prepared chicken soup, brisket and challah bread for the Sabbath. Irish families ate stews and soda bread. Chinese families cooked rice and vegetables with familiar spices. Ingredients could be bought in neighbourhood shops that imported them from abroad. Even so, cooking in a tenement was not easy. Kitchens were small. Many had only a simple gas stove and a sink. Yet families made do. Meals were times for the family to gather and talk. Children learned the old recipes from their parents. Over time, these home kitchens shaped American food. Dishes like pizza and bagels moved from immigrant tables to restaurants across the whole country.

TOPIC: Work in the twenties
===
IMG: garment-district-sewing-machines-workers-loft | an indoor scene, enclosed by brick walls, a plain ceiling, a large garment workshop in New York in 1925 with rows of women working at sewing machines, piles of fabric, tall windows letting in daylight
HEAD: New York's garment district made a large share of all the clothes worn in America.
BODY: In the 1920s, New York was the centre of the American clothing industry. Most ready-made dresses, coats and suits in the country were made there. The industry was concentrated in an area of Manhattan called the garment district. It lay on the west side, around Seventh Avenue. Tall loft buildings were filled with workshops. Rows of workers, many of them immigrant women, sat at sewing machines. Others cut fabric, pressed seams and sewed on buttons. Racks of finished clothes were pushed through the streets to showrooms and shops. Even so, the work was hard. Hours were long and pay was low. Fires were a serious danger. Workers remembered the terrible Triangle fire of 1911, in which many young women died. After that disaster, new safety laws were passed. Unions grew stronger. Yet the garment district remained busy for decades. Seventh Avenue is still sometimes called Fashion Avenue today.
===
IMG: office-typists-1925-skyscraper-desks-windows | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a large bright office in a New York skyscraper in 1925 with rows of young women typing on typewriters at wooden desks, tall windows overlooking the city, soft daylight
HEAD: Thousands of young women worked as typists and secretaries in New York's office buildings.
BODY: The 1920s brought a big change in the world of work. More and more companies needed people to type letters, file papers and answer telephones. Many of these jobs went to young women. They worked as typists, stenographers, secretaries and telephone operators. Most had learned their skills at special business schools. Every morning, crowds of young women in neat dresses and hats poured out of the subway stations. They walked into the tall office buildings of Manhattan. Some lived at home with their families. Others shared small apartments or lived in women's hotels. Their wages were modest, but they had their own money. Even so, most were expected to give up work when they married. Yet these jobs gave many women a new kind of independence. They could buy their own clothes, go to the movies and enjoy the city. For many young women of the twenties, an office job was a door to a new life.
===
IMG: dockworkers-hudson-piers-cargo-ships-1925 | dockworkers in caps and work clothes unloading crates from a large cargo ship at a pier on the Hudson River in 1925, cranes and ropes, warehouses behind, soft morning light
HEAD: The piers along the Hudson and East rivers made New York the busiest port in the world.
BODY: New York's harbour was one of its greatest strengths. Ships could dock along miles of piers in Manhattan, Brooklyn and New Jersey. In the 1920s, it was the busiest port in the world. Cargo ships arrived from Europe, South America, Asia and Africa. Great ocean liners brought passengers from Britain, France and Germany. They docked at piers on the west side of Manhattan. The docks employed many thousands of men. Dockworkers, called longshoremen, loaded and unloaded cargo by hand. They carried sacks of coffee, bales of cotton and crates of machinery. The work was heavy and dangerous. Jobs were often handed out each morning at the pier gate. Men gathered and waited to be chosen. Even so, the docks were full of life. Tugboats pushed huge ships into position. Gulls cried overhead. Yet over the following decades, shipping moved to new container ports in New Jersey. Many of the old Manhattan piers became parks.
===
IMG: newsboy-selling-papers-corner-1925-cap | a young newsboy in a flat cap and knee-length trousers holding up a newspaper on a busy New York street corner in 1925, a stack of papers under his arm, people passing, soft morning light
HEAD: Newsboys sold papers on every busy street corner, shouting out the day's headlines.
BODY: Newspapers were the main source of news in 1925. New York had many of them, in English and in other languages. Some came out in the morning, others in the evening. They were sold on the streets by newsboys. Most were boys between about ten and fifteen years old. They bought papers from the publishers and sold them for a small profit. They stood at busy corners, outside subway stations and near office buildings. They shouted out the headlines to attract customers. Extra, extra, read all about it, was a typical cry. Some newsboys worked before school and others after school. For poor families, the money was very important. Even so, the work could be hard. The boys stood out in all weather. Yet many grew up to be successful. Some famous Americans began their working lives selling newspapers on the street. They remembered it as a good school for life.

TOPIC: Jazz and the Harlem Renaissance
===
IMG: harlem-lenox-avenue-evening-1925-lights | Lenox Avenue in Harlem, New York, on a warm evening in 1925, people in elegant clothes strolling past brownstone buildings and lit shop windows, a club sign glowing softly, warm evening light
HEAD: In the 1920s, Harlem became the centre of Black culture in America.
BODY: Harlem is a neighbourhood in the north of Manhattan. In the early 1900s, many Black families began moving there. Some came from other parts of New York. Many came from the southern United States, where they faced harsh laws and violence. Others came from the Caribbean. By the 1920s, Harlem had become the largest Black community in America. It was a place of churches, newspapers, theatres, nightclubs and shops. It was also a place of poverty and crowded housing. Yet it was full of creative energy. Writers, poets, musicians, painters and thinkers made it their home. This flowering of art and ideas became known as the Harlem Renaissance. Even so, Harlem's residents still faced discrimination. Some of its most famous clubs did not even allow Black customers. Yet the Harlem Renaissance changed American culture. It brought Black voices, stories and music to the centre of the nation's life.
===
IMG: jazz-band-small-stage-trumpet-piano-1925 | a small jazz band on a stage in a Harlem club in 1925 with a trumpet player, a pianist, a drummer and a saxophonist, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm golden stage light
HEAD: In 1924 and 1925, a young trumpet player named Louis Armstrong played in New York and changed the sound of jazz.
BODY: Jazz was the music of the twenties. It began in New Orleans and spread north with Black musicians. By the 1920s, New York had become one of its great centres. In 1924, a young trumpet player from New Orleans arrived in the city. His name was Louis Armstrong. He joined the band of Fletcher Henderson, one of the leading bandleaders in New York. The band played at the Roseland Ballroom in Manhattan. Armstrong's playing amazed other musicians. He played with great warmth, power and freedom. He made the solo, where one musician takes the lead, a central part of jazz. In late 1925, he returned to Chicago. Even so, his time in New York had a lasting effect. Many musicians began to copy his style. Yet Armstrong was just one of many great players in the city. Pianists, singers and bandleaders filled the clubs of Harlem and Broadway with new music every night.
===
IMG: langston-hughes-poet-writing-small-room-window | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a young Black poet in a 1920s shirt and tie writing at a small table by a window in a Harlem room, books and papers around him, soft evening light
HEAD: The poet Langston Hughes published his first book of poems in 1926, after winning a prize in 1925.
BODY: Langston Hughes was one of the great voices of the Harlem Renaissance. He was born in Missouri in 1901. As a young man, he worked many jobs and travelled widely. He wrote poems about the lives of ordinary Black Americans. His poems used the rhythms of jazz and blues. They were simple, musical and deeply felt. In 1925, one of his poems won a prize from a magazine called Opportunity. This brought him attention. His first book of poems, The Weary Blues, was published in 1926. Even so, Hughes was not only a poet. He also wrote stories, plays, essays and newspaper columns. Yet his poems remain his best-known work. One of his most famous early poems, The Negro Speaks of Rivers, was written when he was still a teenager. In it, he imagines rivers flowing through the history of his people. Hughes lived in Harlem for much of his life.
===
IMG: new-negro-book-cover-1925-library-table | an old hardcover book with a simple decorated cover lying on a wooden library table beside a reading lamp, other books stacked nearby, soft warm lamplight
HEAD: In 1925, a book called The New Negro brought together writers of the Harlem Renaissance.
BODY: In 1925, a scholar named Alain Locke edited an important collection of writing. It was called The New Negro. It included poems, stories, essays and art by many Black writers and artists. Among them were Langston Hughes, Countee Cullen and Zora Neale Hurston. Locke wanted to show a new spirit among Black Americans. He believed they were creating a proud and confident culture of their own. The book was widely read and discussed. It helped make the Harlem Renaissance known across the country. Even so, the writers in the book did not all agree. They had different ideas about art, politics and identity. Some wanted to celebrate folk traditions. Others wanted to show refined, modern art. Yet together, they created a remarkable body of work. Locke's book is still seen as one of the most important collections in American literature. It captured a moment of great hope and creativity in Harlem.
===
IMG: rent-party-apartment-piano-dancing-harlem | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a crowded Harlem apartment at night in the 1920s with people dancing around an upright piano, a pianist playing, small lamps and paper decorations, warm light
HEAD: In Harlem, families held rent parties, with music and dancing, to help pay their rent.
BODY: Rent in Harlem was often very high. Landlords knew that Black families had few other places to live. So many families struggled to pay. One solution was the rent party. A family would invite friends and neighbours to their apartment on a Saturday night. Guests paid a small entry fee, perhaps a quarter. Food and drinks were sold. A pianist played lively music. People danced late into the night. By the morning, the family might have enough money for the rent. Some of the best jazz pianists in New York played at rent parties. Fats Waller and James P. Johnson were among them. They developed a powerful style of piano playing called stride. Even so, rent parties were born from hardship. Yet they were also full of joy and community. They showed how people in Harlem turned difficult times into music, laughter and a shared night together.
