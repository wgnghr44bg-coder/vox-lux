TOPIC: Flappers, fashion and dancing
===
IMG: flapper-bobbed-hair-cloche-hat-street-1925 | a young woman with short bobbed hair, a cloche hat and a knee-length drop-waist dress walking confidently along a New York street in 1925, shop windows behind her, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Young women of the twenties who cut their hair short and wore shorter dresses were called flappers.
BODY: The flapper was one of the great symbols of the 1920s. She was a young woman who broke with old rules. She cut her long hair into a short style called a bob. She wore dresses that ended just below the knee. That was much shorter than before. Her dresses were loose and straight, without a tight waist. She wore a small, close-fitting hat called a cloche. She danced, drove cars, smoked and went to jazz clubs. Older people were often shocked. Newspapers wrote about flappers with both excitement and alarm. Even so, most young women were not wild flappers. Many simply enjoyed the new fashions and freedoms in small ways. Yet the flapper image was powerful. It stood for youth, energy and change. The word flapper may have come from a young bird flapping its wings as it learns to fly. It suited a generation trying out new freedoms.
===
IMG: barber-shop-woman-getting-bob-haircut-1920s | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a young woman sitting in a barber's chair getting her long hair cut into a short bob in a 1920s New York barber shop, mirrors and combs, soft daylight
HEAD: When women began cutting their hair short, many went to men's barber shops, because hairdressers did not yet know how.
BODY: Before the 1920s, most women in America had long hair. They wore it pinned up on their heads. Cutting it short was a dramatic step. When the bob became fashionable, many women wanted one. Yet traditional women's hairdressers were not used to cutting short styles. So many women went to men's barber shops instead. Barbers were skilled at short haircuts. Some barbers were happy to have new customers. Others felt uncomfortable with women in their shops. Some refused to cut women's hair at all. Families sometimes argued about it. Fathers and husbands could be upset when a daughter or wife came home with short hair. Some employers even objected. Even so, the bob spread quickly. By the middle of the decade, it was very common. Yet for many women, the first short haircut was a moment of real courage. It was a simple, visible sign that times were changing.

IMG: charleston-dance-couples-ballroom-1925 | young couples dancing the Charleston with lively kicking steps in a large ballroom in New York in 1925, a band playing on a stage, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a high plain ceiling, warm golden light
HEAD: The Charleston was the most famous dance of the 1920s.
BODY: The 1920s were a dancing decade. In ballrooms, clubs and even at home, people danced to the new jazz music. The most famous dance of all was the Charleston. It was fast and energetic. Dancers swung their legs and kicked their feet out to the sides. Their arms moved in time with the music. The dance had roots in Black communities in the South. It became famous across America after it appeared in a Broadway show called Runnin' Wild in 1923. The song The Charleston, written by James P. Johnson, was a great hit. Soon, the dance was everywhere. People held Charleston contests. Some ballrooms put up signs warning against it, because the floors shook too much. Even so, it was mostly harmless fun. Yet many older people found it shocking. For the young, the Charleston was pure joy. It captured the restless, cheerful spirit of the twenties better than almost anything else.

IMG: department-store-window-display-fifth-avenue-dresses | elegant 1920s dresses, hats and shoes displayed in a large department store window on Fifth Avenue in New York, women in coats looking in, soft late afternoon light reflecting on the glass
HEAD: New York's great department stores set the fashions for the whole country.
BODY: Shopping was one of the great pleasures of New York in the 1920s. The city had huge department stores. Macy's, on Herald Square, was one of the largest stores in the world. Fifth Avenue was lined with elegant shops selling the latest fashions from Paris and New York. Store windows were carefully decorated. Crowds stopped to look at the newest dresses, hats and shoes. Inside, there were floors of clothing, furniture, toys and household goods. Lifts carried shoppers up and down. Some stores had restaurants, beauty salons and even small libraries. Even so, many people could not afford these stores. They bought cheaper clothes from smaller shops or from catalogues. Many women still sewed their own clothes, copying the styles they saw in the windows. Yet the department stores shaped what Americans wanted to wear. Fashion magazines and newspapers reported on their displays. What appeared in New York windows soon appeared across the country.
===
IMG: men-1925-suits-fedora-straw-boater-street | men in 1920s suits, fedora hats and straw boater hats walking along a busy New York sidewalk on a summer morning, some reading newspapers, soft warm light
HEAD: In the 1920s, almost every man in New York wore a hat when he went outside.
BODY: Fashion changed for men too in the 1920s, though more slowly than for women. Suits became a little looser and more comfortable. Trousers were wider. In summer, many men wore light-coloured suits and straw hats called boaters. In cooler months, they wore soft felt hats, such as fedoras. Hats were almost essential. A man without a hat on the street looked strange. There was even an unwritten rule about when to change hats. In the middle of September, men were expected to stop wearing straw hats. In some years, young men knocked straw hats off the heads of those who were late to change. Even so, these were mostly playful pranks. Young men in college also liked baggy trousers and colourful sweaters. Yet the basic outfit stayed the same. A suit, a shirt with a tie and a hat were what most working men wore every day in New York.

TOPIC: Radio, newspapers and the movies
===
IMG: family-gathered-around-radio-living-room-1925 | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a family gathered around a large wooden radio set with a horn speaker in their New York living room in 1925, children sitting on the floor listening, a warm table lamp
HEAD: In the 1920s, radio brought music, news and voices into American homes for the first time.
BODY: Radio was one of the great new inventions of the twenties. Regular public broadcasting began in the United States in 1920. By the middle of the decade, millions of Americans owned radios. New York had several important stations. Families gathered around their radio sets in the evening. Early radios were large wooden boxes. Some had a horn-shaped speaker. Others needed headphones. Listeners tuned in carefully, turning knobs until the voice or music became clear. There were dance bands, news, sports, lectures and religious services. Comedy shows and dramas soon followed. Even so, early radio could be crackly and hard to hear. Yet people were amazed by it. For the first time, they could hear a concert or a speech from far away, as it happened. Radio made the country feel smaller. Families in small towns and big cities listened to the same programmes on the same evening.

IMG: tabloid-newspapers-newsstand-1925-headlines | a small newsstand on a New York street corner in 1925 with stacks of newspapers and magazines, a vendor in a cap, people buying papers, soft morning light
HEAD: New York's tabloid newspapers, with big photographs and short stories, became the most popular papers in America.
BODY: In 1919, a new kind of newspaper appeared in New York. It was called the Daily News. It was smaller than traditional newspapers, so it was easy to read on the crowded subway. This size was called a tabloid. It had large photographs and short, lively stories. It focused on crime, sports, gossip and human interest. Serious newspapers looked down on it. Yet readers loved it. By the mid 1920s, the Daily News had the largest circulation of any newspaper in the United States. Other tabloids followed. They competed fiercely for readers. Some used dramatic headlines and staged photographs. Even so, New York also had serious newspapers. The New York Times and the New York Herald Tribune reported news from around the world. Yet the tabloids changed journalism. They showed that ordinary readers wanted news that was quick, visual and close to their own lives.

IMG: the-new-yorker-first-issue-1925-dandy-monocle | an old magazine lying on a wooden table with a cover showing an elegant gentleman in a top hat looking at a butterfly through a monocle, a cup of coffee beside it, soft window light
HEAD: The New Yorker magazine first appeared in February 1925.
BODY: In February 1925, a new weekly magazine was launched in New York. It was called The New Yorker. It was founded by Harold Ross and his wife, Jane Grant. Ross wanted a witty, clever magazine for sophisticated city readers. He famously said it would not be edited for the old lady in Dubuque, a small town in Iowa. The first cover showed an elegant gentleman in a top hat. He was looking at a butterfly through a monocle. The figure was later named Eustace Tilley. At first, the magazine struggled. It lost money, and few people bought it. Even so, Ross kept going. Talented writers, artists and cartoonists joined the staff. Yet slowly, the magazine found its readers. It became famous for its short stories, careful reporting and gentle cartoons. Eustace Tilley still appears on the cover once a year, on the magazine's anniversary.

IMG: movie-palace-grand-lobby-chandeliers-1920s | an indoor scene, enclosed by ornate walls, a high decorated ceiling with chandeliers, the grand lobby of a 1920s New York movie palace with red carpets, gold decorations and a wide staircase, people in evening clothes, warm golden light
HEAD: New York's great movie palaces seated thousands of people and looked like royal palaces.
BODY: Going to the movies was one of the most popular activities of the 1920s. In New York, films were shown in huge, beautiful theatres. They were called movie palaces. They had grand lobbies with chandeliers, marble staircases, gold decorations and thick carpets. Ushers in uniforms showed people to their seats. Some theatres seated several thousand people. The Roxy Theatre, which opened in 1927, had nearly six thousand seats. Before the main film, there were often live shows with singers, dancers and an orchestra. In 1925, most films were still silent. A pianist or an organist played music to go with the pictures. Words appeared on the screen between scenes. Even so, sound was coming. In 1927, The Jazz Singer became the first major film with spoken words. Yet in 1925, audiences were happy with silent films. For a small price, anyone could spend an evening in a palace, watching stories of adventure, love and laughter.

IMG: broadway-times-square-lights-night-1925 | Times Square and Broadway at night in 1925 with bright electric signs and theatre marquees glowing, crowds of people in evening clothes, cars and taxis, warm sparkling light
HEAD: Broadway was called the Great White Way, because of its bright electric lights.
BODY: Broadway is a long avenue that runs the length of Manhattan. Around Times Square, it became the centre of American theatre. Dozens of theatres stood on and near it. In the 1920s, it was busier than ever. In some seasons, more than two hundred new shows opened. There were dramas, comedies, musicals and revues. The Ziegfeld Follies featured beautiful costumes, dancers and comedians. At night, Times Square was covered in electric signs. They advertised shows, products and restaurants. The lights were so bright that the area was called the Great White Way. Visitors from all over the world came to see it. Even so, the lights were not only for theatres. They also advertised chewing gum, cigarettes and drinks. Yet the effect was magical. In 1925, Times Square at night was one of the most famous sights in the world. It still glows brightly today, though now with very different signs.

IMG: great-gatsby-book-1925-long-island-mansion-lights | an elegant mansion on the water at night with lights glowing from its windows and a green light shining at the end of a distant dock across the bay, a calm and dreamy atmosphere
HEAD: In April 1925, F. Scott Fitzgerald published The Great Gatsby, a novel set near New York.
BODY: One of the most famous American novels was published in 1925. It was called The Great Gatsby. It was written by F. Scott Fitzgerald. The story takes place in the summer of 1922, in New York City and on Long Island. It tells of a mysterious, wealthy man named Jay Gatsby. He holds enormous parties at his mansion. He is in love with a woman named Daisy, who lives across the bay. At night, Gatsby looks across the water at a green light on her dock. The novel describes the glamour and emptiness of the rich in the twenties. It is full of parties, cars, music and secret drinking. Even so, the book did not sell well at first. Fitzgerald was disappointed. Yet after his death, it became one of the most loved books in America. Today, millions of students read it. For many people, The Great Gatsby is the picture of New York in the Jazz Age.

TOPIC: Skyscrapers
===
IMG: woolworth-building-gothic-tower-1925-clouds | the tall Gothic-style Woolworth Building with its pointed copper roof and detailed white terracotta decoration rising above lower buildings in New York in 1925, soft clouds and golden morning light
HEAD: In 1925, the tallest building in the world was the Woolworth Building in Manhattan.
BODY: New York was famous for its skyscrapers. In 1925, the tallest building in the world was the Woolworth Building. It stood in lower Manhattan. It had been finished in 1913. It rose fifty-seven floors and about two hundred and forty metres. It was built for Frank Woolworth, who owned a chain of cheap stores where most items cost five or ten cents. He paid for the building in cash. Its style was Gothic, with pointed arches, carved decorations and a green copper roof. People called it the Cathedral of Commerce. Inside, the lobby had a glittering mosaic ceiling. Small carvings showed the architect and Woolworth himself, counting coins. Even so, the building was more than a symbol of wealth. It was also a triumph of engineering. Its steel frame and fast lifts made such height possible. Yet within a few years, new towers would pass it. The race for height was about to begin.
===
IMG: setback-skyscraper-wedding-cake-shape-1925 | a tall 1920s New York skyscraper with a stepped wedding cake shape, each upper section set back from the one below, terraces and stone decorations, soft afternoon light
HEAD: A law of 1916 gave New York's skyscrapers their stepped wedding-cake shapes.
BODY: In the early 1900s, some skyscrapers rose straight up from the pavement like tall boxes. They blocked sunlight and air from the streets below. In 1915, a huge building called the Equitable Building was finished. It cast a shadow over several blocks. People complained. In 1916, New York passed a new zoning law. It required tall buildings to step back as they rose higher. Each upper section had to be smaller than the one below. This let light reach the street. As a result, skyscrapers of the 1920s took on a new shape. They looked like giant stepped pyramids or wedding cakes. Architects made the most of this. They decorated the terraces and set-backs with stone carvings. Even so, the law did not stop the race for height. A tower could still rise very high, as long as it covered only part of the site. Yet the stepped shape gave the New York skyline its famous romantic look.
===
IMG: ironworkers-steel-beams-high-above-city-1920s | ironworkers in caps and work clothes balancing on steel beams high above New York City in the late 1920s, the city stretching far below, clear blue sky and bright sunlight
HEAD: Many of the ironworkers who built New York's skyscrapers were Mohawk men from Canada.
BODY: Building a skyscraper was dangerous work. Ironworkers walked along narrow steel beams hundreds of metres above the street. They bolted and riveted the beams together to form the frame of the building. Among the most famous ironworkers were Mohawk men. They came from Kahnawake, a Mohawk community near Montreal in Canada. They had begun working on steel bridges in the late 1800s. They quickly gained a reputation as skilled and fearless workers on high steel. In the 1920s, many Mohawk families moved to Brooklyn, so that the men could work on New York's buildings and bridges. They helped build many of the city's famous skyscrapers. Even so, they were not truly without fear. They were careful and experienced. Yet their skill became legendary. Some families have worked as ironworkers for several generations. Mohawk ironworkers still help build New York's towers today.

IMG: architects-drawing-office-skyscraper-plans | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a large architects' drawing office in New York in 1925 with draughtsmen at tilted drawing boards working on detailed plans of a tall building, soft daylight from big windows
HEAD: Drawings by the artist Hugh Ferriss imagined the city of the future as a world of giant towers in mist and shadow.
BODY: In the 1920s, many people were excited by the idea of the city of the future. One artist captured this dream very powerfully. His name was Hugh Ferriss. He was an architect who specialised in drawing buildings. He made large charcoal drawings of skyscrapers. They were dark and dramatic. Huge towers rose out of mist, lit by searchlights from below. He showed how the new zoning law could create mountain-like buildings. In 1929, he published a book called The Metropolis of Tomorrow. It imagined a city of enormous towers, spaced apart with wide avenues between them. His drawings influenced architects and film-makers for many years. Even so, the real city never looked exactly like his visions. Yet his work helped people imagine what New York might become. Some of his drawings feel like dreams. They show a city that is grand, quiet and slightly mysterious, glowing in the dark.

IMG: chrysler-building-plans-race-for-height-1928 | a tall silver Art Deco skyscraper spire with curved steel crowns and triangular windows rising into a clear evening sky over New York, soft golden light
HEAD: In the late 1920s, New York's builders raced each other to create the tallest building in the world.
BODY: By the late 1920s, a great race for height began. Several companies wanted the tallest building in the world. The car maker Walter Chrysler planned a new tower on Lexington Avenue. At the same time, a bank was building a tall tower on Wall Street. Both wanted to be the tallest. The Chrysler Building's architect had a secret plan. Inside the tower, his team assembled a long steel spire. In 1929, they raised it through the roof in about ninety minutes. It made the Chrysler Building the tallest in the world. Its shining steel crown, with curved arches and triangular windows, became one of the most beautiful sights in the city. Even so, its record did not last long. In 1931, the Empire State Building opened. It was even taller. Yet in 1925, all of this still lay ahead. The city was only beginning to dream about its tallest towers.

TOPIC: Weekends and summer days
===
IMG: coney-island-beach-crowds-1925-boardwalk | a crowded beach at Coney Island in 1925 with thousands of people in old-fashioned swimsuits, colourful umbrellas, a long wooden boardwalk and amusement rides behind, bright summer sunshine
HEAD: On hot summer Sundays, a million people could crowd onto the beach at Coney Island.
BODY: Coney Island is a seaside area at the southern edge of Brooklyn. In the 1920s, it was the city's great playground. When the subway reached it in 1920, a trip there cost just a nickel. On hot summer weekends, enormous crowds came. On some days, newspapers said a million people were at the beach. The sand was covered with people, umbrellas and picnic baskets. Children built sandcastles and splashed in the waves. In 1923, a long wooden boardwalk was opened along the beach. People strolled on it, eating ice cream and hot dogs. Behind the boardwalk were amusement parks with rides, games and sideshows. Even so, the crowds could be overwhelming. It was hard to find space even to sit down. Yet for working families from hot tenements, Coney Island was a paradise. A day at the sea, with sun, fresh air and a cool swim, was one of the great joys of a New York summer.

IMG: wonder-wheel-coney-island-evening-lights | a large old Ferris wheel with swinging cars glowing with lights at Coney Island on a summer evening in the 1920s, a crowd strolling below, a pink and orange sunset sky
HEAD: The Wonder Wheel at Coney Island opened in 1920 and still turns today.
BODY: Coney Island was famous for its amusement rides. One of the best known was the Wonder Wheel. It was a giant Ferris wheel, opened in 1920. It was about forty-five metres tall. It was unusual because some of its cars slid along curved tracks as the wheel turned. They swung outwards and inwards, giving riders a small thrill. Other cars stayed still at the edge of the wheel, for those who preferred a calm ride. From the top, riders could see the ocean, the beach and the whole of Coney Island. At night, the wheel glowed with lights. Nearby were roller coasters, carousels and fun houses. In 1927, the famous Cyclone roller coaster opened. Even so, many visitors simply walked around, enjoying the lights and music. Yet the Wonder Wheel became a symbol of Coney Island. It has kept turning for more than a century and is still a favourite ride today.

IMG: central-park-sunday-strollers-lake-rowboats | families and couples in 1920s clothes strolling along a path beside a lake in Central Park on a sunny Sunday afternoon, rowboats on the water, tall trees and the city skyline beyond, soft golden light
HEAD: On Sundays, families walked, rowed boats and picnicked in Central Park.
BODY: Central Park lies in the middle of Manhattan. It had been designed in the 1850s by Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux. By the 1920s, it was one of the most loved places in the city. On Sundays, families came to walk, picnic and relax. Couples rented rowboats on the lake. Children sailed toy boats on a small pond. There was a zoo with lions, bears and monkeys. Bands played in the open air. People rode horses on the bridle paths. In winter, they skated on the frozen lake. For people living in crowded apartments, the park was a precious space. It offered trees, grass, fresh air and quiet, right in the heart of the city. Even so, the park was not always in good condition in the 1920s. Some areas were neglected. Yet it remained a place of peace. Its winding paths and green meadows still give New Yorkers a calm escape from the busy streets around them.

IMG: balto-statue-dog-central-park-bronze | a bronze statue of a sled dog standing on a rock in a shady part of Central Park, children gently touching it, autumn leaves on the ground, soft afternoon light
HEAD: In December 1925, a statue of Balto, a famous sled dog, was unveiled in Central Park.
BODY: In January 1925, a terrible illness called diphtheria broke out in Nome, a small town in Alaska. The town needed medicine quickly. But it was winter, and planes could not fly in the cold. So a relay of dog sled teams carried the medicine across Alaska. They travelled more than a thousand kilometres through snow and storms. The final stretch was led by a dog named Balto. When the medicine arrived, the town was saved. The story was reported in newspapers across America. People were deeply moved. In December 1925, a bronze statue of Balto was placed in Central Park. Balto himself attended the ceremony. Even so, some people felt that other dogs deserved more credit. A dog named Togo had run the longest and hardest part of the journey. Yet Balto became the most famous. Children still climb on his statue today. Its bronze back has been polished smooth by many small hands.

IMG: yankee-stadium-1920s-crowd-baseball-game | a large baseball stadium in the Bronx in the 1920s packed with crowds in hats, a game in progress on the green field, flags on the roof, bright afternoon sunshine
HEAD: Yankee Stadium, opened in 1923, was called the House That Ruth Built.
BODY: Baseball was the most popular sport in America in the 1920s. New York had three major league teams. The most famous was the New York Yankees. Their biggest star was Babe Ruth. He hit more home runs than anyone had ever imagined. Crowds came to see him play. In 1923, the Yankees opened a huge new stadium in the Bronx. It was called Yankee Stadium. Because Ruth drew such large crowds, people called it the House That Ruth Built. In his first game there, he hit a home run. Even so, 1925 was a difficult year for Ruth. He fell ill early in the season and missed many games. He also argued with his manager and was fined. Yet the same year brought a quiet beginning. On the first of June 1925, a young player named Lou Gehrig began a remarkable run. He played in every Yankees game for the next fourteen years. Ruth and Gehrig together made the Yankees famous.

IMG: stickball-children-street-broom-handle-1925 | children playing stickball with a broom handle and a small rubber ball on a narrow New York street in 1925, parked cars and tenement buildings, other children watching from the stoops, warm afternoon light
HEAD: City children played stickball in the streets, using a broom handle as a bat.
BODY: Most children in New York did not have a backyard or a playing field. So they played in the streets. One of the favourite games was stickball. It was a simple form of baseball. Instead of a bat, players used an old broom handle. Instead of a hard baseball, they used a small, soft rubber ball. The bases might be a manhole cover, a car fender and a lamp post. Hitting the ball past two sewer covers was a great achievement. Games were stopped whenever a car came down the street. Then they started again. Children also played hopscotch, jump rope, marbles and tag. Even so, playing in the street could be dangerous. As cars became more common, there were more accidents. The city began to build more playgrounds. Yet stickball remained a beloved part of New York childhood for many decades. Some adults still play it today, remembering the streets where they grew up.

HEAD: Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade began in 1924, with live animals borrowed from the zoo.
BODY: In November 1924, the department store Macy's held a parade on Thanksgiving Day. Many of its employees were immigrants. They wanted to celebrate the new American holiday with the kind of festive parade they remembered from Europe. The parade marched from Harlem down to the Macy's store on Herald Square. It had floats, bands and employees dressed as clowns and cowboys. There were also live animals, including bears, elephants and camels, borrowed from the Central Park Zoo. At the end, Santa Claus arrived to welcome the Christmas shopping season. The parade was a great success. It was held again in 1925. In 1927, the famous giant balloons appeared for the first time. They replaced the live animals. Even so, the early parades were simpler and smaller. Yet the tradition grew every year. Today, millions of people watch the parade each Thanksgiving morning, along the streets and at home.

TOPIC: Winter in the city
===
IMG: snow-falling-fifth-avenue-1925-evening-lamps | snow falling softly on Fifth Avenue in New York on a winter evening in 1925, street lamps glowing, a few people with umbrellas and a horse-drawn wagon, warm shop windows, quiet and peaceful
HEAD: When snow fell on New York, thousands of men were hired to shovel the streets.
BODY: New York winters could be cold and snowy. When a big storm came, the city did its best to keep the streets open. In the 1920s, the city hired thousands of extra workers to clear the snow. Many of them were unemployed men who were glad of a few days' work. They shovelled snow into horse-drawn wagons and trucks. The snow was then dumped into the rivers. Some new motor ploughs were used too. Even so, after a heavy storm, the city could be slow for days. Trams and buses got stuck. People walked carefully on icy pavements. Yet snow also brought a special quiet to the city. The noise of traffic was softened. Children came out with sleds and threw snowballs. The parks turned white. For a short time, even busy Manhattan seemed calm and gentle under its white blanket.

IMG: christmas-shop-windows-crowds-1925-department-store | crowds of people in winter coats and hats looking at decorated Christmas windows of a New York department store in 1925, toys, lights and small moving figures in the windows, warm golden light and soft snow
HEAD: Every December, crowds came to see the decorated Christmas windows of the department stores.
BODY: Christmas in New York was a time of lights and shopping. The great department stores decorated their windows with special displays. Some showed toys, dolls and train sets. Others showed winter scenes with snow, trees and small moving figures. Families came from all over the city just to look. Children pressed their faces to the glass. Inside, the stores were full of shoppers. Some stores had a Santa Claus who talked to children and asked what they wanted. Toy departments were crowded with parents and grandparents. Even so, many families could not afford expensive gifts. Children often received simple presents, like an orange, a book or a small toy. Yet the windows were free for everyone. Walking past them in the cold evening air was a treat that cost nothing. Many New Yorkers remembered those Christmas windows as one of the happiest parts of their childhood.

IMG: chestnut-vendor-cart-street-corner-winter-steam | a street vendor roasting chestnuts on a small cart on a New York street corner on a cold winter evening in the 1920s, warm glow from the coals, steam rising, a customer holding a paper bag, soft light
HEAD: On cold days, street vendors sold hot roasted chestnuts and sweet potatoes from small carts.
BODY: Winter brought special foods to the streets of New York. On many corners, vendors set up small carts with charcoal burners. They roasted chestnuts in metal pans over the glowing coals. The smell filled the cold air. Customers bought a paper bag of hot chestnuts for a few cents. They held them in their hands to keep warm as they walked. Other vendors sold baked sweet potatoes, hot pretzels and soup. Many of the chestnut sellers were Italian or Greek immigrants. They worked long hours in the cold. Even so, they were a welcome sight. Their carts glowed in the early darkness of winter evenings. Office workers stopped on their way home. Children spent their pocket money there. Yet the trade was hard and paid little. Still, the tradition lasted. Chestnut vendors still appear on some New York street corners each winter, filling the air with the same warm smell.

IMG: ice-skating-central-park-lake-winter-crowd | many people ice skating on a frozen lake in Central Park on a bright winter day in the 1920s, snowy trees and the city buildings beyond, families watching from the shore, soft clear light
HEAD: When the lake in Central Park froze, a red ball was raised to show that skating was allowed.
BODY: Ice skating was a favourite winter pastime in New York. When the weather was cold enough, the lakes in Central Park froze over. Thousands of people came to skate. For many years, the park had a special signal. When the ice was thick enough, a ball was raised on a pole, or a flag flew. Sometimes streetcars carried a sign too. It told people that skating was safe. Families came with their skates over their shoulders. Young couples skated hand in hand. Children raced each other across the ice. Some people brought hot drinks and sat on benches to watch. Even so, the ice was not always reliable. Warm spells could make it thin and dangerous. Yet on a clear, cold winter day, the frozen lake was a joyful sight. Its tradition continues today. Wollman Rink, opened in 1950, now offers skating in the park all winter long.

HEAD: Since 1907, a lighted ball has been lowered in Times Square to welcome the New Year.
BODY: New Year's Eve in Times Square was already a great tradition by 1925. It had begun in 1904, when the New York Times opened its new building there. The newspaper held a celebration with fireworks. Large crowds came. In 1907, fireworks were banned. So the organisers found another idea. They lowered a large lighted ball down a pole on the top of the building. It reached the bottom exactly at midnight. The idea came from time balls used in harbours to help sailors set their clocks. The first ball was made of iron and wood, covered with light bulbs. Crowds counted down the last seconds together. Even so, during Prohibition, the celebrations were officially dry. Yet many people still brought hidden flasks. In 1925, thousands gathered in the cold to watch the ball drop. The tradition continues today, and the crowd is now much larger.

TOPIC: Children and schools
===
IMG: public-school-classroom-1925-desks-teacher | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a large New York public school classroom in 1925 with rows of wooden desks bolted to the floor, children in neat clothes, a teacher at a blackboard, tall windows with soft daylight
HEAD: New York's public schools taught millions of children, many of them the sons and daughters of immigrants.
BODY: In 1925, New York's public schools were among the largest in the world. Nearly a million children attended them. Many classrooms held forty or more pupils. The desks were made of wood and iron, often bolted to the floor in neat rows. Children learned reading, writing, arithmetic, history and geography. For immigrant children, school was also where they learned English. Many arrived knowing only Italian, Yiddish, Polish or another language. Within a few years, they spoke English fluently. Often they taught their parents. Schools also taught American customs and holidays. Even so, school could be strict. Teachers expected silence and good behaviour. Yet many children loved school. It opened doors that their parents had never had. Some went on to the city's free colleges, like City College. For many families, education was the main hope for a better future in America.
===
IMG: settlement-house-children-reading-room-1925 | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a warm reading room in a New York settlement house in 1925 with children reading books at long tables, a kind volunteer helping them, shelves of books and soft lamplight
HEAD: Settlement houses offered classes, libraries and clubs for children in poor neighbourhoods.
BODY: In the poor neighbourhoods of New York, there were special community centres called settlement houses. The idea came from London. Volunteers, often young educated women, lived in the neighbourhood and worked with local families. One of the most famous was the Henry Street Settlement on the Lower East Side. It was founded by a nurse named Lillian Wald in 1893. Settlement houses offered many services. There were classes in English, cooking, sewing and music. There were reading rooms, playgrounds and summer camps. Nurses visited sick people at home. Children came after school to play, read and take part in clubs. Even so, the settlement houses could not solve poverty. Yet they made a real difference to many lives. They gave children safe places to go and help with their studies. Henry Street Settlement still exists today. It continues to serve families on the Lower East Side.
===
IMG: children-swimming-public-pool-summer-1920s | children in old-fashioned swimsuits splashing in a large public swimming pool in New York on a hot summer day in the 1920s, brick buildings around, bright sunshine
HEAD: On hot days, children cooled off in public pools, river baths and the spray of fire hydrants.
BODY: New York summers were hot, and children wanted to cool off. Some went to public swimming pools. The city had built a number of public baths and pools, especially in poor neighbourhoods. Others swam in the rivers, though the water was often dirty. Some floating baths were moored along the riverbanks. On very hot days, children opened the fire hydrants on the street. Water sprayed out in a great fountain. Children ran through it, laughing and shouting. Firemen sometimes came to close them, but often they let the children play for a while. Even so, this wasted a lot of water and lowered the pressure for firefighting. Later, the city fitted hydrants with special sprinkler caps to make them safer. Yet the fun continued. The sight of children dancing in the spray of a fire hydrant became one of the most loved images of summer in New York.
===
IMG: kids-penny-candy-store-counter-glass-jars | children in 1920s clothes looking at rows of glass jars full of colourful sweets on the counter of a small neighbourhood candy store, a smiling shopkeeper behind it, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Neighbourhood candy stores sold sweets for a penny, along with newspapers and egg creams.
BODY: Almost every New York neighbourhood had a small candy store. It was usually on a corner. It sold newspapers, magazines, cigarettes and sweets. For children, it was a wonderful place. Glass jars were filled with colourful candies. Many cost just a penny. Children spent a long time choosing. Many candy stores also had a soda fountain. They served a famous New York drink called the egg cream. Strangely, it contained neither egg nor cream. It was made from milk, chocolate syrup and soda water, stirred until it foamed. No one is quite sure how it got its name. Even so, it became a classic drink of the city. The candy store was also a meeting place for older children and adults. People read the papers, chatted and made phone calls on the store's telephone. Yet for small children, it was simply the place where a single penny could buy happiness.
===
IMG: children-library-story-hour-1925-librarian | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a small group of children sitting on the floor listening to a librarian read a picture book aloud in a New York branch library in 1925, shelves of books around them, soft afternoon light
HEAD: The New York Public Library ran special children's rooms and story hours in its branch libraries.
BODY: Besides its great main building, the New York Public Library had many smaller branch libraries in the neighbourhoods. Many had been paid for by the businessman Andrew Carnegie. In the early 1900s, the library began to open special rooms for children. This was a new idea. Before, children were often not welcome in libraries. A librarian named Anne Carroll Moore led this work. She believed that children deserved beautiful books and comfortable places to read. The children's rooms had small tables and chairs, picture books and fairy tales. Librarians held story hours, when they read aloud to groups of children. Even so, some people thought this was not serious work. Yet Moore's ideas spread across the country. For immigrant children, the library was a treasure. They borrowed books in English and learned to love reading. Many famous writers later said that a New York branch library had changed their lives.

TOPIC: The end of Prohibition
===
IMG: crowds-cheering-repeal-december-1933-street | crowds of cheerful people in 1930s coats and hats gathered on a New York street at night in December 1933, raising glasses outside a newly reopened bar with bright lights, soft winter evening light
HEAD: Prohibition ended in December 1933, after nearly fourteen years.
BODY: By the end of the 1920s, many Americans had turned against Prohibition. It had not stopped people from drinking. Instead, it had created a huge illegal trade run by criminal gangs. It had filled the courts and prisons. The government had lost huge amounts of money in taxes. When the Great Depression began in 1929, people wanted jobs and tax money more than ever. Making alcohol legal again would create both. In 1933, Congress proposed a new amendment to end Prohibition. It was the Twenty-First Amendment. On the fifth of December 1933, enough states approved it. Prohibition was over. In New York, crowds celebrated in the streets. Bars and restaurants reopened legally. Even so, the change was not complete everywhere. Some states and towns kept their own bans for many years. Yet for New York, it was the end of an era. The age of the speakeasy was over.
===
IMG: old-speakeasy-door-today-quiet-street | a plain old wooden door in a quiet brick building on a New York street today, a small brass number and a dim lamp above it, soft evening light, quiet and calm
HEAD: Some of New York's old speakeasies still exist today as restaurants and bars.
BODY: When Prohibition ended, many speakeasies simply became legal bars. Some kept their old names and decorations. The 21 Club on West Fifty-Second Street became one of the most famous restaurants in New York. Its secret wine cellar survived, and visitors could still see the heavy hidden door. Other old speakeasies continued for decades as quiet neighbourhood bars. In recent years, the idea of the speakeasy has become fashionable again. Some new bars in New York are designed to look like hidden 1920s clubs. They have secret entrances, dim lighting and jazz music. Some even ask for a password. Even so, these are only imitations. They are completely legal. Yet they show how strongly the twenties still capture the imagination. The secret door, the soft knock and the warm room full of music have become part of the city's legend. They remind New Yorkers of a time when an ordinary evening out felt like an adventure.
===
IMG: new-york-1925-rooftop-water-tower-sunset | wooden water towers on the flat rooftops of New York buildings at sunset in the 1920s, the skyline glowing orange and pink behind them, pigeons flying, calm and peaceful
HEAD: The wooden water towers on New York's rooftops, a familiar sight in 1925, are still built by hand today.
BODY: Across the rooftops of New York stands a curious sight. Many buildings have large round wooden tanks standing on their roofs. These are water towers. They became common in the late 1800s, when buildings grew taller than six storeys. The city's water pressure was not strong enough to push water to the upper floors. So water was pumped up into a tank on the roof. From there, gravity carried it down to the taps below. The tanks were made of wood, usually cedar or redwood. Wood was cheap, light and kept the water from freezing in winter. By 1925, thousands of these towers stood on the city's roofs. Even so, they are not just relics of the past. Several thousand are still in use today. Yet the most remarkable thing is how they are made. A few small family companies still build them by hand, using methods that have hardly changed in a hundred years.
===
IMG: new-york-public-library-lions-steps-evening | the wide stone steps and two carved stone lions in front of the grand white New York Public Library building on Fifth Avenue on a calm evening in the 1920s, a few people walking past, soft warm light
HEAD: The two stone lions in front of the New York Public Library were later named Patience and Fortitude.
BODY: On Fifth Avenue stands the main building of the New York Public Library. It opened in 1911. It is a grand white marble building. Inside are huge reading rooms with high painted ceilings and long wooden tables. Anyone could come in and read for free. In the 1920s, it was full of students, writers, immigrants and ordinary readers. Many people who could not afford books educated themselves there. Two large stone lions guard the steps outside. They were carved by the Piccirilli brothers, a family of Italian stone carvers in the Bronx. At first, people gave the lions different nicknames. In the 1930s, Mayor Fiorello La Guardia named them Patience and Fortitude. He said New Yorkers would need those qualities to get through the Great Depression. Even so, in 1925 the lions were simply the lions. Yet they have watched over Fifth Avenue for more than a century. They remain among the most beloved statues in the city.
===
IMG: evening-lights-manhattan-1925-river-quiet | the lights of Manhattan glowing softly across a calm river on a quiet evening in 1925, the dark outlines of tall buildings and a bridge, a few small boats on the water, peaceful and still
HEAD: For many who lived through it, the twenties in New York were remembered as a bright and restless time.
BODY: The years of the mid twenties in New York were full of change. The city was growing taller and richer. New music, new fashions and new ideas filled the streets. Women had won the vote and were finding new freedoms. Radio, movies and jazz were changing how people spent their evenings. Prohibition had pushed drinking into secret rooms, and made breaking the law feel almost ordinary. Even so, not everyone shared in the good times. Many people were still poor. Black New Yorkers faced unfair treatment. Immigrants struggled to make new lives. Yet for those who lived through it, the decade had a special glow. When the stock market crashed in October 1929, the mood changed suddenly. The hard years of the Great Depression began. Looking back, many remembered the twenties as a time of energy, hope and freedom. It was a time when New York seemed to be inventing the future, one bright night at a time.
