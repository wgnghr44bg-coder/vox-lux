{img:01-new-york-street-1925-evening-brownstones-lamps}

In the winter of 1925, New York was the busiest city on earth, and selling a single glass of beer was against the law. Behind plain doors in quiet side streets, small hidden bars glowed with music, while the lights of Broadway shone over the snow.

[long-pause]

In January 1920, it became illegal to make or sell alcohol anywhere in the United States. On the sixteenth of January 1919, the Eighteenth Amendment to the American Constitution was ratified. It banned the making, selling and transporting of alcoholic drinks. The law gave the country one year to prepare. On the seventeenth of January 1920, it came into force. This period became known as Prohibition. Supporters believed it would make America healthier and safer. They hoped it would reduce crime, poverty and violence in the home. Churches and women's groups had campaigned for it for decades. In New York, the reaction was mixed. The city was home to millions of immigrants from Ireland, Italy, Germany and elsewhere. For many of them, wine and beer were part of daily life and religion. Even so, the law was now national. The saloons had to close. Yet very few New Yorkers stopped drinking. Instead, the drinking simply moved behind closed doors.

[long-pause]

{img:02-last-night-saloon-crowd-january-1920}

On the last night before Prohibition, New Yorkers held mock funerals for alcohol. On the evening of the sixteenth of January 1920, the city's bars and hotels were full. It was the last night that alcohol could be sold legally. Many New Yorkers decided to say goodbye in style. In some restaurants, guests held mock funerals. Waiters carried coffins filled with bottles through the dining rooms. Bands played sad music. People wore black and pretended to mourn. Others simply drank as much as they could. Some bought bottles to store at home. The law allowed people to keep alcohol they already owned, as long as it was for private use. Wealthy people had filled their cellars months before. Even so, many ordinary people could not afford to buy in advance. At midnight, the law took effect. The bars went quiet. Yet within days, new ways of drinking had already appeared all over the city.

[long-pause]

For anyone who likes drifting through old city nights like this one, a subscription keeps the next lantern-lit street close by.

[long-pause]

{img:03-prohibition-agents-pouring-liquor-street-drain}

Prohibition agents often emptied seized barrels of alcohol into the city's sewers. Enforcing Prohibition was the job of federal agents. They raided bars, warehouses and secret breweries. When they found alcohol, they destroyed it. Often, they poured it into the street drains, so that it ran away into the sewers. Newspapers loved to photograph these scenes. Crowds gathered to watch. Some people were said to be sad to see good whisky disappear. Yet the agents faced an impossible task. There were only a few thousand of them for the whole country. New York alone had thousands of places selling alcohol. Many agents were poorly paid. Some took bribes and looked the other way. Even so, a few agents became famous for their honesty and success. Yet most New Yorkers saw the law as unfair. In 1923, New York State even repealed its own Prohibition law. After that, the city police no longer had to enforce it. Only the federal agents remained.

[long-pause]

{img:04-izzy-einstein-disguise-agent-hat-street}

Two New York Prohibition agents, Izzy Einstein and Moe Smith, became famous for their clever disguises. The most famous Prohibition agents in New York were Izzy Einstein and Moe Smith. They were an unlikely pair. Izzy had been a postal clerk. He was short and round. Moe was a cigar salesman, and he was also quite heavy. Neither looked like a police officer. That was their secret. They wore disguises to get into illegal bars. They dressed as fishermen, football players, grave diggers and even women. Izzy spoke several languages, which helped him blend in. Once inside, they ordered a drink and then arrested everyone. Together, they made thousands of arrests. Newspapers wrote about them constantly. Readers loved their adventures. Even so, their fame caused problems. Some of their bosses felt they were getting too much attention. In 1925, both men were dismissed from the service. Yet they remained popular. Izzy later wrote a book about his adventures, and both lived quiet lives afterwards.

[long-pause]

{img:05-doctor-prescription-medicinal-whiskey-pharmacy}

During Prohibition, doctors could still prescribe whisky as medicine. Prohibition had some unusual exceptions. One of them was medicine. Doctors were allowed to prescribe whisky to their patients. People could then buy it legally at a pharmacy. Each prescription was written on a special form. Patients could receive about a pint every ten days. Many doctors wrote prescriptions for almost any complaint. Some charged a fee for each one. Pharmacies sold large amounts of medicinal whisky during these years. Another exception was religious wine. Priests and rabbis could obtain wine for religious services. This, too, was sometimes misused. People also made their own wine at home from grape juice. Grape growers in California sold blocks of dried grapes with a warning. The warning said not to mix them with water and leave them, because they might turn into wine. Even so, these were small loopholes. Yet together, they show how hard it was to stop people from drinking.

[long-pause]

{img:06-rum-row-ships-offshore-small-boats-night}

Ships full of alcohol waited just outside American waters, in a line known as Rum Row. Much of the alcohol in New York came by sea. Ships loaded with whisky, rum and gin sailed from Canada, the Caribbean and Europe. They anchored just outside American waters, where United States law could not touch them. At first, this limit was three miles from the coast. Off New York and New Jersey, so many ships waited there that the line became known as Rum Row. At night, fast motorboats raced out to meet them. They loaded cases of alcohol and sped back to quiet beaches and docks. The coast guard tried to stop them, but the smugglers' boats were often faster. One famous smuggler was a sea captain named Bill McCoy. He was known for selling high-quality, undiluted alcohol. Some people say the phrase the real McCoy comes from him. Even so, that story may not be true. The phrase may be older. Yet McCoy's ships were real, and Rum Row was busy for years.

[long-pause]

{img:07-bathtub-gin-kitchen-apartment-bottles}

Home-made gin was so common that it became known as bathtub gin. Real whisky and gin were expensive during Prohibition. So many people made their own. A popular method was to mix industrial alcohol with water and flavourings like juniper oil. The mixture was often made in large containers. The bottles were too tall to fill under a kitchen tap, so people used the bathtub tap instead. That is one explanation for the name bathtub gin. The drink was often harsh and unpleasant. To hide the taste, people mixed it with fruit juice, sugar and other flavours. This helped make cocktails very popular in the 1920s. Even so, home-made alcohol could be dangerous. Some industrial alcohol contained poisons. People who drank badly made liquor could become ill or even blind. Yet many people took the risk. Making a little gin at home became almost an ordinary part of life for some families. It was illegal, but very few were ever caught.

[long-pause]

{img:08-speakeasy-door-peephole-alley-night}

Illegal bars were called speakeasies, because customers had to speak quietly about them. When the saloons closed, new secret bars opened in their place. They were called speakeasies. The name came from the idea of speaking softly, or speaking easy, so that no one outside would hear. Speakeasies were hidden in basements, back rooms, apartments and behind ordinary shops. From the street, they often looked like nothing at all. A customer knocked on a plain door. A small window in the door slid open. A face appeared and looked at the visitor. To get in, people might need a password, a card or a friend who was known inside. Some speakeasies were small and simple, with just a few tables. Others were large and elegant, with music and dancing. Even so, all of them were illegal. They could be raided at any time. Yet the risk was part of the excitement. For many New Yorkers, the speakeasy became the most fashionable place to spend an evening.

[long-pause]

{img:09-elegant-speakeasy-interior-jazz-band-tables-1925}

By the end of the 1920s, New York may have had tens of thousands of speakeasies. No one knows exactly how many speakeasies there were in New York. They were secret, so no one counted them properly. Yet estimates were very high. In 1929, the city's police commissioner suggested there might be more than thirty thousand. That would be more than the number of legal bars before Prohibition. Some streets had a speakeasy in almost every building. West Fifty-Second Street, in Manhattan, had so many that it became famous for them. Later, it became known for its jazz clubs. Speakeasies ranged from rough cellars to fancy nightclubs. Some served good food and had live music. Others offered cheap drinks of uncertain quality. Even so, they all shared one thing. They were full of people breaking the law together. Yet most customers did not feel like criminals. They felt that the law was foolish, and they enjoyed quietly ignoring it.

[long-pause]

{img:10-women-friends-cocktails-speakeasy-table}

Speakeasies welcomed women, who had rarely been allowed into the old saloons. Before Prohibition, most saloons in New York were places for men only. Respectable women did not go into them. Speakeasies were different. Women came too, often in groups or with men. They drank cocktails, danced and smoked cigarettes. This was a big change in social life. It was part of a wider change in the lives of American women. In 1920, the same year Prohibition began, women gained the right to vote across the country. Many young women were working in offices and shops. They had their own money and more freedom than their mothers. Even so, not everyone approved. Older people and religious groups were shocked. Yet the change could not be stopped. By the middle of the 1920s, it was quite normal to see women drinking in speakeasies. In a strange way, a law meant to make life more proper had helped make it more relaxed.

[long-pause]

{img:11-texas-guinan-nightclub-hostess-stage-smile}

The nightclub hostess Texas Guinan greeted her guests with the words "Hello, suckers!" One of the most famous figures of Prohibition New York was a woman called Texas Guinan. She had grown up in Texas and worked as an actress in silent films. In the 1920s, she became the hostess of several nightclubs in Manhattan. She greeted her guests with a cheerful shout. Hello, suckers, she would call as they came in. Customers loved it. Her clubs were lively and noisy. Dancers performed, and guests paid high prices for drinks. Guinan was arrested many times. Her clubs were raided and closed. Each time, she simply opened a new one. When police came, the band sometimes played the song The Prisoner's Song as she was led away. Even so, she was rarely convicted. Juries liked her. Yet her clubs were also connected to powerful criminals who supplied the alcohol. Her cheerful greeting became one of the best-known phrases of the decade.

[long-pause]

{img:12-secret-wine-cellar-hidden-brick-door}

Some speakeasies had hidden doors, secret rooms and systems to make bottles disappear in a raid. Speakeasy owners became very creative. They needed ways to hide their alcohol quickly when the police arrived. Some bars had shelves that could tip over, sending bottles down a chute into the sewer. Others had hidden cupboards behind walls. Doors were disguised as bookcases or kitchen cabinets. Lookouts watched the street and pressed a button to warn the bar inside. Then the bartender quickly hid everything. One famous speakeasy, later known as the 21 Club, had a secret wine cellar. It was hidden behind a door made of brick, which weighed about two tonnes. It looked exactly like a solid wall. It could only be opened by pushing a thin metal rod into a small hole. Even so, many raids still succeeded. Yet these tricks have become part of the legend of the time. Some of these old hidden rooms still survive in New York today. Visitors can still see the secret doors.

[long-pause]

{img:13-cocktail-glasses-bar-counter-shaker-1920s}

Cocktails became popular partly because they could hide the taste of poor-quality alcohol. Mixed drinks had existed long before the 1920s. Yet Prohibition helped make cocktails fashionable. One reason was taste. Much of the alcohol sold in speakeasies was rough and unpleasant. Mixing it with fruit juice, sugar, honey or soda made it much easier to drink. Bartenders created new recipes with clever names. The bee's knees was made with gin, lemon and honey. Sidecars and gin rickeys were also popular. Even so, many of the best American bartenders left the country during Prohibition. They went to work in Paris, London and Havana, where drinking was legal. There, they invented or popularised many famous drinks. Yet in New York, the speakeasy bartender also became a kind of artist. He mixed, shook and stirred behind closed doors. When Prohibition ended, the cocktail culture remained. Many drinks that are still popular today were first made or made famous during these years.

[long-pause]

{img:14-manhattan-skyline-1925-harbour-ferry-morning}

In 1925, New York was one of the largest cities in the world, with around six million people. By the middle of the 1920s, New York City was enormous. Around six million people lived in its five boroughs. These were Manhattan, Brooklyn, the Bronx, Queens and Staten Island. They had been joined together into one city in 1898. By some measures, New York had just passed London to become the largest city on earth. It was a city of contrasts. There were great skyscrapers and crowded tenements. There were millionaires on Fifth Avenue and poor families on the Lower East Side. Ships came and went from its harbour every day. Trains carried thousands of commuters into the city each morning. Even so, New York was not one single place. It was a collection of neighbourhoods, each with its own character. Yet together they made a city that seemed full of energy. Visitors often described the noise, the speed and the excitement of New York in the twenties.

[long-pause]

{img:15-lower-east-side-street-pushcarts-crowds-1925}

The Lower East Side of Manhattan was one of the most crowded neighbourhoods in the world. The Lower East Side lay in the southern part of Manhattan. For decades, it had been the first home of many immigrants. Jewish families from Eastern Europe, Italians, Irish and many others lived there. The streets were lined with tall brick buildings called tenements. Families lived in small apartments, often sharing them with relatives or boarders. In the early 1900s, it was one of the most crowded places on earth. Its streets were busy all day. Pushcart sellers lined the pavements. They sold fruit, vegetables, fish, clothes, pots and pans. Customers bargained loudly in many languages. Children played in the streets, because there was no room indoors. Even so, by 1925 the area was changing. Many families who had saved some money moved to newer neighbourhoods in Brooklyn and the Bronx. Yet the Lower East Side remained full of life, with its synagogues, cafés, theatres and markets.

[long-pause]

{img:16-ellis-island-great-hall-immigrants-1920s}

Millions of immigrants had passed through Ellis Island, but a new law in 1924 slowed their arrival. Ellis Island is a small island in New York Harbor. From 1892, it was the main entry point for immigrants to the United States. More than twelve million people passed through its halls. They arrived on crowded ships from Europe. In the great hall, they waited in long lines. Doctors examined them for signs of illness. Officials asked them questions about their names, their jobs and where they were going. Most were allowed to enter after a few hours. Even so, the 1920s brought great change. Many Americans wanted fewer immigrants. In 1924, Congress passed a strict new law. It set low limits on the number of people allowed to come, especially from southern and eastern Europe. After that, the number of arrivals fell sharply. Yet the families who had already come stayed. In 1925, millions of New Yorkers had been born abroad, or were the children of immigrants. They gave the city its rich mix of languages and cultures.

[long-pause]

{img:17-neighbourhood-little-italy-mulberry-street-festival}

Neighbourhoods like Little Italy kept the food, music and festivals of the old country alive. Many immigrants in New York lived near people from their own country. This created neighbourhoods with strong identities. Little Italy, around Mulberry Street, was home to many Italian families. There were Italian bakeries, butchers and cafés. People spoke Italian in the shops and on the street. Each year, the neighbourhood held religious festivals in honour of saints from their home towns in Italy. The streets were decorated with arches of lights. Bands played and families walked together. Food stalls sold sausages, pastries and sweets. Other neighbourhoods had their own character too. There was Chinatown, the Jewish Lower East Side and the German area of Yorkville. Even so, these neighbourhoods were never closed off. People mixed at work, in schools and on the subway. Yet they gave immigrants a sense of home in a huge and unfamiliar city. Some of the festivals that began in those years are still held today.

[long-pause]

{img:18-brownstone-stoop-neighbours-summer-evening}

On warm evenings, New Yorkers sat on the front steps of their buildings, called stoops, to talk with neighbours. Many New York houses had a set of stone steps leading up to the front door. These were called stoops. The word came from Dutch, from the days when New York was a Dutch colony called New Amsterdam. In summer, the apartments inside were often hot and stuffy. There was no air conditioning in ordinary homes. So people came outside in the evening. They sat on the stoop to cool off and talk. Neighbours gathered to share news and gossip. Children played games on the pavement. Older people watched the street and greeted passers-by. Even so, stoop life was more than a way to keep cool. It created a sense of community. People knew their neighbours and watched out for each other's children. Yet as the century went on, life slowly moved indoors. Radios and other comforts kept people inside. In 1925, though, the stoop was still the heart of many New York streets.

[long-pause]

{img:19-fire-escape-sleeping-summer-night-tenement}

On the hottest nights, many New Yorkers slept on fire escapes, roofs and in parks. New York summers could be very hot and humid. In crowded tenements, the small rooms became almost unbearable. Windows were small, and the air did not move. So people found other places to sleep. Many families carried mattresses out onto the iron fire escapes on the front of their buildings. Others climbed up to the flat roofs. Some went to the parks and slept on the grass. Thousands of people slept in Central Park and at the beaches on the hottest nights. Children especially loved sleeping outside. It felt like an adventure. Even so, it could be uncomfortable and sometimes dangerous. People occasionally rolled off fire escapes in their sleep. Yet for many, a night under the stars was the only way to rest during a heat wave. These hot summer nights became a shared memory for generations of New Yorkers who grew up in the old tenements.

[long-pause]

{img:20-tenement-apartment-interior-1925-kitchen-window}

Many New York families lived in small tenement apartments of just three or four rooms. A tenement was a type of apartment building, usually five or six storeys high. Each floor held several small apartments. A typical apartment had three or four rooms, arranged one behind the other. The kitchen was often the main room. It had a stove, a sink and a table where the family ate, talked and did homework. The other rooms were used for sleeping. Large families often shared a few beds. Older tenements had been built without enough light or air. Some inner rooms had no windows at all. A law passed in 1901 required new buildings to have proper windows, toilets and air shafts. Older buildings had to be improved too. By 1925, many apartments had running water and a toilet inside. Even so, they were still crowded. Yet families made them into homes. They hung curtains, put photographs on the walls and filled the kitchen with the smells of cooking.

[long-pause]

{img:21-new-apartment-building-bronx-grand-concourse-1925}

In the 1920s, many families moved from crowded Manhattan to new apartments in the Bronx and Brooklyn. As the subway reached further out, new neighbourhoods grew quickly. The Bronx and Brooklyn were full of new apartment buildings. These were larger and more modern than the old tenements. They had bigger rooms, more windows, hot water and private bathrooms. Some had elevators and courtyards with gardens. One of the most admired streets was the Grand Concourse in the Bronx. It was a wide boulevard lined with trees and handsome buildings. Many families who had grown up on the Lower East Side moved there. They saw it as a step up in the world. Even so, the move was not always easy. People missed the busy streets and the friends they had left behind. Yet the new apartments offered something precious. There was space, light and quiet. For the children, there were parks nearby and better schools. For many immigrant families, moving to the Bronx or Brooklyn was the dream of a better life.

[long-pause]

{img:22-steam-radiator-hissing-apartment-winter-window}

In winter, New York apartments were heated by steam radiators that hissed and clanked. By the 1920s, many New York apartment buildings had central heating. A large coal boiler in the basement heated water and turned it into steam. The steam travelled up through pipes to cast iron radiators in each apartment. The radiators grew very hot. They hissed, banged and clanked as the steam moved through them. For many New Yorkers, these sounds were part of winter. Each building had a superintendent who looked after the boiler. He shovelled coal into the furnace early in the morning. If he was late, the apartments were cold. Tenants sometimes banged on the pipes to let him know. Even so, the heat was often uneven. Some apartments were too hot, and people opened their windows in the middle of winter. Others stayed cold. Yet the radiators brought real comfort. On frosty mornings, families gathered near them, warming their hands and drying their wet gloves.

[long-pause]

{img:23-washing-lines-between-tenements-backyard-sunny}

Washing was hung on long lines strung between the backs of tenement buildings. Doing the laundry was hard work in 1925. Few families had washing machines. Most clothes were washed by hand in a tub or a sink. Women scrubbed them on a washboard with soap and hot water. Then the wet clothes had to be dried. In crowded tenements, there was no garden. So people strung washing lines from their back windows. The lines ran on pulleys across the yards between buildings. A woman could hang out the washing from her kitchen window and pull it across the yard. When it was dry, she pulled it back. Seen from above, the backyards were criss-crossed with lines of shirts, sheets and dresses. Even so, the city air was often sooty. Clothes could come back greyer than they went out. Yet on a sunny, windy day, the washing dried quickly. The colourful lines between the buildings became one of the classic images of old New York.

[long-pause]

{img:24-superintendent-coal-delivery-chute-basement}

Coal for heating was delivered by truck and poured down a chute into the basement. Most New York buildings were heated by coal in the 1920s. The coal had to be delivered regularly, especially in winter. Coal trucks drove through the streets. They stopped in front of buildings and set up a metal chute. The coal slid down the chute through a small window into the basement. It made a loud rumbling noise. A cloud of black dust often rose from the window. In the basement, the coal was stored in a bin near the furnace. The building's superintendent shovelled it into the boiler several times a day. He also took out the heavy ash and put it in cans on the street to be collected. Even so, coal made the city dirty. Smoke from thousands of chimneys darkened the winter sky. Yet it also kept millions of people warm. The sound of coal sliding down a chute was a familiar part of every New York winter.

[long-pause]

{img:25-subway-car-1925-passengers-straps-wicker-seats}

A ride on the New York subway cost just five cents, and that price stayed the same for more than forty years. The New York subway opened in 1904. By 1925, it was one of the busiest railway systems in the world. Millions of people rode it every day. The fare was a nickel, a five-cent coin. Passengers dropped it into a turnstile and walked onto the platform. The price did not change from 1904 until 1948. Even as wages and other prices rose, the nickel fare stayed the same. This made the subway cheap for everyone. Workers could travel from Brooklyn or the Bronx to jobs in Manhattan. In rush hour, the cars were packed. Passengers stood shoulder to shoulder, holding leather straps hanging from the ceiling. Ceiling fans turned slowly overhead. Even so, the subway was a marvel. It ran day and night. Yet it also changed the city. As new lines reached further out, people moved to new neighbourhoods, knowing they could still reach work in under an hour.

[long-pause]

{img:26-elevated-train-third-avenue-steel-tracks-street}

Elevated trains ran above the streets of New York on iron structures, casting shadows on the avenues below. Before the subway, New York had elevated railways. They were called the els. Trains ran on iron tracks raised high above the streets on tall columns. The first one opened in 1868. By 1925, there were several el lines in Manhattan, Brooklyn and the Bronx. They ran along avenues like Second, Third, Sixth and Ninth. From the train windows, passengers could look into second-floor apartments and offices. Below, the streets were in shade. Sunlight fell through the tracks in stripes. The trains were very noisy. When they passed, conversations stopped. Dust and sometimes sparks fell onto the street. Even so, the els were useful and cheap. Yet many people disliked them. Shopkeepers and residents complained about the noise and darkness. Over the following decades, most of the els were torn down. Today, only a few elevated lines remain in the outer boroughs, still rattling above the streets.

[long-pause]

{img:27-yellow-taxicabs-1920s-fifth-avenue-traffic}

New York's taxis began to be painted yellow in the 1910s and 1920s, so they could be seen easily. Taxis were a common sight in New York by 1925. Thousands of them drove through the streets of Manhattan. Many were painted yellow. A businessman named John Hertz, who ran a taxi company in Chicago, chose yellow after a study suggested it was the easiest colour to see. Soon, yellow cabs were popular in New York as well. Other taxi companies used different colours. A ride was not cheap, so taxis were mostly used by better-off people. Office workers usually took the subway or walked. Traffic was becoming a real problem. Cars, buses, taxis, trucks and horse-drawn wagons all shared the same streets. To manage it, police officers stood in small towers at busy crossings on Fifth Avenue. They used signals to tell traffic when to stop and go. Even so, the city kept growing. Yet these first traffic towers were an early step towards the traffic lights that would soon appear everywhere.

[long-pause]

{img:28-staten-island-ferry-deck-passengers-skyline}

The Staten Island Ferry carried thousands of passengers across New York Harbor every day. New York is a city of islands. Manhattan, Staten Island and much of the rest of the city are surrounded by water. Before many bridges and tunnels were built, ferries were essential. In 1925, dozens of ferry lines crossed the rivers and the harbour. The most famous was the Staten Island Ferry. It sailed between Staten Island and the southern tip of Manhattan. The journey took about twenty-five minutes. It passed close to the Statue of Liberty. Commuters rode it every morning and evening. Some read their newspapers. Others stood at the rail and watched the boats and the skyline. In summer, families took the ferry just for the breeze. Even so, the ferries were slowly being replaced in many places. New bridges and tunnels were built across the rivers. Yet the Staten Island Ferry kept running. It still crosses the harbour today, and the ride is now free.

[long-pause]

{img:29-holland-tunnel-construction-workers-1925}

In 1925, workers were digging the Holland Tunnel, the first long tunnel for cars under the Hudson River. For a long time, the only way to cross the Hudson River between New York and New Jersey was by ferry. As cars became more common, this was too slow. So engineers planned a tunnel for cars beneath the river. It was a huge challenge. Cars produce poisonous exhaust gases. In a long tunnel, these gases could build up and become dangerous. The chief engineer, Clifford Holland, designed a new system of fans and air shafts. Fresh air was blown in through ducts under the road, and dirty air was drawn out through the ceiling. Sadly, Holland died in 1924, before the tunnel was finished. Work continued, and the tunnel was named after him. Even so, building it was slow and hard. Workers dug through mud and rock under the river. Yet when it opened in 1927, it was a great success. The ventilation system worked so well that it became a model for road tunnels around the world.

[long-pause]

{img:30-horse-drawn-ice-wagon-delivery-street-1925}

Many New York homes still had iceboxes, and the iceman delivered blocks of ice by horse and wagon. In 1925, electric refrigerators were new and expensive. Most families kept their food cool in an icebox. It was a wooden cabinet lined with metal. A large block of ice sat in the top compartment. Cold air flowed down and kept the food below fresh. As the ice melted, the water dripped into a pan underneath. Someone had to empty it every day. The iceman delivered fresh blocks of ice several times a week. He drove a wagon, often still pulled by a horse. He carried the heavy blocks up the stairs on his shoulder, using metal tongs. Families put a card in the window to show how much ice they wanted. On hot days, children followed the wagon and begged for small chips of ice to suck. Even so, the iceman's days were numbered. Within twenty years, most homes would have electric refrigerators. Yet many older New Yorkers remembered the iceman with real affection.

[long-pause]

{img:31-automat-wall-glass-doors-food-coins}

At the automat, customers put coins in a slot and took their food from behind a small glass door. One of the most popular places to eat in New York was the automat. The most famous were run by a company called Horn and Hardart. An automat looked like a bright, modern café. Along one wall were rows of small glass doors. Behind each door was a dish, such as a sandwich, a slice of pie or a bowl of macaroni. Customers put coins in a slot beside the door. The door unlocked, and they took their food. Behind the wall, workers quickly refilled the empty compartments. Coffee poured from silver spouts shaped like dolphin heads. Prices were low, often just a nickel or a dime. Office workers, students, actors and families all ate there. Even so, the automat was not really automatic. It needed many workers behind the scenes. Yet it felt modern and fun. For many New Yorkers, a meal at the automat was a small daily pleasure.

[long-pause]

{img:32-jewish-deli-counter-pastrami-sandwiches-1925}

Jewish delicatessens served pastrami, corned beef and pickles to hungry New Yorkers. Jewish immigrants from Eastern Europe brought their food traditions to New York. Delicatessens, or delis, became a key part of the city's life. They sold cured and smoked meats like pastrami and corned beef. Sandwiches were piled high with meat on rye bread. Barrels of pickles stood near the counter. Customers could buy smoked fish, potato salad and soups. Many delis stayed open late, serving workers and theatre-goers. Some became famous. Katz's Delicatessen on the Lower East Side had already been open for decades by 1925. It is still open today. Even so, delis were not only places to eat. They were meeting places where people talked, argued and shared news. Yet the food was what brought people back. Over time, pastrami sandwiches, bagels and cheesecake spread far beyond Jewish neighbourhoods. They became symbols of New York food for everyone.

[long-pause]

{img:33-hot-dog-stand-coney-island-nathans-1920s}

Nathan's hot dogs at Coney Island cost just five cents. In 1916, a Polish immigrant named Nathan Handwerker opened a small hot dog stand at Coney Island. He had worked at another stand that sold hot dogs for ten cents. Nathan sold his for just five cents. At first, some people were suspicious. They wondered why his hot dogs were so cheap. According to a popular story, Nathan hired men to wear white coats and eat at his stand. They looked like doctors. That made customers believe the food was good. Whether the story is true or not, the stand became hugely popular. When the subway reached Coney Island in 1920, crowds arrived from all over the city. Many of them headed straight for Nathan's. Even so, Nathan worked very hard. He and his wife ran the stand themselves for years. Yet his business grew and grew. Nathan's Famous is still at Coney Island today, more than a hundred years later.

[long-pause]

{img:34-pushcart-fruit-seller-street-apples-oranges}

Pushcarts sold fresh fruit, vegetables and many other goods on the streets of New York. Pushcarts were everywhere in the poorer neighbourhoods of New York. They were simple wooden carts with two wheels and long handles. Sellers pushed them through the streets or parked them along the pavements. They sold fruit, vegetables, fish, bread, clothes, shoes, hats and household goods. Many pushcart sellers were recent immigrants. A cart did not cost much to rent. It allowed people to start a small business with very little money. In some streets, hundreds of carts stood side by side, forming an outdoor market. Prices were low, and customers bargained hard. Even so, the city saw the carts as a problem. They blocked traffic and made the streets crowded and dirty. Officials began to build covered markets to replace them. The best known were built in the 1930s. Yet in 1925, the pushcarts were still at the heart of daily shopping for millions of ordinary New Yorkers.

[long-pause]

{img:35-family-dinner-tenement-kitchen-table-1925}

Many immigrant families kept cooking the dishes of their home countries in their small New York kitchens. Food was one of the strongest links between immigrants and their homelands. In the small kitchens of New York tenements, mothers and grandmothers cooked the meals they had learned as children. Italian families made pasta and tomato sauce. Jewish families prepared chicken soup, brisket and challah bread for the Sabbath. Irish families ate stews and soda bread. Chinese families cooked rice and vegetables with familiar spices. Ingredients could be bought in neighbourhood shops that imported them from abroad. Even so, cooking in a tenement was not easy. Kitchens were small. Many had only a simple gas stove and a sink. Yet families made do. Meals were times for the family to gather and talk. Children learned the old recipes from their parents. Over time, these home kitchens shaped American food. Dishes like pizza and bagels moved from immigrant tables to restaurants across the whole country.

[long-pause]

{img:36-garment-district-sewing-machines-workers-loft}

New York's garment district made a large share of all the clothes worn in America. In the 1920s, New York was the centre of the American clothing industry. Most ready-made dresses, coats and suits in the country were made there. The industry was concentrated in an area of Manhattan called the garment district. It lay on the west side, around Seventh Avenue. Tall loft buildings were filled with workshops. Rows of workers, many of them immigrant women, sat at sewing machines. Others cut fabric, pressed seams and sewed on buttons. Racks of finished clothes were pushed through the streets to showrooms and shops. Even so, the work was hard. Hours were long and pay was low. Fires were a serious danger. Workers remembered the terrible Triangle fire of 1911, in which many young women died. After that disaster, new safety laws were passed. Unions grew stronger. Yet the garment district remained busy for decades. Seventh Avenue is still sometimes called Fashion Avenue today.

[long-pause]

{img:37-office-typists-1925-skyscraper-desks-windows}

Thousands of young women worked as typists and secretaries in New York's office buildings. The 1920s brought a big change in the world of work. More and more companies needed people to type letters, file papers and answer telephones. Many of these jobs went to young women. They worked as typists, stenographers, secretaries and telephone operators. Most had learned their skills at special business schools. Every morning, crowds of young women in neat dresses and hats poured out of the subway stations. They walked into the tall office buildings of Manhattan. Some lived at home with their families. Others shared small apartments or lived in women's hotels. Their wages were modest, but they had their own money. Even so, most were expected to give up work when they married. Yet these jobs gave many women a new kind of independence. They could buy their own clothes, go to the movies and enjoy the city. For many young women of the twenties, an office job was a door to a new life.

[long-pause]

{img:38-dockworkers-hudson-piers-cargo-ships-1925}

The piers along the Hudson and East rivers made New York the busiest port in the world. New York's harbour was one of its greatest strengths. Ships could dock along miles of piers in Manhattan, Brooklyn and New Jersey. In the 1920s, it was the busiest port in the world. Cargo ships arrived from Europe, South America, Asia and Africa. Great ocean liners brought passengers from Britain, France and Germany. They docked at piers on the west side of Manhattan. The docks employed many thousands of men. Dockworkers, called longshoremen, loaded and unloaded cargo by hand. They carried sacks of coffee, bales of cotton and crates of machinery. The work was heavy and dangerous. Jobs were often handed out each morning at the pier gate. Men gathered and waited to be chosen. Even so, the docks were full of life. Tugboats pushed huge ships into position. Gulls cried overhead. Yet over the following decades, shipping moved to new container ports in New Jersey. Many of the old Manhattan piers became parks.

[long-pause]

{img:39-newsboy-selling-papers-corner-1925-cap}

Newsboys sold papers on every busy street corner, shouting out the day's headlines. Newspapers were the main source of news in 1925. New York had many of them, in English and in other languages. Some came out in the morning, others in the evening. They were sold on the streets by newsboys. Most were boys between about ten and fifteen years old. They bought papers from the publishers and sold them for a small profit. They stood at busy corners, outside subway stations and near office buildings. They shouted out the headlines to attract customers. Extra, extra, read all about it, was a typical cry. Some newsboys worked before school and others after school. For poor families, the money was very important. Even so, the work could be hard. The boys stood out in all weather. Yet many grew up to be successful. Some famous Americans began their working lives selling newspapers on the street. They remembered it as a good school for life.

[long-pause]

{img:40-harlem-lenox-avenue-evening-1925-lights}

In the 1920s, Harlem became the centre of Black culture in America. Harlem is a neighbourhood in the north of Manhattan. In the early 1900s, many Black families began moving there. Some came from other parts of New York. Many came from the southern United States, where they faced harsh laws and violence. Others came from the Caribbean. By the 1920s, Harlem had become the largest Black community in America. It was a place of churches, newspapers, theatres, nightclubs and shops. It was also a place of poverty and crowded housing. Yet it was full of creative energy. Writers, poets, musicians, painters and thinkers made it their home. This flowering of art and ideas became known as the Harlem Renaissance. Even so, Harlem's residents still faced discrimination. Some of its most famous clubs did not even allow Black customers. Yet the Harlem Renaissance changed American culture. It brought Black voices, stories and music to the centre of the nation's life.

[long-pause]

{img:41-jazz-band-small-stage-trumpet-piano-1925}

In 1924 and 1925, a young trumpet player named Louis Armstrong played in New York and changed the sound of jazz. Jazz was the music of the twenties. It began in New Orleans and spread north with Black musicians. By the 1920s, New York had become one of its great centres. In 1924, a young trumpet player from New Orleans arrived in the city. His name was Louis Armstrong. He joined the band of Fletcher Henderson, one of the leading bandleaders in New York. The band played at the Roseland Ballroom in Manhattan. Armstrong's playing amazed other musicians. He played with great warmth, power and freedom. He made the solo, where one musician takes the lead, a central part of jazz. In late 1925, he returned to Chicago. Even so, his time in New York had a lasting effect. Many musicians began to copy his style. Yet Armstrong was just one of many great players in the city. Pianists, singers and bandleaders filled the clubs of Harlem and Broadway with new music every night.

[long-pause]

{img:42-langston-hughes-poet-writing-small-room-window}

The poet Langston Hughes published his first book of poems in 1926, after winning a prize in 1925. Langston Hughes was one of the great voices of the Harlem Renaissance. He was born in Missouri in 1901. As a young man, he worked many jobs and travelled widely. He wrote poems about the lives of ordinary Black Americans. His poems used the rhythms of jazz and blues. They were simple, musical and deeply felt. In 1925, one of his poems won a prize from a magazine called Opportunity. This brought him attention. His first book of poems, The Weary Blues, was published in 1926. Even so, Hughes was not only a poet. He also wrote stories, plays, essays and newspaper columns. Yet his poems remain his best-known work. One of his most famous early poems, The Negro Speaks of Rivers, was written when he was still a teenager. In it, he imagines rivers flowing through the history of his people. Hughes lived in Harlem for much of his life.

[long-pause]

{img:43-new-negro-book-cover-1925-library-table}

In 1925, a book called The New Negro brought together writers of the Harlem Renaissance. In 1925, a scholar named Alain Locke edited an important collection of writing. It was called The New Negro. It included poems, stories, essays and art by many Black writers and artists. Among them were Langston Hughes, Countee Cullen and Zora Neale Hurston. Locke wanted to show a new spirit among Black Americans. He believed they were creating a proud and confident culture of their own. The book was widely read and discussed. It helped make the Harlem Renaissance known across the country. Even so, the writers in the book did not all agree. They had different ideas about art, politics and identity. Some wanted to celebrate folk traditions. Others wanted to show refined, modern art. Yet together, they created a remarkable body of work. Locke's book is still seen as one of the most important collections in American literature. It captured a moment of great hope and creativity in Harlem.

[long-pause]

{img:44-rent-party-apartment-piano-dancing-harlem}

In Harlem, families held rent parties, with music and dancing, to help pay their rent. Rent in Harlem was often very high. Landlords knew that Black families had few other places to live. So many families struggled to pay. One solution was the rent party. A family would invite friends and neighbours to their apartment on a Saturday night. Guests paid a small entry fee, perhaps a quarter. Food and drinks were sold. A pianist played lively music. People danced late into the night. By the morning, the family might have enough money for the rent. Some of the best jazz pianists in New York played at rent parties. Fats Waller and James P. Johnson were among them. They developed a powerful style of piano playing called stride. Even so, rent parties were born from hardship. Yet they were also full of joy and community. They showed how people in Harlem turned difficult times into music, laughter and a shared night together.

[long-pause]

{img:45-flapper-bobbed-hair-cloche-hat-street-1925}

Young women of the twenties who cut their hair short and wore shorter dresses were called flappers. The flapper was one of the great symbols of the 1920s. She was a young woman who broke with old rules. She cut her long hair into a short style called a bob. She wore dresses that ended just below the knee. That was much shorter than before. Her dresses were loose and straight, without a tight waist. She wore a small, close-fitting hat called a cloche. She danced, drove cars, smoked and went to jazz clubs. Older people were often shocked. Newspapers wrote about flappers with both excitement and alarm. Even so, most young women were not wild flappers. Many simply enjoyed the new fashions and freedoms in small ways. Yet the flapper image was powerful. It stood for youth, energy and change. The word flapper may have come from a young bird flapping its wings as it learns to fly. It suited a generation trying out new freedoms.

[long-pause]

{img:46-barber-shop-woman-getting-bob-haircut-1920s}

When women began cutting their hair short, many went to men's barber shops, because hairdressers did not yet know how. Before the 1920s, most women in America had long hair. They wore it pinned up on their heads. Cutting it short was a dramatic step. When the bob became fashionable, many women wanted one. Yet traditional women's hairdressers were not used to cutting short styles. So many women went to men's barber shops instead. Barbers were skilled at short haircuts. Some barbers were happy to have new customers. Others felt uncomfortable with women in their shops. Some refused to cut women's hair at all. Families sometimes argued about it. Fathers and husbands could be upset when a daughter or wife came home with short hair. Some employers even objected. Even so, the bob spread quickly. By the middle of the decade, it was very common. Yet for many women, the first short haircut was a moment of real courage. It was a simple, visible sign that times were changing.

[long-pause]

{img:47-charleston-dance-couples-ballroom-1925}

The Charleston was the most famous dance of the 1920s. The 1920s were a dancing decade. In ballrooms, clubs and even at home, people danced to the new jazz music. The most famous dance of all was the Charleston. It was fast and energetic. Dancers swung their legs and kicked their feet out to the sides. Their arms moved in time with the music. The dance had roots in Black communities in the South. It became famous across America after it appeared in a Broadway show called Runnin' Wild in 1923. The song The Charleston, written by James P. Johnson, was a great hit. Soon, the dance was everywhere. People held Charleston contests. Some ballrooms put up signs warning against it, because the floors shook too much. Even so, it was mostly harmless fun. Yet many older people found it shocking. For the young, the Charleston was pure joy. It captured the restless, cheerful spirit of the twenties better than almost anything else.

[long-pause]

{img:48-department-store-window-display-fifth-avenue-dresses}

New York's great department stores set the fashions for the whole country. Shopping was one of the great pleasures of New York in the 1920s. The city had huge department stores. Macy's, on Herald Square, was one of the largest stores in the world. Fifth Avenue was lined with elegant shops selling the latest fashions from Paris and New York. Store windows were carefully decorated. Crowds stopped to look at the newest dresses, hats and shoes. Inside, there were floors of clothing, furniture, toys and household goods. Lifts carried shoppers up and down. Some stores had restaurants, beauty salons and even small libraries. Even so, many people could not afford these stores. They bought cheaper clothes from smaller shops or from catalogues. Many women still sewed their own clothes, copying the styles they saw in the windows. Yet the department stores shaped what Americans wanted to wear. Fashion magazines and newspapers reported on their displays. What appeared in New York windows soon appeared across the country.

[long-pause]

{img:49-men-1925-suits-fedora-straw-boater-street}

In the 1920s, almost every man in New York wore a hat when he went outside. Fashion changed for men too in the 1920s, though more slowly than for women. Suits became a little looser and more comfortable. Trousers were wider. In summer, many men wore light-coloured suits and straw hats called boaters. In cooler months, they wore soft felt hats, such as fedoras. Hats were almost essential. A man without a hat on the street looked strange. There was even an unwritten rule about when to change hats. In the middle of September, men were expected to stop wearing straw hats. In some years, young men knocked straw hats off the heads of those who were late to change. Even so, these were mostly playful pranks. Young men in college also liked baggy trousers and colourful sweaters. Yet the basic outfit stayed the same. A suit, a shirt with a tie and a hat were what most working men wore every day in New York.

[long-pause]

{img:50-family-gathered-around-radio-living-room-1925}

In the 1920s, radio brought music, news and voices into American homes for the first time. Radio was one of the great new inventions of the twenties. Regular public broadcasting began in the United States in 1920. By the middle of the decade, millions of Americans owned radios. New York had several important stations. Families gathered around their radio sets in the evening. Early radios were large wooden boxes. Some had a horn-shaped speaker. Others needed headphones. Listeners tuned in carefully, turning knobs until the voice or music became clear. There were dance bands, news, sports, lectures and religious services. Comedy shows and dramas soon followed. Even so, early radio could be crackly and hard to hear. Yet people were amazed by it. For the first time, they could hear a concert or a speech from far away, as it happened. Radio made the country feel smaller. Families in small towns and big cities listened to the same programmes on the same evening.

[long-pause]

{img:51-tabloid-newspapers-newsstand-1925-headlines}

New York's tabloid newspapers, with big photographs and short stories, became the most popular papers in America. In 1919, a new kind of newspaper appeared in New York. It was called the Daily News. It was smaller than traditional newspapers, so it was easy to read on the crowded subway. This size was called a tabloid. It had large photographs and short, lively stories. It focused on crime, sports, gossip and human interest. Serious newspapers looked down on it. Yet readers loved it. By the mid 1920s, the Daily News had the largest circulation of any newspaper in the United States. Other tabloids followed. They competed fiercely for readers. Some used dramatic headlines and staged photographs. Even so, New York also had serious newspapers. The New York Times and the New York Herald Tribune reported news from around the world. Yet the tabloids changed journalism. They showed that ordinary readers wanted news that was quick, visual and close to their own lives.

[long-pause]

{img:52-the-new-yorker-first-issue-1925-dandy-monocle}

The New Yorker magazine first appeared in February 1925. In February 1925, a new weekly magazine was launched in New York. It was called The New Yorker. It was founded by Harold Ross and his wife, Jane Grant. Ross wanted a witty, clever magazine for sophisticated city readers. He famously said it would not be edited for the old lady in Dubuque, a small town in Iowa. The first cover showed an elegant gentleman in a top hat. He was looking at a butterfly through a monocle. The figure was later named Eustace Tilley. At first, the magazine struggled. It lost money, and few people bought it. Even so, Ross kept going. Talented writers, artists and cartoonists joined the staff. Yet slowly, the magazine found its readers. It became famous for its short stories, careful reporting and gentle cartoons. Eustace Tilley still appears on the cover once a year, on the magazine's anniversary.

[long-pause]

{img:53-movie-palace-grand-lobby-chandeliers-1920s}

New York's great movie palaces seated thousands of people and looked like royal palaces. Going to the movies was one of the most popular activities of the 1920s. In New York, films were shown in huge, beautiful theatres. They were called movie palaces. They had grand lobbies with chandeliers, marble staircases, gold decorations and thick carpets. Ushers in uniforms showed people to their seats. Some theatres seated several thousand people. The Roxy Theatre, which opened in 1927, had nearly six thousand seats. Before the main film, there were often live shows with singers, dancers and an orchestra. In 1925, most films were still silent. A pianist or an organist played music to go with the pictures. Words appeared on the screen between scenes. Even so, sound was coming. In 1927, The Jazz Singer became the first major film with spoken words. Yet in 1925, audiences were happy with silent films. For a small price, anyone could spend an evening in a palace, watching stories of adventure, love and laughter.

[long-pause]

{img:54-broadway-times-square-lights-night-1925}

Broadway was called the Great White Way, because of its bright electric lights. Broadway is a long avenue that runs the length of Manhattan. Around Times Square, it became the centre of American theatre. Dozens of theatres stood on and near it. In the 1920s, it was busier than ever. In some seasons, more than two hundred new shows opened. There were dramas, comedies, musicals and revues. The Ziegfeld Follies featured beautiful costumes, dancers and comedians. At night, Times Square was covered in electric signs. They advertised shows, products and restaurants. The lights were so bright that the area was called the Great White Way. Visitors from all over the world came to see it. Even so, the lights were not only for theatres. They also advertised chewing gum, cigarettes and drinks. Yet the effect was magical. In 1925, Times Square at night was one of the most famous sights in the world. It still glows brightly today, though now with very different signs.

[long-pause]

{img:55-great-gatsby-book-1925-long-island-mansion-lights}

In April 1925, F. Scott Fitzgerald published The Great Gatsby, a novel set near New York. One of the most famous American novels was published in 1925. It was called The Great Gatsby. It was written by F. Scott Fitzgerald. The story takes place in the summer of 1922, in New York City and on Long Island. It tells of a mysterious, wealthy man named Jay Gatsby. He holds enormous parties at his mansion. He is in love with a woman named Daisy, who lives across the bay. At night, Gatsby looks across the water at a green light on her dock. The novel describes the glamour and emptiness of the rich in the twenties. It is full of parties, cars, music and secret drinking. Even so, the book did not sell well at first. Fitzgerald was disappointed. Yet after his death, it became one of the most loved books in America. Today, millions of students read it. For many people, The Great Gatsby is the picture of New York in the Jazz Age.

[long-pause]

{img:56-woolworth-building-gothic-tower-1925-clouds}

In 1925, the tallest building in the world was the Woolworth Building in Manhattan. New York was famous for its skyscrapers. In 1925, the tallest building in the world was the Woolworth Building. It stood in lower Manhattan. It had been finished in 1913. It rose fifty-seven floors and about two hundred and forty metres. It was built for Frank Woolworth, who owned a chain of cheap stores where most items cost five or ten cents. He paid for the building in cash. Its style was Gothic, with pointed arches, carved decorations and a green copper roof. People called it the Cathedral of Commerce. Inside, the lobby had a glittering mosaic ceiling. Small carvings showed the architect and Woolworth himself, counting coins. Even so, the building was more than a symbol of wealth. It was also a triumph of engineering. Its steel frame and fast lifts made such height possible. Yet within a few years, new towers would pass it. The race for height was about to begin.

[long-pause]

{img:57-setback-skyscraper-wedding-cake-shape-1925}

A law of 1916 gave New York's skyscrapers their stepped wedding-cake shapes. In the early 1900s, some skyscrapers rose straight up from the pavement like tall boxes. They blocked sunlight and air from the streets below. In 1915, a huge building called the Equitable Building was finished. It cast a shadow over several blocks. People complained. In 1916, New York passed a new zoning law. It required tall buildings to step back as they rose higher. Each upper section had to be smaller than the one below. This let light reach the street. As a result, skyscrapers of the 1920s took on a new shape. They looked like giant stepped pyramids or wedding cakes. Architects made the most of this. They decorated the terraces and set-backs with stone carvings. Even so, the law did not stop the race for height. A tower could still rise very high, as long as it covered only part of the site. Yet the stepped shape gave the New York skyline its famous romantic look.

[long-pause]

{img:58-ironworkers-steel-beams-high-above-city-1920s}

Many of the ironworkers who built New York's skyscrapers were Mohawk men from Canada. Building a skyscraper was dangerous work. Ironworkers walked along narrow steel beams hundreds of metres above the street. They bolted and riveted the beams together to form the frame of the building. Among the most famous ironworkers were Mohawk men. They came from Kahnawake, a Mohawk community near Montreal in Canada. They had begun working on steel bridges in the late 1800s. They quickly gained a reputation as skilled and fearless workers on high steel. In the 1920s, many Mohawk families moved to Brooklyn, so that the men could work on New York's buildings and bridges. They helped build many of the city's famous skyscrapers. Even so, they were not truly without fear. They were careful and experienced. Yet their skill became legendary. Some families have worked as ironworkers for several generations. Mohawk ironworkers still help build New York's towers today.

[long-pause]

{img:59-architects-drawing-office-skyscraper-plans}

Drawings by the artist Hugh Ferriss imagined the city of the future as a world of giant towers in mist and shadow. In the 1920s, many people were excited by the idea of the city of the future. One artist captured this dream very powerfully. His name was Hugh Ferriss. He was an architect who specialised in drawing buildings. He made large charcoal drawings of skyscrapers. They were dark and dramatic. Huge towers rose out of mist, lit by searchlights from below. He showed how the new zoning law could create mountain-like buildings. In 1929, he published a book called The Metropolis of Tomorrow. It imagined a city of enormous towers, spaced apart with wide avenues between them. His drawings influenced architects and film-makers for many years. Even so, the real city never looked exactly like his visions. Yet his work helped people imagine what New York might become. Some of his drawings feel like dreams. They show a city that is grand, quiet and slightly mysterious, glowing in the dark.

[long-pause]

{img:60-chrysler-building-plans-race-for-height-1928}

In the late 1920s, New York's builders raced each other to create the tallest building in the world. By the late 1920s, a great race for height began. Several companies wanted the tallest building in the world. The car maker Walter Chrysler planned a new tower on Lexington Avenue. At the same time, a bank was building a tall tower on Wall Street. Both wanted to be the tallest. The Chrysler Building's architect had a secret plan. Inside the tower, his team assembled a long steel spire. In 1929, they raised it through the roof in about ninety minutes. It made the Chrysler Building the tallest in the world. Its shining steel crown, with curved arches and triangular windows, became one of the most beautiful sights in the city. Even so, its record did not last long. In 1931, the Empire State Building opened. It was even taller. Yet in 1925, all of this still lay ahead. The city was only beginning to dream about its tallest towers.

[long-pause]

{img:61-coney-island-beach-crowds-1925-boardwalk}

On hot summer Sundays, a million people could crowd onto the beach at Coney Island. Coney Island is a seaside area at the southern edge of Brooklyn. In the 1920s, it was the city's great playground. When the subway reached it in 1920, a trip there cost just a nickel. On hot summer weekends, enormous crowds came. On some days, newspapers said a million people were at the beach. The sand was covered with people, umbrellas and picnic baskets. Children built sandcastles and splashed in the waves. In 1923, a long wooden boardwalk was opened along the beach. People strolled on it, eating ice cream and hot dogs. Behind the boardwalk were amusement parks with rides, games and sideshows. Even so, the crowds could be overwhelming. It was hard to find space even to sit down. Yet for working families from hot tenements, Coney Island was a paradise. A day at the sea, with sun, fresh air and a cool swim, was one of the great joys of a New York summer.

[long-pause]

{img:62-wonder-wheel-coney-island-evening-lights}

The Wonder Wheel at Coney Island opened in 1920 and still turns today. Coney Island was famous for its amusement rides. One of the best known was the Wonder Wheel. It was a giant Ferris wheel, opened in 1920. It was about forty-five metres tall. It was unusual because some of its cars slid along curved tracks as the wheel turned. They swung outwards and inwards, giving riders a small thrill. Other cars stayed still at the edge of the wheel, for those who preferred a calm ride. From the top, riders could see the ocean, the beach and the whole of Coney Island. At night, the wheel glowed with lights. Nearby were roller coasters, carousels and fun houses. In 1927, the famous Cyclone roller coaster opened. Even so, many visitors simply walked around, enjoying the lights and music. Yet the Wonder Wheel became a symbol of Coney Island. It has kept turning for more than a century and is still a favourite ride today.

[long-pause]

{img:63-central-park-sunday-strollers-lake-rowboats}

On Sundays, families walked, rowed boats and picnicked in Central Park. Central Park lies in the middle of Manhattan. It had been designed in the 1850s by Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux. By the 1920s, it was one of the most loved places in the city. On Sundays, families came to walk, picnic and relax. Couples rented rowboats on the lake. Children sailed toy boats on a small pond. There was a zoo with lions, bears and monkeys. Bands played in the open air. People rode horses on the bridle paths. In winter, they skated on the frozen lake. For people living in crowded apartments, the park was a precious space. It offered trees, grass, fresh air and quiet, right in the heart of the city. Even so, the park was not always in good condition in the 1920s. Some areas were neglected. Yet it remained a place of peace. Its winding paths and green meadows still give New Yorkers a calm escape from the busy streets around them.

[long-pause]

{img:64-balto-statue-dog-central-park-bronze}

In December 1925, a statue of Balto, a famous sled dog, was unveiled in Central Park. In January 1925, a terrible illness called diphtheria broke out in Nome, a small town in Alaska. The town needed medicine quickly. But it was winter, and planes could not fly in the cold. So a relay of dog sled teams carried the medicine across Alaska. They travelled more than a thousand kilometres through snow and storms. The final stretch was led by a dog named Balto. When the medicine arrived, the town was saved. The story was reported in newspapers across America. People were deeply moved. In December 1925, a bronze statue of Balto was placed in Central Park. Balto himself attended the ceremony. Even so, some people felt that other dogs deserved more credit. A dog named Togo had run the longest and hardest part of the journey. Yet Balto became the most famous. Children still climb on his statue today. Its bronze back has been polished smooth by many small hands.

[long-pause]

{img:65-yankee-stadium-1920s-crowd-baseball-game}

Yankee Stadium, opened in 1923, was called the House That Ruth Built. Baseball was the most popular sport in America in the 1920s. New York had three major league teams. The most famous was the New York Yankees. Their biggest star was Babe Ruth. He hit more home runs than anyone had ever imagined. Crowds came to see him play. In 1923, the Yankees opened a huge new stadium in the Bronx. It was called Yankee Stadium. Because Ruth drew such large crowds, people called it the House That Ruth Built. In his first game there, he hit a home run. Even so, 1925 was a difficult year for Ruth. He fell ill early in the season and missed many games. He also argued with his manager and was fined. Yet the same year brought a quiet beginning. On the first of June 1925, a young player named Lou Gehrig began a remarkable run. He played in every Yankees game for the next fourteen years. Ruth and Gehrig together made the Yankees famous.

[long-pause]

{img:66-stickball-children-street-broom-handle-1925}

City children played stickball in the streets, using a broom handle as a bat. Most children in New York did not have a backyard or a playing field. So they played in the streets. One of the favourite games was stickball. It was a simple form of baseball. Instead of a bat, players used an old broom handle. Instead of a hard baseball, they used a small, soft rubber ball. The bases might be a manhole cover, a car fender and a lamp post. Hitting the ball past two sewer covers was a great achievement. Games were stopped whenever a car came down the street. Then they started again. Children also played hopscotch, jump rope, marbles and tag. Even so, playing in the street could be dangerous. As cars became more common, there were more accidents. The city began to build more playgrounds. Yet stickball remained a beloved part of New York childhood for many decades. Some adults still play it today, remembering the streets where they grew up.

[long-pause]

{img:67-macys-thanksgiving-parade-1920s-floats-crowds}

Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade began in 1924, with live animals borrowed from the zoo. In November 1924, the department store Macy's held a parade on Thanksgiving Day. Many of its employees were immigrants. They wanted to celebrate the new American holiday with the kind of festive parade they remembered from Europe. The parade marched from Harlem down to the Macy's store on Herald Square. It had floats, bands and employees dressed as clowns and cowboys. There were also live animals, including bears, elephants and camels, borrowed from the Central Park Zoo. At the end, Santa Claus arrived to welcome the Christmas shopping season. The parade was a great success. It was held again in 1925. In 1927, the famous giant balloons appeared for the first time. They replaced the live animals. Even so, the early parades were simpler and smaller. Yet the tradition grew every year. Today, millions of people watch the parade each Thanksgiving morning, along the streets and at home.

[long-pause]

{img:68-snow-falling-fifth-avenue-1925-evening-lamps}

When snow fell on New York, thousands of men were hired to shovel the streets. New York winters could be cold and snowy. When a big storm came, the city did its best to keep the streets open. In the 1920s, the city hired thousands of extra workers to clear the snow. Many of them were unemployed men who were glad of a few days' work. They shovelled snow into horse-drawn wagons and trucks. The snow was then dumped into the rivers. Some new motor ploughs were used too. Even so, after a heavy storm, the city could be slow for days. Trams and buses got stuck. People walked carefully on icy pavements. Yet snow also brought a special quiet to the city. The noise of traffic was softened. Children came out with sleds and threw snowballs. The parks turned white. For a short time, even busy Manhattan seemed calm and gentle under its white blanket.

[long-pause]

{img:69-christmas-shop-windows-crowds-1925-department-store}

Every December, crowds came to see the decorated Christmas windows of the department stores. Christmas in New York was a time of lights and shopping. The great department stores decorated their windows with special displays. Some showed toys, dolls and train sets. Others showed winter scenes with snow, trees and small moving figures. Families came from all over the city just to look. Children pressed their faces to the glass. Inside, the stores were full of shoppers. Some stores had a Santa Claus who talked to children and asked what they wanted. Toy departments were crowded with parents and grandparents. Even so, many families could not afford expensive gifts. Children often received simple presents, like an orange, a book or a small toy. Yet the windows were free for everyone. Walking past them in the cold evening air was a treat that cost nothing. Many New Yorkers remembered those Christmas windows as one of the happiest parts of their childhood.

[long-pause]

{img:70-chestnut-vendor-cart-street-corner-winter-steam}

On cold days, street vendors sold hot roasted chestnuts and sweet potatoes from small carts. Winter brought special foods to the streets of New York. On many corners, vendors set up small carts with charcoal burners. They roasted chestnuts in metal pans over the glowing coals. The smell filled the cold air. Customers bought a paper bag of hot chestnuts for a few cents. They held them in their hands to keep warm as they walked. Other vendors sold baked sweet potatoes, hot pretzels and soup. Many of the chestnut sellers were Italian or Greek immigrants. They worked long hours in the cold. Even so, they were a welcome sight. Their carts glowed in the early darkness of winter evenings. Office workers stopped on their way home. Children spent their pocket money there. Yet the trade was hard and paid little. Still, the tradition lasted. Chestnut vendors still appear on some New York street corners each winter, filling the air with the same warm smell.

[long-pause]

{img:71-ice-skating-central-park-lake-winter-crowd}

When the lake in Central Park froze, a red ball was raised to show that skating was allowed. Ice skating was a favourite winter pastime in New York. When the weather was cold enough, the lakes in Central Park froze over. Thousands of people came to skate. For many years, the park had a special signal. When the ice was thick enough, a ball was raised on a pole, or a flag flew. Sometimes streetcars carried a sign too. It told people that skating was safe. Families came with their skates over their shoulders. Young couples skated hand in hand. Children raced each other across the ice. Some people brought hot drinks and sat on benches to watch. Even so, the ice was not always reliable. Warm spells could make it thin and dangerous. Yet on a clear, cold winter day, the frozen lake was a joyful sight. Its tradition continues today. Wollman Rink, opened in 1950, now offers skating in the park all winter long.

[long-pause]

{img:72-new-years-eve-times-square-1925-crowd-ball}

Since 1907, a lighted ball has been lowered in Times Square to welcome the New Year. New Year's Eve in Times Square was already a great tradition by 1925. It had begun in 1904, when the New York Times opened its new building there. The newspaper held a celebration with fireworks. Large crowds came. In 1907, fireworks were banned. So the organisers found another idea. They lowered a large lighted ball down a pole on the top of the building. It reached the bottom exactly at midnight. The idea came from time balls used in harbours to help sailors set their clocks. The first ball was made of iron and wood, covered with light bulbs. Crowds counted down the last seconds together. Even so, during Prohibition, the celebrations were officially dry. Yet many people still brought hidden flasks. In 1925, thousands gathered in the cold to watch the ball drop. The tradition continues today, and the crowd is now much larger.

[long-pause]

{img:73-public-school-classroom-1925-desks-teacher}

New York's public schools taught millions of children, many of them the sons and daughters of immigrants. In 1925, New York's public schools were among the largest in the world. Nearly a million children attended them. Many classrooms held forty or more pupils. The desks were made of wood and iron, often bolted to the floor in neat rows. Children learned reading, writing, arithmetic, history and geography. For immigrant children, school was also where they learned English. Many arrived knowing only Italian, Yiddish, Polish or another language. Within a few years, they spoke English fluently. Often they taught their parents. Schools also taught American customs and holidays. Even so, school could be strict. Teachers expected silence and good behaviour. Yet many children loved school. It opened doors that their parents had never had. Some went on to the city's free colleges, like City College. For many families, education was the main hope for a better future in America.

[long-pause]

{img:74-settlement-house-children-reading-room-1925}

Settlement houses offered classes, libraries and clubs for children in poor neighbourhoods. In the poor neighbourhoods of New York, there were special community centres called settlement houses. The idea came from London. Volunteers, often young educated women, lived in the neighbourhood and worked with local families. One of the most famous was the Henry Street Settlement on the Lower East Side. It was founded by a nurse named Lillian Wald in 1893. Settlement houses offered many services. There were classes in English, cooking, sewing and music. There were reading rooms, playgrounds and summer camps. Nurses visited sick people at home. Children came after school to play, read and take part in clubs. Even so, the settlement houses could not solve poverty. Yet they made a real difference to many lives. They gave children safe places to go and help with their studies. Henry Street Settlement still exists today. It continues to serve families on the Lower East Side.

[long-pause]

{img:75-children-swimming-public-pool-summer-1920s}

On hot days, children cooled off in public pools, river baths and the spray of fire hydrants. New York summers were hot, and children wanted to cool off. Some went to public swimming pools. The city had built a number of public baths and pools, especially in poor neighbourhoods. Others swam in the rivers, though the water was often dirty. Some floating baths were moored along the riverbanks. On very hot days, children opened the fire hydrants on the street. Water sprayed out in a great fountain. Children ran through it, laughing and shouting. Firemen sometimes came to close them, but often they let the children play for a while. Even so, this wasted a lot of water and lowered the pressure for firefighting. Later, the city fitted hydrants with special sprinkler caps to make them safer. Yet the fun continued. The sight of children dancing in the spray of a fire hydrant became one of the most loved images of summer in New York.

[long-pause]

{img:76-kids-penny-candy-store-counter-glass-jars}

Neighbourhood candy stores sold sweets for a penny, along with newspapers and egg creams. Almost every New York neighbourhood had a small candy store. It was usually on a corner. It sold newspapers, magazines, cigarettes and sweets. For children, it was a wonderful place. Glass jars were filled with colourful candies. Many cost just a penny. Children spent a long time choosing. Many candy stores also had a soda fountain. They served a famous New York drink called the egg cream. Strangely, it contained neither egg nor cream. It was made from milk, chocolate syrup and soda water, stirred until it foamed. No one is quite sure how it got its name. Even so, it became a classic drink of the city. The candy store was also a meeting place for older children and adults. People read the papers, chatted and made phone calls on the store's telephone. Yet for small children, it was simply the place where a single penny could buy happiness.

[long-pause]

{img:77-children-library-story-hour-1925-librarian}

The New York Public Library ran special children's rooms and story hours in its branch libraries. Besides its great main building, the New York Public Library had many smaller branch libraries in the neighbourhoods. Many had been paid for by the businessman Andrew Carnegie. In the early 1900s, the library began to open special rooms for children. This was a new idea. Before, children were often not welcome in libraries. A librarian named Anne Carroll Moore led this work. She believed that children deserved beautiful books and comfortable places to read. The children's rooms had small tables and chairs, picture books and fairy tales. Librarians held story hours, when they read aloud to groups of children. Even so, some people thought this was not serious work. Yet Moore's ideas spread across the country. For immigrant children, the library was a treasure. They borrowed books in English and learned to love reading. Many famous writers later said that a New York branch library had changed their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:78-crowds-cheering-repeal-december-1933-street}

Prohibition ended in December 1933, after nearly fourteen years. By the end of the 1920s, many Americans had turned against Prohibition. It had not stopped people from drinking. Instead, it had created a huge illegal trade run by criminal gangs. It had filled the courts and prisons. The government had lost huge amounts of money in taxes. When the Great Depression began in 1929, people wanted jobs and tax money more than ever. Making alcohol legal again would create both. In 1933, Congress proposed a new amendment to end Prohibition. It was the Twenty-First Amendment. On the fifth of December 1933, enough states approved it. Prohibition was over. In New York, crowds celebrated in the streets. Bars and restaurants reopened legally. Even so, the change was not complete everywhere. Some states and towns kept their own bans for many years. Yet for New York, it was the end of an era. The age of the speakeasy was over.

[long-pause]

{img:79-old-speakeasy-door-today-quiet-street}

Some of New York's old speakeasies still exist today as restaurants and bars. When Prohibition ended, many speakeasies simply became legal bars. Some kept their old names and decorations. The 21 Club on West Fifty-Second Street became one of the most famous restaurants in New York. Its secret wine cellar survived, and visitors could still see the heavy hidden door. Other old speakeasies continued for decades as quiet neighbourhood bars. In recent years, the idea of the speakeasy has become fashionable again. Some new bars in New York are designed to look like hidden 1920s clubs. They have secret entrances, dim lighting and jazz music. Some even ask for a password. Even so, these are only imitations. They are completely legal. Yet they show how strongly the twenties still capture the imagination. The secret door, the soft knock and the warm room full of music have become part of the city's legend. They remind New Yorkers of a time when an ordinary evening out felt like an adventure.

[long-pause]

{img:80-new-york-1925-rooftop-water-tower-sunset}

The wooden water towers on New York's rooftops, a familiar sight in 1925, are still built by hand today. Across the rooftops of New York stands a curious sight. Many buildings have large round wooden tanks standing on their roofs. These are water towers. They became common in the late 1800s, when buildings grew taller than six storeys. The city's water pressure was not strong enough to push water to the upper floors. So water was pumped up into a tank on the roof. From there, gravity carried it down to the taps below. The tanks were made of wood, usually cedar or redwood. Wood was cheap, light and kept the water from freezing in winter. By 1925, thousands of these towers stood on the city's roofs. Even so, they are not just relics of the past. Several thousand are still in use today. Yet the most remarkable thing is how they are made. A few small family companies still build them by hand, using methods that have hardly changed in a hundred years.

[long-pause]

{img:81-new-york-public-library-lions-steps-evening}

The two stone lions in front of the New York Public Library were later named Patience and Fortitude. On Fifth Avenue stands the main building of the New York Public Library. It opened in 1911. It is a grand white marble building. Inside are huge reading rooms with high painted ceilings and long wooden tables. Anyone could come in and read for free. In the 1920s, it was full of students, writers, immigrants and ordinary readers. Many people who could not afford books educated themselves there. Two large stone lions guard the steps outside. They were carved by the Piccirilli brothers, a family of Italian stone carvers in the Bronx. At first, people gave the lions different nicknames. In the 1930s, Mayor Fiorello La Guardia named them Patience and Fortitude. He said New Yorkers would need those qualities to get through the Great Depression. Even so, in 1925 the lions were simply the lions. Yet they have watched over Fifth Avenue for more than a century. They remain among the most beloved statues in the city.

[long-pause]

{img:82-evening-lights-manhattan-1925-river-quiet}

For many who lived through it, the twenties in New York were remembered as a bright and restless time. The years of the mid twenties in New York were full of change. The city was growing taller and richer. New music, new fashions and new ideas filled the streets. Women had won the vote and were finding new freedoms. Radio, movies and jazz were changing how people spent their evenings. Prohibition had pushed drinking into secret rooms, and made breaking the law feel almost ordinary. Even so, not everyone shared in the good times. Many people were still poor. Black New Yorkers faced unfair treatment. Immigrants struggled to make new lives. Yet for those who lived through it, the decade had a special glow. When the stock market crashed in October 1929, the mood changed suddenly. The hard years of the Great Depression began. Looking back, many remembered the twenties as a time of energy, hope and freedom. It was a time when New York seemed to be inventing the future, one bright night at a time.
