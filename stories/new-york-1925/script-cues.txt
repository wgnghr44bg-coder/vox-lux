New York in 1925 is a city full of energy and change. Almost six million people live here. Why are the 1920s called the Roaring Twenties? Because it is a time of noise, excitement and change.

[pause]

In 1918, the Great War came to an end. It had been a long and terrible war. Afterwards, many people wanted to forget the hard years and enjoy life again. In America, the factories were busy, and there was work for many people. Many families had more money than ever before. They bought new things: cars, radios, refrigerators, vacuum cleaners and electric irons. Life was becoming more comfortable.

[long-pause]

The car changed everything. A few years before, cars were only for the rich. But now, a car called the Ford Model T is cheap enough for many families to buy. It is black and simple and very strong. People call it the Tin Lizzie. By 1925, millions of Americans have a car. On Sundays, families go for drives in the countryside. The streets of New York are full of cars, honking their horns.

[pause]

{img:ford-model-t-black-car-family-sunday-drive-countryside-road-1925}
Radio is new too. Only a few years ago, there were no radio stations at all. Now, millions of families have a radio in their living room. They listen to music and news, comedy shows and sports. For the first time, people all over the country can hear the same thing at the same time. It feels like magic. Families gather around the radio in the evenings, just as they will later gather around the television.

[long-pause]

There are new things for young people too. Young women, called flappers, cut their hair in short bobs and wear short dresses that swing when they dance. They dance the Charleston, a fast and happy dance with kicking legs. Many women have jobs now, in offices and shops and factories. And since 1920, American women can vote in elections. It feels like a new world.

[pause]

{img:young-women-1925-bobbed-hair-dancing-charleston-dance-hall-band}
There is one strange thing about the 1920s in America. Since 1920, it has been against the law to make or sell alcoholic drinks, like beer and wine and whisky. This is called Prohibition. Many people did not like this law, and did not obey it. In New York, there were thousands of secret bars, called speakeasies, hidden behind ordinary doors. It was a strange time. The law was finally changed in 1933.

[long-pause]

Not everyone in the 1920s is rich, of course. Many families in New York are still poor, working long hours for low wages. Farmers in the countryside are having a hard time. But even poor families can feel the excitement of the time. They can listen to the radio, go to the cinema, and dream of a better life. And in New York, everyone seems to be dreaming.

[pause]

{img:new-york-1925-crowd-street-corner-newsboy-hats-coats-energy-morning}
In 1925, a young writer called F. Scott Fitzgerald published a novel called The Great Gatsby. It is about rich people on Long Island, near New York, with their parties and cars and dreams. It is still one of the most famous American books. It shows the glitter of the Roaring Twenties, and also a little of its sadness. But the family in our story does not go to parties like that. Their New York is the city of ordinary people, working, hoping and making a life.

[pause]

The 1920s are also a time of sport. Millions of people follow baseball, boxing, football and tennis. Sports stars become as famous as film stars. People listen to the games on the radio and read about them in the newspapers. Children collect cards with pictures of their heroes. And on Saturdays, the parks and fields are full of people playing ball games.

[pause]

There are new kinds of shops too. Department stores sell everything under one roof, from clothes to furniture to toys. There are chain stores, with the same shops in many towns. And people begin to buy things on credit, paying a little bit every month. Advertising is everywhere: in newspapers and magazines, on the radio, and on huge signs in the streets. Everyone, it seems, wants something new.

[long-pause]

{img:manhattan-island-1925-aerial-view-rivers-bridges-buildings-central-park}
New York is made of five parts, called boroughs. The most famous is Manhattan, a long, narrow island between two rivers. The others are Brooklyn, Queens, the Bronx and Staten Island. Great bridges and ferries connect them all.

[pause]

Manhattan is the heart of the city. At its southern tip, there are the old streets of downtown, with the banks of Wall Street and the busy harbour. Further north, there are the shops and theatres of Midtown. In the middle of the island, there is a great park, called Central Park, with lakes and meadows and woods. And at the northern end, there is Harlem, a lively neighbourhood full of music.

[long-pause]

The streets of Manhattan are laid out in a grid, like a chessboard. The avenues run from north to south, and the streets run from east to west. Most of them have numbers instead of names. So it is easy to find your way. If you are on Fifth Avenue and Forty-Second Street, you know exactly where you are.

[pause]

{img:elevated-train-tracks-steel-above-street-1925-trains-shadows-shops-below}
Getting around New York is easy, but noisy. There are trolleys, running on rails in the streets. There are buses and taxis, cars and trucks. Above some of the avenues, there are elevated railways, called the El. Trains rattle along on tall iron tracks, high above the street, past the second-floor windows of the buildings. And under the ground, there is the subway, the underground railway, carrying millions of people every day.

[long-pause]

The subway is one of the wonders of New York. It opened about twenty years before, in 1904. A ride costs just five cents, a nickel, and you can go almost anywhere in the city. The trains are crowded and hot in summer. But they are fast. People read their newspapers, chat with friends, or doze as the train rushes through the dark tunnels.

[pause]

{img:subway-car-1925-passengers-straps-newspapers-hats-electric-lights}
Every neighbourhood of New York is different. In Little Italy, people speak Italian, and shops sell pasta and olive oil. In Chinatown, next door, there are Chinese restaurants and shops selling tea and silk. On the Lower East Side, many Jewish families live, and the streets are full of pushcarts selling everything. In Harlem, there are jazz clubs and churches and poets. In each neighbourhood, people keep their own language and customs, but they are all New Yorkers.

[long-pause]

The most famous place in the city is Times Square. It is where Broadway, the street of the theatres, crosses Seventh Avenue. At night, it is lit up with thousands of electric lights. There are huge signs advertising chewing gum and cigarettes, cars and shows. People call Broadway the Great White Way, because of all the lights. Crowds walk there every evening, just to look.

[pause]

{img:times-square-1925-night-electric-signs-crowds-theatres-cars-glow}
For the people of New York, the city is noisy, crowded and sometimes tiring. But it is also exciting and full of life. There is always something to see, something to hear, something new to discover. People say that New York never sleeps. And in 1925, it seems to be true.

[pause]

New York also has many bridges. The most famous is the Brooklyn Bridge, finished in 1883. It is a great suspension bridge, hanging from thick steel cables, between two tall towers of stone. People walk across it on a wooden path above the traffic, enjoying the view of the river and the city. On summer evenings, couples stroll across it, hand in hand, as the sun sets.

[pause]

In the evenings, the streets of New York are busy in a different way. People come out of their offices and factories and head home. The subway and the elevated trains are packed. The cinemas and theatres open their doors. Restaurants and cafés fill up. And in the neighbourhoods, people sit on their stoops and fire escapes, talking with their neighbours in the warm evening air.

[pause]

In the middle of Manhattan, Central Park is a green island in the city of stone. It has lakes with rowing boats, meadows with sheep, and woods with winding paths. There is a zoo, and a carousel for children. On Sundays, families from all over the city come to the park, to walk and picnic and breathe the fresh air. It feels like the countryside, right in the middle of the busiest city in America.

{img:taxi-driver-father-1925-flat-cap-yellow-cab-street-smiling-morning}
Let us meet the family who lives in Little Italy, on the Lower East Side of Manhattan.

[pause]

The father is called Salvatore, but everyone calls him Sal. He is about forty years old, with dark curly hair and a big moustache. He was born in a village near Naples, in Italy. He came to America when he was a young man, with his mother. He worked many jobs: in a bakery, on the docks, in a factory. Now he drives a yellow taxi. He knows every street in New York. He is cheerful and talkative, and he loves his family more than anything.

[pause]

The mother is called Rosa. She was born in New York, to Italian parents. She is about thirty-five, small and quick, with dark eyes and a warm smile. She works as a seamstress in a big dressmaking workshop, in the garment district. She sews dresses for the shops on Fifth Avenue. In the evenings, she cooks and cleans and looks after everyone. She is tired a lot of the time. But she never complains.

[long-pause]

They have two children. The daughter is called Lucia, but at school she is called Lucy. She is twelve years old. She loves music more than anything. She listens to the radio whenever she can, and she knows all the new songs by heart. She sings while she does her homework, and while she helps her mother with the dishes. She dreams of being a singer on the radio one day.

[pause]

{img:girl-twelve-1925-singing-by-radio-kitchen-apartment-bobbed-hair-happy}
The son is called Antonio, but everyone calls him Tony. He is nine years old, and he is never still. He sells newspapers on the street corner, before and after school. He knows everyone in the neighbourhood. He is crazy about baseball, and his great hero is Babe Ruth, the famous player of the New York Yankees. He has a baseball card of Babe Ruth, which he keeps in his pocket at all times.

[long-pause]

The grandmother lives with them too. She is Sal's mother, and her name is Filomena. Everybody calls her Nonna. She is nearly seventy. She came to America with Sal, twenty years ago, through Ellis Island. She speaks only a few words of English. She spends her days cooking, praying and sitting by the window. She makes the best pasta in the whole street. She keeps a little shrine to the Virgin Mary on a shelf, with candles and paper flowers.

[pause]

{img:italian-grandmother-1925-black-dress-making-pasta-kitchen-table-flour}
Nonna does not really understand the new world of radios and cars and flappers. She shakes her head at the short dresses of the young women. She does not trust the telephone. But she loves her family, and she loves New York in her own way. She likes to sit on the stoop of the building in the evening, the steps in front of the door, and talk with the other old Italian women of the street.

[long-pause]

The family is not rich. They live in a small apartment with three rooms, on the fourth floor of an old building. Sal and Rosa both work long hours. Every cent is counted. But they have enough to eat, and a little to spare. Last year, they saved up and bought a radio. It stands in the corner of the kitchen, in a wooden cabinet, and it is the pride of the family.

[pause]

{img:family-dinner-1925-small-kitchen-pasta-bread-wine-radio-corner-evening}
In the evenings, the family eats dinner together at the kitchen table. Nonna has made pasta with tomato sauce, and there is bread and a little cheese. Sal tells them about the funny passengers in his taxi today. Rosa tells them about the beautiful dresses she is sewing. Tony tells them the headlines from the newspapers. Lucy wants to turn on the radio. And Nonna says a prayer before they eat, in Italian, while everyone waits.

[pause]

On Sundays, the family goes to mass at the old church of Saint Patrick, on Mulberry Street. Nonna wears her best black dress and her lace veil. The children are scrubbed clean and wear their best clothes. After mass, they walk along the street, greeting their neighbours. Then they go home for a big Sunday lunch, which Nonna has been cooking since early morning.

[pause]

The family has a little canary, in a cage by the kitchen window. Its name is Caruso, after the famous Italian singer. It sings every morning, as soon as the sun comes up. Nonna talks to it while she cooks, in Italian. She says it is the only one in the family that really listens to her. Everyone laughs. But it is true that the canary sings back whenever she speaks.

[long-pause]

{img:lower-east-side-tenement-buildings-1925-fire-escapes-laundry-pushcarts-street}
The family lives in a tenement building. A tenement is a tall, narrow apartment building, with many families living inside. There are thousands of them on the Lower East Side.

[pause]

The building has five floors, built of red brick, with iron fire escapes zig-zagging down the front. There are four apartments on each floor. About twenty families live in the building, almost all of them Italian. On the ground floor, there is a little shop that sells groceries: pasta and olive oil, cheese and salami, tins of tomatoes and loaves of bread.

[long-pause]

The family's apartment has three small rooms, one behind the other. At the front, there is the kitchen, which is also the living room. It has a big iron stove, which burns coal for cooking and for heat. There is a sink with a cold water tap, a table and chairs, and the radio in the corner. There is a window onto the street, with the fire escape outside.

[pause]

{img:tenement-kitchen-1925-coal-stove-sink-table-radio-window-light}
In the middle, there is a small room where Sal and Rosa sleep. At the back, there is another small room, where the children and Nonna sleep. Lucy and Nonna share one bed, and Tony has a little cot in the corner. The back room has only a tiny window, onto an airshaft, a narrow gap between the buildings. It is dark, even in the daytime.

[long-pause]

Until a few years ago, the toilets were in the back yard, shared by everyone. Now there is a toilet in the hallway on each floor, shared by the families on that floor. There is no bathroom in the apartment. On Saturday nights, Rosa fills a big tin tub with hot water from the stove, and everyone takes a bath in the kitchen, one after another. The children go first, while the water is cleanest.

[pause]

{img:tin-bathtub-kitchen-saturday-night-steam-stove-towels-children}
In summer, the apartment is very hot. The heat rises up through the building, and the little rooms are like ovens. On the hottest nights, many families sleep outside on the fire escapes, or up on the flat roof of the building, to catch a little breeze. Lucy and Tony love sleeping on the roof. They lie on blankets and look up at the sky, and listen to the sounds of the city below.

[long-pause]

In winter, the apartment is cold. The coal stove in the kitchen is the only heat. The family gathers around it in the evenings, and Rosa warms bricks in the oven to put in the beds. Frost makes patterns on the inside of the windows. Tony draws pictures in the frost with his finger.

[pause]

{img:tenement-roof-summer-night-families-sleeping-blankets-city-lights-stars}
The building is crowded and noisy. You can hear the neighbours through the walls: talking, laughing, arguing, singing. You can smell their cooking: garlic and tomatoes, onions and frying fish. Children run up and down the stairs. Babies cry. Radios play. But the neighbours are friendly, and they help each other. When someone is sick, the others bring soup. When someone has a baby, everyone brings presents. It is like one big, noisy family.

[pause]

The building has a janitor, an old Irishman called Mr. Murphy, who lives in the basement. He shovels coal into the furnace, sweeps the stairs and fixes things that break. He is grumpy, but kind. He gives the children candy sometimes, and tells them stories about Ireland. Tony likes him very much, and helps him carry the coal sometimes, after school.

[pause]

Every week, the iceman comes to the building. He carries a big block of ice on his shoulder, with metal tongs, up the stairs to each apartment. The family has an icebox in the kitchen, a wooden cupboard lined with metal. The block of ice goes in the top, and keeps the food cold below. In summer, the children follow the iceman's wagon, hoping for little chips of ice to suck.

[pause]

On summer evenings, the stoop of the building becomes a little meeting place. The women sit on the steps with their sewing, talking in Italian. The men stand at the bottom, smoking and arguing about politics and baseball. The children run around them, playing. Nonna has her own special place, on the top step, on a little cushion. Everyone in the building knows that it is her seat.

[long-pause]

{img:ellis-island-1905-immigrants-arriving-ferry-great-hall-red-brick-towers}
Twenty years before our story, in 1905, Nonna and her son Sal came to America. Like millions of other immigrants, they came through a little island in New York harbour, called Ellis Island.

[pause]

They left their village near Naples in the spring. Nonna's husband had passed away the year before, and life in the village was hard. There was little work and little food. Many people from the village had already gone to America. They wrote letters home, saying that there was work in New York, and a chance for a better life. So Nonna sold everything she had, and bought two tickets on a big steamship.

[long-pause]

The journey across the Atlantic Ocean took about two weeks. Nonna and Sal travelled in steerage, the cheapest part of the ship, deep down below the decks. It was crowded and noisy, with hundreds of people sleeping in rows of bunks. Many people were seasick. The food was simple: bread, soup, potatoes. But everyone was full of hope. They sang songs from home, and talked about what America would be like.

[pause]

{img:immigrants-ship-deck-1905-first-sight-statue-of-liberty-crowd-emotion}
On the last morning, everyone crowded onto the deck. And there, rising out of the mist, was the Statue of Liberty, holding up her torch. Many people cried. Some fell to their knees and prayed. Sal, who was eighteen then, held his mother's hand tightly. Nonna says she will never forget that moment, as long as she lives.

[long-pause]

Then the ship docked, and the passengers from steerage were taken by ferry to Ellis Island. There, they walked into a huge building of red brick, with tall towers. Inside, there was a great hall, with a high arched ceiling. Thousands of people were waiting there, in long lines, with their bags and bundles. They spoke dozens of languages. It was noisy and confusing and a little frightening.

[pause]

{img:great-hall-ellis-island-crowds-lines-benches-arched-ceiling-windows-light}
The immigrants had to pass through several checks. Doctors looked at them, to make sure they were healthy. Officials asked them questions, through interpreters: their name, where they came from, where they were going, whether they had any money, whether they had family in America. Most people passed through in a few hours. A few were held back, or sent home, if they were very ill. Nonna and Sal waited nervously in the long lines.

[long-pause]

At last, an official stamped their papers and said: welcome to America. Nonna did not understand the words, but she understood the smile. They took a ferry to Manhattan, where Sal's cousin was waiting for them. That night, they slept in his crowded apartment in Little Italy. The next day, Sal started working in a bakery. And a new life began.

[pause]

{img:immigrant-mother-son-1905-ferry-manhattan-skyline-bundles-hopeful}
Nonna still has the little bundle she carried from Italy. It is wrapped in a cloth, at the bottom of the chest in the back room. Inside, there is a photograph of her husband, a rosary, a little silver medal of a saint, and a handful of soil from her village. Sometimes, she takes it out and shows it to Lucy and Tony. She tells them about the village, and the ship, and the statue in the mist. And the children listen, and try to imagine their father as a boy of eighteen, arriving in a new world.

[pause]

Ellis Island was busiest in the years around 1905 to 1914. On some days, more than ten thousand people passed through it. Altogether, about twelve million immigrants came to America through Ellis Island, between 1892 and 1954. They came from Italy, Ireland, Germany, Russia, Poland, Greece, and many other countries. Each of them carried a bundle, a suitcase, and a dream.

[pause]

Not everyone's journey was easy. Some families were separated for a while, if one person was ill and had to stay in the hospital on the island. Some people had to wait for days for a relative to come and fetch them. But most people passed through in a few hours, and stepped onto the ferry to Manhattan, full of hope. Ellis Island was called the Island of Hope, and for most people, that is what it was.

[long-pause]

{img:early-morning-tenement-kitchen-mother-making-coffee-stove-family-waking-1925}
Let us spend an ordinary day with the family, in the spring of 1925.

[pause]

The day begins very early. Rosa gets up first, at half past five, while it is still dark. She lights the coal stove in the kitchen and puts the coffee pot on. Soon, the smell of strong coffee fills the little apartment. Nonna gets up next, and starts slicing bread. Then Sal wakes up, and stretches, and comes into the kitchen, yawning.

[long-pause]

Breakfast is quick and simple: bread with butter, a little cheese, and coffee with lots of milk. The children have warm milk with a little coffee in it. Sal kisses everyone goodbye and goes off to get his taxi from the garage. Rosa puts on her hat and coat and hurries off to the workshop. She must be there by seven o'clock. Tony runs down the stairs to collect his newspapers for the morning.

[pause]

{img:newsboy-collecting-bundle-newspapers-distribution-corner-dawn-1925}
Lucy helps Nonna clean up the breakfast things. Then she puts on her coat and walks to school with her friends. The school is a big brick building, a few streets away. There are hundreds of children there, from all over the world. Many of them, like Lucy, are the children of immigrants. At school, they all learn English, and American history, and arithmetic, and to sing American songs.

[long-pause]

Nonna stays at home. She washes the clothes in a big tub, scrubbing them on a washboard. She hangs them on the line outside the window, which runs across to the building opposite on a little pulley. Then she goes down to the street to do the shopping. She buys bread from the baker, vegetables from the pushcarts, and a little piece of meat from the butcher. She speaks Italian to all of them. In Little Italy, she does not need English.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-shopping-pushcarts-mulberry-street-vegetables-italian-vendors}
At lunchtime, Tony and Lucy come home from school for a bowl of soup and some bread. Then they go back to school for the afternoon. After school, Tony goes back to his newspaper corner, to sell the evening papers. Lucy does her homework at the kitchen table, while Nonna starts making the dinner. The radio plays softly in the corner. Lucy sings along while she writes.

[long-pause]

At about six o'clock, Rosa comes home from the workshop, tired. Her fingers ache from sewing all day. Nonna gives her a cup of coffee, and she sits down for a few minutes before helping with dinner. Tony comes in, with a pocket full of pennies from selling papers. He gives them to his mother, proudly. Then Sal comes home, at about seven, and the family sits down to eat together.

[pause]

{img:family-evening-radio-listening-kitchen-1925-lamp-children-floor-parents-chairs}
After dinner, the family sits around the radio. They listen to a music programme, with a dance band playing the latest songs. Lucy sings along, and Sal pretends to dance with Rosa around the little kitchen. Nonna laughs and claps her hands. Tony lies on the floor, reading the comics in the newspaper. And at nine o'clock, Rosa sends the children to bed. Through the thin walls, they can still hear the radio playing, softly, as they fall asleep.

[pause]

On the way to school, Lucy stops at the corner to say good morning to the old woman who sells pretzels. The pretzels are big and soft and warm, covered with salt. When Lucy has a penny, she buys one and shares it with her friends. When she has no penny, the old woman sometimes gives her a small one anyway. Lucy always says thank you in Italian, which makes the old woman smile.

[pause]

In the afternoon, Nonna sits by the window for a little while, resting. She watches the street below: the children playing, the pushcarts, the people hurrying by. She says her rosary, moving the little beads through her fingers. She thinks about her village in Italy, and her husband, and her life. And then she gets up, and goes back to the stove, to start the dinner.

[pause]

Sometimes, after dinner, Sal takes Tony for a walk around the block. They walk past the shops and the cafés, past the church and the little park. Sal buys Tony a lemon ice from a man with a cart. They talk about baseball, and about school, and about what Tony wants to be when he grows up. Tony says he wants to be a baseball player, or a taxi driver, or both. Sal laughs and says both sounds good.

[long-pause]

{img:yellow-checker-taxi-1925-fifth-avenue-traffic-passengers-hats-busy}
Sal drives a yellow taxi. In 1925, taxis are becoming very popular in New York. And many of them are yellow.

[pause]

A few years before, a businessman called John Hertz started a taxi company in Chicago. He painted all his taxis bright yellow, so that people could see them easily from far away. The idea spread to New York. By 1925, there are thousands of taxis in the city, many of them yellow. They are big, square cars, with a tall roof, black leather seats and a little meter that counts the fare.

[long-pause]

Sal does not own his taxi. He rents it from a taxi company, every day. In the morning, he picks it up from the garage, and in the evening, he brings it back. He pays the company a part of the money he earns. The rest is his. On good days, he earns quite a lot. On bad days, when it rains or when the streets are empty, he earns very little.

[pause]

{img:taxi-driver-helping-lady-into-cab-fur-coat-hotel-entrance-doorman}
Sal knows the streets of New York like the back of his hand. He knows every shortcut, every one-way street, every traffic jam. He knows the best hotels and theatres and restaurants. He knows where the rich people live, and where the poor people live. He knows the docks and the railway stations, and the quickest way to get anywhere.

[long-pause]

Every day, Sal meets all kinds of people in his taxi. Rich ladies in fur coats, going shopping on Fifth Avenue. Businessmen in suits, rushing to meetings on Wall Street. Tourists from other countries, looking out of the windows with wide eyes. Actors going to the theatres on Broadway. Sailors coming back from long voyages. Sometimes he even gets famous people. Once, he drove a famous actress to her theatre. She gave him a big tip, and he talked about it for weeks.

[pause]

{img:taxi-interior-1925-driver-view-passenger-back-seat-times-square-lights}
Sal likes to talk to his passengers. He asks them where they are from and what they do. He tells them jokes, and gives them advice about the best restaurants in the city. Most passengers like him. Some of them ask for him by name, when they need a taxi. He gives them a little card with his name and the number of the taxi company.

[long-pause]

Driving in New York is not easy. The streets are full of cars and trucks, trolleys and horse carts, people and bicycles. There are traffic lights now, at some of the big crossings, which are quite new. There are policemen in the middle of the street, directing traffic with white gloves. Everyone honks their horns. Sal stays calm. He says that a good taxi driver needs patience, a good map in his head, and a sense of humour.

[pause]

{img:policeman-white-gloves-directing-traffic-1925-intersection-cars-crowd}
Sometimes, on Sundays, Sal is allowed to take the taxi out for the family. Then they all squeeze into it, even Nonna, and drive out of the city. They drive over the great Brooklyn Bridge, to the beach, or up along the Hudson River, to see the trees and the countryside. The children press their noses against the windows. Nonna holds on tightly and says her prayers. And Sal drives slowly and carefully, singing old Italian songs, all the way.

[pause]

At lunchtime, Sal stops at a little lunch counter near the docks, where many taxi drivers eat. They sit on tall stools at a long counter, and eat sandwiches and soup and drink coffee. They talk about the traffic, the passengers and the baseball. They tell each other where the best fares are, and which streets are blocked. Then they go back to their taxis, and drive off into the busy city.

[pause]

In the evenings, when the theatres let out, Sal often waits outside them for passengers. The crowds pour out onto the pavement, laughing and talking about the show. Women in long dresses and furs, men in black coats and top hats. They wave for taxis, and Sal is always there. These are the best fares of the day. Sometimes the passengers are so happy after the show that they sing all the way home.

[long-pause]

{img:garment-district-1925-sewing-workshop-rows-women-sewing-machines-light-windows}
Rosa works in the garment district of New York. This is the part of the city where clothes are made. And in 1925, it is one of the biggest centres of dressmaking in the world.

[pause]

The garment district is in Midtown Manhattan, around Seventh Avenue. There are dozens of tall buildings there, full of workshops. In the streets, men push racks of dresses and coats on little wheels, from one building to another. Trucks unload rolls of cloth. Buttons and ribbons and thread are sold in little shops. Everyone is busy and in a hurry.

[long-pause]

Rosa works on the eighth floor of a big building. The workshop is a long room with big windows. There are rows of tables, with sewing machines on them. About fifty women work there, most of them Italian or Jewish. They sew dresses for the big department stores and fashion shops. The dresses are the latest fashion: short and loose, with dropped waists and beads and fringes.

[pause]

{img:seamstress-woman-sewing-machine-beaded-dress-close-hands-concentration}
Rosa is a skilled seamstress. She sews the difficult parts: the collars and the sleeves, the fine seams and the beading. She works on an electric sewing machine, which makes a steady humming sound. She works fast and carefully, from seven in the morning until half past five in the evening, with a short break for lunch. It is tiring work, and her back and fingers ache by the end of the day.

[long-pause]

Work in the garment district was once very hard and dangerous. In 1911, there was a terrible fire in a dress factory called the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory. Many young women lost their lives, because the doors were locked and there was no safe way out. It was a great sorrow for the city. After that, new laws were made to make the workshops safer. Now there must be fire escapes and unlocked doors. The workers also joined unions, to ask for better pay and shorter hours. Things are better now, in 1925, though work is still hard.

[pause]

{img:women-workers-lunch-break-workshop-sandwiches-talking-laughing-1925}
At lunchtime, the women sit together at the end of the long tables and eat their sandwiches. They talk and laugh and share news. They talk about their children and their husbands, the prices in the shops, and the latest films. Some of them speak Italian, some Yiddish, some English. They teach each other words in their languages. Rosa has learned quite a lot of Yiddish from her friend Sarah, who sits next to her.

[long-pause]

Sometimes, Rosa brings home leftover pieces of cloth from the workshop. They are too small to use for dresses, but big enough for other things. She uses them to make clothes for Lucy and Tony, or patches for Sal's trousers. Once, she found a beautiful piece of blue silk, with little silver beads on it. She used it to make a collar for Lucy's best dress. Lucy thinks it is the most beautiful thing she has ever worn.

[pause]

{img:mother-sewing-at-home-kitchen-table-evening-lamp-daughter-trying-dress}
Rosa has a dream. One day, she would like to have her own little dress shop, in Little Italy, with her name on the window. She would make dresses for the women of the neighbourhood, for weddings and christenings and feast days. She has been saving a few cents every week, in a little tin box under the bed. It will take many years. But she keeps saving, and she keeps dreaming.

[pause]

Some of the dresses Rosa sews are very beautiful, for rich ladies to wear to parties. They have glittering beads and long silk fringes that swing when the wearer dances. Rosa sometimes holds one up to the window and looks at it, shimmering in the light. She will never wear such a dress herself. But she is proud that her hands have made something so beautiful.

[pause]

On the way home, Rosa walks with her friend Sarah to the subway station. They talk about their children and their plans. Sarah also dreams of having her own shop one day. They joke that they will open a shop together, with Italian and Jewish dresses, and all the women of New York will come. They laugh. But perhaps, Rosa thinks, it is not such a silly idea after all.

[long-pause]

{img:newsboy-1925-flat-cap-shouting-headlines-street-corner-stack-papers}
Tony sells newspapers on a street corner, near the elevated railway station. He is one of thousands of newsboys in New York.

[pause]

In 1925, there are many newspapers in New York. There are morning papers and evening papers, serious papers and fun papers. There are papers in English, Italian, Yiddish, German and Chinese. People read the newspaper every day, to find out the news. And many of them buy it from a newsboy on the corner.

[long-pause]

Every morning, at half past five, Tony walks to a little distribution office near the elevated station. There, he buys a bundle of newspapers, about fifty of them, for a few cents each. Then he carries them to his corner, and sells them for a little more. The difference is his profit. On a good day, he might earn twenty or thirty cents. On a very good day, with exciting news, he can earn a dollar.

[pause]

{img:newsboy-selling-paper-businessman-elevated-station-stairs-morning-rush}
To sell the papers, Tony shouts out the headlines. Extra, extra, read all about it! He shouts the most exciting news, to make people stop and buy. Sometimes he makes the news sound a little more exciting than it really is. The businessmen on their way to work smile, and give him a nickel. Some of them buy a paper from him every day. They know his name, and he knows theirs.

[long-pause]

The newsboys of New York are a tough and lively group. They come from all over the city, and from all kinds of families. They have their own corners, and they guard them carefully. They have their own slang words and their own jokes. Some of them are very young, only seven or eight years old. Many of them give their earnings to their families, to help pay for food and rent.

[pause]

{img:group-newsboys-sitting-steps-counting-coins-laughing-afternoon-1925}
Tony's best friend is another newsboy, called Sammy. Sammy is Jewish, and lives a few streets away. His corner is just across the street from Tony's. In the quiet times, they sit on the steps of a building together, and talk about baseball. Sammy supports the Giants, and Tony supports the Yankees. They argue about it every day. But they are the best of friends.

[long-pause]

Selling newspapers means that Tony always knows the latest news. He reads the headlines, and sometimes the whole front page, while he waits for customers. He knows about the president and the government, about crimes and accidents, about sports and films. In the evening, at dinner, he tells the family all the news. Sal says that Tony is better than the radio.

[pause]

{img:boy-reading-newspaper-front-page-quiet-moment-corner-elevated-train-above}
At the beginning of 1925, in the middle of winter, there was one story that everyone followed. It was about a little town called Nome, far away in Alaska, where children were very ill. Brave teams of sled dogs raced across the snow to bring them medicine. Every day, Tony shouted out the latest news about the dogs. And every day, people bought his papers, to find out what had happened. It was the most exciting story of the year. We will hear more about it later.

[pause]

On Sundays, the newspapers are very thick, with many pages and special sections. There are comic strips in colour, which the children love. There are pages about fashion, and films, and sports. Tony sells more papers on Sunday than on any other day. But they are heavy to carry. He staggers to his corner with the big bundle, and his arms ache all morning.

[pause]

Sometimes, when the news is very big, the newspapers print a special edition in the middle of the day, called an extra. Then all the newsboys rush to collect them, and run through the streets, shouting. Extra, extra! People stop in the street and crowd around them, to buy a copy. On those days, Tony can sell a hundred papers in an hour, and comes home with his pockets full of nickels.

[long-pause]

{img:wooden-radio-cabinet-1925-glowing-dials-family-gathered-kitchen-evening}
In 1925, radio is one of the most exciting new things in the world. And for the family, their radio is a treasure.

[pause]

Only five years before, in 1920, the first proper radio stations started broadcasting in America. At first, very few people had radios. They were complicated machines, with headphones and wires and lots of knobs. But by 1925, radios are becoming cheaper and easier to use. Millions of families have one. They have big wooden cabinets, with dials and a loudspeaker.

[long-pause]

The family bought their radio last Christmas, after saving for months. It stands on a little table in the corner of the kitchen. It has two big dials on the front, and a round loudspeaker on top, like a big ear. When you turn it on, it takes a minute to warm up. Then it starts to hum and crackle. And then, like magic, music or voices come out of it.

[pause]

{img:girl-turning-radio-dial-listening-close-music-smiling-1925}
Lucy is the expert on the radio. She knows how to turn the dials to find the stations. She knows what programmes are on, and at what time. On Monday evenings, there is a dance band from a big hotel. On Tuesday, there is a programme of opera singers. On Saturday, there is a comedy show. And every evening, there is the news, read by a man with a deep, calm voice.

[long-pause]

What the family loves most is the music. They listen to dance bands and jazz bands, to popular songs and to opera. Nonna loves the opera most of all, especially when the singers are Italian. When a famous tenor called Enrico Caruso sang, before he passed away, she listened with tears in her eyes. Lucy loves the new jazz songs and the singers. She knows all the words, and sings along in a clear, sweet voice.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-listening-opera-radio-eyes-closed-hands-folded-kitchen}
Sal loves the sports on the radio, especially the baseball games and the boxing matches. On the radio, the announcer describes everything that is happening, very fast and very excited. You feel as if you are there. When the Yankees play, Sal and Tony sit close to the radio, listening to every word. When the Yankees win, they jump up and cheer, and Nonna tells them to be quiet.

[long-pause]

The radio brings the whole world into the little kitchen in Little Italy. Through it, the family hears the president speaking from Washington. They hear music from Chicago and New Orleans. They hear the news from Europe and Asia. They hear stories and plays and poems. For people who cannot travel, it is like a window onto the world.

[pause]

{img:family-dancing-kitchen-radio-music-father-daughter-laughing-1925}
Sometimes, on Saturday evenings, when the dance band is playing, Sal pushes the kitchen table against the wall. Then the whole family dances around the little kitchen. Sal dances with Rosa, and Tony dances with Nonna, very carefully. Lucy shows them how to do the Charleston, kicking her legs and waving her hands. Everyone laughs and tries to copy her. The neighbours knock on the ceiling. But then they turn up their own radios, and start dancing too.

[pause]

Some evenings, the neighbours who do not have a radio come into the family's kitchen to listen. They sit on chairs and on the floor, squeezed together. Everyone listens quietly to the music or the news. Afterwards, they talk about what they heard. The little kitchen becomes like a small theatre. Rosa makes coffee for everyone, and Nonna brings out a plate of biscuits.

[pause]

In 1925, many radio stations are new and small. Some of them broadcast from hotels and department stores, from little rooms with heavy curtains to keep out the noise. The singers and musicians stand in front of a big microphone. They cannot see their listeners. But they know that thousands of families are listening, in kitchens and living rooms all over the city. Lucy dreams of standing in front of that microphone one day.

[long-pause]

{img:harlem-1925-lenox-avenue-evening-crowds-well-dressed-churches-music}
In the north of Manhattan, there is a neighbourhood called Harlem. In 1925, it is one of the most exciting places in the world.

[pause]

In the years before, many African American families moved to Harlem from the southern states of America. They came looking for work and a better life, away from the unfair laws of the South. By 1925, Harlem is the biggest African American community in the country. It is full of churches and shops, theatres and clubs, newspapers and schools. And it is full of music, art and poetry.

[long-pause]

This time in Harlem is called the Harlem Renaissance. Renaissance means rebirth. Writers, poets, musicians and artists are creating wonderful new work. A young poet called Langston Hughes writes poems about the life of ordinary people, with the rhythm of jazz and the blues. A writer called Zora Neale Hurston writes stories about the South. Painters paint the streets and the people of Harlem. Everyone is talking about it.

[pause]

{img:jazz-band-1925-club-stage-trumpet-piano-drums-dancers-warm-light}
Above all, Harlem is famous for jazz. Jazz is a new kind of music, which began in New Orleans, in the south. It is lively and free, full of rhythm and feeling. The musicians often make it up as they play. In the clubs and dance halls of Harlem, the best jazz musicians in the world are playing. There is a young trumpet player from New Orleans called Louis Armstrong, who has just come to New York. And there is a young pianist and bandleader called Duke Ellington.

[long-pause]

Lucy has never been to a jazz club. She is far too young, and her mother would never allow it. But she hears jazz on the radio, and she loves it. And one Sunday, something special happens. Her friend Ruby, from school, invites her to come to her church in Harlem. Ruby's family moved to Harlem from North Carolina, a few years ago. Lucy asks her mother, and Rosa says yes.

[pause]

{img:harlem-church-gospel-choir-singing-congregation-sunday-morning-joyful}
The church is big and bright, with coloured windows. It is full of people in their Sunday best: men in suits, women in beautiful hats, children with ribbons in their hair. And when the choir begins to sing, Lucy is amazed. She has never heard singing like it. The voices are strong and warm and full of joy. The people clap and sway and sing along. The music fills the whole church, and Lucy's heart.

[long-pause]

After church, Ruby's family invites Lucy to their apartment for lunch. Ruby's mother has made fried chicken, cornbread and greens. It is the first time Lucy has eaten food like this, and she loves it. After lunch, Ruby's older brother plays the piano, in their little living room. He plays jazz, fast and happy, and then slow and sad. Ruby and Lucy sing along. Ruby's grandmother taps her foot and smiles.

[pause]

{img:two-girls-singing-by-piano-apartment-harlem-afternoon-family-listening}
On the way home, on the subway, Lucy cannot stop smiling. She thinks about the singing in the church, and the music on the piano. She tells her mother all about it at dinner. Nonna listens, and nods. Music, she says in Italian, is the same everywhere. It comes from the heart. Lucy thinks that Nonna is right. And she decides that whatever happens, she will always sing.

[pause]

Harlem also has wonderful theatres, where musicians and dancers perform. Some of the best tap dancers in the world are dancing there. A tap dancer has little metal plates on the bottoms of his shoes. When he dances, his feet make quick clicking rhythms on the stage, like a drum. A famous tap dancer called Bill Robinson can dance up and down a staircase, tapping out a rhythm on every step.

[pause]

Harlem also has beautiful old houses, called brownstones, made of brown stone, with wide steps leading up to the front doors. On warm evenings, families sit on these steps, talking and laughing. Children play in the streets. Music drifts from the open windows. And people walk along the wide avenues in their best clothes, greeting their friends. Harlem is a proud and beautiful place.

[long-pause]

{img:woolworth-building-1925-gothic-skyscraper-tower-lower-manhattan-blue-sky}
New York is famous for its skyscrapers. In 1925, there are already many tall buildings in Manhattan. And more are going up every year.

[pause]

A skyscraper is a very tall building, so tall that it seems to scrape the sky. Before the 1880s, buildings could not be very tall. Their walls had to be very thick to hold up all the floors. But then, builders learned to make a frame of steel, like a skeleton, to hold up the building. The walls could be thin, and the building could go up and up. And with the invention of the safe electric elevator, people could reach the top floors easily.

[long-pause]

In 1925, the tallest building in the world is the Woolworth Building, in lower Manhattan. It was finished in 1913. It is fifty-seven storeys high, with a beautiful tower decorated with carvings, like an old cathedral. People call it the Cathedral of Commerce. It was built by Frank Woolworth, who owned a chain of shops where everything cost five or ten cents. He paid for the whole building in cash.

[pause]

{img:ironworkers-1925-walking-steel-beams-high-above-city-rivets-sky}
Every year, new skyscrapers are rising in Manhattan. The men who build them are called ironworkers. They work high up on the steel frames, walking along narrow beams, hundreds of metres above the street. They have no safety ropes or nets. They need great balance and courage. Some of the best ironworkers are Mohawk men, from Native American communities in Canada and New York. They are famous for being calm and sure-footed at great heights.

[long-pause]

The ironworkers work in teams of four. One man heats the metal rivets in a little coal fire, until they glow red hot. He throws them with tongs to another man, who catches them in a metal cup. A third man puts the hot rivet into a hole in the steel beams. And the fourth man hammers it in with a noisy machine. The sound of the riveting hammers echoes all over the city, day after day.

[pause]

{img:ironworkers-lunch-break-sitting-on-beam-high-above-city-sandwiches-1925}
At lunchtime, the ironworkers sit on the steel beams, high above the city, and eat their sandwiches. They dangle their legs over the edge, as if they were sitting on a garden wall. They talk and laugh and look out over the city. From up there, they can see the whole of Manhattan, and the rivers, and the harbour, and the ocean beyond. People in the street below look up at them in amazement.

[long-pause]

Sal sometimes drives past the building sites in his taxi. He slows down to look up at the ironworkers on the steel frames. He shakes his head. Not for all the money in the world, he says. He prefers to keep his feet on the ground, in his taxi. But Tony thinks the ironworkers are the bravest men in New York, after Babe Ruth.

[pause]

{img:new-york-skyline-1925-sunset-skyscrapers-silhouette-towers-golden-light}
In the next few years, even taller buildings will go up in New York. There will be the Chrysler Building, with its shining silver top like a sunburst. And there will be the Empire State Building, which will be the tallest building in the world for forty years. But in 1925, they have not been built yet. People can only dream of them. And every evening, as the sun sets, the towers of Manhattan glow gold against the sky, and the city seems to reach up toward the stars.

[pause]

Tall buildings in New York must follow a new rule, made in 1916. As a building gets taller, it must get narrower, stepping back like a wedding cake. That way, sunlight can still reach the streets below. So the new skyscrapers of the 1920s have a special shape, with steps and terraces getting smaller toward the top. People call them wedding cake buildings.

[pause]

Inside the skyscrapers, there are thousands of offices. Men and women work there as clerks and typists, secretaries and managers. They ride up and down in fast elevators, operated by elevator boys in smart uniforms. At lunchtime, they pour out into the streets, to eat at the little lunch counters and cafeterias. And at five o'clock, they all go home at once, filling the subway and the streets.

[long-pause]

{img:coney-island-1925-beach-crowded-umbrellas-boardwalk-amusement-park-summer}
In summer, New York is very hot. And when it is hot, half the city goes to Coney Island.

[pause]

Coney Island is a long sandy beach at the southern end of Brooklyn, by the Atlantic Ocean. You can get there by subway, for just a nickel. On hot summer Sundays, hundreds of thousands of people go there. The beach is so crowded that you can hardly see the sand. There are umbrellas and blankets, picnic baskets and children, everywhere you look.

[long-pause]

Along the beach, there is a long wooden walkway, called the boardwalk. It was finished just a few years before, in 1923. People stroll along it, in the sea breeze, eating ice cream and hot dogs. Behind the boardwalk, there are amusement parks, full of rides and games and shows. There are roller coasters and merry-go-rounds, Ferris wheels and haunted houses, shooting galleries and fortune tellers. At night, it is all lit up with thousands of electric lights.

[pause]

{img:nathans-hot-dog-stand-1925-coney-island-crowd-counter-summer-sun}
Coney Island is famous for its hot dogs. A Polish immigrant called Nathan Handwerker opened a hot dog stand there, a few years before. He sold hot dogs for just five cents each, half the price of anyone else. Soon, his stand was the most famous in Coney Island. People queued for his hot dogs, and they still do today.

[long-pause]

On a hot Sunday in July, the family goes to Coney Island. They take the subway, which is packed with people carrying towels and picnic baskets. Nonna has made a big picnic: bread, cheese, salami, peaches and a cake. When they arrive, they find a little space on the crowded beach, and spread out their blanket. The sun is hot, and the sea sparkles blue.

[pause]

{img:family-picnic-crowded-beach-blanket-grandmother-umbrella-children-sea}
The children run straight into the sea. The water is cool and wonderful after the hot city. Lucy and Tony jump in the waves and splash each other. Sal comes in too, and carries Tony on his shoulders into the deeper water. Rosa paddles at the edge, holding up her skirt. Nonna stays on the blanket, under a big umbrella, in her black dress. She does not swim. But she watches them and smiles.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, Sal takes the children to the amusement park. They ride on the merry-go-round, on painted wooden horses that go up and down. They try the shooting gallery, and Tony wins a little toy. They eat hot dogs from Nathan's stand, and cotton candy, pink and sweet. And then, bravely, Lucy and Tony go on the roller coaster with their father. They scream all the way down, and laugh all the way back up.

[pause]

{img:coney-island-night-lights-luna-park-ferris-wheel-glowing-crowds-boardwalk}
As the sun goes down, the lights of the amusement parks come on, thousands and thousands of them. The Ferris wheel glows against the evening sky. Music plays from the merry-go-rounds. The family walks slowly along the boardwalk, tired and happy and sunburned, eating ice cream. Then they take the crowded subway home. Tony falls asleep on his father's lap, still holding his little prize. And Lucy looks out of the window at the lights of the city, and hums a song she heard on the boardwalk.

[pause]

On the beach, there are lifeguards in red swimsuits, watching the swimmers from tall wooden chairs. There are men selling ice cream and cold drinks from boxes slung around their necks. There are photographers who take pictures of families on the sand. Rosa pays for a photograph of the whole family, on the beach, with the sea behind them. It costs a lot. But it is the first photograph ever taken of all of them together.

[pause]

There is also a big aquarium at Coney Island, and sideshows with strange and wonderful acts. There are fire eaters and sword swallowers, strong men and acrobats. There is a man who can walk on broken glass, and a woman who can charm snakes. The children stare with their mouths open. Nonna says it is all nonsense, but she watches too, with great interest.

[long-pause]

{img:yankee-stadium-1925-crowd-baseball-game-sunny-afternoon-field-bleachers}
In 1925, the most famous man in New York is not a president or a businessman. He is a baseball player. His name is Babe Ruth.

[pause]

Babe Ruth plays for the New York Yankees. He is big and round, with a wide grin and a big appetite. He is the greatest hitter in the history of baseball. He hits the ball further and higher than anyone has ever done before. When he hits a home run, the ball flies right out of the stadium, and the crowd goes wild.

[long-pause]

Babe Ruth grew up in Baltimore, in a home for boys who needed looking after. There, a kind teacher taught him to play baseball. He became a famous player very quickly. In 1920, the Yankees bought him from another team. And in 1923, they built a huge new stadium in the Bronx, called Yankee Stadium. People called it the House that Ruth Built, because so many people came to see him play.

[pause]

{img:babe-ruth-style-big-baseball-player-swinging-bat-stadium-crowd-cheering}
Babe Ruth is loved by everyone, but especially by children. He visits hospitals and orphanages, and signs autographs for every child who asks. He jokes and laughs and eats enormous meals. In 1925, he was ill for a while at the beginning of the season, and missed many games. The whole city worried about him. But he came back. And the children of New York cheered louder than ever.

[long-pause]

Tony has never seen Babe Ruth play. Tickets cost money, and Yankee Stadium is far away, in the Bronx. But one Saturday in August, Sal says he has a surprise. A passenger in his taxi, a kind old businessman, gave him two tickets for the Yankees game. Tony cannot believe it. He jumps up and down around the kitchen, shouting. Nonna tells him to be quiet. But she is smiling too.

[pause]

{img:father-son-subway-to-bronx-baseball-caps-excited-crowd-fans-1925}
On Saturday, Sal and Tony take the subway to the Bronx. The train is full of baseball fans, wearing their caps and talking about the game. When they come out of the station, there is the stadium: huge and white, with flags flying from the top. They walk in with the crowd, and climb up to their seats, high in the stands. From up there, they can see the whole green field below them, bright in the sunshine.

[long-pause]

The game is exciting. The Yankees are playing against Boston. Tony watches every pitch, every hit, every run. He eats a hot dog and drinks a bottle of soda. And then, in the seventh inning, Babe Ruth comes up to bat. The whole stadium goes quiet. The pitcher throws. Babe Ruth swings. There is a loud crack. And the ball flies up, up, up into the blue sky, over the fence, and into the crowd. A home run!

[pause]

{img:boy-cheering-wildly-stands-home-run-crowd-standing-hats-flying-stadium}
The crowd jumps up and roars. Hats fly into the air. Tony jumps up and down and screams until his voice is hoarse. Sal laughs and hugs him. Babe Ruth trots slowly around the bases, waving his cap to the crowd. On the way home, Tony cannot stop talking about it. That night, he puts his Babe Ruth card under his pillow. And he dreams of baseballs, flying up into the sky, forever.

[pause]

Baseball is called America's pastime. Almost every American boy, and many girls, play it. In New York, there are three big teams: the Yankees, the Giants and the Brooklyn Robins, later called the Dodgers. Each team has its own fans, who argue about which team is best. On summer afternoons, you can hear baseball games on radios from open windows all over the city.

[pause]

Tony keeps the ticket from the game in a little box under his bed, with his Babe Ruth card. He takes it out sometimes and looks at it, remembering the crack of the bat and the ball flying into the sky. He tells everyone about it: his friends, his teacher, the customers on his corner. Sammy, his friend, is very jealous. But he admits that it must have been wonderful.

[long-pause]

{img:children-playing-stickball-street-1925-broomstick-ball-tenements-cheering}
What is life like for children in New York in 1925?

[pause]

Most children go to school, from about six years old until fourteen. The schools are big and crowded, with forty or fifty children in a class. Many of the children are the sons and daughters of immigrants. At home, they speak Italian or Yiddish, Polish or German. At school, they learn English, and to be American. Often, the children teach English to their parents and grandparents at home.

[long-pause]

After school, the streets are the children's playground. There are not many parks in the crowded neighbourhoods. So the children play in the streets, between the cars and the pushcarts. The boys play stickball, a street version of baseball. They use a broomstick for a bat, and a rubber ball. The bases are a lamp post, a fire hydrant and a parked car. When a car comes, everyone shouts, and runs to the side.

[pause]

{img:girls-jumping-rope-sidewalk-1925-singing-rhymes-tenement-stoop-friends}
The girls play hopscotch, drawing squares on the pavement with chalk. They skip with ropes, singing rhymes. They play jacks, a game with little metal stars and a small ball. They play with dolls on the stoops of the buildings. Everyone plays tag and hide and seek, running up and down the streets and in and out of the doorways.

[long-pause]

In summer, when it is very hot, the firemen sometimes open the fire hydrants in the street. Water shoots out in a great fountain. All the children of the street run through it, laughing and shrieking, in their clothes. It is the best way to cool down. The mothers watch from the windows and the fire escapes, and pretend to be cross. But they are smiling.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-fire-hydrant-spray-summer-street-laughing-wet-1925}
There are also places for children in the city. There are the public libraries, with rooms full of books for children. Lucy goes to the library every Saturday, and borrows three books. She reads them in the evenings, by the light of the kitchen lamp. There are settlement houses, community centres where children can take classes in music and art, sports and crafts. Lucy takes singing lessons at one, once a week, for free.

[long-pause]

There are also the cinemas. In 1925, the films are silent, in black and white. A piano player in the cinema plays music to go with the film. The words people say appear on cards on the screen. On Saturday afternoons, the children go to the matinee, the afternoon show, for a few cents. They watch funny films with Charlie Chaplin and Buster Keaton. They watch cowboy films and adventure films. They laugh and cheer and boo, all together.

[pause]

{img:children-cinema-1925-silent-film-screen-piano-player-laughing-audience}
In the evenings, the children sit on the stoop of their building with their friends. They tell stories and jokes, and talk about the films they have seen. They swap baseball cards and comic strips. The older girls talk about the latest songs and fashions. The mothers call down from the windows. And slowly, one by one, the children go in for the night, as the street lamps come on, and the city settles into the evening.

[pause]

Children also earn a little money doing jobs. Some sell newspapers, like Tony. Some shine shoes on the street corners. Some run errands for shopkeepers, or carry packages. Some help their mothers with sewing at home. The money they earn usually goes to their families, to help pay the rent and buy food. But sometimes they keep a penny or two, for candy or the cinema.

[pause]

On rainy days, the children play indoors, in the crowded apartments. They play cards and checkers and dominoes. They draw pictures and read comics. They listen to the radio. They play in the hallways and on the stairs, until the neighbours complain. And sometimes, they go up to the flat roof of the building, and play there under the grey sky, watching the rain fall on the city.

[long-pause]

{img:sled-dog-team-snow-blizzard-alaska-1925-musher-lantern-night-brave}
Let us go back to the beginning of 1925, to the story that everyone in New York followed: the race to Nome.

[pause]

Nome is a little town in Alaska, far in the north, near the Arctic. In January 1925, many children in Nome became very ill with a sickness called diphtheria. The doctor needed medicine to help them. But the nearest medicine was in a city more than a thousand kilometres away. It was the middle of winter. The sea was frozen. The planes of that time could not fly in such cold. The only way to bring the medicine was by dog sled.

[long-pause]

So a relay of dog sled teams was organised. About twenty mushers, as the sled drivers are called, with more than a hundred and fifty dogs, carried the medicine across the frozen land. Each team took it a part of the way, and then passed it on to the next team. They travelled day and night, through snow and wind and terrible cold. It was forty, fifty degrees below zero. Sometimes there were blizzards, and they could not see anything at all.

[pause]

{img:musher-handing-medicine-package-next-team-snowy-cabin-night-relay}
All over America, people followed the race in the newspapers and on the radio. Every day, Tony shouted the news on his corner. The children of New York read about the brave dogs and their mushers. Would they reach Nome in time? Everyone was hoping. One of the longest and hardest parts was run by a musher called Leonhard Seppala and his lead dog, Togo. They crossed a frozen sea, with the ice cracking around them.

[long-pause]

The last part of the journey was run by a musher called Gunnar Kaasen, with a lead dog called Balto. They travelled through a terrible blizzard, in the dark. Kaasen could not see the trail. But Balto could feel it under the snow, and led the team safely through. Early in the morning of the second of February, they arrived in Nome, with the medicine. The journey had taken less than six days. The children of Nome were saved.

[pause]

{img:balto-husky-lead-dog-arriving-nome-dawn-snow-townspeople-welcoming}
The whole country cheered. Balto and the other dogs became famous. And in December 1925, the city of New York put up a bronze statue of Balto, in Central Park. It shows a strong husky dog, standing proudly on a rock. Under it, there are words honouring all the dogs who carried the medicine through the snow. The words say: Endurance, Fidelity, Intelligence.

[long-pause]

On a cold Saturday in December, the family goes to Central Park to see the new statue. There is snow on the ground, and the trees are bare. There are lots of children there, admiring the bronze dog. Tony stands in front of it for a long time, reading the words. Lucy strokes its bronze ear. Nonna says that it is a beautiful dog, and that it looks like the dogs in her village in Italy.

[pause]

{img:children-touching-bronze-balto-statue-central-park-snow-winter-1925}
Children have been touching the statue of Balto ever since, for a hundred years. They have climbed on it and stroked its ears and sat on its back. In some places, the bronze has been rubbed shiny and smooth by thousands and thousands of little hands. The statue is still there today, in Central Park. And children still come to see it, and to hear the story of the brave dogs who ran through the snow.

[pause]

Later, people learned that Togo, Seppala's dog, had run the longest and hardest part of the race. Togo was a small dog, but very strong and brave. In recent years, people have honoured Togo too, with books and films and statues of his own. Both dogs, and all the dogs of the race, were heroes. And all of them are remembered today.

[pause]

After the race, Balto and his team went on a tour of America, to meet the people who had followed their story. Crowds came to see them in many cities. Later, Balto spent his last years in a zoo in Cleveland, where children could visit him. He was well looked after, and much loved. And his statue in Central Park reminds everyone of his courage, still today.

[long-pause]

{img:macys-thanksgiving-parade-1925-floats-marchers-crowds-broadway-cold-morning}
In November, there is a big holiday in America called Thanksgiving. It is a day for families to come together, eat a big meal, and give thanks for the good things in their lives.

[pause]

In New York, there is something special on Thanksgiving morning. It is a great parade, organised by Macy's, the biggest department store in the city. The parade began just the year before, in 1924. And in 1925, it is even bigger. People come from all over the city to watch it.

[long-pause]

The parade marches down Broadway, from the north of Manhattan to the big Macy's store at Herald Square. There are floats, big decorated wagons, with scenes from fairy tales and nursery rhymes. There are marching bands, playing loud cheerful music. There are clowns and acrobats, people in funny costumes, and people on stilts. And there are animals from the zoo: elephants and camels, bears and donkeys, walking slowly down the street.

[pause]

{img:parade-elephants-camels-broadway-1925-crowds-children-pointing-amazed}
At the end of the parade, there is Santa Claus himself, sitting on a big sleigh. When he reaches the Macy's store, he climbs up onto the front of the building. And that means the Christmas season has begun. The children cheer and wave, and shout out what they want for Christmas.

[long-pause]

The family goes to watch the parade early in the morning. It is cold, and they wrap up in their warmest coats and scarves. They find a place on the pavement, near the front, so the children can see. Sal lifts Tony onto his shoulders. Lucy stands at the front, beside her mother. Nonna stays at home, cooking. She says she has seen enough parades in her life, and someone must cook the dinner.

[pause]

{img:boy-on-fathers-shoulders-watching-parade-santa-sleigh-crowd-cheering}
When the elephants come past, Tony nearly falls off his father's shoulders with excitement. He has never seen an elephant before, except in pictures. They are huge and grey, with wrinkled skin and swaying trunks. They walk slowly and calmly down Broadway, as if they do it every day. Lucy loves the bands most of all. She claps along to the music, and hums the tunes all the way home.

[long-pause]

At home, Nonna has been cooking all morning. The family's Thanksgiving dinner is a mix of American and Italian. There is a roast turkey, which is the American tradition. But there is also a big dish of lasagne, and meatballs, and Nonna's special bread. There is cranberry sauce and sweet potatoes, and an apple pie that Rosa bought from the bakery. It is the biggest meal of the year.

[pause]

{img:thanksgiving-dinner-italian-american-family-turkey-lasagne-table-crowded-1925}
Before they eat, Sal asks everyone to say what they are thankful for. Tony says he is thankful for Babe Ruth's home run. Lucy says she is thankful for the radio and her singing lessons. Rosa says she is thankful that everyone is healthy. Sal says he is thankful for his family, and for America. And Nonna, in Italian, says she is thankful that she crossed the ocean, twenty years ago, and found such a good life for her son and his children. Everyone is quiet for a moment. Then Sal raises his glass, and they begin to eat.

[pause]

After dinner, the family is so full that nobody can move. Sal falls asleep in his chair, with his newspaper on his face. The children play cards on the floor. Rosa and Nonna wash up, and talk about the old days. Neighbours come by to wish them a happy Thanksgiving, and taste Nonna's lasagne. And the radio plays softly in the corner, all evening long.

[pause]

In the days before Thanksgiving, the whole neighbourhood is busy cooking. The bakeries are full of pies: apple and pumpkin and pecan. The butchers have turkeys hanging in their windows. The markets are full of cranberries and sweet potatoes and chestnuts. And in every kitchen, mothers and grandmothers are cooking, filling the stairways with delicious smells.

[pause]

Rosa also takes a plate of food to an old widow on the second floor, who lives alone. The old woman's children have moved away, to other cities. She is very happy to see Rosa, and invites her in for a cup of coffee. They sit together for a little while, talking. When Rosa leaves, the old woman holds her hand and thanks her. That, Rosa thinks, is what Thanksgiving is really about.

[long-pause]

{img:new-york-christmas-1925-fifth-avenue-shop-windows-snow-crowds-lights}
In December, New York gets ready for Christmas. And the city is beautiful in the snow.

[pause]

On Fifth Avenue, the big department stores have decorated their windows. There are moving toys and Christmas scenes, trains and dolls, little villages covered in fake snow. Crowds of people walk slowly past, looking at the windows. Children press their noses against the glass. Street sellers sell roasted chestnuts and hot pretzels. And from the churches, you can hear the sound of choirs singing carols.

[long-pause]

In Little Italy, the families prepare for Christmas in the Italian way. In the windows, there are little nativity scenes, with Mary and Joseph and the baby Jesus in the stable, and shepherds and animals. Nonna has a beautiful one, which she brought from Italy, with little figures of painted clay. She sets it up on the shelf in the kitchen, with candles around it. The children help her arrange the little sheep and shepherds.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-children-arranging-nativity-scene-clay-figures-kitchen-candles}
On Christmas Eve, Italian families have a big feast of fish. Nonna cooks for two days. There is fried fish and baked fish, fish soup and fish with pasta. There are clams and shrimps and salted cod. Then, at midnight, the whole family goes to church, to the old church in Little Italy. The church is full of candles and flowers, and the choir sings beautiful songs. Lucy sings along, in her clear sweet voice.

[long-pause]

One afternoon in the week before Christmas, Sal takes the children ice skating in Central Park. There is a big lake in the park, and when it freezes, the whole city comes to skate on it. There are hundreds of people on the ice: children and grown-ups, families and couples, good skaters and bad ones. Some of them glide gracefully, and some fall down, laughing.

[pause]

{img:ice-skating-central-park-lake-1925-crowds-snow-trees-city-buildings}
Lucy and Tony have never skated before. Sal rents skates for them from a little hut beside the lake. At first, they wobble and fall, again and again. Sal holds their hands and pulls them gently along. Slowly, they learn to stay up. By the end of the afternoon, Lucy can skate on her own, slowly and carefully. Tony still falls down a lot. But he laughs every time, and gets up again.

[long-pause]

On Christmas morning, the children wake up early and run into the kitchen. There are presents on the table, wrapped in brown paper and string. Tony gets a new baseball glove, real leather. Lucy gets a little book of songs, with the words and music of all the latest hits. Rosa gets a new pair of scissors for her sewing. Sal gets a warm scarf, knitted by Nonna. And Nonna gets a little framed photograph of the whole family, taken at a studio in the autumn.

[pause]

{img:family-christmas-morning-presents-kitchen-baseball-glove-songbook-happy-1925}
Nonna holds the photograph for a long time, looking at all the faces. Then she puts it on the shelf, next to the nativity scene and the old photograph of her husband. Twenty years ago, she says, I came here with nothing. Now look. She wipes her eyes with her apron. The children hug her. And outside, the snow begins to fall softly on the busy streets of New York.

[pause]

On New Year's Eve, the family goes to Times Square at midnight, as many New Yorkers do. The square is packed with people, wearing hats and blowing horns. At midnight, a great ball of lights drops slowly down a pole on top of a building. Everyone counts down together. Then everyone cheers and hugs and wishes each other a happy new year. Lucy and Tony have never stayed up so late in their lives.

[pause]

On Christmas Day, the family visits relatives in Brooklyn. They take the subway, carrying presents and a big tray of Nonna's biscuits. The relatives' apartment is full of people: aunts and uncles, cousins and grandparents. Everyone talks at once, in Italian and English. The children play together, and the grown-ups eat and drink and sing old songs. It is noisy and crowded and full of love.

[long-pause]

{img:empire-state-building-construction-1930-steel-frame-rising-skyline-later}
The Roaring Twenties did not last forever. In October 1929, four years after our story, something happened that changed everything.

[pause]

On Wall Street, in the south of Manhattan, people buy and sell shares in companies. In the 1920s, many people bought shares, hoping to get rich quickly. Prices went up and up. But in October 1929, prices suddenly fell, very fast. Many people lost their savings. Banks closed. Factories shut down. Millions of people lost their jobs. This was the beginning of a hard time called the Great Depression. It lasted for about ten years.

[long-pause]

Those were difficult years for many families in New York. We remember them gently. Many people had little money and little food. But people helped each other. Neighbours shared what they had. Families moved in together. Churches and charities gave out soup and bread. And slowly, over the years, things got better again.

[pause]

{img:people-sharing-soup-kitchen-1930s-queue-coats-hats-kindness-later}
Even in those hard years, New York kept building. The Chrysler Building was finished in 1930, with its shining silver tower. The Empire State Building was finished in 1931, after only about a year of building. It was the tallest building in the world for forty years. Thousands of men worked on it, including many ironworkers, high up on the steel beams. It gave people work, and hope.

[long-pause]

The music of the twenties lived on, and grew. Jazz became more and more popular, all over the world. The big bands of the 1930s played swing music, and people danced in ballrooms everywhere. Louis Armstrong and Duke Ellington became some of the most famous musicians in the world. And the poems and stories of the Harlem Renaissance are still read and loved today.

[pause]

{img:big-band-swing-1930s-ballroom-dancers-orchestra-later-joy}
What about the family in our story? We can only imagine. Perhaps Sal kept driving his taxi through the hard years, and later bought his own car. Perhaps Rosa finally opened her little dress shop in Little Italy, with her name on the window. Perhaps Tony grew up and played baseball, or became a newspaper reporter. And perhaps Nonna lived to see her great-grandchildren, born in America.

[long-pause]

And perhaps Lucy became a singer. Perhaps she sang on the radio, as she dreamed, or in the clubs of New York. Or perhaps she became a music teacher, and taught the children of the neighbourhood to sing. And perhaps, when she was an old woman, she sat by the window of her apartment, with her grandchildren around her. She told them about the radio in the kitchen, and the singing church in Harlem, and the elephants in the Thanksgiving parade.

[pause]

{img:old-woman-lucy-singing-to-grandchildren-apartment-window-new-york-later}
She told them about the year 1925, when she was twelve years old, and New York was roaring. When the city was full of music and lights, and everything seemed new and possible. And she sang them an old song from the twenties, softly, as the lights of the city came on outside the window.

[pause]

New York also kept welcoming new people from all over the world. After the Second World War, new immigrants came from Puerto Rico and the Caribbean, from Asia and Latin America, from Africa and the Middle East. They brought their own foods and music and languages. And New York became even more mixed and lively than in 1925.

[pause]

Little Italy changed too. Over the years, many of the old Italian families moved away, to Brooklyn and Queens and the suburbs, where they could have bigger homes and gardens. Chinatown grew bigger, right next door. But the Italian families never forgot the old neighbourhood. They still come back for the feast days, to eat in the old restaurants, and to remember where their families began.

[long-pause]

{img:new-york-public-library-1925-fifth-avenue-marble-lions-steps-people}
On Fifth Avenue, in the middle of Manhattan, there is a great white marble building. It is the New York Public Library. And for Lucy, it is one of the most wonderful places in the whole city.

[pause]

The library was opened in 1911, fourteen years before our story. It is huge and beautiful, built like a palace. In front of it, at the top of a wide flight of steps, there are two big stone lions, one on each side. They look calm and wise and patient. Later, they will be given names: Patience and Fortitude. Children love to climb on them and sit between their paws.

[long-pause]

Inside, there are marble halls and wide staircases. And on the top floor, there is the great reading room. It is one of the biggest rooms in the world, almost as long as a football field. It has a high painted ceiling, with clouds and sky. There are tall windows, and long wooden tables with little brass lamps. Hundreds of people sit there quietly, reading and writing. Anyone can come in, and it is free.

[pause]

{img:rose-main-reading-room-long-tables-brass-lamps-readers-painted-ceiling-1925}
In 1925, the library is a place for everyone. Rich people and poor people, old people and young people, immigrants and Americans born in the city. People come to read the newspapers, to study for exams, to learn English, to find out about the world. Writers come to write their books. Students come to study. And many immigrants, who had no chance to go to school in their old countries, come to teach themselves.

[long-pause]

There are also many smaller libraries, in every neighbourhood of the city. There is one near Little Italy, in a little brick building, with a children's room on the ground floor. That is where Lucy goes every Saturday. The children's librarian is a kind young woman called Miss Moore, who knows every book on the shelves. She always has a new book for Lucy, one that she thinks Lucy will like.

[pause]

{img:childrens-library-room-1925-small-chairs-shelves-librarian-girl-choosing-book}
Lucy reads everything. She reads fairy tales and adventure stories, books about faraway countries, and books about famous singers. She reads the books in English, and sometimes reads them aloud to Nonna, translating them into Italian as she goes. Nonna loves this. She sits in her chair by the window and listens, and asks questions, and laughs at the funny parts.

[long-pause]

One Saturday, Lucy goes with her mother to the great library on Fifth Avenue. Rosa has never been inside before. They climb the steps between the stone lions, and walk through the great doors. Rosa looks around at the marble halls with wide eyes. They go up to the great reading room. Rosa stands in the doorway, looking at the long tables and the painted ceiling and the hundreds of quiet readers. She says nothing for a long time.

[pause]

{img:mother-daughter-standing-doorway-great-reading-room-awe-1925}
Then she whispers to Lucy: all of this, for everyone? Free? Lucy nods. Rosa shakes her head slowly. In the village where my parents came from, she says, there was not one single book. Not one. And here, there are millions. For anyone who wants them. On the way home, Rosa is quiet, thinking. And that evening, she asks Lucy to teach her to read a little better, in English.

[pause]

The library also has special collections of old and rare things. There are old maps and letters, ancient books and manuscripts. There is even a copy of one of the first printed Bibles, from more than four hundred years before. Scholars come from all over the world to study them. But the library is also for ordinary people, like Lucy and Rosa. And that, Rosa says, is the most wonderful thing about it.

[pause]

Rosa learns quickly. In the evenings, after the dishes are done, she sits with Lucy at the kitchen table, with a simple book. Lucy points to the words, and Rosa reads them slowly, aloud. Nonna listens and nods. Sal says he is proud of both of them. By the spring, Rosa can read the newspaper on her own. And on Saturdays, she goes to the library with Lucy, to borrow books of her own.

[long-pause]

{img:new-york-today-skyline-skyscrapers-empire-state-statue-liberty-harbour-today}
Today, New York is still one of the most exciting cities in the world. More than eight million people live there, from every country on Earth.

[pause]

The skyline of Manhattan is now full of skyscrapers, much taller than in 1925. But the old ones are still there too. The Woolworth Building still stands in lower Manhattan, with its beautiful tower. The Empire State Building and the Chrysler Building still rise above Midtown. And the Statue of Liberty still holds up her torch in the harbour, welcoming ships and visitors from all over the world.

[long-pause]

Ellis Island is now a museum. Visitors can walk through the great hall, where millions of immigrants once waited. They can see the old photographs and suitcases, and read the stories of the people who came. Many Americans come there to look for the names of their grandparents and great-grandparents, in the old lists of passengers. About forty percent of all Americans have an ancestor who came through Ellis Island.

[pause]

{img:ellis-island-museum-today-great-hall-visitors-old-suitcases-display-today}
Little Italy is much smaller today than in 1925. Many Italian families moved to other parts of the city, or out to the suburbs. But there are still Italian restaurants and cafés on Mulberry Street, with tables on the pavement. And every September, there is a big festival in Little Italy, for San Gennaro, the patron saint of Naples. The street is decorated with lights, and there is music and food and dancing.

[long-pause]

Coney Island is still there, with its beach and its boardwalk and its rides. Nathan's hot dog stand is still there too. Every summer, crowds of New Yorkers go there on hot days, as they did in 1925. The old wooden roller coaster called the Cyclone, built in 1927, is still running. People still scream all the way down, and laugh all the way back up.

[pause]

{img:coney-island-today-boardwalk-wonder-wheel-cyclone-beach-crowds-summer-today}
In Central Park, children still skate on the ice in winter, and still climb on the bronze statue of Balto. Yankee Stadium has been rebuilt, but the Yankees still play there, and the crowds still cheer. The great public library on Fifth Avenue still welcomes everyone, and the stone lions still guard its steps. And the Macy's parade still marches through the streets every Thanksgiving, with giant balloons now, floating high above the crowds.

[long-pause]

Harlem is still a lively neighbourhood, full of music and history. The famous Apollo Theater, which opened a few years after our story, still has shows and concerts. Jazz clubs still play in the evenings. On Sunday mornings, the churches are still full of beautiful singing, and visitors come from all over the world to hear it.

[pause]

{img:harlem-today-apollo-theater-marquee-street-evening-people-lights-today}
And the yellow taxis are still everywhere, honking their horns in the busy streets. The subway still rattles under the city, day and night. The newspapers are now on phones and computers, but people still read the news on their way to work. New York has changed in a hundred ways. But it is still a city of immigrants and dreamers, of music and energy. And, people say, it still never sleeps.

[pause]

The Lower East Side, where so many immigrants lived, still has some of the old tenement buildings. One of them has been turned into a museum. Inside, the little apartments have been arranged just as they were, a hundred years ago. You can see the kitchens with their coal stoves, the tiny bedrooms, the tin bathtubs. Guides tell the stories of the real families who lived there. It feels as if they have just stepped out for a moment.

[pause]

And every evening, as in 1925, the lights of New York come on, one by one, as the sun goes down. The skyscrapers glow against the evening sky. The bridges are outlined with lights. The ferries cross the harbour, with their windows shining. And the Statue of Liberty stands in the harbour with her torch held high, as she did when Nonna first saw her, long ago, through the morning mist.

[long-pause]

{img:new-york-1925-evening-dusk-skyline-lights-coming-on-little-italy-street}
So we leave New York tonight, in the year 1925, as the evening falls.

[pause]

The sun is setting behind the tall buildings of Manhattan. The sky turns orange, then pink, then a deep soft blue. One by one, the lights of the city come on. Windows glow yellow in the skyscrapers. The electric signs of Times Square blaze into life. And down in the harbour, the Statue of Liberty stands quietly against the darkening sky, with her torch held high.

[long-pause]

In Little Italy, the street is busy with the evening. The pushcart sellers are packing up their carts. Mothers are calling their children in for dinner. Old men are sitting on the stoops, smoking their pipes and talking. Somewhere, someone is playing an accordion, an old song from Naples. And from the open windows, the smells of cooking drift out into the street: garlic and tomatoes, fresh bread and frying onions.

[pause]

{img:tenement-kitchen-evening-family-dinner-lamp-radio-glowing-1925-cozy}
In the family's little kitchen on the fourth floor, the lamp is lit. Nonna has made her special soup, with beans and pasta. Sal has just come home, and he is telling a funny story about a passenger who forgot his hat. Rosa is laughing. Tony is setting the table, with his Babe Ruth card sticking out of his shirt pocket. And Lucy is turning the dial of the radio, looking for music.

[long-pause]

After dinner, the radio plays softly. A dance band is playing a slow, sweet song. Lucy sings along, quietly. Sal puts his arm around Rosa, and they sway a little to the music, by the stove. Nonna sits in her chair by the window, with her rosary in her hands, looking out at the lights of the city. Tony is lying on the floor, half asleep, with his head on a cushion.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-window-rosary-city-lights-night-girl-singing-radio-softly}
Later, Rosa sends the children to bed. Lucy lies in the bed she shares with Nonna, looking at the ceiling. Through the thin walls, she can hear the radio still playing in the kitchen, and her parents talking softly. She can hear the elevated train rattling past, and a car honking far away. She can hear a neighbour singing a lullaby to a baby. And she thinks about all the music in the world, and all the songs she will sing one day.

[long-pause]

Nonna comes to bed, slowly, and says her prayers in Italian, very softly. She says a prayer for each of her family, one by one. Then she lies down beside Lucy, and pats her hand.
