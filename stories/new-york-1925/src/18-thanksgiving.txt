In November, there is a big holiday in America called Thanksgiving. It is a day for families to come together, eat a big meal, and give thanks for the good things in their lives.

In New York, there is something special on Thanksgiving morning. It is a great parade, organised by Macy's, the biggest department store in the city. The parade began just the year before, in 1924. And in 1925, it is even bigger. People come from all over the city to watch it.

--

The parade marches down Broadway, from the north of Manhattan to the big Macy's store at Herald Square. There are floats, big decorated wagons, with scenes from fairy tales and nursery rhymes. There are marching bands, playing loud cheerful music. There are clowns and acrobats, people in funny costumes, and people on stilts. And there are animals from the zoo: elephants and camels, bears and donkeys, walking slowly down the street.

{img:parade-elephants-camels-broadway-1925-crowds-children-pointing-amazed}
At the end of the parade, there is Santa Claus himself, sitting on a big sleigh. When he reaches the Macy's store, he climbs up onto the front of the building. And that means the Christmas season has begun. The children cheer and wave, and shout out what they want for Christmas.

--

The family goes to watch the parade early in the morning. It is cold, and they wrap up in their warmest coats and scarves. They find a place on the pavement, near the front, so the children can see. Sal lifts Tony onto his shoulders. Lucy stands at the front, beside her mother. Nonna stays at home, cooking. She says she has seen enough parades in her life, and someone must cook the dinner.

{img:boy-on-fathers-shoulders-watching-parade-santa-sleigh-crowd-cheering}
When the elephants come past, Tony nearly falls off his father's shoulders with excitement. He has never seen an elephant before, except in pictures. They are huge and grey, with wrinkled skin and swaying trunks. They walk slowly and calmly down Broadway, as if they do it every day. Lucy loves the bands most of all. She claps along to the music, and hums the tunes all the way home.

--

At home, Nonna has been cooking all morning. The family's Thanksgiving dinner is a mix of American and Italian. There is a roast turkey, which is the American tradition. But there is also a big dish of lasagne, and meatballs, and Nonna's special bread. There is cranberry sauce and sweet potatoes, and an apple pie that Rosa bought from the bakery. It is the biggest meal of the year.

{img:thanksgiving-dinner-italian-american-family-turkey-lasagne-table-crowded-1925}
Before they eat, Sal asks everyone to say what they are thankful for. Tony says he is thankful for Babe Ruth's home run. Lucy says she is thankful for the radio and her singing lessons. Rosa says she is thankful that everyone is healthy. Sal says he is thankful for his family, and for America. And Nonna, in Italian, says she is thankful that she crossed the ocean, twenty years ago, and found such a good life for her son and his children. Everyone is quiet for a moment. Then Sal raises his glass, and they begin to eat.

After dinner, the family is so full that nobody can move. Sal falls asleep in his chair, with his newspaper on his face. The children play cards on the floor. Rosa and Nonna wash up, and talk about the old days. Neighbours come by to wish them a happy Thanksgiving, and taste Nonna's lasagne. And the radio plays softly in the corner, all evening long.

In the days before Thanksgiving, the whole neighbourhood is busy cooking. The bakeries are full of pies: apple and pumpkin and pecan. The butchers have turkeys hanging in their windows. The markets are full of cranberries and sweet potatoes and chestnuts. And in every kitchen, mothers and grandmothers are cooking, filling the stairways with delicious smells.

Rosa also takes a plate of food to an old widow on the second floor, who lives alone. The old woman's children have moved away, to other cities. She is very happy to see Rosa, and invites her in for a cup of coffee. They sit together for a little while, talking. When Rosa leaves, the old woman holds her hand and thanks her. That, Rosa thinks, is what Thanksgiving is really about.
