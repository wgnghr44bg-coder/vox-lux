On Fifth Avenue, in the middle of Manhattan, there is a great white marble building. It is the New York Public Library. And for Lucy, it is one of the most wonderful places in the whole city.

The library was opened in 1911, fourteen years before our story. It is huge and beautiful, built like a palace. In front of it, at the top of a wide flight of steps, there are two big stone lions, one on each side. They look calm and wise and patient. Later, they will be given names: Patience and Fortitude. Children love to climb on them and sit between their paws.

--

Inside, there are marble halls and wide staircases. And on the top floor, there is the great reading room. It is one of the biggest rooms in the world, almost as long as a football field. It has a high painted ceiling, with clouds and sky. There are tall windows, and long wooden tables with little brass lamps. Hundreds of people sit there quietly, reading and writing. Anyone can come in, and it is free.

{img:rose-main-reading-room-long-tables-brass-lamps-readers-painted-ceiling-1925}
In 1925, the library is a place for everyone. Rich people and poor people, old people and young people, immigrants and Americans born in the city. People come to read the newspapers, to study for exams, to learn English, to find out about the world. Writers come to write their books. Students come to study. And many immigrants, who had no chance to go to school in their old countries, come to teach themselves.

--

There are also many smaller libraries, in every neighbourhood of the city. There is one near Little Italy, in a little brick building, with a children's room on the ground floor. That is where Lucy goes every Saturday. The children's librarian is a kind young woman called Miss Moore, who knows every book on the shelves. She always has a new book for Lucy, one that she thinks Lucy will like.

{img:childrens-library-room-1925-small-chairs-shelves-librarian-girl-choosing-book}
Lucy reads everything. She reads fairy tales and adventure stories, books about faraway countries, and books about famous singers. She reads the books in English, and sometimes reads them aloud to Nonna, translating them into Italian as she goes. Nonna loves this. She sits in her chair by the window and listens, and asks questions, and laughs at the funny parts.

--

One Saturday, Lucy goes with her mother to the great library on Fifth Avenue. Rosa has never been inside before. They climb the steps between the stone lions, and walk through the great doors. Rosa looks around at the marble halls with wide eyes. They go up to the great reading room. Rosa stands in the doorway, looking at the long tables and the painted ceiling and the hundreds of quiet readers. She says nothing for a long time.

{img:mother-daughter-standing-doorway-great-reading-room-awe-1925}
Then she whispers to Lucy: all of this, for everyone? Free? Lucy nods. Rosa shakes her head slowly. In the village where my parents came from, she says, there was not one single book. Not one. And here, there are millions. For anyone who wants them. On the way home, Rosa is quiet, thinking. And that evening, she asks Lucy to teach her to read a little better, in English.

The library also has special collections of old and rare things. There are old maps and letters, ancient books and manuscripts. There is even a copy of one of the first printed Bibles, from more than four hundred years before. Scholars come from all over the world to study them. But the library is also for ordinary people, like Lucy and Rosa. And that, Rosa says, is the most wonderful thing about it.

Rosa learns quickly. In the evenings, after the dishes are done, she sits with Lucy at the kitchen table, with a simple book. Lucy points to the words, and Rosa reads them slowly, aloud. Nonna listens and nods. Sal says he is proud of both of them. By the spring, Rosa can read the newspaper on her own. And on Saturdays, she goes to the library with Lucy, to borrow books of her own.
