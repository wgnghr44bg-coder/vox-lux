Let us go back to the beginning of 1925, to the story that everyone in New York followed: the race to Nome.

Nome is a little town in Alaska, far in the north, near the Arctic. In January 1925, many children in Nome became very ill with a sickness called diphtheria. The doctor needed medicine to help them. But the nearest medicine was in a city more than a thousand kilometres away. It was the middle of winter. The sea was frozen. The planes of that time could not fly in such cold. The only way to bring the medicine was by dog sled.

--

So a relay of dog sled teams was organised. About twenty mushers, as the sled drivers are called, with more than a hundred and fifty dogs, carried the medicine across the frozen land. Each team took it a part of the way, and then passed it on to the next team. They travelled day and night, through snow and wind and terrible cold. It was forty, fifty degrees below zero. Sometimes there were blizzards, and they could not see anything at all.

{img:musher-handing-medicine-package-next-team-snowy-cabin-night-relay}
All over America, people followed the race in the newspapers and on the radio. Every day, Tony shouted the news on his corner. The children of New York read about the brave dogs and their mushers. Would they reach Nome in time? Everyone was hoping. One of the longest and hardest parts was run by a musher called Leonhard Seppala and his lead dog, Togo. They crossed a frozen sea, with the ice cracking around them.

--

The last part of the journey was run by a musher called Gunnar Kaasen, with a lead dog called Balto. They travelled through a terrible blizzard, in the dark. Kaasen could not see the trail. But Balto could feel it under the snow, and led the team safely through. Early in the morning of the second of February, they arrived in Nome, with the medicine. The journey had taken less than six days. The children of Nome were saved.

{img:balto-husky-lead-dog-arriving-nome-dawn-snow-townspeople-welcoming}
The whole country cheered. Balto and the other dogs became famous. And in December 1925, the city of New York put up a bronze statue of Balto, in Central Park. It shows a strong husky dog, standing proudly on a rock. Under it, there are words honouring all the dogs who carried the medicine through the snow. The words say: Endurance, Fidelity, Intelligence.

--

On a cold Saturday in December, the family goes to Central Park to see the new statue. There is snow on the ground, and the trees are bare. There are lots of children there, admiring the bronze dog. Tony stands in front of it for a long time, reading the words. Lucy strokes its bronze ear. Nonna says that it is a beautiful dog, and that it looks like the dogs in her village in Italy.

{img:children-touching-bronze-balto-statue-central-park-snow-winter-1925}
Children have been touching the statue of Balto ever since, for a hundred years. They have climbed on it and stroked its ears and sat on its back. In some places, the bronze has been rubbed shiny and smooth by thousands and thousands of little hands. The statue is still there today, in Central Park. And children still come to see it, and to hear the story of the brave dogs who ran through the snow.

Later, people learned that Togo, Seppala's dog, had run the longest and hardest part of the race. Togo was a small dog, but very strong and brave. In recent years, people have honoured Togo too, with books and films and statues of his own. Both dogs, and all the dogs of the race, were heroes. And all of them are remembered today.

After the race, Balto and his team went on a tour of America, to meet the people who had followed their story. Crowds came to see them in many cities. Later, Balto spent his last years in a zoo in Cleveland, where children could visit him. He was well looked after, and much loved. And his statue in Central Park reminds everyone of his courage, still today.
