What is life like for children in New York in 1925?

Most children go to school, from about six years old until fourteen. The schools are big and crowded, with forty or fifty children in a class. Many of the children are the sons and daughters of immigrants. At home, they speak Italian or Yiddish, Polish or German. At school, they learn English, and to be American. Often, the children teach English to their parents and grandparents at home.

--

After school, the streets are the children's playground. There are not many parks in the crowded neighbourhoods. So the children play in the streets, between the cars and the pushcarts. The boys play stickball, a street version of baseball. They use a broomstick for a bat, and a rubber ball. The bases are a lamp post, a fire hydrant and a parked car. When a car comes, everyone shouts, and runs to the side.

{img:girls-jumping-rope-sidewalk-1925-singing-rhymes-tenement-stoop-friends}
The girls play hopscotch, drawing squares on the pavement with chalk. They skip with ropes, singing rhymes. They play jacks, a game with little metal stars and a small ball. They play with dolls on the stoops of the buildings. Everyone plays tag and hide and seek, running up and down the streets and in and out of the doorways.

--

In summer, when it is very hot, the firemen sometimes open the fire hydrants in the street. Water shoots out in a great fountain. All the children of the street run through it, laughing and shrieking, in their clothes. It is the best way to cool down. The mothers watch from the windows and the fire escapes, and pretend to be cross. But they are smiling.

{img:children-playing-fire-hydrant-spray-summer-street-laughing-wet-1925}
There are also places for children in the city. There are the public libraries, with rooms full of books for children. Lucy goes to the library every Saturday, and borrows three books. She reads them in the evenings, by the light of the kitchen lamp. There are settlement houses, community centres where children can take classes in music and art, sports and crafts. Lucy takes singing lessons at one, once a week, for free.

--

There are also the cinemas. In 1925, the films are silent, in black and white. A piano player in the cinema plays music to go with the film. The words people say appear on cards on the screen. On Saturday afternoons, the children go to the matinee, the afternoon show, for a few cents. They watch funny films with Charlie Chaplin and Buster Keaton. They watch cowboy films and adventure films. They laugh and cheer and boo, all together.

{img:children-cinema-1925-silent-film-screen-piano-player-laughing-audience}
In the evenings, the children sit on the stoop of their building with their friends. They tell stories and jokes, and talk about the films they have seen. They swap baseball cards and comic strips. The older girls talk about the latest songs and fashions. The mothers call down from the windows. And slowly, one by one, the children go in for the night, as the street lamps come on, and the city settles into the evening.

Children also earn a little money doing jobs. Some sell newspapers, like Tony. Some shine shoes on the street corners. Some run errands for shopkeepers, or carry packages. Some help their mothers with sewing at home. The money they earn usually goes to their families, to help pay the rent and buy food. But sometimes they keep a penny or two, for candy or the cinema.

On rainy days, the children play indoors, in the crowded apartments. They play cards and checkers and dominoes. They draw pictures and read comics. They listen to the radio. They play in the hallways and on the stairs, until the neighbours complain. And sometimes, they go up to the flat roof of the building, and play there under the grey sky, watching the rain fall on the city.
