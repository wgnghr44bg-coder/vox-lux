The Roaring Twenties did not last forever. In October 1929, four years after our story, something happened that changed everything.

On Wall Street, in the south of Manhattan, people buy and sell shares in companies. In the 1920s, many people bought shares, hoping to get rich quickly. Prices went up and up. But in October 1929, prices suddenly fell, very fast. Many people lost their savings. Banks closed. Factories shut down. Millions of people lost their jobs. This was the beginning of a hard time called the Great Depression. It lasted for about ten years.

--

Those were difficult years for many families in New York. We remember them gently. Many people had little money and little food. But people helped each other. Neighbours shared what they had. Families moved in together. Churches and charities gave out soup and bread. And slowly, over the years, things got better again.

{img:people-sharing-soup-kitchen-1930s-queue-coats-hats-kindness-later}
Even in those hard years, New York kept building. The Chrysler Building was finished in 1930, with its shining silver tower. The Empire State Building was finished in 1931, after only about a year of building. It was the tallest building in the world for forty years. Thousands of men worked on it, including many ironworkers, high up on the steel beams. It gave people work, and hope.

--

The music of the twenties lived on, and grew. Jazz became more and more popular, all over the world. The big bands of the 1930s played swing music, and people danced in ballrooms everywhere. Louis Armstrong and Duke Ellington became some of the most famous musicians in the world. And the poems and stories of the Harlem Renaissance are still read and loved today.

{img:big-band-swing-1930s-ballroom-dancers-orchestra-later-joy}
What about the family in our story? We can only imagine. Perhaps Sal kept driving his taxi through the hard years, and later bought his own car. Perhaps Rosa finally opened her little dress shop in Little Italy, with her name on the window. Perhaps Tony grew up and played baseball, or became a newspaper reporter. And perhaps Nonna lived to see her great-grandchildren, born in America.

--

And perhaps Lucy became a singer. Perhaps she sang on the radio, as she dreamed, or in the clubs of New York. Or perhaps she became a music teacher, and taught the children of the neighbourhood to sing. And perhaps, when she was an old woman, she sat by the window of her apartment, with her grandchildren around her. She told them about the radio in the kitchen, and the singing church in Harlem, and the elephants in the Thanksgiving parade.

{img:old-woman-lucy-singing-to-grandchildren-apartment-window-new-york-later}
She told them about the year 1925, when she was twelve years old, and New York was roaring. When the city was full of music and lights, and everything seemed new and possible. And she sang them an old song from the twenties, softly, as the lights of the city came on outside the window.

New York also kept welcoming new people from all over the world. After the Second World War, new immigrants came from Puerto Rico and the Caribbean, from Asia and Latin America, from Africa and the Middle East. They brought their own foods and music and languages. And New York became even more mixed and lively than in 1925.

Little Italy changed too. Over the years, many of the old Italian families moved away, to Brooklyn and Queens and the suburbs, where they could have bigger homes and gardens. Chinatown grew bigger, right next door. But the Italian families never forgot the old neighbourhood. They still come back for the feast days, to eat in the old restaurants, and to remember where their families began.
