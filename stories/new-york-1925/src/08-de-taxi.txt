Sal drives a yellow taxi. In 1925, taxis are becoming very popular in New York. And many of them are yellow.

A few years before, a businessman called John Hertz started a taxi company in Chicago. He painted all his taxis bright yellow, so that people could see them easily from far away. The idea spread to New York. By 1925, there are thousands of taxis in the city, many of them yellow. They are big, square cars, with a tall roof, black leather seats and a little meter that counts the fare.

--

Sal does not own his taxi. He rents it from a taxi company, every day. In the morning, he picks it up from the garage, and in the evening, he brings it back. He pays the company a part of the money he earns. The rest is his. On good days, he earns quite a lot. On bad days, when it rains or when the streets are empty, he earns very little.

{img:taxi-driver-helping-lady-into-cab-fur-coat-hotel-entrance-doorman}
Sal knows the streets of New York like the back of his hand. He knows every shortcut, every one-way street, every traffic jam. He knows the best hotels and theatres and restaurants. He knows where the rich people live, and where the poor people live. He knows the docks and the railway stations, and the quickest way to get anywhere.

--

Every day, Sal meets all kinds of people in his taxi. Rich ladies in fur coats, going shopping on Fifth Avenue. Businessmen in suits, rushing to meetings on Wall Street. Tourists from other countries, looking out of the windows with wide eyes. Actors going to the theatres on Broadway. Sailors coming back from long voyages. Sometimes he even gets famous people. Once, he drove a famous actress to her theatre. She gave him a big tip, and he talked about it for weeks.

{img:taxi-interior-1925-driver-view-passenger-back-seat-times-square-lights}
Sal likes to talk to his passengers. He asks them where they are from and what they do. He tells them jokes, and gives them advice about the best restaurants in the city. Most passengers like him. Some of them ask for him by name, when they need a taxi. He gives them a little card with his name and the number of the taxi company.

--

Driving in New York is not easy. The streets are full of cars and trucks, trolleys and horse carts, people and bicycles. There are traffic lights now, at some of the big crossings, which are quite new. There are policemen in the middle of the street, directing traffic with white gloves. Everyone honks their horns. Sal stays calm. He says that a good taxi driver needs patience, a good map in his head, and a sense of humour.

{img:policeman-white-gloves-directing-traffic-1925-intersection-cars-crowd}
Sometimes, on Sundays, Sal is allowed to take the taxi out for the family. Then they all squeeze into it, even Nonna, and drive out of the city. They drive over the great Brooklyn Bridge, to the beach, or up along the Hudson River, to see the trees and the countryside. The children press their noses against the windows. Nonna holds on tightly and says her prayers. And Sal drives slowly and carefully, singing old Italian songs, all the way.

At lunchtime, Sal stops at a little lunch counter near the docks, where many taxi drivers eat. They sit on tall stools at a long counter, and eat sandwiches and soup and drink coffee. They talk about the traffic, the passengers and the baseball. They tell each other where the best fares are, and which streets are blocked. Then they go back to their taxis, and drive off into the busy city.

In the evenings, when the theatres let out, Sal often waits outside them for passengers. The crowds pour out onto the pavement, laughing and talking about the show. Women in long dresses and furs, men in black coats and top hats. They wave for taxis, and Sal is always there. These are the best fares of the day. Sometimes the passengers are so happy after the show that they sing all the way home.
