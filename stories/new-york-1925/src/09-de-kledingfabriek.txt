Rosa works in the garment district of New York. This is the part of the city where clothes are made. And in 1925, it is one of the biggest centres of dressmaking in the world.

The garment district is in Midtown Manhattan, around Seventh Avenue. There are dozens of tall buildings there, full of workshops. In the streets, men push racks of dresses and coats on little wheels, from one building to another. Trucks unload rolls of cloth. Buttons and ribbons and thread are sold in little shops. Everyone is busy and in a hurry.

--

Rosa works on the eighth floor of a big building. The workshop is a long room with big windows. There are rows of tables, with sewing machines on them. About fifty women work there, most of them Italian or Jewish. They sew dresses for the big department stores and fashion shops. The dresses are the latest fashion: short and loose, with dropped waists and beads and fringes.

{img:seamstress-woman-sewing-machine-beaded-dress-close-hands-concentration}
Rosa is a skilled seamstress. She sews the difficult parts: the collars and the sleeves, the fine seams and the beading. She works on an electric sewing machine, which makes a steady humming sound. She works fast and carefully, from seven in the morning until half past five in the evening, with a short break for lunch. It is tiring work, and her back and fingers ache by the end of the day.

--

Work in the garment district was once very hard and dangerous. In 1911, there was a terrible fire in a dress factory called the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory. Many young women lost their lives, because the doors were locked and there was no safe way out. It was a great sorrow for the city. After that, new laws were made to make the workshops safer. Now there must be fire escapes and unlocked doors. The workers also joined unions, to ask for better pay and shorter hours. Things are better now, in 1925, though work is still hard.

{img:women-workers-lunch-break-workshop-sandwiches-talking-laughing-1925}
At lunchtime, the women sit together at the end of the long tables and eat their sandwiches. They talk and laugh and share news. They talk about their children and their husbands, the prices in the shops, and the latest films. Some of them speak Italian, some Yiddish, some English. They teach each other words in their languages. Rosa has learned quite a lot of Yiddish from her friend Sarah, who sits next to her.

--

Sometimes, Rosa brings home leftover pieces of cloth from the workshop. They are too small to use for dresses, but big enough for other things. She uses them to make clothes for Lucy and Tony, or patches for Sal's trousers. Once, she found a beautiful piece of blue silk, with little silver beads on it. She used it to make a collar for Lucy's best dress. Lucy thinks it is the most beautiful thing she has ever worn.

{img:mother-sewing-at-home-kitchen-table-evening-lamp-daughter-trying-dress}
Rosa has a dream. One day, she would like to have her own little dress shop, in Little Italy, with her name on the window. She would make dresses for the women of the neighbourhood, for weddings and christenings and feast days. She has been saving a few cents every week, in a little tin box under the bed. It will take many years. But she keeps saving, and she keeps dreaming.

Some of the dresses Rosa sews are very beautiful, for rich ladies to wear to parties. They have glittering beads and long silk fringes that swing when the wearer dances. Rosa sometimes holds one up to the window and looks at it, shimmering in the light. She will never wear such a dress herself. But she is proud that her hands have made something so beautiful.

On the way home, Rosa walks with her friend Sarah to the subway station. They talk about their children and their plans. Sarah also dreams of having her own shop one day. They joke that they will open a shop together, with Italian and Jewish dresses, and all the women of New York will come. They laugh. But perhaps, Rosa thinks, it is not such a silly idea after all.
