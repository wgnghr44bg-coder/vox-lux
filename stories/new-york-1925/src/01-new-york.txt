Tonight we go back about a hundred years, to the year 1925. We go to the biggest city in America, and one of the most exciting cities in the world. It is New York.

--

It is early morning in spring, and a soft mist lies over the harbour. Out on the water, ferries are crossing back and forth, with little puffs of smoke rising from their chimneys. Tugboats push big ships slowly toward the piers. On a small island in the harbour, the Statue of Liberty holds up her torch, green against the pale sky. And beyond her, rising out of the mist, are the tall towers of Manhattan.

The sun comes up behind the buildings, and their windows begin to shine gold. On the streets below, the city is waking up. Milkmen are delivering bottles of milk from horse-drawn carts. Newsboys are shouting the morning headlines on the corners. Trains rattle along on the elevated tracks, high above the streets. And the smell of coffee and fresh bread drifts out of the little cafés and bakeries.

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

--

{img:busy-new-york-street-1925-model-t-cars-trolley-people-hats-shops}
New York in 1925 is a city full of energy and change. Almost six million people live here. They come from all over the world: from Italy and Ireland, from Germany and Russia, from Poland and China, and from all over America. The streets are full of cars, trucks, trolleys and horse carts. New buildings are going up everywhere, taller and taller. There is music in the air, and new ideas, and a feeling that anything is possible.

People call this time the Roaring Twenties. The great war ended a few years ago, and many people feel that life is good again. There are new inventions everywhere. Radios in the living room, telephones on the wall, cars in the street, and moving pictures in the cinemas. There is a new kind of music called jazz, and new dances like the Charleston. Young women cut their hair short and wear shorter dresses. Everything is new and fast and modern.

{img:italian-american-family-1925-tenement-window-little-italy-morning-fire-escape}
Tonight we will spend a year in New York with one ordinary family. They live in a small apartment in a neighbourhood called Little Italy. The father drives a yellow taxi through the busy streets. The mother sews dresses in a big workshop. Their daughter loves the radio and dreams of being a singer. Their son sells newspapers on the street corner. And their grandmother, who came from Italy long ago, still speaks almost no English.

--

We will ride the subway and the elevated trains. We will visit the bright lights of Times Square and the jazz clubs of Harlem. We will watch the great baseball player Babe Ruth at Yankee Stadium. We will spend a hot summer day at the beach of Coney Island. We will follow a brave sled dog called Balto. And we will see the city in the snow at Christmas, with ice skaters in Central Park.

{img:girl-boy-fire-escape-morning-looking-down-street-pushcarts-laundry-lines}
So let us begin in Little Italy, on a spring morning in 1925. On the iron fire escape outside a fourth-floor window, a girl of twelve is sitting with her knees pulled up. Her little brother is beside her, eating a piece of bread. Below them, the street is filling with people and pushcarts. Washing hangs on lines between the buildings. And somewhere, a radio is playing a happy new song, through an open window.

On the corners, the street sweepers are already at work, pushing their brooms along the gutters. A policeman in a blue uniform walks slowly along the pavement, swinging his baton and greeting the shopkeepers. A coal truck stops in front of a building, and a man pours coal down a chute into the cellar. And a flower seller sets up her stand at the entrance to the subway, with buckets of tulips and daffodils.

From the window of their apartment, the children can see the whole street. They can see the bakery on the corner, with its warm yellow light. They can see the old man who sells chestnuts, setting up his little cart. They can see the iceman, carrying a big block of ice on his shoulder. And they can see their father, far below, walking to the garage to fetch his taxi, with his cap on his head.
