In December, New York gets ready for Christmas. And the city is beautiful in the snow.

On Fifth Avenue, the big department stores have decorated their windows. There are moving toys and Christmas scenes, trains and dolls, little villages covered in fake snow. Crowds of people walk slowly past, looking at the windows. Children press their noses against the glass. Street sellers sell roasted chestnuts and hot pretzels. And from the churches, you can hear the sound of choirs singing carols.

--

In Little Italy, the families prepare for Christmas in the Italian way. In the windows, there are little nativity scenes, with Mary and Joseph and the baby Jesus in the stable, and shepherds and animals. Nonna has a beautiful one, which she brought from Italy, with little figures of painted clay. She sets it up on the shelf in the kitchen, with candles around it. The children help her arrange the little sheep and shepherds.

{img:grandmother-children-arranging-nativity-scene-clay-figures-kitchen-candles}
On Christmas Eve, Italian families have a big feast of fish. Nonna cooks for two days. There is fried fish and baked fish, fish soup and fish with pasta. There are clams and shrimps and salted cod. Then, at midnight, the whole family goes to church, to the old church in Little Italy. The church is full of candles and flowers, and the choir sings beautiful songs. Lucy sings along, in her clear sweet voice.

--

One afternoon in the week before Christmas, Sal takes the children ice skating in Central Park. There is a big lake in the park, and when it freezes, the whole city comes to skate on it. There are hundreds of people on the ice: children and grown-ups, families and couples, good skaters and bad ones. Some of them glide gracefully, and some fall down, laughing.

{img:ice-skating-central-park-lake-1925-crowds-snow-trees-city-buildings}
Lucy and Tony have never skated before. Sal rents skates for them from a little hut beside the lake. At first, they wobble and fall, again and again. Sal holds their hands and pulls them gently along. Slowly, they learn to stay up. By the end of the afternoon, Lucy can skate on her own, slowly and carefully. Tony still falls down a lot. But he laughs every time, and gets up again.

--

On Christmas morning, the children wake up early and run into the kitchen. There are presents on the table, wrapped in brown paper and string. Tony gets a new baseball glove, real leather. Lucy gets a little book of songs, with the words and music of all the latest hits. Rosa gets a new pair of scissors for her sewing. Sal gets a warm scarf, knitted by Nonna. And Nonna gets a little framed photograph of the whole family, taken at a studio in the autumn.

{img:family-christmas-morning-presents-kitchen-baseball-glove-songbook-happy-1925}
Nonna holds the photograph for a long time, looking at all the faces. Then she puts it on the shelf, next to the nativity scene and the old photograph of her husband. Twenty years ago, she says, I came here with nothing. Now look. She wipes her eyes with her apron. The children hug her. And outside, the snow begins to fall softly on the busy streets of New York.

On New Year's Eve, the family goes to Times Square at midnight, as many New Yorkers do. The square is packed with people, wearing hats and blowing horns. At midnight, a great ball of lights drops slowly down a pole on top of a building. Everyone counts down together. Then everyone cheers and hugs and wishes each other a happy new year. Lucy and Tony have never stayed up so late in their lives.

On Christmas Day, the family visits relatives in Brooklyn. They take the subway, carrying presents and a big tray of Nonna's biscuits. The relatives' apartment is full of people: aunts and uncles, cousins and grandparents. Everyone talks at once, in Italian and English. The children play together, and the grown-ups eat and drink and sing old songs. It is noisy and crowded and full of love.
