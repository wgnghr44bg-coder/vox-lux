Tonight we travel about two and a half thousand years back in time. We go to the south of Greece, to a green valley between high mountains. The year is around 480 before Christ. And in this valley lies one of the most famous cities of the ancient world. It is called Sparta.

[long-pause]

It is early morning, and the air is cool and fresh. Mist lies over the river in the bottom of the valley. To the west, a great wall of mountains rises up, with snow still on the highest peaks. The first sunlight touches the tops of the mountains and turns them pink. Below, in the valley, there are olive trees and fields of barley. There are small houses with roofs of red tiles. Smoke rises slowly from a few cooking fires.

[pause]

A cockerel crows somewhere, and a dog answers it. A woman comes out of a house with a clay jug, on her way to the well. A boy leads a few goats along a dusty path. And on a low hill, a small temple catches the morning light. It is quiet here, and very peaceful.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

Most people have heard of Sparta. They think of soldiers with red cloaks and bronze shields. They think of a hard, strict life, with no comfort and no fun. Some of that is true. The Spartans were famous soldiers, and their way of life was strict and simple. But there is much more to Sparta than soldiers.

[pause]

Sparta was also a place of families, of farms and of festivals. It was a place where girls ran races and sang in choirs. It was a place where women owned land and spoke their minds. It was a place of music and dancing, of temples and quiet olive groves. And most of the people who lived there were not Spartans at all. They were farmers who worked the land, whose lives were often hard.

[pause]

Tonight we will stay mostly at home, in the valley, with one Spartan family. We will meet a mother who runs her house and her farm with a firm hand. We will meet her daughter, who loves to run faster than anyone. We will meet her young son, who is about to leave home for the famous Spartan school. We will meet an old grandmother who remembers everything. And we will meet the farming family who work their land, and whose story matters too.

[long-pause]

We will visit the great common meals where the men eat together every evening. We will taste the famous black broth, which nobody outside Sparta liked very much. We will watch the summer festivals, with choirs of boys and girls singing in the sun. We will learn about the strange old laws of Sparta, and its two kings. And later, we will hear how news of a famous battle reached the quiet valley.

So let us begin beside the river Eurotas, on a spring morning long ago. The reeds are moving softly in the breeze. A pair of swans glides slowly along the water. The Spartans say that swans have always lived on this river. And far away, a bell rings softly on the neck of a goat. The day is just beginning in Sparta.

[pause]

In a small house near the river, an old woman is already sitting in her doorway. She has a basket of wool beside her and a spindle in her hand. She watches the sun come up over the hills, as she has done every morning for seventy years. A little boy comes and sits beside her, still sleepy. She puts her arm around him, and together they watch the light spread slowly over the valley.

[long-pause]

{img:laconia-landscape-green-valley-olive-groves-mountains-both-sides}
Sparta lies in a region called Laconia, in the south of the land of Greece. Laconia is a long valley, with mountains on both sides. To the west are the Taygetos mountains, high and steep and rocky. To the east is a lower range of hills. And down the middle of the valley runs the river Eurotas, flowing south to the sea.

[pause]

The valley is one of the richest farming lands in all of Greece. The soil is deep and dark, and there is plenty of water. Olive trees grow in long silver-green rows on the lower slopes. Barley and wheat grow in the flat fields near the river. There are vineyards for grapes and gardens full of figs and vegetables. In spring, the meadows are covered with red poppies and yellow flowers.

[long-pause]

The Taygetos mountains are a world of their own. Their lower slopes are covered with forests of oak and pine. Higher up, there are bare rocks and steep cliffs. In winter, snow covers the tops, and in spring it melts into clear streams. Shepherds take their flocks up into the mountains in summer, where the grass is fresh. Wild goats and deer live there, and sometimes bears and wolves.

[pause]

{img:taygetos-mountains-snow-peaks-shepherd-flock-pine-slopes-spring}
For the Spartans, the mountains are a kind of wall. They protect the valley from the west. There are only a few narrow passes through them, and those are easy to guard. To the north, more hills close off the valley. So Sparta is like a great green room, with mountains for walls and the sky for a roof.

[pause]

The weather in Laconia is warm for most of the year. Summers are long and hot and dry, and the cicadas sing all day in the trees. Winters are short and mild in the valley, with rain and cool winds. Up in the mountains, though, it can be very cold. On clear winter mornings, the Spartans can look up and see the white peaks shining against the blue sky.

[long-pause]

The river Eurotas is the heart of the valley. Its water is clear and cold, coming down from the hills. Tall reeds grow along its banks, and the Spartans use them for many things. They weave baskets and mats from them. They cut them for the roofs of sheds and barns. And young Spartans sleep on beds made from reeds that they cut themselves, by hand.

[pause]

{img:eurotas-riverbank-tall-reeds-young-people-cutting-reeds-summer}
On summer evenings, the river is a lovely place to be. People come down to the water to wash, and to let their animals drink. Children splash in the shallow places and catch little fish with their hands. Frogs croak in the reeds, and swallows skim low over the water. The light turns golden, and the mountains turn purple. And everything feels calm and slow.

[long-pause]

About forty kilometres south of Sparta, the river reaches the sea. There is a small harbour there, where fishing boats and trading ships come and go. But the Spartans themselves are not great sailors. They are people of the land. They prefer their valley, their fields and their mountains. Many Spartans live their whole lives without ever going to sea.

[pause]

{img:laconian-farmhouse-olive-press-courtyard-evening-mountains-behind}
In the valley, there are many small farms, each with its own house and barns. There are olive presses, where the olives are crushed to make oil. There are threshing floors, flat round places where the grain is separated from the straw. There are wells and little shrines to the gods of the fields. And there are paths that wind between them, worn smooth by thousands of feet and hooves over the years.

[pause]

This is the land that the Spartans love, and that they will do anything to keep. It is beautiful and rich, and it gives them everything they need. And now, let us go to the city itself, in the middle of the valley.

[pause]

In spring, the shepherds lead their flocks up the mountain paths. The sheep wear little bells, and the sound carries far across the slopes. The shepherds stay up there all summer, living in small stone huts. They make cheese from the sheep's milk and sleep under the stars. In autumn, they come down again, brown from the sun, with their flocks fat and healthy. Their families come out to meet them on the path, and there is a small feast.

[long-pause]

{img:sparta-five-villages-no-walls-acropolis-hill-temples-480bc-overview}
Sparta is not like other Greek cities. Most Greek cities have strong stone walls all around them. They have a great central square, with fine temples and grand buildings of marble. Athens, for example, is full of beautiful buildings. But Sparta is different.

[pause]

Sparta has no walls at all. The Spartans say they do not need them. Their soldiers are their walls, they say. And Sparta is not really one city. It is a group of five villages, standing close together in the valley. Each village has its own houses, gardens and little temples. Together, they make Sparta.

[long-pause]

The buildings of Sparta are quite simple and plain. Houses are made of mud brick on a base of stone, with roofs of red clay tiles. The doors are made of rough wood. There is very little decoration, because the Spartans think that fancy things make people soft. A famous old law says that Spartan houses should be built with only an axe and a saw. No fine tools, and no fine carving.

[pause]

{img:spartan-simple-house-street-mud-brick-tiled-roof-women-talking}
In the streets, people walk past each other and nod. Women talk at the wells and doorways. Children run and play between the houses. Old men sit on benches in the sun and watch everything. There are no big markets full of luxury goods, like in other cities. There are a few small stalls selling bread and vegetables, pots and simple tools. Life is simple here, and people like it that way.

[long-pause]

In the middle of the five villages, there is a low hill. On top of it stand the most important temples of Sparta. The greatest is the temple of Athena, the goddess of wisdom. Its walls are decorated with plates of bronze, and so the Spartans call it the House of Bronze. When the sun shines on it, the bronze glows warm and golden.

[pause]

{img:temple-athena-bronze-house-sparta-hill-sunlight-worshippers-steps}
Below the hill there is an open space called the agora, the meeting place. Here the Spartan citizens gather for their assembly. They stand together and vote on important matters by shouting. Whoever gets the loudest shout wins the vote. It is a noisy way of deciding things. But the Spartans are proud of it.

[pause]

There are other special places in Sparta too. There is a running track beside the river, where young people race each other. There is a theatre on a hillside, where choirs sing at the festivals. And there are many small shrines, to heroes and to gods, scattered among the houses.

[long-pause]

Beside the river, a little way from the houses, there is a very old sanctuary. It belongs to Artemis, the goddess of wild animals and of young people. People call her Artemis Orthia here. Spartan families come to this place to pray for their children. They leave small gifts at the altar. There are little lead figures of animals and soldiers and dancers, and small clay masks. Archaeologists later found thousands of these little gifts in the ground.

[pause]

{img:sanctuary-artemis-orthia-riverside-altar-small-offerings-lead-figures}
A visitor from Athens once said something interesting about Sparta. If Sparta were one day left empty, he said, people in the future would never believe how powerful it had been. There are so few grand buildings, and so little to see. He was right. Today, very little of old Sparta is left. But in its own time, this simple group of villages was one of the strongest places in the whole Greek world.

[pause]

In the evening, the five villages grow quiet. Lamps are lit in the houses, small flames in clay bowls of oil. The smell of cooking drifts along the streets. And the mountains watch over the valley, dark against the stars.

[pause]

There is a lovely custom in Sparta about old people. When an old man walks past, the young men stand up to show respect. They step aside to let him pass, and they listen when he speaks. The old people of Sparta are honoured more than in almost any other Greek city. A visitor once said that Sparta was the best place in the world to grow old.

[long-pause]

{img:spartan-mother-firm-kind-face-woollen-peplos-courtyard-household}
Let us meet a Spartan family who lives in one of the five villages.

[pause]

The mother is called Agiatis. She is about thirty-five years old, tall and strong, with dark hair tied back. She runs the house and the family's farm in the valley. She is calm and firm, and people listen when she speaks. Spartan women are known all over Greece for being confident and bold. Agiatis is no exception.

[pause]

Her husband is called Damon. He is a Spartan soldier, like all Spartan men. He does not eat at home most evenings. He eats with his group of men at the common meals, as the law requires. He spends many days training with the other soldiers. So, much of the time, Agiatis is the one in charge at home.

[long-pause]

They have two children. The daughter is called Kleo, and she is twelve years old. Kleo has long legs and quick feet, and she loves to run. She is the fastest girl in her village, and she knows it. She also loves to sing, and she sings in the girls' choir at the festivals. She is cheerful and a little bit proud.

[pause]

{img:spartan-girl-twelve-running-path-hair-flying-olive-trees-sunlight}
The son is called Nikandros, and he is seven years old. He is small for his age, with big brown eyes and a quiet voice. He likes to sit with his grandmother and listen to stories. He likes to watch the ants and the beetles in the garden. And very soon, he will leave home to begin the famous Spartan training. Everyone in the family is thinking about this, though nobody says much about it.

[long-pause]

The grandmother lives with them too. She is Damon's mother, and she is nearly seventy. Her name is Eupolia. Her back is bent, and her hands are knotted from years of spinning wool. But her mind is very sharp. She remembers the old days and the old songs. She knows every family in the village and every story about them. And she is not afraid to tell anyone what she thinks.

[pause]

{img:old-spartan-grandmother-spinning-wool-doorway-boy-listening-sunlight}
There is one more family that matters very much to this household. On the family's farm, out in the valley, there lives a farming family who work the land. The father is called Lykos. He and his wife and their children grow the barley and the olives. They give half of what they grow to Agiatis's family. They are not free to leave. In Sparta, people like them are called helots. We will learn more about them later. Their life is much harder than the Spartans' life.

[long-pause]

The house of the family is simple, but comfortable enough. There is a courtyard in the middle, with an old olive tree and a well. Around the courtyard there are a few rooms, with floors of beaten earth. There is a kitchen with a hearth, and a storeroom full of jars. There is a room with a loom, where the women weave. And there are sleeping rooms, with simple wooden beds and woollen blankets.

[pause]

{img:spartan-house-courtyard-olive-tree-well-loom-room-jars-evening}
In the evenings, Agiatis sits in the courtyard with her mother-in-law. They talk about the farm and the weather, the neighbours and the children. Kleo practises her songs, quietly, so she will not forget the words. Nikandros sits close to his grandmother and leans against her arm. Damon comes home late, after the common meal, and sits with them for a while. He asks Nikandros if he is ready. The boy nods, but he does not look up.

[pause]

This spring, everything in the family is about to change. Nikandros will leave home soon. And far away, beyond the mountains and the sea, a great king is gathering a huge army. But for now, the evening is quiet, and the stars are coming out over the courtyard.

[pause]

There is also a dog in the family, a big brown hunting dog called Argos. He belongs to Damon, and he follows him everywhere. But in the evenings, when Damon is at the common meal, Argos lies by the courtyard door. He puts his head on his paws and waits. When he hears his master's footsteps in the street, his tail begins to thump on the ground. Nikandros thinks this is the funniest thing in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:spartan-kitchen-hearth-clay-pots-barley-bread-morning-light}
Let us spend a little more time inside the family's house, on an ordinary morning.

[pause]

The day starts early, before the sun has come over the eastern hills. Agiatis is the first one up. She stirs the ashes in the hearth and adds a few dry sticks. Soon a small fire is crackling, and the kitchen fills with a soft orange light. She puts a clay pot of water on the fire to warm. Then she goes out to the well in the courtyard, to fill another jug.

[pause]

Breakfast in Sparta is simple. There is barley bread, dipped in a little wine mixed with water. There are a few figs or olives, and perhaps a piece of cheese. The family eats sitting on low stools in the kitchen, or outside in the courtyard when the weather is warm. Nobody eats very much in the morning. The main meal comes later in the day.

[long-pause]

The storeroom is one of the most important rooms in the house. It is cool and dark, with a floor of beaten earth. Along the walls stand big clay jars, almost as tall as Nikandros. Some are full of barley, and some are full of olive oil. Others hold wine, figs, cheese and dried meat. Agiatis knows exactly what is in every jar, and how much is left. She checks them every week.

[pause]

{img:spartan-storeroom-tall-clay-jars-olive-oil-barley-dim-light}
Most of what is in the storeroom comes from the family's farm in the valley. Lykos and his family bring it to the house after each harvest. They bring barley and wine in the autumn, olive oil in the winter, and figs in late summer. Agiatis writes nothing down, because most Spartans do not write much. But she keeps it all in her head, and she never forgets.

[long-pause]

The weaving room is where the women spend many hours. In the middle of the room stands a tall wooden loom. The threads hang down from a beam at the top, held straight by little clay weights at the bottom. Agiatis and her mother-in-law weave cloth for the family's clothes. They weave thick wool for cloaks and blankets. They weave finer cloth for tunics and dresses.

[pause]

{img:spartan-women-weaving-upright-loom-clay-weights-wool-afternoon}
In Sparta, the free women do not have to spin and weave all day, as women in other cities often do. Helot women and servants do much of that work. But many Spartan women still like to weave. Agiatis finds it calming. The steady movement of the shuttle back and forth helps her to think. And her mother-in-law hums old songs while she spins, the spindle turning and turning in her hand.

[long-pause]

Spartan clothes are simple and plain. Women wear a peplos, a long woollen dress pinned at the shoulders. It is open down one side, so they can move and run freely. People in other cities think this is rather shocking. They call Spartan women the thigh-showers, and they shake their heads. But the Spartan women do not care. They like to move freely, and they think the women of Athens are far too shut away.

[pause]

Men wear a short tunic and a cloak. The cloak of a Spartan soldier is dyed a deep red colour. It is the most famous piece of clothing in all of Greece. Children wear simple tunics, and they often go barefoot, even in winter. The Spartans believe that going barefoot makes your feet strong.

[pause]

{img:spartan-red-cloak-hanging-peg-wall-bronze-helmet-shelf-lamplight}
In the family's house, Damon's red cloak hangs on a wooden peg by the door. His bronze helmet sits on a shelf above it, polished and shining. His great round shield leans against the wall in the corner. Nikandros sometimes stands and looks at them for a long time. They seem very big and very heavy. He wonders if he will ever be strong enough to carry them.

[pause]

At night, the family sleeps on simple wooden beds with mattresses stuffed with straw. They pull woollen blankets over themselves when it is cold. The house is quiet and dark, except for a small lamp left burning in the courtyard. Outside, a dog barks once and then is quiet. And the whole village sleeps under the stars.

[pause]

In the corner of the courtyard, there is a small altar to the gods of the household. Every morning, Agiatis pours a few drops of wine onto it. She asks the gods to keep the family safe and healthy. The grandmother places a few olives or a piece of bread there, as a little gift. It only takes a moment. But it is an important part of every day in the house.

[long-pause]

{img:spartan-woman-walking-to-farm-valley-path-morning-olive-groves}
Let us follow Agiatis through one ordinary day in spring.

[pause]

After breakfast, she puts on her cloak and walks out to the family's farm in the valley. It is about an hour's walk, along a path between olive groves and fields. The morning air smells of earth and wild thyme. Birds are singing in the hedges, and bees are busy among the flowers. She walks quickly, with long steps, because she has much to do today.

[pause]

At the farm, Lykos is already at work in the barley field. He is pulling out weeds, bent over in the rows of green shoots. When he sees Agiatis coming, he straightens up and comes to meet her. They talk about the crops and the weather. The barley looks good this year, he says. If the rain comes at the right time, there will be a fine harvest.

[long-pause]

Agiatis walks around the farm with him. She looks at the olive trees, which are just beginning to flower. She looks at the vines, with their new green leaves. She checks the goats and the sheep in their pen. Lykos's wife comes out of the farmhouse with a bowl of water and some bread. Agiatis thanks her and drinks.

[pause]

{img:helot-farmer-barley-field-weeding-spartan-woman-talking-spring}
The two women do not talk very much. They come from very different worlds. Agiatis is free, and Lykos's family is not. Agiatis knows this, and so does Lykos's wife. It is something that lies between them, quiet and heavy, like a stone. But on this spring morning, they talk about the children and the goats. And for a little while, they are simply two mothers standing in the sun.

[long-pause]

By midday, Agiatis is back home in the village. She eats a simple lunch with her mother-in-law and the children. There is bread and cheese, some boiled beans with olive oil, and a few onions. Afterwards, the grandmother takes a short rest in the shade of the olive tree. The children go outside to play.

[pause]

{img:spartan-women-talking-at-well-village-noon-water-jugs-shade}
In the afternoon, Agiatis goes to visit a friend in the next street. The women of Sparta visit each other often. They sit in each other's courtyards and talk about everything. They talk about their farms, their husbands and their children. They talk about the news from the assembly, and the latest gossip in the five villages. Spartan women have strong opinions, and they share them freely.

[pause]

Later, she goes to the river with some other women to watch the girls running races. Kleo is there, with her friends from the village. They race along the track by the river, their feet pounding on the hard earth. Agiatis watches with a small smile. She was a fast runner too, when she was young.

[long-pause]

As the sun goes down, the men of the village walk off to their common meals. Damon stops by the house on his way and kisses his mother on the forehead. He ruffles Nikandros's hair and asks Kleo how the racing went. I won, she says, of course. He laughs and walks on down the street, his red cloak swinging behind him.

[pause]

{img:spartan-family-supper-courtyard-lamplight-mother-grandmother-children}
At home, Agiatis eats supper with the grandmother and the children. There is barley porridge, a little fish from the river, and some figs. They eat by the light of a small oil lamp in the courtyard. The evening is warm, and the crickets are singing. The grandmother tells a story about a fox who tried to steal grapes. Nikandros laughs at the funny parts. Kleo pretends she is too old for stories, but she listens to every word.

[pause]

When the children are in bed, Agiatis sits alone in the courtyard for a while. She looks up at the stars, which are bright and clear tonight. She thinks about her son, and about the day very soon when he will leave. She knows it is the Spartan way. All Spartan boys must go. But tonight, she lets herself feel a little sad. Then she blows out the lamp and goes to bed.

[pause]

On the way home from the farm, Agiatis stops at a small spring beside the path. The water comes out of the rock, cold and clear, into a little stone basin. She cups her hands and drinks. A small shrine to the nymphs of the spring stands nearby, with a few flowers left by other travellers. She adds a wild flower of her own. Then she walks on, feeling refreshed.

[long-pause]

{img:helot-family-farmhouse-valley-evening-children-goats-simple-life}
Now we must talk about the people who do most of the work in Sparta. They are called helots.

[pause]

The helots are the farmers of Laconia, and of the land of Messenia over the mountains to the west. Long ago, before the time of our story, the Spartans took over their lands. Since then, the helots have had to work the farms for the Spartans. They are not free. They cannot leave their farms, and they cannot choose a different life. Each year, they must give a large share of their harvest to their Spartan masters.

[pause]

There are many more helots than Spartans. Perhaps seven helots for every Spartan citizen. It is because of the helots that the Spartan men do not have to farm or work at a trade. They can spend all their time training to be soldiers. Without the helots, Sparta as we know it could not exist.

[long-pause]

Life for the helots is hard. They work from dawn to dusk in the fields and groves. They plough with oxen, sow the barley, and bring in the harvest. They prune the olive trees and pick the olives in winter. They tend the vines and press the grapes. And they must hand over much of what they grow.

[pause]

{img:helots-picking-olives-winter-baskets-ladders-grey-sky-valley}
The Spartans did not treat the helots kindly. They looked down on them, and kept them under strict control. They were always a little afraid that the helots might one day rise up against them. And sometimes, over the years, they did. We will not dwell on the hard parts of this story tonight. But we remember the helots with respect, because their lives mattered too.

[long-pause]

Let us visit the family of Lykos, on Agiatis's farm. Their house is small, made of stone and mud brick, with a roof of reeds. Inside there is one main room, with a hearth in the middle. The family sleeps on mats on the floor. There is a little garden beside the house, where they grow beans and onions and cabbages. And there are a few goats and some chickens, scratching in the yard.

[pause]

{img:helot-house-interior-hearth-mats-family-meal-lentils-bread-evening}
Lykos has a wife called Melitta, and three children. The eldest is a boy of thirteen, who already works in the fields with his father. The middle one is a girl of ten, who helps her mother in the house and the garden. And the youngest is a little boy of four, who follows the goats around and talks to them.

[pause]

In the evening, the family eats together around the fire. There is lentil soup and barley bread, and perhaps a few olives. Melitta tells the children stories from the old days. She tells them about Messenia, the land beyond the mountains, where her grandmother's family came from. She tells them about the free farmers who lived there long ago. The children listen quietly in the firelight.

[long-pause]

The helots have their own festivals and their own gods. They pray to Demeter, the goddess of the harvest, for good crops. They pray to Poseidon, for rain and for safe journeys. They sing their own songs as they work in the fields. Some of these songs are sad, and some are funny. They help to make the long days pass more quickly.

[pause]

{img:helot-family-praying-small-field-shrine-harvest-offering-sunset}
After the harvest, the helot families gather together to celebrate. They have a feast of bread and cheese and new wine. The children run and play among the threshing floors. The young people dance in a circle to the sound of a flute. And for one evening, the hard work is forgotten.

[pause]

Lykos sits by the fire with the other men and looks out over the dark valley. He thinks about his son, who is growing strong and tall. He thinks about his little daughter, and about the youngest one, asleep on his mother's lap. He hopes that their lives will be a little easier than his own. He does not know if that will ever be true. But he hopes, as fathers everywhere do. And then he goes to bed, because tomorrow the work begins again.

[pause]

On winter evenings, Lykos makes things by the fire. He carves wooden spoons and bowls, and mends the handles of the tools. He weaves baskets from the reeds of the river. His eldest son sits beside him and watches, learning. Sometimes the boy makes a little wooden animal for his youngest brother. The little boy carries it everywhere, and sleeps with it under his blanket.

[long-pause]

{img:perioikoi-town-laconia-coast-craftsmen-workshops-harbour-boats}
There is one more group of people in the Spartan world. They are called the perioikoi. That is a Greek word that means the people who live around.

[pause]

The perioikoi live in small towns all around Laconia, outside the five villages of Sparta. They are free people, not like the helots. They have their own houses, their own land and their own little towns. But they are not Spartan citizens. They cannot vote in the Spartan assembly. And when Sparta goes to war, they must send soldiers too.

[long-pause]

The perioikoi do many of the jobs that the Spartans themselves do not do. Spartan men are not allowed to work as craftsmen or traders. They must spend all their time as soldiers. So the perioikoi are the smiths and the potters, the builders and the merchants. They make the bronze helmets and shields that the Spartan soldiers carry. They make the pots and the tools and the furniture. And they trade with other parts of Greece.

[pause]

{img:bronze-smith-workshop-hammering-shield-fire-glowing-laconia}
Let us visit a bronze workshop in one of these towns. It is hot and smoky inside, and the air smells of metal and charcoal. A smith sits by a small, very hot fire. His young helper pumps the bellows, a bag of leather that blows air onto the coals. The coals glow bright orange. The smith holds a sheet of bronze in the fire with long tongs. When it is hot enough, he lays it on an anvil and hammers it into shape.

[pause]

It takes many days to make a good bronze shield. The smith must hammer the metal thin and even, so it is strong but not too heavy. He shapes it into a gentle curve, like a shallow bowl. Then he fixes it onto a frame of wood. The finished shield is about a metre across, and it shines like gold in the sun.

[long-pause]

Laconian craftsmen are famous for other things too. They make beautiful bronze bowls and jugs, decorated with little figures of animals and people. They make fine pottery, painted in black and red, with pictures of the gods. And they make little bronze statues, of soldiers and girls running and horses. Some of these little statues have been found far away, in other parts of Greece and even Italy.

[pause]

{img:laconian-potter-painting-black-figure-cup-workshop-sunlight}
In a pottery workshop, a potter sits at his wheel. His son turns the wheel with his foot, round and round. The potter shapes the wet clay with his hands, slowly and carefully. A cup grows up from the lump of clay, thin and smooth and round. When it is dry, the potter's wife paints it with fine brushes. She paints a picture of a man riding a horse, and a bird flying above him. Then the cup goes into the kiln to be fired.

[long-pause]

The towns of the perioikoi are small and friendly. There are little markets in the squares, where people buy and sell food and goods. There are small temples to the local gods. There are harbours on the coast, where fishing boats come in every morning with their catch. Traders arrive from other parts of Greece, bringing wine and cloth and things to sell.

[pause]

The people here live quieter lives than the Spartans. They do not have to train for war every day. They can choose their own work, and they can travel. In many ways, their lives are more like the lives of ordinary Greeks everywhere. They grumble about the Spartans sometimes, as people do. But they also know that Sparta keeps the whole region safe.

[pause]

{img:small-laconian-harbour-fishing-boats-nets-drying-evening-town}
In the evening, the fishermen of a small harbour town sit on the quay and mend their nets. The boats bob gently in the water, tied to wooden posts. Women carry baskets of fish up to the town. Children sit on the warm stones and dangle their feet over the edge. The sun sinks into the sea, and the sky turns orange and pink.

[pause]

An old fisherman tells a story to the children about a dolphin that once saved a boy from drowning. The children have heard it many times. But they listen again, with their chins in their hands. And as the first stars appear, their mothers call them home for supper.

[pause]

Every few weeks, a trader comes from one of these towns to the five villages of Sparta. He brings his goods on the backs of two donkeys. There are pots and lamps, little bronze pins and needles. The women come out to look and to bargain. Agiatis buys a new oil lamp and a bronze needle for her mother-in-law. The trader tells them news from the coast, and then he moves on to the next village.

[long-pause]

{img:spartan-boy-seven-leaving-home-mother-doorway-early-morning}
In Sparta, when a boy turns seven years old, he leaves home. He goes to live with other boys his age, in a group led by an older boy. This is the beginning of the famous Spartan training, which is called the agoge. It will last until he is a young man. Every Spartan boy must go through it, even the sons of the kings.

[pause]

For Nikandros, the day has now come.

[long-pause]

It is early in the morning, and the sky is just turning grey. Agiatis wakes him gently and helps him get ready. There is not much to pack. In the agoge, the boys are given very little. A simple cloak, and nothing more. Their hair is cut short. They go barefoot. They will learn to make do with almost nothing.

[pause]

{img:spartan-grandmother-hugging-small-boy-courtyard-dawn-quiet-farewell}
The grandmother is already up, sitting in the courtyard. She holds out her arms, and Nikandros goes to her. She hugs him for a long time. Then she presses something small into his hand. It is a smooth river stone, with a white line running through it. Keep this, she whispers. And when you are tired or sad, hold it, and remember us. He closes his hand around it tightly.

[pause]

Kleo comes out, rubbing her eyes. She does not say much. But she gives her little brother a quick, hard hug. Then she turns away so he will not see her face. Damon is there too, standing by the door. He puts his hand on his son's shoulder. Be brave, he says quietly. And be kind to the other boys. They will be your brothers now.

[long-pause]

Then Agiatis walks with Nikandros to the place where the boys are gathering. There are several other boys there, all about seven years old. Some are trying to look brave. Some are holding their mothers' hands. An older boy of about eighteen is waiting for them. His name is Archias, and he will be their leader. He looks serious, but not unkind.

[pause]

{img:group-young-spartan-boys-short-hair-cloaks-older-youth-leader-field}
Spartan mothers are famous for being strong when their sons leave. It is said that they do not cry in front of others. Agiatis kneels down and looks at Nikandros for a moment. She straightens his cloak. Then she stands up and gives him a little push toward the other boys. Go on, she says. And he goes.

[pause]

She watches the group walk away down the path, toward the training grounds by the river. Nikandros looks back once. She raises her hand. Then he is gone, around the corner. Agiatis stands there for a long time. Then she walks home slowly, and does not say a word to anyone all morning.

[long-pause]

What is life like in the agoge? It is strict and simple, and it is often hard. The boys sleep together in groups, on beds of reeds from the river. They get just enough food to grow, but not much more. They learn to be tough, to be patient, and to work together. They learn to run and wrestle and swim. They learn to march in step, and to obey their leaders.

[pause]

But they also learn other things. They learn to read and write a little. They learn the old songs and poems of Sparta by heart. They learn to dance, which the Spartans think is very important. And they learn to speak in short, clever sentences. The Spartans are famous for saying a lot in very few words.

[pause]

{img:spartan-boys-learning-songs-older-teacher-circle-grass-shade}
In the first evening, Nikandros lies on his reed bed among the other boys. The reeds are prickly, and the night is cool. Some of the boys are whispering. One is crying softly, very quietly, so the older boys will not hear. Nikandros holds the river stone in his hand. He thinks about his grandmother and her stories. He thinks about his mother, and the courtyard, and the olive tree. And slowly, with the stone in his hand, he falls asleep.

[pause]

In the first weeks, the boys learn to look after each other. When one of them is cold, the others share their cloaks. When one of them is sad, the others sit close beside him. Archias, their leader, watches carefully. He was once a small boy here too. He remembers how hard the first weeks were. So, now and then, when nobody is looking, he gives the smallest boys a little extra bread.

[long-pause]

{img:spartan-girls-training-running-track-river-morning-mothers-watching}
In most Greek cities, girls stay at home. They learn to spin and weave and cook, and they rarely go outside. But Sparta is different. In Sparta, girls are trained too.

[pause]

Spartan girls run races, and they wrestle and throw the javelin. They do exercises outdoors, in the sunshine, just like the boys. The Spartans believe that strong mothers will have strong children. So they want their girls to be healthy and strong. People in other parts of Greece found this very strange. But the Spartan girls were proud of it.

[long-pause]

Kleo trains with the other girls of her age, by the river, most mornings. They warm up by running slowly along the bank. Then they race each other, two or three at a time. Their feet thud on the hard earth, and their hair flies out behind them. The older women watch and call out advice. Lift your knees, they shout. Breathe, breathe!

[pause]

{img:spartan-girls-javelin-throwing-meadow-laughing-sunlight-tunics}
After the running, they practise throwing the javelin, a light spear made of wood. They throw it as far as they can, across the meadow. Kleo is not very good at the javelin. Her throws often wobble and fall short. Her friend Gorgo is the best at it. Gorgo's javelin flies straight and far, every time. Kleo is a little jealous. But she cheers for her friend anyway.

[long-pause]

The girls also learn music and dance. They learn to sing in choirs, all together, in clear high voices. At the big festivals, the girls' choirs sing and dance in front of the whole city. It is a great honour to be chosen for the choir. Kleo was chosen last year, and she is in it again this year.

[pause]

Their teacher is an old woman with a strong voice and sharp ears. She teaches them the old songs of Sparta, line by line. Some of the songs were written long ago by a famous poet called Alcman. They are about the beauty of the girls, the dawn, and the goddesses. Kleo loves one song especially. It says that the leader of the choir shines like the sun.

[pause]

{img:spartan-girls-choir-singing-practice-older-woman-teacher-courtyard}
The girls practise in a courtyard near the temple, in the late afternoon. They stand in two rows and sing to each other, back and forth. The teacher claps out the rhythm. Sometimes someone sings the wrong word, and everyone giggles. Then the teacher frowns, and they start again. Slowly, the song becomes beautiful and smooth.

[long-pause]

Spartan girls marry later than girls in other Greek cities. They marry at about eighteen or twenty, when they are grown up. And as women, they have much more freedom than women in other cities. They can own land and inherit it from their parents. In fact, a large part of the land in Sparta is owned by women. They run their households and their farms. And they speak their minds, even to men.

[pause]

There is a famous story about a Spartan queen called Gorgo, from about the time of our story. A woman from another city once said to her, you Spartan women are the only ones who rule your men. Gorgo answered, that is because we are the only ones who give birth to men. People repeated that answer all over Greece.

[pause]

{img:spartan-mother-daughter-walking-home-river-path-sunset-talking}
On the way home from training, Kleo walks with her mother along the river path. The sun is setting behind the mountains, and the sky is gold and pink. Kleo tells her mother about the race and the javelin and the song. Then she goes quiet for a while. I miss Nikandros, she says at last. Agiatis puts an arm around her daughter's shoulders. So do I, she says softly. They walk home together in the dusk, without saying anything more.

[pause]

At home, Kleo also learns the work of the house. Her grandmother teaches her to spin, to weave and to make cheese. Her mother teaches her how to manage the storeroom and the farm. One day, Kleo will run a household of her own. She does not always like the lessons. She would rather be running by the river. But she listens, because she knows these things matter too.

[long-pause]

{img:spartan-men-common-meal-syssitia-long-tables-bowls-evening-lamps}
Every evening, the Spartan men eat together in groups, not at home with their families. These shared meals are called the syssitia. Every Spartan citizen must belong to one of these groups. It is one of the oldest and most important customs of Sparta.

[pause]

Each group has about fifteen men, of all different ages. There are young men just out of the agoge, and old men with white beards. They eat together every evening, for most of their lives. They become like a second family. They know each other very well, and they look after each other.

[long-pause]

Every man must bring his share of food to the meal each month. He brings barley flour, wine, cheese, figs, and a little money for meat. This food comes from his farm, worked by the helots. If a man cannot pay his share, he loses his place in the group. And then he is no longer a full Spartan citizen. So the shared meals are very important to every Spartan family.

[pause]

{img:spartan-black-broth-pot-cooking-fire-cook-stirring-bowls}
The most famous food at the shared meals is the black broth. It is a soup made from pork, cooked in its own blood, with vinegar and salt. It is dark and thick, and it has a strong, sour taste. The Spartans eat it every evening. Visitors from other cities found it terrible. One famous story tells of a rich man who tasted it. Now I understand, he said, why the Spartans are not afraid of anything.

[pause]

But there is other food too. There is barley bread and cheese, figs and olives. Sometimes there is fish, or a hare or a deer that one of the men has hunted. When a man has hunted something, he brings it to share with the group. That is a mark of honour. The wine is mixed with water, as all Greeks do. And no one is allowed to drink too much.

[long-pause]

The meals are not only about food. They are also a time for talking. The men talk about the news, about farming, and about the army. The older men tell stories of battles and deeds from long ago. The young men listen and learn. Sometimes there is joking and teasing. The Spartans love clever, short jokes, and they laugh at a good one.

[pause]

{img:spartan-boys-serving-at-common-meal-listening-older-men-talking}
Boys from the agoge sometimes come to the shared meals to serve and listen. They pour the wine and carry the bread. They learn how men should talk and behave. The older men sometimes ask them questions, to see how quick they are. A boy who gives a clever, short answer is praised. A boy who talks too much is gently laughed at.

[pause]

When a boy first comes to the meal, the oldest man points to the door. Nothing that is said in this room, he says, goes out through that door. The men must be able to speak freely. And they trust each other to keep what is said among themselves.

[long-pause]

Damon's group meets in a long low building near the agora. There are wooden tables and benches, and a hearth at one end where the food is cooked. Oil lamps hang from the beams. On cold winter evenings, the room is warm and smoky and full of voices.

[pause]

Tonight, an old man called Leon is telling a story. He tells about a time when he was young, and a storm caught his group on the mountain. They spent the whole night in a cave, wrapped in their cloaks, telling jokes to keep warm. In the morning, they came down covered in snow, laughing. The other men laugh too, although they have heard the story many times.

[pause]

{img:spartan-man-walking-home-dark-street-red-cloak-stars-after-meal}
After the meal, the men walk home through the dark streets. There are no lamps outside, and the Spartans are not allowed to carry torches. It is a rule, to teach them to walk safely in the dark. Damon walks slowly, looking up at the stars. He thinks about his son, sleeping on his reed bed somewhere by the river. Then he reaches his own door, and goes quietly inside.

[pause]

After the meal, the men sometimes sing together. They sing the old marching songs of Tyrtaeus, about courage and about Sparta. One man starts, and the others join in, their deep voices filling the room. Then someone tells a joke, and everyone laughs. The lamps flicker, and the fire burns low. It is a warm and friendly place, for all its plain food and simple benches.

[long-pause]

{img:spartan-soldiers-training-field-shields-spears-morning-dust-mountains}
Every Spartan man is a soldier. That is his only job, from the age of twenty until the age of sixty. He does not farm, or trade, or make things. He trains, and he is always ready to defend Sparta.

[pause]

The Spartan army fights in a special way. The soldiers stand close together, in long rows, shoulder to shoulder. Each man holds a big round shield on his left arm. His shield also covers part of the man standing beside him. So every man protects his neighbour. This way of fighting is called a phalanx. It only works if every man stays in his place and trusts the others.

[long-pause]

So the Spartan soldiers practise together almost every day. They march in step to the music of a flute. The Spartans are the only Greeks who march to music in this way. The flute plays a steady, calm tune, and the men move together as one. It looks almost like a slow dance. Visitors who watched them were amazed at how calm and orderly they were.

[pause]

{img:spartan-soldiers-marching-flute-player-rows-shields-valley-calm}
The soldiers also keep themselves fit. They run and wrestle and exercise in the gymnasium. They hunt in the mountains, which is good training. They practise with their shields and long spears. And they take great care of their equipment. Their bronze helmets and shields must always be polished and ready.

[long-pause]

Spartan soldiers wear their hair long, unlike most other Greeks. They take great care of it, combing it and braiding it before they go out. There is a famous saying about this. Long hair makes a handsome man more handsome. And it makes an ugly man look more frightening. The Spartans liked that saying very much.

[pause]

{img:spartan-soldiers-combing-long-hair-camp-morning-red-cloaks-relaxed}
Damon trains with his group in the mornings, on the flat ground near the river. Afterwards, he often goes hunting in the hills with his friends. They take their dogs with them. Spartan hunting dogs are famous all over Greece, fast and clever and loyal. Damon has a big brown dog called Argos. Argos goes everywhere with him, except to the common meal.

[pause]

When he has time, Damon goes out to the farm to talk with Lykos. He is not supposed to farm himself. But he likes to walk among the olive trees and look at the barley. He grew up on this land, and he loves it. Sometimes he and Lykos stand together at the edge of the field, looking at the crops. They talk about the rain and the harvest, like any two farmers.

[long-pause]

The Spartans are proud of their army, but they do not love war for its own sake. They often prefer to stay at home in their valley. They are slow to go to war, and they think carefully first. Many other Greek cities ask Sparta for help when they are in danger. Because everyone knows that the Spartan soldiers will not run away.

[pause]

{img:spartan-mother-giving-shield-to-son-saying-farewell-doorway-dawn}
There is a famous old saying of the Spartan mothers. When a son left for war, his mother would give him his shield. Come back with this, she would say, or on it. It meant, come back with honour. These sayings were collected later, in a little book of Spartan sayings. They show how the Spartans wanted to be remembered: brave, short of words, and strong.

[pause]

But behind the famous sayings, there were ordinary families too. Mothers who worried, and wives who waited, and children who missed their fathers. They did not write down their feelings, as people do today. But we can be sure they felt them, just as we would.

[pause]

In the evenings, before the common meal, Damon often sits with his mother for a while. He tells her about the training and the hunting, and about his friends. She tells him about the neighbours and the farm. He listens patiently, even when she tells him the same story twice. Then he kisses her forehead and goes off to the meal. The grandmother watches him go, and she smiles to herself.

[long-pause]

{img:lycurgus-legendary-lawgiver-old-man-robes-speaking-spartans-assembly}
Why is Sparta so different from other Greek cities? The Spartans had an answer to that question. They said it was because of one man, long ago. His name was Lycurgus.

[pause]

Lycurgus lived a long time before our story, perhaps three hundred years earlier. Nobody is quite sure when, or even whether he really lived at all. But the Spartans believed in him completely. They said he had given them all their laws. And they said that those laws had made Sparta strong.

[long-pause]

The old stories say that Lycurgus travelled widely when he was young. He visited Crete and Egypt and many other lands. He studied their laws and their ways of life. Then he came home to Sparta, which was in trouble at that time. There were rich people and poor people, and they did not get along. Lycurgus decided to change everything.

[pause]

{img:lycurgus-visiting-oracle-delphi-temple-mountains-priestess-ancient}
First, he went to the famous oracle at Delphi, to ask the god Apollo for advice. The oracle was a priestess who spoke the words of the god. She told Lycurgus that his laws would be the best in the world. So he went back to Sparta and began to make new laws.

[pause]

He divided the land into equal parts, so every Spartan family had a farm. He started the shared meals, so the rich and poor would eat the same food together. He started the agoge, to train the boys. And he made a very strange rule about money.

[long-pause]

In Sparta, people were not allowed to use gold or silver coins. Instead, Lycurgus ordered that money should be made of iron. And not small iron coins, but heavy iron bars. Iron bars were very heavy and not worth much. To pay for something, you needed a whole cartload of them. And to keep a large amount, you needed a whole room.

[pause]

{img:iron-bar-money-spartan-storeroom-heavy-rods-cart-oxen-funny}
The idea was simple. If money was heavy and hard to use, nobody would want to collect too much of it. People would not become greedy. Merchants from other cities would not come to sell expensive things. And Spartans would live simply, without luxury. It worked, at least for a long time. Later Spartans did not always keep to it. But in the time of our story, the iron money is still in use.

[long-pause]

When his laws were in place, Lycurgus did one last thing. He made the Spartans promise not to change the laws until he came back. Then he left Sparta and travelled to Delphi. And the story says he never came back. He stayed away for the rest of his life. So the Spartans had to keep their promise. And they kept his laws for hundreds of years.

[pause]

There is also a famous saying about the laws of Lycurgus. They were never written down. The Spartans had to learn them by heart, from their parents and teachers. Lycurgus believed that laws you know in your heart are stronger than laws written on stone.

[pause]

{img:old-spartan-man-teaching-children-laws-under-plane-tree-evening}
In the evening, the grandmother Eupolia sometimes tells Kleo about Lycurgus. She tells it the way her own grandmother told it to her. Kleo asks if it is all true. The grandmother shrugs and smiles. Who knows, she says. But it is a good story. And good stories, my child, are worth keeping.

[pause]

Because of the iron money, there were very few shops in Sparta. There were no fancy goods to buy, no perfumes or fine jewellery. The Spartans did not seem to miss them. They had good bread and cheese, warm cloaks and strong houses. They had their festivals, their music and their friends. And they believed that this simple life made them strong and happy.

[long-pause]

{img:two-spartan-kings-side-by-side-elders-council-hall-dignified}
Sparta has one more unusual thing. It has not one king, but two. They rule at the same time, side by side.

[pause]

The two kings come from two old royal families. The Spartans say that both families go back to the great hero Heracles. When a king passes away, his son becomes king in his place. So there are always two kings, one from each family. Nobody else in Greece has two kings. The Spartans found it perfectly normal.

[long-pause]

Why two kings? Perhaps because it stops one man from becoming too powerful. Each king can keep an eye on the other. In wartime, one king leads the army, while the other stays at home. In peacetime, the kings lead the religious ceremonies. They offer sacrifices to the gods on behalf of the whole city. And they sit on the council of elders.

[pause]

{img:gerousia-council-elders-white-beards-sitting-stone-benches-meeting}
The council of elders is called the gerousia. It has twenty-eight men, all over sixty years old, plus the two kings. They are chosen for life. They discuss the important matters of the city, and they act as judges. When an old man wants to join the council, there is a strange way of choosing. The candidates walk one by one across the meeting place. The people shout for each one. Men in a closed room listen to the shouts, without seeing who is walking. The man who gets the loudest shout is chosen.

[long-pause]

There are also five officials called ephors. They are chosen every year by the citizens. The ephors are very powerful. They watch over the kings, and they can even put a king on trial. They manage the daily business of the city. And every year, when they start their job, they make a strange announcement. They tell all the Spartan men to shave their moustaches. Nobody knows quite why. It was simply an old custom.

[pause]

{img:five-ephors-meeting-room-oil-lamps-scrolls-discussing-evening}
In the time of our story, one of the two kings is called Leonidas. He is about sixty years old, and he has been king for about ten years. He is married to Queen Gorgo, the clever queen we heard about earlier. She is much younger than him. They have a young son called Pleistarchos.

[pause]

People say that Leonidas is a calm and quiet man. He speaks little, as the Spartans like. When he does speak, people listen. His name will soon become one of the most famous names in all of history. But in the spring of 480 before Christ, he is simply one of the two kings of Sparta, living quietly in the valley.

[long-pause]

The kings of Sparta do not live in great palaces. Their houses are only a little larger than other Spartan houses. They eat at the common meals with the other men, although they get a double portion of food. That is not because they eat more. It is so they can share it with a guest, as a sign of honour.

[pause]

{img:spartan-queen-gorgo-courtyard-young-son-wise-face-evening-light}
Queen Gorgo is famous for her cleverness. There is a story about her when she was only a small girl. A foreign visitor came to her father, who was king then. The visitor offered the king a large bag of silver to help him in a war. Little Gorgo was standing nearby. Father, she said, you should send this man away. Otherwise he will spoil you with his money. Her father laughed, and he sent the visitor away.

[pause]

Kleo loves the stories about Queen Gorgo. Sometimes, when the girls' choir sings at the festivals, the queen is there to watch. Kleo always sings a little louder then. She hopes the queen will notice her. And once, last year, the queen smiled at her. Kleo told everyone about it for weeks.

[pause]

On feast days, the kings walk through the five villages with the priests. People come out of their houses to watch them pass. The kings stop to talk to the old people, and to pat the children on the head. They are not grand or distant. In a small place like Sparta, everybody knows them. And they know everybody too.

[long-pause]

{img:hyakinthia-festival-sparta-procession-amyclae-flowers-music-summer}
The Spartans work hard and live simply, but they also love their festivals. There are many of them through the year. And at festival time, the whole valley comes alive with music, singing and dancing.

[pause]

One of the most important festivals is the Hyakinthia, in early summer. It is held in honour of Apollo, at his old sanctuary at Amyclae, a short walk south of Sparta. It remembers a sad old story. A young man called Hyakinthos was a friend of the god Apollo. One day, they were playing a game of throwing the discus. The discus hit Hyakinthos by accident, and he passed away. Where he fell, a beautiful flower grew. We still call it the hyacinth.

[long-pause]

The festival lasts three days. On the first day, people are quiet and sad, remembering Hyakinthos. They do not wear flower crowns, and they eat only plain bread. But on the second day, everything changes. Now it is a day of joy. Everyone puts on their best clothes and walks in a great procession to Amyclae. There is music and singing, and the boys and girls ride in decorated carts.

[pause]

{img:spartan-boys-choir-singing-festival-sunlit-sanctuary-crowd-flowers}
At the sanctuary, there are choirs and dances. Boys play the lyre and sing the old hymns to Apollo. Young men ride horses in a parade. Girls ride in beautiful carts, some shaped like swans. Families picnic on the grass under the trees. Even the helots and the servants have a share in the feast. For this one day, everyone eats well and is merry.

[long-pause]

Another festival is the Gymnopaidia, in the hottest part of summer. For several days, choirs of boys and men sing and dance in the open air. They dance in the agora, in the blazing sun. It is a test of strength and endurance as well as music. The old men sing the songs of the past. The young men sing about what they will do. And the boys sing about what they will become.

[pause]

{img:gymnopaidia-dancing-choirs-agora-summer-sun-old-men-young-men-boys}
The third great festival is the Karneia, in late summer. It is in honour of Apollo too. During the Karneia, the Spartans do not go to war. That is a very old and important rule. For nine days, the men camp in tents, like soldiers. But instead of fighting, they feast and sing and race. Young men chase a runner wearing woollen ribbons. If they catch him, it is a good sign for the year.

[long-pause]

This year, the whole family goes to the Hyakinthia together. Even Nikandros is allowed to come, with the boys of his group. Agiatis sees him across the crowd, standing in a line with the other boys. He looks thinner and browner than before. His hair is cut very short. But when he sees his mother, he grins and waves. She waves back, and her heart feels light.

[pause]

{img:spartan-family-picnic-festival-grass-trees-bread-figs-laughing}
Kleo sings in the girls' choir at the festival. She stands in the front row, in a white dress with a crown of flowers. Her voice rises clear and high above the others. Agiatis and the grandmother watch from the crowd. The grandmother has tears in her eyes. When the song is over, the crowd cheers. Kleo looks around for her family and finds them. She does not smile, because that is not the Spartan way. But her eyes are shining.

[pause]

In the evening, the family sits on the grass with Damon and some neighbours. They share bread and cheese and figs and a little wine. Lykos's family is there too, sitting a little way off with the other helots. The children play together on the grass for a while, helot and Spartan, as children will. The sun goes down behind the Taygetos, and the sky turns gold. And for this one evening, the whole valley is at peace.

[pause]

On the way home from the festival, the grandmother falls asleep in the donkey cart. Kleo walks beside it, holding her crown of flowers. Agiatis walks on the other side, and Damon leads the donkey. The stars come out over the dark road. Somewhere behind them, people are still singing. And the sound follows them softly all the way home.

[long-pause]

{img:spartan-lyre-player-young-man-courtyard-evening-listeners}
People often think the Spartans had no time for music or poetry. But that is not true at all. In fact, Sparta was one of the most musical places in all of Greece.

[pause]

Long before the time of our story, some of the most famous poets and musicians in Greece lived in Sparta. There was Terpander, who was said to have added strings to the lyre. There was Alcman, who wrote the songs for the girls' choirs. And there was Tyrtaeus, who wrote songs for the soldiers to sing on the march. Their songs were sung in Sparta for hundreds of years.

[long-pause]

The lyre is the most important instrument. It is a small harp, made from a tortoise shell with two curved arms of wood. Strings made of gut stretch from the shell to a bar between the arms. The player holds it on his knee and plucks the strings with his fingers, or with a little pick. It makes a soft, sweet sound. Every Spartan boy learns to play it in the agoge.

[pause]

{img:aulos-double-flute-player-spartan-soldiers-marching-sound}
The other important instrument is the aulos, a double flute. The player blows into two pipes at the same time. It makes a strong, clear sound, a little like a bagpipe. The aulos is played at festivals and at sacrifices. And it is played when the Spartan army marches, to keep the soldiers in step.

[long-pause]

Dance is very important in Sparta too. There are dances for every festival and every age. There are slow, stately dances for the old men, and quick, lively dances for the girls. There are dances that look like soldiers fighting, with shields and spears. These are called war dances. But they are really a kind of exercise and a kind of game.

[pause]

{img:spartan-girls-circle-dance-meadow-flutes-spring-flowers}
Kleo loves to dance. The girls dance in a circle, holding hands, moving to the sound of the flute. They step forward and back, and turn around, all together. Their bare feet move lightly on the grass. Sometimes they dance faster and faster, until someone stumbles and everyone falls down laughing. Then the teacher claps her hands, and they start again.

[pause]

There is also a funny old dance called the bibasis. The girls jump up and kick their heels behind them, touching their bottoms. They count how many times they can do it in a row. The record, people said, was a thousand times. Kleo has tried it many times. Her best is two hundred and thirty-one. She is determined to do better.

[long-pause]

In the agoge, Nikandros is learning to play the lyre. At first, his fingers are clumsy, and the strings make ugly twanging sounds. The older boys laugh at him. But his teacher is patient. He shows Nikandros how to hold his hand, and how to pluck gently. After a few weeks, Nikandros can play a simple tune. It is an old song about the river Eurotas.

[pause]

{img:young-spartan-boy-learning-lyre-older-boy-teaching-riverbank-evening}
In the evenings, the boys sit by the river and practise their music. The sound of the lyres drifts out over the water. Frogs croak in the reeds, and the stars come out above the mountains. Nikandros plays his song about the river, softly. He thinks that one day he will play it for his grandmother. And he smiles to himself in the dark.

[pause]

The old Spartan poet Alcman wrote a beautiful song about the night. It says that the mountain peaks are sleeping, and the valleys too. The birds are sleeping, and the bees, and the creatures of the dark sea. Everything is resting under the quiet sky. It is one of the oldest lullabies in the world. And it was written in Sparta.

[pause]

The grandmother remembers the dances of her own youth. Sometimes, on a warm evening, she stands up and shows Kleo an old step. Her feet are slow now, and her back is bent. But she still knows every movement. Kleo copies her, laughing. And for a moment, the grandmother looks young again, dancing in the lamplight of the courtyard.

[long-pause]

{img:barley-harvest-laconia-helots-sickles-golden-field-early-summer}
In early summer, the barley in the valley turns golden. It is time for the harvest.

[pause]

On Agiatis's farm, Lykos and his family begin cutting the barley at dawn. They work with curved sickles, cutting the stalks close to the ground. The eldest son cuts beside his father, row after row. Melitta and her daughter follow behind, gathering the stalks into bundles. They tie each bundle with a twist of straw. The little boy carries water to everyone in a small clay jug.

[pause]

It is hot, hard work. By midday, the sun is blazing, and everyone is sweating. They rest for a while in the shade of an old fig tree. They eat bread and cheese and drink water mixed with a little vinegar. It is refreshing in the heat. Then they go back to the field, and keep cutting until the light fades.

[long-pause]

When all the barley is cut, it is carried to the threshing floor. This is a flat, round place of hard beaten earth, near the farmhouse. The bundles are spread out on the floor. Then oxen are led round and round over them. The hooves of the oxen shake the grain loose from the stalks.

[pause]

{img:threshing-floor-oxen-walking-circle-barley-farmhouse-summer-dust}
After that comes the winnowing. On a windy afternoon, the family tosses the grain into the air with wooden shovels. The wind blows away the light straw and dust. The heavy grain falls back down onto the floor in a golden heap. It is beautiful to watch, the grain falling like rain, shining in the sun.

[long-pause]

Agiatis comes out to the farm to see the harvest. She brings Kleo with her, and the grandmother rides in a small cart pulled by a donkey. The grandmother likes to see the harvest every year. She sits in the shade and watches, remembering the harvests of her youth. Kleo helps to gather up the last bundles. She is surprised how heavy they are, and how quickly her back starts to ache.

[pause]

{img:spartan-girl-helping-harvest-carrying-barley-bundle-helot-girl-together}
Kleo works beside Lykos's daughter, a girl of ten called Thalia. At first they do not talk. But then Thalia shows her a clever way of tying a bundle quickly. Kleo tries it, and it works. They laugh together. By the end of the afternoon, they are chatting like old friends. Thalia tells Kleo about her little brother and the goats. Kleo tells Thalia about running and the choir.

[pause]

When it is time to go, Kleo waves to Thalia from the cart. She thinks about her new friend all the way home. She thinks about how hard Thalia works, every single day. And for the first time, she wonders what it would be like to be in Thalia's place.

[long-pause]

At the end of the harvest, the grain is measured out. Half goes to Agiatis's family, as the law requires. It is carried to the house in the five villages, in big sacks on the backs of donkeys. The other half stays with Lykos's family. It must last them until next year. Lykos looks at his heap of grain carefully. It is a good harvest. There will be enough this year.

[pause]

{img:harvest-festival-helots-evening-fire-dancing-flute-threshing-floor}
In the evening, the helot families of the valley celebrate the end of the harvest. They light a bonfire beside the threshing floor. They roast a young goat and share bread and new wine. A man plays the flute, and the young people dance around the fire. The old people sit and watch, clapping in time. Lykos sits with his wife and children, tired but content.

[pause]

The stars come out over the valley, bright and many. The fire crackles and sends sparks up into the night. Melitta sings an old harvest song, and the others join in. It is a song about the golden fields and the kind goddess of the grain. And the sound of it drifts softly across the dark fields, all the way to the river.

[pause]

The next day, Agiatis sends a gift to Lykos's family. It is a small jar of honey and a piece of good cheese. It is not much, but it is a thank you for the harvest. Melitta opens the jar and lets each child dip a finger into the honey. The little boy licks his finger slowly, with his eyes closed. It is the sweetest thing he has tasted all year.

[long-pause]

{img:persian-army-distant-plain-tents-banners-many-soldiers-480bc-calm}
All this time, far away in the east, something important has been happening. A great king has been gathering an enormous army. He is Xerxes, the king of Persia. Persia is the biggest empire in the world. And Xerxes wants to bring all of Greece under his rule.

[pause]

His army is huge, bigger than anything the Greeks have ever seen. There are soldiers from dozens of different lands. There are hundreds of ships. In the summer of 480 before Christ, this great army crosses into Greece from the north. And the Greek cities must decide what to do.

[long-pause]

Many Greek cities decide to stand together against Persia. They ask Sparta to lead them. But there is a problem. It is the time of the Karneia festival, and during the Karneia, the Spartans may not go to war. That is a sacred rule. The Spartan leaders talk for a long time. In the end, they decide that the main army will wait until the festival is over. But one king will go ahead with a small group of men.

[pause]

{img:king-leonidas-leaving-sparta-small-group-soldiers-dawn-families-watching}
The king who goes is Leonidas. He chooses three hundred Spartan men to go with him. He chooses only men who already have sons, so that their families will go on. Many soldiers from other Greek cities join them along the way. Together, perhaps seven thousand Greeks march north.

[pause]

The people of Sparta come out to watch them leave. Queen Gorgo stands at the edge of the crowd. People say she asked Leonidas what she should do if he did not return. Marry a good man, he told her, and have good children. Then he turned and walked away with his men. Damon is not among the three hundred. He stays at home, with the main army, waiting for the festival to end.

[long-pause]

Leonidas and his men go to a narrow pass called Thermopylae. The name means the hot gates, because there are warm springs there. On one side of the pass there are steep mountains. On the other side, there is the sea. The way through is very narrow, only wide enough for a few carts side by side. In such a narrow place, a small army can hold back a very large one.

[pause]

{img:thermopylae-narrow-pass-mountains-sea-warm-springs-mist-ancient}
For two days, the small Greek army holds the pass. The great Persian army cannot get through. But on the third day, the Persians find a hidden mountain path. They can now go around the Greeks. Leonidas understands what this means. He sends most of the Greek soldiers away, to be safe. But he and his three hundred Spartans stay, with a few hundred others. They stay to give the others time to get away.

[pause]

We will not follow what happened next. It was a brave and very sad day. And by the end of it, Leonidas and his men had all passed away. We will leave them there, in the narrow pass between the mountains and the sea, with great respect.

[long-pause]

In Sparta, the news arrives some days later. A messenger comes running into the five villages. People gather in the streets and the agora to hear. The news spreads from house to house, very quietly. In many homes, there is sorrow. Families who have lost someone sit together in silence.

[pause]

{img:spartan-women-gathered-quietly-evening-courtyard-sad-news-lamps}
Agiatis hears the news at the well. She walks home slowly and tells the grandmother. The grandmother closes her eyes for a long time. She knew some of those men when they were boys. Kleo sits down on the step of the courtyard and puts her face in her hands. Damon comes home early from the common meal. He sits with his family in the courtyard, and nobody says anything for a long while.

[pause]

Later, the Greeks put up a stone at Thermopylae. On it, they carved a short poem. It said: Go tell the Spartans, passer-by, that here, obedient to their laws, we lie. Those few words became some of the most famous words in the world. And the story of the three hundred has been told ever since, for two and a half thousand years.

[pause]

In the weeks that follow, the families of Sparta help each other. Neighbours bring food to the houses where someone has been lost. The women sit together in the courtyards and weave and talk quietly. The children play together, a little more gently than before. And slowly, as the weeks pass, the five villages find their way back to everyday life.

[long-pause]

{img:greek-ships-salamis-bay-calm-after-battle-sunset-islands}
The story of the war does not end at Thermopylae. A few weeks later, the Greek ships meet the Persian ships near a small island called Salamis. The Greek ships are fewer, but they are clever. They lure the Persian ships into a narrow strait, and there the Greeks win a great victory at sea.

[pause]

After that, King Xerxes goes back home to Persia. But he leaves part of his army in Greece. And so, the next summer, there must be one more great meeting of armies.

[long-pause]

This time, the whole Spartan army marches out. It is led by a young man called Pausanias, who is a cousin of the boy king. Damon goes with them. So do almost all the men of the five villages. They march north with the flute playing and their red cloaks swaying. The women and children and old people stand by the road and watch them go.

[pause]

{img:spartan-army-marching-north-road-red-cloaks-flute-families-watching}
Agiatis stands with Kleo and the grandmother at the edge of the village. She gives Damon his shield, as Spartan wives always do. She does not cry in front of the others. But she holds his hand for a moment longer than usual. Then he is gone, marching north with the others. And the waiting begins.

[long-pause]

The weeks at home are long and quiet. Agiatis goes on running the house and the farm, as always. She goes to the farm and talks with Lykos about the olives. She weaves at the loom in the evenings. Kleo trains by the river with the other girls, and sings in the choir. The grandmother spins wool in the courtyard and tells stories. But every day, all of them are listening for news.

[pause]

{img:spartan-woman-weaving-loom-evening-lamp-waiting-thoughtful}
At last, the news comes. There has been a great battle near a town called Plataea, far to the north. The Greeks have won. The Persian army has gone home, and it will not come back. Greece is free. In the five villages of Sparta, people come out into the streets. Some of them laugh, and some of them cry. Many of them do both at once.

[long-pause]

Some days later, the army comes home. The families line the road to watch them come. Agiatis stands at the front, with Kleo beside her. She looks along the long line of red cloaks, searching every face. Her heart is beating fast. And then she sees him. Damon, dusty and tired, with a bandage on his arm. But walking, and smiling at her.

[pause]

{img:spartan-soldier-returning-home-family-reunion-courtyard-evening-gold-light}
That evening, the whole family sits together in the courtyard. Damon tells them a little about the journey and the camp, but not about the battle. The grandmother holds his hand and will not let go. Kleo sits close beside him, leaning against his shoulder. And for a special treat, Nikandros is allowed to come home from the agoge for one night.

[pause]

Nikandros brings his lyre. After supper, he sits on the step and plays his song about the river Eurotas. His fingers are much quicker now. The notes rise softly into the warm evening air. The grandmother closes her eyes and listens. When he finishes, nobody says anything for a moment. Then Damon puts his hand on his son's head. That was well played, he says quietly. And Nikandros's face glows in the lamplight.

[pause]

The next morning, Nikandros goes back to the agoge with the other boys. This time, he does not look back with sad eyes. He waves cheerfully to his family and runs to catch up with his friends. Agiatis watches him go, and she smiles. He is growing up, she thinks. And he will be all right.

[long-pause]

{img:sparta-later-centuries-quiet-valley-temples-olive-groves-fading-power}
After the great war with Persia, Sparta was one of the two most powerful cities in Greece. The other was Athens. For a while, they were friends. But slowly, they grew suspicious of each other. Athens grew rich from the sea and built a great fleet. Sparta stayed in its valley, with its army and its old ways.

[pause]

Some fifty years after our story, Athens and Sparta went to war with each other. It was a long and hard war that lasted almost thirty years. In the end, Sparta won. For a short time, it was the strongest city in all of Greece.

[long-pause]

But Sparta's way of life had a weakness. There were never very many Spartan citizens, and over the years there were fewer and fewer. Fewer men could pay their share of the common meals. More and more land ended up in the hands of a few rich families. And the iron money and the simple life were slowly forgotten. Some Spartans even began to keep gold and silver, secretly.

[pause]

{img:messenian-farmers-free-again-building-new-city-ithome-mountain}
About a hundred and ten years after our story, Sparta lost a great battle to another Greek city, Thebes. After that, the Thebans helped the helots of Messenia to become free. They built a new city on a mountain called Ithome. For the first time in hundreds of years, the people of Messenia could farm their own land for themselves. It was a great day for them. The old stories say they danced in the streets.

[long-pause]

After that, Sparta was never as strong again. But it went on as a city for a very long time. Later, the Romans came and ruled all of Greece. Under the Romans, Sparta became a kind of famous old curiosity. Visitors came from all over the Roman world to see it. They wanted to see the famous Spartan ways, which by then were mostly a show for visitors.

[pause]

{img:roman-era-visitors-sanctuary-artemis-orthia-theatre-seats-watching}
The Romans even built a little theatre beside the sanctuary of Artemis Orthia. Visitors could sit there and watch the old Spartan customs. Rich Romans sent their sons to Sparta to see the training. The Spartans of that time were proud of their past. They kept the old festivals going, and they sang the old songs.

[long-pause]

Much later, the old city slowly emptied. In the Middle Ages, people moved up into the hills, to a new town called Mystras. It was built on a steep slope, with churches and houses and a castle at the top. The old valley was left to the farmers and the olive trees. The old temples crumbled and were covered by earth and grass.

[pause]

{img:mystras-hillside-medieval-churches-castle-above-sparta-valley-later}
But the name of Sparta was never forgotten. People all over the world kept telling the stories. They told of the three hundred at Thermopylae, and of the brave Spartan mothers. They told of the black broth and the iron money and the short, clever sayings. Even today, when someone says that a room is spartan, they mean it is very plain and simple. And when someone gives a short, clever answer, we still say it is laconic. That word comes from Laconia, the valley of Sparta.

[pause]

In the villages of the valley, life went on through all those centuries. Farmers ploughed the fields and picked the olives, as they always had. Children played by the river and splashed in the shallows. And every spring, the poppies came back to the meadows, red and bright, just as they did in the time of Agiatis and Kleo.

[pause]

Many travellers visited the valley over the centuries. Some came to see the ruins, and some came to see the famous river. They sat under the plane trees by the water and ate bread and olives. They looked at the mountains and tried to imagine the old city. And they went home and told their friends about the quiet, beautiful valley where Sparta had been.

[long-pause]

{img:archaeologists-1900s-digging-sanctuary-artemis-orthia-riverbank-tents}
About a hundred and twenty years ago, archaeologists came to Sparta to dig. They wanted to find out what was left of the famous old city.

[pause]

At first, they were a little disappointed. Just as the old visitor from Athens had said, there was very little to see. There were no grand marble temples, and no great palaces. There were only a few low walls and some scattered stones. But they kept digging. And slowly, the ground began to give up its secrets.

[long-pause]

The most exciting place was the sanctuary of Artemis Orthia, by the river. Here the archaeologists found thousands and thousands of small gifts. Families had left them for the goddess over hundreds of years. There were little lead figures, no bigger than a fingertip. They showed soldiers and dancers, horses and deer, wreaths and little animals. There were more than a hundred thousand of them altogether.

[pause]

{img:tiny-lead-figurines-soldiers-dancers-animals-museum-case-later}
There were also clay masks, painted in bright colours. Some were funny, with big noses and wide grins. Some were stern faces of old men. Some looked like monsters. Nobody is quite sure how they were used. Perhaps they were worn in plays or dances at the festivals. Perhaps they were copies of masks that were worn, given as gifts. Looking at them today, you can almost hear the music and the laughter of the old festivals.

[long-pause]

On the hill of the old acropolis, the archaeologists found the remains of the House of Bronze, the temple of Athena. Only the foundations were left. But they found a few small bronze pieces too, little bells and figures and pins. And they found a beautiful marble statue of a Spartan soldier. It is the head and shoulders of a man in a helmet, with a calm face. People called it Leonidas, although nobody knows who it really shows.

[pause]

{img:marble-statue-spartan-warrior-helmet-calm-face-museum-light-later}
Near Amyclae, where the Hyakinthia festival was held, archaeologists found the remains of Apollo's sanctuary. They found pieces of the great throne where his statue once sat. And on a hill east of the river, they found an older shrine, to Menelaus and Helen. These were the king and queen of Sparta in the old stories about the war of Troy. People were still bringing gifts there in the time of our story.

[long-pause]

The archaeologists also found the old theatre, built into the side of a hill. In Roman times, it was one of the biggest theatres in Greece. Today you can still sit on its stone seats and look out over the valley. The mountains rise up in front of you, just as they did two and a half thousand years ago.

[pause]

{img:ancient-theatre-sparta-stone-seats-hillside-view-taygetos-sunset-today}
Most of what we know about daily life in Sparta does not come from digging. It comes from old books, written by Greeks and Romans who visited or heard about Sparta. One of them was a man called Plutarch, who lived about six hundred years after our story. He collected stories about Lycurgus and the old customs. He also collected the famous sayings of the Spartan women. Without him, we would know much less.

[pause]

But we should remember that most of these writers were not Spartans. The Spartans themselves wrote very little down. So we see them mostly through the eyes of outsiders. Some of those outsiders admired Sparta, and some disliked it. The real Sparta was probably somewhere in between. It was a place of strict rules and hard lives, but also of families, music and quiet evenings by the river.

[pause]

The archaeologists lived in tents by the river while they worked. In the mornings, they dug in the cool hours before the heat. At midday, they rested in the shade and ate bread and cheese from the village. In the evenings, they cleaned their finds with soft brushes and wrote careful notes. Local farmers often came by to watch, and sometimes they helped with the digging.

[long-pause]

{img:modern-sparti-town-orange-trees-squares-taygetos-snow-behind-today}
Today, there is a small town in the valley called Sparti. It was built about two hundred years ago, on the place where the old city stood. It is a pleasant, quiet town with wide streets and squares.

[pause]

Orange trees line the streets, and in spring the air smells of their blossom. People sit in cafés in the main square, drinking coffee and talking. Children play on the pavements in the evening. And above the town, the Taygetos mountains rise up, with snow on their peaks in winter. It is the same view the Spartans saw, long ago.

[long-pause]

At the north end of the town, there is a statue of Leonidas. He stands with his shield and his spear, looking out over the valley. Visitors from all over the world come to take photographs of it. Near the statue, there is a small museum. Inside, you can see the little lead figures from the sanctuary, and the painted masks. You can see the marble soldier with the calm face.

[pause]

{img:sparti-museum-visitors-looking-clay-masks-glass-case-quiet-today}
If you walk to the edge of town, you come to the old acropolis hill. It is covered with olive trees now. Among them are the low remains of walls and temples. You can find the stone seats of the theatre, and sit there in the sunshine. Lizards sun themselves on the warm stones. And the only sound is the wind in the olive leaves.

[long-pause]

Down by the river, the sanctuary of Artemis Orthia is quiet and overgrown. There are only a few stones and the seats of the little Roman theatre. The river Eurotas still flows past, clear and cold. Reeds still grow along its banks. And sometimes, people say, you can still see swans there.

[pause]

{img:eurotas-river-today-reeds-plane-trees-quiet-water-evening-light}
The valley around the town is still full of farms. The olive groves stretch for kilometres along the slopes. The farmers still pick the olives in winter, with nets spread under the trees. The olive oil of Laconia is still famous in Greece. There are orange groves too, and vineyards and fields of vegetables. In spring, the meadows are still red with poppies.

[long-pause]

Every year, a great running race is held from Athens to Sparta. It follows the path of a famous messenger from long ago. His name was Pheidippides, and he ran all the way to Sparta to ask for help in a war. It is about two hundred and fifty kilometres. Today, runners from all over the world take part. They run through the night and arrive in Sparta the next day. At the end, they touch the foot of the statue of Leonidas. Then local girls give them a crown of olive leaves and a cup of water.

[pause]

{img:runner-arriving-sparta-statue-leonidas-olive-crown-crowd-dusk-today}
Many of the families in the valley have lived here for a very long time. The grandmothers still tell stories in the evenings, sitting in the doorways. The children still run races along the river. The old men still sit on benches in the sun and watch everything that happens. And in the evening, when the sun goes down behind the Taygetos, the whole valley turns gold, just as it always has.

[pause]

In the evenings, the people of Sparti walk slowly through the streets and squares. This evening walk is an old Greek custom. Families stroll together, greeting their neighbours. Old couples walk arm in arm. Children run ahead and back again. And the smell of grilled food and orange blossom drifts through the warm evening air.

[long-pause]

{img:sparta-valley-evening-golden-light-five-villages-smoke-mountains-480bc}
So we leave Sparta tonight, in the year 479 before Christ, as the evening falls.

[pause]

The sun is setting behind the great mountains to the west. Their long shadows stretch slowly across the valley. The sky turns gold, then pink, then a deep, soft blue. In the five villages, smoke rises from the cooking fires. Lamps are lit in the courtyards, one by one. The swallows are flying low over the river, catching the last insects of the day.

[long-pause]

Out on the farm in the valley, Lykos is walking home from the olive grove. His eldest son walks beside him, carrying the tools. Melitta is standing in the doorway, waiting for them. The little boy is trying to catch a goat that will not come in. Thalia is helping her mother with the soup. The fire glows warm inside the small stone house.

[pause]

{img:helot-farmhouse-evening-fire-glow-family-coming-home-olive-grove}
In the five villages, the men are walking to their common meals, with their cloaks over their shoulders. Damon walks slowly, with his brown dog Argos trotting beside him. His arm is healing well. He stops at the corner and looks back at his house. Then he smiles and walks on.

[pause]

In the training grounds by the river, the boys of the agoge are cutting reeds for their beds. Nikandros is laughing with the other boys. He is not so homesick anymore. In his cloak, he still keeps the smooth river stone with the white line. He touches it sometimes, without thinking. And he hums his song about the river.

[long-pause]

{img:spartan-courtyard-night-lamp-grandmother-girl-mother-quiet-stars}
In the family's courtyard, the lamp is burning beneath the olive tree. The grandmother is sitting on her stool, with her spindle turning slowly in her hand. Kleo sits beside her, practising a new song for the next festival, very softly. Agiatis comes out of the weaving room and sits down with them.
