Sparta is not like other Greek cities. Most Greek cities have strong stone walls all around them. They have a great central square, with fine temples and grand buildings of marble. Athens, for example, is full of beautiful buildings. But Sparta is different.

Sparta has no walls at all. The Spartans say they do not need them. Their soldiers are their walls, they say. And Sparta is not really one city. It is a group of five villages, standing close together in the valley. Each village has its own houses, gardens and little temples. Together, they make Sparta.

--

The buildings of Sparta are quite simple and plain. Houses are made of mud brick on a base of stone, with roofs of red clay tiles. The doors are made of rough wood. There is very little decoration, because the Spartans think that fancy things make people soft. A famous old law says that Spartan houses should be built with only an axe and a saw. No fine tools, and no fine carving.

{img:spartan-simple-house-street-mud-brick-tiled-roof-women-talking}
In the streets, people walk past each other and nod. Women talk at the wells and doorways. Children run and play between the houses. Old men sit on benches in the sun and watch everything. There are no big markets full of luxury goods, like in other cities. There are a few small stalls selling bread and vegetables, pots and simple tools. Life is simple here, and people like it that way.

--

In the middle of the five villages, there is a low hill. On top of it stand the most important temples of Sparta. The greatest is the temple of Athena, the goddess of wisdom. Its walls are decorated with plates of bronze, and so the Spartans call it the House of Bronze. When the sun shines on it, the bronze glows warm and golden.

{img:temple-athena-bronze-house-sparta-hill-sunlight-worshippers-steps}
Below the hill there is an open space called the agora, the meeting place. Here the Spartan citizens gather for their assembly. They stand together and vote on important matters by shouting. Whoever gets the loudest shout wins the vote. It is a noisy way of deciding things. But the Spartans are proud of it.

There are other special places in Sparta too. There is a running track beside the river, where young people race each other. There is a theatre on a hillside, where choirs sing at the festivals. And there are many small shrines, to heroes and to gods, scattered among the houses.

--

Beside the river, a little way from the houses, there is a very old sanctuary. It belongs to Artemis, the goddess of wild animals and of young people. People call her Artemis Orthia here. Spartan families come to this place to pray for their children. They leave small gifts at the altar. There are little lead figures of animals and soldiers and dancers, and small clay masks. Archaeologists later found thousands of these little gifts in the ground.

{img:sanctuary-artemis-orthia-riverside-altar-small-offerings-lead-figures}
A visitor from Athens once said something interesting about Sparta. If Sparta were one day left empty, he said, people in the future would never believe how powerful it had been. There are so few grand buildings, and so little to see. He was right. Today, very little of old Sparta is left. But in its own time, this simple group of villages was one of the strongest places in the whole Greek world.

In the evening, the five villages grow quiet. Lamps are lit in the houses, small flames in clay bowls of oil. The smell of cooking drifts along the streets. And the mountains watch over the valley, dark against the stars.

There is a lovely custom in Sparta about old people. When an old man walks past, the young men stand up to show respect. They step aside to let him pass, and they listen when he speaks. The old people of Sparta are honoured more than in almost any other Greek city. A visitor once said that Sparta was the best place in the world to grow old.
