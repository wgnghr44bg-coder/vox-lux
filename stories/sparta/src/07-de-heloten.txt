Now we must talk about the people who do most of the work in Sparta. They are called helots.

The helots are the farmers of Laconia, and of the land of Messenia over the mountains to the west. Long ago, before the time of our story, the Spartans took over their lands. Since then, the helots have had to work the farms for the Spartans. They are not free. They cannot leave their farms, and they cannot choose a different life. Each year, they must give a large share of their harvest to their Spartan masters.

There are many more helots than Spartans. Perhaps seven helots for every Spartan citizen. It is because of the helots that the Spartan men do not have to farm or work at a trade. They can spend all their time training to be soldiers. Without the helots, Sparta as we know it could not exist.

--

Life for the helots is hard. They work from dawn to dusk in the fields and groves. They plough with oxen, sow the barley, and bring in the harvest. They prune the olive trees and pick the olives in winter. They tend the vines and press the grapes. And they must hand over much of what they grow.

{img:helots-picking-olives-winter-baskets-ladders-grey-sky-valley}
The Spartans did not treat the helots kindly. They looked down on them, and kept them under strict control. They were always a little afraid that the helots might one day rise up against them. And sometimes, over the years, they did. We will not dwell on the hard parts of this story tonight. But we remember the helots with respect, because their lives mattered too.

--

Let us visit the family of Lykos, on Agiatis's farm. Their house is small, made of stone and mud brick, with a roof of reeds. Inside there is one main room, with a hearth in the middle. The family sleeps on mats on the floor. There is a little garden beside the house, where they grow beans and onions and cabbages. And there are a few goats and some chickens, scratching in the yard.

{img:helot-house-interior-hearth-mats-family-meal-lentils-bread-evening}
Lykos has a wife called Melitta, and three children. The eldest is a boy of thirteen, who already works in the fields with his father. The middle one is a girl of ten, who helps her mother in the house and the garden. And the youngest is a little boy of four, who follows the goats around and talks to them.

In the evening, the family eats together around the fire. There is lentil soup and barley bread, and perhaps a few olives. Melitta tells the children stories from the old days. She tells them about Messenia, the land beyond the mountains, where her grandmother's family came from. She tells them about the free farmers who lived there long ago. The children listen quietly in the firelight.

--

The helots have their own festivals and their own gods. They pray to Demeter, the goddess of the harvest, for good crops. They pray to Poseidon, for rain and for safe journeys. They sing their own songs as they work in the fields. Some of these songs are sad, and some are funny. They help to make the long days pass more quickly.

{img:helot-family-praying-small-field-shrine-harvest-offering-sunset}
After the harvest, the helot families gather together to celebrate. They have a feast of bread and cheese and new wine. The children run and play among the threshing floors. The young people dance in a circle to the sound of a flute. And for one evening, the hard work is forgotten.

Lykos sits by the fire with the other men and looks out over the dark valley. He thinks about his son, who is growing strong and tall. He thinks about his little daughter, and about the youngest one, asleep on his mother's lap. He hopes that their lives will be a little easier than his own. He does not know if that will ever be true. But he hopes, as fathers everywhere do. And then he goes to bed, because tomorrow the work begins again.

On winter evenings, Lykos makes things by the fire. He carves wooden spoons and bowls, and mends the handles of the tools. He weaves baskets from the reeds of the river. His eldest son sits beside him and watches, learning. Sometimes the boy makes a little wooden animal for his youngest brother. The little boy carries it everywhere, and sleeps with it under his blanket.
