IMG: rome-seven-hills-tiber-river-winter-morning-44bc | an ancient city of red tiled roofs, temples and columns spread over green hills beside a winding river, thin smoke rising from many hearths, pale blue winter morning sky
HEAD: The Romans did not count years from a starting date, but named each year after two men.
BODY: It is January, more than two thousand years ago, in the city of Rome. We call this year 44 before Christ. But the Romans did not count their years that way. Christ had not been born yet, of course. Instead, they named each year after the two consuls. These were the two chief magistrates, elected for one year at a time. So this year was called the year of the consulship of Caesar and Antony. One of the consuls was Gaius Julius Caesar himself, for the fifth time. The other was his friend and follower, Mark Antony. If you asked a Roman how old a building was, he might say it was built when so-and-so were consuls. Then he would have to work out the rest in his head. It sounds complicated to us. Yet it made sense to them. Every year was a little piece of history, with two names attached to it.

IMG: roof-tiles-frost-smoke-rising-hills-pale-sky | frost on red clay roof tiles in an ancient city at dawn, thin columns of wood smoke rising into a pale clear sky, hills in the distance
HEAD: Winter in Rome was mild, and people warmed their hands over little bowls of glowing charcoal.
BODY: The winter in Rome was mild, compared with the winters of the north. There was rain, and sometimes a cold wind blew down from the mountains. The river Tiber rose and flooded the low parts of the city. On some mornings, there was frost on the roof tiles. People's breath steamed in the air. But snow was rare. The days were often bright and clear, with a pale blue sky over the hills. Smoke rose from thousands of hearths. In the evening, families gathered around their braziers. A brazier was a small iron bowl full of glowing charcoal. You held your hands over it to warm them. Then people ate supper by the light of little clay oil lamps. Each lamp had a single small flame, flickering at the spout. Can you picture it? A small room, a bowl of red embers, and the soft yellow glow of a lamp on a wooden table. That was a winter evening in Rome.

HEAD: At the heart of Rome lay the Forum, a great open square where the whole city came to talk.
BODY: Let's climb one of the hills early in the morning and look down. Below us, in the valley between the hills, is the Forum. It was the great open square at the heart of Rome. Temples surrounded it, with tall columns and red tiled roofs. There were great halls called basilicas. Inside them, the law courts met and the merchants did business. In the middle stood the Rostra. This was a stone platform from which politicians spoke to the people. It was decorated with the bronze beaks of enemy warships, captured long ago. The word rostra means beaks. Around the Forum, the city spread out on every side. There was a sea of red roofs and narrow winding streets. There were tall apartment houses and small gardens, baths, markets and workshops. Even at dawn, the square was beginning to fill. Someone was always there, talking, arguing or simply watching the world go by.

HEAD: On the Capitoline Hill stood the temple of Jupiter, with a roof that shone like gold.
BODY: On the Capitoline Hill, above the Forum, stood the greatest temple in Rome. It was the Temple of Jupiter, the king of the gods. Its roof was covered with gilded tiles that shone in the morning sun. You could see it from all over the city. On the Palatine Hill, on the other side, were the fine houses of the rich and powerful. They had gardens and colonnades and looked down on the Forum. Beyond the Palatine, in a long valley, lay the Circus Maximus. This was the great racecourse, where chariot races were held. It could hold perhaps a hundred and fifty thousand people. Around the whole city ran the old walls. They had been built hundreds of years before. The city had long since grown past them. Houses, gardens and tombs now spread far beyond the gates. Rome had simply outgrown its own walls, like a child outgrowing a coat.

IMG: crowded-roman-street-people-many-lands-morning | a busy ancient street with people of many lands in tunics, cloaks and colourful robes, merchants with baskets, a donkey carrying jars, soft morning light
HEAD: Rome was the biggest city in the Western world, and in its streets you could hear a dozen languages.
BODY: How many people lived in Rome in 44 before Christ? Nobody knows exactly. Historians think there were perhaps three quarters of a million, or even close to a million. It was by far the largest city in the Western world. Only a few cities in China may have been bigger. And people came to Rome from everywhere. There were Romans and Italians, of course. But there were also Greeks, Syrians and Jews. There were Egyptians, Gauls, Spaniards and Africans. There were people from every land that Rome had conquered or traded with. Some were free, and some were enslaved. In the streets, you could hear a dozen languages. Latin and Greek were heard most of all. But you might also hear Aramaic, Gaulish and many others. Imagine walking down a single street and hearing the whole world talking. Rome was not one people. It was many peoples, crowded together on seven hills.

HEAD: Grain from Egypt, wine from Spain and marble from Greece all flowed into Rome.
BODY: Rome had grown rich and powerful by conquest. For hundreds of years, Roman armies had marched out. First they conquered the other peoples of Italy. Then they took the lands around the Mediterranean Sea. They took Spain, North Africa and Greece. They took Asia Minor and Syria. And in the last few years, Caesar himself had conquered the whole of Gaul. That is the land we now call France and Belgium. He had even crossed the sea, twice, to the misty island of Britain. The wealth of all these lands flowed into Rome. Grain came from Egypt, Sicily and Africa. Wine and olive oil came from Spain and Greece. Marble came from Greek quarries. Gold and silver, works of art and spices from the East all poured in. So did captives, sold as slaves. Rome had become the centre of its world. Yet all that wealth had come at a great price, paid by many other peoples.

HEAD: For a hundred years, the great men of Rome had quarrelled and fought for power.
BODY: Rome was also a city that had lived through terrible times. For about a hundred years, its great men had quarrelled and fought for power. There had been riots in the streets. There had been civil wars, with Romans fighting against Romans. Just a few years earlier, Caesar had fought a great civil war of his own. His rival was Pompey the Great, supported by much of the Senate. Caesar had won. Now he ruled Rome. Most ordinary people hoped there would be peace at last. They were tired of fighting. They wanted to work, eat, raise their children and go to the races. But some of the great men of the Senate were not happy. They felt that the old ways of Rome were slipping away. And as the winter turned to spring, something was going to happen in this city. It would be remembered for more than two thousand years. But that is still far ahead. For now, let's simply walk, and look.

TOPIC: Who was Julius Caesar?
BRIDGE: The lamps in the Forum are flickering. Before we walk on, let's meet the man whose name was on everyone's lips.

IMG: julius-caesar-portrait-laurel-wreath-toga-calm | a tall slim Roman man of about fifty-five with dark eyes and a high forehead, wearing a wreath of laurel leaves and a white toga, calm thoughtful face, soft golden light
HEAD: Caesar was going bald, and he liked to wear a wreath of laurel leaves to hide it.
BODY: In the winter of 44 before Christ, Julius Caesar was fifty-five years old. He was tall, slim and pale. He had dark, piercing eyes and a high forehead. And he was going bald, which he was rather sensitive about. The Senate had given him the right to wear a wreath of laurel leaves at all times. The ancient writers say he was very glad of it. It covered the top of his head nicely. He dressed carefully, and a little unusually. He wore a loose belt and long sleeves, fringed at the wrists. Some old-fashioned Romans thought this looked careless, even a bit dangerous. Yet people who met him said he had great charm. He could be warm and funny. He remembered names and faces. He could talk to a general or a baker with the same ease. Statues of him still exist. If you look closely, you can see a thin, clever face, a little tired, with lines around the eyes.

IMG: subura-crowded-street-shops-apartments | a crowded narrow ancient street lined with small shops and tall apartment houses, laundry hanging from windows, children and traders, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Caesar's family claimed to descend from the goddess Venus, yet he grew up in a noisy, crowded neighbourhood.
BODY: Caesar was born in the year 100 before Christ, in Rome. His family was old and noble. They were called the Julii. They claimed to be descended from the goddess Venus herself. The story went that her grandson, Iulus, had escaped from the burning city of Troy. Yet in Caesar's childhood, the family was not rich, or very powerful. He grew up in an ordinary part of Rome called the Subura. It was a crowded, noisy neighbourhood, full of shops, workshops and tall apartment houses. Street sellers shouted their wares. Children played in the lanes. Neighbours argued from their windows. Perhaps this is why Caesar always understood the common people of Rome. He had lived among them as a boy. He knew how they talked and what they worried about. And perhaps that is also why so many of them loved him. To them, he was not a distant lord. He was a boy from the Subura who had done well.

IMG: roman-matron-mother-teaching-boy-courtyard | a dignified Roman woman in a long draped dress sitting with a young boy in a quiet house courtyard with a small fountain, scrolls beside them, soft morning light
HEAD: Caesar's mother, Aurelia, raised him alone and stayed one of the most important people in his life.
BODY: Caesar's father died when the boy was fifteen. After that, his mother brought him up. Her name was Aurelia. She was a wise and strong woman, and very well respected in Rome. The ancient writers praise her good sense. She ran the household, looked after the family's money and kept a close eye on her son. Even when Caesar was a grown man and an important politician, he lived with her for many years. She helped to manage his household. She received his visitors and kept his secrets. She was one of the most important people in his life. When she died, about ten years before our story, Caesar was far away, fighting in Gaul. He could not come home for her funeral. We do not know exactly how he felt. But his friends knew he had lost his greatest supporter. Behind many famous men, there is someone like Aurelia, quietly holding everything together.

HEAD: Caesar once owed so much money that he could not leave Rome until a rich friend promised to pay.
BODY: As a young man, Caesar was clever, charming and very ambitious. He was a brilliant speaker, one of the best in Rome. He also wrote well, in a clear, plain, simple Latin. Schoolchildren still read his books today. He was very generous too. He spent huge sums on games, shows and feasts for the people, to win their favour. Much of that money was borrowed. At one point, he owed so much that his creditors would not let him leave the city. He was about to go and govern a province in Spain. Only when the richest man in Rome, Marcus Crassus, promised to pay his debts could Caesar set off. It was a big gamble. But Caesar was a gambler by nature. He believed that if he spent boldly now, he would win it all back later. And, as it turned out, he did. Would you have lent him money? Many sensible Romans would have said no. Some of them later wished they had said yes.

HEAD: When pirates captured the young Caesar, he told them his ransom was far too low.
BODY: There is a famous story about Caesar as a young man. When he was about twenty-five, he sailed for the island of Rhodes, in Greece, to study. On the way, pirates captured his ship. They held him prisoner on a small island and demanded a ransom of twenty talents of silver. Caesar laughed. He said he was worth at least fifty. So they asked for fifty instead. While he waited for the money to arrive, he made himself quite at home. He joined in the pirates' games and exercises. He read them his poems and speeches. If they did not applaud, he called them uneducated. When he wanted to sleep, he sent word for them to be quiet. And he told them, laughing, that one day he would come back and punish them. They thought it was a joke. It was not. When the ransom was paid and he was set free, he gathered some ships, sailed back and captured them, just as he had promised.

HEAD: As chief priest of Rome, Caesar was given a fine house right in the middle of the Forum.
BODY: Step by step, Caesar rose to the top of Roman politics. He was elected to one office after another. He became Pontifex Maximus, the chief priest of Rome. It was a great honour, and it lasted for life. It also gave him a fine official house, right in the Forum, on the oldest street in the city. Then he became governor of part of Spain. In 59 before Christ, he was elected consul, the highest office in the Republic. After that, he became governor of Gaul. For nine years, he fought there at the head of his legions. He conquered the whole country. He became enormously rich and enormously famous. His soldiers loved him. He shared their hardships, knew many of them by name, and called them comrades. Yet back in Rome, his enemies in the Senate grew afraid. They thought he was becoming far too powerful. And they whispered that he wanted to make himself king.

IMG: river-rubicon-small-stream-winter-soldiers-crossing | a small quiet river in winter countryside with soldiers in red cloaks wading across a shallow ford, bare trees on the banks, grey early morning light
HEAD: Rome's war began when Caesar led his army across a small river called the Rubicon.
BODY: The Romans hated the very idea of a king. Hundreds of years earlier, they had driven out their last king, Tarquin the Proud. They had set up the Republic instead. In the Republic, the people elected their leaders every year, and no one man could hold power for long. For the Romans, the word for king, rex, was one of the most hated words in their language. In January of 49 before Christ, the Senate ordered Caesar to give up his army. He was to return to Rome as an ordinary citizen. Caesar knew what that meant. His enemies would put him on trial, and his career would be over. So instead, he led his soldiers across a little river called the Rubicon. It was the border between his province and Italy. Crossing it with an army was an act of war. As he crossed, he is supposed to have said: the die is cast. We still say crossing the Rubicon today, for a choice that cannot be undone.

HEAD: Caesar was proud of forgiving his enemies, and some of them could not bear it.
BODY: The civil war lasted four years. It was fought all around the Mediterranean. Caesar's great rival, Pompey, was defeated in Greece. He fled to Egypt, where he was killed by the advisers of the young king there. When Caesar arrived and heard what had happened, it is said that he turned away and wept. Then Caesar won battle after battle. After one quick victory in Asia, he sent home a famous message of just three words. Veni, vidi, vici. I came, I saw, I conquered. At last, in 45 before Christ, the war was over. Caesar came home as master of the Roman world. The Senate made him dictator for life. Yet he was not cruel, for a Roman of his time. He forgave many of his old enemies. He let them come home and keep their property. He even gave some of them important jobs. He was proud of this mercy. The Romans called it clementia. But some of the men he forgave did not want to owe him their lives.

TOPIC: An ordinary day in Rome
BRIDGE: Enough of generals and great men, for a while. Let's spend one ordinary winter day in the city, from first light to the last lamp.

IMG: dawn-roman-street-shutters-opening-shops-bakers | an ancient street at dawn with bakers taking round loaves from a brick oven, a donkey turning a stone mill, shopkeepers lifting wooden shutters, soft early light
HEAD: In Rome, an hour was longer in summer than in winter.
BODY: The day in Rome began very early, before dawn. The Romans had no clocks like ours. They divided the daylight into twelve hours, from sunrise to sunset. So in winter, the hours were short, and in summer they were long. A winter hour might last only forty-five minutes. A summer hour might last an hour and a quarter. Most people got up at first light, or even earlier. In the dark, the bakers were already at work. Their bakeries stood on street corners. Donkeys walked round and round, turning great stone mills to grind the grain. The bakers shaped round loaves, marked into sections like a wheel, and baked them in brick ovens. Soon the smell of warm bread drifted through the streets. Shopkeepers took down the wooden shutters from the front of their shops. The shops opened straight onto the street. And slowly, the streets began to fill with people, voices and the clatter of the new day.

IMG: roman-school-arcade-children-wax-tablets-teacher | children sitting on benches in an open arcade beside a street with small wooden wax tablets on their knees, a teacher in a simple tunic beside them, soft morning light
HEAD: The schools met in open arcades, and the neighbours complained about the children chanting.
BODY: As the light grew, the schoolmasters opened their schools. Most schools were not buildings at all. They were simply a corner of an open arcade along the street, behind a curtain. The children arrived early, carrying their wax tablets. A wax tablet was a small wooden board covered in a thin layer of wax. You wrote on it by scratching with a pointed stylus. The other end of the stylus was flat, for smoothing out mistakes. Boys and girls learned reading, writing and counting. They chanted their lessons out loud, all together. They learned long passages of poetry by heart. The neighbours often complained about the noise. One Roman poet grumbled that schoolmasters woke him before the roosters did. The teachers were often Greek freedmen, poorly paid and rather strict. Lessons ran from dawn until about midday. Then the children were free to run home through the busy streets.

HEAD: Every morning, poorer Romans put on their togas and went to say good morning to a great man.
BODY: In the houses of the rich, the day began with the morning greeting. Every important Roman had his clients. These were poorer men who depended on him for help, protection and small gifts of money or food. In return, they supported him in politics. They followed him through the streets and voted for him in elections. So every morning at dawn, the clients put on their togas. They walked to the great man's house and waited in his entrance hall to wish him good morning. The more clients a man had, the more important he looked. The greatest men, like Caesar, had hundreds. Their halls were packed with people every morning. It could be a dull business for the clients. They stood around, yawning and gossiping, waiting their turn. Afterwards, some of them walked across the city to greet a second patron, and a third. A clever client could collect several breakfasts' worth of gifts in one morning.

IMG: atrium-ancestor-masks-shrine-lares-pool | the tall hall of a Roman house with wax masks of ancestors in small wooden cupboards, a little household shrine with a lamp, a shallow pool beneath a roof opening, warm soft light
HEAD: In the entrance hall of a Roman house, the faces of the ancestors looked down from little cupboards.
BODY: The great hall of a Roman house was called the atrium. It was a tall room with an opening in the middle of the roof. This let in light and rain. Beneath it lay a shallow pool in the floor, to catch the rainwater. Around the walls stood little wooden cupboards. Inside them were wax masks of the family's ancestors. Each had a label telling which offices that person had held. On special days, the family took the masks out and wore them in processions. There was also a small shrine to the household gods, the Lares. The family made offerings there every day. A little bread, a drop of wine, a pinch of incense. Beyond the atrium lay a garden with a covered walk around it. It had flowers, fountains and statues. Around the garden were the family's rooms. The bedrooms were small and dark. The kitchen was simple. In a house like this, you lived with your ancestors watching kindly over your shoulder.

HEAD: In the Forum, bankers changed money, lawyers made speeches and idle men gossiped about Caesar.
BODY: After the morning greeting, the great man went down to the Forum. His clients followed behind him like a little parade. The Forum in the morning was busy and noisy. The law courts met in the basilicas. Lawyers made long speeches. Crowds gathered to listen, to cheer and to jeer. Bankers sat at small tables, changing money and lending it. Politicians stood on the temple steps, talking to friends and shaking hands with voters. There were fortune tellers and letter writers. There were pickpockets, too, slipping quietly through the crowd. And everywhere, there were idle men, standing around and gossiping. What was the latest news? What would Caesar do next? Who was in favour, and who was out? Rumours flew from one end of the square to the other. A story told at breakfast could be all over the city by lunch. In a way, the Forum was Rome's newspaper, its stock market and its law court, all at once.

HEAD: By midday, the work of the day was mostly done, and many Romans took a nap.
BODY: At about midday, the work of the day was mostly finished. The Romans ate a light lunch. It might be bread and cheese and olives. It might be cold meat, or leftovers from the night before. Then many of them lay down for a short nap. In summer, the heat made this a necessity. Even in winter, it was a pleasant habit. The streets grew quieter. Shutters were half closed. A dog slept beside a stone fountain, and water trickled gently into its basin. Somewhere a pigeon cooed on a rooftop. Then, in the afternoon, the city woke again. People headed for the baths, which were one of the great pleasures of Roman life. We'll visit them a little later. In the late afternoon, they ate the main meal of the day. It was called the cena. For ordinary people, it was simple. A bowl of porridge, or a stew of beans and lentils, with bread and a little wine mixed with water.

HEAD: Caesar banned heavy carts by day, so all night long, wagons rumbled through the streets.
BODY: As darkness fell, Rome went to bed. The streets had no lights at all, except for little lamps in the windows. Rich men coming home from dinner had slaves walking ahead with torches, to light the way. Most sensible people stayed indoors at night. The dark streets were not safe. Yet Rome was never quite silent. Caesar himself had made a law that heavy carts could not enter the city during the day. The streets were simply too crowded. So the carts came at night instead. All night long, wagons rumbled through the narrow streets. They carried stone, timber, wine and grain. The drivers shouted. The oxen bellowed. The wheels crunched over the paving stones. And the people in the apartments above lay awake and complained, as people in cities always will. One Roman poet wrote that in Rome, only the very rich could afford to sleep. Do you sleep well in a city? Many Romans did not.

TOPIC: Dinner on three couches
BRIDGE: Not every evening ended with a bowl of lentils, though. Let's put on our softest slippers and go out to dinner at a rich man's house.

IMG: triclinium-dining-room-painted-walls-three-couches | a Roman dining room with three wide couches around a low table, walls painted with gardens and birds in red and yellow, bronze oil lamps on tall stands, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: At a Roman dinner party, nine guests lay on three couches, leaning on their left elbows.
BODY: A dinner party began in the late afternoon, while it was still light. The guests arrived freshly bathed, in light dinner clothes. Their slaves took off their shoes at the door, washed their feet and gave them soft slippers. The dining room was called the triclinium, which means three couches. Three wide couches stood around three sides of a low square table. The fourth side was left open, so the servants could bring the food. On each couch, three people lay side by side. They propped themselves on cushions and leaned on their left elbows. That made nine guests in all. Every place was arranged by importance. The guest of honour had a special place at the corner of the middle couch. The walls were painted with gardens, birds and landscapes, in bright reds and yellows. Bronze oil lamps hung from tall stands and gave a soft, flickering light. Have you ever tried eating lying down? The Romans thought it was the most relaxing way of all.

IMG: roman-starters-eggs-olives-oysters-silver-plates | silver plates on a low table with boiled eggs, olives, oysters, lettuce and small sausages, a jug of honeyed wine beside them, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: The Romans had a saying for a long dinner: from the egg to the apples.
BODY: The dinner had three parts. First came the starters. There were eggs and olives, lettuce and mushrooms. There were oysters, little salted fish and sausages. With them, the guests drank a sweet wine mixed with honey, called mulsum. The Romans had a saying about a long meal. They called it from the egg to the apples. That was because dinner began with eggs and ended with fruit. You might use the saying the way we say from start to finish. Then came the main courses. There might be roast pork, kid or hare. There might be chicken, duck, or fish of many kinds. The rich especially loved a fish called grey mullet. They paid huge sums for a big one. On special occasions, there were stranger dishes. There might be dormice, rolled in honey and poppy seeds. There might be a whole roast boar, or even a peacock, served with its tail feathers spread out like a fan.

IMG: clay-amphorae-garum-jars-kitchen-shelf-herbs | tall clay jars and small pots on a wooden kitchen shelf in a Roman kitchen, bundles of dried herbs hanging, a mortar and pestle, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: Romans poured a sauce made from fish left in the sun for weeks onto almost everything.
BODY: Almost everything at a Roman dinner was flavoured with a sauce called garum. It was made from small fish, salted and left in the sun for weeks, in big jars. Slowly, the fish turned into a clear, salty, brown liquid. It smelled very strong while it was being made. In fact, it smelled so strong that garum factories had to be outside the towns. But the Romans loved it. They put it on everything, the way we use salt or soy sauce. The best garum came from Spain, and it was very expensive. Roman cooks also used a great deal of honey. They used pepper, which came all the way from India. They used herbs like lovage, coriander, cumin and mint. Romans liked strong flavours, sweet and sour and salty, all together. A Roman cookbook has survived from a little later. Some people today try its recipes at home. They say the results are surprisingly good, if you are brave.

HEAD: At a Roman dinner there were no forks, no tomatoes and no sugar.
BODY: Last came the dessert. There were apples and pears, figs and grapes, dates and nuts. There were little honey cakes, and cheese. Now think about what was missing. There were no tomatoes or potatoes. There was no chocolate, coffee or tea. There was no sugar. All of these came from lands the Romans had never heard of, or that were far too distant. And there were no forks. The Romans ate with their fingers, with spoons and with little knives. Servants brought bowls of water and napkins so guests could wash their hands between courses. Guests often brought their own napkins. At the end of the evening, they wrapped up leftovers in them to take home. The wine was always mixed with water. In winter, it was sometimes mixed with hot water. Only barbarians, the Romans believed, drank their wine unmixed. It is a nice thought, that someone two thousand years ago also took home a doggy bag.

HEAD: While they ate, the guests listened to music, poetry, or a clever servant telling jokes.
BODY: While they ate, the guests were entertained. There might be a musician playing the lyre or the double pipes. There might be a servant reading aloud from a book of poems or history. There might be dancers from Spain, clicking castanets. Or the guests themselves might sing, or recite poetry. But the most important part of all was the conversation. They talked about philosophy and books. They talked about history, the news of the day and the gossip of the city. In the winter of 44, one subject came up at every table. Caesar. What would he do next? Would he make himself king? People lowered their voices when they said it. You never quite knew who was listening. A dinner party could last for hours, deep into the night. HEAD: When Caesar came to dinner at Cicero's house, he brought two thousand soldiers with him.
BODY: There is a lovely story about one dinner party. In December of 45, Caesar came to dinner at Cicero's country house by the sea. Cicero was the most famous speaker in Rome, and a wonderful writer of letters. Caesar did not arrive alone. He brought about two thousand soldiers with him. They all had to be fed, and they camped in the fields around the house. Cicero must have been quite worried. Yet the evening went very well. Caesar had a bath and a massage. Then he lay down to dinner and ate and drank with great enjoyment. They talked about books and literature, and not about politics at all. Afterwards, Cicero wrote to his best friend about it. It had been a pleasant evening, he wrote. But Caesar was not the kind of guest to whom you say: do drop in again, whenever you are passing. You can almost hear him sighing with relief as he wrote it.
