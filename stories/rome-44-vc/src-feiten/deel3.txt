HEAD: Before Caesar, the Roman calendar had drifted nearly three months out of step with the seasons.
BODY: Before Caesar, the Roman calendar was a terrible muddle. You might celebrate a harvest festival when nothing was ripe. The old Roman year had only three hundred and fifty-five days. That's ten days shorter than the real year, the time it takes the Earth to go around the sun. To keep the calendar in step, the priests were supposed to add an extra month every two years or so. But the priests were also politicians. They added the extra month, or left it out, as it suited them and their friends. They might make a year longer when a friend was in office, or shorter when an enemy was. During the civil wars, the extra months were forgotten altogether. By the time Caesar came to power, the calendar was nearly three months wrong. The harvest festivals came before the crops were ripe. The spring festivals came in the middle of winter. Imagine celebrating the first flowers of spring, wrapped in your warmest cloak, while frost lay on the roofs.

IMG: sosigenes-astronomer-instruments-night-sky-roof | an old astronomer in Greek robes on a flat rooftop at night with simple bronze instruments, looking up at a sky full of stars, a small lamp beside him
HEAD: With an astronomer from Alexandria, Caesar created the calendar we still use today, leap years and all.
BODY: So Caesar decided to fix it. You're using his calendar right now, every time you check the date. With the help of an astronomer from Alexandria named Sosigenes, he made a new calendar based on the sun. The year would have three hundred and sixty-five days, in twelve months. And every fourth year, one extra day would be added, in February. That made up for the quarter of a day left over each year. It was the first leap year. This is the calendar we still use today, almost unchanged. About sixteen hundred years later, a pope named Gregory made a small correction. Caesar's year was still a few minutes too long, and over the centuries those minutes had added up. But the calendar on your wall, or on your phone, is still essentially Caesar's calendar. Isn't that remarkable? Every birthday you have ever had, every holiday and every Monday morning, has been counted in a way that was worked out in Caesar's Rome.

BODY: The Romans believed in signs and omens. They believed the gods told people what was coming through the flight of birds, thunder, dreams and strange events. HEAD: Every day, people still leave fresh flowers for Caesar in the Roman Forum.
BODY: A little further on, in the Roman Forum, among the ruins of temples and halls, lie the remains of the Temple of the Divine Julius. You can leave one yourself, if you're ever there. It was built by Augustus, on the spot where the people of Rome had burned Caesar's body. There isn't much left of it now. There's only a low platform of rough stone. In a small half-round niche, under a simple roof, are the remains of the altar. Yet every day, people still leave flowers there. There are fresh roses and lilies, wreaths, little notes and coins. They leave them for Caesar, more than two thousand years later. And every year on the Ides of March, people gather there to lay wreaths and remember him. Caesar's name lived on in other ways too. It became the title of the Roman emperors. Much later, it became Kaiser in German and Tsar in Russian. His calendar still counts our days. Isn't it moving, after all this time? And his month still brings us summer.
