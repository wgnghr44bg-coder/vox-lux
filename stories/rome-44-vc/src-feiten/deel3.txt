TOPIC: A new calendar and great plans
===
IMG: astronomers-sundial-scrolls-calendar-tablets-courtyard | scholars in Greek robes studying a sundial and spreading scrolls and wax tablets on a table in a sunny courtyard, columns behind them, soft morning light
HEAD: Before Caesar's reform, the Roman calendar had drifted almost three months out of step with the seasons.
BODY: The old Roman calendar had only three hundred and fifty-five days. To keep it in line with the seasons, priests were supposed to add an extra month every few years. This task belonged to the college of priests, led by the Pontifex Maximus. But the priests did not always add the month at the right time. Sometimes they added it to lengthen the term of their political friends, or forgot it during times of war. By the 40s before Christ, the calendar was badly out of step. Festivals that were meant to celebrate the harvest fell at the wrong time of year. Historians know about this problem from writers such as Suetonius and Censorinus, who explained how the calendar worked. Even so, the old system had served Rome for centuries. Caesar, as chief priest, had the authority to change it. After his victories in the civil war, he finally had the time and power to carry out a complete reform.
===
IMG: sosigenes-astronomer-instruments-night-sky-roof | an old astronomer in Greek robes on a flat rooftop at night with simple bronze instruments, looking up at a sky full of stars, a small lamp beside him
HEAD: With the help of an astronomer from Alexandria, Caesar created the calendar that is still the basis of the one used today.
BODY: Caesar consulted the Alexandrian astronomer Sosigenes, who based the new calendar on the length of the solar year, the time the Earth takes to travel around the Sun. The new year had three hundred and sixty-five days, with one extra day added every fourth year. This extra day is the origin of the leap year. The new calendar began on the first of January, 45 before Christ. Historians call it the Julian calendar, after Caesar's family name. It was much more accurate than the old one. Even so, it was not perfect. The solar year is slightly shorter than three hundred and sixty-five and a quarter days, so the Julian calendar slowly drifted again, by about one day every hundred and twenty-eight years. In 1582, Pope Gregory the Thirteenth introduced a small correction. The result, the Gregorian calendar, is the one used in most of the world today. Its months and leap years still follow Caesar's design closely.
===
IMG: roman-family-counting-days-calendar-wall | a Roman family in a lamplit room looking at a painted calendar of days on the wall, the father pointing and the children laughing, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm soft light
HEAD: To catch up with the seasons, Caesar made the year 46 before Christ four hundred and forty-five days long.
BODY: Before the new calendar could begin, the old one had to be brought back into line with the seasons. To do this, Caesar made the year 46 before Christ extraordinarily long. He added the usual extra month, plus two more months between November and December. The year lasted four hundred and forty-five days in total. Romans later called it the year of confusion. Historians know about it from several ancient writers, including Macrobius and Censorinus. People had to adjust contracts, rents and plans to a year that was almost three months longer than normal. Some Romans grumbled. According to Plutarch, when someone remarked that a certain star would rise the next day, Cicero joked that it would do so by order, meaning that even the stars now obeyed Caesar. Even so, the reform was a lasting success. From 45 before Christ onward, the seasons and the calendar stayed in step, and festivals fell once more at the right times of the year.
===
IMG: architects-plans-model-temple-table-workshop | architects in tunics leaning over large plans and a small wooden model of a temple in a sunny workshop, measuring tools on the table, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: Caesar planned Rome's first public library and a canal through Greece that was only completed in 1893.
BODY: Caesar had many ambitious plans for Rome and the empire. According to Suetonius, he wanted to build a huge temple of Mars and a great theatre at the foot of the Capitoline Hill. He planned Rome's first public library and asked the scholar Marcus Terentius Varro to collect Greek and Latin books for it. He also planned to drain marshes near Rome, to improve harbours and to collect Roman laws into a clear code. One of his most remarkable ideas was to cut a canal through the narrow isthmus of Corinth in Greece. Earlier rulers had also considered it, and later the emperor Nero began digging. Historians note that the canal was finally completed only in 1893. Even so, most of Caesar's plans were never finished, because he died soon afterwards. Some were later carried out by Augustus. The first public library in Rome was opened a few years after Caesar's death by one of his supporters, Gaius Asinius Pollio.
===
IMG: forum-of-caesar-new-paving-colonnades-equestrian-statue | a long new city square paved with pale stone and lined with colonnades and shops, an elegant temple at the far end, a bronze statue of a rider on a horse in front, bright morning light
HEAD: In 44 before Christ, Caesar was preparing to leave Rome for a great war in the East, planned for the eighteenth of March.
BODY: One of Caesar's building projects was already in use in 44 before Christ. It was the Forum of Caesar, a new square next to the old Forum, dedicated in 46 before Christ. It was surrounded by covered walkways and shops. At one end stood the Temple of Venus Genetrix, Venus the Mother, honouring the goddess from whom Caesar's family claimed descent. In front of the temple stood a statue of Caesar on horseback. Meanwhile, Caesar was planning a major military campaign. He intended to attack the Parthian Empire in the East, which had defeated a Roman army under Crassus in 53 before Christ. Historians believe he also planned to campaign against the Dacians near the Danube. His departure was set for the eighteenth of March. Even so, many senators feared that, once away with his armies, Caesar would become even more powerful. Some believed that time was running out if anything was to be done. The remains of the Forum of Caesar can still be seen in Rome today.

TOPIC: Brutus, Cassius and the Lupercalia
===
IMG: marcus-brutus-serious-roman-reading-philosophy-lamp | a serious Roman man of about forty in a simple toga reading a scroll by the light of an oil lamp in a quiet study, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: Marcus Junius Brutus was a serious, studious senator, respected in Rome for his honesty.
BODY: Marcus Junius Brutus was about forty years old in 44 before Christ. He was known as a thoughtful man who loved philosophy and wrote books himself. His family claimed descent from Lucius Junius Brutus, who, according to tradition, had driven out the last king of Rome and founded the Republic in 509 before Christ. This history weighed heavily on him. During the civil war, Brutus had fought on Pompey's side. After Pompey's defeat at Pharsalus, Caesar pardoned him and gave him important positions. In 44 before Christ, Brutus held the office of praetor, one of the senior magistrates. Historians know about him from Plutarch's biography, from Cicero's letters and from his coins. Even so, his character is difficult to judge. Some ancient writers saw him as a principled defender of liberty, while others saw ingratitude. Plutarch wrote that even his enemies believed he acted from honest motives. That reputation made him very valuable to anyone planning action against Caesar.
===
IMG: statue-capitol-night-small-notes-scrolls | an old bronze statue of a Roman man standing on a stone base on a hill at night, small scraps of writing tucked at its foot, moonlight on the bronze, quiet empty square
HEAD: According to ancient writers, notes were left for Brutus asking whether he was asleep.
BODY: Brutus's mother was Servilia, a clever and well-connected noblewoman. For many years, she had been one of the women Caesar loved most, and Caesar treated Brutus with great favour. Some ancient gossip even suggested that Caesar might be Brutus's father, though historians generally consider this unlikely, given their ages. In early 44 before Christ, according to Plutarch and Suetonius, notes began to appear for Brutus. Some were written on the statue of his famous ancestor, the founder of the Republic. Others were left on the seat where Brutus sat as praetor. They contained short messages, such as Brutus, are you asleep, and you are not a true Brutus. The aim was to remind him of his family's history and push him into action. Historians note that these notes may have been placed by people who wanted him to join a plot. Even so, they show the pressure on Brutus. He was respected, and his involvement would make any action seem honourable rather than selfish.
===
IMG: roman-woman-noble-lamp-thoughtful-night | a dignified Roman woman in a long dark dress sitting by a small lamp at night, looking thoughtful and determined, a small writing table beside her, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft warm light
HEAD: Brutus's wife, Porcia, was the daughter of Cato, one of Caesar's most stubborn opponents.
BODY: Brutus was married to Porcia, the daughter of Marcus Porcius Cato. Cato had been Caesar's most determined opponent in the Senate. He refused to accept Caesar's pardon and died in North Africa in 46 before Christ rather than live under Caesar's rule. Porcia was known for her strong character, like her father. According to Plutarch, she noticed that Brutus was troubled and could not sleep. To prove that she could be trusted with a secret, she made a deep cut in her thigh with a small knife and bore the pain in silence. Only then did Brutus tell her about the plot. Historians cannot confirm this story, but it was widely repeated in ancient times. Even so, it shows how Romans imagined the courage of noble women. Porcia was one of the few people outside the conspiracy who knew what was planned. According to later sources, she was so anxious on the morning of the Ides of March that she fainted.
===
IMG: cassius-lean-roman-soldier-senator-intense-gaze | a lean Roman man with a sharp, serious face in a dark cloak standing in a shadowy colonnade, arms folded, watchful eyes, soft evening light
HEAD: Gaius Cassius Longinus, Brutus's brother-in-law, was one of the main organisers of the plot against Caesar.
BODY: Gaius Cassius Longinus was an experienced soldier. In 53 before Christ, he had served under Crassus in the disastrous campaign against Parthia and had led the survivors to safety. Later he fought for Pompey in the civil war, but, like Brutus, he was pardoned by Caesar. He was married to Junia Tertia, Brutus's half-sister. Ancient writers describe Cassius as sharp, proud and quick-tempered. Historians believe he was one of the first to plan the conspiracy and that he worked to win Brutus over. Some ancient writers suggested personal grievances, such as disappointment at not receiving higher office. Others believed he was genuinely committed to the Republic. Even so, Cassius understood that Brutus's reputation was essential. A plot led by Cassius alone might look like personal ambition. With Brutus, it could be presented as a defence of liberty. Shakespeare later famously described Cassius as having a lean and hungry look, drawing on Plutarch's portrait.
===
IMG: two-men-walking-garden-path-dusk-quiet-talk | two Roman men in togas walking slowly side by side along a garden path at dusk, talking quietly, tall cypress trees and a small fountain, soft blue evening light
HEAD: More than sixty senators joined the conspiracy against Caesar, including several of his own friends.
BODY: According to Suetonius, more than sixty men took part in the plot against Caesar. Some were former supporters of Pompey who had been pardoned. Others were Caesar's own friends and officers, who had fought beside him in Gaul and in the civil war. Among them was Decimus Junius Brutus, a trusted general, and Gaius Trebonius, who had served Caesar for years. Historians have long tried to understand why such men turned against him. Possible reasons include anger at his near-royal power, disappointment over rewards and offices, and a sincere belief that the Republic was being destroyed. Even so, the plotters had no clear plan for what would happen afterwards. They believed that removing Caesar would be enough to restore the old Republic. Meetings took place quietly in private houses. Remarkably, the secret was kept among so many people for weeks. The plotters debated where to act and decided on a meeting of the Senate on the fifteenth of March, the Ides.
===
IMG: palatine-hill-cave-lupercal-fig-tree-february | a small cave at the foot of a green hill with an old fig tree beside it, young men gathering in a crowd nearby, garlands on the rocks, soft February morning light
HEAD: The Lupercalia was a very old Roman festival, held every year on the fifteenth of February.
BODY: The Lupercalia was one of the oldest festivals in Rome. Its origins were so ancient that even the Romans were unsure of its meaning. It took place on the fifteenth of February and was connected with purification and fertility. The ceremonies began at the Lupercal, a cave at the foot of the Palatine Hill. According to legend, this was where a she-wolf had cared for Romulus and Remus, the twin founders of Rome. Priests sacrificed goats and a dog. Then young men, wearing only goatskins, ran around the Palatine, striking people they met with strips of goatskin. Women believed that being struck would help them have children. Historians know about the festival from writers such as Plutarch and Ovid. Even so, many details remain uncertain. The festival continued for centuries, even after Rome became Christian, until it was finally ended at the end of the fifth century. In 44 before Christ, it became the scene of a famous political moment.
===
IMG: forum-rostra-golden-chair-purple-robe-crowd | a man in a purple robe seated on a golden chair on a stone speakers' platform above a large festive crowd in an ancient city square, temples behind, bright February sunlight
HEAD: At the Lupercalia of 44 before Christ, Mark Antony offered Caesar a crown, and Caesar refused it.
BODY: In 44 before Christ, a new group of runners took part in the Lupercalia, named in Caesar's honour. They were led by Mark Antony, Caesar's fellow consul and close ally. Antony was about thirty-nine, strong and energetic. Caesar watched the festival from the speakers' platform in the Forum, seated on a golden chair and dressed in a purple robe. During the ceremony, Antony approached and offered him a diadem, a band worn by kings. Caesar pushed it away, and the crowd cheered loudly. Antony offered it again, and again Caesar refused. He ordered it to be taken to the Temple of Jupiter, saying that Jupiter alone was king of Rome. Historians still debate the meaning of this scene. Some think it was a test of public opinion, others that Caesar wanted to show he rejected kingship. Even so, the event increased suspicion. To many senators, it seemed that Caesar was considering becoming king, a title Romans had rejected for nearly five hundred years.

TOPIC: The Ides of March
===
IMG: haruspex-old-etruscan-priest-temple-steps-robes | an old priest in long robes standing on temple steps looking up at birds circling in the sky, a few onlookers below, soft spring morning light
HEAD: A soothsayer named Spurinna warned Caesar of danger that would come by the Ides of March.
BODY: Romans believed that the gods sent signs about the future. Special priests called haruspices, trained in an old Etruscan tradition, interpreted these signs, especially by examining the organs of sacrificed animals. According to Suetonius, a haruspex named Spurinna warned Caesar to beware of a danger that would come no later than the Ides of March, the fifteenth of the month. Ancient writers also describe other strange signs in the weeks before, such as lights in the sky and birds behaving oddly. Historians treat these stories with caution. Many may have been added after the event, when people looked back for warnings. Even so, the warning of Spurinna is mentioned by several ancient writers, so it may have a real basis. Caesar was known for ignoring omens when they did not suit him. He had dismissed his bodyguard of Spanish soldiers, saying that it was better to die once than to live always in fear. The phrase beware the Ides of March later became famous through Shakespeare.
===
IMG: lepidus-house-dinner-evening-lamps-friends | a small group of Roman men reclining at a quiet dinner in a lamplit room, one older man signing letters on his couch, wine cups on a low table, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm soft light
HEAD: On the evening before the Ides of March, Caesar dined with friends at the house of Lepidus.
BODY: On the evening of the fourteenth of March, Caesar went to dinner at the house of Marcus Aemilius Lepidus, his Master of the Horse and second in command. It was an ordinary evening among friends. Caesar was reportedly signing letters as the others talked, a habit of his. According to Plutarch and Suetonius, the conversation turned to the question of what kind of death was best. Before anyone else could answer, Caesar looked up and said, a sudden one. Historians note that this story was remembered because of what happened the next day. It may have been told and retold to give the evening a sense of fate. Even so, it reflects Caesar's known attitude. He did not like to dwell on danger. After dinner, he returned to his home in the Forum, the house of the chief priest, where Calpurnia was waiting. Lepidus himself later became one of the three men who ruled Rome after Caesar's death.
===
IMG: roman-bedroom-night-shutters-open-moonlight-curtains | a dark Roman bedroom at night with wooden shutters swung open and pale moonlight falling across the floor, light curtains stirring in a soft breeze, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: According to ancient writers, Calpurnia dreamed during the night that she was holding Caesar in her arms and weeping.
BODY: Ancient writers describe the night before the Ides of March as restless. Plutarch says that all the doors and windows of Caesar's bedroom suddenly flew open at once, waking him. In the moonlight, he saw that Calpurnia was asleep but crying and murmuring. She was dreaming that she held Caesar in her arms after he had been killed. Other versions say she dreamed that a decoration on their house had fallen. Historians cannot know what Calpurnia really dreamed. Stories of dreams and signs were common in ancient accounts of famous deaths, and they were often added later. Even so, several writers agree that she was deeply troubled when she woke. In the morning, she begged Caesar not to go to the Senate that day. Caesar was not usually affected by such fears, but this time he hesitated. According to Suetonius, he was also feeling unwell and thought of postponing the meeting. Calpurnia's worry, therefore, very nearly changed the course of events.
===
IMG: calpurnia-pleading-caesar-atrium-morning-grey-light | a worried Roman woman holding the hands of a tall man in a white toga in the entrance hall of a house, a visitor waiting by the door, soft grey morning light
HEAD: On the morning of the Ides, Decimus Brutus persuaded Caesar to go to the Senate meeting after all.
BODY: Caesar decided to send Mark Antony to cancel the Senate meeting. Then a visitor arrived at the house. He was Decimus Junius Brutus, one of Caesar's most trusted generals. Decimus was also one of the conspirators. He knew that if the meeting was postponed, the plot might be discovered. According to Plutarch, he laughed gently at the omens. He told Caesar that the senators were waiting and were ready to grant him new honours. He said it would look foolish to send them home because of a dream. Then he took Caesar by the hand and led him out. Historians see this moment as decisive. Without Decimus, Caesar might have stayed at home that day. Even so, it is not certain how much Decimus said or how the conversation really went. What is clear is that Caesar trusted him completely. In his will, Caesar had even named Decimus as one of his heirs in second line. Caesar left the house late in the morning.
===
IMG: litter-carried-through-crowded-street-petitioners | a curtained litter carried on poles through a crowded ancient street, people reaching up with small scrolls and letters, an old man watching from a doorway, bright late morning light
HEAD: On his way to the Senate, Caesar reportedly joked to the soothsayer that the Ides of March had come.
BODY: Caesar was carried through the streets in a litter, a covered chair carried by servants. Crowds gathered, and people handed him petitions and notes. According to Plutarch, one man, a teacher named Artemidorus, gave him a note warning him of the plot and urged him to read it at once. Caesar kept it in his hand but did not read it in the crowd. Ancient writers also say that he saw the soothsayer Spurinna along the way. Caesar called out with a smile that the Ides of March had come. Spurinna is said to have replied, yes, they have come, but they have not yet gone. Historians treat these stories as part of the tradition rather than certain fact. Even so, they have become some of the most famous moments in Roman history. The meeting that day was not in the usual Senate house in the Forum, which was being rebuilt. It was held in the hall built by Pompey, at the end of the garden behind his theatre.
===
IMG: distant-view-columns-hall-doorway-figures-crowding | a distant view through a garden colonnade toward the open doorway of a large columned hall, small figures in white togas gathered inside, fountains and plane trees in the foreground, soft midday light
HEAD: Caesar was killed in the hall of Pompey's theatre on the fifteenth of March, 44 before Christ.
BODY: Outside the hall, one of the conspirators, probably Gaius Trebonius, kept Mark Antony talking, so that he would not enter. Brutus had insisted that Antony should not be harmed. Inside, Caesar took his seat. The conspirators gathered around him, pretending to support a petition. Then, at a signal, they attacked him. Within a few moments, Caesar fell at the foot of a statue of Pompey, his old rival. According to Suetonius, he received twenty-three wounds, but a doctor later judged that only one had been fatal. Ancient writers report different last words. Suetonius records that Caesar said nothing, though some claimed he spoke in Greek to Brutus, saying, you too, my child. The famous Latin phrase et tu, Brute comes from Shakespeare and later writers. Historians note that the details vary between sources. Even so, the main events are clear. The most powerful man in Rome died in a few moments, in a hall built by the man he had defeated.
===
IMG: senators-hurrying-away-colonnade-togas-confusion | Roman senators in white togas hurrying away along a long colonnade, some looking back over their shoulders, empty garden behind them, hazy midday light
HEAD: After Caesar's death, the senators fled, and the city of Rome waited in uncertainty.
BODY: Brutus turned to speak to the senators, wanting to explain that the act had been done for liberty and the Republic. But the senators did not stay to listen. They fled from the hall into the streets in panic. News spread quickly through the city. Shops closed, and people stayed in their homes, unsure what would happen next. The conspirators walked to the Capitoline Hill, declaring that Rome was free again. Historians note that they had made few plans for what would follow. They expected the people to welcome them, but the response was cautious and uncertain. Caesar's body lay in the empty hall for some time, until three enslaved servants carried it home on a litter to Calpurnia. Even so, Caesar's supporters, especially Mark Antony and Lepidus, soon took control of the situation. Within days, a compromise was reached in the Senate. The conspirators were given an amnesty, but Caesar's laws and decisions were confirmed, leaving the future of Rome unsettled.

TOPIC: After the Ides, and Rome today
===
IMG: gardens-of-caesar-across-tiber-villa-view-city | a beautiful riverside garden with shady walks, fountains and statues, a villa on the slope, the city on its hills across the river, warm golden afternoon light
HEAD: In his will, Caesar left his gardens to the people of Rome and a gift of money to every citizen.
BODY: A few days after his death, Caesar's will was opened and read publicly. It had been kept safely by the Vestal Virgins. The will named his great-nephew, Gaius Octavius, as his main heir and adopted him as a son. It also contained generous gifts to the people. According to Suetonius, Caesar left his gardens across the Tiber to the public as a park, and three hundred sesterces to every Roman citizen. When the will became known, public feeling turned strongly in Caesar's favour. At his funeral in the Forum, Mark Antony gave a speech and showed the crowd Caesar's bloodstained toga. The people were deeply moved. They built a funeral pyre in the Forum itself and burned his body there. Historians note that Shakespeare's famous speech beginning friends, Romans, countrymen is a later invention, though based on ancient accounts. Even so, the funeral was a turning point. Many conspirators soon had to leave Rome for their safety, and the struggle for power began.
===
IMG: young-octavius-reading-letter-apollonia-seaside-camp | a slender pale young man of eighteen with fair curly hair reading a letter outside a tent in a seaside camp, a friend standing beside him, calm sea behind, soft spring light
HEAD: Caesar's heir, Gaius Octavius, was only eighteen years old and was at first not taken seriously.
BODY: At the time of Caesar's death, Gaius Octavius was studying at Apollonia, a town on the coast of what is now Albania, where he was preparing to join the Parthian campaign. He was Caesar's great-nephew, the grandson of Caesar's sister Julia. He received the news in late March and decided to return to Italy and claim his inheritance, against the advice of his family. As Caesar's adopted son, he took the name Gaius Julius Caesar Octavianus. Historians often call him Octavian. Many experienced politicians, including Cicero and Mark Antony, saw him as a young boy who could easily be controlled. Cicero hoped to use him against Antony. Even so, Octavian proved extremely skilful. He used Caesar's name and popularity, especially with the soldiers, to build his own power. Ancient writers describe him as slight, often in poor health, but calm and determined. Within a short time, he had raised an army and become one of the most important figures in Rome.
===
IMG: comet-over-rome-night-summer-sky | a bright comet with a long glowing tail in a clear summer night sky over the dark rooftops and temples of an ancient city, people on rooftops looking up
HEAD: In July 44 before Christ, a bright comet appeared over Rome during games held in Caesar's honour.
BODY: In July 44 before Christ, Octavian paid for public games in honour of Caesar, to celebrate his victories. During the games, a bright comet appeared in the sky and was visible for seven days. According to Pliny the Elder and Suetonius, the people believed it was the soul of Caesar rising to join the gods. Octavian encouraged this belief. He placed a star on the head of Caesar's statues and later had the comet shown on coins. The comet became known as the Julian star. Historians and astronomers have studied the event. Chinese records also mention a comet visible around this time, which supports the Roman accounts. In the same year, the month of Quintilis, in which Caesar was born, was renamed Julius in his honour. This is the origin of the name July. Even so, the comet was also used for political purposes. Two years later, in 42 before Christ, the Senate officially declared Caesar a god, and Octavian could call himself the son of a god.
===
IMG: coastal-villa-formiae-sea-evening-calm-cypress | a quiet villa by the sea at evening, tall cypress trees along a path, calm water reflecting a pink sky, a small boat at a jetty
HEAD: Years later, the emperor Augustus found his grandson reading a book by Cicero and gave it back with kind words.
BODY: The conspirators had hoped to restore the old Republic, but instead there were years of struggle and war. Cicero spoke out bravely against Mark Antony in a series of speeches called the Philippics. But in 43 before Christ, Octavian, Antony and Lepidus joined forces and drew up lists of enemies to be killed. Cicero was on the list. He was caught and killed near his villa at Formiae, on the coast south of Rome, in December 43 before Christ. Brutus and Cassius were defeated at the Battle of Philippi in Greece in 42 before Christ, and both took their own lives. Many years later, according to Plutarch, Augustus found one of his grandsons holding a book by Cicero. The boy tried to hide it, afraid. Augustus took the book, read part of it, and gave it back. He said that Cicero had been a learned man, and one who loved his country. Historians see this story as a sign of Cicero's lasting reputation.
===
IMG: augustus-mature-statue-toga-calm-forum-dignified | a dignified marble statue of a calm Roman leader in a toga standing in a sunlit ancient square, temples and columns behind, soft golden light
HEAD: Caesar's heir became Augustus, the first Roman emperor, and his rule brought decades of peace.
BODY: After defeating his rivals, including Mark Antony and Cleopatra at the Battle of Actium in 31 before Christ, Octavian became the sole master of the Roman world. He was then only in his early thirties. He had learned from Caesar's fate. He avoided the title of king and even refused to be called dictator. Instead, he claimed to have restored the Republic, while keeping real power in his own hands. In 27 before Christ, the Senate gave him the name Augustus, meaning revered. Historians regard him as the first Roman emperor. His long rule lasted until his death in 14 after Christ. It began a period known as the Pax Romana, or Roman peace, which lasted about two hundred years, though with many wars on the frontiers. The month of Sextilis was renamed August in his honour. Even so, the old Republic never returned. Caesar's death had not saved it. Instead, it led to the rule of emperors that lasted for centuries.
===
IMG: largo-argentina-ruins-below-street-level-cats-sun | the ruins of four ancient temples with broken columns and old stone steps in a sunken square below street level, many cats sleeping peacefully in the sun on the warm stones
HEAD: Today, cats sleep in the sun among the ruins near the place where Caesar was killed.
BODY: In the centre of modern Rome lies a square called Largo di Torre Argentina. Below street level, there is a sunken area of ancient ruins, uncovered in the late 1920s. It contains the remains of four temples from the time of the Republic. Behind them lie the foundations of part of the Curia of Pompey, the hall where Caesar was killed. Archaeologists have identified a large block of concrete there, which some believe was placed later to mark the spot. Historians note that the exact site of Caesar's death cannot be known for certain. Today, the ruins are also home to a famous colony of cats. Volunteers have looked after them for many years, and the cats can often be seen sleeping on the old stones in the sun. In 2023, after restoration, walkways were opened so that visitors could enter the area. Even so, many people simply look down from the street. Every year on the Ides of March, people gather nearby to remember Caesar.
===
IMG: curia-senate-house-bronze-doors-forum-sunlight | a tall plain brick senate house with great bronze doors standing among ancient ruins and columns in a forum, a few visitors walking slowly, soft golden morning light
HEAD: The Senate house that Caesar began to rebuild still stands in the Roman Forum.
BODY: The Roman Forum is today a large archaeological park in the heart of the city. Visitors walk along the Sacred Way, the oldest street of Rome, past the ruins of temples, arches and law courts. Caesar's funeral procession passed along it. One of the best-preserved buildings is the Curia Julia, the Senate house. Caesar began its reconstruction, and Augustus completed it. The present building is a later restoration, made after a fire in the third century after Christ, under the emperor Diocletian. Its tall brick walls survived because it was turned into a church in the seventh century. The original ancient bronze doors were moved in the seventeenth century to the Basilica of Saint John Lateran, where they can still be seen. Historians and archaeologists continue to study the Forum and make new discoveries. Even so, it is now a peaceful place. In the afternoon sun, visitors wander among the ruins where the politics of the Roman world were once decided.

HEAD: Every year, people still leave flowers for Caesar at the remains of his temple in the Roman Forum.
BODY: After Caesar was declared a god, Augustus built a temple in his honour in the Roman Forum. It stood on the spot where the people had burned Caesar's body after the funeral. The temple was dedicated in 29 before Christ and was called the Temple of the Divine Julius. Today, only the concrete core of its platform survives, with a small curved niche at the front. It is believed to mark the place of the altar built over the site of the funeral pyre. Visitors often leave flowers, notes and coins there. On the fifteenth of March each year, many people come to lay flowers, and events are held to remember Caesar's death. Historians note how remarkable it is that such a custom has continued for so long. Even so, the Forum is quiet for most of the year. In the evening, as the light fades, the ruins grow calm, and the flowers in the small niche remain as a reminder of a story that is more than two thousand years old.
