TOPIC: The people of the streets
BRIDGE: The dinner guests are walking home by torchlight now. Yet most Romans never lay on a couch at all. Let's see how they lived.
===
IMG: insula-apartment-block-five-storeys-shops-below | a tall ancient apartment block of brick and timber, five storeys high, with small shops and taverns opening onto the street below and windows above, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Most Romans lived in tall apartment blocks called islands.
BODY: Most people in Rome did not live in fine houses with gardens and fountains. They lived in apartment blocks called insulae. The word means islands, because each block stood alone, surrounded by streets. The insulae were tall buildings of brick and timber, four, five or even six storeys high. They were crowded together along the narrow streets. On the ground floor, facing the street, were shops, workshops and taverns. Above them were the apartments. The flats on the first floor were the best and the most expensive. They were large, with several rooms and real windows. Some even had running water, piped in from the aqueducts. But the higher you climbed, the smaller and cheaper the rooms became. It was the opposite of today, when the top floor often has the best view. In Rome, nobody wanted to climb six flights of stairs with a heavy jar of water. So the poorest people lived at the very top.
===
IMG: narrow-street-tall-buildings-laundry-lines-light | a narrow ancient street between tall apartment houses with washing hung on lines between windows, flower pots on small balconies, a shaft of soft light falling into the street
HEAD: A Roman poet joked that you should make your will before walking under the windows at night.
BODY: At the top of the insulae, under the roof, the poorest people lived in tiny dark rooms. They were hot in summer and cold in winter. There was no water, no kitchen and no toilet. People had to carry their water up all the stairs from the public fountain in the street. Then they had to carry their rubbish down again. Or sometimes, they simply threw it out of the window. The poet Juvenal, who lived a little later, made a famous joke about this. He said that if you walked through Rome at night, you should make your will first. You never knew what might fall on your head from the windows above. Yet the people in those rooms made them homely, too. They kept pots of herbs and flowers on the window ledges. They hung washing on lines across the street. Neighbours called to each other from window to window. It was cramped and noisy. But it was also full of life.
===
IMG: crassus-slaves-water-buckets-street-smoke | a line of men passing leather buckets of water along a narrow ancient street toward a smoking wooden building, people watching from a distance, hazy afternoon light
HEAD: The richest man in Rome grew even richer by buying burning buildings cheaply.
BODY: The insulae were often badly built by greedy landlords. They wanted to make as much money as possible and spend as little as they could. So the buildings were sometimes unsafe. Worse, they often caught fire. There were braziers, oil lamps and cooking fires in every flat. One spark could set the timber alight. Rome had no proper fire brigade at this time. That gave one man a clever and rather ruthless idea. His name was Marcus Crassus, and he was the richest man in Rome. When a building caught fire, Crassus would appear with his own team of slaves trained to fight fires. Then he would offer to buy the burning building, cheaply, from its desperate owner. Only when the owner agreed would his men put out the flames. In this way, Crassus came to own a great deal of Rome. It is not a kind story. But it shows us how much a city needs things we take for granted, like firemen.
===
IMG: roman-snack-bar-counter-clay-jars-street | a small street snack bar with a stone counter set with big clay jars of hot food, a cook ladling stew, customers standing at the counter, warm evening light
HEAD: Rome was full of street snack bars, with hot stew kept in clay jars set into the counter.
BODY: The people of the insulae did not spend much time at home. Their rooms were mostly for sleeping. They lived their lives in the streets. And the streets of Rome were full of shops of every kind. There were butchers and fishmongers. There were fruit sellers and vegetable sellers. There were wine shops, potters, shoemakers and tailors. Barbers shaved men right in the street, sitting on stools. And there were snack bars everywhere. Each one had a stone counter opening onto the street. Big clay jars were set into the counter. They held hot food, like stews, soups, sausages and spiced wine. Many Romans had no kitchen at home. So they bought their meals here, the way people today buy a sandwich on the way to work. Similar counters have been found in the ruins of Pompeii. Some still have food remains in their jars. Can you smell it? Lentil stew, warm bread and a hint of wood smoke.
===
IMG: craftsmen-street-blacksmith-potter-acrobat | an ancient street of craftsmen with a potter at his wheel and a carpenter at work, an acrobat entertaining a small crowd of children nearby, warm golden afternoon light
HEAD: Each trade had its own street, and jugglers and snake charmers performed on the corners.
BODY: There were craftsmen everywhere in Rome. There were blacksmiths, carpenters and goldsmiths. There were lamp makers who shaped clay lamps by the thousand. There were fullers, who washed and cleaned clothes by trampling them in great tubs. Each trade had its own street or neighbourhood. If you wanted sandals, you went to the street of the shoemakers. If you wanted a new pot, you went to the potters. And everywhere there were street sellers, crying out what they had to sell. There were beggars, and street performers. There were jugglers and acrobats, snake charmers and storytellers. Children followed the performers from corner to corner. Many Roman street names came from the trades practised there. Some of those names have survived, in a changed form, in the streets of Rome today. Isn't that a lovely thought? People still walk down streets named after craftsmen who worked there two thousand years ago.
===
IMG: grain-barges-tiber-oxen-towpath-warehouses | heavy wooden barges loaded with sacks of grain being pulled up a calm river by oxen walking along a towpath, warehouses on the bank, soft morning light
HEAD: Caesar counted the people receiving free grain house by house, and halved the list.
BODY: Rome was so large that the farms of Italy could not feed it. So most of its grain came by sea. Great ships carried it from Egypt, Sicily and North Africa to the port of Ostia, at the mouth of the Tiber. From there, it went upriver to Rome in barges. Oxen pulled the barges, walking slowly along a path beside the river. For many years, the poorer citizens of Rome had been given free grain by the state every month. This kept them from going hungry, and it kept them from rioting. When Caesar came to power, more than three hundred thousand people were on the list. Caesar decided to check it. His officials counted carefully, street by street and house by house. Many names on the list belonged to people who did not need the grain, or who did not exist at all. In the end, he cut the number to about a hundred and fifty thousand, the people who really needed it.
===
IMG: aqueduct-arches-campagna-countryside-evening-light | a long line of tall stone arches carrying an aqueduct across green countryside toward a distant city, sheep grazing beneath, warm golden evening light
HEAD: The water of Rome flowed in from the hills along stone channels, day and night, without stopping.
BODY: Water came to Rome from the hills, along the aqueducts. At this time, there were four of them. They were great stone channels that carried fresh, clean water for many miles. Sometimes they ran underground. Sometimes they ran on tall arches across the valleys. They sloped very gently all the way, so the water flowed slowly down into the city. In Rome, the water poured into fountains on the street corners. People filled their jars there. It flowed into the public baths and into the houses of the rich, who paid for it. The water never stopped flowing, day or night. The overflow ran away into the great sewer, the Cloaca Maxima, and on into the Tiber. That old sewer is still working today, more than two thousand years later. Can you hear the fountains? In a Roman street at night, when the carts were quiet, there was always the soft sound of running water.
===
IMG: household-workers-kitchen-courtyard-morning | household workers in simple tunics preparing food and carrying water jars in the courtyard kitchen of a Roman house, a young woman writing accounts on a wax tablet, soft morning light
HEAD: Perhaps one in every four or five people in Rome was enslaved, yet many were later set free.
BODY: Many of the people of Rome were enslaved. Perhaps one person in every four or five belonged to someone else, with no rights at all. They came from all over the world. Some had been captured in war. Some had been born to enslaved mothers. Some had been sold by pirates. Caesar's wars in Gaul alone had brought many thousands of captives to be sold. Some worked in the houses of the rich, as cooks, cleaners and nursemaids. Some worked as secretaries, teachers and doctors, because many educated Greeks were enslaved. Others worked in workshops, on the docks and on building sites. Their lives were often very hard. Yet in Rome, something unusual happened. Many enslaved people were eventually set free by their owners. They became freedmen and freedwomen. Their children were born as Roman citizens. Some freedmen became rich merchants. A few of their grandchildren even sat in the Senate. It was a cruel system, but not always a closed one.
===
IMG: saturnalia-feast-family-gifts-candles-merry-room | a cheerful Roman family and their household sharing a feast together in a room decorated with green branches, small gifts and candles on the table, everyone wearing soft felt caps, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: At the merriest festival of the year, masters served dinner to their own slaves.
BODY: The Roman year was full of festivals. These were holy days for the gods, when people did not work. There were processions, sacrifices, feasts and games. The merriest festival of all came in December. It was called the Saturnalia. For a few days, the ordinary rules of life were turned upside down. People gave each other presents, often small candles or little clay figures. Everyone wore soft felt caps, the caps of freed slaves, as a sign that all were equal for a while. Masters served dinner to their own slaves. People gambled with dice in the open, which was normally forbidden. They played games, sang songs and ate and drank too much. Friends shouted to each other in the street: Io Saturnalia! A later writer, Pliny, even built himself a quiet room, just to escape the noise. Does it sound familiar? Many people think some of our own winter customs, like giving small gifts in the dark days of December, have a little of the Saturnalia in them.

TOPIC: Baths and races
BRIDGE: The water from the hills does not only fill the fountains. It also fills the great steaming pools, where half of Rome spends its afternoons.
===
IMG: roman-public-baths-entrance-columns-afternoon-crowd | the columned entrance of an ancient public bath house with people arriving carrying towels and small flasks of oil, steam drifting from a vent on the roof, warm afternoon light
HEAD: A visit to the baths cost the smallest coin in Rome, and children often got in free.
BODY: In the afternoon, the people of Rome went to the baths. There were many public baths at this time, perhaps more than a hundred and fifty. Most were small and simple. They were run by private owners who charged a tiny fee. It was a quarter of an as, the smallest coin there was. Almost anyone could afford it. Children were often let in free. The huge, famous baths with great domes, which you may have seen in pictures, did not exist yet. They were built later, by the emperors. At the baths, you left your clothes in a changing room, in little niches in the wall. You might pay a slave to guard them, because thieves were common. Then you went into the exercise yard. You lifted weights, threw a ball with friends, wrestled or ran. You kept going until you were warm and sweaty. Only then were you ready for the water.
===
IMG: caldarium-steam-hot-pool-bathers-soft-light | a steamy hot bath room with a warm pool, bathers relaxing on stone benches, light falling softly through a high window, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: The Romans did not use soap, but scraped themselves clean with a curved metal tool.
BODY: After exercise, you went through the bath rooms in order. First came the warm room, the tepidarium, where you sat and relaxed. Next was the hot room, the caldarium. It was full of steam. It was heated by hot air flowing under the floor and inside the walls, from a great furnace. There was a hot pool, and a basin of cool water to splash your face. The Romans did not use soap. Instead, they rubbed olive oil on their skin. Then they scraped it off again, with the sweat and the dirt, using a curved metal tool called a strigil. It sounds odd, but it worked rather well. Last of all, you went into the cold room, the frigidarium. You plunged into the cold pool to wake yourself up. Then you dressed and went home for dinner, clean and refreshed. Doesn't that sound lovely, on a chilly winter afternoon? Warm stone under your feet, and steam curling slowly toward the ceiling.
===
IMG: bath-house-courtyard-gossip-snack-seller | people in tunics chatting and laughing in the colonnaded courtyard of an ancient bath house, a snack seller carrying a tray, a barber trimming hair, soft afternoon light
HEAD: A philosopher who lived above a bath house complained about the man who sang in the bath.
BODY: The baths were not just for washing. They were places to meet friends and gossip. People did business there, and made plans. Sellers walked around crying their snacks: sausages, cakes and drinks. You could have your hair cut, or your arm hair plucked. The philosopher Seneca lived a little later. For a while, he rented rooms above a bath house. He wrote a letter complaining about the noise. He described the grunting of the weightlifters. He described the slapping of the masseurs on bare shoulders. He described the shouts of the ball players and the cries of the sausage sellers. And worst of all, he said, was the man who liked to sing in the bath. Some things never change, it seems. Somewhere, at this very moment, someone is probably singing in a shower. Seneca would have understood your neighbours perfectly.
===
IMG: circus-maximus-chariots-racing-crowds-sunlit-sand | four-horse chariots racing around a long sandy track in a vast ancient stadium, huge crowds in the stands, statues on a low central barrier, bright sunshine and golden dust
HEAD: At the chariot races, rich and poor, men and women all sat together and cheered.
BODY: The Romans loved shows of every kind. On the great festivals, there were games, paid for by the state and by ambitious politicians. The greatest of all were the chariot races in the Circus Maximus. Caesar had recently rebuilt and enlarged it. Now it could hold perhaps a hundred and fifty thousand people. The chariots were light and fast, pulled by four horses. The drivers wore tunics in the colours of their teams. At this time there were the Reds and the Whites. Later, the Blues and the Greens joined them. They raced seven times around a long, narrow track. A low barrier ran down the middle, decorated with statues and shrines. On it stood seven great wooden eggs. One egg was turned over after each lap, so everyone could count. The turns at each end were the most exciting part. The crowd roared and bet on their favourite drivers. And Romans of every class came, rich and poor, men and women, all sitting together.
===
IMG: theatre-of-pompey-garden-colonnade-statues-fountains | a long garden behind an ancient theatre surrounded by colonnades, shady plane trees, statues and fountains, people strolling slowly, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Behind Rome's only stone theatre lay a long garden, and a hall where the Senate sometimes met.
BODY: There were plays too, comedies and tragedies, mimes, dancers and musicians. At this time, Rome had only one permanent stone theatre. It was the Theatre of Pompey, built eleven years earlier by Pompey the Great. It stood on the Field of Mars, outside the old city. It was huge and splendid, with a great curved bank of seats and a stage. Behind the stage lay a long garden. It was surrounded by colonnades and full of statues, fountains and shady trees. People strolled there on warm afternoons. At the far end of the garden was a large hall. The Senate sometimes met there. The old Senate house in the Forum had burned down a few years before, and it was being rebuilt. Inside the hall stood a great statue of Pompey himself. It is a peaceful place, with fountains and plane trees. Let's remember it, quietly. Our story will come back to this garden, one spring morning.

TOPIC: Cicero and his letters
BRIDGE: Now let's leave the noise of the crowds. Somewhere in a quiet study, a man is dipping his pen and beginning a letter to his friend.
===
IMG: cicero-older-man-writing-letter-study-scrolls | an older Roman man with a kind, clever face writing a letter at a wooden desk in a quiet study, scrolls on shelves, a small oil lamp, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: More than nine hundred of Cicero's letters have survived, so we can still hear his voice.
BODY: Of all the people in Rome in 44 before Christ, there is one whose voice we can still hear clearly. His name was Marcus Tullius Cicero. He was sixty-two years old that spring. He was the greatest speaker and writer of his time, and one of the greatest in all history. He also wrote a great many letters. More than nine hundred of them have survived. Many of the replies he received have survived too. He wrote about politics and books. He wrote about his houses and his gardens, his health and his money troubles. He wrote about his family and the latest gossip. He wrote when he was happy and when he was sad, when he was angry and when he was afraid. Because of these letters, we know more about Cicero than almost anyone else in the ancient world. Reading them is like opening someone's post, two thousand years later. You can feel his moods change from one page to the next.
===
IMG: two-friends-talking-garden-bench-scrolls | two older Roman men in simple tunics sitting on a stone bench in a quiet garden, one holding a scroll, both smiling, cypress trees and a small fountain, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Cicero's dearest friend was a quiet, wise man who stayed out of politics and was liked by everyone.
BODY: Cicero wrote most of all to his dearest friend, Titus Pomponius Atticus. Atticus was a rich, quiet and wise man. He stayed out of politics completely. He helped people on every side, and everyone liked him. That was very rare in those troubled times. Atticus also ran a team of copyists. They made copies of Cicero's books by hand, and Atticus sent them to friends and sold them. So in a way, he was Cicero's publisher too. Cicero told him everything. He shared his worries, his jokes and his plans. Sometimes he wrote to him several times a day. He once wrote that talking to Atticus was like talking to himself, only better. When Cicero was sad, Atticus calmed him. When Cicero was boastful, Atticus teased him gently. Do you have a friend like that? Someone you can tell anything, who still likes you afterwards? For Cicero, that friend was Atticus, for his whole life.
===
IMG: arpinum-hill-town-stone-houses-valley | a small ancient hill town of stone houses and walls on a green hillside above a river valley, olive trees and mountains behind, soft golden evening light
HEAD: Cicero came from a small hill town, and was the first in his family to reach the top.
BODY: Cicero was not born into a great Roman family. He came from Arpinum, a small town in the hills about seventy miles from Rome. As a young man, he came to the city and made his name as a lawyer. He won case after case with his brilliant speeches. Step by step, he rose to the highest office. In 63 before Christ, he became consul. He was the first man in his family ever to do so. That year, he uncovered a plot against the Republic and stopped it. The Senate called him the father of his country. He never forgot it. He was proud, and rather vain. He talked about that great year so often that his friends found it a little tiresome. Yet he was also kind, warm-hearted, funny and loyal. And he loved the Republic with all his heart. It is easy to smile at his vanity. But it is also easy to like him. He was, above all, very human.
===
IMG: cicero-walking-garden-colonnade-scroll-thinking | an older Roman man walking slowly alone along a shady garden colonnade, holding a scroll, looking thoughtful, soft late afternoon light through the columns
HEAD: After his beloved daughter died, Cicero comforted himself by writing books about friendship and old age.
BODY: Cicero was not happy in the year 44. In the civil war, he had taken Pompey's side, against Caesar. Caesar forgave him. They were polite, even friendly. Caesar admired Cicero's writing very much. But Cicero no longer had any power. The Senate did whatever Caesar wanted. Cicero felt the Republic he loved was gone. And there had been a great sadness in his life. His beloved daughter, Tullia, had died a year before, in her early thirties. Cicero was heartbroken. For months he could hardly bear to see anyone. He walked alone in the woods and wept. To comfort himself, he wrote. In two years, he wrote an astonishing number of books. He wrote about friendship, old age and duty. He wrote about the gods and about how to live a good life. In one book, he wrote that life is like a play. A wise person, he said, does not mind when the last act comes. People still read these books for comfort today.
===
IMG: roman-villa-library-shelves-scroll-cases-sunlight | a quiet villa library with wooden shelves of scrolls in round leather cases, a reading couch and a window looking onto a garden, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm sunlight
HEAD: Cicero wrote that if you have a garden and a library, you have everything you need.
BODY: Cicero had several country houses, and he loved them all. His favourite was at Tusculum, in the hills southeast of Rome. It had gardens and a library. It had two covered walks, where he liked to stroll with friends and talk about philosophy. He also had a house by the sea at Formiae, and others besides. He spent a great deal of money on them, and on Greek statues to decorate them. So he was nearly always in debt. But he loved books more than anything. He once wrote to Atticus that a room without books is like a body without a soul. And in another letter, he wrote that if you have a garden and a library, you have everything you need. Perhaps you feel that way too, on a quiet evening. A few good books, a window onto something green. What more could anyone want? Cicero would have understood completely. He spent his happiest hours exactly like that.
===
IMG: freedman-secretary-writing-shorthand-tablet | a gentle-looking Roman man in a plain tunic writing quickly on a wax tablet while an older man speaks beside him, scrolls on a table, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft lamplight
HEAD: Cicero's secretary invented a kind of shorthand, and some of his signs are still used today.
BODY: Cicero had a loyal secretary named Tiro. Tiro was a clever, gentle man. He had once been enslaved in Cicero's household, but Cicero had set him free. Tiro was often ill. Cicero wrote him warm letters, telling him to rest, eat well and get better soon. In one letter, he told Tiro that his only job now was to get well. Tiro invented a system of quick signs, a kind of shorthand. With it, he could write down Cicero's speeches as fast as Cicero could speak. One of his signs, a little mark for the word and, is still used in some languages today. And after Cicero's death, it was Tiro who gathered his letters together and kept them safe. Without Tiro, we would have lost them. So when we read Cicero's letters now, we are really reading a gift from a quiet, faithful secretary. He lived to be very old, they say, on a small farm of his own.

IMG: cicero-at-window-evening-looking-toward-city | an older Roman man standing at the window of a country villa in the evening, looking toward a distant city on the hills, a lamp lit behind him, soft dusky light
HEAD: In the early months of 44, Cicero watched Rome from a distance, and wrote that nobody knew what would happen.
BODY: In the early months of 44, Cicero spent much of his time away from Rome. He stayed in his country houses, writing. He watched Caesar and the city from a distance, with sadness. He wrote to Atticus that he did not know what would happen next. Nobody did. In the evenings, he sometimes stood at his window and looked toward Rome. The city lay far off on its hills, a faint glow of lamps in the dusk. He thought about the old days, when the Senate had mattered. He thought about Tullia. He thought about his son, who was studying in Athens. Young Marcus seemed to prefer parties to philosophy, and his father worried about him. Cicero did not know that, in a few weeks, everything would change. And he did not know that some of the men he knew best were already meeting in secret. In the evenings, they talked quietly about the Republic, and about Caesar.

TOPIC: Scrolls, schools and the gods
BRIDGE: Cicero closes his window, and the lamp burns on. All over Rome, other people are reading too, and praying, and teaching their children.
===
IMG: roman-bookshop-scroll-shelves-arcade-customers | a small bookshop in an arcade with shelves of rolled scrolls, each with a little label hanging from it, customers browsing, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Roman books were long rolls of papyrus, and you read them by unrolling with one hand.
BODY: Rome in the year 44 was a city of books. They were not books like ours, with pages. They were scrolls, long rolls of papyrus. Papyrus was a kind of paper made in Egypt, from a reed that grows along the Nile. The writing was set out in columns along the scroll. You unrolled it with one hand and rolled it up with the other as you read. There were bookshops near the Forum. You could buy poems, histories and speeches there. You could buy plays, and books about farming, cooking and medicine. Every book was copied by hand. Teams of educated slaves sat in rows, writing as someone read the text aloud. A popular book might be copied hundreds of times. Rich Romans had libraries at home. Each scroll had a little label hanging from it with the title. And the Romans almost always read aloud, even alone, murmuring the words. Silent reading was so unusual that people commented on it.
===
IMG: grammaticus-teaching-boys-homer-scroll-courtyard | a teacher reading from a scroll to a few older boys in a sunny courtyard of a house, the boys listening on a stone bench, potted plants, soft morning light
HEAD: Rich Roman boys studied Homer, and then learned the art of making speeches.
BODY: Most children who went to school stopped after the first school, at about eleven or twelve. Many girls learned to run a household at home after that. Some girls from rich families were taught by private tutors and became very learned. The boys of rich families went on to a second school. There, they read the great Greek and Latin poets, above all Homer. They studied history, myths, geography and the stars. Then, at about sixteen, they moved on to the teacher of speaking. There they learned how to make speeches. That was the most important skill for any young Roman who wanted to be a lawyer or a politician. They practised with imaginary court cases and famous questions from history. The best students went on to study in Greece, in Athens or Rhodes. Caesar himself had studied on Rhodes, with a famous teacher. It was on his way there that the pirates had caught him.
===
IMG: temple-vesta-round-sacred-fire-priestesses | a small round temple with slender columns, a sacred fire glowing through its open door, two priestesses in long white robes and veils standing quietly beside it, soft evening light
HEAD: Six priestesses kept a sacred fire burning in Rome, day and night, and it was never allowed to go out.
BODY: Next to Caesar's house in the Forum stood a small round temple. It belonged to Vesta, the goddess of the hearth. Her priestesses were called the Vestal Virgins. There were six of them. They were chosen as little girls from noble families, and they served the goddess for thirty years. Their most important duty was to keep a sacred fire burning in the round temple. It burned day and night and was never allowed to go out. The fire was the hearth of the whole Roman people. As long as it burned, Rome was safe. The Vestals were deeply respected. They had seats of honour at the games. When they walked through the streets, an attendant went ahead of them, and everyone made way. Important people often left their wills with the Vestals for safekeeping. Caesar had left his will with them too. Imagine tending a little flame, through every night, for thirty years. It must have felt like looking after the heart of a city.
===
IMG: family-offering-household-shrine-incense-bread-wine | a Roman family gathered at a small painted household shrine in their home, offering bread, wine and incense beside a little lamp, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm soft light
HEAD: The Romans believed there was a god of the doorway, and even a goddess of the hinges.
BODY: The Romans lived surrounded by gods. There were the great ones, of course. There was Jupiter, king of the gods, with his wife Juno and Minerva, goddess of wisdom. There was Mars, god of war, and Venus, goddess of love. There were Apollo, Mercury, Neptune and many more. But there were also thousands of little gods and spirits everywhere. There was a god of the doorway, a goddess of the hinges and a god of the threshold. There were spirits of fields, springs, rivers and woods. Every house had its own household gods, who guarded the family and the store cupboard. At every crossroads in the city stood a little shrine. The neighbours left offerings there and held a festival together each winter. It is a gentle way of seeing the world, isn't it? A small spirit watching over your front door, your cupboard and your street. Nothing was too humble to be cared for.
===
IMG: temple-interior-cella-large-statue-goddess-dim-light | the inside of a quiet ancient temple with one tall marble statue of a goddess at the far end, painted walls and gifts placed around it, dim soft light from the doorway, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: When a Roman prayed, he covered his head with a fold of his toga, so nothing unlucky could interrupt.
BODY: For the Romans, religion was a kind of agreement. You gave the gods what was due to them: sacrifices, offerings, prayers and festivals. Everything had to be done exactly right, with the right words and the right gestures, in the right order. In return, the gods protected you, your family and your city. If you made a mistake in the words of a prayer, you had to start all over again. When a Roman prayed, he covered his head with a fold of his toga. That way, he would not see or hear anything unlucky that might spoil the ceremony. A Roman temple was the house of a god. Inside, in a single room, stood the god's statue. The people did not usually go in to worship. The altar was outside, at the foot of the steps. The ceremonies took place there, in the open air, where everyone could see. Temples were also museums and treasuries. The state treasury of Rome was kept beneath the Temple of Saturn.

BRIDGE: Let's walk back across the Forum, past the little round temple and its glowing fire, to the house where Caesar lives.
===
IMG: calpurnia-roman-matron-garden-quiet-morning | a gentle Roman woman of about thirty in a long pale dress sitting quietly in a house garden with a small fountain and flowers, looking thoughtful, soft morning light
HEAD: Caesar's wife Calpurnia had been married to him for fifteen years, though he was nearly always away.
BODY: As chief priest, Caesar lived in an official house in the Forum. It was called the Domus Publica, the public house. His wife was Calpurnia. She was about thirty years old, the daughter of an important senator. She had been married to Caesar for fifteen years. It was his third marriage. His first wife, Cornelia, whom he had loved very much, had died many years before. She had given him his only daughter, Julia. Julia had married Pompey, and she too had died, ten years earlier, to Caesar's great grief. Calpurnia and Caesar had no children. For much of their marriage, he had been far away, fighting in Gaul and in the civil war. We know very little about her. But the ancient stories say she was gentle, loyal and devoted. They say she loved her husband very much, and worried about him. Perhaps she hoped that now, at last, he would stay home a little.

BODY: Caesar was a very hard worker. He got up early and worked all day and late into the night. He read letters and answered them. The ancient writers say he could dictate several letters at once, to several secretaries, while reading yet another. He saw visitors, made decisions and gave orders. When he travelled, he kept working in his carriage. He ate and drank very little, and he did not care for fancy food. There is a nice story about that. Once, at a dinner, the host served asparagus dressed with the wrong oil, a scented oil instead of olive oil. The other guests made faces and complained. Caesar ate his asparagus without a word. Afterwards, he scolded his friends for embarrassing their host. It is a small story. Yet it tells us something. He could be proud and ambitious, but he also had good manners and a kind heart for an anxious host.

BODY: Caesar's health was not perfect. He suffered from headaches. He also had fainting fits, which the Romans called the falling sickness. Yet he was very strong and tough. In his wars, he had marched with his soldiers bareheaded, in sun and rain. He swam rivers and rode for days without rest. He was a superb horseman. It was said that as a young man, he could ride at full gallop with his hands clasped behind his back. His favourite horse was a strange one. Its hooves were split, almost like toes. Fortune tellers said this meant its rider would rule the world. Caesar raised the horse himself, and nobody else was ever allowed to ride it. But now, in 44, Caesar was fifty-five, and tired. He had been fighting, almost without a break, for fourteen years. Some of his friends thought he no longer cared much whether he lived or died. He only seemed to want to keep working.

HEAD: Cleopatra spoke many languages, and she was the first of her family to learn Egyptian.
BODY: Across the river, in Caesar's gardens, lived a very special guest. She was Cleopatra, the queen of Egypt. She was about twenty-five years old. Her family, the Ptolemies, were not Egyptians but Greeks. They were descended from one of the generals of Alexander the Great. For three hundred years, they had ruled Egypt from their city of Alexandria, by the sea. Cleopatra was very clever. She spoke many languages, perhaps nine or ten. She was the first of all her family to learn Egyptian, the language of her own people. She had studied mathematics, philosophy and medicine. A Greek writer named Plutarch wrote about her a century and a half later. He said her beauty was not so remarkable in itself. But her conversation was irresistible. Her voice, he said, was like an instrument with many strings. People who met her never forgot her. Not because of her face, but because of her mind and her charm.

HEAD: The story says Cleopatra had herself smuggled in to meet Caesar, rolled up in a bedroll.
BODY: Cleopatra had met Caesar four years earlier, in Alexandria, when she was twenty-one. She was at war with her younger brother over the throne of Egypt. Her brother's advisers had driven her out of the city. When Caesar arrived, she wanted to see him and ask for his help. But her brother's soldiers were guarding the palace. So, the story goes, she found a clever way in. A loyal friend rowed her across the harbour at night in a small boat. Then he carried her into the palace, rolled up in a bedroll, and unrolled her right in front of Caesar. Caesar, it is said, was charmed by her courage and her wit. He took her side. After a short war, she became queen of Egypt. Later, Cleopatra gave birth to a son. The people of Alexandria called him Caesarion, which means little Caesar. Is the bedroll story true? We cannot be sure. But it is a very good story.

HEAD: Caesar put a golden statue of Cleopatra in a Roman temple, and the whole city whispered.
BODY: In 46 before Christ, Cleopatra came to Rome with Caesarion and her court. Caesar let them live in his villa across the Tiber. Many Romans did not like her. They distrusted foreigners, and they especially disliked kings and queens. Cicero visited her and wrote that he could not stand her. She had once promised him some books, he grumbled, and never sent them. Then Caesar did something that shocked many people. He placed a golden statue of Cleopatra inside his new temple to Venus, the goddess from whom his family claimed to descend. A statue of a living foreign queen, beside a goddess, in a Roman temple! People whispered. They said Caesar wanted to marry her and make himself king. They said he would move the capital to Alexandria. None of these things were true, as far as we know. But the whispers went on, from dinner table to dinner table. And in Rome, whispers could be very dangerous.

HEAD: In Cleopatra's villa, Roman senators met scholars and astronomers from Alexandria.
BODY: What was it like in Cleopatra's villa across the river? We can imagine it. Her household was full of Greek and Egyptian servants, courtiers and scholars. They all spoke Greek, the language of her court. There was Egyptian furniture and fine linen. There were painted vases and the scent of perfume. Visitors came and went. There were Roman senators and poets. There were scholars and astronomers, all eager to meet the famous queen. Little Caesarion, about three years old, played in the garden. In the evenings, lamps glowed on the terrace above the river. Across the water, the lights of Rome flickered on its hills. Some historians think that astronomers from Cleopatra's Alexandria helped Caesar with one of his greatest achievements. It was something we still use today, every single day. It was a new calendar.
