TOPIC: The people of the streets
===
IMG: insula-apartment-block-five-storeys-shops-below | a tall ancient apartment block of brick and timber, five storeys high, with small shops and taverns opening onto the street below and windows above, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Most people in ancient Rome lived in tall apartment blocks called insulae.
BODY: Only the rich lived in large private houses with gardens. Most Romans rented rooms in blocks of flats called insulae, a word that means islands. These buildings often stood four or five storeys high, and some were even taller. On the ground floor there were shops and workshops, open to the street. Above them were flats of different sizes. The best and largest rooms were on the lower floors, where it was easier to escape in case of fire. The higher the floor, the smaller and cheaper the rooms became. Historians know about insulae from Roman writers and from the ruins of Ostia, Rome's port, where several of these buildings still stand. A later record from the fourth century counted more than forty thousand insulae in Rome, compared with under two thousand private houses. Even so, life in an insula was not always unhappy. Neighbours knew each other well, and the busy streets below were full of life from early morning.
===
IMG: narrow-street-tall-buildings-laundry-lines-light | a narrow ancient street between tall apartment houses with washing hung on lines between windows, flower pots on small balconies, a shaft of soft light falling into the street
HEAD: The upper floors of Roman apartment blocks were small, dark and often uncomfortable.
BODY: At the top of the insulae, under the roof, the poorest people lived in tiny rooms. These rooms were hot in summer and cold in winter. There was no running water, no kitchen and no toilet. People carried water up the stairs from public fountains. They used pots for toilets and emptied them into large jars at the bottom of the stairs, or into the street. Cooking was often done on small portable stoves, though many people bought food from street stalls instead. The poet Juvenal, writing more than a century later, joked that anyone walking under the windows at night should make a will first, because of all the things thrown from above. Historians treat his poems as satire, but they reflect real problems. Even so, people did their best to make their rooms pleasant. They hung cloth, kept plants on window ledges and decorated walls with simple paintings. From the high windows, there were wide views across the roofs of the city.
===
IMG: crassus-slaves-water-buckets-street-smoke | a line of men passing leather buckets of water along a narrow ancient street toward a smoking wooden building, people watching from a distance, hazy afternoon light
HEAD: Fires were a constant danger in Rome, and the rich politician Crassus profited from them.
BODY: Many insulae were built cheaply, with wooden floors and thin walls. Landlords wanted to make as much money as possible and spend as little as they could. As a result, buildings sometimes collapsed, and fires spread quickly. There was no public fire brigade in Rome during the time of the Republic. According to the writer Plutarch, Marcus Licinius Crassus, the richest man in Rome, kept a private team of hundreds of slaves trained as builders and firefighters. When a building caught fire, Crassus would offer to buy it, and the buildings next to it, at a very low price. Once the owners had agreed to sell, his men would put out the fire. In this way, he became owner of a large part of the city. Historians note that this story may be exaggerated, but it fits what is known about Crassus. Even so, the problem was real. Later, the emperor Augustus created a public fire brigade, called the vigiles, with thousands of men.
===
IMG: roman-snack-bar-counter-clay-jars-street | a small street snack bar with a stone counter set with big clay jars of hot food, a cook ladling stew, customers standing at the counter, warm evening light
HEAD: Rome was full of street snack bars that sold hot food and drink from jars set into stone counters.
BODY: Since many people had no kitchen at home, they ate out. Rome had many small eating places, open to the street. Their counters were made of stone, with large clay jars set into the top to hold hot food, such as stews, beans and lentils. Customers could buy bread, cheese, olives, sausages and wine. Archaeologists have found many such counters at Pompeii and Herculaneum, the towns buried by Vesuvius in the year 79. One in Pompeii, uncovered in 2020, still had bright paintings of ducks, a rooster and a dog on its front. Scientists found traces of duck, pork, goat, fish and snails in its jars. These places are often called thermopolia, though the Romans may have used other names. Even so, the idea is clear. Eating out was a normal part of daily life for ordinary people in Roman towns. People stopped for a hot bowl of food on their way home and talked with neighbours at the counter.
===
IMG: craftsmen-street-blacksmith-potter-acrobat | an ancient street of craftsmen with a potter at his wheel and a carpenter at work, an acrobat entertaining a small crowd of children nearby, warm golden afternoon light
HEAD: Rome was full of craftsmen, and many trades had their own street or neighbourhood.
BODY: The city depended on the work of thousands of skilled people. There were blacksmiths, carpenters, potters and goldsmiths. There were makers of shoes, rope, lamps, perfume and cloth. Many trades gathered in particular streets, so that people knew where to go for each kind of goods. Some streets were even named after the trades practised there. Shops were small, often a single room opening onto the street, with the family living in a room above or behind. Historians know about these trades from inscriptions, tomb carvings and the remains of workshops. Many craftsmen set up carved stones that showed their tools and work, so their trade would be remembered after death. Even so, the streets were not only for work. Jugglers, acrobats and storytellers performed on corners, and sellers called out their goods. Ancient writers often complained about the noise of Rome, which went on from dawn until late at night. For most Romans, the street was the centre of daily life.
===
IMG: grain-barges-tiber-oxen-towpath-warehouses | heavy wooden barges loaded with sacks of grain being pulled up a calm river by oxen walking along a towpath, warehouses on the bank, soft morning light
HEAD: Caesar reduced the number of Romans receiving free grain by checking the lists house by house.
BODY: Rome was so large that the farms of Italy could not feed it. Most of its grain came by sea from Sicily, Sardinia, North Africa and Egypt. Ships brought it to the port of Ostia, at the mouth of the Tiber. From there, barges were pulled up the river by oxen walking along a towpath, and the grain was stored in huge warehouses near the city. Since the time of the politician Gaius Gracchus, poorer citizens could buy grain at a low price, and later it was given out free. By Caesar's time, around three hundred and twenty thousand people received free grain. According to the historian Suetonius, Caesar ordered a careful count, street by street, with the help of the owners of each apartment block. He reduced the list to about one hundred and fifty thousand. Historians note that this was both a saving and a political risk. Even so, the grain supply remained one of the most important tasks of any Roman leader.
===
IMG: aqueduct-arches-campagna-countryside-evening-light | a long line of tall stone arches carrying an aqueduct across green countryside toward a distant city, sheep grazing beneath, warm golden evening light
HEAD: Fresh water reached Rome through long stone channels called aqueducts.
BODY: Rome needed large amounts of water for drinking, bathing and fountains. The Romans brought it from springs in the hills, sometimes many miles away, using aqueducts. Most of an aqueduct ran underground, through tunnels and covered channels. Only where it crossed valleys did it rise on the famous lines of arches. The water flowed gently downhill the whole way, using only gravity. In 44 before Christ, Rome had four aqueducts. The oldest, the Aqua Appia, had been built in 312 before Christ. The longest, the Aqua Marcia, was more than fifty miles long. Historians and engineers have studied their routes in detail, and parts of them can still be seen in the countryside around Rome. Even so, water was not equally shared. Wealthy people could pay to bring a private pipe into their homes, while most people used public fountains. Later emperors built more aqueducts, until Rome had eleven, bringing an enormous supply of fresh water to the city.
===
IMG: household-workers-kitchen-courtyard-morning | household workers in simple tunics preparing food and carrying water jars in the courtyard kitchen of a Roman house, a young woman writing accounts on a wax tablet, soft morning light
HEAD: A large part of Rome's population was enslaved, but many enslaved people were later freed.
BODY: Slavery was a basic part of Roman society. Historians estimate that perhaps one in four or one in five people in the city of Rome was enslaved, though exact numbers are unknown. Enslaved people came from many places. Some had been captured in wars, others were sold by traders, and many were born into slavery. They worked in houses, shops, workshops and farms. Some were teachers, doctors, secretaries or skilled craftsmen. Their lives varied greatly, from hard labour in mines to trusted positions in wealthy homes. Yet all of them could be bought and sold, and they had almost no rights. Unlike in many other slave societies, Romans often freed their slaves, especially those who worked closely with them. Freed people, called freedmen and freedwomen, became Roman citizens, and their children were fully free. Historians find many freedmen in inscriptions, running businesses and building fine tombs. Even so, freedom was never guaranteed, and many enslaved people lived and died without it.
===
IMG: saturnalia-feast-family-gifts-candles-merry-room | a cheerful Roman family and their household sharing a feast together in a room decorated with green branches, small gifts and candles on the table, everyone wearing soft felt caps, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: At the festival of Saturnalia in December, Romans exchanged gifts and masters sometimes served their slaves.
BODY: The Roman year was full of religious festivals, and the merriest of all was the Saturnalia. It began on the seventeenth of December and, by Caesar's time, lasted several days. It honoured Saturn, an ancient god of farming. During the festival, schools and courts closed, and normal rules were turned upside down. People wore relaxed clothing and a soft felt cap, a sign of freedom. Families held feasts and gave each other gifts, often candles and small clay figures. In many households, masters served dinner to their slaves or let them speak freely. Historians know about the Saturnalia from writers such as Seneca, Catullus and Macrobius. The poet Catullus called it the best of days. Even so, the reversal of roles was only for a short time. When the festival ended, ordinary life returned. Some historians suggest that customs of the Saturnalia, such as gift giving and candles in midwinter, later influenced the ways Christmas was celebrated.

TOPIC: Baths and races
===
IMG: roman-public-baths-entrance-columns-afternoon-crowd | the columned entrance of an ancient public bath house with people arriving carrying towels and small flasks of oil, steam drifting from a vent on the roof, warm afternoon light
HEAD: A visit to the public baths in Rome cost the smallest coin, and children often entered free.
BODY: In the afternoon, after the day's work, many Romans went to the baths. In 44 before Christ, there were already many public bathhouses in the city, most of them privately owned. The entrance fee was very low, usually a quadrans, the smallest Roman coin. Children often entered free. This meant that almost everyone could afford to bathe regularly. The baths were not only places for washing. They had courtyards for exercise, rooms for massage and areas where people could talk, eat and do business. Historians know about Roman baths from writers and from many surviving ruins across the empire. The great imperial baths of Rome, such as the Baths of Caracalla, were built later and were enormous, with libraries and gardens. Even so, the baths of Caesar's time were already a central part of city life. For many Romans, a daily visit to the baths was one of life's simple pleasures, a place to relax, meet friends and hear the latest news.
===
IMG: caldarium-steam-hot-pool-bathers-soft-light | a steamy hot bath room with a warm pool, bathers relaxing on stone benches, light falling softly through a high window, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: Romans did not use soap, but cleaned themselves with oil and a curved metal scraper.
BODY: Bathers followed a regular order through the rooms of the baths. After exercising, they went to the warm room, called the tepidarium, to relax. Then they entered the hot room, the caldarium, which was full of steam and had a pool of hot water. Floors were often heated from below by hot air from a furnace, a system called a hypocaust. The floors could be so hot that bathers wore wooden sandals. Instead of soap, Romans rubbed oil onto their skin and then scraped it off, together with sweat and dirt, using a curved metal tool called a strigil. Finally, many bathers took a cold plunge in the frigidarium. Archaeologists have found many strigils, often with small oil flasks attached. Historians note that soap existed, but Romans used it mainly for hair or as medicine. Even so, the oil and strigil method left the skin clean and soft. After the bath, people often rested, chatted or had a snack before going home for dinner.
===
IMG: bath-house-courtyard-gossip-snack-seller | people in tunics chatting and laughing in the colonnaded courtyard of an ancient bath house, a snack seller carrying a tray, a barber trimming hair, soft afternoon light
HEAD: A Roman philosopher who lived above a bathhouse complained about all the noise.
BODY: Roman baths were lively and noisy places. People talked, laughed, exercised and did business. Sellers walked around offering sausages, cakes and drinks. The philosopher Seneca, writing about a century after Caesar, described living in a room above a bathhouse. In one of his letters, he complained about the grunting of men lifting weights, the slap of hands during massages and the shouts of ball players. He mentioned a man who loved to sing in the bath and the cries of the sausage seller and the cake seller. Above all, he mentioned someone shouting whenever a thief was caught stealing clothes. Historians enjoy this letter because it gives such a vivid picture of everyday life. Even so, Seneca used it to make a philosophical point. He argued that a calm mind could stay peaceful even in the middle of noise. For ordinary Romans, the bustle of the baths was part of the pleasure, a daily meeting place for the whole neighbourhood.
===
IMG: circus-maximus-chariots-racing-crowds-sunlit-sand | four-horse chariots racing around a long sandy track in a vast ancient stadium, huge crowds in the stands, statues on a low central barrier, bright sunshine and golden dust
HEAD: At the chariot races in the Circus Maximus, rich and poor, men and women sat together.
BODY: The Romans loved public entertainment. The most popular events were chariot races, held in the Circus Maximus, a long racing track in the valley between the Palatine and Aventine hills. Caesar enlarged the Circus, and historians estimate it could hold around one hundred and fifty thousand spectators in his time. Later it became even larger. Teams of horses pulled light chariots around a long central barrier, seven laps per race. Drivers belonged to teams known by their colours, such as the Reds and the Whites, and later also the Greens and the Blues. Fans supported their colours with great passion. Unlike at the theatre and the gladiator games, men and women could sit together at the races. The poet Ovid, writing later, even advised young men that the races were a good place to meet someone. Even so, racing was dangerous, and crashes were common. The shape of the Circus Maximus can still be seen today as a long, grassy park.
===
IMG: theatre-of-pompey-garden-colonnade-statues-fountains | a long garden behind an ancient theatre surrounded by colonnades, shady plane trees, statues and fountains, people strolling slowly, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Rome's first permanent stone theatre, built by Pompey, had a large garden and a hall where the Senate sometimes met.
BODY: For a long time, Romans built only temporary wooden theatres, which were taken down after each festival. Some senators thought that permanent theatres would encourage idleness. In 55 before Christ, Pompey built the first permanent stone theatre in Rome, in the Field of Mars. To avoid criticism, he placed a temple of Venus at the top of the seating, so that the theatre could be described as steps leading to a temple. Behind the stage lay a large garden surrounded by covered walkways, with fountains, statues and shady trees. At the far end of this garden stood a hall, the Curia of Pompey, where the Senate sometimes met while the main Senate house was being rebuilt. Historians know about the complex from ancient writers, a marble map of Rome and archaeological remains. Even so, little of it is visible today, because later buildings were built on top. The curve of the theatre can still be traced in the shape of modern streets.

TOPIC: Cicero and his letters
===
IMG: cicero-older-man-writing-letter-study-scrolls | an older Roman man with a kind, clever face writing a letter at a wooden desk in a quiet study, scrolls on shelves, a small oil lamp, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: More than nine hundred letters written by or to Cicero have survived.
BODY: Marcus Tullius Cicero was the most famous speaker and writer of the late Roman Republic. Fortunately for historians, a large collection of his letters survives. It includes letters to his friend Atticus, to his family and to many political figures, as well as some letters written to him. Together, they number more than nine hundred. They were collected and published after his death, probably by his secretary Tiro and others. Historians value them because they show the thoughts, worries and daily concerns of a real person living through the end of the Republic. Cicero wrote about politics, money, his health, his houses and his family. He was sometimes proud, sometimes anxious and often funny. Even so, the letters must be read carefully. Cicero wrote for different readers and sometimes changed his tone depending on who would read them. Yet no other figure of the ancient world is known in such personal detail. His letters remain one of the most important sources for the year 44 before Christ.
===
IMG: two-friends-talking-garden-bench-scrolls | two older Roman men in simple tunics sitting on a stone bench in a quiet garden, one holding a scroll, both smiling, cypress trees and a small fountain, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Cicero's closest friend, Atticus, stayed out of politics and was respected by people on every side.
BODY: Titus Pomponius Atticus was a wealthy Roman of the equestrian class, the rank just below senators. He spent many years living in Athens, where he earned his nickname, Atticus, meaning a man of Attica. Unlike Cicero, Atticus avoided politics and took no side in the civil wars. He helped friends on all sides with money and advice, and he was respected by many leaders who were enemies of one another. He also published books, using educated slaves to copy texts. Cicero wrote to him more often than to anyone else. Around four hundred of these letters survive, though Atticus's replies are lost. Historians know about Atticus's life from these letters and from a short biography written by his friend Cornelius Nepos. Even so, his careful neutrality was not always easy. In dangerous times, staying out of politics required great skill and caution. Atticus survived the civil wars and died peacefully in 32 before Christ, an unusual fate for a prominent Roman of his time.
===
IMG: arpinum-hill-town-stone-houses-valley | a small ancient hill town of stone houses and walls on a green hillside above a river valley, olive trees and mountains behind, soft golden evening light
HEAD: Cicero came from a small hill town called Arpinum and was the first in his family to become consul.
BODY: Cicero was born in 106 before Christ in Arpinum, a small town in the hills about seventy miles southeast of Rome. His family was respectable and well off, but no member had ever held high office in Rome. Romans called such a man a new man, novus homo. As a young man, Cicero came to Rome to study law and public speaking. He became famous as a lawyer through his brilliant speeches in court. In 63 before Christ, he was elected consul, the highest office of the Republic. During that year, he exposed a plot led by a nobleman named Catiline and had several conspirators executed. He was praised as father of his country, but the executions were later used against him. Historians note that Arpinum also produced another famous Roman, the general Gaius Marius. Even so, Cicero never forgot his origins. He loved his home town and described the beauty of its rivers and hills in his writings.
===
IMG: cicero-walking-garden-colonnade-scroll-thinking | an older Roman man walking slowly alone along a shady garden colonnade, holding a scroll, looking thoughtful, soft late afternoon light through the columns
HEAD: After the death of his daughter Tullia, Cicero found comfort in writing books of philosophy.
BODY: During the civil war, Cicero had taken the side of Pompey against Caesar. After Pompey's defeat, Caesar pardoned him, and the two men treated each other politely. Caesar admired Cicero's writing. Even so, Cicero had little role in politics under Caesar. Then, in February 45 before Christ, his beloved daughter Tullia died, shortly after giving birth. Cicero was overwhelmed with grief. His letters from this time show deep sadness. He withdrew to his country houses and turned to writing. In the following two years, he wrote an astonishing number of works on philosophy, including books on friendship, old age, duty and the nature of the gods. Historians consider this one of the most productive periods of any ancient writer. His book on old age, written in 44 before Christ, describes how a person can grow old with dignity. Even so, writing did not remove his pain. Yet these books became some of the most read works of the ancient world.
===
IMG: roman-villa-library-shelves-scroll-cases-sunlight | a quiet villa library with wooden shelves of scrolls in round leather cases, a reading couch and a window looking onto a garden, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm sunlight
HEAD: Cicero wrote to a friend that a garden and a library were all a person needed.
BODY: Cicero owned several country houses in Italy, and he loved them all. His favourite was at Tusculum, in the hills southeast of Rome. It had gardens, shady walkways and a library. Like other wealthy Romans, Cicero collected books, which were rolls of papyrus kept in wooden cupboards or boxes. He asked Atticus for help in arranging his library and employed skilled slaves to repair and label the scrolls. In a letter to his friend Varro, Cicero wrote that if one has a garden in the library, nothing will be lacking. Historians often quote this line, though it is usually simplified as a garden and a library. Even so, Cicero's houses were also places of work. He wrote many of his books there, walking in his gardens as he thought. The remains of grand Roman villas around Tusculum can still be seen today. They show how educated Romans combined comfort, nature and learning in their country homes.
===
IMG: freedman-secretary-writing-shorthand-tablet | a gentle-looking Roman man in a plain tunic writing quickly on a wax tablet while an older man speaks beside him, scrolls on a table, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft lamplight
HEAD: Cicero's secretary Tiro created a system of shorthand, and one of its signs is still used in Ireland.
BODY: Marcus Tullius Tiro had been born into slavery in Cicero's household. He was well educated and became Cicero's secretary and trusted helper. In 53 before Christ, Cicero freed him. Tiro remained loyal for the rest of Cicero's life. Cicero's letters to him are full of warmth, often asking about his health, since Tiro was frequently ill. Tiro is believed to have developed a system of shorthand, a way of writing quickly using signs. It was later known as Tironian notes and was used for centuries in Europe. One sign, a simple mark meaning and, is still used today on some road signs in Ireland. Historians note that the system was later expanded by others, so not every sign came from Tiro. Even so, his work made it possible to record speeches word for word. After Cicero's death, Tiro helped collect and publish his letters and speeches. He lived to a great age, reportedly almost a hundred, on a small farm.
===
IMG: cicero-at-window-evening-looking-toward-city | an older Roman man standing at the window of a country villa in the evening, looking toward a distant city on the hills, a lamp lit behind him, soft dusky light
HEAD: In the early months of 44 before Christ, Cicero watched events in Rome with growing unease.
BODY: In early 44 before Christ, Cicero spent much of his time away from Rome, writing at his country houses. He followed events in the city through letters and visitors. Caesar had been named dictator for life, and the Senate gave him one honour after another. His statue was placed among those of the ancient kings, and his image appeared on coins, something new for a living Roman. Many senators who had fought for the Republic felt that their freedom was gone. Cicero shared these feelings, but he did not take part in any plot. Historians note that he was not invited, perhaps because the conspirators feared that he was too cautious or too talkative. In his letters, he wrote that the future was uncertain. Even so, he could not imagine what would happen in March. His letters from these months show a man waiting and watching, unsure how the Republic he loved could survive under the rule of one man.

TOPIC: Scrolls, schools and the gods
===
IMG: roman-bookshop-scroll-shelves-arcade-customers | a small bookshop in an arcade with shelves of rolled scrolls, each with a little label hanging from it, customers browsing, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Roman books were long scrolls of papyrus, read by unrolling them from one side to the other.
BODY: In Caesar's time, books were not made of pages bound together. They were scrolls, long rolls of papyrus, a writing material made from a plant that grew in Egypt. A single scroll could be several metres long. To read it, a person held it in both hands and unrolled it slowly from one side, rolling it up on the other. Each scroll held only part of a long work, so a large book needed many scrolls. Books were copied by hand, often by educated slaves working together in workshops. Bookshops stood in busy streets near the Forum. Customers could browse and order copies of popular works. Historians know about the book trade from writers such as Cicero, Catullus and later Martial, who mentioned where his own poems could be bought. Even so, books were expensive. Most were owned by wealthy people. The book with pages, called a codex, came into common use only several centuries later.
===
IMG: grammaticus-teaching-boys-homer-scroll-courtyard | a teacher reading from a scroll to a few older boys in a sunny courtyard of a house, the boys listening on a stone bench, potted plants, soft morning light
HEAD: Children of wealthy Roman families studied Greek poetry and then the art of public speaking.
BODY: Most Roman children who went to school at all attended a primary school, learning to read, write and count. Boys from wealthy families continued to a second teacher, called a grammaticus. He taught Greek and Latin literature, especially the poems of Homer, which every educated Roman knew. Pupils read texts aloud, learned them by heart and studied grammar and style. Many teachers were Greek, and some had been enslaved before being freed. After this stage, young men studied rhetoric, the art of public speaking, which was essential for careers in law and politics. Some went to Greece, to Athens or Rhodes, to complete their education. Historians know about Roman schools from writers such as Quintilian, who wrote a long book on how to train a speaker. Even so, girls rarely received this kind of education, though some daughters of rich families were taught at home. Cicero's daughter Tullia, for example, was known as an educated and intelligent woman.
===
IMG: temple-vesta-round-sacred-fire-priestesses | a small round temple with slender columns, a sacred fire glowing through its open door, two priestesses in long white robes and veils standing quietly beside it, soft evening light
HEAD: Six priestesses called Vestal Virgins kept a sacred fire burning in Rome day and night.
BODY: In the Forum, close to Caesar's official residence, stood a small round temple dedicated to Vesta, the goddess of the hearth. Inside burned a sacred fire, which represented the life of the Roman state. Six priestesses, known as the Vestal Virgins, guarded it. They were chosen as young girls from respected families and served for at least thirty years. During this time, they had to remain unmarried. If the fire went out, it was seen as a terrible sign, and the priestess responsible was punished. The Vestals were highly honoured. They had special seats at games, could own property and were trusted to keep important documents, including wills. Historians know about them from many ancient writers and from inscriptions and statues found in their house beside the temple. Even so, their lives were strictly controlled. Caesar's own will was kept in their care. The remains of the round temple and the House of the Vestals can still be seen in the Forum today.
===
IMG: family-offering-household-shrine-incense-bread-wine | a Roman family gathered at a small painted household shrine in their home, offering bread, wine and incense beside a little lamp, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm soft light
HEAD: Romans believed in many gods, including spirits that protected the house, the door and even the hinges.
BODY: Roman religion included the great gods of the state, such as Jupiter, Juno, Minerva, Mars and Venus. But Romans also honoured many smaller spirits connected with daily life. Each household had its own protective gods, called the Lares and Penates. Their images were kept in a small shrine, often in the main hall or kitchen. Families offered them food, wine and incense, especially on special days. The god Janus watched over doorways and beginnings, and the month of January is named after him. Later writers listed even smaller divine powers, such as a goddess of door hinges. Historians know about household worship from writers and from shrines found in houses at Pompeii, many still decorated with paintings of the Lares. Even so, religion was less about personal belief than about correct practice. Romans believed that if they performed the rituals properly, the gods would protect them. These small daily offerings were part of family life in every Roman home.
===
IMG: temple-interior-cella-large-statue-goddess-dim-light | the inside of a quiet ancient temple with one tall marble statue of a goddess at the far end, painted walls and gifts placed around it, dim soft light from the doorway, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: When praying, Romans covered their heads with a fold of their toga.
BODY: For Romans, religion worked like an agreement between people and gods. People gave the gods sacrifices, offerings and prayers. In return, they hoped for protection, good harvests and success. Everything had to be done correctly, with the right words and actions. If a mistake was made, the ritual had to be repeated from the beginning. When praying or making a sacrifice, a Roman man pulled a fold of his toga over his head. This was believed to block out unlucky sights and sounds. Statues of this pose survive, including a famous one of the emperor Augustus as a priest. Inside temples stood large statues of the gods, often made of marble or bronze, lit by lamps. Most ceremonies, however, took place outside, at altars in front of the temple. Historians note that Roman religion was closely tied to politics. Priests were often senators, and Caesar himself was chief priest. Even so, ordinary people took part in festivals and offerings throughout the year.

TOPIC: Caesar at home, and a queen across the river
===
IMG: calpurnia-roman-matron-garden-quiet-morning | a gentle Roman woman of about thirty in a long pale dress sitting quietly in a house garden with a small fountain and flowers, looking thoughtful, soft morning light
HEAD: Caesar's wife, Calpurnia, had been married to him since 59 before Christ.
BODY: Calpurnia was Caesar's third wife. She was the daughter of Lucius Calpurnius Piso, a powerful senator. They married in 59 before Christ, the year Caesar was consul, and the marriage helped strengthen his political alliances. Calpurnia was much younger than Caesar, perhaps by more than twenty years. For much of their marriage, Caesar was away fighting in Gaul and in the civil war, so they spent long periods apart. They had no children. Historians know very little about Calpurnia's own thoughts and character, since no letters of hers survive. Ancient writers describe her as loyal and devoted. Even so, the marriage was mainly a political arrangement, as was common among Roman nobles. Caesar also had a famous relationship with Cleopatra during these years. Calpurnia lived with Caesar in the official house of the chief priest in the Forum. She appears most clearly in the story of the night before the Ides of March, when she is said to have had a frightening dream.

HEAD: Ancient writers said Caesar could dictate several letters at the same time.
BODY: Caesar was famous for his energy and capacity for work. He rose early and worked late, reading reports, writing letters and giving orders. According to the writer Pliny the Elder, Caesar could dictate up to four letters at once to different secretaries, or even seven when he was not doing anything else. Plutarch adds that he dictated letters while riding on horseback. Historians treat these claims with some caution, as ancient writers liked to make great men seem extraordinary. Yet they reflect a real reputation for speed and efficiency. Caesar also wrote clear and simple Latin. His books about his wars in Gaul and the civil war are still read today, and many students learn Latin from them. Suetonius reports that Caesar used a simple code in his private letters, shifting each letter of the alphabet by three places. This is now known as the Caesar cipher. Even so, his heavy workload left little time for rest. Some friends worried about his health.
===
IMG: roman-horseman-galloping-countryside-hands-behind | a Roman rider in a red cloak galloping a fine horse across open green countryside, hills behind, warm golden evening light
HEAD: According to Plutarch, Caesar could ride a galloping horse with his hands clasped behind his back.
BODY: Caesar was not a large or naturally strong man, but he trained his body hard. Ancient writers describe him as an excellent horseman and a tireless traveller. Plutarch writes that, as a young man, Caesar could ride at full gallop with his hands held behind his back. On campaign, he often marched on foot at the head of his men, in rain or sun. He could travel enormous distances quickly, sometimes covering a hundred miles a day in a light carriage. Caesar also suffered from health problems. Ancient writers mention headaches and fainting fits, which some described as epilepsy. In his later years, these attacks may have become more frequent. Historians and doctors have discussed what illness he might have had, but no certain answer is possible. Even so, his toughness impressed his soldiers. He shared their hardships, knew many of them by name and was admired for his courage. This close bond with his army was one of the foundations of his power.

IMG: cleopatra-young-queen-egyptian-greek-dress-calm | a young queen of about twenty-five in a pale Greek-style dress with a simple gold band in her dark hair, standing calmly in a sunlit palace hall with columns, clever and composed
HEAD: Cleopatra, queen of Egypt, spoke many languages and was the first of her royal family to learn Egyptian.
BODY: Cleopatra the Seventh was born in 69 before Christ. She belonged to the Ptolemaic dynasty, a Greek-speaking royal family that had ruled Egypt for nearly three hundred years, since the time of Alexander the Great. According to Plutarch, she was highly intelligent and spoke many languages. She was said to be the first ruler of her family who learned to speak Egyptian, the language of the people she governed. Ancient writers suggest that her charm came less from her appearance than from her conversation and character. Coins with her portrait show a woman with a strong nose and a determined expression. Historians note that much of what Roman writers said about her was hostile, since she later became an enemy of Rome. Even so, it is clear that she was a skilled and ambitious ruler. In 44 before Christ, she was about twenty-five years old and was staying in Rome, at a villa belonging to Caesar across the Tiber.

IMG: small-boat-evening-harbour-palace-steps-alexandria | a small rowing boat gliding toward the marble steps of a palace by the sea in the evening, a single lamp on the boat, a tall lighthouse glowing in the distance, deep blue dusk
HEAD: According to Plutarch, Cleopatra was secretly carried to Caesar inside a bedroll.
BODY: Cleopatra first met Caesar in 48 before Christ in Alexandria, when she was about twenty-one. At that time, she was in conflict with her younger brother, Ptolemy the Thirteenth, over the throne of Egypt. His advisers had driven her out of the city. Caesar had arrived in Alexandria in pursuit of Pompey. Cleopatra needed to meet him, but her brother's soldiers controlled the palace. According to Plutarch, she travelled secretly by small boat at dusk with a single companion, a Sicilian named Apollodorus. He wrapped her in a bedroll, or a sack for bedding, tied it with a strap and carried it into the palace to Caesar. The story is often told as a rolled carpet, but Plutarch describes bedding. Historians cannot confirm every detail. Even so, the meeting changed history. Caesar supported Cleopatra, and after a short war, she became queen of Egypt. In 47 before Christ, she gave birth to a son, Caesarion, whom she said was Caesar's child.

HEAD: Caesar placed a golden statue of Cleopatra in a temple in Rome, which caused much gossip.
BODY: In 46 before Christ, Cleopatra came to Rome with her young son Caesarion and her court. Caesar allowed them to stay at his villa and gardens on the far side of the Tiber. Many Romans disliked her presence. Romans were suspicious of foreign rulers, especially queens, and they disapproved of Caesar's relationship with her. Caesar also placed a golden statue of Cleopatra in the new Temple of Venus Genetrix in his forum. This caused much talk, since it placed a foreign queen beside a goddess. Cicero visited Cleopatra at the villa and later wrote in a letter that he disliked her, complaining of her pride. Historians use these sources carefully, as Roman writers were often hostile. Even so, Cleopatra's visit shows the international nature of Caesar's world. She remained in Rome until after Caesar's death. In April 44 before Christ, she left for Egypt with her son, where she continued to rule for many more years.

IMG: villa-interior-egyptian-greek-furnishings-lamps-evening | the inside of an elegant villa with Egyptian and Greek furnishings, fine linen curtains, painted vases and bronze lamps glowing, guests talking quietly, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm evening light
HEAD: Cleopatra's household in Rome included scholars and servants from Alexandria.
BODY: Cleopatra's court in Rome brought something of Alexandria to the city. Alexandria was one of the greatest centres of learning in the ancient world, with its famous library and scholars of every kind. Her household was full of Greek and Egyptian servants, advisers and educated companions. Visitors to the villa would have heard Greek spoken, the language of her court, and seen luxurious furnishings from Egypt. Roman senators and important figures came to visit, some out of interest and some out of duty. Historians know few specific details of daily life at the villa. Even so, the connection with Alexandria had real effects on Rome. One of the scholars associated with Caesar at this time was the Alexandrian astronomer Sosigenes, who helped him create a new calendar. Historians see Caesar's interest in Egyptian knowledge as part of his wider plans to reform Roman life. The meeting of Roman and Egyptian worlds in this villa was brief, but it left lasting traces.
