At the Theatre of Pompey, the senators have been waiting all morning.

The plotters are there, early, with their togas wrapped around them. Brutus and Cassius are there, doing their work as judges, in the colonnade of the gardens, listening to cases, calmly, as if it were an ordinary day.

--

But it has been a long and anxious morning for them.

A message comes to Brutus that his wife, Porcia, who knows everything, has fainted at home from the strain, and may be dying. Brutus is deeply troubled, but he stays. Porcia, in fact, recovers soon after.

A senator, who is not part of the plot, comes up to Brutus and Cassius, and says to them quietly: I wish you success in what you have in mind. But be quick. Everyone is talking about it. And then he walks away, leaving them wondering how much he knows.

And when Caesar's litter arrives, at last, another senator goes up to Caesar, and talks with him earnestly, for a long time. The plotters watch, and wonder, and fear that they have been betrayed. But at the end, the senator kisses Caesar's hand, and goes away. He was only asking a favour.

--

Before Caesar goes into the hall, one of the plotters, Trebonius, takes Mark Antony aside, outside the door, and keeps him talking there.

The plotters had decided not to harm Antony. Brutus insisted on it. It must not look like a quarrel between parties, he said. It must be an act of justice, against one man only, the man who had made himself master of Rome. They would harm no one else.

{img:hall-entrance-columns-two-men-talking-outside}
So Caesar goes into the hall alone.

--

The senators rise from their seats, as he comes in, as a mark of respect. And he goes to his golden chair, at the far end of the hall, and sits down.

Behind him, there is the great statue of Pompey the Great, his old friend, and his son-in-law, and his rival, whom he defeated, looking down.

Then the plotters gather around Caesar, as if to greet him, and to ask him favours, as senators always do.

--

One of them, Tillius Cimber, comes forward with a petition, asking Caesar to let his brother, who is in exile, come home. Caesar says that he will consider it another time. Cimber takes hold of Caesar's toga, as if pleading with him, and pulls it from his shoulders.

That is the signal.

{img:distant-view-columns-hall-doorway-figures-crowding}
And there, in the hall of Pompey, at the foot of Pompey's statue, the senators surround Caesar, and strike him down.

--

It is over in a few moments.

The ancient writers say that, at first, Caesar tries to defend himself. But when he sees Brutus among the men around him, he stops, and draws his toga over his face, and lets himself fall.

Some people say that, as he saw Brutus, Caesar spoke in Greek, the language of the educated Romans: kai su, teknon. You too, my child? Others say that he did not speak at all. The famous words, Et tu, Brute, were written more than sixteen hundred years later, by William Shakespeare, for his play.

--

Gaius Julius Caesar, the greatest man of his age, has passed away, at the age of fifty-five, on the Ides of March, in the year 44 before Christ, at the foot of the statue of the man he defeated.

Brutus turns to the senators, to make a speech. He wants to explain to them why it was done: for liberty, for the Republic. But the senators do not wait to listen. They run, all of them, out of the hall, and out into the streets, in panic.

{img:senators-hurrying-away-colonnade-togas-confusion}
The news spreads through the city like the wind. People close their shops, and run home, and lock their doors. Nobody knows what will happen next. Will there be fighting in the streets? Will Caesar's soldiers come, and take revenge?

--

The plotters walk through the streets, from the Theatre of Pompey, to the Capitol, with their togas wrapped around their left arms, calling out to the people that they have killed the tyrant, and that Rome is free.

But the people do not cheer. They are silent, and afraid, and they stay in their houses.

The plotters climb up to the Capitol, the hill of the Temple of Jupiter, and stay there, under the protection of a band of gladiators that Decimus Brutus has hired, and wait to see what will happen.

--

In the empty hall of Pompey, Caesar lies alone, for several hours, at the foot of the statue.

At last, three of his slaves come, and gently lift him into a litter, and carry him home, through the streets, to the Domus Publica, to Calpurnia. As they pass, people come out of their doors, and look, and weep.

{img:three-slaves-carrying-litter-quiet-street-dusk}
And Calpurnia's dream has come true.
