Rome, in the year 44, is a city of books.

Not books like ours, with pages, but scrolls: long rolls of papyrus, a kind of paper, made in Egypt, from the stems of the papyrus reed, which grows along the Nile. The text is written in columns, side by side, along the scroll, and you unroll it with one hand, and roll it up with the other, as you read.

--

There are bookshops in Rome, along the streets near the Forum, where you can buy the latest books: poems, and histories, and speeches, and plays, and books about farming, and cooking, and medicine.

The books are copied by hand, by teams of educated slaves, sitting in rows, writing as someone reads the text aloud to them. A popular book might be copied hundreds of times. Atticus, Cicero's friend, has a great team of copyists, and publishes Cicero's books, and sends copies to his friends, and sells them.

Rich Romans have libraries in their houses, with shelves full of scrolls, each scroll with a little label hanging from it, giving the title. Cicero, as we have heard, loves his libraries more than anything.

--

Roman children go to school when they are about seven years old, if their parents can afford it.

The school is usually a single room, or a corner of an arcade, opening onto the street, behind a curtain. The teacher, the ludi magister, is often a Greek freedman, poorly paid, and rather strict. Lessons begin at dawn, and go on until midday.

{img:roman-school-arcade-children-wax-tablets-teacher}
The children learn to read and write, in Latin and in Greek, by copying letters onto little wooden tablets, covered with wax, with a pointed stylus. The other end of the stylus is flat, for rubbing out mistakes. They learn to count, using their fingers, and little pebbles, and the abacus. They learn long passages of poetry by heart, chanting them together, in loud voices.

--

Girls often go to the first school too, with their brothers, and learn to read and write. But most of them stop going to school at about twelve, and learn to run a household, and spin and weave, at home. Some girls from rich families are educated at home, by tutors, and become very learned.

The boys of rich families go on, to the school of the grammaticus, where they read the great Greek and Latin poets, Homer, above all, and learn about history, and mythology, and geography, and astronomy.

{img:grammaticus-teaching-boys-homer-scroll-courtyard}
And then, at about sixteen, they go on to the school of the rhetor, the teacher of speaking. There, they learn how to make speeches, which is the most important skill for a young Roman who wants to be a lawyer, or a politician. They practise making speeches about imaginary cases, and famous questions from history.

--

The best students, like Cicero's son, and Caesar's great-nephew Octavius, go on to study in Greece: in Athens, or in Rhodes, or in Apollonia, with the most famous teachers of philosophy and rhetoric.

Caesar himself studied in Rhodes, as a young man, with the great teacher Apollonius Molon, who also taught Cicero. It was on his way there that he was captured by the pirates.

There are also many learned men in Rome itself: Greek philosophers and scholars, doctors and astronomers and architects, who come to Rome to teach, and to work for the great Roman families. Caesar has given Roman citizenship to every doctor and every teacher of the liberal arts who lives in Rome, so that more of them will come.

--

In the evenings, in the houses of the rich, people read aloud to each other.

The Romans almost always read aloud, even when they are alone, murmuring the words. Silent reading is so unusual that people remark on it when they see it.

{img:evening-reading-aloud-family-oil-lamp-scroll-quiet}
And in the year 44, the books that everyone is reading include Caesar's own books, his commentaries on the war in Gaul, written in the third person, as if about someone else, in that clear, plain style, which Cicero himself admires. The book begins with words that schoolchildren still learn today: Gaul, as a whole, is divided into three parts.
