The Romans believe in signs and omens.

They believe that the gods tell people what is going to happen, through signs: the flight of birds, and thunder and lightning, and dreams, and strange happenings in nature. And they have special priests, the augurs and the haruspices, whose work is to read these signs, and tell what they mean.

--

In the weeks before the Ides of March, the ancient writers tell us, there are many strange signs.

Of course, the writers lived many years later, and wrote these stories down long after the event, and some of them may have been invented, or improved, afterwards. But they are part of the story, and the Romans believed them.

They say that there were strange lights in the sky, and noises in the night. That lonely birds came down into the Forum. That the horses that Caesar had dedicated to the gods, when he crossed the Rubicon, and set free to graze by the river, refused to eat, and wept.

--

And they say that an old soothsayer, a haruspex, named Spurinna, warned Caesar, while he was offering a sacrifice, that he should beware of a great danger, which would come no later than the Ides of March.

Caesar, who does not believe very much in omens, takes no notice.

{img:caesar-passing-soothsayer-street-crowd-sunlight}
Caesar's friends are worried about him. They have heard rumours. They beg him to take a bodyguard. For some time, he has had a guard of Spanish soldiers, who went with him everywhere. But he has sent them away. He says that it is better to die once than to live always in fear. He says that his safety is more important to Rome than to himself. If anything happens to him, Rome will have no peace, and will fall into civil war again.

--

On the evening of the fourteenth of March, the day before the Ides, Caesar goes to dinner at the house of his friend Marcus Aemilius Lepidus, his master of horse, his second in command.

It is a pleasant evening, with friends. While the others talk, Caesar reclines on his couch, and signs letters, as he always does.

And during the dinner, the conversation turns, as conversations often do among the Romans, who are very interested in philosophy, to a serious question: what is the best kind of death?

And Caesar looks up from his letters, and says, before anyone else can answer: a sudden one. One that is unexpected.

--

That night, in the Domus Publica, in the Forum, Caesar and Calpurnia sleep badly.

The ancient writers say that, in the night, all the doors and windows of their bedroom fly open at once, with a great noise, and wake Caesar up. And in the moonlight, he sees Calpurnia, asleep beside him, moaning and murmuring words he cannot understand.

{img:roman-bedroom-night-shutters-open-moonlight-curtains}
She is dreaming. In her dream, she tells him afterwards, she held him in her arms, and wept for him.

--

In the morning, the fifteenth of March, Calpurnia begs Caesar not to go to the Senate.

She has never been superstitious, or anxious, before. But now she is frightened, and pleads with him to stay at home, and to put off the meeting of the Senate until another day.

{img:calpurnia-pleading-caesar-atrium-morning-grey-light}
And Caesar, who has also not been feeling well, and has perhaps had a dizzy spell that morning, is moved by her fear. He orders the priests to make sacrifices, and to see what the signs say. And the signs, the priests tell him, are bad.

So Caesar decides to stay at home. He will send Mark Antony to the Senate, to tell them that the meeting is put off.

--

But then Decimus Brutus arrives at the house.

Decimus is one of the plotters. And he knows that, if the meeting is put off, the plot may be discovered. So he speaks to Caesar, cheerfully, and laughs at the omens. He says that the senators are all waiting for him, ready to vote him great honours. He asks what people would say, if Caesar told the Senate to go home, and come back another day, when Calpurnia had better dreams.

He says that, if Caesar really thinks the day is unlucky, he should at least go to the Senate himself, and tell them so, in person.

And he takes Caesar by the hand, and leads him out of the house.

--

It is about the fifth hour of the morning, about eleven o'clock, by our time.

Caesar is carried through the streets, in his litter, from the Forum, toward the Field of Mars, and the Theatre of Pompey. As always, the crowds gather around him, and people push forward, to give him letters and petitions.

{img:litter-carried-through-crowded-street-petitioners}
One of them, the story says, is a Greek teacher named Artemidorus, who has somehow found out about the plot. He pushes his way through the crowd, and gives Caesar a little scroll, and says: Read this, Caesar, by yourself, and quickly. It concerns you closely.

Caesar takes it, and holds it in his hand. But so many people are pressing round him, that he never has a chance to read it.

--

As he passes the old soothsayer, Spurinna, Caesar calls out to him, with a smile: the Ides of March have come.

And Spurinna answers, quietly: yes, they have come. But they have not yet gone.
