Among the senators of Rome, in the year 44, there is a quiet, serious man of about forty, named Marcus Junius Brutus.

Brutus is a thoughtful man, a lover of philosophy, especially the philosophy of Plato. He reads a great deal, and writes books, and is respected by everyone for his honesty, and his seriousness, and his learning. He is not a great soldier, or a great speaker. But people trust him.

--

Brutus has a famous ancestor. Or at least, he believes he does.

More than four hundred and fifty years ago, a man named Lucius Junius Brutus led the people of Rome in driving out their last king, Tarquin the Proud, and founded the Republic. Marcus Brutus believes that he is descended from him. And on the Capitol, there is an old statue of that first Brutus, with a drawn sword in his hand.

Brutus's mother, Servilia, is a remarkable woman, clever and strong and well connected, who was, for many years, the woman Caesar loved most. There were even rumours, in Rome, that Brutus might be Caesar's own son, although Caesar was only fifteen when Brutus was born, so it is very unlikely.

--

In the civil war, Brutus fought on Pompey's side, against Caesar.

But after the battle of Pharsalus, Caesar, who had given orders that Brutus should not be harmed in the battle, forgave him at once, and welcomed him, and since then has given him one honour after another. In the year 44, Brutus is praetor, one of the chief judges of Rome, the second highest office. Caesar has promised him the consulship in a few years.

{img:praetor-tribunal-seat-forum-citizens-cases-morning}
Caesar is fond of Brutus, and trusts him. He is said to have remarked that whatever Brutus wants, he wants very much.

--

Brutus is married to Porcia, the daughter of Marcus Porcius Cato, who was the sternest and most stubborn of all Caesar's enemies, and who took his own life two years ago in Africa, rather than accept Caesar's forgiveness.

Porcia is as brave and as proud as her father. The story is told that, when she sees that Brutus is troubled by some great secret, which he will not tell her, she wounds herself in the leg, with a little knife, to show him that she is strong enough to keep any secret, and to bear any pain. And then he tells her everything.

And the walls of Rome begin to speak to Brutus too. Someone writes messages, at night, on the statue of the old Brutus: if only you were alive now. And on the chair where Marcus Brutus sits as judge, in the Forum, someone leaves little notes, saying: Brutus, are you asleep? You are not really Brutus.

--

The man who first brings Brutus into the plot is his brother-in-law, Gaius Cassius Longinus.

Cassius is very different from Brutus. He is a soldier, hot-tempered, sharp, and proud. Nine years ago, he served with Crassus in the war against the Parthians, and was one of the few who escaped from the disaster, and saved what was left of the army. He is married to Brutus's half-sister, Junia Tertia.

{img:cassius-lean-roman-soldier-senator-intense-gaze}
Cassius, like Brutus, fought against Caesar in the civil war, and was forgiven. He too is praetor this year. But he is bitter, because Caesar gave the most important praetorship to Brutus, not to him. And he hates the idea of one man ruling Rome.

--

Cassius talks to Brutus, quietly, in the early weeks of the year 44.

He tells him that the Republic is dying, and that only they can save it. That the people of Rome expect it of Brutus, because of his name, and his ancestor. And that if Caesar is made king, it will be too late.

{img:two-men-walking-garden-path-dusk-quiet-talk}
And Brutus, after much thought, and many sleepless nights, agrees.

--

Others join them. In the end, more than sixty senators are part of the plot.

Some of them are old enemies of Caesar, who fought against him, and were forgiven. But some of them are his own friends, and his own officers, who fought beside him in Gaul, and whom he has rewarded with great honours. Among them is Decimus Junius Brutus, a distant cousin of Marcus Brutus, one of Caesar's most trusted generals, whom Caesar has named in his will as one of his heirs, if his first heirs should not live. And Gaius Trebonius, who served under Caesar in Gaul for years, and whom Caesar made consul.

They have many reasons. Some believe truly in the Republic, and in liberty. Some are jealous, or ambitious, or bitter. Some think that Caesar has not rewarded them enough. Some simply do not want to be ruled by anyone.

--

They meet secretly, in small groups, in each other's houses, and talk, in low voices.

They do not ask Cicero to join them. He is too old, they think, and too talkative, and too cautious. They want to act quickly, before Caesar leaves for the East.

{img:city-rooftops-night-few-lit-windows-stars-quiet}
And they choose a day. The Ides of March. The fifteenth of March. Three days before Caesar is due to leave Rome. On that day, the Senate will meet, in the hall of the Theatre of Pompey.
