In the afternoon, the people of Rome go to the baths.

There are many public baths in Rome, at this time, perhaps more than a hundred and fifty. Most of them are small, and simple, run by private owners, who charge a small fee, a quarter of an as, the smallest coin, which almost anyone can afford. Children are often allowed in free. The great imperial baths, which were built later, with their huge halls and domes, do not exist yet.

--

At the baths, you leave your clothes in a changing room, in little niches in the wall, and perhaps pay a slave to guard them, because thieves are common.

Then you go into the exercise yard, the palaestra, and exercise: you lift weights, or throw a ball with your friends, or wrestle, or run, until you are warm and sweaty.

Then you go into the bath rooms. First the warm room, the tepidarium, where you sit and relax. Then the hot room, the caldarium, full of steam, heated by hot air flowing under the floor and inside the walls, from a great furnace. There is a hot pool, and a basin of cool water to splash your face.

--

The Romans do not use soap. Instead, they rub olive oil on their skin, and then scrape it off, with the sweat and the dirt, with a curved metal tool called a strigil.

Then, last of all, you go into the cold room, the frigidarium, and plunge into the cold pool, to close your pores, and wake you up. And then you dress, and go home for dinner, clean and refreshed.

{img:caldarium-steam-hot-pool-bathers-soft-light}
But the baths are not just for washing. They are places to meet your friends, and gossip, and do business, and eat snacks bought from the sellers who walk around crying their wares, and get your hair cut, or your arms plucked. The philosopher Seneca, who once lived above a bath house, complained about the noise: the grunting of the weightlifters, the slapping of the masseurs, the shouts of the ball players, the cries of the sausage sellers, and the man who liked to sing in the bath.

--

And then there are the games.

The Romans love shows of every kind. And on the days of the great festivals, there are games, ludi, paid for by the state, and by the magistrates, who hope to win the favour of the people.

{img:circus-maximus-chariots-racing-crowds-sunlit-sand}
The greatest of all are the chariot races, in the Circus Maximus, the great racecourse in the valley between the Palatine and the Aventine hills. Caesar has recently rebuilt and enlarged it, so that it can hold perhaps a hundred and fifty thousand spectators.

--

The chariots are light and fast, pulled by four horses, and the drivers wear bright tunics in the colours of their teams: the Reds, and the Whites, and later also the Blues and the Greens.

They race seven times around the long, narrow track, around a low barrier in the middle, called the spina, decorated with statues and shrines, and with seven great wooden eggs, and later seven bronze dolphins, which are turned over, one by one, to count the laps. The turns at each end are the most exciting part, because the chariots must turn very sharply, and often collide.

{img:lap-counter-eggs-spina-statues-circus-dust}
The crowd roars, and cheers, and shouts, and waves their hands, and bets on their favourite drivers and horses. Romans of every class come to the races: rich and poor, men and women, free and enslaved, all sitting together. The poet Ovid, a little later, writes that the races are a good place for a young man to meet a girl.

--

There are also plays, in the theatres, comedies and tragedies, and mimes, and dancers, and musicians.

At this time, Rome has only one permanent stone theatre, the great Theatre of Pompey, which was built eleven years ago by Pompey the Great, on the Field of Mars, outside the old city. It is a huge and splendid building, with a great curved hall of seats, and a stage, and behind the stage, a long garden, surrounded by colonnades, full of statues and fountains and shady trees, where people can walk.

At the far end of the garden, there is a large hall, where the Senate sometimes meets, because the old Senate house in the Forum burned down some years ago, and is being rebuilt. In the hall, there is a great statue of Pompey himself. Remember this place. We shall come back to it.

--

And there are the gladiator shows.

At this time, these are not yet held in a great amphitheatre, because the Colosseum will not be built for more than a hundred years. They are held in the Forum, on wooden stands put up for the occasion. Caesar has given magnificent shows, with hundreds of gladiators, all dressed in silver armour.

{img:empty-wooden-stands-forum-banners-before-games}
We will not go to watch them tonight. But we will remember that, for the Romans, these shows were among the greatest entertainments of all, and that Caesar, who understood the people of Rome very well, gave them more and grander shows than anyone before him.
