Most of the people of Rome do not live in fine houses with gardens and fountains. They live in apartment blocks, called insulae, which means islands.

The insulae are tall buildings, four or five or even six storeys high, built of brick and timber, crowded together along the narrow streets. On the ground floor, facing the street, there are shops and workshops and taverns. Above them, there are the apartments.

--

The apartments on the first floor are the best, and the most expensive. They are large, with several rooms, and windows, and sometimes even running water, piped in from the aqueducts.

But the higher you go, the smaller and cheaper and worse the apartments become. At the top, under the roof, the poorest people live in tiny, dark rooms, hot in summer and cold in winter, with no water, and no kitchen, and no toilet. They must carry their water up all the stairs from the public fountain in the street, and carry their rubbish down, or, sometimes, simply throw it out of the window.

The poet Juvenal, who lived a little later, wrote that if you walk through the streets of Rome at night, you should make your will first, because of all the things that might fall on your head from the windows above.

--

The insulae are often badly built, by greedy landlords, who want to make as much money as possible.

Sometimes they fall down. And very often, they catch fire, from the braziers and the oil lamps and the cooking fires. Rome has no proper fire brigade at this time. One of the richest men in Rome, Marcus Crassus, who passed away nine years ago, became even richer by buying burning buildings cheaply from their desperate owners, and then sending his own team of slaves to put out the fire.

{img:narrow-street-tall-buildings-laundry-lines-light}
But the people of the insulae do not spend much time at home. Their homes are only for sleeping. They live their lives in the streets.

--

And the streets of Rome are full of life.

There are shops of every kind: butchers and fishmongers, and fruit sellers and vegetable sellers, and wine shops, and potters, and shoemakers, and tailors, and barbers, who shave men in the street, sitting on stools. There are snack bars, with counters opening onto the street, with great clay jars set into them, full of hot food: stews, and soups, and sausages, and spiced wine.

There are craftsmen everywhere: blacksmiths, and carpenters, and goldsmiths, and lamp makers, and fullers, who wash and clean clothes, by trampling them in great tubs. Each trade has its own street, or its own neighbourhood. And everywhere, there are street sellers, crying their wares, and beggars, and street performers: jugglers, and acrobats, and snake charmers, and storytellers.

--

Most of the food of Rome comes from far away.

The city is so large that the farms of Italy cannot feed it. So the grain comes by sea, in great ships, from Egypt, and Sicily, and North Africa, to the port of Ostia, at the mouth of the Tiber, and then up the river to Rome, in barges, pulled by oxen walking along the bank.

{img:grain-barges-tiber-oxen-towpath-warehouses}
For many years, the poor citizens of Rome have been given free grain, every month, by the state, so that they will not go hungry, and will not riot. When Caesar came to power, more than three hundred thousand people were receiving the free grain. Caesar counts them carefully, street by street, house by house, and reduces the number to a hundred and fifty thousand, those who really need it.

--

Water comes to Rome from the hills, along the aqueducts.

At this time, there are four of them, great channels of stone, carrying fresh, clean water for many miles, from springs and rivers in the hills, sometimes underground, sometimes on tall arches across the valleys, sloping very gently all the way, so that the water flows down into the city.

{img:aqueduct-arches-campagna-countryside-evening-light}
In the city, the water flows into fountains on the street corners, where the people fill their jars, and into the public baths, and into the houses of the rich, who pay for it. The water never stops flowing, day and night, and the overflow runs away into the great sewer, the Cloaca Maxima, which carries the waste of the city to the Tiber.

--

Many of the people of Rome are enslaved.

Perhaps one person in every four or five in the city is a slave, owned by another person, and with no rights at all. They come from all over the world, captured in war, or born to enslaved mothers, or sold by pirates. Caesar's wars in Gaul alone brought hundreds of thousands of captives to be sold.

Some of them work in the houses of the rich, as cooks and cleaners and nursemaids, and as secretaries and teachers and doctors, because many educated Greeks are enslaved. Some work in the workshops, and on the docks, and in the building trade. Their lives are often hard. But in Rome, many slaves are eventually set free by their owners, and become freedmen, and their children are born Roman citizens.

--

And there are the festivals.

The Roman year is full of festivals, holy days for the gods, when the people do not work, and there are processions, and sacrifices, and feasts, and games. In December, there is the Saturnalia, the merriest festival of all, when everyone exchanges presents, and the slaves are waited on by their masters, and people wear funny hats, and gamble, and play games, and eat and drink too much.

{img:saturnalia-feast-family-gifts-candles-merry-room}
In February, there are festivals for the dead, when families visit the tombs of their ancestors outside the city, and bring them food and flowers. And in the middle of February, there is one of the oldest and strangest festivals of all, the Lupercalia. We shall come back to that one.
