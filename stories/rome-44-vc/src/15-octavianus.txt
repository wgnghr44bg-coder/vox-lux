In Apollonia, across the sea, in what is now Albania, the young Gaius Octavius hears the news at the end of March.

He is eighteen years old, a slender, pale, rather sickly young man, with fair, curly hair, and clear, bright eyes. He has been studying there, with his friends, and with his teachers, waiting for Caesar to come and lead the great campaign against the Parthians.

--

Octavius is the grandson of Caesar's sister, Julia. His father passed away when he was four, and he was brought up by his mother, Atia, and his stepfather, a careful, sensible man named Philippus.

Caesar was fond of him, and saw something special in him, and took him along on his last campaign, in Spain, and taught him. But nobody else thinks of him as very important. He is just a clever, quiet boy, of eighteen.

His closest friend, who is with him in Apollonia, is a young man of the same age, Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa, from a modest family, who will become one of the greatest generals and builders in Roman history. They will stay friends all their lives.

--

When Octavius hears that Caesar has adopted him as his son, and left him his name and his fortune, his mother and his stepfather write to him, and beg him not to accept it. It is too dangerous, they say. You are too young.

But Octavius accepts. He takes the name Gaius Julius Caesar. And he sails across the sea to Italy, and travels to Rome, to claim his inheritance.

{img:ship-crossing-adriatic-calm-sea-spring-italy-coast}
From now on, he calls himself Caesar. Historians will later call him Octavian, to tell him apart from his great-uncle. And many years later, he will be given another name, by which the whole world will remember him: Augustus.

--

In Rome, Mark Antony does not take him seriously at first.

Antony calls him a boy who owes everything to a name. He refuses to give Octavian the money from Caesar's treasure. So Octavian sells his own property, and borrows money from his friends, and pays out, himself, to every citizen of Rome, the three hundred sesterces that Caesar had left them in his will.

{img:young-octavian-greeting-citizens-forum-crowd}
The people of Rome begin to love him. And Caesar's old soldiers, the veterans of the legions, begin to come to him, because he bears Caesar's name.

--

In July, the month of Caesar's birth, there are games, in Caesar's honour, the games of Caesar's Victory, and Octavian pays for them.

And during the games, for seven nights in a row, a bright comet appears in the sky, in the north, at about the eleventh hour, just before sunset, shining brightly over Rome.

The people of Rome believe that it is the soul of Caesar, rising up into the heavens, to take his place among the gods. They call it the Julian star.

And Octavian, who is very clever, puts a star on the head of every statue of Caesar. And from now on, he calls himself the son of a god.

--

And the month of Caesar's birth, which the Romans have always called Quintilis, the fifth month, because the old Roman year began in March, is now given a new name, in Caesar's honour: Iulius.

July.

{img:summer-roman-fields-harvest-golden-july-light}
We still call it that today. Every year, when the month of July comes around, we remember, without thinking about it, Gaius Julius Caesar.
