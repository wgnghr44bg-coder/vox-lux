In the winter of 44 before Christ, Rome was the greatest city of the Mediterranean, crowded onto its seven hills beside the river Tiber. Julius Caesar ruled it as dictator. Here is how ordinary Romans lived in his last months.

[long-pause]

The Romans did not number their years from a fixed date, but named each year after its two consuls. In ancient Rome, each year was identified by the names of the two consuls, the highest elected officials of the Republic. The year now called 44 before Christ was known in Rome as the year of the consulship of Gaius Julius Caesar and Marcus Antonius. Lists of consuls, called the fasti, were kept carefully and carved in stone, so that Romans could identify past years. Some writers also counted years from the traditional founding of the city, which Romans placed in 753 before Christ. By that system, 44 before Christ was roughly the seven hundred and tenth year of Rome. Historians use the surviving consular lists to date events precisely. Fragments of these lists, called the Fasti Capitolini, are now displayed in the Capitoline Museums. Even so, the dating system used today, counting from the birth of Christ, was devised only in the sixth century after Christ. In the winter of 44 before Christ, Rome was a large and busy city spread across its famous hills.

[long-pause]

{img:02-roof-tiles-frost-smoke-rising-hills-pale-sky}

Winter in Rome was mild, and people warmed their rooms with small braziers of glowing charcoal. Rome lies in central Italy, about fifteen miles from the sea, and its winters are relatively mild. Frost and snow were unusual, though cold winds and heavy rain could make the season uncomfortable. Most Roman homes had no central heating. Wealthy houses sometimes had heated floors, called hypocausts, especially in their baths, but this was rare in ordinary homes. Instead, people used braziers, metal or clay containers filled with burning charcoal, to warm their rooms. They wore extra tunics and thick woollen cloaks. Historians know about Roman heating from archaeological finds, such as braziers excavated at Pompeii and Herculaneum, and from the writings of ancient authors. Braziers were dangerous, because charcoal fumes could be harmful in closed rooms and fires could spread easily. Even so, they were the usual way of keeping warm. On winter mornings, smoke from countless braziers, ovens and lamps rose above the tiled roofs of the city.

{img:03-forum-valley-temples-basilicas-morning}

At the centre of Rome lay the Forum, the main public square for politics, law, business and religion. The Roman Forum was a long open space in the valley between the Palatine and Capitoline hills. Originally a marshy area, it had been drained in early times and paved. By 44 before Christ, it was surrounded by temples, law courts and government buildings. The Senate met in the Curia, and speakers addressed the people from a platform called the Rostra, decorated with the bows of captured ships. Large covered halls called basilicas housed courts and business. The Basilica Aemilia and the Basilica Julia, which Caesar had begun, stood on opposite sides. Historians reconstruct the Forum from ancient descriptions and from excavations carried out since the nineteenth century. The ruins can still be visited today. Even so, the Forum changed constantly, as leaders built and rebuilt monuments to display their power. In Caesar's time, it was crowded, noisy and full of life, the meeting place of the whole city.

[long-pause]

{img:04-capitoline-temple-jupiter-gilded-roof-morning-sun}

On the Capitoline Hill stood the great temple of Jupiter, with a roof of gilded bronze tiles. The Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus, meaning Jupiter Best and Greatest, was the most important temple in Rome. It stood on the Capitoline Hill above the Forum and was shared with the goddesses Juno and Minerva. The first temple on the site was traditionally dated to the earliest years of the Republic. It burned down in 83 before Christ and was rebuilt in grand style. The new temple was dedicated in 69 before Christ by Quintus Lutatius Catulus. Its roof was covered with tiles of gilded bronze, which shone in the sun. Historians know about the temple from ancient writers and from the remains of its huge foundations, which can still be seen in Rome. Victorious generals ended their triumphal processions here, offering sacrifices to Jupiter. Even so, the temple was damaged and rebuilt several more times in later centuries. In 44 before Christ, it was the religious heart of the Roman state.

[long-pause]

{img:05-crowded-roman-street-people-many-lands-morning}

Rome was the largest city in the Mediterranean world, with people from many lands speaking many languages. Historians estimate that Rome had perhaps three quarters of a million to a million inhabitants around this time. There was no modern census of the whole population, so the figures are approximate. They are based on records of grain distribution, the size of the city and comparisons with other ancient cities. Rome attracted people from all over the Mediterranean. There were Italians, Greeks, Syrians, Egyptians, Jews, Gauls, Spaniards and many others. Some came as merchants, teachers, doctors or artists. Many others were brought as enslaved people. Latin was the main language, but Greek was widely spoken, especially among educated Romans and immigrants from the eastern Mediterranean. Inscriptions from Rome show names from many regions. Even so, Roman citizens remained a privileged group, and newcomers did not always have the same rights. The mixture of peoples gave the city great variety. Visitors described Rome as the meeting place of the whole world.

[long-pause]

{img:06-grain-ships-harbour-amphorae-unloading}

Grain from Egypt, Sicily and North Africa, wine from Spain and marble from Greece were shipped to Rome. Rome had grown rich through centuries of conquest. By 44 before Christ, it controlled Italy, Spain, Greece, much of North Africa, Gaul and lands in the eastern Mediterranean. Goods from all these regions flowed to the capital. Grain arrived from Sicily, Sardinia and North Africa, and increasingly from Egypt. Wine and olive oil came from Spain and Italy, and fine marble from Greece. Most goods arrived by sea at the port of Ostia, at the mouth of the Tiber, and were carried upriver to Rome by barge. Historians study this trade through shipwrecks, writings and the remains of storage jars, called amphorae. Near the river in Rome stands Monte Testaccio, an artificial hill made of millions of broken oil amphorae, mostly from a later period. Even so, the supply system was not always reliable. Storms, pirates and wars could interrupt deliveries. Feeding the huge city remained a constant political concern.

[long-pause]

{img:07-quiet-forum-evening-lamps-few-people-columns}

For about a century before 44 before Christ, Rome had been troubled by political violence and civil wars. The late Roman Republic was a period of great instability. From the time of the brothers Tiberius and Gaius Gracchus in the second century before Christ, Roman politics became increasingly violent. Reformers and their opponents were killed in street fighting. In the 80s before Christ, the generals Marius and Sulla fought a civil war, and Sulla published lists of enemies who could be killed and their property seized. Later, the politicians Pompey, Crassus and Caesar formed an informal alliance to share power. Historians call this the First Triumvirate. When it broke down, civil war followed between Caesar and Pompey. Ancient writers such as Sallust, Cicero and later Plutarch and Appian describe these conflicts. Historians debate the causes of the Republic's crisis. Explanations include the power of ambitious generals, the loyalty of soldiers to their commanders, inequality and the difficulty of governing a large empire with city-state institutions. Even so, many Romans still hoped for stability and peace.

[long-pause]

{img:08-julius-caesar-portrait-laurel-wreath-toga-calm}

Ancient writers reported that Julius Caesar liked to wear a laurel wreath, partly to hide his baldness. In early 44 before Christ, Gaius Julius Caesar was fifty-five years old. The historian Suetonius, writing about a hundred and fifty years later, described him as tall, with a fair complexion and dark, lively eyes. He was careful about his appearance. Suetonius wrote that Caesar was sensitive about his baldness. He combed his thin hair forward and welcomed the honour of wearing a laurel wreath at all times, which the Senate had granted him. Historians rely on Suetonius, Plutarch and other ancient writers for such personal details, but they treat them with some caution. These authors wrote long after Caesar's death and sometimes included gossip. Portraits of Caesar on coins issued in 44 before Christ show a thin face with a lined neck. Even so, no portrait can be certain. Several marble heads are believed to show Caesar, but scholars disagree about which are most accurate. The image of Caesar has been shaped by centuries of art and legend.

[long-pause]

{img:09-subura-crowded-street-shops-apartments}

Caesar's family claimed descent from the goddess Venus, yet he grew up in the crowded Subura district. Caesar was born in Rome in 100 before Christ, into the Julii, an old patrician family. The family claimed descent from Iulus, the son of the Trojan hero Aeneas, and through him from the goddess Venus. Despite this distinguished ancestry, the family was not especially rich or powerful in Caesar's youth. According to Suetonius, Caesar grew up in the Subura, a busy and crowded district of Rome. The Subura lay in a valley northeast of the Forum and was known for its noisy streets, shops and apartment blocks. Historians note that it was a lively, sometimes rough neighbourhood. Caesar later used his family's legendary descent to build his reputation. He dedicated a temple to Venus Genetrix, Venus the Mother, in his new forum. Even so, his childhood among ordinary Romans may have helped him understand the people of the city. His popularity with the urban crowd became an important part of his political success.

[long-pause]

{img:10-roman-matron-mother-teaching-boy-courtyard}

Caesar's mother, Aurelia, raised him after his father's death and remained an important figure in his life. Caesar's father, also called Gaius Julius Caesar, died suddenly around 85 before Christ, when his son was about fifteen. Caesar's mother, Aurelia, then took charge of the household. She came from a well-connected family, the Aurelii Cottae. Ancient writers praised her as a strict, intelligent and capable woman. The historian Tacitus later named her as an example of a mother who guided her children's education with great care. Aurelia lived with Caesar for much of her life. When he became chief priest, she lived in his official residence. Historians note that Roman mothers of the upper classes often played important roles in their sons' careers, using family connections and giving advice. Aurelia is also mentioned in the story of a scandal in 62 before Christ, when she discovered a man disguised as a woman at a religious ceremony in Caesar's house. Even so, few details of her life survive. She died in 54 before Christ.

[long-pause]

{img:11-roman-orator-speaking-crowd-forum-rostra-sunlit}

Caesar once owed so much money that he could not leave Rome until the wealthy Crassus guaranteed his debts. As a young politician, Caesar spent heavily to win popularity. He paid for public games, entertainments and buildings, and he borrowed large sums to do so. Ancient writers describe him as a brilliant speaker, almost equal to Cicero, and as a clear and elegant writer. His accounts of his wars, the Commentaries on the Gallic War and on the Civil War, are still read today. In 61 before Christ, Caesar was appointed governor of Further Spain. According to Plutarch, his creditors tried to stop him from leaving Rome until his debts were paid. Marcus Licinius Crassus, the richest man in Rome, agreed to guarantee a large part of the debt. Historians note that political careers in the late Republic were extremely expensive. Many politicians borrowed money in the hope of repaying it through a profitable governorship. Even so, Caesar's debts were exceptional. His success in Spain and later in Gaul made him very wealthy.

[long-pause]

{img:12-ancient-pirate-ship-calm-sea-island-coast}

According to ancient writers, the young Caesar told pirates who captured him that his ransom was too low. Around 75 before Christ, Caesar sailed to the island of Rhodes to study rhetoric with a famous teacher. On the way, his ship was captured by pirates near the island of Pharmacusa, off the coast of Asia Minor. Plutarch tells that the pirates demanded a ransom of twenty talents. Caesar laughed and said they did not know who they had captured, and offered to pay fifty. He spent about thirty-eight days as their prisoner while his companions collected the money. During this time, he joked with the pirates, wrote poems and speeches, and told them he would have them crucified. After his release, he gathered ships, captured the pirates and carried out his threat. Historians note that piracy was a serious problem in the Mediterranean at the time. Even so, the details of this story come from writers who lived long after the event. It may have been exaggerated to show Caesar's confidence and determination.

[long-pause]

{img:13-domus-publica-house-forum-temple-vesta-round}

As chief priest of Rome, Caesar lived in an official residence in the Forum called the Domus Publica. In 63 before Christ, Caesar was elected Pontifex Maximus, the chief priest of the Roman state religion. This was a position for life and carried great prestige. Caesar was only about thirty-seven years old and faced older, more experienced candidates. According to ancient writers, he borrowed heavily to win the election. The Pontifex Maximus oversaw religious law, the calendar and the Vestal Virgins. He lived in an official house called the Domus Publica, on the Sacred Way in the Forum, next to the House of the Vestals and the Temple of Vesta. Historians note that the position gave Caesar both influence and a prominent home at the centre of the city. Archaeologists have studied the area and identified remains associated with the residence. Even so, the exact layout of the Domus Publica in Caesar's time is uncertain. Caesar continued to hold the office until his death, even while he was away on campaigns.

[long-pause]

{img:14-river-rubicon-small-stream-winter-soldiers-crossing}

In January 49 before Christ, Caesar led his army across the Rubicon, a small river, starting a civil war. The Rubicon was a small river in northern Italy that marked the boundary between the province of Cisalpine Gaul and Italy itself. Roman law forbade a governor to bring his army out of his province into Italy without permission. In January 49 before Christ, Caesar's command in Gaul was coming to an end, and his opponents in the Senate wanted to put him on trial. Rather than give up his army, Caesar crossed the Rubicon with a single legion. According to Suetonius, he said that the die was cast. Historians regard this moment as the start of a civil war. The exact location of the ancient Rubicon is uncertain, because rivers in the area have changed their course. A river in the region was officially named the Rubicone in the twentieth century. Even so, the phrase crossing the Rubicon has become a common expression for passing a point of no return.

[long-pause]

{img:15-caesar-receiving-former-enemies-courtyard-gesture}

Caesar became known for pardoning his defeated enemies, a policy called clementia. The civil war between Caesar and his opponents lasted from 49 to 45 before Christ and was fought across the Mediterranean. Caesar's great rival, Pompey, was defeated at the Battle of Pharsalus in Greece in 48 before Christ. He fled to Egypt, where he was killed on the orders of advisers to the young king Ptolemy the Thirteenth. Caesar went on to defeat other opponents in Africa and Spain. Unlike earlier leaders such as Sulla, Caesar did not execute large numbers of his enemies. Instead, he pardoned many of them and even gave them positions. This policy became known as Caesar's clemency, or clementia. Historians note that it won Caesar some loyalty but also caused resentment. Some proud nobles disliked owing their lives to his mercy, because it implied that he had power over them. Even so, clemency helped end the war. Several of the men later involved in Caesar's assassination had been pardoned by him.

[long-pause]

{img:16-dawn-roman-street-shutters-opening-shops-bakers}

The Romans divided daylight into twelve hours, so an hour was longer in summer than in winter. The Roman day began at sunrise. Romans divided the time between sunrise and sunset into twelve equal hours. Because the length of daylight changed through the year, the length of an hour changed too. In midwinter, an hour was about forty-five modern minutes. In midsummer, it was about seventy-five minutes. The night was divided into four watches. Romans measured time with sundials and water clocks. The first sundial in Rome was brought from Sicily in the third century before Christ. Historians note that the timing of daily life followed daylight closely. Shops opened early, and bakers began work before dawn. The poet Martial, writing later, complained about bakers and schoolmasters disturbing his sleep. Even so, most people did not need precise timekeeping. They followed the rhythm of the sun and of public signals, such as announcements in the Forum. The day was busiest in the morning, when most business was done.

[long-pause]

{img:17-roman-school-arcade-children-wax-tablets-teacher}

Roman primary schools often met in open arcades, and neighbours complained about the noise. Education in Rome was private. There was no state school system, and parents paid teachers directly. Children of families who could afford it attended a primary school, called a ludus, from about the age of seven. The teacher, known as a litterator or ludi magister, taught reading, writing and basic arithmetic. Many schools were held in shops or under covered arcades opening onto the street. Pupils wrote on wax tablets with a pointed stylus and recited lessons aloud. Historians know about Roman schools from writers such as Quintilian, Horace and Martial. Martial complained about schoolmasters shouting and beating pupils early in the morning. Discipline was strict, and corporal punishment was common. Even so, education varied greatly. Some children were taught at home by educated slaves or tutors. Many poor children received little or no formal schooling. Literacy levels in Rome are debated by historians, but many people could read at least simple notices.

[long-pause]

{img:18-atrium-clients-waiting-togas-morning-light-pool}

Every morning, poorer Romans visited the homes of powerful patrons to greet them in a custom called the salutatio. Roman society was organised partly through the relationship between patrons and clients. A patron was a powerful man who offered protection, help and sometimes money. Clients were less powerful citizens who gave support in return, such as votes, loyalty and public attendance. Each morning, clients dressed in their togas and went to the patron's house for the salutatio, or morning greeting. They waited in the entrance hall to pay their respects. The patron received them, sometimes gave them small gifts or food, and listened to requests. Afterwards, clients often accompanied him to the Forum, showing his importance. Historians note that the number of clients reflected a man's status. Writers such as Cicero, and later Martial and Juvenal, describe this custom. Even so, the system was not only about power. Patrons were expected to help clients in legal and personal matters. The relationship was a basic part of Roman life.

[long-pause]

{img:19-atrium-ancestor-masks-shrine-lares-pool}

Noble Roman families kept wax masks of their ancestors in cupboards in the atrium. The atrium was the main hall of a traditional Roman house. It had an opening in the roof, called the compluvium, which let in light and rain. Below it was a shallow pool, the impluvium, which collected the water. Around the atrium were doorways to other rooms. In noble families, the atrium displayed the imagines, wax masks of ancestors who had held high office. They were kept in small cupboards, with labels describing each person's achievements. The Greek historian Polybius described how these masks were worn by actors at funerals, so that the ancestors seemed to walk in procession. Historians note that the masks expressed the importance of family history in Roman politics. The household shrine, called the lararium, often stood in or near the atrium. It held images of the household gods, the Lares and Penates. Even so, no Roman ancestor masks have survived. They are known only from written descriptions and some related portraits.

[long-pause]

{img:20-forum-morning-crowds-basilica-arcades-lawyers}

In the Forum, bankers changed money, lawyers argued cases and crowds gathered to hear news and gossip. After the morning greeting, many important Romans went to the Forum with their clients. The Forum was busiest in the morning. In the basilicas, magistrates heard legal cases, and lawyers made speeches before juries and spectators. Bankers and moneychangers worked at tables in arcades near the square. Politicians met supporters, and senators gathered before meetings. Historians know about these activities from the speeches of Cicero, many of which were delivered in the Forum. People also came to hear announcements, read public notices and exchange news. The playwright Plautus, writing more than a century earlier, joked about the different types of people found in different parts of the Forum. In 44 before Christ, a common subject of conversation was Caesar himself, his honours and his plans. Even so, the Forum was also a place of ordinary business. Shopkeepers, vendors and idle onlookers mixed with the powerful and the famous.

[long-pause]

{img:21-roman-street-afternoon-quiet-shade-fountain-dogs}

By midday, much of the day's work in Rome was done, and many people rested. Roman working days generally started early and ended early. Many public activities, including courts and the Senate, took place in the morning. Around midday, people ate a light meal called the prandium. It might include bread, cheese, olives, fruit, cold meat or leftovers from the previous evening. Afterwards, many Romans rested during the warmest part of the day. This custom was especially common in summer. The word siesta comes from the Latin sexta hora, meaning the sixth hour, which was around noon. Historians note that the afternoon was often spent at the baths, exercising or meeting friends. Letters of Cicero and later writers describe daily routines of this kind. Even so, routines varied greatly by social class. Wealthy Romans had more freedom to rest, while workers, shopkeepers and enslaved people often continued working. In winter, with shorter days, the timetable was more compressed. Streets became quieter in the early afternoon.

[long-pause]

{img:22-dark-roman-street-night-torch-slaves-lighting-way}

A law of Caesar's banned most heavy carts from the streets of Rome during the day, so wagons moved at night. Rome's streets were narrow and crowded. To reduce congestion, a law passed in Caesar's time, recorded in an inscription called the Tabula Heracleensis, banned most wheeled vehicles from the city during the first ten hours of the day. Exceptions were made for carts carrying building materials for temples and public works, and for certain religious ceremonies. As a result, carts delivering goods moved through the city at night. Later writers, especially the poet Juvenal, complained that the noise of wagons made sleep impossible. Historians note that this law is one of the earliest known traffic regulations. At night, the streets had no public lighting. Wealthy people returning from dinner were accompanied by slaves carrying torches. Others carried small oil lamps. Even so, the night streets could be dangerous. Ancient writers mention thieves, falling objects and drunken young men. Most Romans stayed at home after dark and went to bed early.

[long-pause]

{img:23-triclinium-dining-room-painted-walls-three-couches}

At a Roman dinner party, guests reclined on three couches arranged around a table, leaning on their left elbows. The main meal of the day for wealthy Romans was dinner, called the cena. It began in the late afternoon, often after a visit to the baths. Dinner parties were held in a dining room called the triclinium, named after its three couches. Each couch usually held three diners, so the ideal party had nine guests. The couches were arranged in a U shape around a low table, leaving one side open for servants. Guests reclined on their left side, leaning on cushions and eating with their right hand. The seating followed rules of precedence, with places of honour for important guests. Historians know these customs from literature, wall paintings and archaeological remains of dining rooms, especially at Pompeii. Women in Caesar's time often reclined with men at dinner, though customs varied. Even so, most ordinary Romans did not dine this way. They ate simple meals sitting on stools or at home in small apartments.

[long-pause]

{img:24-roman-starters-eggs-olives-oysters-silver-plates}

A Roman saying described a long dinner as lasting from the egg to the apples. A formal Roman dinner had three main parts. First came the gustatio, or starters. These could include eggs, olives, lettuce, mushrooms, shellfish, small salted fish and sausages. Diners often drank mulsum, a mixture of wine and honey. Next came the main courses, with meat, poultry and fish. Pork was especially popular, along with game, ducks and fish from the sea and rivers. Finally came dessert, called secundae mensae, meaning second tables, with fruit, nuts and sweet cakes. Because meals often began with eggs and ended with apples, the poet Horace used the phrase ab ovo usque ad mala, from the egg to the apples, meaning from beginning to end. Historians know about Roman food from writers, from a later cookbook attributed to Apicius and from archaeological remains of food. Even so, such dinners were for the wealthy. Most Romans ate much simpler meals of grain, vegetables and a little meat or fish.

[long-pause]

{img:25-clay-amphorae-garum-jars-kitchen-shelf-herbs}

Romans flavoured many dishes with garum, a sauce made from fermented fish. Garum was one of the most popular flavourings in Roman cooking. It was made by layering small fish, such as anchovies or mackerel, with salt in large containers and leaving them in the sun for weeks or months. The fish broke down and fermented, producing a salty liquid. This liquid was strained and stored in amphorae. Garum was used in many dishes, both savoury and sweet. Historians note that the process was smelly, so production often took place outside towns. Large garum factories have been found along the coasts of Spain, North Africa and Portugal. Garum from certain places was highly prized and expensive. Cheaper versions, often called liquamen, were used by ordinary people. Even so, the flavour of garum may have been less strong than its method suggests. Modern experiments suggest that it tasted similar to fish sauces used in Southeast Asian cooking today. It added a rich, savoury taste to Roman food.

[long-pause]

{img:26-roman-dessert-figs-grapes-honey-cakes-lamp}

Roman dinners had no forks, tomatoes, potatoes or sugar. Many foods common today were unknown in ancient Rome. Tomatoes, potatoes, maize, peppers and chocolate all came from the Americas and reached Europe only in the sixteenth century. Sugar was known only as a rare medicine from India. Instead, Romans sweetened their food with honey and with grape syrups. Desserts included apples, pears, figs, grapes, dates, nuts, cheese and cakes made with honey. Diners ate with their fingers, spoons and knives. Forks were not used for eating in ancient Rome, though larger forks were used in kitchens. Napkins were used to wipe hands, and guests sometimes took food home wrapped in them. Historians use ancient texts and archaeological evidence, such as seeds and bones found at Roman sites, to reconstruct the diet. Even so, the Roman diet was varied and sophisticated for the wealthy. It included many herbs and spices, such as pepper imported from India, which was very popular.

[long-pause]

{img:27-musician-lyre-playing-dinner-guests-listening-soft}

Guests at Roman dinner parties were entertained by music, poetry readings, conversation and performers. Entertainment was an important part of Roman dinner parties. Musicians played the lyre or double pipes. Educated slaves might read poetry or literature aloud. Dancers, singers and acrobats sometimes performed. Witty conversation was highly valued, and hosts aimed to bring together interesting guests. Some Romans disapproved of extravagant entertainments and preferred quiet discussions of philosophy or literature. Cicero's writings describe dinners filled with conversation about politics and books. Historians note that dinner parties were also occasions for building political alliances and friendships. The poet Horace and later the writer Pliny the Younger described their ideal dinners, with simple food and good company. Even so, some dinners were known for excess. Later satirists, such as Petronius, mocked wealthy hosts who displayed their riches in vulgar ways. In Caesar's time, dinner parties reflected the host's taste and status. They could last for many hours, often into the night.

[long-pause]

{img:28-seaside-villa-dinner-winter-evening-guests}

In December 45 before Christ, Caesar visited Cicero's villa near the sea, accompanied by about two thousand soldiers. One of Cicero's letters describes a visit from Caesar in December 45 before Christ. Caesar was travelling along the coast of Campania, near the Bay of Naples, and stopped at Cicero's villa near Puteoli. He came with a large escort, which Cicero estimated at about two thousand soldiers. Cicero worried about where they would all stay. Caesar spent the morning at a neighbour's house, then bathed and dined with Cicero. Cicero wrote to his friend Atticus that the dinner went well. The conversation was pleasant and avoided politics, focusing mainly on literature. He added that Caesar was not the kind of guest to whom one says, please come again. Historians value this letter for its personal, humorous view of Caesar only a few months before his death. Even so, it also shows the tension between the two men. Cicero was polite, but he did not support Caesar's political dominance. The letter survives among Cicero's correspondence to Atticus.

[long-pause]

{img:29-insula-apartment-block-five-storeys-shops-below}

Most people in ancient Rome lived in tall apartment blocks called insulae. Only the rich lived in large private houses with gardens. Most Romans rented rooms in blocks of flats called insulae, a word that means islands. These buildings often stood four or five storeys high, and some were even taller. On the ground floor there were shops and workshops, open to the street. Above them were flats of different sizes. The best and largest rooms were on the lower floors, where it was easier to escape in case of fire. The higher the floor, the smaller and cheaper the rooms became. Historians know about insulae from Roman writers and from the ruins of Ostia, Rome's port, where several of these buildings still stand. A later record from the fourth century counted more than forty thousand insulae in Rome, compared with under two thousand private houses. Even so, life in an insula was not always unhappy. Neighbours knew each other well, and the busy streets below were full of life from early morning.

[long-pause]

{img:30-narrow-street-tall-buildings-laundry-lines-light}

The upper floors of Roman apartment blocks were small, dark and often uncomfortable. At the top of the insulae, under the roof, the poorest people lived in tiny rooms. These rooms were hot in summer and cold in winter. There was no running water, no kitchen and no toilet. People carried water up the stairs from public fountains. They used pots for toilets and emptied them into large jars at the bottom of the stairs, or into the street. Cooking was often done on small portable stoves, though many people bought food from street stalls instead. The poet Juvenal, writing more than a century later, joked that anyone walking under the windows at night should make a will first, because of all the things thrown from above. Historians treat his poems as satire, but they reflect real problems. Even so, people did their best to make their rooms pleasant. They hung cloth, kept plants on window ledges and decorated walls with simple paintings. From the high windows, there were wide views across the roofs of the city.

[long-pause]

{img:31-crassus-slaves-water-buckets-street-smoke}

Fires were a constant danger in Rome, and the rich politician Crassus profited from them. Many insulae were built cheaply, with wooden floors and thin walls. Landlords wanted to make as much money as possible and spend as little as they could. As a result, buildings sometimes collapsed, and fires spread quickly. There was no public fire brigade in Rome during the time of the Republic. According to the writer Plutarch, Marcus Licinius Crassus, the richest man in Rome, kept a private team of hundreds of slaves trained as builders and firefighters. When a building caught fire, Crassus would offer to buy it, and the buildings next to it, at a very low price. Once the owners had agreed to sell, his men would put out the fire. In this way, he became owner of a large part of the city. Historians note that this story may be exaggerated, but it fits what is known about Crassus. Even so, the problem was real. Later, the emperor Augustus created a public fire brigade, called the vigiles, with thousands of men.

[long-pause]

{img:32-roman-snack-bar-counter-clay-jars-street}

Rome was full of street snack bars that sold hot food and drink from jars set into stone counters. Since many people had no kitchen at home, they ate out. Rome had many small eating places, open to the street. Their counters were made of stone, with large clay jars set into the top to hold hot food, such as stews, beans and lentils. Customers could buy bread, cheese, olives, sausages and wine. Archaeologists have found many such counters at Pompeii and Herculaneum, the towns buried by Vesuvius in the year 79. One in Pompeii, uncovered in 2020, still had bright paintings of ducks, a rooster and a dog on its front. Scientists found traces of duck, pork, goat, fish and snails in its jars. These places are often called thermopolia, though the Romans may have used other names. Even so, the idea is clear. Eating out was a normal part of daily life for ordinary people in Roman towns. People stopped for a hot bowl of food on their way home and talked with neighbours at the counter.

[long-pause]

{img:33-craftsmen-street-blacksmith-potter-acrobat}

Rome was full of craftsmen, and many trades had their own street or neighbourhood. The city depended on the work of thousands of skilled people. There were blacksmiths, carpenters, potters and goldsmiths. There were makers of shoes, rope, lamps, perfume and cloth. Many trades gathered in particular streets, so that people knew where to go for each kind of goods. Some streets were even named after the trades practised there. Shops were small, often a single room opening onto the street, with the family living in a room above or behind. Historians know about these trades from inscriptions, tomb carvings and the remains of workshops. Many craftsmen set up carved stones that showed their tools and work, so their trade would be remembered after death. Even so, the streets were not only for work. Jugglers, acrobats and storytellers performed on corners, and sellers called out their goods. Ancient writers often complained about the noise of Rome, which went on from dawn until late at night. For most Romans, the street was the centre of daily life.

[long-pause]

{img:34-grain-barges-tiber-oxen-towpath-warehouses}

Caesar reduced the number of Romans receiving free grain by checking the lists house by house. Rome was so large that the farms of Italy could not feed it. Most of its grain came by sea from Sicily, Sardinia, North Africa and Egypt. Ships brought it to the port of Ostia, at the mouth of the Tiber. From there, barges were pulled up the river by oxen walking along a towpath, and the grain was stored in huge warehouses near the city. Since the time of the politician Gaius Gracchus, poorer citizens could buy grain at a low price, and later it was given out free. By Caesar's time, around three hundred and twenty thousand people received free grain. According to the historian Suetonius, Caesar ordered a careful count, street by street, with the help of the owners of each apartment block. He reduced the list to about one hundred and fifty thousand. Historians note that this was both a saving and a political risk. Even so, the grain supply remained one of the most important tasks of any Roman leader.

[long-pause]

{img:35-aqueduct-arches-campagna-countryside-evening-light}

Fresh water reached Rome through long stone channels called aqueducts. Rome needed large amounts of water for drinking, bathing and fountains. The Romans brought it from springs in the hills, sometimes many miles away, using aqueducts. Most of an aqueduct ran underground, through tunnels and covered channels. Only where it crossed valleys did it rise on the famous lines of arches. The water flowed gently downhill the whole way, using only gravity. In 44 before Christ, Rome had four aqueducts. The oldest, the Aqua Appia, had been built in 312 before Christ. The longest, the Aqua Marcia, was more than fifty miles long. Historians and engineers have studied their routes in detail, and parts of them can still be seen in the countryside around Rome. Even so, water was not equally shared. Wealthy people could pay to bring a private pipe into their homes, while most people used public fountains. Later emperors built more aqueducts, until Rome had eleven, bringing an enormous supply of fresh water to the city.

[long-pause]

{img:36-household-workers-kitchen-courtyard-morning}

A large part of Rome's population was enslaved, but many enslaved people were later freed. Slavery was a basic part of Roman society. Historians estimate that perhaps one in four or one in five people in the city of Rome was enslaved, though exact numbers are unknown. Enslaved people came from many places. Some had been captured in wars, others were sold by traders, and many were born into slavery. They worked in houses, shops, workshops and farms. Some were teachers, doctors, secretaries or skilled craftsmen. Their lives varied greatly, from hard labour in mines to trusted positions in wealthy homes. Yet all of them could be bought and sold, and they had almost no rights. Unlike in many other slave societies, Romans often freed their slaves, especially those who worked closely with them. Freed people, called freedmen and freedwomen, became Roman citizens, and their children were fully free. Historians find many freedmen in inscriptions, running businesses and building fine tombs. Even so, freedom was never guaranteed, and many enslaved people lived and died without it.

[long-pause]

{img:37-saturnalia-feast-family-gifts-candles-merry-room}

At the festival of Saturnalia in December, Romans exchanged gifts and masters sometimes served their slaves. The Roman year was full of religious festivals, and the merriest of all was the Saturnalia. It began on the seventeenth of December and, by Caesar's time, lasted several days. It honoured Saturn, an ancient god of farming. During the festival, schools and courts closed, and normal rules were turned upside down. People wore relaxed clothing and a soft felt cap, a sign of freedom. Families held feasts and gave each other gifts, often candles and small clay figures. In many households, masters served dinner to their slaves or let them speak freely. Historians know about the Saturnalia from writers such as Seneca, Catullus and Macrobius. The poet Catullus called it the best of days. Even so, the reversal of roles was only for a short time. When the festival ended, ordinary life returned. Some historians suggest that customs of the Saturnalia, such as gift giving and candles in midwinter, later influenced the ways Christmas was celebrated.

[long-pause]

{img:38-roman-public-baths-entrance-columns-afternoon-crowd}

A visit to the public baths in Rome cost the smallest coin, and children often entered free. In the afternoon, after the day's work, many Romans went to the baths. In 44 before Christ, there were already many public bathhouses in the city, most of them privately owned. The entrance fee was very low, usually a quadrans, the smallest Roman coin. Children often entered free. This meant that almost everyone could afford to bathe regularly. The baths were not only places for washing. They had courtyards for exercise, rooms for massage and areas where people could talk, eat and do business. Historians know about Roman baths from writers and from many surviving ruins across the empire. The great imperial baths of Rome, such as the Baths of Caracalla, were built later and were enormous, with libraries and gardens. Even so, the baths of Caesar's time were already a central part of city life. For many Romans, a daily visit to the baths was one of life's simple pleasures, a place to relax, meet friends and hear the latest news.

[long-pause]

{img:39-caldarium-steam-hot-pool-bathers-soft-light}

Romans did not use soap, but cleaned themselves with oil and a curved metal scraper. Bathers followed a regular order through the rooms of the baths. After exercising, they went to the warm room, called the tepidarium, to relax. Then they entered the hot room, the caldarium, which was full of steam and had a pool of hot water. Floors were often heated from below by hot air from a furnace, a system called a hypocaust. The floors could be so hot that bathers wore wooden sandals. Instead of soap, Romans rubbed oil onto their skin and then scraped it off, together with sweat and dirt, using a curved metal tool called a strigil. Finally, many bathers took a cold plunge in the frigidarium. Archaeologists have found many strigils, often with small oil flasks attached. Historians note that soap existed, but Romans used it mainly for hair or as medicine. Even so, the oil and strigil method left the skin clean and soft. After the bath, people often rested, chatted or had a snack before going home for dinner.

[long-pause]

{img:40-bath-house-courtyard-gossip-snack-seller}

A Roman philosopher who lived above a bathhouse complained about all the noise. Roman baths were lively and noisy places. People talked, laughed, exercised and did business. Sellers walked around offering sausages, cakes and drinks. The philosopher Seneca, writing about a century after Caesar, described living in a room above a bathhouse. In one of his letters, he complained about the grunting of men lifting weights, the slap of hands during massages and the shouts of ball players. He mentioned a man who loved to sing in the bath and the cries of the sausage seller and the cake seller. Above all, he mentioned someone shouting whenever a thief was caught stealing clothes. Historians enjoy this letter because it gives such a vivid picture of everyday life. Even so, Seneca used it to make a philosophical point. He argued that a calm mind could stay peaceful even in the middle of noise. For ordinary Romans, the bustle of the baths was part of the pleasure, a daily meeting place for the whole neighbourhood.

[long-pause]

{img:41-circus-maximus-chariots-racing-crowds-sunlit-sand}

At the chariot races in the Circus Maximus, rich and poor, men and women sat together. The Romans loved public entertainment. The most popular events were chariot races, held in the Circus Maximus, a long racing track in the valley between the Palatine and Aventine hills. Caesar enlarged the Circus, and historians estimate it could hold around one hundred and fifty thousand spectators in his time. Later it became even larger. Teams of horses pulled light chariots around a long central barrier, seven laps per race. Drivers belonged to teams known by their colours, such as the Reds and the Whites, and later also the Greens and the Blues. Fans supported their colours with great passion. Unlike at the theatre and the gladiator games, men and women could sit together at the races. The poet Ovid, writing later, even advised young men that the races were a good place to meet someone. Even so, racing was dangerous, and crashes were common. The shape of the Circus Maximus can still be seen today as a long, grassy park.

[long-pause]

{img:42-theatre-of-pompey-garden-colonnade-statues-fountains}

Rome's first permanent stone theatre, built by Pompey, had a large garden and a hall where the Senate sometimes met. For a long time, Romans built only temporary wooden theatres, which were taken down after each festival. Some senators thought that permanent theatres would encourage idleness. In 55 before Christ, Pompey built the first permanent stone theatre in Rome, in the Field of Mars. To avoid criticism, he placed a temple of Venus at the top of the seating, so that the theatre could be described as steps leading to a temple. Behind the stage lay a large garden surrounded by covered walkways, with fountains, statues and shady trees. At the far end of this garden stood a hall, the Curia of Pompey, where the Senate sometimes met while the main Senate house was being rebuilt. Historians know about the complex from ancient writers, a marble map of Rome and archaeological remains. Even so, little of it is visible today, because later buildings were built on top. The curve of the theatre can still be traced in the shape of modern streets.

[long-pause]

{img:43-cicero-older-man-writing-letter-study-scrolls}

More than nine hundred letters written by or to Cicero have survived. Marcus Tullius Cicero was the most famous speaker and writer of the late Roman Republic. Fortunately for historians, a large collection of his letters survives. It includes letters to his friend Atticus, to his family and to many political figures, as well as some letters written to him. Together, they number more than nine hundred. They were collected and published after his death, probably by his secretary Tiro and others. Historians value them because they show the thoughts, worries and daily concerns of a real person living through the end of the Republic. Cicero wrote about politics, money, his health, his houses and his family. He was sometimes proud, sometimes anxious and often funny. Even so, the letters must be read carefully. Cicero wrote for different readers and sometimes changed his tone depending on who would read them. Yet no other figure of the ancient world is known in such personal detail. His letters remain one of the most important sources for the year 44 before Christ.

[long-pause]

{img:44-two-friends-talking-garden-bench-scrolls}

Cicero's closest friend, Atticus, stayed out of politics and was respected by people on every side. Titus Pomponius Atticus was a wealthy Roman of the equestrian class, the rank just below senators. He spent many years living in Athens, where he earned his nickname, Atticus, meaning a man of Attica. Unlike Cicero, Atticus avoided politics and took no side in the civil wars. He helped friends on all sides with money and advice, and he was respected by many leaders who were enemies of one another. He also published books, using educated slaves to copy texts. Cicero wrote to him more often than to anyone else. Around four hundred of these letters survive, though Atticus's replies are lost. Historians know about Atticus's life from these letters and from a short biography written by his friend Cornelius Nepos. Even so, his careful neutrality was not always easy. In dangerous times, staying out of politics required great skill and caution. Atticus survived the civil wars and died peacefully in 32 before Christ, an unusual fate for a prominent Roman of his time.

[long-pause]

{img:45-arpinum-hill-town-stone-houses-valley}

Cicero came from a small hill town called Arpinum and was the first in his family to become consul. Cicero was born in 106 before Christ in Arpinum, a small town in the hills about seventy miles southeast of Rome. His family was respectable and well off, but no member had ever held high office in Rome. Romans called such a man a new man, novus homo. As a young man, Cicero came to Rome to study law and public speaking. He became famous as a lawyer through his brilliant speeches in court. In 63 before Christ, he was elected consul, the highest office of the Republic. During that year, he exposed a plot led by a nobleman named Catiline and had several conspirators executed. He was praised as father of his country, but the executions were later used against him. Historians note that Arpinum also produced another famous Roman, the general Gaius Marius. Even so, Cicero never forgot his origins. He loved his home town and described the beauty of its rivers and hills in his writings.

[long-pause]

{img:46-cicero-walking-garden-colonnade-scroll-thinking}

After the death of his daughter Tullia, Cicero found comfort in writing books of philosophy. During the civil war, Cicero had taken the side of Pompey against Caesar. After Pompey's defeat, Caesar pardoned him, and the two men treated each other politely. Caesar admired Cicero's writing. Even so, Cicero had little role in politics under Caesar. Then, in February 45 before Christ, his beloved daughter Tullia died, shortly after giving birth. Cicero was overwhelmed with grief. His letters from this time show deep sadness. He withdrew to his country houses and turned to writing. In the following two years, he wrote an astonishing number of works on philosophy, including books on friendship, old age, duty and the nature of the gods. Historians consider this one of the most productive periods of any ancient writer. His book on old age, written in 44 before Christ, describes how a person can grow old with dignity. Even so, writing did not remove his pain. Yet these books became some of the most read works of the ancient world.

[long-pause]

{img:47-roman-villa-library-shelves-scroll-cases-sunlight}

Cicero wrote to a friend that a garden and a library were all a person needed. Cicero owned several country houses in Italy, and he loved them all. His favourite was at Tusculum, in the hills southeast of Rome. It had gardens, shady walkways and a library. Like other wealthy Romans, Cicero collected books, which were rolls of papyrus kept in wooden cupboards or boxes. He asked Atticus for help in arranging his library and employed skilled slaves to repair and label the scrolls. In a letter to his friend Varro, Cicero wrote that if one has a garden in the library, nothing will be lacking. Historians often quote this line, though it is usually simplified as a garden and a library. Even so, Cicero's houses were also places of work. He wrote many of his books there, walking in his gardens as he thought. The remains of grand Roman villas around Tusculum can still be seen today. They show how educated Romans combined comfort, nature and learning in their country homes.

[long-pause]

{img:48-freedman-secretary-writing-shorthand-tablet}

Cicero's secretary Tiro created a system of shorthand, and one of its signs is still used in Ireland. Marcus Tullius Tiro had been born into slavery in Cicero's household. He was well educated and became Cicero's secretary and trusted helper. In 53 before Christ, Cicero freed him. Tiro remained loyal for the rest of Cicero's life. Cicero's letters to him are full of warmth, often asking about his health, since Tiro was frequently ill. Tiro is believed to have developed a system of shorthand, a way of writing quickly using signs. It was later known as Tironian notes and was used for centuries in Europe. One sign, a simple mark meaning and, is still used today on some road signs in Ireland. Historians note that the system was later expanded by others, so not every sign came from Tiro. Even so, his work made it possible to record speeches word for word. After Cicero's death, Tiro helped collect and publish his letters and speeches. He lived to a great age, reportedly almost a hundred, on a small farm.

[long-pause]

{img:49-cicero-at-window-evening-looking-toward-city}

In the early months of 44 before Christ, Cicero watched events in Rome with growing unease. In early 44 before Christ, Cicero spent much of his time away from Rome, writing at his country houses. He followed events in the city through letters and visitors. Caesar had been named dictator for life, and the Senate gave him one honour after another. His statue was placed among those of the ancient kings, and his image appeared on coins, something new for a living Roman. Many senators who had fought for the Republic felt that their freedom was gone. Cicero shared these feelings, but he did not take part in any plot. Historians note that he was not invited, perhaps because the conspirators feared that he was too cautious or too talkative. In his letters, he wrote that the future was uncertain. Even so, he could not imagine what would happen in March. His letters from these months show a man waiting and watching, unsure how the Republic he loved could survive under the rule of one man.

[long-pause]

{img:50-roman-bookshop-scroll-shelves-arcade-customers}

Roman books were long scrolls of papyrus, read by unrolling them from one side to the other. In Caesar's time, books were not made of pages bound together. They were scrolls, long rolls of papyrus, a writing material made from a plant that grew in Egypt. A single scroll could be several metres long. To read it, a person held it in both hands and unrolled it slowly from one side, rolling it up on the other. Each scroll held only part of a long work, so a large book needed many scrolls. Books were copied by hand, often by educated slaves working together in workshops. Bookshops stood in busy streets near the Forum. Customers could browse and order copies of popular works. Historians know about the book trade from writers such as Cicero, Catullus and later Martial, who mentioned where his own poems could be bought. Even so, books were expensive. Most were owned by wealthy people. The book with pages, called a codex, came into common use only several centuries later.

[long-pause]

{img:51-grammaticus-teaching-boys-homer-scroll-courtyard}

Children of wealthy Roman families studied Greek poetry and then the art of public speaking. Most Roman children who went to school at all attended a primary school, learning to read, write and count. Boys from wealthy families continued to a second teacher, called a grammaticus. He taught Greek and Latin literature, especially the poems of Homer, which every educated Roman knew. Pupils read texts aloud, learned them by heart and studied grammar and style. Many teachers were Greek, and some had been enslaved before being freed. After this stage, young men studied rhetoric, the art of public speaking, which was essential for careers in law and politics. Some went to Greece, to Athens or Rhodes, to complete their education. Historians know about Roman schools from writers such as Quintilian, who wrote a long book on how to train a speaker. Even so, girls rarely received this kind of education, though some daughters of rich families were taught at home. Cicero's daughter Tullia, for example, was known as an educated and intelligent woman.

[long-pause]

{img:52-temple-vesta-round-sacred-fire-priestesses}

Six priestesses called Vestal Virgins kept a sacred fire burning in Rome day and night. In the Forum, close to Caesar's official residence, stood a small round temple dedicated to Vesta, the goddess of the hearth. Inside burned a sacred fire, which represented the life of the Roman state. Six priestesses, known as the Vestal Virgins, guarded it. They were chosen as young girls from respected families and served for at least thirty years. During this time, they had to remain unmarried. If the fire went out, it was seen as a terrible sign, and the priestess responsible was punished. The Vestals were highly honoured. They had special seats at games, could own property and were trusted to keep important documents, including wills. Historians know about them from many ancient writers and from inscriptions and statues found in their house beside the temple. Even so, their lives were strictly controlled. Caesar's own will was kept in their care. The remains of the round temple and the House of the Vestals can still be seen in the Forum today.

[long-pause]

{img:53-family-offering-household-shrine-incense-bread-wine}

Romans believed in many gods, including spirits that protected the house, the door and even the hinges. Roman religion included the great gods of the state, such as Jupiter, Juno, Minerva, Mars and Venus. But Romans also honoured many smaller spirits connected with daily life. Each household had its own protective gods, called the Lares and Penates. Their images were kept in a small shrine, often in the main hall or kitchen. Families offered them food, wine and incense, especially on special days. The god Janus watched over doorways and beginnings, and the month of January is named after him. Later writers listed even smaller divine powers, such as a goddess of door hinges. Historians know about household worship from writers and from shrines found in houses at Pompeii, many still decorated with paintings of the Lares. Even so, religion was less about personal belief than about correct practice. Romans believed that if they performed the rituals properly, the gods would protect them. These small daily offerings were part of family life in every Roman home.

[long-pause]

{img:54-temple-interior-cella-large-statue-goddess-dim-light}

When praying, Romans covered their heads with a fold of their toga. For Romans, religion worked like an agreement between people and gods. People gave the gods sacrifices, offerings and prayers. In return, they hoped for protection, good harvests and success. Everything had to be done correctly, with the right words and actions. If a mistake was made, the ritual had to be repeated from the beginning. When praying or making a sacrifice, a Roman man pulled a fold of his toga over his head. This was believed to block out unlucky sights and sounds. Statues of this pose survive, including a famous one of the emperor Augustus as a priest. Inside temples stood large statues of the gods, often made of marble or bronze, lit by lamps. Most ceremonies, however, took place outside, at altars in front of the temple. Historians note that Roman religion was closely tied to politics. Priests were often senators, and Caesar himself was chief priest. Even so, ordinary people took part in festivals and offerings throughout the year.

[long-pause]

{img:55-calpurnia-roman-matron-garden-quiet-morning}

Caesar's wife, Calpurnia, had been married to him since 59 before Christ. Calpurnia was Caesar's third wife. She was the daughter of Lucius Calpurnius Piso, a powerful senator. They married in 59 before Christ, the year Caesar was consul, and the marriage helped strengthen his political alliances. Calpurnia was much younger than Caesar, perhaps by more than twenty years. For much of their marriage, Caesar was away fighting in Gaul and in the civil war, so they spent long periods apart. They had no children. Historians know very little about Calpurnia's own thoughts and character, since no letters of hers survive. Ancient writers describe her as loyal and devoted. Even so, the marriage was mainly a political arrangement, as was common among Roman nobles. Caesar also had a famous relationship with Cleopatra during these years. Calpurnia lived with Caesar in the official house of the chief priest in the Forum. She appears most clearly in the story of the night before the Ides of March, when she is said to have had a frightening dream.

[long-pause]

{img:56-caesar-dictating-letters-secretaries-busy-room}

Ancient writers said Caesar could dictate several letters at the same time. Caesar was famous for his energy and capacity for work. He rose early and worked late, reading reports, writing letters and giving orders. According to the writer Pliny the Elder, Caesar could dictate up to four letters at once to different secretaries, or even seven when he was not doing anything else. Plutarch adds that he dictated letters while riding on horseback. Historians treat these claims with some caution, as ancient writers liked to make great men seem extraordinary. Yet they reflect a real reputation for speed and efficiency. Caesar also wrote clear and simple Latin. His books about his wars in Gaul and the civil war are still read today, and many students learn Latin from them. Suetonius reports that Caesar used a simple code in his private letters, shifting each letter of the alphabet by three places. This is now known as the Caesar cipher. Even so, his heavy workload left little time for rest. Some friends worried about his health.

[long-pause]

{img:57-roman-horseman-galloping-countryside-hands-behind}

According to Plutarch, Caesar could ride a galloping horse with his hands clasped behind his back. Caesar was not a large or naturally strong man, but he trained his body hard. Ancient writers describe him as an excellent horseman and a tireless traveller. Plutarch writes that, as a young man, Caesar could ride at full gallop with his hands held behind his back. On campaign, he often marched on foot at the head of his men, in rain or sun. He could travel enormous distances quickly, sometimes covering a hundred miles a day in a light carriage. Caesar also suffered from health problems. Ancient writers mention headaches and fainting fits, which some described as epilepsy. In his later years, these attacks may have become more frequent. Historians and doctors have discussed what illness he might have had, but no certain answer is possible. Even so, his toughness impressed his soldiers. He shared their hardships, knew many of them by name and was admired for his courage. This close bond with his army was one of the foundations of his power.

[long-pause]

{img:58-cleopatra-young-queen-egyptian-greek-dress-calm}

Cleopatra, queen of Egypt, spoke many languages and was the first of her royal family to learn Egyptian. Cleopatra the Seventh was born in 69 before Christ. She belonged to the Ptolemaic dynasty, a Greek-speaking royal family that had ruled Egypt for nearly three hundred years, since the time of Alexander the Great. According to Plutarch, she was highly intelligent and spoke many languages. She was said to be the first ruler of her family who learned to speak Egyptian, the language of the people she governed. Ancient writers suggest that her charm came less from her appearance than from her conversation and character. Coins with her portrait show a woman with a strong nose and a determined expression. Historians note that much of what Roman writers said about her was hostile, since she later became an enemy of Rome. Even so, it is clear that she was a skilled and ambitious ruler. In 44 before Christ, she was about twenty-five years old and was staying in Rome, at a villa belonging to Caesar across the Tiber.

[long-pause]

{img:59-small-boat-evening-harbour-palace-steps-alexandria}

According to Plutarch, Cleopatra was secretly carried to Caesar inside a bedroll. Cleopatra first met Caesar in 48 before Christ in Alexandria, when she was about twenty-one. At that time, she was in conflict with her younger brother, Ptolemy the Thirteenth, over the throne of Egypt. His advisers had driven her out of the city. Caesar had arrived in Alexandria in pursuit of Pompey. Cleopatra needed to meet him, but her brother's soldiers controlled the palace. According to Plutarch, she travelled secretly by small boat at dusk with a single companion, a Sicilian named Apollodorus. He wrapped her in a bedroll, or a sack for bedding, tied it with a strap and carried it into the palace to Caesar. The story is often told as a rolled carpet, but Plutarch describes bedding. Historians cannot confirm every detail. Even so, the meeting changed history. Caesar supported Cleopatra, and after a short war, she became queen of Egypt. In 47 before Christ, she gave birth to a son, Caesarion, whom she said was Caesar's child.

[long-pause]

{img:60-villa-terrace-queen-small-boy-servants-roman-garden}

Caesar placed a golden statue of Cleopatra in a temple in Rome, which caused much gossip. In 46 before Christ, Cleopatra came to Rome with her young son Caesarion and her court. Caesar allowed them to stay at his villa and gardens on the far side of the Tiber. Many Romans disliked her presence. Romans were suspicious of foreign rulers, especially queens, and they disapproved of Caesar's relationship with her. Caesar also placed a golden statue of Cleopatra in the new Temple of Venus Genetrix in his forum. This caused much talk, since it placed a foreign queen beside a goddess. Cicero visited Cleopatra at the villa and later wrote in a letter that he disliked her, complaining of her pride. Historians use these sources carefully, as Roman writers were often hostile. Even so, Cleopatra's visit shows the international nature of Caesar's world. She remained in Rome until after Caesar's death. In April 44 before Christ, she left for Egypt with her son, where she continued to rule for many more years.

[long-pause]

{img:61-villa-interior-egyptian-greek-furnishings-lamps-evening}

Cleopatra's household in Rome included scholars and servants from Alexandria. Cleopatra's court in Rome brought something of Alexandria to the city. Alexandria was one of the greatest centres of learning in the ancient world, with its famous library and scholars of every kind. Her household was full of Greek and Egyptian servants, advisers and educated companions. Visitors to the villa would have heard Greek spoken, the language of her court, and seen luxurious furnishings from Egypt. Roman senators and important figures came to visit, some out of interest and some out of duty. Historians know few specific details of daily life at the villa. Even so, the connection with Alexandria had real effects on Rome. One of the scholars associated with Caesar at this time was the Alexandrian astronomer Sosigenes, who helped him create a new calendar. Historians see Caesar's interest in Egyptian knowledge as part of his wider plans to reform Roman life. The meeting of Roman and Egyptian worlds in this villa was brief, but it left lasting traces.

[long-pause]

{img:62-astronomers-sundial-scrolls-calendar-tablets-courtyard}

Before Caesar's reform, the Roman calendar had drifted almost three months out of step with the seasons. The old Roman calendar had only three hundred and fifty-five days. To keep it in line with the seasons, priests were supposed to add an extra month every few years. This task belonged to the college of priests, led by the Pontifex Maximus. But the priests did not always add the month at the right time. Sometimes they added it to lengthen the term of their political friends, or forgot it during times of war. By the 40s before Christ, the calendar was badly out of step. Festivals that were meant to celebrate the harvest fell at the wrong time of year. Historians know about this problem from writers such as Suetonius and Censorinus, who explained how the calendar worked. Even so, the old system had served Rome for centuries. Caesar, as chief priest, had the authority to change it. After his victories in the civil war, he finally had the time and power to carry out a complete reform.

[long-pause]

{img:63-sosigenes-astronomer-instruments-night-sky-roof}

With the help of an astronomer from Alexandria, Caesar created the calendar that is still the basis of the one used today. Caesar consulted the Alexandrian astronomer Sosigenes, who based the new calendar on the length of the solar year, the time the Earth takes to travel around the Sun. The new year had three hundred and sixty-five days, with one extra day added every fourth year. This extra day is the origin of the leap year. The new calendar began on the first of January, 45 before Christ. Historians call it the Julian calendar, after Caesar's family name. It was much more accurate than the old one. Even so, it was not perfect. The solar year is slightly shorter than three hundred and sixty-five and a quarter days, so the Julian calendar slowly drifted again, by about one day every hundred and twenty-eight years. In 1582, Pope Gregory the Thirteenth introduced a small correction. The result, the Gregorian calendar, is the one used in most of the world today. Its months and leap years still follow Caesar's design closely.

[long-pause]

{img:64-roman-family-counting-days-calendar-wall}

To catch up with the seasons, Caesar made the year 46 before Christ four hundred and forty-five days long. Before the new calendar could begin, the old one had to be brought back into line with the seasons. To do this, Caesar made the year 46 before Christ extraordinarily long. He added the usual extra month, plus two more months between November and December. The year lasted four hundred and forty-five days in total. Romans later called it the year of confusion. Historians know about it from several ancient writers, including Macrobius and Censorinus. People had to adjust contracts, rents and plans to a year that was almost three months longer than normal. Some Romans grumbled. According to Plutarch, when someone remarked that a certain star would rise the next day, Cicero joked that it would do so by order, meaning that even the stars now obeyed Caesar. Even so, the reform was a lasting success. From 45 before Christ onward, the seasons and the calendar stayed in step, and festivals fell once more at the right times of the year.

[long-pause]

{img:65-architects-plans-model-temple-table-workshop}

Caesar planned Rome's first public library and a canal through Greece that was only completed in 1893. Caesar had many ambitious plans for Rome and the empire. According to Suetonius, he wanted to build a huge temple of Mars and a great theatre at the foot of the Capitoline Hill. He planned Rome's first public library and asked the scholar Marcus Terentius Varro to collect Greek and Latin books for it. He also planned to drain marshes near Rome, to improve harbours and to collect Roman laws into a clear code. One of his most remarkable ideas was to cut a canal through the narrow isthmus of Corinth in Greece. Earlier rulers had also considered it, and later the emperor Nero began digging. Historians note that the canal was finally completed only in 1893. Even so, most of Caesar's plans were never finished, because he died soon afterwards. Some were later carried out by Augustus. The first public library in Rome was opened a few years after Caesar's death by one of his supporters, Gaius Asinius Pollio.

[long-pause]

{img:66-forum-of-caesar-new-paving-colonnades-equestrian-statue}

In 44 before Christ, Caesar was preparing to leave Rome for a great war in the East, planned for the eighteenth of March. One of Caesar's building projects was already in use in 44 before Christ. It was the Forum of Caesar, a new square next to the old Forum, dedicated in 46 before Christ. It was surrounded by covered walkways and shops. At one end stood the Temple of Venus Genetrix, Venus the Mother, honouring the goddess from whom Caesar's family claimed descent. In front of the temple stood a statue of Caesar on horseback. Meanwhile, Caesar was planning a major military campaign. He intended to attack the Parthian Empire in the East, which had defeated a Roman army under Crassus in 53 before Christ. Historians believe he also planned to campaign against the Dacians near the Danube. His departure was set for the eighteenth of March. Even so, many senators feared that, once away with his armies, Caesar would become even more powerful. Some believed that time was running out if anything was to be done. The remains of the Forum of Caesar can still be seen in Rome today.

[long-pause]

{img:67-marcus-brutus-serious-roman-reading-philosophy-lamp}

Marcus Junius Brutus was a serious, studious senator, respected in Rome for his honesty. Marcus Junius Brutus was about forty years old in 44 before Christ. He was known as a thoughtful man who loved philosophy and wrote books himself. His family claimed descent from Lucius Junius Brutus, who, according to tradition, had driven out the last king of Rome and founded the Republic in 509 before Christ. This history weighed heavily on him. During the civil war, Brutus had fought on Pompey's side. After Pompey's defeat at Pharsalus, Caesar pardoned him and gave him important positions. In 44 before Christ, Brutus held the office of praetor, one of the senior magistrates. Historians know about him from Plutarch's biography, from Cicero's letters and from his coins. Even so, his character is difficult to judge. Some ancient writers saw him as a principled defender of liberty, while others saw ingratitude. Plutarch wrote that even his enemies believed he acted from honest motives. That reputation made him very valuable to anyone planning action against Caesar.

[long-pause]

{img:68-statue-capitol-night-small-notes-scrolls}

According to ancient writers, notes were left for Brutus asking whether he was asleep. Brutus's mother was Servilia, a clever and well-connected noblewoman. For many years, she had been one of the women Caesar loved most, and Caesar treated Brutus with great favour. Some ancient gossip even suggested that Caesar might be Brutus's father, though historians generally consider this unlikely, given their ages. In early 44 before Christ, according to Plutarch and Suetonius, notes began to appear for Brutus. Some were written on the statue of his famous ancestor, the founder of the Republic. Others were left on the seat where Brutus sat as praetor. They contained short messages, such as Brutus, are you asleep, and you are not a true Brutus. The aim was to remind him of his family's history and push him into action. Historians note that these notes may have been placed by people who wanted him to join a plot. Even so, they show the pressure on Brutus. He was respected, and his involvement would make any action seem honourable rather than selfish.

[long-pause]

{img:69-roman-woman-noble-lamp-thoughtful-night}

Brutus's wife, Porcia, was the daughter of Cato, one of Caesar's most stubborn opponents. Brutus was married to Porcia, the daughter of Marcus Porcius Cato. Cato had been Caesar's most determined opponent in the Senate. He refused to accept Caesar's pardon and died in North Africa in 46 before Christ rather than live under Caesar's rule. Porcia was known for her strong character, like her father. According to Plutarch, she noticed that Brutus was troubled and could not sleep. To prove that she could be trusted with a secret, she made a deep cut in her thigh with a small knife and bore the pain in silence. Only then did Brutus tell her about the plot. Historians cannot confirm this story, but it was widely repeated in ancient times. Even so, it shows how Romans imagined the courage of noble women. Porcia was one of the few people outside the conspiracy who knew what was planned. According to later sources, she was so anxious on the morning of the Ides of March that she fainted.

[long-pause]

{img:70-cassius-lean-roman-soldier-senator-intense-gaze}

Gaius Cassius Longinus, Brutus's brother-in-law, was one of the main organisers of the plot against Caesar. Gaius Cassius Longinus was an experienced soldier. In 53 before Christ, he had served under Crassus in the disastrous campaign against Parthia and had led the survivors to safety. Later he fought for Pompey in the civil war, but, like Brutus, he was pardoned by Caesar. He was married to Junia Tertia, Brutus's half-sister. Ancient writers describe Cassius as sharp, proud and quick-tempered. Historians believe he was one of the first to plan the conspiracy and that he worked to win Brutus over. Some ancient writers suggested personal grievances, such as disappointment at not receiving higher office. Others believed he was genuinely committed to the Republic. Even so, Cassius understood that Brutus's reputation was essential. A plot led by Cassius alone might look like personal ambition. With Brutus, it could be presented as a defence of liberty. Shakespeare later famously described Cassius as having a lean and hungry look, drawing on Plutarch's portrait.

[long-pause]

{img:71-two-men-walking-garden-path-dusk-quiet-talk}

More than sixty senators joined the conspiracy against Caesar, including several of his own friends. According to Suetonius, more than sixty men took part in the plot against Caesar. Some were former supporters of Pompey who had been pardoned. Others were Caesar's own friends and officers, who had fought beside him in Gaul and in the civil war. Among them was Decimus Junius Brutus, a trusted general, and Gaius Trebonius, who had served Caesar for years. Historians have long tried to understand why such men turned against him. Possible reasons include anger at his near-royal power, disappointment over rewards and offices, and a sincere belief that the Republic was being destroyed. Even so, the plotters had no clear plan for what would happen afterwards. They believed that removing Caesar would be enough to restore the old Republic. Meetings took place quietly in private houses. Remarkably, the secret was kept among so many people for weeks. The plotters debated where to act and decided on a meeting of the Senate on the fifteenth of March, the Ides.

[long-pause]

{img:72-palatine-hill-cave-lupercal-fig-tree-february}

The Lupercalia was a very old Roman festival, held every year on the fifteenth of February. The Lupercalia was one of the oldest festivals in Rome. Its origins were so ancient that even the Romans were unsure of its meaning. It took place on the fifteenth of February and was connected with purification and fertility. The ceremonies began at the Lupercal, a cave at the foot of the Palatine Hill. According to legend, this was where a she-wolf had cared for Romulus and Remus, the twin founders of Rome. Priests sacrificed goats and a dog. Then young men, wearing only goatskins, ran around the Palatine, striking people they met with strips of goatskin. Women believed that being struck would help them have children. Historians know about the festival from writers such as Plutarch and Ovid. Even so, many details remain uncertain. The festival continued for centuries, even after Rome became Christian, until it was finally ended at the end of the fifth century. In 44 before Christ, it became the scene of a famous political moment.

[long-pause]

{img:73-forum-rostra-golden-chair-purple-robe-crowd}

At the Lupercalia of 44 before Christ, Mark Antony offered Caesar a crown, and Caesar refused it. In 44 before Christ, a new group of runners took part in the Lupercalia, named in Caesar's honour. They were led by Mark Antony, Caesar's fellow consul and close ally. Antony was about thirty-nine, strong and energetic. Caesar watched the festival from the speakers' platform in the Forum, seated on a golden chair and dressed in a purple robe. During the ceremony, Antony approached and offered him a diadem, a band worn by kings. Caesar pushed it away, and the crowd cheered loudly. Antony offered it again, and again Caesar refused. He ordered it to be taken to the Temple of Jupiter, saying that Jupiter alone was king of Rome. Historians still debate the meaning of this scene. Some think it was a test of public opinion, others that Caesar wanted to show he rejected kingship. Even so, the event increased suspicion. To many senators, it seemed that Caesar was considering becoming king, a title Romans had rejected for nearly five hundred years.

[long-pause]

{img:74-haruspex-old-etruscan-priest-temple-steps-robes}

A soothsayer named Spurinna warned Caesar of danger that would come by the Ides of March. Romans believed that the gods sent signs about the future. Special priests called haruspices, trained in an old Etruscan tradition, interpreted these signs, especially by examining the organs of sacrificed animals. According to Suetonius, a haruspex named Spurinna warned Caesar to beware of a danger that would come no later than the Ides of March, the fifteenth of the month. Ancient writers also describe other strange signs in the weeks before, such as lights in the sky and birds behaving oddly. Historians treat these stories with caution. Many may have been added after the event, when people looked back for warnings. Even so, the warning of Spurinna is mentioned by several ancient writers, so it may have a real basis. Caesar was known for ignoring omens when they did not suit him. He had dismissed his bodyguard of Spanish soldiers, saying that it was better to die once than to live always in fear. The phrase beware the Ides of March later became famous through Shakespeare.

[long-pause]

{img:75-lepidus-house-dinner-evening-lamps-friends}

On the evening before the Ides of March, Caesar dined with friends at the house of Lepidus. On the evening of the fourteenth of March, Caesar went to dinner at the house of Marcus Aemilius Lepidus, his Master of the Horse and second in command. It was an ordinary evening among friends. Caesar was reportedly signing letters as the others talked, a habit of his. According to Plutarch and Suetonius, the conversation turned to the question of what kind of death was best. Before anyone else could answer, Caesar looked up and said, a sudden one. Historians note that this story was remembered because of what happened the next day. It may have been told and retold to give the evening a sense of fate. Even so, it reflects Caesar's known attitude. He did not like to dwell on danger. After dinner, he returned to his home in the Forum, the house of the chief priest, where Calpurnia was waiting. Lepidus himself later became one of the three men who ruled Rome after Caesar's death.

[long-pause]

{img:76-roman-bedroom-night-shutters-open-moonlight-curtains}

According to ancient writers, Calpurnia dreamed during the night that she was holding Caesar in her arms and weeping. Ancient writers describe the night before the Ides of March as restless. Plutarch says that all the doors and windows of Caesar's bedroom suddenly flew open at once, waking him. In the moonlight, he saw that Calpurnia was asleep but crying and murmuring. She was dreaming that she held Caesar in her arms after he had been killed. Other versions say she dreamed that a decoration on their house had fallen. Historians cannot know what Calpurnia really dreamed. Stories of dreams and signs were common in ancient accounts of famous deaths, and they were often added later. Even so, several writers agree that she was deeply troubled when she woke. In the morning, she begged Caesar not to go to the Senate that day. Caesar was not usually affected by such fears, but this time he hesitated. According to Suetonius, he was also feeling unwell and thought of postponing the meeting. Calpurnia's worry, therefore, very nearly changed the course of events.

[long-pause]

{img:77-calpurnia-pleading-caesar-atrium-morning-grey-light}

On the morning of the Ides, Decimus Brutus persuaded Caesar to go to the Senate meeting after all. Caesar decided to send Mark Antony to cancel the Senate meeting. Then a visitor arrived at the house. He was Decimus Junius Brutus, one of Caesar's most trusted generals. Decimus was also one of the conspirators. He knew that if the meeting was postponed, the plot might be discovered. According to Plutarch, he laughed gently at the omens. He told Caesar that the senators were waiting and were ready to grant him new honours. He said it would look foolish to send them home because of a dream. Then he took Caesar by the hand and led him out. Historians see this moment as decisive. Without Decimus, Caesar might have stayed at home that day. Even so, it is not certain how much Decimus said or how the conversation really went. What is clear is that Caesar trusted him completely. In his will, Caesar had even named Decimus as one of his heirs in second line. Caesar left the house late in the morning.

[long-pause]

{img:78-litter-carried-through-crowded-street-petitioners}

On his way to the Senate, Caesar reportedly joked to the soothsayer that the Ides of March had come. Caesar was carried through the streets in a litter, a covered chair carried by servants. Crowds gathered, and people handed him petitions and notes. According to Plutarch, one man, a teacher named Artemidorus, gave him a note warning him of the plot and urged him to read it at once. Caesar kept it in his hand but did not read it in the crowd. Ancient writers also say that he saw the soothsayer Spurinna along the way. Caesar called out with a smile that the Ides of March had come. Spurinna is said to have replied, yes, they have come, but they have not yet gone. Historians treat these stories as part of the tradition rather than certain fact. Even so, they have become some of the most famous moments in Roman history. The meeting that day was not in the usual Senate house in the Forum, which was being rebuilt. It was held in the hall built by Pompey, at the end of the garden behind his theatre.

[long-pause]

{img:79-distant-view-columns-hall-doorway-figures-crowding}

Caesar was killed in the hall of Pompey's theatre on the fifteenth of March, 44 before Christ. Outside the hall, one of the conspirators, probably Gaius Trebonius, kept Mark Antony talking, so that he would not enter. Brutus had insisted that Antony should not be harmed. Inside, Caesar took his seat. The conspirators gathered around him, pretending to support a petition. Then, at a signal, they attacked him. Within a few moments, Caesar fell at the foot of a statue of Pompey, his old rival. According to Suetonius, he received twenty-three wounds, but a doctor later judged that only one had been fatal. Ancient writers report different last words. Suetonius records that Caesar said nothing, though some claimed he spoke in Greek to Brutus, saying, you too, my child. The famous Latin phrase et tu, Brute comes from Shakespeare and later writers. Historians note that the details vary between sources. Even so, the main events are clear. The most powerful man in Rome died in a few moments, in a hall built by the man he had defeated.

[long-pause]

{img:80-senators-hurrying-away-colonnade-togas-confusion}

After Caesar's death, the senators fled, and the city of Rome waited in uncertainty. Brutus turned to speak to the senators, wanting to explain that the act had been done for liberty and the Republic. But the senators did not stay to listen. They fled from the hall into the streets in panic. News spread quickly through the city. Shops closed, and people stayed in their homes, unsure what would happen next. The conspirators walked to the Capitoline Hill, declaring that Rome was free again. Historians note that they had made few plans for what would follow. They expected the people to welcome them, but the response was cautious and uncertain. Caesar's body lay in the empty hall for some time, until three enslaved servants carried it home on a litter to Calpurnia. Even so, Caesar's supporters, especially Mark Antony and Lepidus, soon took control of the situation. Within days, a compromise was reached in the Senate. The conspirators were given an amnesty, but Caesar's laws and decisions were confirmed, leaving the future of Rome unsettled.

[long-pause]

{img:81-gardens-of-caesar-across-tiber-villa-view-city}

In his will, Caesar left his gardens to the people of Rome and a gift of money to every citizen. A few days after his death, Caesar's will was opened and read publicly. It had been kept safely by the Vestal Virgins. The will named his great-nephew, Gaius Octavius, as his main heir and adopted him as a son. It also contained generous gifts to the people. According to Suetonius, Caesar left his gardens across the Tiber to the public as a park, and three hundred sesterces to every Roman citizen. When the will became known, public feeling turned strongly in Caesar's favour. At his funeral in the Forum, Mark Antony gave a speech and showed the crowd Caesar's bloodstained toga. The people were deeply moved. They built a funeral pyre in the Forum itself and burned his body there. Historians note that Shakespeare's famous speech beginning friends, Romans, countrymen is a later invention, though based on ancient accounts. Even so, the funeral was a turning point. Many conspirators soon had to leave Rome for their safety, and the struggle for power began.

[long-pause]

{img:82-young-octavius-reading-letter-apollonia-seaside-camp}

Caesar's heir, Gaius Octavius, was only eighteen years old and was at first not taken seriously. At the time of Caesar's death, Gaius Octavius was studying at Apollonia, a town on the coast of what is now Albania, where he was preparing to join the Parthian campaign. He was Caesar's great-nephew, the grandson of Caesar's sister Julia. He received the news in late March and decided to return to Italy and claim his inheritance, against the advice of his family. As Caesar's adopted son, he took the name Gaius Julius Caesar Octavianus. Historians often call him Octavian. Many experienced politicians, including Cicero and Mark Antony, saw him as a young boy who could easily be controlled. Cicero hoped to use him against Antony. Even so, Octavian proved extremely skilful. He used Caesar's name and popularity, especially with the soldiers, to build his own power. Ancient writers describe him as slight, often in poor health, but calm and determined. Within a short time, he had raised an army and become one of the most important figures in Rome.

[long-pause]

{img:83-comet-over-rome-night-summer-sky}

In July 44 before Christ, a bright comet appeared over Rome during games held in Caesar's honour. In July 44 before Christ, Octavian paid for public games in honour of Caesar, to celebrate his victories. During the games, a bright comet appeared in the sky and was visible for seven days. According to Pliny the Elder and Suetonius, the people believed it was the soul of Caesar rising to join the gods. Octavian encouraged this belief. He placed a star on the head of Caesar's statues and later had the comet shown on coins. The comet became known as the Julian star. Historians and astronomers have studied the event. Chinese records also mention a comet visible around this time, which supports the Roman accounts. In the same year, the month of Quintilis, in which Caesar was born, was renamed Julius in his honour. This is the origin of the name July. Even so, the comet was also used for political purposes. Two years later, in 42 before Christ, the Senate officially declared Caesar a god, and Octavian could call himself the son of a god.

[long-pause]

{img:84-coastal-villa-formiae-sea-evening-calm-cypress}

Years later, the emperor Augustus found his grandson reading a book by Cicero and gave it back with kind words. The conspirators had hoped to restore the old Republic, but instead there were years of struggle and war. Cicero spoke out bravely against Mark Antony in a series of speeches called the Philippics. But in 43 before Christ, Octavian, Antony and Lepidus joined forces and drew up lists of enemies to be killed. Cicero was on the list. He was caught and killed near his villa at Formiae, on the coast south of Rome, in December 43 before Christ. Brutus and Cassius were defeated at the Battle of Philippi in Greece in 42 before Christ, and both took their own lives. Many years later, according to Plutarch, Augustus found one of his grandsons holding a book by Cicero. The boy tried to hide it, afraid. Augustus took the book, read part of it, and gave it back. He said that Cicero had been a learned man, and one who loved his country. Historians see this story as a sign of Cicero's lasting reputation.

[long-pause]

{img:85-augustus-mature-statue-toga-calm-forum-dignified}

Caesar's heir became Augustus, the first Roman emperor, and his rule brought decades of peace. After defeating his rivals, including Mark Antony and Cleopatra at the Battle of Actium in 31 before Christ, Octavian became the sole master of the Roman world. He was then only in his early thirties. He had learned from Caesar's fate. He avoided the title of king and even refused to be called dictator. Instead, he claimed to have restored the Republic, while keeping real power in his own hands. In 27 before Christ, the Senate gave him the name Augustus, meaning revered. Historians regard him as the first Roman emperor. His long rule lasted until his death in 14 after Christ. It began a period known as the Pax Romana, or Roman peace, which lasted about two hundred years, though with many wars on the frontiers. The month of Sextilis was renamed August in his honour. Even so, the old Republic never returned. Caesar's death had not saved it. Instead, it led to the rule of emperors that lasted for centuries.

[long-pause]

{img:86-largo-argentina-ruins-below-street-level-cats-sun}

Today, cats sleep in the sun among the ruins near the place where Caesar was killed. In the centre of modern Rome lies a square called Largo di Torre Argentina. Below street level, there is a sunken area of ancient ruins, uncovered in the late 1920s. It contains the remains of four temples from the time of the Republic. Behind them lie the foundations of part of the Curia of Pompey, the hall where Caesar was killed. Archaeologists have identified a large block of concrete there, which some believe was placed later to mark the spot. Historians note that the exact site of Caesar's death cannot be known for certain. Today, the ruins are also home to a famous colony of cats. Volunteers have looked after them for many years, and the cats can often be seen sleeping on the old stones in the sun. In 2023, after restoration, walkways were opened so that visitors could enter the area. Even so, many people simply look down from the street. Every year on the Ides of March, people gather nearby to remember Caesar.

[long-pause]

{img:87-curia-senate-house-bronze-doors-forum-sunlight}

The Senate house that Caesar began to rebuild still stands in the Roman Forum. The Roman Forum is today a large archaeological park in the heart of the city. Visitors walk along the Sacred Way, the oldest street of Rome, past the ruins of temples, arches and law courts. Caesar's funeral procession passed along it. One of the best-preserved buildings is the Curia Julia, the Senate house. Caesar began its reconstruction, and Augustus completed it. The present building is a later restoration, made after a fire in the third century after Christ, under the emperor Diocletian. Its tall brick walls survived because it was turned into a church in the seventh century. The original ancient bronze doors were moved in the seventeenth century to the Basilica of Saint John Lateran, where they can still be seen. Historians and archaeologists continue to study the Forum and make new discoveries. Even so, it is now a peaceful place. In the afternoon sun, visitors wander among the ruins where the politics of the Roman world were once decided.

[long-pause]

{img:88-temple-divine-julius-ruins-niche-flowers-forum}

Every year, people still leave flowers for Caesar at the remains of his temple in the Roman Forum. After Caesar was declared a god, Augustus built a temple in his honour in the Roman Forum. It stood on the spot where the people had burned Caesar's body after the funeral. The temple was dedicated in 29 before Christ and was called the Temple of the Divine Julius. Today, only the concrete core of its platform survives, with a small curved niche at the front. It is believed to mark the place of the altar built over the site of the funeral pyre. Visitors often leave flowers, notes and coins there. On the fifteenth of March each year, many people come to lay flowers, and events are held to remember Caesar's death. Historians note how remarkable it is that such a custom has continued for so long. Even so, the Forum is quiet for most of the year. In the evening, as the light fades, the ruins grow calm, and the flowers in the small niche remain as a reminder of a story that is more than two thousand years old.

[long-pause]

That was Rome in the time of Caesar: the tall apartment blocks and street kitchens, the baths and the chariot races, Cicero's letters, a new calendar, and the quiet morning of the Ides of March.
