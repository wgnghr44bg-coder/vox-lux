{img:01-rome-seven-hills-tiber-river-winter-morning-44bc}

Most of us know Julius Caesar's last words. Et tu, Brute. You too, Brutus? We have heard them in films, in schoolbooks and on the stage. Yet Caesar almost certainly never said them. They were written more than sixteen hundred years later, by William Shakespeare, for a play. The ancient writers tell a quieter story. And most of what we imagine about Caesar's Rome, the marble and the swords and the shouting, is only a small part of the truth.

[pause]

The real city was full of bakers at dawn and children chanting their lessons. It had steam rising from the bath houses and carts rumbling through the night. There were letters written by lamplight, and a calendar that was three months wrong. So what did ordinary Romans actually do, in the winter and spring when Caesar ruled their city? Come with me through its narrow streets, slowly, and we'll find out.

[pause]

Before we go in through the city gate, a small thought. If you would like to keep me company on more evenings like this, a subscription lets you find your way back. No pressure at all. Let the walk decide.

[pause]

And now I'm curious about you. What sort of room are you resting in, and what can you hear outside? Tell me the hour on your clock, too, if you feel like writing a line.

[pause]

Then close your eyes, if they are not closed already. Let your breathing slow down. Feel the mild winter air of Rome, and the soft smell of wood smoke drifting over the hills.

[long-pause]

The Romans did not count years from a starting date, but named each year after two men.

[pause]

It is January, more than two thousand years ago, in the city of Rome. We call this year 44 before Christ. But the Romans did not count their years that way. Christ had not been born yet, of course. Instead, they named each year after the two consuls. These were the two chief magistrates, elected for one year at a time. So this year was called the year of the consulship of Caesar and Antony. One of the consuls was Gaius Julius Caesar himself, for the fifth time. The other was his friend and follower, Mark Antony. If you asked a Roman how old a building was, he might say it was built when so-and-so were consuls. Then he would have to work out the rest in his head. It sounds complicated to us. Yet it made sense to them. Every year was a little piece of history, with two names attached to it.

[long-pause]

{img:02-roof-tiles-frost-smoke-rising-hills-pale-sky}

Winter in Rome was mild, and people warmed their hands over little bowls of glowing charcoal.

[pause]

The winter in Rome was mild, compared with the winters of the north. There was rain, and sometimes a cold wind blew down from the mountains. The river Tiber rose and flooded the low parts of the city. On some mornings, there was frost on the roof tiles. People's breath steamed in the air. But snow was rare. The days were often bright and clear, with a pale blue sky over the hills. Smoke rose from thousands of hearths. In the evening, families gathered around their braziers. A brazier was a small iron bowl full of glowing charcoal. You held your hands over it to warm them. Then people ate supper by the light of little clay oil lamps. Each lamp had a single small flame, flickering at the spout. Can you picture it? A small room, a bowl of red embers, and the soft yellow glow of a lamp on a wooden table. That was a winter evening in Rome.

[long-pause]

{img:03-forum-valley-temples-basilicas-morning}

At the heart of Rome lay the Forum, a great open square where the whole city came to talk.

[pause]

Let's climb one of the hills early in the morning and look down. Below us, in the valley between the hills, is the Forum. It was the great open square at the heart of Rome. Temples surrounded it, with tall columns and red tiled roofs. There were great halls called basilicas. Inside them, the law courts met and the merchants did business. In the middle stood the Rostra. This was a stone platform from which politicians spoke to the people. It was decorated with the bronze beaks of enemy warships, captured long ago. The word rostra means beaks. Around the Forum, the city spread out on every side. There was a sea of red roofs and narrow winding streets. There were tall apartment houses and small gardens, baths, markets and workshops. Even at dawn, the square was beginning to fill. Someone was always there, talking, arguing or simply watching the world go by.

[long-pause]

{img:04-capitoline-temple-jupiter-gilded-roof-morning-sun}

On the Capitoline Hill stood the temple of Jupiter, with a roof that shone like gold.

[pause]

On the Capitoline Hill, above the Forum, stood the greatest temple in Rome. It was the Temple of Jupiter, the king of the gods. Its roof was covered with gilded tiles that shone in the morning sun. You could see it from all over the city. On the Palatine Hill, on the other side, were the fine houses of the rich and powerful. They had gardens and colonnades and looked down on the Forum. Beyond the Palatine, in a long valley, lay the Circus Maximus. This was the great racecourse, where chariot races were held. It could hold perhaps a hundred and fifty thousand people. Around the whole city ran the old walls. They had been built hundreds of years before. The city had long since grown past them. Houses, gardens and tombs now spread far beyond the gates. Rome had simply outgrown its own walls, like a child outgrowing a coat.

[long-pause]

{img:05-crowded-roman-street-people-many-lands-morning}

Rome was the biggest city in the Western world, and in its streets you could hear a dozen languages.

[pause]

How many people lived in Rome in 44 before Christ? Nobody knows exactly. Historians think there were perhaps three quarters of a million, or even close to a million. It was by far the largest city in the Western world. Only a few cities in China may have been bigger. And people came to Rome from everywhere. There were Romans and Italians, of course. But there were also Greeks, Syrians and Jews. There were Egyptians, Gauls, Spaniards and Africans. There were people from every land that Rome had conquered or traded with. Some were free, and some were enslaved. In the streets, you could hear a dozen languages. Latin and Greek were heard most of all. But you might also hear Aramaic, Gaulish and many others. Imagine walking down a single street and hearing the whole world talking. Rome was not one people. It was many peoples, crowded together on seven hills.

[long-pause]

{img:06-grain-ships-harbour-amphorae-unloading}

Grain from Egypt, wine from Spain and marble from Greece all flowed into Rome.

[pause]

Rome had grown rich and powerful by conquest. For hundreds of years, Roman armies had marched out. First they conquered the other peoples of Italy. Then they took the lands around the Mediterranean Sea. They took Spain, North Africa and Greece. They took Asia Minor and Syria. And in the last few years, Caesar himself had conquered the whole of Gaul. That is the land we now call France and Belgium. He had even crossed the sea, twice, to the misty island of Britain. The wealth of all these lands flowed into Rome. Grain came from Egypt, Sicily and Africa. Wine and olive oil came from Spain and Greece. Marble came from Greek quarries. Gold and silver, works of art and spices from the East all poured in. So did captives, sold as slaves. Rome had become the centre of its world. Yet all that wealth had come at a great price, paid by many other peoples.

[long-pause]

{img:07-quiet-forum-evening-lamps-few-people-columns}

For a hundred years, the great men of Rome had quarrelled and fought for power.

[pause]

Rome was also a city that had lived through terrible times. For about a hundred years, its great men had quarrelled and fought for power. There had been riots in the streets. There had been civil wars, with Romans fighting against Romans. Just a few years earlier, Caesar had fought a great civil war of his own. His rival was Pompey the Great, supported by much of the Senate. Caesar had won. Now he ruled Rome. Most ordinary people hoped there would be peace at last. They were tired of fighting. They wanted to work, eat, raise their children and go to the races. But some of the great men of the Senate were not happy. They felt that the old ways of Rome were slipping away. And as the winter turned to spring, something was going to happen in this city. It would be remembered for more than two thousand years. But that is still far ahead. For now, let's simply walk, and look.

[long-pause]

{img:08-julius-caesar-portrait-laurel-wreath-toga-calm}

The lamps in the Forum are flickering. Before we walk on, let's meet the man whose name was on everyone's lips.

[pause]

Caesar was going bald, and he liked to wear a wreath of laurel leaves to hide it.

[pause]

In the winter of 44 before Christ, Julius Caesar was fifty-five years old. He was tall, slim and pale. He had dark, piercing eyes and a high forehead. And he was going bald, which he was rather sensitive about. The Senate had given him the right to wear a wreath of laurel leaves at all times. The ancient writers say he was very glad of it. It covered the top of his head nicely. He dressed carefully, and a little unusually. He wore a loose belt and long sleeves, fringed at the wrists. Some old-fashioned Romans thought this looked careless, even a bit dangerous. Yet people who met him said he had great charm. He could be warm and funny. He remembered names and faces. He could talk to a general or a baker with the same ease. Statues of him still exist. If you look closely, you can see a thin, clever face, a little tired, with lines around the eyes.

[long-pause]

{img:09-subura-crowded-street-shops-apartments}

Caesar's family claimed to descend from the goddess Venus, yet he grew up in a noisy, crowded neighbourhood.

[pause]

Caesar was born in the year 100 before Christ, in Rome. His family was old and noble. They were called the Julii. They claimed to be descended from the goddess Venus herself. The story went that her grandson, Iulus, had escaped from the burning city of Troy. Yet in Caesar's childhood, the family was not rich, or very powerful. He grew up in an ordinary part of Rome called the Subura. It was a crowded, noisy neighbourhood, full of shops, workshops and tall apartment houses. Street sellers shouted their wares. Children played in the lanes. Neighbours argued from their windows. Perhaps this is why Caesar always understood the common people of Rome. He had lived among them as a boy. He knew how they talked and what they worried about. And perhaps that is also why so many of them loved him. To them, he was not a distant lord. He was a boy from the Subura who had done well.

[long-pause]

{img:10-roman-matron-mother-teaching-boy-courtyard}

Caesar's mother, Aurelia, raised him alone and stayed one of the most important people in his life.

[pause]

Caesar's father died when the boy was fifteen. After that, his mother brought him up. Her name was Aurelia. She was a wise and strong woman, and very well respected in Rome. The ancient writers praise her good sense. She ran the household, looked after the family's money and kept a close eye on her son. Even when Caesar was a grown man and an important politician, he lived with her for many years. She helped to manage his household. She received his visitors and kept his secrets. She was one of the most important people in his life. When she died, about ten years before our story, Caesar was far away, fighting in Gaul. He could not come home for her funeral. We do not know exactly how he felt. But his friends knew he had lost his greatest supporter. Behind many famous men, there is someone like Aurelia, quietly holding everything together.

[long-pause]

{img:11-roman-orator-speaking-crowd-forum-rostra-sunlit}

Caesar once owed so much money that he could not leave Rome until a rich friend promised to pay.

[pause]

As a young man, Caesar was clever, charming and very ambitious. He was a brilliant speaker, one of the best in Rome. He also wrote well, in a clear, plain, simple Latin. Schoolchildren still read his books today. He was very generous too. He spent huge sums on games, shows and feasts for the people, to win their favour. Much of that money was borrowed. At one point, he owed so much that his creditors would not let him leave the city. He was about to go and govern a province in Spain. Only when the richest man in Rome, Marcus Crassus, promised to pay his debts could Caesar set off. It was a big gamble. But Caesar was a gambler by nature. He believed that if he spent boldly now, he would win it all back later. And, as it turned out, he did. Would you have lent him money? Many sensible Romans would have said no. Some of them later wished they had said yes.

[long-pause]

{img:12-ancient-pirate-ship-calm-sea-island-coast}

When pirates captured the young Caesar, he told them his ransom was far too low.

[pause]

There is a famous story about Caesar as a young man. When he was about twenty-five, he sailed for the island of Rhodes, in Greece, to study. On the way, pirates captured his ship. They held him prisoner on a small island and demanded a ransom of twenty talents of silver. Caesar laughed. He said he was worth at least fifty. So they asked for fifty instead. While he waited for the money to arrive, he made himself quite at home. He joined in the pirates' games and exercises. He read them his poems and speeches. If they did not applaud, he called them uneducated. When he wanted to sleep, he sent word for them to be quiet. And he told them, laughing, that one day he would come back and punish them. They thought it was a joke. It was not. When the ransom was paid and he was set free, he gathered some ships, sailed back and captured them, just as he had promised.

[long-pause]

{img:13-domus-publica-house-forum-temple-vesta-round}

As chief priest of Rome, Caesar was given a fine house right in the middle of the Forum.

[pause]

Step by step, Caesar rose to the top of Roman politics. He was elected to one office after another. He became Pontifex Maximus, the chief priest of Rome. It was a great honour, and it lasted for life. It also gave him a fine official house, right in the Forum, on the oldest street in the city. Then he became governor of part of Spain. In 59 before Christ, he was elected consul, the highest office in the Republic. After that, he became governor of Gaul. For nine years, he fought there at the head of his legions. He conquered the whole country. He became enormously rich and enormously famous. His soldiers loved him. He shared their hardships, knew many of them by name, and called them comrades. Yet back in Rome, his enemies in the Senate grew afraid. They thought he was becoming far too powerful. And they whispered that he wanted to make himself king.

[long-pause]

{img:14-river-rubicon-small-stream-winter-soldiers-crossing}

Rome's war began when Caesar led his army across a small river called the Rubicon.

[pause]

The Romans hated the very idea of a king. Hundreds of years earlier, they had driven out their last king, Tarquin the Proud. They had set up the Republic instead. In the Republic, the people elected their leaders every year, and no one man could hold power for long. For the Romans, the word for king, rex, was one of the most hated words in their language. In January of 49 before Christ, the Senate ordered Caesar to give up his army. He was to return to Rome as an ordinary citizen. Caesar knew what that meant. His enemies would put him on trial, and his career would be over. So instead, he led his soldiers across a little river called the Rubicon. It was the border between his province and Italy. Crossing it with an army was an act of war. As he crossed, he is supposed to have said: the die is cast. We still say crossing the Rubicon today, for a choice that cannot be undone.

[long-pause]

{img:15-caesar-receiving-former-enemies-courtyard-gesture}

Caesar was proud of forgiving his enemies, and some of them could not bear it.

[pause]

The civil war lasted four years. It was fought all around the Mediterranean. Caesar's great rival, Pompey, was defeated in Greece. He fled to Egypt, where he was killed by the advisers of the young king there. When Caesar arrived and heard what had happened, it is said that he turned away and wept. Then Caesar won battle after battle. After one quick victory in Asia, he sent home a famous message of just three words. Veni, vidi, vici. I came, I saw, I conquered. At last, in 45 before Christ, the war was over. Caesar came home as master of the Roman world. The Senate made him dictator for life. Yet he was not cruel, for a Roman of his time. He forgave many of his old enemies. He let them come home and keep their property. He even gave some of them important jobs. He was proud of this mercy. The Romans called it clementia. But some of the men he forgave did not want to owe him their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:16-dawn-roman-street-shutters-opening-shops-bakers}

Enough of generals and great men, for a while. Let's spend one ordinary winter day in the city, from first light to the last lamp.

[pause]

In Rome, an hour was longer in summer than in winter.

[pause]

The day in Rome began very early, before dawn. The Romans had no clocks like ours. They divided the daylight into twelve hours, from sunrise to sunset. So in winter, the hours were short, and in summer they were long. A winter hour might last only forty-five minutes. A summer hour might last an hour and a quarter. Most people got up at first light, or even earlier. In the dark, the bakers were already at work. Their bakeries stood on street corners. Donkeys walked round and round, turning great stone mills to grind the grain. The bakers shaped round loaves, marked into sections like a wheel, and baked them in brick ovens. Soon the smell of warm bread drifted through the streets. Shopkeepers took down the wooden shutters from the front of their shops. The shops opened straight onto the street. And slowly, the streets began to fill with people, voices and the clatter of the new day.

[long-pause]

{img:17-roman-school-arcade-children-wax-tablets-teacher}

The schools met in open arcades, and the neighbours complained about the children chanting.

[pause]

As the light grew, the schoolmasters opened their schools. Most schools were not buildings at all. They were simply a corner of an open arcade along the street, behind a curtain. The children arrived early, carrying their wax tablets. A wax tablet was a small wooden board covered in a thin layer of wax. You wrote on it by scratching with a pointed stylus. The other end of the stylus was flat, for smoothing out mistakes. Boys and girls learned reading, writing and counting. They chanted their lessons out loud, all together. They learned long passages of poetry by heart. The neighbours often complained about the noise. One Roman poet grumbled that schoolmasters woke him before the roosters did. The teachers were often Greek freedmen, poorly paid and rather strict. Lessons ran from dawn until about midday. Then the children were free to run home through the busy streets.

[long-pause]

{img:18-atrium-clients-waiting-togas-morning-light-pool}

Every morning, poorer Romans put on their togas and went to say good morning to a great man.

[pause]

In the houses of the rich, the day began with the morning greeting. Every important Roman had his clients. These were poorer men who depended on him for help, protection and small gifts of money or food. In return, they supported him in politics. They followed him through the streets and voted for him in elections. So every morning at dawn, the clients put on their togas. They walked to the great man's house and waited in his entrance hall to wish him good morning. The more clients a man had, the more important he looked. The greatest men, like Caesar, had hundreds. Their halls were packed with people every morning. It could be a dull business for the clients. They stood around, yawning and gossiping, waiting their turn. Afterwards, some of them walked across the city to greet a second patron, and a third. A clever client could collect several breakfasts' worth of gifts in one morning.

[long-pause]

{img:19-atrium-ancestor-masks-shrine-lares-pool}

In the entrance hall of a Roman house, the faces of the ancestors looked down from little cupboards.

[pause]

The great hall of a Roman house was called the atrium. It was a tall room with an opening in the middle of the roof. This let in light and rain. Beneath it lay a shallow pool in the floor, to catch the rainwater. Around the walls stood little wooden cupboards. Inside them were wax masks of the family's ancestors. Each had a label telling which offices that person had held. On special days, the family took the masks out and wore them in processions. There was also a small shrine to the household gods, the Lares. The family made offerings there every day. A little bread, a drop of wine, a pinch of incense. Beyond the atrium lay a garden with a covered walk around it. It had flowers, fountains and statues. Around the garden were the family's rooms. The bedrooms were small and dark. The kitchen was simple. In a house like this, you lived with your ancestors watching kindly over your shoulder.

[long-pause]

{img:20-forum-morning-crowds-basilica-arcades-lawyers}

In the Forum, bankers changed money, lawyers made speeches and idle men gossiped about Caesar.

[pause]

After the morning greeting, the great man went down to the Forum. His clients followed behind him like a little parade. The Forum in the morning was busy and noisy. The law courts met in the basilicas. Lawyers made long speeches. Crowds gathered to listen, to cheer and to jeer. Bankers sat at small tables, changing money and lending it. Politicians stood on the temple steps, talking to friends and shaking hands with voters. There were fortune tellers and letter writers. There were pickpockets, too, slipping quietly through the crowd. And everywhere, there were idle men, standing around and gossiping. What was the latest news? What would Caesar do next? Who was in favour, and who was out? Rumours flew from one end of the square to the other. A story told at breakfast could be all over the city by lunch. In a way, the Forum was Rome's newspaper, its stock market and its law court, all at once.

[long-pause]

{img:21-roman-street-afternoon-quiet-shade-fountain-dogs}

By midday, the work of the day was mostly done, and many Romans took a nap.

[pause]

At about midday, the work of the day was mostly finished. The Romans ate a light lunch. It might be bread and cheese and olives. It might be cold meat, or leftovers from the night before. Then many of them lay down for a short nap. In summer, the heat made this a necessity. Even in winter, it was a pleasant habit. The streets grew quieter. Shutters were half closed. A dog slept beside a stone fountain, and water trickled gently into its basin. Somewhere a pigeon cooed on a rooftop. Then, in the afternoon, the city woke again. People headed for the baths, which were one of the great pleasures of Roman life. We'll visit them a little later. In the late afternoon, they ate the main meal of the day. It was called the cena. For ordinary people, it was simple. A bowl of porridge, or a stew of beans and lentils, with bread and a little wine mixed with water.

[long-pause]

{img:22-dark-roman-street-night-torch-slaves-lighting-way}

Caesar banned heavy carts by day, so all night long, wagons rumbled through the streets.

[pause]

As darkness fell, Rome went to bed. The streets had no lights at all, except for little lamps in the windows. Rich men coming home from dinner had slaves walking ahead with torches, to light the way. Most sensible people stayed indoors at night. The dark streets were not safe. Yet Rome was never quite silent. Caesar himself had made a law that heavy carts could not enter the city during the day. The streets were simply too crowded. So the carts came at night instead. All night long, wagons rumbled through the narrow streets. They carried stone, timber, wine and grain. The drivers shouted. The oxen bellowed. The wheels crunched over the paving stones. And the people in the apartments above lay awake and complained, as people in cities always will. One Roman poet wrote that in Rome, only the very rich could afford to sleep. Do you sleep well in a city? Many Romans did not.

[long-pause]

{img:23-triclinium-dining-room-painted-walls-three-couches}

Not every evening ended with a bowl of lentils, though. Let's put on our softest slippers and go out to dinner at a rich man's house.

[pause]

At a Roman dinner party, nine guests lay on three couches, leaning on their left elbows.

[pause]

A dinner party began in the late afternoon, while it was still light. The guests arrived freshly bathed, in light dinner clothes. Their slaves took off their shoes at the door, washed their feet and gave them soft slippers. The dining room was called the triclinium, which means three couches. Three wide couches stood around three sides of a low square table. The fourth side was left open, so the servants could bring the food. On each couch, three people lay side by side. They propped themselves on cushions and leaned on their left elbows. That made nine guests in all. Every place was arranged by importance. The guest of honour had a special place at the corner of the middle couch. The walls were painted with gardens, birds and landscapes, in bright reds and yellows. Bronze oil lamps hung from tall stands and gave a soft, flickering light. Have you ever tried eating lying down? The Romans thought it was the most relaxing way of all.

[long-pause]

{img:24-roman-starters-eggs-olives-oysters-silver-plates}

The Romans had a saying for a long dinner: from the egg to the apples.

[pause]

The dinner had three parts. First came the starters. There were eggs and olives, lettuce and mushrooms. There were oysters, little salted fish and sausages. With them, the guests drank a sweet wine mixed with honey, called mulsum. The Romans had a saying about a long meal. They called it from the egg to the apples. That was because dinner began with eggs and ended with fruit. You might use the saying the way we say from start to finish. Then came the main courses. There might be roast pork, kid or hare. There might be chicken, duck, or fish of many kinds. The rich especially loved a fish called grey mullet. They paid huge sums for a big one. On special occasions, there were stranger dishes. There might be dormice, rolled in honey and poppy seeds. There might be a whole roast boar, or even a peacock, served with its tail feathers spread out like a fan.

[long-pause]

{img:25-clay-amphorae-garum-jars-kitchen-shelf-herbs}

Romans poured a sauce made from fish left in the sun for weeks onto almost everything.

[pause]

Almost everything at a Roman dinner was flavoured with a sauce called garum. It was made from small fish, salted and left in the sun for weeks, in big jars. Slowly, the fish turned into a clear, salty, brown liquid. It smelled very strong while it was being made. In fact, it smelled so strong that garum factories had to be outside the towns. But the Romans loved it. They put it on everything, the way we use salt or soy sauce. The best garum came from Spain, and it was very expensive. Roman cooks also used a great deal of honey. They used pepper, which came all the way from India. They used herbs like lovage, coriander, cumin and mint. Romans liked strong flavours, sweet and sour and salty, all together. A Roman cookbook has survived from a little later. Some people today try its recipes at home. They say the results are surprisingly good, if you are brave.

[long-pause]

{img:26-roman-dessert-figs-grapes-honey-cakes-lamp}

At a Roman dinner there were no forks, no tomatoes and no sugar.

[pause]

Last came the dessert. There were apples and pears, figs and grapes, dates and nuts. There were little honey cakes, and cheese. Now think about what was missing. There were no tomatoes or potatoes. There was no chocolate, coffee or tea. There was no sugar. All of these came from lands the Romans had never heard of, or that were far too distant. And there were no forks. The Romans ate with their fingers, with spoons and with little knives. Servants brought bowls of water and napkins so guests could wash their hands between courses. Guests often brought their own napkins. At the end of the evening, they wrapped up leftovers in them to take home. The wine was always mixed with water. In winter, it was sometimes mixed with hot water. Only barbarians, the Romans believed, drank their wine unmixed. It is a nice thought, that someone two thousand years ago also took home a doggy bag.

[long-pause]

{img:27-musician-lyre-playing-dinner-guests-listening-soft}

While they ate, the guests listened to music, poetry, or a clever servant telling jokes.

[pause]

While they ate, the guests were entertained. There might be a musician playing the lyre or the double pipes. There might be a servant reading aloud from a book of poems or history. There might be dancers from Spain, clicking castanets. Or the guests themselves might sing, or recite poetry. But the most important part of all was the conversation. They talked about philosophy and books. They talked about history, the news of the day and the gossip of the city. In the winter of 44, one subject came up at every table. Caesar. What would he do next? Would he make himself king? People lowered their voices when they said it. You never quite knew who was listening. A dinner party could last for hours, deep into the night. Then the guests rose, a little stiffly, from their couches. Their slaves lit torches, and they set off home through the dark streets, full and sleepy and talking still.

[long-pause]

{img:28-seaside-villa-dinner-winter-evening-guests}

When Caesar came to dinner at Cicero's house, he brought two thousand soldiers with him.

[pause]

There is a lovely story about one dinner party. In December of 45, Caesar came to dinner at Cicero's country house by the sea. Cicero was the most famous speaker in Rome, and a wonderful writer of letters. Caesar did not arrive alone. He brought about two thousand soldiers with him. They all had to be fed, and they camped in the fields around the house. Cicero must have been quite worried. Yet the evening went very well. Caesar had a bath and a massage. Then he lay down to dinner and ate and drank with great enjoyment. They talked about books and literature, and not about politics at all. Afterwards, Cicero wrote to his best friend about it. It had been a pleasant evening, he wrote. But Caesar was not the kind of guest to whom you say: do drop in again, whenever you are passing. You can almost hear him sighing with relief as he wrote it.

[long-pause]

{img:29-insula-apartment-block-five-storeys-shops-below}

The dinner guests are walking home by torchlight now. Yet most Romans never lay on a couch at all. Let's see how they lived.

[pause]

Most Romans lived in tall apartment blocks called islands.

[pause]

Most people in Rome did not live in fine houses with gardens and fountains. They lived in apartment blocks called insulae. The word means islands, because each block stood alone, surrounded by streets. The insulae were tall buildings of brick and timber, four, five or even six storeys high. They were crowded together along the narrow streets. On the ground floor, facing the street, were shops, workshops and taverns. Above them were the apartments. The flats on the first floor were the best and the most expensive. They were large, with several rooms and real windows. Some even had running water, piped in from the aqueducts. But the higher you climbed, the smaller and cheaper the rooms became. It was the opposite of today, when the top floor often has the best view. In Rome, nobody wanted to climb six flights of stairs with a heavy jar of water. So the poorest people lived at the very top.

[long-pause]

{img:30-narrow-street-tall-buildings-laundry-lines-light}

A Roman poet joked that you should make your will before walking under the windows at night.

[pause]

At the top of the insulae, under the roof, the poorest people lived in tiny dark rooms. They were hot in summer and cold in winter. There was no water, no kitchen and no toilet. People had to carry their water up all the stairs from the public fountain in the street. Then they had to carry their rubbish down again. Or sometimes, they simply threw it out of the window. The poet Juvenal, who lived a little later, made a famous joke about this. He said that if you walked through Rome at night, you should make your will first. You never knew what might fall on your head from the windows above. Yet the people in those rooms made them homely, too. They kept pots of herbs and flowers on the window ledges. They hung washing on lines across the street. Neighbours called to each other from window to window. It was cramped and noisy. But it was also full of life.

[long-pause]

{img:31-crassus-slaves-water-buckets-street-smoke}

The richest man in Rome grew even richer by buying burning buildings cheaply.

[pause]

The insulae were often badly built by greedy landlords. They wanted to make as much money as possible and spend as little as they could. So the buildings were sometimes unsafe. Worse, they often caught fire. There were braziers, oil lamps and cooking fires in every flat. One spark could set the timber alight. Rome had no proper fire brigade at this time. That gave one man a clever and rather ruthless idea. His name was Marcus Crassus, and he was the richest man in Rome. When a building caught fire, Crassus would appear with his own team of slaves trained to fight fires. Then he would offer to buy the burning building, cheaply, from its desperate owner. Only when the owner agreed would his men put out the flames. In this way, Crassus came to own a great deal of Rome. It is not a kind story. But it shows us how much a city needs things we take for granted, like firemen.

[long-pause]

{img:32-roman-snack-bar-counter-clay-jars-street}

Rome was full of street snack bars, with hot stew kept in clay jars set into the counter.

[pause]

The people of the insulae did not spend much time at home. Their rooms were mostly for sleeping. They lived their lives in the streets. And the streets of Rome were full of shops of every kind. There were butchers and fishmongers. There were fruit sellers and vegetable sellers. There were wine shops, potters, shoemakers and tailors. Barbers shaved men right in the street, sitting on stools. And there were snack bars everywhere. Each one had a stone counter opening onto the street. Big clay jars were set into the counter. They held hot food, like stews, soups, sausages and spiced wine. Many Romans had no kitchen at home. So they bought their meals here, the way people today buy a sandwich on the way to work. Similar counters have been found in the ruins of Pompeii. Some still have food remains in their jars. Can you smell it? Lentil stew, warm bread and a hint of wood smoke.

[long-pause]

{img:33-craftsmen-street-blacksmith-potter-acrobat}

Each trade had its own street, and jugglers and snake charmers performed on the corners.

[pause]

There were craftsmen everywhere in Rome. There were blacksmiths, carpenters and goldsmiths. There were lamp makers who shaped clay lamps by the thousand. There were fullers, who washed and cleaned clothes by trampling them in great tubs. Each trade had its own street or neighbourhood. If you wanted sandals, you went to the street of the shoemakers. If you wanted a new pot, you went to the potters. And everywhere there were street sellers, crying out what they had to sell. There were beggars, and street performers. There were jugglers and acrobats, snake charmers and storytellers. Children followed the performers from corner to corner. Many Roman street names came from the trades practised there. Some of those names have survived, in a changed form, in the streets of Rome today. Isn't that a lovely thought? People still walk down streets named after craftsmen who worked there two thousand years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:34-grain-barges-tiber-oxen-towpath-warehouses}

Caesar counted the people receiving free grain house by house, and halved the list.

[pause]

Rome was so large that the farms of Italy could not feed it. So most of its grain came by sea. Great ships carried it from Egypt, Sicily and North Africa to the port of Ostia, at the mouth of the Tiber. From there, it went upriver to Rome in barges. Oxen pulled the barges, walking slowly along a path beside the river. For many years, the poorer citizens of Rome had been given free grain by the state every month. This kept them from going hungry, and it kept them from rioting. When Caesar came to power, more than three hundred thousand people were on the list. Caesar decided to check it. His officials counted carefully, street by street and house by house. Many names on the list belonged to people who did not need the grain, or who did not exist at all. In the end, he cut the number to about a hundred and fifty thousand, the people who really needed it.

[long-pause]

{img:35-aqueduct-arches-campagna-countryside-evening-light}

The water of Rome flowed in from the hills along stone channels, day and night, without stopping.

[pause]

Water came to Rome from the hills, along the aqueducts. At this time, there were four of them. They were great stone channels that carried fresh, clean water for many miles. Sometimes they ran underground. Sometimes they ran on tall arches across the valleys. They sloped very gently all the way, so the water flowed slowly down into the city. In Rome, the water poured into fountains on the street corners. People filled their jars there. It flowed into the public baths and into the houses of the rich, who paid for it. The water never stopped flowing, day or night. The overflow ran away into the great sewer, the Cloaca Maxima, and on into the Tiber. That old sewer is still working today, more than two thousand years later. Can you hear the fountains? In a Roman street at night, when the carts were quiet, there was always the soft sound of running water.

[long-pause]

{img:36-household-workers-kitchen-courtyard-morning}

Perhaps one in every four or five people in Rome was enslaved, yet many were later set free.

[pause]

Many of the people of Rome were enslaved. Perhaps one person in every four or five belonged to someone else, with no rights at all. They came from all over the world. Some had been captured in war. Some had been born to enslaved mothers. Some had been sold by pirates. Caesar's wars in Gaul alone had brought many thousands of captives to be sold. Some worked in the houses of the rich, as cooks, cleaners and nursemaids. Some worked as secretaries, teachers and doctors, because many educated Greeks were enslaved. Others worked in workshops, on the docks and on building sites. Their lives were often very hard. Yet in Rome, something unusual happened. Many enslaved people were eventually set free by their owners. They became freedmen and freedwomen. Their children were born as Roman citizens. Some freedmen became rich merchants. A few of their grandchildren even sat in the Senate. It was a cruel system, but not always a closed one.

[long-pause]

{img:37-saturnalia-feast-family-gifts-candles-merry-room}

At the merriest festival of the year, masters served dinner to their own slaves.

[pause]

The Roman year was full of festivals. These were holy days for the gods, when people did not work. There were processions, sacrifices, feasts and games. The merriest festival of all came in December. It was called the Saturnalia. For a few days, the ordinary rules of life were turned upside down. People gave each other presents, often small candles or little clay figures. Everyone wore soft felt caps, the caps of freed slaves, as a sign that all were equal for a while. Masters served dinner to their own slaves. People gambled with dice in the open, which was normally forbidden. They played games, sang songs and ate and drank too much. Friends shouted to each other in the street: Io Saturnalia! A later writer, Pliny, even built himself a quiet room, just to escape the noise. Does it sound familiar? Many people think some of our own winter customs, like giving small gifts in the dark days of December, have a little of the Saturnalia in them.

[long-pause]

{img:38-roman-public-baths-entrance-columns-afternoon-crowd}

The water from the hills does not only fill the fountains. It also fills the great steaming pools, where half of Rome spends its afternoons.

[pause]

A visit to the baths cost the smallest coin in Rome, and children often got in free.

[pause]

In the afternoon, the people of Rome went to the baths. There were many public baths at this time, perhaps more than a hundred and fifty. Most were small and simple. They were run by private owners who charged a tiny fee. It was a quarter of an as, the smallest coin there was. Almost anyone could afford it. Children were often let in free. The huge, famous baths with great domes, which you may have seen in pictures, did not exist yet. They were built later, by the emperors. At the baths, you left your clothes in a changing room, in little niches in the wall. You might pay a slave to guard them, because thieves were common. Then you went into the exercise yard. You lifted weights, threw a ball with friends, wrestled or ran. You kept going until you were warm and sweaty. Only then were you ready for the water.

[long-pause]

{img:39-caldarium-steam-hot-pool-bathers-soft-light}

The Romans did not use soap, but scraped themselves clean with a curved metal tool.

[pause]

After exercise, you went through the bath rooms in order. First came the warm room, the tepidarium, where you sat and relaxed. Next was the hot room, the caldarium. It was full of steam. It was heated by hot air flowing under the floor and inside the walls, from a great furnace. There was a hot pool, and a basin of cool water to splash your face. The Romans did not use soap. Instead, they rubbed olive oil on their skin. Then they scraped it off again, with the sweat and the dirt, using a curved metal tool called a strigil. It sounds odd, but it worked rather well. Last of all, you went into the cold room, the frigidarium. You plunged into the cold pool to wake yourself up. Then you dressed and went home for dinner, clean and refreshed. Doesn't that sound lovely, on a chilly winter afternoon? Warm stone under your feet, and steam curling slowly toward the ceiling.

[long-pause]

{img:40-bath-house-courtyard-gossip-snack-seller}

A philosopher who lived above a bath house complained about the man who sang in the bath.

[pause]

The baths were not just for washing. They were places to meet friends and gossip. People did business there, and made plans. Sellers walked around crying their snacks: sausages, cakes and drinks. You could have your hair cut, or your arm hair plucked. The philosopher Seneca lived a little later. For a while, he rented rooms above a bath house. He wrote a letter complaining about the noise. He described the grunting of the weightlifters. He described the slapping of the masseurs on bare shoulders. He described the shouts of the ball players and the cries of the sausage sellers. And worst of all, he said, was the man who liked to sing in the bath. Some things never change, it seems. Somewhere, at this very moment, someone is probably singing in a shower. Seneca would have understood your neighbours perfectly.

[long-pause]

{img:41-circus-maximus-chariots-racing-crowds-sunlit-sand}

At the chariot races, rich and poor, men and women all sat together and cheered.

[pause]

The Romans loved shows of every kind. On the great festivals, there were games, paid for by the state and by ambitious politicians. The greatest of all were the chariot races in the Circus Maximus. Caesar had recently rebuilt and enlarged it. Now it could hold perhaps a hundred and fifty thousand people. The chariots were light and fast, pulled by four horses. The drivers wore tunics in the colours of their teams. At this time there were the Reds and the Whites. Later, the Blues and the Greens joined them. They raced seven times around a long, narrow track. A low barrier ran down the middle, decorated with statues and shrines. On it stood seven great wooden eggs. One egg was turned over after each lap, so everyone could count. The turns at each end were the most exciting part. The crowd roared and bet on their favourite drivers. And Romans of every class came, rich and poor, men and women, all sitting together.

[long-pause]

{img:42-theatre-of-pompey-garden-colonnade-statues-fountains}

Behind Rome's only stone theatre lay a long garden, and a hall where the Senate sometimes met.

[pause]

There were plays too, comedies and tragedies, mimes, dancers and musicians. At this time, Rome had only one permanent stone theatre. It was the Theatre of Pompey, built eleven years earlier by Pompey the Great. It stood on the Field of Mars, outside the old city. It was huge and splendid, with a great curved bank of seats and a stage. Behind the stage lay a long garden. It was surrounded by colonnades and full of statues, fountains and shady trees. People strolled there on warm afternoons. At the far end of the garden was a large hall. The Senate sometimes met there. The old Senate house in the Forum had burned down a few years before, and it was being rebuilt. Inside the hall stood a great statue of Pompey himself. It is a peaceful place, with fountains and plane trees. Let's remember it, quietly. Our story will come back to this garden, one spring morning.

[long-pause]

{img:43-cicero-older-man-writing-letter-study-scrolls}

Now let's leave the noise of the crowds. Somewhere in a quiet study, a man is dipping his pen and beginning a letter to his friend.

[pause]

More than nine hundred of Cicero's letters have survived, so we can still hear his voice.

[pause]

Of all the people in Rome in 44 before Christ, there is one whose voice we can still hear clearly. His name was Marcus Tullius Cicero. He was sixty-two years old that spring. He was the greatest speaker and writer of his time, and one of the greatest in all history. He also wrote a great many letters. More than nine hundred of them have survived. Many of the replies he received have survived too. He wrote about politics and books. He wrote about his houses and his gardens, his health and his money troubles. He wrote about his family and the latest gossip. He wrote when he was happy and when he was sad, when he was angry and when he was afraid. Because of these letters, we know more about Cicero than almost anyone else in the ancient world. Reading them is like opening someone's post, two thousand years later. You can feel his moods change from one page to the next.

[long-pause]

{img:44-two-friends-talking-garden-bench-scrolls}

Cicero's dearest friend was a quiet, wise man who stayed out of politics and was liked by everyone.

[pause]

Cicero wrote most of all to his dearest friend, Titus Pomponius Atticus. Atticus was a rich, quiet and wise man. He stayed out of politics completely. He helped people on every side, and everyone liked him. That was very rare in those troubled times. Atticus also ran a team of copyists. They made copies of Cicero's books by hand, and Atticus sent them to friends and sold them. So in a way, he was Cicero's publisher too. Cicero told him everything. He shared his worries, his jokes and his plans. Sometimes he wrote to him several times a day. He once wrote that talking to Atticus was like talking to himself, only better. When Cicero was sad, Atticus calmed him. When Cicero was boastful, Atticus teased him gently. Do you have a friend like that? Someone you can tell anything, who still likes you afterwards? For Cicero, that friend was Atticus, for his whole life.

[long-pause]

{img:45-arpinum-hill-town-stone-houses-valley}

Cicero came from a small hill town, and was the first in his family to reach the top.

[pause]

Cicero was not born into a great Roman family. He came from Arpinum, a small town in the hills about seventy miles from Rome. As a young man, he came to the city and made his name as a lawyer. He won case after case with his brilliant speeches. Step by step, he rose to the highest office. In 63 before Christ, he became consul. He was the first man in his family ever to do so. That year, he uncovered a plot against the Republic and stopped it. The Senate called him the father of his country. He never forgot it. He was proud, and rather vain. He talked about that great year so often that his friends found it a little tiresome. Yet he was also kind, warm-hearted, funny and loyal. And he loved the Republic with all his heart. It is easy to smile at his vanity. But it is also easy to like him. He was, above all, very human.

[long-pause]

{img:46-cicero-walking-garden-colonnade-scroll-thinking}

After his beloved daughter died, Cicero comforted himself by writing books about friendship and old age.

[pause]

Cicero was not happy in the year 44. In the civil war, he had taken Pompey's side, against Caesar. Caesar forgave him. They were polite, even friendly. Caesar admired Cicero's writing very much. But Cicero no longer had any power. The Senate did whatever Caesar wanted. Cicero felt the Republic he loved was gone. And there had been a great sadness in his life. His beloved daughter, Tullia, had died a year before, in her early thirties. Cicero was heartbroken. For months he could hardly bear to see anyone. He walked alone in the woods and wept. To comfort himself, he wrote. In two years, he wrote an astonishing number of books. He wrote about friendship, old age and duty. He wrote about the gods and about how to live a good life. In one book, he wrote that life is like a play. A wise person, he said, does not mind when the last act comes. People still read these books for comfort today.

[long-pause]

{img:47-roman-villa-library-shelves-scroll-cases-sunlight}

Cicero wrote that if you have a garden and a library, you have everything you need.

[pause]

Cicero had several country houses, and he loved them all. His favourite was at Tusculum, in the hills southeast of Rome. It had gardens and a library. It had two covered walks, where he liked to stroll with friends and talk about philosophy. He also had a house by the sea at Formiae, and others besides. He spent a great deal of money on them, and on Greek statues to decorate them. So he was nearly always in debt. But he loved books more than anything. He once wrote to Atticus that a room without books is like a body without a soul. And in another letter, he wrote that if you have a garden and a library, you have everything you need. Perhaps you feel that way too, on a quiet evening. A few good books, a window onto something green. What more could anyone want? Cicero would have understood completely. He spent his happiest hours exactly like that.

[long-pause]

{img:48-freedman-secretary-writing-shorthand-tablet}

Cicero's secretary invented a kind of shorthand, and some of his signs are still used today.

[pause]

Cicero had a loyal secretary named Tiro. Tiro was a clever, gentle man. He had once been enslaved in Cicero's household, but Cicero had set him free. Tiro was often ill. Cicero wrote him warm letters, telling him to rest, eat well and get better soon. In one letter, he told Tiro that his only job now was to get well. Tiro invented a system of quick signs, a kind of shorthand. With it, he could write down Cicero's speeches as fast as Cicero could speak. One of his signs, a little mark for the word and, is still used in some languages today. And after Cicero's death, it was Tiro who gathered his letters together and kept them safe. Without Tiro, we would have lost them. So when we read Cicero's letters now, we are really reading a gift from a quiet, faithful secretary. He lived to be very old, they say, on a small farm of his own.

[long-pause]

{img:49-cicero-at-window-evening-looking-toward-city}

In the early months of 44, Cicero watched Rome from a distance, and wrote that nobody knew what would happen.

[pause]

In the early months of 44, Cicero spent much of his time away from Rome. He stayed in his country houses, writing. He watched Caesar and the city from a distance, with sadness. He wrote to Atticus that he did not know what would happen next. Nobody did. In the evenings, he sometimes stood at his window and looked toward Rome. The city lay far off on its hills, a faint glow of lamps in the dusk. He thought about the old days, when the Senate had mattered. He thought about Tullia. He thought about his son, who was studying in Athens. Young Marcus seemed to prefer parties to philosophy, and his father worried about him. Cicero did not know that, in a few weeks, everything would change. And he did not know that some of the men he knew best were already meeting in secret. In the evenings, they talked quietly about the Republic, and about Caesar.

[long-pause]

{img:50-roman-bookshop-scroll-shelves-arcade-customers}

Cicero closes his window, and the lamp burns on. All over Rome, other people are reading too, and praying, and teaching their children.

[pause]

Roman books were long rolls of papyrus, and you read them by unrolling with one hand.

[pause]

Rome in the year 44 was a city of books. They were not books like ours, with pages. They were scrolls, long rolls of papyrus. Papyrus was a kind of paper made in Egypt, from a reed that grows along the Nile. The writing was set out in columns along the scroll. You unrolled it with one hand and rolled it up with the other as you read. There were bookshops near the Forum. You could buy poems, histories and speeches there. You could buy plays, and books about farming, cooking and medicine. Every book was copied by hand. Teams of educated slaves sat in rows, writing as someone read the text aloud. A popular book might be copied hundreds of times. Rich Romans had libraries at home. Each scroll had a little label hanging from it with the title. And the Romans almost always read aloud, even alone, murmuring the words. Silent reading was so unusual that people commented on it.

[long-pause]

{img:51-grammaticus-teaching-boys-homer-scroll-courtyard}

Rich Roman boys studied Homer, and then learned the art of making speeches.

[pause]

Most children who went to school stopped after the first school, at about eleven or twelve. Many girls learned to run a household at home after that. Some girls from rich families were taught by private tutors and became very learned. The boys of rich families went on to a second school. There, they read the great Greek and Latin poets, above all Homer. They studied history, myths, geography and the stars. Then, at about sixteen, they moved on to the teacher of speaking. There they learned how to make speeches. That was the most important skill for any young Roman who wanted to be a lawyer or a politician. They practised with imaginary court cases and famous questions from history. The best students went on to study in Greece, in Athens or Rhodes. Caesar himself had studied on Rhodes, with a famous teacher. It was on his way there that the pirates had caught him.

[long-pause]

{img:52-temple-vesta-round-sacred-fire-priestesses}

Six priestesses kept a sacred fire burning in Rome, day and night, and it was never allowed to go out.

[pause]

Next to Caesar's house in the Forum stood a small round temple. It belonged to Vesta, the goddess of the hearth. Her priestesses were called the Vestal Virgins. There were six of them. They were chosen as little girls from noble families, and they served the goddess for thirty years. Their most important duty was to keep a sacred fire burning in the round temple. It burned day and night and was never allowed to go out. The fire was the hearth of the whole Roman people. As long as it burned, Rome was safe. The Vestals were deeply respected. They had seats of honour at the games. When they walked through the streets, an attendant went ahead of them, and everyone made way. Important people often left their wills with the Vestals for safekeeping. Caesar had left his will with them too. Imagine tending a little flame, through every night, for thirty years. It must have felt like looking after the heart of a city.

[long-pause]

{img:53-family-offering-household-shrine-incense-bread-wine}

The Romans believed there was a god of the doorway, and even a goddess of the hinges.

[pause]

The Romans lived surrounded by gods. There were the great ones, of course. There was Jupiter, king of the gods, with his wife Juno and Minerva, goddess of wisdom. There was Mars, god of war, and Venus, goddess of love. There were Apollo, Mercury, Neptune and many more. But there were also thousands of little gods and spirits everywhere. There was a god of the doorway, a goddess of the hinges and a god of the threshold. There were spirits of fields, springs, rivers and woods. Every house had its own household gods, who guarded the family and the store cupboard. At every crossroads in the city stood a little shrine. The neighbours left offerings there and held a festival together each winter. It is a gentle way of seeing the world, isn't it? A small spirit watching over your front door, your cupboard and your street. Nothing was too humble to be cared for.

[long-pause]

{img:54-temple-interior-cella-large-statue-goddess-dim-light}

When a Roman prayed, he covered his head with a fold of his toga, so nothing unlucky could interrupt.

[pause]

For the Romans, religion was a kind of agreement. You gave the gods what was due to them: sacrifices, offerings, prayers and festivals. Everything had to be done exactly right, with the right words and the right gestures, in the right order. In return, the gods protected you, your family and your city. If you made a mistake in the words of a prayer, you had to start all over again. When a Roman prayed, he covered his head with a fold of his toga. That way, he would not see or hear anything unlucky that might spoil the ceremony. A Roman temple was the house of a god. Inside, in a single room, stood the god's statue. The people did not usually go in to worship. The altar was outside, at the foot of the steps. The ceremonies took place there, in the open air, where everyone could see. Temples were also museums and treasuries. The state treasury of Rome was kept beneath the Temple of Saturn.

[long-pause]

{img:55-calpurnia-roman-matron-garden-quiet-morning}

Let's walk back across the Forum, past the little round temple and its glowing fire, to the house where Caesar lives.

[pause]

Caesar's wife Calpurnia had been married to him for fifteen years, though he was nearly always away.

[pause]

As chief priest, Caesar lived in an official house in the Forum. It was called the Domus Publica, the public house. His wife was Calpurnia. She was about thirty years old, the daughter of an important senator. She had been married to Caesar for fifteen years. It was his third marriage. His first wife, Cornelia, whom he had loved very much, had died many years before. She had given him his only daughter, Julia. Julia had married Pompey, and she too had died, ten years earlier, to Caesar's great grief. Calpurnia and Caesar had no children. For much of their marriage, he had been far away, fighting in Gaul and in the civil war. We know very little about her. But the ancient stories say she was gentle, loyal and devoted. They say she loved her husband very much, and worried about him. Perhaps she hoped that now, at last, he would stay home a little.

[long-pause]

{img:56-caesar-dictating-letters-secretaries-busy-room}

Caesar could dictate several letters at once, to several secretaries, while reading another.

[pause]

Caesar was a very hard worker. He got up early and worked all day and late into the night. He read letters and answered them. The ancient writers say he could dictate several letters at once, to several secretaries, while reading yet another. He saw visitors, made decisions and gave orders. When he travelled, he kept working in his carriage. He ate and drank very little, and he did not care for fancy food. There is a nice story about that. Once, at a dinner, the host served asparagus dressed with the wrong oil, a scented oil instead of olive oil. The other guests made faces and complained. Caesar ate his asparagus without a word. Afterwards, he scolded his friends for embarrassing their host. It is a small story. Yet it tells us something. He could be proud and ambitious, but he also had good manners and a kind heart for an anxious host.

[long-pause]

{img:57-roman-horseman-galloping-countryside-hands-behind}

As a young man, Caesar could ride at full gallop with his hands clasped behind his back.

[pause]

Caesar's health was not perfect. He suffered from headaches. He also had fainting fits, which the Romans called the falling sickness. Yet he was very strong and tough. In his wars, he had marched with his soldiers bareheaded, in sun and rain. He swam rivers and rode for days without rest. He was a superb horseman. It was said that as a young man, he could ride at full gallop with his hands clasped behind his back. His favourite horse was a strange one. Its hooves were split, almost like toes. Fortune tellers said this meant its rider would rule the world. Caesar raised the horse himself, and nobody else was ever allowed to ride it. But now, in 44, Caesar was fifty-five, and tired. He had been fighting, almost without a break, for fourteen years. Some of his friends thought he no longer cared much whether he lived or died. He only seemed to want to keep working.

[long-pause]

{img:58-cleopatra-young-queen-egyptian-greek-dress-calm}

Cleopatra spoke many languages, and she was the first of her family to learn Egyptian.

[pause]

Across the river, in Caesar's gardens, lived a very special guest. She was Cleopatra, the queen of Egypt. She was about twenty-five years old. Her family, the Ptolemies, were not Egyptians but Greeks. They were descended from one of the generals of Alexander the Great. For three hundred years, they had ruled Egypt from their city of Alexandria, by the sea. Cleopatra was very clever. She spoke many languages, perhaps nine or ten. She was the first of all her family to learn Egyptian, the language of her own people. She had studied mathematics, philosophy and medicine. A Greek writer named Plutarch wrote about her a century and a half later. He said her beauty was not so remarkable in itself. But her conversation was irresistible. Her voice, he said, was like an instrument with many strings. People who met her never forgot her. Not because of her face, but because of her mind and her charm.

[long-pause]

{img:59-small-boat-evening-harbour-palace-steps-alexandria}

The story says Cleopatra had herself smuggled in to meet Caesar, rolled up in a bedroll.

[pause]

Cleopatra had met Caesar four years earlier, in Alexandria, when she was twenty-one. She was at war with her younger brother over the throne of Egypt. Her brother's advisers had driven her out of the city. When Caesar arrived, she wanted to see him and ask for his help. But her brother's soldiers were guarding the palace. So, the story goes, she found a clever way in. A loyal friend rowed her across the harbour at night in a small boat. Then he carried her into the palace, rolled up in a bedroll, and unrolled her right in front of Caesar. Caesar, it is said, was charmed by her courage and her wit. He took her side. After a short war, she became queen of Egypt. Later, Cleopatra gave birth to a son. The people of Alexandria called him Caesarion, which means little Caesar. Is the bedroll story true? We cannot be sure. But it is a very good story.

[long-pause]

{img:60-villa-terrace-queen-small-boy-servants-roman-garden}

Caesar put a golden statue of Cleopatra in a Roman temple, and the whole city whispered.

[pause]

In 46 before Christ, Cleopatra came to Rome with Caesarion and her court. Caesar let them live in his villa across the Tiber. Many Romans did not like her. They distrusted foreigners, and they especially disliked kings and queens. Cicero visited her and wrote that he could not stand her. She had once promised him some books, he grumbled, and never sent them. Then Caesar did something that shocked many people. He placed a golden statue of Cleopatra inside his new temple to Venus, the goddess from whom his family claimed to descend. A statue of a living foreign queen, beside a goddess, in a Roman temple! People whispered. They said Caesar wanted to marry her and make himself king. They said he would move the capital to Alexandria. None of these things were true, as far as we know. But the whispers went on, from dinner table to dinner table. And in Rome, whispers could be very dangerous.

[long-pause]

{img:61-villa-interior-egyptian-greek-furnishings-lamps-evening}

In Cleopatra's villa, Roman senators met scholars and astronomers from Alexandria.

[pause]

What was it like in Cleopatra's villa across the river? We can imagine it. Her household was full of Greek and Egyptian servants, courtiers and scholars. They all spoke Greek, the language of her court. There was Egyptian furniture and fine linen. There were painted vases and the scent of perfume. Visitors came and went. There were Roman senators and poets. There were scholars and astronomers, all eager to meet the famous queen. Little Caesarion, about three years old, played in the garden. In the evenings, lamps glowed on the terrace above the river. Across the water, the lights of Rome flickered on its hills. Some historians think that astronomers from Cleopatra's Alexandria helped Caesar with one of his greatest achievements. It was something we still use today, every single day. It was a new calendar. Let's step into the next room, where the astronomers are bent over their charts.

[long-pause]

{img:62-astronomers-sundial-scrolls-calendar-tablets-courtyard}

The astronomers lean over their tables, measuring shadows and counting days. They are about to fix something that has been wrong for a very long time.

[pause]

Before Caesar, the Roman calendar had drifted nearly three months out of step with the seasons.

[pause]

Before Caesar, the Roman calendar was a terrible muddle. The old Roman year had only three hundred and fifty-five days. That is ten days shorter than the real year, the time it takes the Earth to go around the sun. To keep the calendar in step, the priests were supposed to add an extra month every two years or so. But the priests were also politicians. They added the extra month, or left it out, as it suited them and their friends. They might make a year longer when a friend was in office, or shorter when an enemy was. During the civil wars, the extra months were forgotten altogether. By the time Caesar came to power, the calendar was nearly three months wrong. The harvest festivals came before the crops were ripe. The spring festivals came in the middle of winter. Imagine celebrating the first flowers of spring, wrapped in your warmest cloak, while frost lay on the roofs.

[long-pause]

{img:63-sosigenes-astronomer-instruments-night-sky-roof}

With an astronomer from Alexandria, Caesar created the calendar we still use today, leap years and all.

[pause]

So Caesar decided to fix it. With the help of an astronomer from Alexandria named Sosigenes, he made a new calendar based on the sun. The year would have three hundred and sixty-five days, in twelve months. And every fourth year, one extra day would be added, in February. That made up for the quarter of a day left over each year. It was the first leap year. This is the calendar we still use today, almost unchanged. About sixteen hundred years later, a pope named Gregory made a small correction. Caesar's year was still a few minutes too long, and over the centuries those minutes had added up. But the calendar on your wall, or on your phone, is still essentially Caesar's calendar. Isn't that remarkable? Every birthday you have ever had, every holiday and every Monday morning, has been counted in a way that was worked out in Caesar's Rome.

[long-pause]

{img:64-roman-family-counting-days-calendar-wall}

To catch up with the seasons, Caesar made one year four hundred and forty-five days long.

[pause]

To bring the calendar back into step, Caesar made the year 46 before Christ very long indeed. He added two extra months to it, between November and December. He also kept the usual extra month in February. So that year had four hundred and forty-five days. The Romans called it the year of confusion. People argued about when rents and debts were due. Some joked that they had grown older without noticing. Then, on the first of January, 45 before Christ, the new calendar began. Not everyone was pleased. Cicero made a joke about it. Someone mentioned that a certain group of stars would rise in the sky the next night. Yes, Cicero replied, by order. As if even the stars must now obey Caesar. It was a clever little joke. But underneath it, there was a real worry. Some Romans felt that Caesar now controlled everything, even time itself.

[long-pause]

{img:65-architects-plans-model-temple-table-workshop}

Caesar planned Rome's first public library, and a canal through Greece that was not dug until 1893.

[pause]

Caesar had many more plans. He wanted to build a great new temple to Mars, the greatest in the world. He wanted a huge new theatre at the foot of the Capitol. He wanted to build the first public library in Rome, full of Greek and Latin books. He had already asked the great scholar Varro to collect the books for it. He planned to drain the marshes south of Rome to make new farmland. He wanted to dig a canal through a narrow neck of land at Corinth, in Greece. That would save ships the long voyage around the south of Greece. He wanted to gather all the laws of Rome into one clear collection. Many of these plans were carried out later, by other people. Some were never carried out at all. The canal at Corinth, for instance, was not finished until 1893. That is almost two thousand years after Caesar first imagined it. Big dreams, it seems, can take a very long time.

[long-pause]

{img:66-forum-of-caesar-new-paving-colonnades-equestrian-statue}

Caesar planned to leave Rome for a great war in the East on the eighteenth of March.

[pause]

One of Caesar's projects was already finished. People walked through it every day. It was the new Forum of Caesar. It was a long open square, lined with colonnades and small shops. At one end stood his beautiful temple to Venus the Mother. In front of it stood a bronze statue of Caesar on his favourite horse, the one with the strange hooves. And there was one more plan, the biggest of all. Caesar was going to war again. He was going east, to fight the Parthians. Their great empire lay beyond the river Euphrates, in what is now Iran and Iraq. Nine years earlier, they had defeated a Roman army and captured its sacred standards, the eagles. Caesar wanted them back. Sixteen legions were already gathering across the sea. Caesar planned to leave Rome on the eighteenth of March. He expected to be gone for three years. Some senators were alarmed. If he won, he would come home more powerful than ever.

[long-pause]

{img:67-marcus-brutus-serious-roman-reading-philosophy-lamp}

As the plans are drawn and the legions gather, a few quiet men are watching. Let's meet them now, gently, one by one.

[pause]

Brutus was a quiet lover of philosophy, and everyone in Rome trusted his honesty.

[pause]

Among the senators of Rome was a quiet, serious man of about forty. His name was Marcus Junius Brutus. He was a thoughtful man, a lover of philosophy, especially the philosophy of Plato. He read a great deal and wrote books of his own. He was not a great soldier or a great speaker. But everyone respected his honesty and his learning. People trusted him. Brutus had a famous ancestor, or at least he believed he did. Hundreds of years earlier, a man named Lucius Junius Brutus had led the Romans in driving out their last king. He had founded the Republic. Marcus Brutus believed he was descended from him. On the Capitol stood an old statue of that first Brutus, holding a drawn sword. In the civil war, Marcus Brutus had fought against Caesar. Yet Caesar had forgiven him at once and welcomed him back. Since then, he had given Brutus one honour after another. Caesar was very fond of him.

[long-pause]

{img:68-statue-capitol-night-small-notes-scrolls}

Someone left little notes on Brutus's chair: Brutus, are you asleep?

[pause]

Brutus's mother was a remarkable woman named Servilia. She was clever, strong and well connected. For many years, she was the woman Caesar loved most. Caesar trusted Brutus completely. He is said to have remarked that whatever Brutus wants, he wants very much. In the year 44, Brutus was a praetor, one of the chief judges of Rome. Caesar had promised him even higher office in a few years. Yet something was troubling Brutus. Someone had begun writing messages at night on the statue of his famous ancestor. If only you were alive now, they said. And on the chair where Brutus sat as judge in the Forum, someone left little notes. Brutus, are you asleep? You are not really a Brutus. He found these notes in the mornings and read them in silence. They pressed on him, like a weight. What did his name demand of him? He did not sleep well, those weeks.

[long-pause]

{img:69-roman-woman-noble-lamp-thoughtful-night}

Brutus's wife Porcia was as brave and proud as her stubborn father.

[pause]

Brutus was married to Porcia. She was the daughter of Cato, the sternest and most stubborn of all Caesar's enemies. Cato had refused to accept Caesar's forgiveness. He had died in Africa rather than owe Caesar anything. Porcia was as brave and as proud as her father. The old story says she saw that Brutus was troubled by some great secret. He would not tell her what it was. So she found a way to prove that she was strong enough to share it. She showed him she could bear pain without a word. Then, the story says, Brutus told her everything. Whether every detail is true, we cannot know. But the ancient writers admired her deeply. They saw her as a woman of great courage, in a time when women had little say in public life. Behind closed doors, she shared the heaviest secret in Rome. And she kept it.

[long-pause]

{img:70-cassius-lean-roman-soldier-senator-intense-gaze}

The man who first drew Brutus into the plot was his sharp, proud brother-in-law, Cassius.

[pause]

The man who first brought Brutus into the plot was his brother-in-law, Gaius Cassius Longinus. Cassius was very different from Brutus. He was a soldier, quick-tempered, sharp and proud. Years earlier, he had served in the disastrous war against the Parthians. He was one of the few officers who escaped, and he had saved what was left of the army. Like Brutus, he had fought against Caesar and been forgiven. He too was a praetor that year. But he was bitter. Caesar had given the most important judge's post to Brutus, not to him. And Cassius hated the idea of one man ruling Rome. In the early weeks of 44, he talked to Brutus quietly. The Republic is dying, he said, and only we can save it. The people expect it of you, because of your name. And if Caesar is made king, it will be too late. After many sleepless nights, Brutus agreed.

[long-pause]

{img:71-two-men-walking-garden-path-dusk-quiet-talk}

More than sixty senators joined the plot, including some of Caesar's own trusted friends.

[pause]

Others joined them. In the end, more than sixty senators were part of the plot. Some were old enemies of Caesar who had been forgiven. But some were his own friends and officers. They had fought beside him in Gaul, and he had rewarded them richly. One of them was Decimus Brutus, a distant cousin of Marcus Brutus. He was one of Caesar's most trusted generals. Caesar had even named him in his will. They had many different reasons. Some truly believed in the Republic and in liberty. Some were jealous, or ambitious, or bitter. Some felt that Caesar had not rewarded them enough. Some simply did not want to be ruled by anyone. They met in secret, in small groups, in each other's houses, and talked in low voices. They did not ask Cicero to join them. They thought he was too old, too talkative and too cautious. And they chose a day: the fifteenth of March, called the Ides.

[long-pause]

{img:72-palatine-hill-cave-lupercal-fig-tree-february}

The Lupercalia was so old that even the Romans were not sure what it meant.

[pause]

On the fifteenth of February, Rome celebrated the Lupercalia. It was one of the oldest festivals in the city. It was so old that even the Romans were not quite sure what it meant. It began at the Lupercal, a cave at the foot of the Palatine Hill. According to legend, this was where a she-wolf had found the twins Romulus and Remus as babies. She had fed them with her milk. The twins grew up to found Rome. At the festival, young men of good families gathered at the cave. They offered the old sacrifices. Then they ran through the city, laughing, around the Palatine and through the Forum. They wore only little goatskins around their waists. They carried strips of goatskin and tapped everyone they met, lightly. Young women held out their hands to be touched. They believed it would bring good luck and help them have children. It was cheerful, noisy and rowdy, and the whole city came to watch.

[long-pause]

{img:73-forum-rostra-golden-chair-purple-robe-crowd}

When Mark Antony offered Caesar a crown, Caesar pushed it away, and the crowd roared.

[pause]

That year, a new group of runners took part, named in Caesar's honour. Their leader was the consul, Mark Antony. Antony was thirty-nine, big, strong and handsome, with a curly beard. He liked to think he looked like the hero Hercules. He was a fine soldier and Caesar's most loyal follower. He also loved feasts and wine rather a lot. Caesar sat on the speakers' platform in the Forum. He sat on a golden chair, in a purple robe, watching. Antony came running in. He climbed up and held out to Caesar a diadem. It was a white band of cloth around a laurel wreath, the sign of a king. A few people clapped. Most of the crowd was silent. Caesar pushed it away, and the whole crowd cheered. Antony offered it again. Again Caesar refused, and again the crowd roared. Caesar sent the diadem to the Temple of Jupiter. Jupiter alone, he said, is king of the Romans. Did he mean it? Nobody knew then, and nobody knows now.

[long-pause]

{img:74-haruspex-old-etruscan-priest-temple-steps-robes}

February turns to March, and almond blossom appears in the fields outside the city. Let's walk through the last days slowly, and quietly.

[pause]

An old soothsayer warned Caesar to beware of a danger that would come by the Ides of March.

[pause]

The Romans believed in signs and omens. They believed the gods told people what was coming through the flight of birds, thunder, dreams and strange events. Special priests read these signs and explained them. The ancient writers tell us there were many strange signs in the weeks before the Ides. They wrote these stories down long afterwards, of course. Some may have been improved along the way. But they are part of the story, and the Romans believed them. They say there were odd lights in the sky and noises in the night. They say lonely birds flew down into the Forum. And they say an old soothsayer named Spurinna gave Caesar a warning. Beware, he said, of a great danger that will come no later than the Ides of March. Caesar did not believe much in omens. He took no notice. His friends begged him to keep a bodyguard. But he had sent his guards away. He said it was better to die once than to live always in fear.

[long-pause]

{img:75-lepidus-house-dinner-evening-lamps-friends}

At dinner the night before, friends asked what was the best kind of death, and Caesar answered first.

[pause]

On the evening of the fourteenth of March, Caesar went to dinner at the house of his friend Lepidus. Lepidus was his second in command. It was a pleasant evening among friends. While the others talked, Caesar lay on his couch and signed letters, as he often did. During the dinner, the conversation turned to a serious question. The Romans liked to discuss philosophy over their wine. What, someone asked, is the best kind of death? Caesar looked up from his letters. Before anyone else could answer, he said: a sudden one. One that is unexpected. Then he went back to his letters. The others must have remembered that moment for the rest of their lives. It is strange how small moments become heavy, once we know what follows. At the time, it was just a question over dinner. A lamp flickered. Someone poured more wine. And the evening went quietly on.

[long-pause]

{img:76-roman-bedroom-night-shutters-open-moonlight-curtains}

That night, Calpurnia dreamed that she was holding Caesar in her arms and weeping.

[pause]

That night, in the house in the Forum, Caesar and Calpurnia slept badly. The ancient writers say that all the doors and windows of their bedroom flew open at once, with a great noise. It woke Caesar up. In the moonlight, he saw Calpurnia asleep beside him. She was moaning and murmuring words he could not understand. She was dreaming. In her dream, she told him afterwards, she held him in her arms and wept for him. In the morning, the fifteenth of March, she begged him not to go to the Senate. She had never been anxious or superstitious before. But now she was frightened. She pleaded with him to stay at home and put the meeting off. Caesar had not been feeling well that morning either. And he was moved by her fear. The priests made sacrifices, and they said the signs were bad. So Caesar decided to stay at home. He would send Antony to tell the Senate the meeting was put off.

[long-pause]

{img:77-calpurnia-pleading-caesar-atrium-morning-grey-light}

A trusted friend laughed at the omens, took Caesar by the hand and led him out of the house.

[pause]

But then Decimus Brutus arrived at the house. Decimus was one of Caesar's most trusted generals. He was also one of the plotters. He knew that if the meeting was put off, the plot might be discovered. So he spoke to Caesar cheerfully, and laughed at the omens. The senators are all waiting for you, he said. They are ready to vote you great honours. What will people say if you send them home until Calpurnia has better dreams? If you really think the day is unlucky, he said, at least go yourself and tell them. Then he took Caesar by the hand and led him out of the house. It was late in the morning, around the fifth hour of the Roman day. Caesar was carried through the streets in his litter. As always, crowds gathered around him. People pushed forward to hand him letters and requests. One man, it is said, gave him a small scroll and begged him to read it quickly. But so many people pressed around that Caesar never had the chance.

[long-pause]

{img:78-litter-carried-through-crowded-street-petitioners}

Caesar called out that the Ides of March had come, and the old soothsayer answered: but not yet gone.

[pause]

As his litter passed through the streets, Caesar saw the old soothsayer, Spurinna, standing in the crowd. Caesar called out to him with a smile. The Ides of March have come, he said. Spurinna answered quietly. Yes, they have come. But they have not yet gone. The litter moved on toward the Field of Mars and the Theatre of Pompey. At the theatre, the senators had been waiting all morning. The plotters were there early, with their togas wrapped around them. Brutus and Cassius sat as judges in the garden colonnade. They listened to cases calmly, as if it were an ordinary day. But inside, they were very anxious. A message came that Porcia had fainted at home from the strain. Brutus was deeply troubled, but he stayed. A senator who knew nothing for certain came up and said quietly: I wish you success. But be quick. Then he walked away, leaving them wondering how much he knew.

[long-pause]

{img:79-distant-view-columns-hall-doorway-figures-crowding}

In the hall beneath Pompey's statue, it was over in a few moments.

[pause]

Outside the door of the hall, one of the plotters kept Mark Antony talking. Brutus had insisted that Antony must not be harmed. It must be against one man only, he said. So Caesar went into the hall alone. The senators rose as he came in. He walked to his golden chair at the far end and sat down. Behind him stood the great statue of Pompey, his old rival, looking down. The plotters gathered around him, as if to ask favours. Then, at a signal, they surrounded him. We will not stay to watch. It was over in a few moments. The ancient writers say that when Caesar saw Brutus among them, he stopped resisting. He drew his toga over his face. Some say he spoke a few words in Greek: you too, my child? Others say he said nothing at all. Julius Caesar was fifty-five years old. He lay at the foot of the statue of the man he had once defeated, in that quiet garden of fountains and plane trees.

[long-pause]

{img:80-senators-hurrying-away-colonnade-togas-confusion}

The senators ran out into the streets, and all of Rome closed its shutters and waited.

[pause]

Brutus turned to speak to the senators. He wanted to explain why it had been done, for liberty and for the Republic. But the senators did not wait to listen. They ran out of the hall and into the streets. The news spread through the city like the wind. People closed their shops, hurried home and bolted their doors. Nobody knew what would happen next. Would there be fighting? Would Caesar's soldiers come? The plotters walked through the streets toward the Capitol. They called out that Rome was free. But the people did not cheer. They stayed silent in their houses, and afraid. In the empty hall, Caesar lay alone for several hours. At last, three of his slaves came. Gently, they lifted him into a litter and carried him home through the streets to Calpurnia. As they passed, people came out of their doors to look. Many of them wept. And Calpurnia's dream had come true.

[long-pause]

{img:81-gardens-of-caesar-across-tiber-villa-view-city}

Night falls over a silent city. Let's step gently forward, past the hard days that followed, toward the long years of peace and the Rome we can visit today.

[pause]

In his will, Caesar left his gardens to the people of Rome, and money to every citizen.

[pause]

A few days later, Caesar's will was opened and read. The Vestal Virgins had kept it safe. In it, Caesar left his beautiful gardens across the Tiber to the people of Rome, as a public park. These were the gardens where Cleopatra had been living. He also left every citizen of Rome a gift of three hundred sesterces. That was several months' wages for an ordinary working man. The people were deeply moved. Caesar had remembered them. And there was something else in the will. Caesar had adopted his great-nephew, the young Gaius Octavius, as his son. He left him most of his fortune, and his name. On the day of the funeral, the Forum was packed with mourners. They would not let Caesar be carried away. Instead, they built a great fire there and then, in the heart of the city. They stayed beside its glowing embers all night. Among them were the Jews of Rome. Caesar had protected their right to worship, and they came back night after night to mourn him.

[long-pause]

{img:82-young-octavius-reading-letter-apollonia-seaside-camp}

Caesar's heir was a quiet, sickly boy of eighteen, whom nobody took seriously at first.

[pause]

Across the sea, in a town in what is now Albania, the young Gaius Octavius heard the news at the end of March. He was eighteen years old, slender and pale, with fair curly hair and clear bright eyes. He was often ill. He had been studying there with his friends, waiting for Caesar to arrive for the great war in the East. His closest friend was a young man of the same age named Agrippa. Agrippa would one day become one of the greatest builders in Roman history. They stayed friends all their lives. When Octavius heard that Caesar had adopted him, his mother begged him to refuse. It is too dangerous, she wrote. You are too young. But Octavius accepted. He took the name Gaius Julius Caesar and sailed for Italy. At first, Mark Antony laughed at him. He called him a boy who owed everything to a name. So Octavius sold his own property and paid every citizen the gift Caesar had promised them.

[long-pause]

{img:83-comet-over-rome-night-summer-sky}

That summer, a bright comet shone over Rome for seven nights, and the month of Caesar's birth was renamed July.

[pause]

In July, the month of Caesar's birth, games were held in his honour. Young Octavius paid for them. And during the games, something wonderful happened. For seven nights in a row, a bright comet appeared in the northern sky. It shone over Rome just after sunset. The people believed it was Caesar's soul, rising into the heavens to take its place among the gods. They called it the Julian star. Octavius was very clever. He placed a star on the head of every statue of Caesar. The month of Caesar's birth had always been called Quintilis, the fifth month. The old Roman year had begun in March. Now it was given a new name in Caesar's honour. Iulius. July. We still call it that today. Every year, when the warm days of July come round, we say Caesar's name without even thinking about it. And the month after it would later be renamed too, after Octavius himself.

[long-pause]

{img:84-coastal-villa-formiae-sea-evening-calm-cypress}

Years later, the emperor found his grandson hiding a book by Cicero, and gave it back with gentle words.

[pause]

The plotters had hoped that, with Caesar gone, the old Republic would return. It did not. Instead, there were more years of struggle and war. Cicero spoke out bravely against Antony, in a series of great speeches. They were among the finest of his life. But in the troubled times that followed, he lost his life, at his house by the sea, at the age of sixty-three. Brutus and Cassius also fell, two years after the Ides, far away in Greece. There is a gentle story told about Cicero, many years later. Octavius, by then an old emperor, found one of his grandsons reading a book by Cicero. The boy was frightened and tried to hide it under his cloak. The emperor took the book and read a long part of it, standing there. Then he handed it back. He was a learned man, my child, he said. A learned man, and a lover of his country. Cicero's books are still read today, all over the world.

[long-pause]

{img:85-augustus-mature-statue-toga-calm-forum-dignified}

Caesar's heir became Augustus, the first emperor, and gave the Roman world forty years of peace.

[pause]

In the end, Octavius became master of the whole Roman world. He was thirty-three years old. He was clever enough not to call himself king, or even dictator. He remembered what had happened to Caesar. He said he had restored the Republic. He kept the Senate, the consuls and all the old offices. But in fact, he held all the power. In 27 before Christ, the Senate gave him a new name: Augustus, which means the revered one. He ruled for forty-one years, until he died as an old man in the year 14 after Christ. He brought peace to the Roman world after a century of civil wars. The Romans called it the Roman peace. He was the first of the Roman emperors. And the month after July, which had been called Sextilis, the sixth month, was renamed in his honour. August. So the uncle and the boy he adopted still sit side by side in our calendar, summer after summer.

[long-pause]

{img:86-largo-argentina-ruins-below-street-level-cats-sun}

Where Caesar fell, cats now sleep in the sun among the old stones.

[pause]

Today, more than two thousand years later, you can still visit the places where these things happened. In the middle of modern Rome, among the busy streets, there is a square called Largo di Torre Argentina. In the middle of it, a few metres below street level, lie the ruins of four ancient temples. They have columns, steps and old weathered stones. Behind them, partly under the modern street, are the remains of a great stone platform. That was the hall of Pompey, where the Senate met on the Ides of March. It was here, more or less, that Caesar died. Today, the ruins are home to a colony of cats. There are dozens of them, sleeping in the sun on the warm stones and broken columns. Volunteers look after them in a little shelter in the corner of the square. Visitors come from all over the world to see them. A place of great history, now full of sleeping cats. Somehow, that feels peaceful.

[long-pause]

{img:87-curia-senate-house-bronze-doors-forum-sunlight}

The Senate house that Caesar began to build is still standing in the Forum, with its tall bronze doors.

[pause]

The Roman Forum itself is a wonderful place to walk today. You can follow the Sacred Way, the oldest street in Rome. Caesar's funeral procession passed along it. The young runners of the Lupercalia ran along it too, on that February morning. You can see the Rostra, the speakers' platform where Antony held out the crown. You can see the little round Temple of Vesta, and the garden of the Vestal Virgins with its long pools. Nearby is the spot where Caesar's house once stood. And you can see the Curia, the Senate house. Caesar began to build it, and it was finished after his death. It was rebuilt later by other emperors, but it is still standing. Its great bronze doors are copies of the old ones, which now stand in a church nearby. Next to it lies the Forum of Caesar. Three tall columns of his temple to Venus still rise there. Once, a golden statue of Cleopatra stood among them.

[long-pause]

{img:88-temple-divine-julius-ruins-niche-flowers-forum}

Every day, people still leave fresh flowers for Caesar in the Roman Forum.

[pause]

A little further on, in the Roman Forum, among the ruins of temples and halls, lie the remains of the Temple of the Divine Julius. It was built by Augustus, on the spot where the people of Rome had burned Caesar's body. There is not much left of it now. There is only a low platform of rough stone. In a small half-round niche, under a simple roof, are the remains of the altar. Yet every day, people still leave flowers there. There are fresh roses and lilies, wreaths, little notes and coins. They leave them for Caesar, more than two thousand years later. And every year on the Ides of March, people gather there to lay wreaths and remember him. Caesar's name lived on in other ways too. It became the title of the Roman emperors. Much later, it became Kaiser in German and Tsar in Russian. His calendar still counts our days. And his month still brings us summer.

So our slow walk through Caesar's Rome comes to a close.

Evening settles over the seven hills. The bakers have banked their ovens for the night. In the little round temple, the sacred fire glows softly, tended by a quiet figure in white. Down by the river, the water slides past the bridges, dark and calm. In a hundred small rooms, people blow out their clay lamps, one after another. Somewhere far off, a cart rumbles over the stones, and then it too is gone.
