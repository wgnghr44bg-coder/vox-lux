Tonight we are going more than two thousand years back, to the city of Rome, in the winter and spring of the year 44 before Christ.

[long-pause]

Rome is the greatest city in the world. It sits on its seven hills, beside the river Tiber, crowded with temples and markets and houses, full of people from every land around the Mediterranean Sea.

[pause]

And in this city, in this year, one man holds more power than any Roman has ever held before. His name is Gaius Julius Caesar.

[pause]

If you enjoy slow evenings in the distant past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, and come back for more of them.

[long-pause]

It is January, in the year that the Romans call the year of the consulship of Caesar and Antony.

[pause]

The Romans do not count their years from the birth of Christ, of course, because that has not happened yet. They name each year after the two consuls, the two chief magistrates, who are elected for that year. And this year, one of the consuls is Caesar himself, for the fifth time, and the other is his friend and follower, Mark Antony.

[long-pause]

The winter in Rome is mild, compared with the winters of the north.

[pause]

There is rain, and sometimes a cold wind blows down from the mountains, and the Tiber rises and floods the low parts of the city. On some mornings, there is frost on the roof tiles, and the breath of the people steams in the air. But snow is rare, and the days are often bright and clear, with a pale blue sky over the hills, and the smoke rising from a hundred thousand fires.

[pause]

{img:roof-tiles-frost-smoke-rising-hills-pale-sky}
In the evening, the people of Rome gather around their braziers, little iron bowls of glowing charcoal, to keep warm, and eat their supper by the light of small clay oil lamps.

[long-pause]

Let us go up onto one of the hills, early in the morning, and look down at the city.

[pause]

Below us, in the valley between the hills, is the Forum, the great open square at the heart of Rome. It is surrounded by temples, with tall columns and red tiled roofs, and by great halls, called basilicas, where the law courts meet, and the merchants do business. In the middle of it, there is the Rostra, the great platform from which the politicians speak to the people.

[pause]

Around the Forum, on every side, the city spreads out, up the hills and down into the valleys, a great sea of red tiled roofs, and narrow, winding streets, and high apartment houses, and little gardens, and temples, and baths, and markets, and workshops.

[long-pause]

On the Capitoline Hill, above the Forum, stands the greatest temple of Rome, the Temple of Jupiter, the king of the gods, with its golden roof shining in the morning sun.

[pause]

On the Palatine Hill, on the other side, are the fine houses of the rich and the powerful, with their gardens and their colonnades, looking down on the Forum. And beyond the Palatine, in the long valley between the hills, lies the Circus Maximus, the great racecourse, where the chariot races are held, which can hold perhaps a hundred and fifty thousand people.

[pause]

{img:capitoline-temple-jupiter-gilded-roof-morning-sun}
And all around the city, there are the old walls, built hundreds of years ago, which the city has long since outgrown.

[long-pause]

How many people live in Rome, in the year 44 before Christ?

[pause]

Nobody knows exactly. But historians believe that there were perhaps three quarters of a million, or perhaps close to a million people, crowded into the city. It is by far the largest city in the world at this time, except perhaps for some cities in China.

[pause]

{img:crowded-roman-street-people-many-lands-morning}
They come from everywhere. There are Romans and Italians, of course. But there are also Greeks, and Syrians, and Jews, and Egyptians, and Gauls, and Spaniards, and Africans, and people from every land that Rome has conquered, or trades with. Some of them are free, and some of them are enslaved. You can hear a dozen languages spoken in the streets: Latin and Greek, above all, but also Aramaic, and Gaulish, and many others.

[long-pause]

Rome has grown rich and powerful by conquest.

[pause]

For hundreds of years, the Roman armies have marched out, and conquered the other peoples of Italy, and then the lands around the Mediterranean Sea: Spain, and North Africa, and Greece, and Asia Minor, and Syria. And in the last few years, Caesar himself has conquered the whole of Gaul, the great land that is now France and Belgium, and has even crossed the sea, twice, to the misty island of Britain.

[pause]

The wealth of all these lands flows into Rome: grain from Egypt and Sicily and Africa, wine and olive oil from Spain and Greece, marble from Greece, and gold and silver, and slaves, and works of art, and spices from the East. Rome is the centre of the world.

[long-pause]

But Rome is also a city that has been through terrible times.

[pause]

For a hundred years, the great men of Rome have quarrelled and fought for power. There have been riots in the streets, and civil wars, Romans fighting against Romans. And just a few years ago, Caesar fought a great civil war against his rival, Pompey the Great, and against the Senate, and won.

[pause]

{img:quiet-forum-evening-lamps-few-people-columns}
Now Caesar rules Rome. The people hope that there will be peace at last. But some of the great men of the Senate are not happy. And as the winter turns to spring, in this year of 44 before Christ, something is going to happen, in the city of Rome, that will be remembered for more than two thousand years.

[long-pause]

{img:julius-caesar-portrait-laurel-wreath-toga-calm}
Who is Julius Caesar?

[pause]

In the winter of 44 before Christ, he is fifty-five years old. He is tall, and slim, and pale, with dark, piercing eyes, and a high forehead. He is going bald, and is rather sensitive about it, and likes to wear a wreath of laurel leaves on his head, which the Senate has given him the right to wear at all times. He dresses carefully, and rather unusually, with a loose belt, and long sleeves, fringed at the wrists.

[long-pause]

He was born in the year 100 before Christ, in Rome, into an old and noble family, the Julii, who claim to be descended from the goddess Venus herself, through her grandson Iulus, the son of the hero Aeneas, who escaped from the burning city of Troy.

[pause]

But his family is not rich, or very powerful, when he is young. He grows up in an ordinary part of the city, the Subura, a crowded, noisy neighbourhood of shops and apartment houses, among ordinary people. Perhaps this is why he always understands the common people of Rome, and why they love him.

[pause]

His father dies when he is fifteen. His mother, Aurelia, a wise and strong woman, brings him up, and is one of the most important people in his life, until she passes away, ten years before the events of tonight.

[long-pause]

As a young man, Caesar is clever, and charming, and very ambitious.

[pause]

He is a brilliant speaker, one of the best in Rome. He writes well too, in a clear, plain, simple Latin, which is still read in schools today. He is generous, and spends huge sums of money, much of it borrowed, on games and shows and feasts for the people, to win their favour. At one time, he owes so much money that he cannot leave Rome until a rich friend promises to pay his debts.

[pause]

{img:roman-orator-speaking-crowd-forum-rostra-sunlit}
There is a famous story about him. When he is about twenty-five, he is captured by pirates, on his way to Greece, and held for ransom. The pirates ask for twenty talents of silver. Caesar laughs, and says that he is worth at least fifty. While he waits for the ransom to arrive, he joins in the pirates' games, and reads them his poems and speeches, and tells them, laughing, that one day he will come back and punish them all. They think it is a joke. It is not. When the ransom is paid, and he is free, he gathers some ships, and comes back, and captures them, just as he said he would.

[long-pause]

Step by step, Caesar rises to the top of Roman politics.

[pause]

He is elected to one office after another. He becomes Pontifex Maximus, the chief priest of Rome, which is a great honour, and gives him a fine house in the Forum. He becomes governor of Spain. And in 59 before Christ, he is elected consul, the highest office in the Republic, for the first time.

[pause]

Then he becomes governor of Gaul. And for nine years, he fights in Gaul, at the head of his legions, and conquers the whole country, and becomes enormously rich, and enormously famous, and his soldiers love him.

[long-pause]

But his enemies in the Senate are afraid of him. They think he is becoming too powerful, and that he wants to make himself king.

[pause]

The Romans hate the idea of a king. More than four hundred and fifty years ago, they drove out their last king, Tarquin the Proud, and set up the Republic, in which the people elect their own leaders, every year, and no one man can hold power for long. For the Romans, the word king, rex, is the most hateful word in the language.

[pause]

{img:river-rubicon-small-stream-winter-soldiers-crossing}
In January of the year 49 before Christ, the Senate orders Caesar to give up his army, and come back to Rome as an ordinary citizen. Caesar knows that, if he does, his enemies will put him on trial, and his career will be over. So instead, he leads his army across a little river, the Rubicon, which is the border between his province and Italy. It is an act of war. He is supposed to have said, as he crossed: the die is cast.

[long-pause]

The civil war lasts for four years, and is fought all around the Mediterranean: in Italy, and Spain, and Greece, and Egypt, and Africa.

[pause]

Caesar's great rival, Pompey the Great, is defeated at the battle of Pharsalus, in Greece, and flees to Egypt, where he is killed, on the beach, by the advisers of the young king of Egypt, who hope to please Caesar. When Caesar arrives in Egypt, and is shown the head of his old friend and rival, it is said that he turns away, and weeps.

[pause]

{img:caesar-tent-maps-lamp-night-campaign-thoughtful}
In Egypt, Caesar meets the young queen, Cleopatra, and becomes her friend and her lover, and helps her to win the throne of Egypt. Then he goes on to fight in Asia Minor, where he wins a battle so quickly that he sends a famous message home to Rome, just three words: veni, vidi, vici. I came, I saw, I conquered.

[long-pause]

At last, in 45 before Christ, after the last battle, in Spain, the civil war is over, and Caesar comes home to Rome, as master of the Roman world.

[pause]

He celebrates great triumphs, magnificent processions through the streets of Rome, with his soldiers and his captives and the treasures he has won, and gives feasts for the whole city, at thousands of tables, and games and shows, and money to every citizen.

[pause]

And the Senate, full of his friends, gives him one honour after another. He is made dictator, an old office that was meant to be held for only six months, in times of emergency. First for ten years. And then, in February of the year 44 before Christ, dictator for life. Dictator perpetuo.

[long-pause]

Caesar is not a cruel man, for a Roman of his time.

[pause]

After the civil war, he forgives many of his enemies, and lets them come back to Rome, and keep their property, and even gives them important positions. He is proud of his mercy, his clementia, as the Romans call it. He does not want to be like the dictators of the past, who killed their enemies by the thousand.

[pause]

{img:caesar-receiving-former-enemies-courtyard-gesture}
But some of the men he has forgiven do not want to be forgiven. They do not want to owe their lives to Caesar. And some of them begin to think that, for the sake of the Republic, Caesar must be stopped.

[long-pause]

{img:dawn-roman-street-shutters-opening-shops-bakers}
Let us spend an ordinary day in Rome, in the winter of 44 before Christ.

[pause]

The day in Rome begins early, before dawn. The Romans have no clocks, as we do. They divide the daylight into twelve hours, from sunrise to sunset, so that in winter the hours are short, and in summer they are long. And most people get up at the first light, or even before.

[long-pause]

In the dark, the bakers are already at work, in their bakeries on the street corners, grinding the grain in great stone mills, turned by donkeys walking round and round, and baking the round loaves of bread in their brick ovens.

[pause]

Soon the smell of fresh bread drifts through the streets. The shopkeepers take down the wooden shutters from the front of their shops, which open straight onto the street. And the streets begin to fill with people.

[pause]

The schoolmasters open their schools, in the open arcades along the streets, and the children arrive, carrying their wax tablets, and the boys and girls begin their lessons, reading and writing and counting, in loud voices, which the neighbours complain about.

[long-pause]

In the houses of the rich, the day begins with the salutatio, the morning greeting.

[pause]

Every important Roman has his clients: poorer men, who depend on him for help and protection, and for gifts of money and food, and who in return must support him in politics, and follow him through the streets, and vote for him in elections. Every morning, at dawn, the clients put on their togas, and go to the great man's house, and wait in the entrance hall, to greet him, and wish him good morning.

[pause]

{img:atrium-clients-waiting-togas-morning-light-pool}
The more clients a man has, the more important he is. The great men, like Caesar, have hundreds, and the crowds in their entrance halls are enormous.

[long-pause]

The great hall of a Roman house is called the atrium.

[pause]

It is a tall room, with an opening in the roof, to let in the light and the rain, and a shallow pool in the floor beneath it, to catch the rain water. Around the walls stand the wax masks of the family's ancestors, in little cupboards, with labels telling of the offices they held. There is a little shrine to the household gods, the Lares, where the family makes offerings of food and wine and incense every day.

[pause]

Beyond the atrium, there is a garden, surrounded by a colonnade, with flowers and fountains and statues. And around it, the rooms of the family: the dining room, and the bedrooms, small and dark, and the kitchen, and the rooms of the household slaves.

[long-pause]

After the morning greeting, the great man goes down to the Forum, with his clients following behind him, to do his business.

[pause]

The Forum, in the morning, is a busy and noisy place. The law courts are meeting in the basilicas, and lawyers are making long speeches, and crowds are gathering to listen, and to cheer, and to jeer. The bankers sit at their tables, changing money, and lending it. Politicians stand on the steps of the temples, and talk to their friends and their supporters, and shake hands with the voters.

[pause]

{img:forum-morning-crowds-basilica-arcades-lawyers}
There are fortune tellers, and letter writers, and pickpockets, and idle men, standing around, gossiping, about the latest news, and about Caesar, and about what he will do next.

[long-pause]

At about midday, the work of the day is mostly done.

[pause]

The Romans eat a light lunch, of bread and cheese and olives, or cold meat, or leftovers from the night before. Many of them take a short nap, in the heat of the day, if there is any heat. And then, in the afternoon, they go to the baths.

[pause]

{img:roman-street-afternoon-quiet-shade-fountain-dogs}
The baths are one of the great pleasures of Roman life. We shall visit them a little later.

[long-pause]

In the late afternoon, the Romans eat the main meal of the day, the cena, the dinner.

[pause]

For ordinary people, it is a simple meal, eaten at home, or bought from a little hot food shop on the street corner: a bowl of porridge made of wheat or barley, or a stew of beans and lentils and vegetables, with bread, and a little cheap wine, mixed with water.

[pause]

For the rich, the dinner is a long and splendid affair, lasting for hours, with many courses, and guests, and entertainment. The guests lie on couches, three on each couch, around a low table, leaning on their left elbows, and eating with their fingers, while slaves bring the dishes, and pour the wine, and musicians play.

[long-pause]

And as darkness falls, Rome goes to bed.

[pause]

The streets are dark, without any lights, except the little lamps in the windows, and the torches carried by slaves, lighting the way home for their masters, coming back from dinner. Most sensible people stay indoors at night. The streets are not safe.

[pause]

{img:dark-roman-street-night-torch-slaves-lighting-way}
But Rome is never quite silent. Because there is a law, made by Caesar himself, that heavy carts may not enter the city during the day, when the streets are crowded. So all night long, the wagons rumble through the narrow streets, loaded with stone and timber and wine and grain, and the drivers shout, and the oxen bellow, and the people in the apartments above lie awake, and complain, as people in cities always will.

[long-pause]

{img:triclinium-dining-room-painted-walls-three-couches}
Let us go to dinner, in the house of a rich Roman, on an evening in the winter of 44.

[pause]

The dinner begins in the late afternoon, at about the ninth hour, while it is still light. The guests arrive, freshly bathed, in their light dinner clothes, with their slaves, who take off their shoes at the door, and wash their feet, and give them soft slippers.

[long-pause]

The dining room is called the triclinium, which means three couches.

[pause]

There are three wide couches, arranged around three sides of a low square table, with the fourth side left open, for the slaves to serve. On each couch, three people lie, side by side, propped up on cushions, leaning on their left elbows, with their feet toward the outside. Nine guests in all. The places are carefully arranged, according to importance. The most honoured guest has a special place, at the corner of the middle couch.

[pause]

The walls of the room are painted with beautiful pictures: gardens, and birds, and landscapes, and scenes from the old Greek stories, in bright reds and yellows and blacks. Oil lamps of bronze hang from tall stands, and from the ceiling, and give a soft, warm, flickering light.

[long-pause]

The dinner has three parts.

[pause]

First, the starters, the gustatio: eggs, and olives, and lettuce, and mushrooms, and oysters, and little salted fish, and sausages, with a sweet wine mixed with honey, called mulsum. The Romans have a saying, about a long dinner: from the egg to the apples. Because dinner begins with eggs, and ends with fruit.

[pause]

{img:roman-starters-eggs-olives-oysters-silver-plates}
Then the main courses, the prima mensa. There might be roast pork, or kid, or hare, or chicken, or duck, or fish of many kinds, especially the grey mullet, which the rich Romans love, and pay huge sums for. And on special occasions, there are strange and wonderful dishes, like dormice, rolled in honey and poppy seeds, or a whole roast boar, or a peacock, served with its tail feathers spread out.

[long-pause]

Almost everything is flavoured with a sauce called garum, or liquamen.

[pause]

It is made from small fish, salted and left in the sun for weeks, in great jars, until they turn into a clear, salty, brown liquid. It smells very strong while it is being made, so strong that the garum factories must be outside the towns. But the Romans love it, and put it on everything, as we use salt, or soy sauce. The best garum, from Spain, is very expensive.

[pause]

{img:clay-amphorae-garum-jars-kitchen-shelf-herbs}
The Roman cooks also use a great deal of honey, and pepper, which comes all the way from India, and herbs: lovage, and coriander, and cumin, and mint, and rue. Romans like strong flavours, sweet and sour and salty, all together.

[long-pause]

Last of all, there is the dessert, the secunda mensa: fruit, apples and pears, figs and grapes and dates, and nuts, and little honey cakes, and cheese.

[pause]

There are no tomatoes, or potatoes, or chocolate, or coffee, or tea, or sugar, because these come from lands that the Romans have never heard of, or that are too far away. And there are no forks. The Romans eat with their fingers, and with spoons, and with little knives. Slaves bring bowls of water, and napkins, to wash their hands between the courses. The guests often bring their own napkins, and take home leftovers in them.

[pause]

The wine is always mixed with water, sometimes hot water, in winter. Only barbarians, the Romans think, drink wine without water.

[long-pause]

While they eat, the guests are entertained.

[pause]

There might be a musician, playing the lyre, or the double pipes. Or a slave reading aloud, from a book of poems, or history. Or dancers, from Spain, with castanets. Or a clever slave telling jokes. Or the guests themselves might sing, or recite poetry, or talk.

[pause]

{img:musician-lyre-playing-dinner-guests-listening-soft}
And the conversation is the most important part of all. They talk about philosophy, and literature, and history, and the news of the day, and the gossip of the city. At this time, in the winter of 44, there is only one subject that everyone is talking about. Caesar. What will he do next? Will he make himself king?

[long-pause]

Cicero, who loves dinner parties, and has a famous wit, once has Caesar himself to dinner, at his country house by the sea, in December of the year 45.

[pause]

Caesar arrives with two thousand soldiers, who must all be fed. But the dinner is a great success. Caesar has a bath, and a massage, and eats and drinks with great enjoyment, and they talk about literature, and not about politics at all. Cicero writes to Atticus afterwards that it was a pleasant evening, but that Caesar is not the kind of guest to whom you say: do come again, when you are passing.

[long-pause]

{img:insula-apartment-block-five-storeys-shops-below}
Most of the people of Rome do not live in fine houses with gardens and fountains. They live in apartment blocks, called insulae, which means islands.

[pause]

The insulae are tall buildings, four or five or even six storeys high, built of brick and timber, crowded together along the narrow streets. On the ground floor, facing the street, there are shops and workshops and taverns. Above them, there are the apartments.

[long-pause]

The apartments on the first floor are the best, and the most expensive. They are large, with several rooms, and windows, and sometimes even running water, piped in from the aqueducts.

[pause]

But the higher you go, the smaller and cheaper and worse the apartments become. At the top, under the roof, the poorest people live in tiny, dark rooms, hot in summer and cold in winter, with no water, and no kitchen, and no toilet. They must carry their water up all the stairs from the public fountain in the street, and carry their rubbish down, or, sometimes, simply throw it out of the window.

[pause]

The poet Juvenal, who lived a little later, wrote that if you walk through the streets of Rome at night, you should make your will first, because of all the things that might fall on your head from the windows above.

[long-pause]

The insulae are often badly built, by greedy landlords, who want to make as much money as possible.

[pause]

Sometimes they fall down. And very often, they catch fire, from the braziers and the oil lamps and the cooking fires. Rome has no proper fire brigade at this time. One of the richest men in Rome, Marcus Crassus, who passed away nine years ago, became even richer by buying burning buildings cheaply from their desperate owners, and then sending his own team of slaves to put out the fire.

[pause]

{img:narrow-street-tall-buildings-laundry-lines-light}
But the people of the insulae do not spend much time at home. Their homes are only for sleeping. They live their lives in the streets.

[long-pause]

And the streets of Rome are full of life.

[pause]

There are shops of every kind: butchers and fishmongers, and fruit sellers and vegetable sellers, and wine shops, and potters, and shoemakers, and tailors, and barbers, who shave men in the street, sitting on stools. There are snack bars, with counters opening onto the street, with great clay jars set into them, full of hot food: stews, and soups, and sausages, and spiced wine.

[pause]

There are craftsmen everywhere: blacksmiths, and carpenters, and goldsmiths, and lamp makers, and fullers, who wash and clean clothes, by trampling them in great tubs. Each trade has its own street, or its own neighbourhood. And everywhere, there are street sellers, crying their wares, and beggars, and street performers: jugglers, and acrobats, and snake charmers, and storytellers.

[long-pause]

Most of the food of Rome comes from far away.

[pause]

The city is so large that the farms of Italy cannot feed it. So the grain comes by sea, in great ships, from Egypt, and Sicily, and North Africa, to the port of Ostia, at the mouth of the Tiber, and then up the river to Rome, in barges, pulled by oxen walking along the bank.

[pause]

{img:grain-barges-tiber-oxen-towpath-warehouses}
For many years, the poor citizens of Rome have been given free grain, every month, by the state, so that they will not go hungry, and will not riot. When Caesar came to power, more than three hundred thousand people were receiving the free grain. Caesar counts them carefully, street by street, house by house, and reduces the number to a hundred and fifty thousand, those who really need it.

[long-pause]

Water comes to Rome from the hills, along the aqueducts.

[pause]

At this time, there are four of them, great channels of stone, carrying fresh, clean water for many miles, from springs and rivers in the hills, sometimes underground, sometimes on tall arches across the valleys, sloping very gently all the way, so that the water flows down into the city.

[pause]

{img:aqueduct-arches-campagna-countryside-evening-light}
In the city, the water flows into fountains on the street corners, where the people fill their jars, and into the public baths, and into the houses of the rich, who pay for it. The water never stops flowing, day and night, and the overflow runs away into the great sewer, the Cloaca Maxima, which carries the waste of the city to the Tiber.

[long-pause]

Many of the people of Rome are enslaved.

[pause]

Perhaps one person in every four or five in the city is a slave, owned by another person, and with no rights at all. They come from all over the world, captured in war, or born to enslaved mothers, or sold by pirates. Caesar's wars in Gaul alone brought hundreds of thousands of captives to be sold.

[pause]

Some of them work in the houses of the rich, as cooks and cleaners and nursemaids, and as secretaries and teachers and doctors, because many educated Greeks are enslaved. Some work in the workshops, and on the docks, and in the building trade. Their lives are often hard. But in Rome, many slaves are eventually set free by their owners, and become freedmen, and their children are born Roman citizens.

[long-pause]

And there are the festivals.

[pause]

The Roman year is full of festivals, holy days for the gods, when the people do not work, and there are processions, and sacrifices, and feasts, and games. In December, there is the Saturnalia, the merriest festival of all, when everyone exchanges presents, and the slaves are waited on by their masters, and people wear funny hats, and gamble, and play games, and eat and drink too much.

[pause]

{img:saturnalia-feast-family-gifts-candles-merry-room}
In February, there are festivals for the dead, when families visit the tombs of their ancestors outside the city, and bring them food and flowers. And in the middle of February, there is one of the oldest and strangest festivals of all, the Lupercalia. We shall come back to that one.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-public-baths-entrance-columns-afternoon-crowd}
In the afternoon, the people of Rome go to the baths.

[pause]

There are many public baths in Rome, at this time, perhaps more than a hundred and fifty. Most of them are small, and simple, run by private owners, who charge a small fee, a quarter of an as, the smallest coin, which almost anyone can afford. Children are often allowed in free. The great imperial baths, which were built later, with their huge halls and domes, do not exist yet.

[long-pause]

At the baths, you leave your clothes in a changing room, in little niches in the wall, and perhaps pay a slave to guard them, because thieves are common.

[pause]

Then you go into the exercise yard, the palaestra, and exercise: you lift weights, or throw a ball with your friends, or wrestle, or run, until you are warm and sweaty.

[pause]

Then you go into the bath rooms. First the warm room, the tepidarium, where you sit and relax. Then the hot room, the caldarium, full of steam, heated by hot air flowing under the floor and inside the walls, from a great furnace. There is a hot pool, and a basin of cool water to splash your face.

[long-pause]

The Romans do not use soap. Instead, they rub olive oil on their skin, and then scrape it off, with the sweat and the dirt, with a curved metal tool called a strigil.

[pause]

Then, last of all, you go into the cold room, the frigidarium, and plunge into the cold pool, to close your pores, and wake you up. And then you dress, and go home for dinner, clean and refreshed.

[pause]

{img:caldarium-steam-hot-pool-bathers-soft-light}
But the baths are not just for washing. They are places to meet your friends, and gossip, and do business, and eat snacks bought from the sellers who walk around crying their wares, and get your hair cut, or your arms plucked. The philosopher Seneca, who once lived above a bath house, complained about the noise: the grunting of the weightlifters, the slapping of the masseurs, the shouts of the ball players, the cries of the sausage sellers, and the man who liked to sing in the bath.

[long-pause]

And then there are the games.

[pause]

The Romans love shows of every kind. And on the days of the great festivals, there are games, ludi, paid for by the state, and by the magistrates, who hope to win the favour of the people.

[pause]

{img:circus-maximus-chariots-racing-crowds-sunlit-sand}
The greatest of all are the chariot races, in the Circus Maximus, the great racecourse in the valley between the Palatine and the Aventine hills. Caesar has recently rebuilt and enlarged it, so that it can hold perhaps a hundred and fifty thousand spectators.

[long-pause]

The chariots are light and fast, pulled by four horses, and the drivers wear bright tunics in the colours of their teams: the Reds, and the Whites, and later also the Blues and the Greens.

[pause]

They race seven times around the long, narrow track, around a low barrier in the middle, called the spina, decorated with statues and shrines, and with seven great wooden eggs, and later seven bronze dolphins, which are turned over, one by one, to count the laps. The turns at each end are the most exciting part, because the chariots must turn very sharply, and often collide.

[pause]

{img:lap-counter-eggs-spina-statues-circus-dust}
The crowd roars, and cheers, and shouts, and waves their hands, and bets on their favourite drivers and horses. Romans of every class come to the races: rich and poor, men and women, free and enslaved, all sitting together. The poet Ovid, a little later, writes that the races are a good place for a young man to meet a girl.

[long-pause]

There are also plays, in the theatres, comedies and tragedies, and mimes, and dancers, and musicians.

[pause]

At this time, Rome has only one permanent stone theatre, the great Theatre of Pompey, which was built eleven years ago by Pompey the Great, on the Field of Mars, outside the old city. It is a huge and splendid building, with a great curved hall of seats, and a stage, and behind the stage, a long garden, surrounded by colonnades, full of statues and fountains and shady trees, where people can walk.

[pause]

At the far end of the garden, there is a large hall, where the Senate sometimes meets, because the old Senate house in the Forum burned down some years ago, and is being rebuilt. In the hall, there is a great statue of Pompey himself. Remember this place. We shall come back to it.

[long-pause]

And there are the gladiator shows.

[pause]

At this time, these are not yet held in a great amphitheatre, because the Colosseum will not be built for more than a hundred years. They are held in the Forum, on wooden stands put up for the occasion. Caesar has given magnificent shows, with hundreds of gladiators, all dressed in silver armour.

[pause]

{img:empty-wooden-stands-forum-banners-before-games}
We will not go to watch them tonight. But we will remember that, for the Romans, these shows were among the greatest entertainments of all, and that Caesar, who understood the people of Rome very well, gave them more and grander shows than anyone before him.

[long-pause]

{img:cicero-older-man-writing-letter-study-scrolls}
Of all the people who live in Rome in the year 44 before Christ, there is one whose voice we can still hear today, as clearly as if he were speaking to us. His name is Marcus Tullius Cicero.

[pause]

Cicero is sixty-two years old, in the spring of 44. He is the greatest speaker, and the greatest writer, of his time, and one of the greatest in all of history. And he is a great writer of letters.

[long-pause]

More than nine hundred of his letters have survived, written to his friends, and to his family, and to the great men of his time, and many of the letters they wrote back to him.

[pause]

He writes about politics, and about books, and about his houses and his gardens, and his health, and his money troubles, and his family, and the latest gossip of Rome. He writes when he is happy, and when he is sad, and when he is angry, and when he is afraid. Through his letters, we know more about Cicero than about almost anyone else in the ancient world.

[pause]

His dearest friend, to whom he writes most of all, is Titus Pomponius Atticus, a rich, quiet, wise man, who stays out of politics, and helps everyone, on every side, and is liked by all.

[long-pause]

Cicero was not born into a great Roman family. He comes from a small town in the hills, Arpinum, about seventy miles from Rome, the same town where the great general Marius was born.

[pause]

He came to Rome as a young man, and made his name as a lawyer, by his brilliant speeches in the law courts. He rose, step by step, to the highest office, the consulship, in the year 63 before Christ, the first man of his family ever to do so. In that year, he discovered and stopped a plot against the Republic, led by a man named Catiline, and the Senate called him the father of his country.

[pause]

{img:senate-chamber-orator-standing-senators-listening}
He has never forgotten it. He is very proud, and rather vain, and talks about his great year a great deal, which his friends find a little tiresome. But he is also kind, and warm-hearted, and funny, and loyal, and he loves the Republic, with all his heart.

[long-pause]

Cicero is not happy in the year 44.

[pause]

He took Pompey's side in the civil war, against Caesar. Caesar forgave him, and they are polite to each other, and even friendly. Caesar admires Cicero's writing very much. But Cicero has no power any more. The Senate does whatever Caesar wants, and Cicero feels that the Republic, which he loved, is gone.

[pause]

And there has been a great sadness in his life. His beloved daughter, Tullia, passed away a year ago, in February of 45, after the birth of a baby son, in her early thirties. Cicero is heartbroken. For months, he can hardly bear to see anyone. He goes away to his country houses, and walks alone in the woods, and weeps.

[long-pause]

To comfort himself, he writes.

[pause]

In the last two years, since Tullia's death, he has written an extraordinary number of books: about philosophy, about the gods, about old age, about friendship, about duty, about how to live a good life. He wants to bring the wisdom of the Greek philosophers to the Romans, in the Latin language.

[pause]

{img:cicero-walking-garden-colonnade-scroll-thinking}
His books will be read for the next two thousand years, in every school in Europe. And in one of them, On Old Age, he writes that a man who has lived well can face old age, and even death, calmly, because life is like a play, and the wise man does not mind when the last act comes.

[long-pause]

Cicero has many country houses, which he loves.

[pause]

There is his favourite, at Tusculum, in the hills just southeast of Rome, with its gardens, and its library, and its two covered walks, where he likes to talk philosophy with his friends. There is a house at Formiae, by the sea, and another at Pompeii, and others too. He spends a lot of money on them, and on Greek statues to decorate them, and he is always in debt.

[pause]

{img:roman-villa-library-shelves-scroll-cases-sunlight}
He loves books more than anything. He writes to Atticus that a room without books is like a body without a soul. And that if you have a garden and a library, you have everything you need.

[long-pause]

Cicero has a son, also called Marcus, who is about twenty, and is studying philosophy in Athens, at Cicero's expense. Young Marcus seems to prefer drinking and parties to philosophy, and Cicero worries about him.

[pause]

He also has a brother, Quintus, and a nephew. And he has a loyal secretary, Tiro, a clever, gentle man, who was once enslaved in his household, and whom Cicero has set free. Tiro is often ill, and Cicero writes him affectionate letters, telling him to take care of himself, and get well soon.

[pause]

Tiro has invented a system of shorthand, of quick signs, so that he can write down Cicero's speeches as fast as he speaks them. Some of these signs are still used today. And after Cicero's death, Tiro will collect his letters, and keep them safe, so that we can still read them.

[long-pause]

In the early months of 44, Cicero spends much of his time away from Rome, in his country houses, writing.

[pause]

He watches Caesar, and the city, from a distance, with sadness. He writes to Atticus that he does not know what will happen. Nobody does.

[pause]

{img:cicero-at-window-evening-looking-toward-city}
He does not know that, in a few weeks, everything will change. And he does not know that some of the men he knows best are already meeting in secret, talking quietly, in the evenings, about the Republic, and about Caesar.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-bookshop-scroll-shelves-arcade-customers}
Rome, in the year 44, is a city of books.

[pause]

Not books like ours, with pages, but scrolls: long rolls of papyrus, a kind of paper, made in Egypt, from the stems of the papyrus reed, which grows along the Nile. The text is written in columns, side by side, along the scroll, and you unroll it with one hand, and roll it up with the other, as you read.

[long-pause]

There are bookshops in Rome, along the streets near the Forum, where you can buy the latest books: poems, and histories, and speeches, and plays, and books about farming, and cooking, and medicine.

[pause]

The books are copied by hand, by teams of educated slaves, sitting in rows, writing as someone reads the text aloud to them. A popular book might be copied hundreds of times. Atticus, Cicero's friend, has a great team of copyists, and publishes Cicero's books, and sends copies to his friends, and sells them.

[pause]

Rich Romans have libraries in their houses, with shelves full of scrolls, each scroll with a little label hanging from it, giving the title. Cicero, as we have heard, loves his libraries more than anything.

[long-pause]

Roman children go to school when they are about seven years old, if their parents can afford it.

[pause]

The school is usually a single room, or a corner of an arcade, opening onto the street, behind a curtain. The teacher, the ludi magister, is often a Greek freedman, poorly paid, and rather strict. Lessons begin at dawn, and go on until midday.

[pause]

{img:roman-school-arcade-children-wax-tablets-teacher}
The children learn to read and write, in Latin and in Greek, by copying letters onto little wooden tablets, covered with wax, with a pointed stylus. The other end of the stylus is flat, for rubbing out mistakes. They learn to count, using their fingers, and little pebbles, and the abacus. They learn long passages of poetry by heart, chanting them together, in loud voices.

[long-pause]

Girls often go to the first school too, with their brothers, and learn to read and write. But most of them stop going to school at about twelve, and learn to run a household, and spin and weave, at home. Some girls from rich families are educated at home, by tutors, and become very learned.

[pause]

The boys of rich families go on, to the school of the grammaticus, where they read the great Greek and Latin poets, Homer, above all, and learn about history, and mythology, and geography, and astronomy.

[pause]

{img:grammaticus-teaching-boys-homer-scroll-courtyard}
And then, at about sixteen, they go on to the school of the rhetor, the teacher of speaking. There, they learn how to make speeches, which is the most important skill for a young Roman who wants to be a lawyer, or a politician. They practise making speeches about imaginary cases, and famous questions from history.

[long-pause]

The best students, like Cicero's son, and Caesar's great-nephew Octavius, go on to study in Greece: in Athens, or in Rhodes, or in Apollonia, with the most famous teachers of philosophy and rhetoric.

[pause]

Caesar himself studied in Rhodes, as a young man, with the great teacher Apollonius Molon, who also taught Cicero. It was on his way there that he was captured by the pirates.

[pause]

There are also many learned men in Rome itself: Greek philosophers and scholars, doctors and astronomers and architects, who come to Rome to teach, and to work for the great Roman families. Caesar has given Roman citizenship to every doctor and every teacher of the liberal arts who lives in Rome, so that more of them will come.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, in the houses of the rich, people read aloud to each other.

[pause]

The Romans almost always read aloud, even when they are alone, murmuring the words. Silent reading is so unusual that people remark on it when they see it.

[pause]

{img:evening-reading-aloud-family-oil-lamp-scroll-quiet}
And in the year 44, the books that everyone is reading include Caesar's own books, his commentaries on the war in Gaul, written in the third person, as if about someone else, in that clear, plain style, which Cicero himself admires. The book begins with words that schoolchildren still learn today: Gaul, as a whole, is divided into three parts.

[long-pause]

{img:domus-publica-house-forum-temple-vesta-round}
Where does Caesar live?

[pause]

As Pontifex Maximus, the chief priest of Rome, Caesar lives in an official house, the Domus Publica, the public house, in the heart of the Forum, on the Sacred Way, the oldest street in Rome. Next to it, there is the little round Temple of Vesta, the goddess of the hearth, and the House of the Vestal Virgins, her priestesses.

[long-pause]

The Vestal Virgins are six women, chosen as little girls, from noble families, who serve the goddess Vesta for thirty years.

[pause]

Their most important duty is to keep the sacred fire burning in the round temple, day and night, without ever letting it go out. The fire is the hearth of the whole Roman people. As long as it burns, Rome is safe. If it ever goes out, it is a terrible sign.

[pause]

The Vestals are greatly respected. They have their own seats of honour at the games, and when they walk through the streets, they are preceded by an attendant, carrying the rods of authority, and everyone makes way for them. Important people often leave their wills with the Vestals, for safekeeping. Caesar has left his will with them.

[long-pause]

Caesar's wife is called Calpurnia.

[pause]

She is about thirty years old, the daughter of an important senator, Lucius Calpurnius Piso. She has been married to Caesar for fifteen years. It is his third marriage. His first wife, Cornelia, whom he loved very much, passed away many years ago, after giving him his only legitimate child, a daughter, Julia. And Julia, who married Pompey, passed away too, ten years ago, in childbirth, to Caesar's great grief.

[pause]

{img:calpurnia-roman-matron-garden-quiet-morning}
Calpurnia and Caesar have no children. For much of their marriage, Caesar has been far away, fighting in Gaul, and in the civil war. We do not know much about her. But the stories say that she is a gentle, loyal, and devoted wife, and that she loves her husband, and worries about him.

[long-pause]

Caesar is a very hard worker.

[pause]

He gets up early, and works all day, and late into the night. He reads letters and writes them, sometimes dictating several letters at once, to several secretaries, while he reads another. He sees visitors, and makes decisions, and gives orders, all day long. When he travels, he reads and dictates in his carriage, or in his litter.

[pause]

{img:caesar-dictating-letters-secretaries-busy-room}
He eats and drinks very little. He is not fond of fancy food. Once, when he is a guest at a dinner, and the host serves asparagus dressed with the wrong kind of oil, perfume instead of olive oil, and the other guests complain, Caesar eats it without a word, and scolds them, afterwards, for embarrassing their host.

[long-pause]

His health is not perfect. He is subject to fainting fits, and sometimes to seizures, which the Romans call the falling sickness. He also suffers from headaches.

[pause]

But he is very strong, and tough, and can endure any hardship. In his wars, he marched with his soldiers, bareheaded, in the sun and the rain, and swam rivers, and rode for days without rest. He is a superb horseman. It is said that, as a young man, he could ride at full gallop with his hands clasped behind his back.

[pause]

But now, in the year 44, he is fifty-five, and tired. He has been fighting, almost without a break, for fourteen years. Some of his friends think that he no longer cares very much whether he lives or dies.

[long-pause]

Caesar has a great house and gardens across the river, too, on the far bank of the Tiber, on the slopes of the Janiculum hill. They are called the Gardens of Caesar.

[pause]

They are beautiful gardens, with walks, and trees, and fountains, and statues, and a fine villa, looking across the river toward the city. And in this villa, in the year 44, there is a very special guest.

[pause]

{img:gardens-of-caesar-across-tiber-villa-view-city}
It is the queen of Egypt, Cleopatra. She has come to Rome with her little son, and with her court, and she is living in Caesar's villa, across the river.

[long-pause]

{img:small-street-shrine-crossroads-lares-flowers-lamp}
The Romans live surrounded by gods.

[pause]

There are the great gods, of course: Jupiter, the king of the gods, and his wife Juno, and Minerva, the goddess of wisdom, who share the great temple on the Capitol. There is Mars, the god of war, and Venus, the goddess of love, and Apollo, and Mercury, and Neptune, and Ceres, the goddess of the harvest, and many more.

[long-pause]

But there are also thousands of little gods, and spirits, everywhere.

[pause]

There is a god of the doorway, and a goddess of the hinges of the door, and a god of the threshold. There are gods of the fields, and of the boundary stones, and of the springs and the rivers and the woods. Every house has its own household gods, the Lares and the Penates, who protect the family, and the store cupboard. And every man has his own guardian spirit, his genius, and every woman hers, her juno.

[pause]

{img:family-offering-household-shrine-incense-bread-wine}
At every crossroads in the city, there is a little shrine to the Lares of the crossroads, where the people of the neighbourhood leave offerings, and celebrate a festival together every winter.

[long-pause]

The Romans do not pray to their gods quite as we might imagine.

[pause]

For them, religion is a kind of agreement. You give the gods what is due to them: sacrifices, and offerings, and prayers, and festivals, all done exactly right, with the right words, and the right gestures, in the right order. And in return, the gods protect you, and your family, and your city. If you make a mistake in the words of a prayer, you must start all over again.

[pause]

When a Roman prays, or offers a sacrifice, he covers his head with a fold of his toga, so that he will not see or hear anything unlucky, which would spoil the ceremony.

[long-pause]

In Rome, there are hundreds of temples.

[pause]

A Roman temple is the house of a god. Inside, in a single room, there is the statue of the god, often very large, and beautifully made, of marble or bronze, or even gold and ivory. The people do not usually go inside to worship. The altar is outside, in front of the temple, at the foot of the steps, and the ceremonies take place there, in the open air, where everyone can see.

[pause]

{img:temple-interior-cella-large-statue-goddess-dim-light}
The temples are also used as museums, full of works of art, and gifts to the gods, and as treasuries, and as meeting places. The state treasury of Rome is kept in the basement of the Temple of Saturn, in the Forum.

[long-pause]

And the Romans are open to new gods, from all over the world.

[pause]

There are temples in Rome to the Great Mother, from Asia Minor, and to Isis, the goddess from Egypt, who is becoming very popular, especially among women. The Jews of Rome worship their one God, in their own meeting houses. And many educated Romans read the Greek philosophers, and wonder whether the old gods are real at all, or only stories.

[pause]

{img:quiet-temple-steps-evening-dove-columns-warm-light}
Caesar himself, as Pontifex Maximus, is in charge of the whole religion of the Roman state. But he is said not to have been very religious himself. He does what the old customs require, and leaves the rest to the gods.

[long-pause]

{img:cleopatra-young-queen-egyptian-greek-dress-calm}
Cleopatra is about twenty-five years old, in the year 44 before Christ.

[pause]

She is the queen of Egypt, the seventh queen of that name, of the family of the Ptolemies, who are not Egyptians, but Greeks, descended from one of the generals of Alexander the Great, who took Egypt as his kingdom three hundred years ago. For three hundred years, the Ptolemies have ruled Egypt, from their great city of Alexandria, by the sea.

[long-pause]

Cleopatra is very clever. She speaks many languages, perhaps nine or ten. She is the first of all the Ptolemies who has bothered to learn the Egyptian language, the language of her own people. She has studied mathematics, and philosophy, and medicine, and astronomy.

[pause]

The Greek writer Plutarch, who lived a hundred and fifty years later, writes that her beauty was not, in itself, so remarkable. But that her conversation was irresistible, and that her voice was like an instrument of many strings, and that there was a charm in her presence, and in everything she said and did, that no one could resist.

[pause]

Her city, Alexandria, is the second greatest city in the world, after Rome, and in many ways more beautiful and more learned. It has the great lighthouse, the Pharos, one of the Seven Wonders of the World, and the great Library, with hundreds of thousands of books, and the tomb of Alexander the Great himself.

[long-pause]

Cleopatra met Caesar four years ago, in Alexandria, when she was twenty-one.

[pause]

She was at war with her younger brother, who was also her husband, as was the custom of the Ptolemies, for the throne of Egypt. Her brother's advisers had driven her out of Alexandria. And when Caesar came, she wanted to see him, to ask for his help. But her brother's soldiers were guarding the palace.

[pause]

{img:small-boat-evening-harbour-palace-steps-alexandria}
So, the story goes, she had herself carried into the palace, at night, by a loyal friend, rolled up in a bedroll, or a carpet, and unrolled in front of Caesar. Caesar, it is said, was charmed by her courage and her cleverness. He took her side, and after a short war, she became queen of Egypt.

[long-pause]

Afterwards, Caesar stayed in Egypt with Cleopatra for several months.

[pause]

The stories say that they sailed together up the river Nile, in a great royal barge, past the temples and the pyramids and the green fields along the river, as far as the borders of Ethiopia. Some historians think that this journey was much shorter, or never happened at all. But it is a beautiful story.

[pause]

And in the summer after Caesar left Egypt, Cleopatra gave birth to a son. She named him Ptolemy Caesar. But the people of Alexandria called him Caesarion, which means little Caesar.

[long-pause]

In the year 46, Cleopatra came to Rome, with Caesarion, and her court, and her younger brother, who was now her husband and co-ruler.

[pause]

Caesar gave them his villa across the Tiber, in his gardens, to live in. And there she has stayed, on and off, ever since.

[pause]

{img:villa-terrace-queen-small-boy-servants-roman-garden}
Many Romans do not like her. They do not like foreigners, and they especially do not like kings and queens. They think she is proud, and arrogant. Cicero visits her, and writes to Atticus that he hates the queen, although he does not explain why, except that she once promised him some books, and did not send them, and that she was arrogant to him, once, in her gardens.

[long-pause]

Caesar has put a golden statue of Cleopatra in the new temple that he has built, in his new Forum, the Temple of Venus Genetrix, Venus the Mother, the goddess from whom the Julian family is descended.

[pause]

This shocks many Romans. A statue of a living foreign queen, beside the statue of the goddess, in a Roman temple. People whisper. They whisper that Caesar wants to marry Cleopatra, and make himself king, and move the capital of the empire from Rome to Alexandria. None of these things are true, as far as we know. But the whispers go on.

[pause]

Caesarion, the little boy, is now about three years old. Caesar does not acknowledge him in public as his son, and he has not named him in his will. But everyone knows.

[long-pause]

What is it like, in Cleopatra's villa, across the river?

[pause]

We can imagine it: the queen's household, of Greek and Egyptian servants and courtiers and scholars, speaking Greek, the language of the court. Egyptian furniture and ornaments, and perfumes, and fine linen. Visitors coming and going: Roman senators, and scholars, and astronomers, and poets, all eager to meet the famous queen.

[pause]

{img:villa-interior-egyptian-greek-furnishings-lamps-evening}
Some historians believe that Cleopatra's astronomers, from Alexandria, helped Caesar with one of his greatest achievements: a new calendar.

[long-pause]

{img:astronomers-sundial-scrolls-calendar-tablets-courtyard}
Among all the things that Caesar did, there is one that we still use, every single day, more than two thousand years later. It is the calendar.

[pause]

Before Caesar, the Roman calendar was a terrible muddle.

[long-pause]

The old Roman year had only three hundred and fifty-five days. That is ten days shorter than the real year, the time it takes the earth to go around the sun.

[pause]

To keep the calendar in step with the seasons, the priests were supposed to add an extra month, every two years or so. But the priests were also politicians. And they added the extra month, or did not, as it suited them and their friends: to make a year longer, when a friend was in office, or shorter, when an enemy was.

[pause]

And during the civil wars, the extra months were forgotten altogether. So, by the time Caesar came to power, the calendar was nearly three months out of step with the seasons. The festivals of the harvest came before the crops were ripe. The festivals of spring came in the middle of winter.

[long-pause]

So Caesar decides to fix it.

[pause]

With the help of an astronomer from Alexandria, named Sosigenes, he makes a new calendar, based on the sun. The year will have three hundred and sixty-five days, in twelve months, with one extra day added every four years, in February, to make up for the quarter day left over. A leap year.

[pause]

{img:sosigenes-astronomer-instruments-night-sky-roof}
It is the calendar that we still use today, almost unchanged. Sixteen hundred years later, a pope, Gregory the Thirteenth, will make a small correction to it, because Caesar's year was still a few minutes too long. But the calendar on your wall is still, essentially, Caesar's calendar.

[long-pause]

To bring the calendar back into step with the seasons, Caesar makes the year 46 before Christ very long indeed.

[pause]

He adds two extra months to it, between November and December, as well as the usual extra month in February. So that year has four hundred and forty-five days. The Romans call it the year of confusion. And on the first of January, 45 before Christ, the new calendar begins.

[pause]

Not everyone is pleased. Cicero makes a joke. When someone tells him that the constellation Lyra will rise tomorrow, Cicero says: yes, by decree. As if even the stars must now obey Caesar.

[long-pause]

And Caesar has many more plans.

[pause]

He plans to build a great new temple to Mars, the god of war, the greatest temple in the world. He plans to build a huge new theatre, at the foot of the Capitoline hill. He plans to build the first public library in Rome, full of Greek and Latin books, and he has asked the great scholar Varro to collect the books for it.

[pause]

{img:architects-plans-model-temple-table-workshop}
He plans to drain the great marshes south of Rome, to make new farmland, and to dig a canal through the narrow neck of land at Corinth, in Greece, to save ships the long voyage around the south of Greece. He plans to write down all the laws of Rome, which are scattered and confused, in a single collection. He plans to divert the river Tiber, to make room for the city to grow.

[long-pause]

Many of these plans will be carried out later, by other men. Some of them will never be carried out at all.

[pause]

The Corinth canal, for instance, will not be built for almost two thousand years, until 1893.

[pause]

{img:forum-of-caesar-new-paving-colonnades-equestrian-statue}
But the new Forum of Caesar is already finished, and the people walk in it every day: a long open square, surrounded by colonnades, with shops along the sides, and at one end, the beautiful Temple of Venus Genetrix, and in front of it, a bronze statue of Caesar, on his favourite horse, a horse with strange, almost human feet, like toes, which nobody else was ever allowed to ride.

[long-pause]

And there is one more plan, the greatest of all.

[pause]

Caesar is going to war again. He is going east, to fight against the Parthians, the great empire beyond the river Euphrates, in what is now Iran and Iraq. Nine years ago, the Parthians defeated a Roman army, under Marcus Crassus, the richest man in Rome, who lost his life in the battle, and they captured the eagles, the sacred standards of the legions. Caesar wants to win them back.

[pause]

Sixteen legions are already gathering, in Greece and across the sea, waiting for him. Caesar plans to leave Rome on the eighteenth of March, in the year 44. He expects to be gone for three years.

[long-pause]

Some of the senators are even more alarmed by this.

[pause]

If Caesar conquers Parthia, as well as Gaul, he will be the greatest conqueror in Roman history, greater than Pompey, greater even than Alexander the Great. And he will come back more powerful than ever.

[pause]

{img:senators-talking-quietly-colonnade-shadows-evening}
And there is a rumour, which goes around the city, that an old book of prophecies, the Sibylline books, says that the Parthians can only be conquered by a king. And that at the next meeting of the Senate, someone is going to propose that Caesar should be called king, everywhere outside Italy.

[long-pause]

{img:marcus-brutus-serious-roman-reading-philosophy-lamp}
Among the senators of Rome, in the year 44, there is a quiet, serious man of about forty, named Marcus Junius Brutus.

[pause]

Brutus is a thoughtful man, a lover of philosophy, especially the philosophy of Plato. He reads a great deal, and writes books, and is respected by everyone for his honesty, and his seriousness, and his learning. He is not a great soldier, or a great speaker. But people trust him.

[long-pause]

Brutus has a famous ancestor. Or at least, he believes he does.

[pause]

More than four hundred and fifty years ago, a man named Lucius Junius Brutus led the people of Rome in driving out their last king, Tarquin the Proud, and founded the Republic. Marcus Brutus believes that he is descended from him. And on the Capitol, there is an old statue of that first Brutus, with a drawn sword in his hand.

[pause]

Brutus's mother, Servilia, is a remarkable woman, clever and strong and well connected, who was, for many years, the woman Caesar loved most. There were even rumours, in Rome, that Brutus might be Caesar's own son, although Caesar was only fifteen when Brutus was born, so it is very unlikely.

[long-pause]

In the civil war, Brutus fought on Pompey's side, against Caesar.

[pause]

But after the battle of Pharsalus, Caesar, who had given orders that Brutus should not be harmed in the battle, forgave him at once, and welcomed him, and since then has given him one honour after another. In the year 44, Brutus is praetor, one of the chief judges of Rome, the second highest office. Caesar has promised him the consulship in a few years.

[pause]

{img:praetor-tribunal-seat-forum-citizens-cases-morning}
Caesar is fond of Brutus, and trusts him. He is said to have remarked that whatever Brutus wants, he wants very much.

[long-pause]

Brutus is married to Porcia, the daughter of Marcus Porcius Cato, who was the sternest and most stubborn of all Caesar's enemies, and who took his own life two years ago in Africa, rather than accept Caesar's forgiveness.

[pause]

Porcia is as brave and as proud as her father. The story is told that, when she sees that Brutus is troubled by some great secret, which he will not tell her, she wounds herself in the leg, with a little knife, to show him that she is strong enough to keep any secret, and to bear any pain. And then he tells her everything.

[pause]

And the walls of Rome begin to speak to Brutus too. Someone writes messages, at night, on the statue of the old Brutus: if only you were alive now. And on the chair where Marcus Brutus sits as judge, in the Forum, someone leaves little notes, saying: Brutus, are you asleep? You are not really Brutus.

[long-pause]

The man who first brings Brutus into the plot is his brother-in-law, Gaius Cassius Longinus.

[pause]

Cassius is very different from Brutus. He is a soldier, hot-tempered, sharp, and proud. Nine years ago, he served with Crassus in the war against the Parthians, and was one of the few who escaped from the disaster, and saved what was left of the army. He is married to Brutus's half-sister, Junia Tertia.

[pause]

{img:cassius-lean-roman-soldier-senator-intense-gaze}
Cassius, like Brutus, fought against Caesar in the civil war, and was forgiven. He too is praetor this year. But he is bitter, because Caesar gave the most important praetorship to Brutus, not to him. And he hates the idea of one man ruling Rome.

[long-pause]

Cassius talks to Brutus, quietly, in the early weeks of the year 44.

[pause]

He tells him that the Republic is dying, and that only they can save it. That the people of Rome expect it of Brutus, because of his name, and his ancestor. And that if Caesar is made king, it will be too late.

[pause]

{img:two-men-walking-garden-path-dusk-quiet-talk}
And Brutus, after much thought, and many sleepless nights, agrees.

[long-pause]

Others join them. In the end, more than sixty senators are part of the plot.

[pause]

Some of them are old enemies of Caesar, who fought against him, and were forgiven. But some of them are his own friends, and his own officers, who fought beside him in Gaul, and whom he has rewarded with great honours. Among them is Decimus Junius Brutus, a distant cousin of Marcus Brutus, one of Caesar's most trusted generals, whom Caesar has named in his will as one of his heirs, if his first heirs should not live. And Gaius Trebonius, who served under Caesar in Gaul for years, and whom Caesar made consul.

[pause]

They have many reasons. Some believe truly in the Republic, and in liberty. Some are jealous, or ambitious, or bitter. Some think that Caesar has not rewarded them enough. Some simply do not want to be ruled by anyone.

[long-pause]

They meet secretly, in small groups, in each other's houses, and talk, in low voices.

[pause]

They do not ask Cicero to join them. He is too old, they think, and too talkative, and too cautious. They want to act quickly, before Caesar leaves for the East.

[pause]

{img:city-rooftops-night-few-lit-windows-stars-quiet}
And they choose a day. The Ides of March. The fifteenth of March. Three days before Caesar is due to leave Rome. On that day, the Senate will meet, in the hall of the Theatre of Pompey.

[long-pause]

{img:palatine-hill-cave-lupercal-fig-tree-february}
On the fifteenth of February, in the year 44, Rome celebrates the Lupercalia.

[pause]

It is one of the oldest festivals in Rome, so old that even the Romans are not quite sure what it means. It is held at the Lupercal, a cave at the foot of the Palatine hill, where, according to the legend, a she-wolf found the twins Romulus and Remus, the founders of Rome, abandoned as babies, and fed them with her milk.

[long-pause]

At the Lupercalia, the priests, called the Luperci, who are young men of good families, gather at the cave, and offer the old sacrifices to the gods.

[pause]

Then the young men, wearing only a little goatskin around their waists, run through the streets of the city, around the Palatine hill, and along the Sacred Way through the Forum, laughing, and carrying strips of goatskin, with which they strike, lightly, everyone they meet.

[pause]

Young women stand along the streets, and hold out their hands to be touched by the goatskin strips. They believe that it will bring them good luck, and help them to have children. It is a cheerful, noisy, rather rowdy festival, and the whole city comes out to watch.

[long-pause]

This year, there is a new group of runners, the Julian Luperci, named in honour of Caesar. And their leader is the consul himself, Mark Antony.

[pause]

Mark Antony is thirty-nine years old, a big, strong, handsome man, with a thick neck and a curly beard, who likes to think that he looks like the hero Hercules. He is a fine soldier, and a brave one, and Caesar's most loyal follower. But he is also a lover of feasts and wine and actresses, and his behaviour sometimes shocks the more serious Romans.

[pause]

{img:mark-antony-strong-roman-cheerful-festival-crowd}
Caesar is sitting in the Forum, on the speakers' platform, the Rostra, on a golden chair, dressed in a purple robe, the robe of the ancient kings of Rome, which the Senate has given him the right to wear, watching the festival.

[long-pause]

Antony comes running into the Forum, with the other runners, and the crowd parts for him.

[pause]

He comes to the Rostra, and climbs up, and holds out to Caesar a diadem, a white band of cloth, wrapped around a wreath of laurel. A diadem is the sign of a king.

[pause]

There is a little applause, from a few people in the crowd, who have perhaps been placed there for the purpose. But most of the crowd is silent.

[pause]

Caesar pushes the diadem away.

[pause]

And the whole crowd cheers, loudly.

[long-pause]

Antony offers the diadem again. Again, a little applause, from a few. Again, Caesar refuses it. And again, the crowd roars its approval.

[pause]

Then Caesar stands up, and orders that the diadem should be taken up to the Capitol, to the Temple of Jupiter, because Jupiter alone, he says, is king of the Romans. And he orders it to be written in the official records that, on this day, the consul Mark Antony offered Caesar the crown, at the wish of the people, and that Caesar refused it.

[pause]

{img:temple-steps-capitol-diadem-carried-crowd-below}
What does it mean? Did Caesar want to be king, and was he testing the crowd, to see what they would think? Or did he want to show everyone, once and for all, that he did not want to be king? Nobody knew then, and nobody knows today.

[long-pause]

But the plotters take note.

[pause]

The crowd cheered when Caesar refused the crown. The people of Rome do not want a king either, they think. When Caesar is gone, the people will be on our side.

[pause]

Meanwhile, the city whispers. Some time before, someone put a diadem on one of Caesar's statues, and two of the tribunes of the people had it removed, and arrested the men who had hailed Caesar as king in the street. Caesar was angry, and had the tribunes removed from office. He said that they had made it look as if he wanted to be king, when he did not.

[long-pause]

The weeks pass. February turns to March.

[pause]

Caesar goes on working, getting ready for the war in the East. He appoints the magistrates for the next few years, because he will be away. He has already sent his young great-nephew, Gaius Octavius, who is eighteen, across the sea to Apollonia, in what is now Albania, to study, and to wait with the legions, so that he can join Caesar on the campaign.

[pause]

{img:spring-roman-countryside-almond-blossom-march}
In the country around Rome, the almond trees are in blossom, and the fields are green, and the swallows come back from Africa. Spring is coming.

[long-pause]

{img:haruspex-old-etruscan-priest-temple-steps-robes}
The Romans believe in signs and omens.

[pause]

They believe that the gods tell people what is going to happen, through signs: the flight of birds, and thunder and lightning, and dreams, and strange happenings in nature. And they have special priests, the augurs and the haruspices, whose work is to read these signs, and tell what they mean.

[long-pause]

In the weeks before the Ides of March, the ancient writers tell us, there are many strange signs.

[pause]

Of course, the writers lived many years later, and wrote these stories down long after the event, and some of them may have been invented, or improved, afterwards. But they are part of the story, and the Romans believed them.

[pause]

They say that there were strange lights in the sky, and noises in the night. That lonely birds came down into the Forum. That the horses that Caesar had dedicated to the gods, when he crossed the Rubicon, and set free to graze by the river, refused to eat, and wept.

[long-pause]

And they say that an old soothsayer, a haruspex, named Spurinna, warned Caesar, while he was offering a sacrifice, that he should beware of a great danger, which would come no later than the Ides of March.

[pause]

Caesar, who does not believe very much in omens, takes no notice.

[pause]

{img:caesar-passing-soothsayer-street-crowd-sunlight}
Caesar's friends are worried about him. They have heard rumours. They beg him to take a bodyguard. For some time, he has had a guard of Spanish soldiers, who went with him everywhere. But he has sent them away. He says that it is better to die once than to live always in fear. He says that his safety is more important to Rome than to himself. If anything happens to him, Rome will have no peace, and will fall into civil war again.

[long-pause]

On the evening of the fourteenth of March, the day before the Ides, Caesar goes to dinner at the house of his friend Marcus Aemilius Lepidus, his master of horse, his second in command.

[pause]

It is a pleasant evening, with friends. While the others talk, Caesar reclines on his couch, and signs letters, as he always does.

[pause]

And during the dinner, the conversation turns, as conversations often do among the Romans, who are very interested in philosophy, to a serious question: what is the best kind of death?

[pause]

And Caesar looks up from his letters, and says, before anyone else can answer: a sudden one. One that is unexpected.

[long-pause]

That night, in the Domus Publica, in the Forum, Caesar and Calpurnia sleep badly.

[pause]

The ancient writers say that, in the night, all the doors and windows of their bedroom fly open at once, with a great noise, and wake Caesar up. And in the moonlight, he sees Calpurnia, asleep beside him, moaning and murmuring words he cannot understand.

[pause]

{img:roman-bedroom-night-shutters-open-moonlight-curtains}
She is dreaming. In her dream, she tells him afterwards, she held him in her arms, and wept for him.

[long-pause]

In the morning, the fifteenth of March, Calpurnia begs Caesar not to go to the Senate.

[pause]

She has never been superstitious, or anxious, before. But now she is frightened, and pleads with him to stay at home, and to put off the meeting of the Senate until another day.

[pause]

{img:calpurnia-pleading-caesar-atrium-morning-grey-light}
And Caesar, who has also not been feeling well, and has perhaps had a dizzy spell that morning, is moved by her fear. He orders the priests to make sacrifices, and to see what the signs say. And the signs, the priests tell him, are bad.

[pause]

So Caesar decides to stay at home. He will send Mark Antony to the Senate, to tell them that the meeting is put off.

[long-pause]

But then Decimus Brutus arrives at the house.

[pause]

Decimus is one of the plotters. And he knows that, if the meeting is put off, the plot may be discovered. So he speaks to Caesar, cheerfully, and laughs at the omens. He says that the senators are all waiting for him, ready to vote him great honours. He asks what people would say, if Caesar told the Senate to go home, and come back another day, when Calpurnia had better dreams.

[pause]

He says that, if Caesar really thinks the day is unlucky, he should at least go to the Senate himself, and tell them so, in person.

[pause]

And he takes Caesar by the hand, and leads him out of the house.

[long-pause]

It is about the fifth hour of the morning, about eleven o'clock, by our time.

[pause]

Caesar is carried through the streets, in his litter, from the Forum, toward the Field of Mars, and the Theatre of Pompey. As always, the crowds gather around him, and people push forward, to give him letters and petitions.

[pause]

{img:litter-carried-through-crowded-street-petitioners}
One of them, the story says, is a Greek teacher named Artemidorus, who has somehow found out about the plot. He pushes his way through the crowd, and gives Caesar a little scroll, and says: Read this, Caesar, by yourself, and quickly. It concerns you closely.

[pause]

Caesar takes it, and holds it in his hand. But so many people are pressing round him, that he never has a chance to read it.

[long-pause]

As he passes the old soothsayer, Spurinna, Caesar calls out to him, with a smile: the Ides of March have come.

[pause]

And Spurinna answers, quietly: yes, they have come. But they have not yet gone.

[long-pause]

{img:portico-pompey-garden-senators-gathering-morning}
At the Theatre of Pompey, the senators have been waiting all morning.

[pause]

The plotters are there, early, with their togas wrapped around them. Brutus and Cassius are there, doing their work as judges, in the colonnade of the gardens, listening to cases, calmly, as if it were an ordinary day.

[long-pause]

But it has been a long and anxious morning for them.

[pause]

A message comes to Brutus that his wife, Porcia, who knows everything, has fainted at home from the strain, and may be dying. Brutus is deeply troubled, but he stays. Porcia, in fact, recovers soon after.

[pause]

A senator, who is not part of the plot, comes up to Brutus and Cassius, and says to them quietly: I wish you success in what you have in mind. But be quick. Everyone is talking about it. And then he walks away, leaving them wondering how much he knows.

[pause]

And when Caesar's litter arrives, at last, another senator goes up to Caesar, and talks with him earnestly, for a long time. The plotters watch, and wonder, and fear that they have been betrayed. But at the end, the senator kisses Caesar's hand, and goes away. He was only asking a favour.

[long-pause]

Before Caesar goes into the hall, one of the plotters, Trebonius, takes Mark Antony aside, outside the door, and keeps him talking there.

[pause]

The plotters had decided not to harm Antony. Brutus insisted on it. It must not look like a quarrel between parties, he said. It must be an act of justice, against one man only, the man who had made himself master of Rome. They would harm no one else.

[pause]

{img:hall-entrance-columns-two-men-talking-outside}
So Caesar goes into the hall alone.

[long-pause]

The senators rise from their seats, as he comes in, as a mark of respect. And he goes to his golden chair, at the far end of the hall, and sits down.

[pause]

Behind him, there is the great statue of Pompey the Great, his old friend, and his son-in-law, and his rival, whom he defeated, looking down.

[pause]

Then the plotters gather around Caesar, as if to greet him, and to ask him favours, as senators always do.

[long-pause]

One of them, Tillius Cimber, comes forward with a petition, asking Caesar to let his brother, who is in exile, come home. Caesar says that he will consider it another time. Cimber takes hold of Caesar's toga, as if pleading with him, and pulls it from his shoulders.

[pause]

That is the signal.

[pause]

{img:distant-view-columns-hall-doorway-figures-crowding}
And there, in the hall of Pompey, at the foot of Pompey's statue, the senators surround Caesar, and strike him down.

[long-pause]

It is over in a few moments.

[pause]

The ancient writers say that, at first, Caesar tries to defend himself. But when he sees Brutus among the men around him, he stops, and draws his toga over his face, and lets himself fall.

[pause]

Some people say that, as he saw Brutus, Caesar spoke in Greek, the language of the educated Romans: kai su, teknon. You too, my child? Others say that he did not speak at all. The famous words, Et tu, Brute, were written more than sixteen hundred years later, by William Shakespeare, for his play.

[long-pause]

Gaius Julius Caesar, the greatest man of his age, has passed away, at the age of fifty-five, on the Ides of March, in the year 44 before Christ, at the foot of the statue of the man he defeated.

[pause]

Brutus turns to the senators, to make a speech. He wants to explain to them why it was done: for liberty, for the Republic. But the senators do not wait to listen. They run, all of them, out of the hall, and out into the streets, in panic.

[pause]

{img:senators-hurrying-away-colonnade-togas-confusion}
The news spreads through the city like the wind. People close their shops, and run home, and lock their doors. Nobody knows what will happen next. Will there be fighting in the streets? Will Caesar's soldiers come, and take revenge?

[long-pause]

The plotters walk through the streets, from the Theatre of Pompey, to the Capitol, with their togas wrapped around their left arms, calling out to the people that they have killed the tyrant, and that Rome is free.

[pause]

But the people do not cheer. They are silent, and afraid, and they stay in their houses.

[pause]

The plotters climb up to the Capitol, the hill of the Temple of Jupiter, and stay there, under the protection of a band of gladiators that Decimus Brutus has hired, and wait to see what will happen.

[long-pause]

In the empty hall of Pompey, Caesar lies alone, for several hours, at the foot of the statue.

[pause]

At last, three of his slaves come, and gently lift him into a litter, and carry him home, through the streets, to the Domus Publica, to Calpurnia. As they pass, people come out of their doors, and look, and weep.

[pause]

{img:three-slaves-carrying-litter-quiet-street-dusk}
And Calpurnia's dream has come true.

[long-pause]

{img:rome-morning-after-quiet-shuttered-shops-grey}
The next morning, the sixteenth of March, Rome wakes up to a strange, uneasy quiet.

[pause]

The shops are closed. The streets are almost empty. People stay at home, and talk in whispers, and wait. Nobody knows who is in charge of Rome now.

[long-pause]

Mark Antony, the consul, escaped from the Theatre of Pompey, in the confusion, disguised as a slave, and went home, and barricaded his house. But now he comes out, and begins to act.

[pause]

He goes to Calpurnia, and she gives him Caesar's papers, and a great sum of money, Caesar's private treasure, for safekeeping. And Lepidus, Caesar's master of horse, brings his soldiers into the city, into the Forum, to keep order.

[pause]

Cicero hurries back to Rome. He is delighted. He believes that the Republic has been saved. He goes up to the Capitol, to congratulate the plotters, and he writes to a friend that the Ides of March have filled him with joy. But he also writes, a little later, that the plotters acted with the courage of men, but the planning of children. Because they have no plan for what to do next.

[long-pause]

On the seventeenth of March, the Senate meets, in the Temple of Tellus, the goddess of the earth.

[pause]

There is a long and difficult debate. Some senators want to declare Caesar a tyrant, and throw his body in the Tiber, and cancel all his laws. But many senators owe their offices to Caesar's laws. If his laws are cancelled, they will lose everything.

[pause]

{img:temple-of-tellus-senators-debating-columns-daylight}
In the end, there is a compromise, proposed by Cicero. The plotters will not be punished. They are given an amnesty, a pardon. But all of Caesar's laws, and all his appointments, will remain. And Caesar will be given a public funeral, as a great Roman.

[pause]

That evening, the plotters come down from the Capitol. Antony invites Cassius to dinner, and Lepidus invites Brutus. It seems, for a moment, that there will be peace.

[long-pause]

Then Caesar's will is opened, and read, at Antony's house.

[pause]

It was kept safe by the Vestal Virgins. And in it, Caesar has left his gardens across the Tiber, the beautiful gardens where Cleopatra has been living, to the people of Rome, as a public park. And he has left to every citizen of Rome a gift of three hundred sesterces, which is several months' wages for an ordinary working man.

[pause]

The people of Rome are deeply moved. Caesar loved them. He remembered them. And the plotters have taken him from them.

[pause]

And there is something else in the will. Caesar has adopted, as his son, his great-nephew, the young Gaius Octavius, who is far away in Apollonia, across the sea. And he has left him three quarters of his fortune, and his name.

[long-pause]

On the twentieth of March, there is the funeral.

[pause]

A great procession carries Caesar's body through the streets, on an ivory couch, covered with purple and gold, to the Forum. In front of the Rostra, a little golden shrine has been built, like the Temple of Venus Genetrix, and the couch is placed inside it, with Caesar's robe hanging beside it.

[pause]

{img:funeral-procession-forum-ivory-couch-mourners-crowds}
The whole city has come. The Forum is packed with people, in mourning: citizens, and soldiers, and freedmen, and slaves, and foreigners. The Jews of Rome are there too, in great numbers, because Caesar was kind to them, and protected their right to worship in their own way. They come back, night after night, to mourn him.

[long-pause]

Mark Antony climbs up onto the Rostra, to give the funeral speech.

[pause]

We do not know exactly what he said. Shakespeare, more than sixteen hundred years later, will write a famous speech for him: Friends, Romans, countrymen, lend me your ears. I come to bury Caesar, not to praise him. Those are Shakespeare's words, not Antony's.

[pause]

But the ancient writers say that Antony spoke only a few words of his own. He had the herald read out the honours that the Senate had given to Caesar, and the oath that all the senators had sworn, to protect him. And then he held up Caesar's toga, for all the people to see.

[long-pause]

The crowd is overcome with grief.

[pause]

They do not want Caesar carried away, to be burned on the Field of Mars, as had been planned. They want him here, in the Forum, at the heart of Rome. They bring benches, and tables, and wood from the shops, and build a great pyre, there and then, in the middle of the Forum, and burn his body there.

[pause]

{img:night-forum-glow-distant-crowd-mourning-silhouettes}
The veterans of his legions throw their weapons and their medals onto the pyre. The women throw their jewellery, and the children their little lockets. Actors and musicians throw their costumes. And the people stay all night, in the Forum, beside the glowing embers, weeping.

[long-pause]

After the funeral, the mood of the city changes.

[pause]

The people are angry with the plotters. Brutus and Cassius, and the others, are no longer safe in Rome. In April, they quietly leave the city, and go to their country houses, and later to the provinces, across the sea.

[pause]

And in the middle of April, Cleopatra, with her little son, Caesarion, and her court, leaves the villa in the gardens across the river, and sails home to Egypt. Cicero writes to Atticus that he is not sorry to see her go.

[long-pause]

The place in the Forum where Caesar's body was burned becomes a holy place.

[pause]

The people put up an altar there, and a column of marble, with the words: to the father of his country. And they come to make offerings there, and to pray, and to swear oaths in Caesar's name.

[pause]

{img:simple-altar-forum-flowers-offerings-morning-light}
Years later, a temple will be built on that spot, the Temple of the Divine Julius. And the remains of it are still there today.

[long-pause]

{img:young-octavius-reading-letter-apollonia-seaside-camp}
In Apollonia, across the sea, in what is now Albania, the young Gaius Octavius hears the news at the end of March.

[pause]

He is eighteen years old, a slender, pale, rather sickly young man, with fair, curly hair, and clear, bright eyes. He has been studying there, with his friends, and with his teachers, waiting for Caesar to come and lead the great campaign against the Parthians.

[long-pause]

Octavius is the grandson of Caesar's sister, Julia. His father passed away when he was four, and he was brought up by his mother, Atia, and his stepfather, a careful, sensible man named Philippus.

[pause]

Caesar was fond of him, and saw something special in him, and took him along on his last campaign, in Spain, and taught him. But nobody else thinks of him as very important. He is just a clever, quiet boy, of eighteen.

[pause]

His closest friend, who is with him in Apollonia, is a young man of the same age, Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa, from a modest family, who will become one of the greatest generals and builders in Roman history. They will stay friends all their lives.

[long-pause]

When Octavius hears that Caesar has adopted him as his son, and left him his name and his fortune, his mother and his stepfather write to him, and beg him not to accept it. It is too dangerous, they say. You are too young.

[pause]

But Octavius accepts. He takes the name Gaius Julius Caesar. And he sails across the sea to Italy, and travels to Rome, to claim his inheritance.

[pause]

{img:ship-crossing-adriatic-calm-sea-spring-italy-coast}
From now on, he calls himself Caesar. Historians will later call him Octavian, to tell him apart from his great-uncle. And many years later, he will be given another name, by which the whole world will remember him: Augustus.

[long-pause]

In Rome, Mark Antony does not take him seriously at first.

[pause]

Antony calls him a boy who owes everything to a name. He refuses to give Octavian the money from Caesar's treasure. So Octavian sells his own property, and borrows money from his friends, and pays out, himself, to every citizen of Rome, the three hundred sesterces that Caesar had left them in his will.

[pause]

{img:young-octavian-greeting-citizens-forum-crowd}
The people of Rome begin to love him. And Caesar's old soldiers, the veterans of the legions, begin to come to him, because he bears Caesar's name.

[long-pause]

In July, the month of Caesar's birth, there are games, in Caesar's honour, the games of Caesar's Victory, and Octavian pays for them.

[pause]

And during the games, for seven nights in a row, a bright comet appears in the sky, in the north, at about the eleventh hour, just before sunset, shining brightly over Rome.

[pause]

The people of Rome believe that it is the soul of Caesar, rising up into the heavens, to take his place among the gods. They call it the Julian star.

[pause]

And Octavian, who is very clever, puts a star on the head of every statue of Caesar. And from now on, he calls himself the son of a god.

[long-pause]

And the month of Caesar's birth, which the Romans have always called Quintilis, the fifth month, because the old Roman year began in March, is now given a new name, in Caesar's honour: Iulius.

[pause]

July.

[pause]

{img:summer-roman-fields-harvest-golden-july-light}
We still call it that today. Every year, when the month of July comes around, we remember, without thinking about it, Gaius Julius Caesar.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-countryside-road-legions-marching-distance-autumn}
The plotters had hoped that, with Caesar gone, the old Republic would come back, and Rome would be free and at peace again.

[pause]

It does not happen. Instead, there are more wars.

[long-pause]

For more than a year, Antony and Octavian and the Senate and the plotters struggle against each other for power, in a confusing series of quarrels and alliances and battles.

[pause]

At first, Octavian joins the Senate against Antony. Then, in the autumn of 43, he changes sides, and makes an alliance with Antony, and with Lepidus. The three men meet on a little island in a river near Bononia, the modern Bologna, and agree to rule Rome together, as a commission of three, a triumvirate.

[pause]

They draw up a list of their enemies, who are to be put to death. It is a cruel and terrible time. Many good men lose their lives, and many more flee from Italy.

[long-pause]

Cicero is on the list.

[pause]

Antony hates him. Because in the year after Caesar's death, Cicero gave a series of fourteen great speeches against Antony, in the Senate and in the Forum, which he called the Philippics, after the speeches that the great Greek orator Demosthenes once gave against King Philip of Macedon. They are the last and some of the finest speeches of his life.

[pause]

{img:coastal-villa-formiae-sea-evening-calm-cypress}
In December of 43, Cicero is at his house by the sea, at Formiae. His servants try to carry him to the coast in a litter, to escape by ship. But the soldiers find him on the road, among the trees. Cicero, the story says, leans out of his litter, calmly, and looks at them, and does not try to escape. He is sixty-three years old.

[long-pause]

Years later, the story goes, Octavian, now the emperor Augustus, finds one of his grandsons reading a book by Cicero, and the boy, frightened, tries to hide it under his cloak.

[pause]

Augustus takes the book, and stands there, reading a long part of it. Then he gives it back to the boy, and says: he was a learned man, my child, a learned man, and a lover of his country.

[pause]

Cicero's books, and his letters, saved by his faithful secretary Tiro, and by his friend Atticus, will be read for two thousand years. In the Middle Ages, and in the Renaissance, he will be the great model of how to write Latin. And the founders of the United States will read him, and think about his ideas of the Republic, and of liberty, and of the duties of a citizen.

[long-pause]

Brutus and Cassius, meanwhile, have gone to the East, to Greece and Asia Minor, and have gathered great armies.

[pause]

In the autumn of 42, at Philippi, in northern Greece, there are two great battles, between the armies of Brutus and Cassius, on one side, and the armies of Antony and Octavian, on the other.

[pause]

{img:plains-philippi-greece-hills-autumn-quiet-distance}
After the first battle, Cassius, thinking wrongly that all is lost, takes his own life. And after the second battle, three weeks later, Brutus, defeated, does the same.

[pause]

Plutarch tells a story, that one night before the battles, Brutus, sitting alone in his tent, by the light of a lamp, reading, as he always did late into the night, saw a strange figure standing beside him, who said: I am your evil spirit, Brutus. You will see me at Philippi.

[long-pause]

Almost all of the plotters lose their lives within three years of the Ides of March, in battle, or by their own hand, or in other ways. None of them, it is said, outlives Caesar by more than a few years.

[pause]

After Philippi, Antony goes to the East, and Octavian stays in Rome. And in the East, Antony meets Cleopatra, the queen of Egypt, again, at Tarsus, where she comes to meet him, sailing up the river in a golden barge, with purple sails, and silver oars, dressed as the goddess Venus.

[pause]

{img:golden-barge-purple-sails-river-tarsus-sunset}
Antony falls in love with her, and goes with her to Alexandria, and stays there. And they have three children together.

[long-pause]

For years, Antony and Octavian share the Roman world: Antony in the East, Octavian in the West. But at last, they quarrel.

[pause]

In the year 31 before Christ, at Actium, off the coast of Greece, Octavian's fleet, commanded by his old friend Agrippa, defeats the fleet of Antony and Cleopatra. The next year, in Alexandria, Antony and Cleopatra both take their own lives, rather than be taken prisoner and led through the streets of Rome.

[pause]

Cleopatra's son by Caesar, Caesarion, who is seventeen, is also lost, on Octavian's orders. There can be only one son of Caesar.

[long-pause]

And Octavian, at the age of thirty-three, is now the sole master of the whole Roman world.

[pause]

He is clever enough not to call himself king, or even dictator. He remembers what happened to Caesar. He says that he has restored the Republic. He keeps the Senate, and the consuls, and all the old offices, and the old forms. But in fact, he holds all the power.

[pause]

{img:augustus-mature-statue-toga-calm-forum-dignified}
In the year 27 before Christ, the Senate gives him the name Augustus, which means the revered one. He rules for forty-one years, until he passes away, an old man, in the year 14 after Christ. He brings peace to the Roman world, after a century of civil wars. The Romans will call it the Pax Augusta, or the Pax Romana, the Roman peace.

[long-pause]

He is the first of the Roman emperors. The Republic, which Brutus and Cassius wanted to save, is gone forever.

[pause]

And the month after July, which was called Sextilis, the sixth month, is renamed in his honour: Augustus. August.

[long-pause]

{img:largo-argentina-ruins-below-street-level-cats-sun}
Today, more than two thousand years later, you can still visit the places in Rome where these things happened.

[pause]

In the middle of modern Rome, among the busy streets and the traffic and the trams, there is a square called the Largo di Torre Argentina. In the middle of the square, a few metres below the level of the street, there are the ruins of four ancient temples, from the time of the Republic, with their columns and their steps and their old stones.

[long-pause]

And behind them, partly under the modern street, there are the remains of a great platform of stone, which was the hall of Pompey, where the Senate met, on the Ides of March, in the year 44 before Christ.

[pause]

It was here, more or less, that Julius Caesar passed away. The rest of the Theatre of Pompey has disappeared. But its great curved shape can still be seen, in the curve of the streets and the old houses that were later built on top of its foundations.

[pause]

Today, the ruins in the Largo di Torre Argentina are home to a colony of cats, perhaps a hundred and fifty of them, who sleep in the sun, on the old stones and the broken columns, and are looked after by volunteers, in a little cat shelter in the corner of the square. Visitors come from all over the world to see the cats, sleeping where Caesar once fell.

[long-pause]

A little further on, in the Roman Forum, among the ruins of the temples and the basilicas, there are the remains of the Temple of the Divine Julius, built by Octavian, on the place where the people of Rome burned Caesar's body, after Antony's speech.

[pause]

There is not much left of it: just a low platform of rough stone, and, in a little half-round niche, under a modern roof, the remains of the altar.

[pause]

{img:temple-divine-julius-ruins-niche-flowers-forum}
But every day, people still leave flowers there. Fresh flowers, roses and lilies and wreaths, and little notes, and coins, for Caesar, more than two thousand years later. And on the Ides of March, every year, the people of Rome come, and put on a play of his death, and lay wreaths, and remember him.

[long-pause]

The Forum itself is a wonderful place to walk.

[pause]

You can walk along the Sacred Way, where Caesar's funeral procession passed, and where the Luperci ran on that February day. You can see the Rostra, the speakers' platform, where Antony offered Caesar the crown. You can see the round Temple of Vesta, and the House of the Vestal Virgins, with its garden and its pools, and the statues of the Vestals, and the place where the Domus Publica stood, where Caesar and Calpurnia lived.

[pause]

And you can see the Curia, the Senate house, which Caesar began to build, and which was finished after his death, and which is still standing, rebuilt by later emperors, with its tall bronze doors.

[long-pause]

Next to it, there is the Forum of Caesar, with three tall columns of the Temple of Venus Genetrix still standing, where the golden statue of Cleopatra once stood.

[pause]

On the Capitoline hill, there are the great museums, with statues and portraits of the Romans of that time: Caesar, and Cicero, and Augustus, and many others, whose faces we can still look at, in marble, as they looked two thousand years ago.

[pause]

{img:marble-busts-museum-gallery-soft-window-light}
And across the river, in Trastevere, where the Gardens of Caesar once were, and where Cleopatra lived, there are now narrow streets, and little squares, and old churches, and restaurants, where people eat outside, in the warm evenings.

[long-pause]

Caesar's name lives on, in many ways.

[pause]

His name, Caesar, became the title of the Roman emperors. And from it, more than a thousand years later, come the titles of the emperors of Germany and Austria, the Kaiser, and the emperors of Russia, the Tsar.

[pause]

His calendar is still the basis of our calendar. His month, July, still bears his name. His books about the wars in Gaul and the civil war are still read. And his story has been told, over and over again, in history books, and plays, and films, for more than two thousand years.

[long-pause]

So the story of Rome, in the year when Caesar died, comes to its end tonight.

[pause]

The great city on its seven hills, the crowded streets and the quiet gardens, the speeches and the letters and the festivals, and the spring day when everything changed.
