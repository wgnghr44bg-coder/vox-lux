For more than fifteen hundred years, alchemists searched for the philosopher's stone, a substance said to turn lead into gold and to cure every illness. Around the year 1600, the search reached its height, in candlelit workshops and at the court of an emperor in Prague. This story follows the alchemists from ancient Alexandria and Baghdad to the laboratories of Europe, with their furnaces, glass vessels and secret symbols. It meets physicians, kings, frauds, women practitioners, and scientists such as Boyle and Newton, and ends with the birth of modern chemistry.

[long-pause]

For many hundreds of years, alchemists searched for a substance called the philosopher's stone. The philosopher's stone was a legendary substance that alchemists believed could perfect metals and the human body. According to their writings, a small amount could turn common metals, such as lead or copper, into gold or silver. This change was called transmutation, or chrysopoeia, from Greek words meaning making gold. Many alchemists also believed that the stone could cure disease and lengthen life. In this form, it was called the elixir of life. The search for the stone lasted from ancient times until the 1700s. Scholars know about it from thousands of surviving old texts, printed books, letters and lab notes. Many of these are now kept in libraries such as the British Library and the Wellcome Collection in London. Even so, no one ever succeeded in making the stone. Yet the search was not foolish in its own time. It was based on real ideas about nature and led to many hands-on discoveries.

[long-pause]

{img:02-alchemist-holding-red-powder-small-vial}

Alchemists believed that all metals were slowly growing toward gold inside the earth. To grasp alchemy, it helps to know how people around 1600 thought about nature. Many educated people believed that metals were formed slowly underground, much like plants growing in soil. According to this view, all metals were striving toward perfection, and gold was the most perfect metal of all. Lead, tin, iron and copper were simply metals that had not yet matured. If nature could turn base metals into gold over thousands of years, alchemists reasoned, perhaps skilled people could speed up the process. The philosopher's stone was the agent that would make this possible. Scholars say that this idea was in line with the science of the time, which drew on the writings of Aristotle. Even so, the theory was wrong. Metals are chemical elements and cannot be changed into one another by common chemical means. Yet within its own framework, the search made logical sense to many thoughtful people.

[long-pause]

{img:03-four-elements-diagram-old-book-candle}

Alchemy was based on the ancient idea that all was made of four elements. Since ancient Greece, many scholars believed that all matter was made of four elements: earth, water, air and fire. This idea was worked out by the philosophers Empedocles and Aristotle. Each element had two qualities. Fire was hot and dry, air was hot and wet, water was cold and wet, and earth was cold and dry. By changing these qualities, one substance could in theory be changed into one more. Alchemists also used a later idea that metals were made from two principles, called sulphur and mercury. These were not the common substances of those names, but philosophical principles representing qualities such as combustibility and fluidity. In the 1500s, the doctor Paracelsus added a third principle, salt. Scholars call this the tria prima. Even so, these theories were slowly replaced by modern chemistry in the seventeenth and eighteenth hundreds of years. Yet they provided a framework for tests for well over a thousand years.

[long-pause]

{img:04-gold-silver-lead-metals-on-wooden-table}

Alchemists linked each of the seven known metals with one of the seven planets known at the time. Around 1600, seven metals were known: gold, silver, mercury, copper, iron, tin and lead. Alchemists connected each with one of the seven heavenly bodies that moved across the sky. Gold was linked with the Sun and silver with the Moon. Mercury, the liquid metal, shared its name with the planet Mercury. Copper was linked with Venus, iron with Mars, tin with Jupiter and lead with Saturn. Each metal had its own symbol, which alchemists used in their writings. This system reflected a belief in deep connections between the heavens and the earth. A famous phrase expressed it: as above, so below. Even so, the symbols also had practical uses. They allowed alchemists to write quickly and at times secretly. Some of these symbols survived into early chemistry. The name mercury is still used today for the liquid metal, a reminder of these old connections between planets and metals.

[long-pause]

{img:05-alchemist-reading-manuscript-night-study}

Alchemists studied old texts carefully, believing that ancient writers held secret knowledge. Alchemy was not only hands-on work. It was also a scholarly tradition based on reading. Alchemists collected and studied texts written by earlier masters, some of them over a thousand years old. Many believed that ancient writers had known the secret of the philosopher's stone but had hidden it in symbolic language. Texts were copied by hand, translated and later printed. Large collections of alchemy writings were published in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth hundreds of years. One of the main was the Theatrum Chemicum, first printed in 1602, which gathered dozens of treatises in some volumes. Scholars use these collections to study how ideas spread. Even so, the texts were often confusing and contradictory. Many were attributed to famous names but were written by others. Alchemists spent years trying to read them. Reading and testing went hand in hand. Many hours were spent at the desk as well as at the furnace.

[long-pause]

{img:06-wealthy-patron-visiting-alchemist-lab}

Kings, princes and wealthy patrons often paid alchemists to work for them. Alchemy required costly tools, fuel and materials. Many alchemists depended on wealthy patrons, such as kings, princes, nobles and merchants. Patrons hoped that alchemists would produce gold to fill their treasuries, or cures to preserve their health. Some rulers kept alchemists at their courts and provided them with labs. In return, they expected regular reports and results. Scholars have found contracts between patrons and alchemists in archives across Europe. These documents often specify payments, materials and deadlines. Even so, the relationship could be risky. Patrons grew impatient when no gold appeared. Some alchemists were jailed or punished. Others moved from court to court, seeking new support. Patronage also brought prestige. A ruler who supported alchemy showed interest in learning and the secrets of nature. Around 1600, alchemy was fashionable among the powerful, above all in Central Europe, where some courts became famous centres of alchemy work.

[long-pause]

{img:07-alexandria-ancient-harbour-lighthouse-sunset}

Alchemy in the Western tradition began in Egypt, in the city of Alexandria, about two thousand years ago. The earliest surviving alchemy writings in the Western tradition come from Egypt during the period of Roman rule. They were written in Greek, mainly in the city of Alexandria, between about the first and fourth hundreds of years after Christ. Alexandria was a great centre of learning, with scholars from many cultures. Egyptian craftsmen had long experience in working metals, dyeing cloth and making imitation gemstones. Greek philosophy provided theories about matter. These traditions combined to produce early alchemy. Scholars know about it from papyri and later copied old texts. Two papyri, known as the Leiden and Stockholm papyri, contain recipes for making imitation gold and silver, dyes and gems. Even so, it is not always clear whether early writers wanted to imitate gold or truly transform metals. The word chemistry itself may derive from the Greek word chemeia, though its origins are debated. Some scholars connect it to an ancient name for Egypt.

[long-pause]

{img:08-zosimos-scholar-writing-scroll-lamp}

Zosimos of Panopolis, who lived around the year 300, is one of the earliest known alchemy writers. Zosimos came from Panopolis, a town in Upper Egypt, and lived around the end of the third and beginning of the fourth century. He wrote many works on alchemy, of which fragments survive in later Greek old texts. His writings describe lab apparatus, such as devices for distillation and sublimation. They also contain visions and dreams with spiritual meanings. Zosimos wrote to a woman named Theosebeia, who seems to have been his student or colleague. Scholars consider his works a key link between hands-on metalworking and the spiritual side of alchemy. He referred to earlier authorities, such as a woman alchemist known as Maria the Jewess. Even so, much of his work is lost, and what remains is hard to read. His writings were copied by Byzantine scholars and preserved in libraries for hundreds of years. Through them, the ideas of Alexandrian alchemy passed into the later traditions of the Arabic and European worlds.

[long-pause]

{img:09-maria-the-jewess-water-bath-apparatus}

Maria the Jewess, an early alchemist, is remembered for inventing lab tools, such as a gentle water bath. Maria the Jewess, also called Maria the Prophetess, lived in Alexandria probably in the first to third hundreds of years after Christ. None of her own writings survive complete, but later authors, above all Zosimos, quoted her and described her inventions. She is credited with devising some types of apparatus. One was the kerotakis, a device for exposing metals to vapours. Another was a still with three arms, called the tribikos. She is also traditionally associated with the water bath, a method of heating substances gently by placing them in a container of hot water. In French, this is still called a bain-marie, after her name. The attribution is based on tradition, and certainty is impossible. Even so, Maria is regarded as one of the first known women in the history of chemistry. Her name survives in kitchens around the world, where cooks melt chocolate gently in a bain-marie.

[long-pause]

{img:10-baghdad-house-of-wisdom-scholars-books}

Alchemy grew further in the Islamic world, where scholars translated and expanded Greek knowledge. After the seventh century, the lands of the Islamic caliphates stretched from Spain to Central Asia. Scholars in cities such as Baghdad, Damascus and Cairo translated many Greek works into Arabic, such as texts on alchemy. They added their own notes and tests. The word alchemy itself comes from the Arabic al-kimiya. Many other words connected with chemistry also come from Arabic, such as alembic, elixir and alcohol, although the meaning of alcohol later changed. Islamic scholars made key advances in lab methods, above all distillation, crystallisation and the classification of substances. Even so, much of this work was later known in Europe only through Latin translations. In some cases, the original Arabic texts are lost. Islamic alchemy was a major bridge between ancient and European science. Without it, many of the ideas that later reached alchemists around 1600 would not have survived.

[long-pause]

{img:11-jabir-scholar-workshop-glass-alembic}

A large body of Arabic alchemy writings was attributed to a figure named Jabir ibn Hayyan. Jabir ibn Hayyan is traditionally said to have lived in the eighth century. Hundreds of Arabic works on alchemy, medicine and other subjects were attributed to him. Scholars now believe that many of these works were written by some authors over a long period, perhaps in the ninth and tenth hundreds of years. The Jabirian writings worked out the theory that metals were formed from sulphur and mercury in other proportions. They also emphasised the importance of balance and number in nature. In Europe, Latin works were later published under the name Geber, a Latin form of Jabir. Some of the main of these, such as the Summa Perfectionis from around 1300, were actually written by a European author, possibly Paul of Taranto. Even so, they were widely believed to be Arabic. The name Geber became one of the greatest authorities in European alchemy. The English word gibberish is at times said to come from his name, though this is uncertain.

[long-pause]

{img:12-al-razi-physician-laboratory-flasks-patients}

The Persian doctor al-Razi classified chemical substances and described lab tools in detail. Abu Bakr al-Razi, known in Europe as Rhazes, lived from about 865 to 925. He was a famous doctor who directed hospitals in Rayy, near modern Tehran, and in Baghdad. He also wrote on alchemy. In his Book of Secrets, he described substances, tools and procedures in a hands-on and orderly way. He divided substances into groups, such as minerals, plants and animal products, and described apparatus such as flasks, furnaces and stills. Scholars regard al-Razi as a key figure in the development of chemical methods. His approach was more hands-on than mystical. He believed in transmutation, but his accounts of lab work were clear and useful. Even so, his medical works were more much read in Europe than his alchemy. His books on smallpox and measles were studied for hundreds of years. His careful notes show how alchemy and medicine were closely linked, a connection that remained strong around 1600.

[long-pause]

{img:13-toledo-translators-manuscripts-twelfth-century}

In the 1100s, European scholars began translating Arabic works on alchemy into Latin. During the 1100s, scholars from across Europe travelled to Spain and Sicily, where Christian, Muslim and Jewish cultures met. In cities such as Toledo, they translated Arabic books on science, medicine, mathematics and philosophy into Latin. Alchemy was among these subjects. In 1144, an English scholar named Robert of Chester completed a translation called the Book of the Composition of Alchemy. Scholars often describe it as the first alchemy text translated into Latin. Its preface noted that Latin readers did not yet know what alchemy was. Other translations followed, bringing the works of Jabir, al-Razi and others into Europe. Even so, translations were at times inaccurate, and the meaning of technical terms was often unclear. European scholars had to read hard texts in an unfamiliar subject. Over the following hundreds of years, alchemy became an set up part of European learning, studied by monks, doctors, scholars and craftsmen.

[long-pause]

{img:14-medieval-monk-scholar-roger-bacon-study}

Old scholars such as Roger Bacon and Albertus Magnus wrote about alchemy and the nature of metals. In the 1200s, alchemy attracted the interest of some of Europe's leading scholars. Albertus Magnus, a German friar and teacher, wrote about minerals and metals and discussed alchemy cautiously. Roger Bacon, an English Franciscan friar, praised alchemy as a useful science that could improve medicine and prolong life. Both men emphasised the importance of observation and experience. Many later alchemy texts were falsely attributed to famous scholars such as these, to give them authority. Even so, it is clear that alchemy was taken seriously in old universities and religious orders. It was discussed alongside natural philosophy and medicine. At the same time, it attracted suspicion. Some religious authorities worried that alchemy encouraged fraud or forbidden knowledge. The tension between respect and suspicion followed alchemy throughout its history. It was still present at the courts of Europe around 1600.

[long-pause]

{img:15-flamel-house-paris-medieval-stone-street}

Nicolas Flamel was a real scribe in old Paris, but the legend of his success as an alchemist began long after his death. Nicolas Flamel lived in Paris from about 1330 to 1418. He worked as a scribe and bookseller and became fairly wealthy. With his wife Pernelle, he gave money to churches and charities. Records of his property and gifts survive in Paris archives. About two hundred years after his death, books began to appear claiming that Flamel had found the philosopher's stone. The main, published in 1612, contained a story of how he found a strange book and learned its secrets. Scholars have shown that this text was written long after Flamel's lifetime and was falsely attributed to him. Even so, the legend became extremely popular. People searched his houses for hidden gold. A house that Flamel built in 1407 still stands in Paris, on the rue de Montmorency. It is often described as one of the oldest stone houses in the city. It is now a restaurant.

[long-pause]

{img:16-pope-papal-decree-parchment-seal}

Old authorities passed laws against fraudulent alchemists who claimed to make gold. As alchemy spread, so did fraud. Some people claimed to make gold or silver but actually used tricks, such as hidden gold or coloured alloys. In 1317, Pope John the Twenty-Second issued a decree condemning alchemists who promised what they could not deliver and produced false gold. In England, a law passed in 1404 under King Henry the Fourth made it a crime to multiply gold or silver. Scholars say that rulers feared that false gold could damage trade and the currency. Some also worried that if alchemists succeeded, the value of gold would collapse. Even so, these laws did not stop alchemy. Some rulers granted special licences to alchemists they trusted. The English law against multiplying gold remained in force until 1689, when it was repealed by Parliament. The scientist Robert Boyle supported its repeal. Laws like these show that people took the idea of making gold seriously.

[long-pause]

{img:17-printing-press-alchemy-book-woodcut}

The invention of printing spread alchemy books widely across Europe in the 1500s. After Johannes Gutenberg worked out printing with movable type around 1450, books became much cheaper and more easy to find. Alchemy texts, which had circulated in old texts, began to appear in print. By the 1500s, printers in cities such as Basel, Frankfurt, Strasbourg and Venice were producing many alchemy books. Some were translations of older works, while others were new. Woodcuts and engravings illustrated furnaces, vessels and symbolic images. Scholars say that printing made alchemy accessible to a wider audience, such as doctors, apothecaries, craftsmen and educated townspeople. Large collections, such as the Theatrum Chemicum, helped preserve and spread texts. Even so, print also made it easier for fraudulent or confused works to circulate. Readers had to judge which books to trust. Around 1600, interest in alchemy was at a high point. Printed books fed this interest and connected alchemists across Europe in a shared culture of reading and test.

[long-pause]

{img:18-town-apothecary-shop-1600-jars-shelves}

Around 1600, alchemy methods were used by apothecaries, metalworkers and makers of dyes and cures. Alchemy was not practised only by scholars seeking gold. Many of its methods were used in daily trades. Apothecaries used distillation to prepare cures and herbal waters. Metalworkers used furnaces and crucibles to refine and test metals. Assayers tested the purity of gold and silver in coins and ores. Makers of dyes, pigments, glass, gunpowder and soap all used processes like those described in alchemy texts. Scholars such as Pamela Smith and Lawrence Principe have emphasised these hands-on connections. They show that alchemy was part of a broad world of craft knowledge. Even so, the line between alchemy and common chemistry was not clear. The word chymistry is at times used by scholars to describe this combined field in the 1500s and 1600s. In towns across Europe, apothecary shops were filled with jars, bottles and distilled waters, products of methods worked out by alchemists for hundreds of years.

[long-pause]

{img:19-athanor-brick-furnace-tower-slow-fire}

An alchemist's lab around 1600 was built around a special furnace called the athanor. The athanor was a furnace designed to provide steady, gentle heat for long periods, at times for weeks or months. It usually had a tall tower filled with charcoal, which slowly fed the fire below. This design allowed the temperature to remain fairly constant with little attention. Many alchemy processes required slow heating, since alchemists believed they were imitating the gentle warmth of nature. The athanor was at times compared to a hen warming an egg. Scholars know the design of athanors from illustrations in printed books and from surviving accounts. Some labs also had other furnaces for strong heat, used for melting metals. Even so, controlling heat precisely was very hard without thermometers, which were invented only in the 1600s. Alchemists used their experience, watching colours and sounds, to judge the fire. Keeping the athanor burning steadily was one of the main tasks in the lab.

[long-pause]

{img:20-alembic-distillation-glass-copper-drips}

Distillation with an alembic was one of the main methods in alchemy. Distillation separates substances by heating a liquid until it turns into vapour, then cooling the vapour so that it becomes liquid again. Alchemists used a piece of tools called an alembic. It consisted of a lower vessel, called a cucurbit, where the liquid was heated, and an upper head with a long spout. Vapour rose into the head, cooled and ran down the spout into a receiving flask. With this method, alchemists could make strong alcohol, called aqua vitae, or water of life. Many herbal waters and acids. Scholars say that distillation methods improved greatly in the Middle Ages and spread far in the 1500s. Even so, glass vessels often cracked under heat. Copper and pottery vessels were also used. Alembics appear in many paintings and engravings of labs. Distillation remains one of the basic methods of chemistry, and the same principle is still used today.

[long-pause]

{img:21-retort-crucible-pelican-vessels-shelf}

Alchemists used many specialised vessels, such as retorts, crucibles and a looped flask called the pelican. Alchemy labs contained a variety of vessels, each with a particular purpose. A retort was a round flask with a long, bent neck, used for distillation of strong substances. A crucible was a small heat-resistant pot used to melt metals in a hot fire. An aludel was used for sublimation, the process of turning solids directly into vapour. One unusual vessel was called the pelican. It had two curved arms leading from the neck back into the body, so that vapours could condense and flow back repeatedly. Its name came from its resemblance to a pelican feeding its young. Scholars and archaeologists have found many such objects. At Oberstockstall in Austria, a 1500s lab was found under a church sacristy in 1980, containing hundreds of crucibles, flasks and other items. Even so, most labs have disappeared. Yet surviving objects and images show a well-equipped working space.

[long-pause]

{img:22-bellows-charcoal-sacks-assistant-working}

Lab work required assistants, large amounts of fuel and much physical labour. Alchemy was hard physical work. Furnaces had to be fed with charcoal, and strong fires needed bellows to blow air into them. Materials had to be ground, washed, filtered and stirred. Many alchemists employed assistants, at times called laborants, who did much of this work. Scholars have found records of payments for charcoal, glass, metals and wages in the accounts of princely labs. These show that alchemy could be very costly. A lab at a royal court might consume large amounts of fuel every month. Even so, the work could also be dangerous. Fumes from mercury, lead, arsenic and other substances were poisonous. Explosions and fires happened. Some alchemists suffered from illnesses caused by their work, although they did not always grasp the cause. The lab was a place of skill and labour, not just mystery. The success of any test depended on careful, patient effort, often over many weeks.

[long-pause]

{img:23-alchemical-symbols-chalk-wall-notes}

Alchemists recorded their work in notes full of symbols, codes and abbreviations. Alchemists kept notebooks recording their tests. Some surviving notebooks are remarkably detailed. They list materials, quantities, temperatures, colours and results. Others are brief and hard to grasp. Alchemists used symbols for metals, salts, processes and times, which made writing faster. Some also used codes to keep their work secret. Scholars have studied notebooks from the period, such as those of the American alchemist George Starkey in the 1600s, who wrote under the name Eirenaeus Philalethes. These notebooks show careful, methodical work. Even so, alchemy writing was often deliberately obscure. Authors used metaphors, such as dragons, kings and queens, to describe chemical processes. They believed that the secrets should be understood only by those who were worthy. Scholars such as Lawrence Principe have reproduced some of these processes in modern labs. By following old recipes, they found that many accounts referred to real chemical reactions.

[long-pause]

{img:24-laboratory-and-oratory-prayer-desk-furnace}

Many alchemists saw their work as both a hands-on and a spiritual task. A famous engraving from 1595, by the German alchemist Heinrich Khunrath, shows a room divided into two parts. One side is a lab with a furnace and vessels. The other is an oratory, a place of prayer. Khunrath believed that alchemy required both prayer and work. This idea was summed up in the phrase ora et labora, pray and work. Many alchemists around 1600 were devout Christians. They saw the purification of metals as connected with the purification of the soul. Some believed that only a person of good character could succeed. Even so, scholars caution against seeing all alchemy as purely spiritual. Most alchemists were also very keen on real substances and hands-on results. The spiritual and the practical were combined, not separated. The engraving by Khunrath, called the Amphitheatre of Eternal Wisdom, remains one of the most famous images of alchemy and is often reproduced today.

[long-pause]

{img:25-early-morning-lab-fire-rekindled-dawn}

Alchemy processes could last for weeks, so the lab fire had to be tended day and night. Many alchemy procedures required continuous heating for long periods. Some recipes called for a vessel to be kept warm for forty days or more. This meant that the fire had to be watched constantly. Alchemists or their assistants took turns tending the furnace, adding charcoal and checking the temperature. Scholars know about these routines from lab notes, letters and instructions in alchemy books. Some accounts mention sleepless nights spent watching colours change inside sealed flasks. Even so, accidents often interrupted the work. A flask might crack, a fire might go out, or a vessel might overheat. Then the process had to begin again. Patience was seen as one of the main qualities of an alchemist. Many texts warned against hurrying. A common saying held that haste was the work of the devil. The slow rhythm of the lab, marked by the steady glow of the furnace, shaped the daily life of alchemists.

[long-pause]

{img:26-grinding-materials-mortar-pestle-table}

Preparing materials by grinding, washing and dissolving took up much of an alchemist's time. Before any substance could be heated or combined, it had to be prepared. Minerals and metals were ground into fine powders using mortars and pestles. They were washed, dissolved in acids or other liquids, filtered and dried. Alchemists worked with substances such as antimony, sulphur, mercury, saltpetre, vitriol and many kinds of salts and ores. Each required special handling. Scholars say that alchemists worked out key hands-on knowledge about the properties of these materials. They learned how substances dissolved, reacted and crystallised. Some of this knowledge was later used in medicine, mining and industry. Even so, the purpose of these preparations was often based on theories that later proved wrong. Alchemists might spend months purifying a material they believed contained the seed of gold. Yet the careful methods they used were sound. They formed the basis of lab skills that chemists still learn today.

[long-pause]

{img:27-colour-changes-sealed-flask-black-white-red}

Alchemists believed the philosopher's stone formed through a series of colour changes, from black to white to red. According to many alchemy texts, the making of the philosopher's stone passed through some stages, each marked by a colour. The first was blackness, called nigredo. It represented decay and dissolution. Next came whiteness, called albedo, a stage of purification. Some texts mentioned a yellow stage, called citrinitas. The final stage was redness, called rubedo, when the stone was believed to be complete. Scholars say that these colour changes were based partly on real notes of chemical reactions. Heating certain mixtures of metals and other substances does produce a series of colours. Even so, alchemists also gave these stages symbolic and spiritual meanings. Blackness was linked with death and redness with resurrection. Many illustrations show these stages as birds, such as a black crow, a white swan and a red phoenix. The colours guided alchemists in judging the progress of their work.

[long-pause]

{img:28-alchemist-weighing-results-scales-careful}

Some alchemists made careful measurements and weighed their materials precisely. Although alchemy is often imagined as strange, many alchemists worked carefully with measurements. They used balances to weigh materials before and after tests. Assayers, who tested metals for purity, worked out very accurate methods for determining how much gold or silver a sample contained. Some alchemists used these assaying methods to check their results. Scholars have found that the notebooks of 1600s workers, such as George Starkey, contain detailed weights and quantities. These show a methodical approach to test. Even so, alchemists often readed their results according to their beliefs. A slight increase in weight or a change of colour could be seen as a sign of success. The habit of careful measurement later became central to modern chemistry. The French chemist Antoine Lavoisier, in the late eighteenth century, built his new chemistry on precise weighing. In this sense, some alchemy practices prepared the way for later science.

[long-pause]

{img:29-evening-lab-quiet-notes-candle-cat}

At the end of the day, alchemists recorded their notes and planned the next steps. When the day's work was done, alchemists often wrote down what had happened. They recorded colours, smells, sounds, weights and the state of the fire. They compared their results with the accounts in books and tried to read them. Letters between alchemists show that they shared results and asked each other for advice. Scholars have found extensive correspondence between alchemists across Europe in the 1500s and 1600s. Some letters are full of hope, others of frustration. Even so, the evening was also a time of rest. Many alchemists lived in their labs or in rooms nearby. The furnace, kept burning through the night, provided warmth in winter. The life of an alchemist could be lonely, above all for those who worked in secret. Yet many were part of wider networks of correspondents, patrons and fellow workers. Their notes, letters and books form the main evidence for understanding their work today.

[long-pause]

{img:30-paracelsus-portrait-sword-book-1530s}

The Swiss doctor Paracelsus used alchemy to develop new kinds of medicine in the 1500s. Paracelsus was born in 1493 in Einsiedeln, in what is now Switzerland. His real name was Theophrastus von Hohenheim. He trained as a doctor and travelled widely across Europe, learning from doctors, miners, barbers and folk healers. He was known for his fierce temper and his attacks on traditional medicine. In 1527, while teaching at Basel, he reportedly burned a copy of the works of the respected old doctor Avicenna, to show his rejection of old authorities. Paracelsus argued that alchemy should be used mainly to make cures rather than gold. He believed that diseases had specific causes and could be treated with specific chemical cures. Scholars see him as a founder of iatrochemistry, the use of chemistry in medicine. Even so, his writings mixed practical advice with mystical ideas, which makes them hard to read. He died in Salzburg in 1541. But his followers spread his ideas widely in the decades that followed.

[long-pause]

{img:31-dose-makes-poison-apothecary-scale}

Paracelsus is remembered for the idea that the dose makes the poison. One of the most famous ideas linked with Paracelsus is that all things are poisons, and only the dose determines whether something is harmful or helpful. He wrote this in a work defending his use of strong mineral cures. This principle is still taught in toxicology today, usually summarised as the dose makes the poison. Paracelsus used substances such as mercury, antimony, sulphur and iron in his cures. Some of these were dangerous, and his opponents criticised him for using poisons. Scholars say that mercury was indeed effective against syphilis, a disease that spread far in Europe after the 1490s, although it also caused real side effects. Even so, the idea of carefully controlling doses was key. It encouraged more precise preparation of cures. Paracelsus also wrote about the illnesses of miners, describing diseases caused by breathing harmful dust and fumes. This work is seen as one of the earliest studies of occupational disease.

[long-pause]

{img:32-paracelsian-physician-preparing-mineral-remedy}

By 1600, followers of Paracelsus were preparing chemical cures across Europe. After Paracelsus died, his writings were collected and published, many for the first time in the 1560s and 1570s. His followers, called Paracelsians, promoted chemical cures in universities, courts and towns. They prepared cures by distillation, sublimation and dissolution of minerals and metals. Scholars say that this led to fierce debates. Traditional doctors, following the ancient Greek doctor Galen, relied mainly on herbal cures and the theory of the four humours. Paracelsians argued that chemistry offered stronger and more precise treatments. Gradually, many doctors adopted a mixture of both approaches. In 1618, the London College of Doctors published an official pharmacopoeia, a list of approved cures, which included some chemical cures. Even so, the debate went on for many decades. The rise of chemical medicine shows how alchemy was connected with health and healing. Many people around 1600 who called themselves alchemists were actually making cures rather than searching for gold.

[long-pause]

{img:33-drinkable-gold-elixir-glass-goblet}

Some alchemists tried to make a drinkable form of gold, believing it could heal the body. Because gold did not rust or decay, many people believed it had special powers to preserve life and health. Alchemists tried to make a drinkable gold, called aurum potabile. Recipes varied, but many involved dissolving gold or gold leaf in liquids. Some products contained very little real gold. Scholars have found that drinkable gold was sold by doctors and apothecaries and was very costly. It was given to wealthy patients, such as members of royal families. One English doctor, Francis Anthony, became famous in the early 1600s for selling his own version. He was criticised by the College of Doctors. Even so, the idea of drinkable gold remained popular for a long time. Modern research shows that gold has no general health benefits when swallowed, although certain gold compounds were later used to treat some forms of arthritis in the 1900s. The belief in drinkable gold reflected the close link between alchemy, medicine and the dream of long life.

[long-pause]

{img:34-herbal-distillation-spirits-rose-water}

Distilled herbal waters and spirits were among the most hands-on products of alchemy methods. Distillation was widely used to make herbal waters and medicinal spirits. Rose water, lavender water and other fragrant liquids were made by distilling flowers and herbs. These were used as cures, perfumes and flavourings. Strong alcohol, called aqua vitae, was seen as a powerful medicine and was used to extract the qualities of plants. Printed books on distillation caught on in the 1500s. One of the best known was written by the German doctor Hieronymus Brunschwig, first published in 1500. Scholars say that such books were read by doctors, apothecaries and wealthy households. Many noblewomen ran their own distilleries to make cures for their families and communities. Even so, the effectiveness of many of these cures was limited. Some contained useful substances, while others had little real effect. Yet distillation spread hands-on chemical knowledge widely. The production of spirits such as brandy also worked out from these methods during this period.

[long-pause]

{img:35-anna-of-saxony-distillery-noblewoman}

Anna of Saxony ran one of the largest distilleries in Europe for making cures in the late sixteenth century. Anna of Denmark, Electress of Saxony, lived from 1532 to 1585. She was married to Augustus, the ruler of Saxony. Anna had a strong interest in medicine and plants. She set up a large lab and distillery at her castle, such as at Annaburg, where many assistants prepared cures. She corresponded with other noblewomen and doctors, exchanging recipes and cures. Her cures were sent to family members, courtiers and other rulers. Scholars have studied her letters and recipe collections, which survive in German archives. They show that she was a skilled and knowledgeable worker. Her husband Augustus also supported alchemists and had his own lab in Dresden. Even so, Anna's work was focused on medicine rather than gold. She represents many women who practised chemistry in households and courts, though their work is often less visible in historical records than that of men.

[long-pause]

{img:36-prague-castle-hradcany-river-mist-1600}

Around 1600, the court of Emperor Rudolf the Second in Prague became the most famous centre of alchemy in Europe. Rudolf the Second was Holy Roman Emperor from 1576 until 1612. In 1583, he moved his court from Vienna to Prague, in Bohemia. Prague Castle, standing on a hill above the Vltava river, became the centre of his court. Rudolf was very keen on art, science and the secrets of nature. He invited painters, sculptors, astronomers, astrologers and alchemists from across Europe. Scholars describe his court as one of the most remarkable cultural centres of its time. Among those who worked there were the astronomers Tycho Brahe and Johannes Kepler, who made major contributions to astronomy. Alchemists came in large numbers, hoping for the emperor's patronage. Even so, Rudolf's interests distracted him from politics. His reign was marked by growing religious and political tensions. In 1611, he was forced to give up most of his power to his brother Matthias. He died the following year in Prague.

[long-pause]

{img:37-rudolf-ii-emperor-portrait-court-collection}

Emperor Rudolf the Second was a passionate collector who was fascinated by alchemy and the natural world. Rudolf the Second was a quiet and reserved man who preferred his collections and studies to the duties of government. He is said to have suffered from periods of deep melancholy. In Prague Castle, he built a huge collection known as a Kunstkammer, or cabinet of art and curiosities. It contained paintings, sculptures, clocks, scientific instruments, minerals, shells, exotic animals and strange objects from around the world. Scholars say that such collections were meant to represent the whole of nature and human skill. Rudolf personally took part in alchemy tests, according to of the time reports. He supported many alchemists and paid for labs. A famous portrait by the painter Giuseppe Arcimboldo shows Rudolf as Vertumnus, the Roman god of the seasons, with his face made of fruits and vegetables. Even so, much of his collection was later scattered. Swedish troops took large parts of it during the Thirty Years' War in 1648.

[long-pause]

{img:38-golden-lane-tiny-colourful-houses-castle}

Golden Lane in Prague Castle is linked with alchemists by legend, though scholars say it was home to guards and goldsmiths. Golden Lane, or Zlatá ulička in Czech, is a narrow street of tiny houses built into the northern wall of Prague Castle. The houses were built in the late 1500s for the castle guards. Later, goldsmiths and other craftsmen lived there, which may explain the name. According to a popular legend, Emperor Rudolf housed his alchemists in Golden Lane, where they worked to make gold. Scholars have found no evidence for this. The emperor's alchemists worked in other parts of the castle and in houses in the city. Even so, the legend remains popular, and many visitors associate the lane with alchemy. In the early twentieth century, the writer Franz Kafka lived and worked for a short time at house number twenty-two, which belonged to his sister. Today, Golden Lane is a museum street. Some houses show how people lived there in other hundreds of years, and the colourful little buildings attract many visitors.

[long-pause]

{img:39-alchemists-arriving-prague-courtyard-cart}

Alchemists from all over Europe travelled to Prague hoping to gain the emperor's support. News of Rudolf's interest in alchemy spread across Europe. Alchemists from Germany, Italy, Poland, England and elsewhere travelled to Prague. Some were real scholars and skilled workers. Others were adventurers or frauds hoping to make their fortune. Scholars estimate that dozens of alchemists worked in Prague at other times during Rudolf's reign. Many received payments, titles or labs. Others were disappointed. Records from the imperial court show payments to alchemists and purchases of materials. Some alchemists worked for nobles in Bohemia rather than for the emperor directly. One key patron was the nobleman Vilém of Rožmberk, who had his own alchemy labs. Even so, life at court could be not sure. Alchemists who failed to produce results could fall from favour or be jailed. The atmosphere of Prague around 1600 was a mixture of real learning, hope, competition and suspicion, in a city full of new ideas.

[long-pause]

{img:40-sendivogius-polish-alchemist-demonstration}

The Polish alchemist Michael Sendivogius was one of the most respected alchemists at Rudolf's court. Michael Sendivogius was born in 1566 in Poland. He studied at some universities and became a diplomat and alchemist. He worked at the court of Rudolf the Second in Prague and later for King Sigismund the Third of Poland. According to stories told after his death, he performed a successful change of metal into gold in front of the emperor, who rewarded him. Scholars treat these stories with caution, since they come from later sources. Sendivogius wrote a much read book called A New Light of Alchemy, published in 1604. In it, he described a strange substance in the air, which he called the food of life, essential for life and combustion. Some scholars have suggested that this idea anticipated the later discovery of oxygen, although this reading is debated. Even so, his book was translated into many languages and reprinted dozens of times. Sendivogius died in 1636. He was one of the most influential alchemy writers of the 1600s.

[long-pause]

{img:41-tycho-brahe-kepler-observatory-instruments}

Rudolf's court also supported astronomers, such as Tycho Brahe and Johannes Kepler. Rudolf the Second's interest in the secrets of nature extended to astronomy and astrology. In 1599, the Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe arrived in Prague as imperial mathematician. He had made the most accurate notes of the stars and planets before the invention of the telescope. Tycho was also keen on alchemy and had a lab at his earlier observatory in Denmark. After Tycho died in 1601, his assistant Johannes Kepler succeeded him. Using Tycho's notes, Kepler found that planets move in elliptical orbits. He published these findings in 1609 in a book called New Astronomy. Scholars see this as a major step in the Scientific Revolution. Even so, Kepler also cast horoscopes for the emperor and others, as was expected of an imperial mathematician. Astronomy, astrology and alchemy were closely connected at the time. All of them sought to grasp the hidden order of nature and its influence on human life.

[long-pause]

{img:42-prague-old-town-square-astronomical-clock}

Prague's old astronomical clock reflects the city's long interest in the heavens and in clever mechanisms. The Prague astronomical clock, called the Orloj, is mounted on the tower of the Old Town Hall. Its oldest parts date from 1410, making it one of the oldest working astronomical clocks in the world. It shows the positions of the Sun and the Moon, the signs of the zodiac and some systems of time. Later additions include moving figures of the apostles, which appear every hour. In Rudolf's time, the clock was already almost two hundred years old. It represents the long tradition of learning about the heavens in Prague. The city was home to scholars, instrument makers and clockmakers, as well as alchemists. Even so, the clock has been damaged and repaired many times, most recently after the Second World War and in a major restoration completed in 2018. Today, crowds gather in the Old Town Square every hour to watch the figures move, much as people have done for hundreds of years.

[long-pause]

{img:43-john-dee-scholar-white-beard-study-books}

John Dee was an English mathematician, astronomer and adviser to Queen Elizabeth the First who also studied alchemy. John Dee was born in London in 1527. He studied at Cambridge and became one of the most learned men in England. He was an expert in mathematics, navigation, astronomy and geography. He advised English explorers about routes to Asia and promoted the idea of a British empire at sea. He also served as an adviser to Queen Elizabeth the First and is said to have chosen the date of her coronation using astrology. Dee built one of the largest private libraries in England at his house in Mortlake, near London, with thousands of books and old texts. Dee combined science with mystical interests. He studied alchemy, astrology and the possibility of communicating with angels. Even so, he was a real scholar, and his mathematical work was respected. His life shows how science and mysticism could exist side by side in the 1500s. Some of his books survive today in libraries in Britain.

[long-pause]

{img:44-edward-kelley-scrying-mirror-crystal}

Edward Kelley worked with John Dee, claiming to receive messages from angels through a crystal and a mirror. In 1582, John Dee met Edward Kelley, a younger man who claimed to be able to see and hear angels in a crystal stone or a polished mirror. This practice is called scrying. Kelley would describe what he saw, and Dee recorded the messages in detail. These sessions went on for some years. Dee's records survive and fill many pages of old texts. Scholars debate whether Kelley believed in these visions or was deceiving Dee. Kelley had a doubtful past, and according to some reports, he had been punished earlier in life for forgery. Even so, Dee trusted him for a long time. A black obsidian mirror believed to have belonged to Dee is now in the British Museum in London. It was probably made in Mexico by the Aztecs and brought to Europe after the Spanish conquest. This mirror links Dee's strange studies with the wider world of the 1500s.

[long-pause]

{img:45-travelling-coach-forest-road-central-europe}

In 1583, Dee and Kelley travelled to Central Europe, where they visited the courts of Poland and Bohemia. In 1583, John Dee, Edward Kelley and their families left England for Central Europe. They hoped to find patrons keen on their angelic messages and alchemy. They visited the court of King Stephen Báthory of Poland and then travelled to Prague, where they met Emperor Rudolf the Second. Scholars know about their journey from Dee's diaries and letters. Rudolf received them, but did not become their main patron. They later found support from the Bohemian nobleman Vilém of Rožmberk, who gave them a place to work at his castle in Třeboň. Even so, the relationship between Dee and Kelley became increasingly strained. In 1587, Kelley reported that the angels had instructed the two men to share all, such as their wives. Dee was deeply troubled but reportedly agreed for a time. The partnership broke up soon afterwards. In 1589, Dee returned to England, while Kelley remained in Bohemia.

[long-pause]

{img:46-kelley-knighted-bohemian-castle-hall}

Edward Kelley became wealthy and was given a noble title in Bohemia, but he later fell from favour. After Dee left, Edward Kelley stayed in Bohemia and concentrated on alchemy. He claimed to be able to perform transmutation and impressed many people. Emperor Rudolf granted him a title of nobility, and he became known as Sir Edward Kelley. He acquired estates, houses and great wealth. His fame reached England, where Queen Elizabeth's advisers tried to persuade him to return and make gold for England. Even so, Kelley's fortunes changed suddenly. In 1591, Rudolf had him arrested and jailed at Křivoklát Castle. The reasons are not entirely clear. Some sources say he killed an official in a duel, while others suggest he failed to deliver the promised gold. He was released for a time and then jailed again. According to tradition, Kelley died in 1597 after injuring himself while trying to escape from prison. Scholars are uncertain about the exact circumstances of his death.

[long-pause]

{img:47-mortlake-house-thames-library-ruined}

When John Dee returned to England in 1589, he found that much of his library had been damaged or stolen. During Dee's absence, his house at Mortlake had been entered. Many of his books, old texts and instruments had been taken or damaged. Scholars believe that some of the losses were caused by people Dee had trusted to look after the house, while others may have been the result of theft. Dee was deeply upset and spent years trying to recover his books. Some later appeared in other collections. He received a position as warden of a college in Manchester in 1595. But he was not happy there and faced financial hardies. Even so, his reputation remained mixed. Some admired his learning, while others suspected him of dealing with spirits. After the death of Queen Elizabeth in 1603, he lost royal support. He died in poverty at Mortlake around 1608 or 1609. Scholars have reassessed Dee in recent decades, recognising his importance as a scholar, mathematician and collector, as well as his interest in mysticism.

[long-pause]

{img:48-dee-manuscript-angelic-alphabet-pages}

John Dee's records of his sessions with Kelley include a strange language that Kelley said came from angels. In his records, John Dee wrote down messages that Kelley said were dictated by angels. Some were written in a language that Dee called the angelic language. Today it is often called Enochian. It has its own alphabet and grammar. Scholars and linguists have studied these texts. Most conclude that the language was invented, probably by Kelley, though it is internally in line in some ways. Dee's old texts recording these sessions are now kept in the British Library and the Bodleian Library in Oxford. Some were printed in 1659, long after Dee's death, by a scholar named Méric Casaubon, who presented them as evidence that Dee had been deceived by evil spirits. Even so, the old texts are key historical documents. They show how a respected scholar of the 1500s searched for knowledge in many directions. The story of Dee and Kelley has inspired many novels, plays and an opera.

[long-pause]

{img:49-fraud-alchemist-hollow-stirring-rod-trick}

Fraudulent alchemists used clever tricks to make it appear that they had created gold. Many people around 1600 claimed to be able to make gold. Some were sincere but mistaken. Others were deliberate frauds. They used many tricks to fool their audiences. One common method used a hollow stirring rod filled with gold dust and sealed with wax. When the rod was used to stir a hot crucible, the wax melted and the gold fell into the mixture. Another trick involved crucibles with false bottoms hiding gold. Some frauds used coins coated with mercury or other substances, which could be changed in appearance. Scholars know about these tricks from books written to warn the public. The English playwright Ben Jonson mocked such deceivers in his comedy The Alchemist, first performed in 1610. Paintings of the period also show fraudulent alchemists. Even so, it was not always easy to tell frauds from sincere workers. Some alchemists believed their own mistaken results. The hardy of testing claims made it easy for deception to flourish.

[long-pause]

{img:50-gilded-gallows-stuttgart-1597-town-square}

In 1597, an alchemist who failed to make gold was hanged on a gilded gallows in Stuttgart. Some patrons punished alchemists who deceived them. One famous case took place in Stuttgart, in the German duchy of Württemberg. An alchemist named Georg Honauer had promised Duke Frederick the First that he could turn iron into gold. The duke provided him with large amounts of iron and money. When Honauer failed and tried to flee, he was arrested. In 1597, he was hanged on a gallows covered with gold-coloured metal, reportedly made from the very iron he had promised to transform. Gilded gallows were used in some cases to mock failed alchemists. Records of such punishments survive in German archives. Even so, these cases were not common. Most failed alchemists were simply dismissed or jailed. The duke of Württemberg himself went on to support other alchemists. The story of the gilded gallows became famous as a warning about the dangers of promising gold to powerful patrons.

[long-pause]

{img:51-alchemist-locked-cell-castle-tower}

Some rulers jailed alchemists to force them to reveal the secret of making gold. Alchemists who claimed to know how to make gold could become valuable prisoners. Some rulers held alchemists captive, hoping to force them to work. The case of Edward Kelley in Bohemia is one example. Another famous case involved a Scottish alchemist named Alexander Seton, who was reportedly jailed and tortured in Saxony around 1603 by the elector Christian the Second, who wanted his secret. According to later accounts, Michael Sendivogius helped him escape, but Seton died soon afterwards. Scholars are cautious about these stories, since many details come from later, dramatic accounts. Even so, they reflect real dangers. In the early eighteenth century, the young apothecary Johann Friedrich Böttger was held by Augustus the Strong of Saxony after claiming he could make gold. His captivity would lead to an unexpected discovery. These cases show how alchemy could become entangled with power, greed and the hopes of rulers seeking wealth.

[long-pause]

{img:52-coins-mint-assay-balance-officials}

Mints and assayers tested gold and silver carefully, and fake alchemy gold could threaten the currency. In the 1500s, coins were made of real gold and silver. Their value depended on the purity of the metal. Governments employed assayers to test metals at mints. They used methods such as the touchstone, where a metal was rubbed on a dark stone and the colour of the streak compared with samples. A more precise method was cupellation, which separated precious metals from other metals by heating. Scholars say that these methods were highly worked out by 1600. A famous treatise on mining and metals, De Re Metallica by Georgius Agricola, published in 1556, described them in detail with many illustrations. Even so, false gold made from alloys could fool people outside the mints. Rulers worried that it might enter circulation. This was one reason why laws against multiplying gold existed. Assayers were therefore some of the main judges of alchemy claims. Many alchemy gold samples failed their tests.

[long-pause]

{img:53-michael-maier-physician-writing-emblems}

Michael Maier, doctor to Emperor Rudolf, wrote alchemy books combining images, poems and music. Michael Maier was born in 1568 in Holstein, in northern Germany. He studied medicine and became a doctor at the court of Rudolf the Second, who gave him the title of count palatine. After Rudolf's death, Maier travelled and published many books on alchemy. His most famous work, Atalanta Fugiens, was published in 1617. It contains fifty emblems, each combining an engraved image, a short poem, an explanatory text and a piece of music for three voices. The music takes the form of fugues, in which the voices chase one another, like the characters of the Greek myth of Atalanta. Scholars describe it as one of the first multimedia books. In recent years, scholars and musicians have recorded the music. Even so, the meaning of the emblems is complex and symbolic. Maier believed that alchemy contained deep truths hidden in ancient myths. He died in 1622 in Magdeburg, during the early years of the Thirty Years' War.

[long-pause]

{img:54-royal-treasury-empty-chests-chamberlain}

Many rulers who supported alchemy hoped it would solve their financial problems. In the 1500s and 1600s, European rulers faced rising costs for armies, courts and buildings. Wars were above all costly. Taxes were often unpopular and hard to collect. Alchemy offered the hope of a simple solution: making gold to fill the treasury. This hope explains much of the support given to alchemists by princes. Some rulers spent large sums on labs and alchemists. Records from courts in Saxony, Württemberg, Hesse and Bohemia show regular payments. Even so, no ruler ever gained gold from alchemy. Some lost large amounts of money. Yet support for alchemy was not always irrational. Alchemists also produced useful things, such as cures, dyes, metal treatments and knowledge of mining. Scholars such as Tara Nummedal have studied these relationships closely. They found that alchemists often worked under contracts, with promises of hands-on services as well as gold. Alchemy was part of the economy of the courts.

[long-pause]

{img:55-emerald-tablet-green-stone-inscription-candle}

Alchemists honoured a short, strange text called the Emerald Tablet, attributed to Hermes Trismegistus. The Emerald Tablet is a brief text that was seen as one of the main in alchemy. It was attributed to Hermes Trismegistus, a legendary wise figure combining the Greek god Hermes and the Egyptian god Thoth. The oldest known versions are in Arabic and date from around the eighth or ninth century. The text was translated into Latin in the 1100s. It contains the famous phrase often summarised as as above, so below, expressing the idea that the heavens and the earth reflect each other. Alchemists read it as a secret description of the making of the philosopher's stone. The legend of an actual emerald tablet, found in a tomb, appears in later stories and has no basis in evidence. Even so, the text inspired many commentaries. In the 1600s, Isaac Newton made his own English translation of the Emerald Tablet, which survives among his papers.

[long-pause]

{img:56-ouroboros-serpent-eating-tail-manuscript}

The ouroboros, a serpent eating its own tail, was a common alchemy symbol of unity and endless cycles. The ouroboros is an image of a serpent or dragon forming a circle by swallowing its own tail. It is one of the oldest symbols associated with alchemy. It appears in a Greek alchemy manuscript from the Byzantine period, with the words one is all. The symbol represented the idea that all matter is one and that nature moves in endless cycles of destruction and renewal. Scholars say that such symbols appear in ancient Egyptian art and in other cultures. In alchemy, it could also represent the repeated distillation of a substance, in which vapour rises and falls back again. Even so, alchemy symbols often had some meanings at once. Their reading depended on the author and the context. The ouroboros remains a popular symbol today. It appears in art, literature and modern designs. In the history of chemistry, it is at times connected with the story of the German chemist August Kekulé, who said in 1890 that a dream of a serpent biting its tail helped him grasp the structure of benzene.

[long-pause]

{img:57-green-lion-devouring-sun-painting}

Alchemists used strange images such as a green lion devouring the sun to describe chemical processes. Alchemy books and old texts are full of remarkable pictures. Kings and queens, dragons, lions, eagles, suns and moons appear in many combinations. One famous image shows a green lion devouring the sun. Scholars and chemists have readed many of these images as accounts of real chemical reactions. The green lion may represent a strong solvent or a substance such as impure vitriol, while the sun represents gold. The image may show gold being dissolved. Another common image, a king and queen joined together, often represented the combination of two substances, such as sulphur and mercury. Even so, the meanings were deliberately hidden. Alchemists believed that their knowledge was precious and should not be easily available. The historian Lawrence Principe has recreated some such processes in a lab, following the symbolic instructions. He found that some images corresponded to real reactions that produce striking results, such as a metallic tree growing in a flask.

[long-pause]

{img:58-splendor-solis-illuminated-manuscript-gold}

The Splendor Solis is one of the most beautiful illuminated alchemy papers, made in the 1500s. The Splendor Solis, meaning the splendour of the sun, is an illustrated alchemy treatise. The earliest surviving copy was made in Germany around 1532 to 1535 and is now kept in Berlin. A later and above all famous copy, dated 1582, is in the British Library in London. It contains twenty-two full-page paintings, richly decorated with gold. The images show alchemy stages through symbolic scenes, such as a knight standing on two fountains, a king drowning in the sea and a series of flasks containing birds and animals. Around the flasks are scenes of daily life, such as people working, children playing and travellers on roads. Scholars value the manuscript both as a work of art and as a source for alchemy ideas. Even so, its exact author is unknown. The text was once attributed to a legendary alchemist named Salomon Trismosin. The paintings show how alchemy combined science, art and spiritual imagery.

[long-pause]

{img:59-rosicrucian-manifesto-printed-pages-1614}

In 1614, strange pamphlets announced the existence of a secret brotherhood of the Rosy Cross. Between 1614 and 1616, three pamphlets were published in Germany describing a secret society called the Brotherhood of the Rosy Cross, or the Rosicrucians. They told the story of a legendary founder, Christian Rosenkreutz, who had supposedly travelled to the East to learn hidden wisdom. The brotherhood was said to work for the reform of knowledge and society. The third pamphlet, The Chymical Wedding of Christian Rosenkreutz, published in 1616, is an allegorical story full of alchemy imagery. Scholars believe it was written by a young Lutheran theologian named Johann Valentin Andreae. The pamphlets caused great excitement across Europe. Many people tried to contact the brotherhood. Even so, there is no evidence that it existed as an organised group at the time. Scholars see the Rosicrucian manifestos as part of a wider movement of religious and intellectual reform. They show how closely alchemy was linked with hopes for a better understanding of the world.

[long-pause]

{img:60-alchemical-emblem-engraving-garden-figures}

Printed emblem books made alchemy imagery easy to find in the early seventeenth century. In the early seventeenth century, publishers in Germany produced many illustrated alchemy books. Engravers such as Matthäus Merian created detailed images for them. These books presented alchemy ideas through emblems, combinations of pictures and short texts. Readers were invited to contemplate the images and discover their hidden meanings. These books were costly and were bought by educated readers, doctors and nobles. Many of the most famous alchemy images known today come from this period. They include the engravings in Michael Maier's Atalanta Fugiens and in collections such as the Musaeum Hermeticum, published in Frankfurt in 1625. Even so, the purpose of these images was not only decorative. They expressed the belief that nature contained hidden meanings that could be understood through symbols. Today, these engravings are admired as works of art. They appear in museums, books and exhibitions about the history of science and the imagination.

[long-pause]

{img:61-isabella-cortese-venetian-book-recipes}

In 1561, a book of secrets attributed to a woman named Isabella Cortese was published in Venice. In 1561, a book called The Secrets, in Italian, was published in Venice under the name of Isabella Cortese. It contained recipes for cures, cosmetics, perfumes, dyes and alchemy processes. The book was much loved and was reprinted many times. In its introduction, the author advised readers to avoid wasting their money on false alchemists and to follow hands-on methods. Scholars are uncertain who Isabella Cortese seriously was. Little is known about her life. Some scholars have suggested that the name may have been used by a publisher. Even so, the book shows that women were recognised as workers of chemical arts. Books of secrets were a popular genre in the 1500s. They combined useful household knowledge with the promise of hidden wonders. Readers used them to make cures and cosmetics at home. Such books helped spread chemical methods beyond universities and courts into common households.

[long-pause]

{img:62-marie-meurdrac-chemistry-book-1666-women}

In 1666, the French chemist Marie Meurdrac published a chemistry book written above all for women. Marie Meurdrac was a Frenchwoman who lived in the 1600s. In 1666, she published a book called La Chymie charitable et facile, en faveur des dames, meaning Charitable and Easy Chemistry for Ladies. It described lab tools, methods and recipes for cures and cosmetics. In her preface, she wrote that she had hesitated before publishing, since teaching was not usually seen as a woman's role. She concluded that minds have no sex and that knowledge should be shared. Scholars regard her book as one of the first chemistry books written by a woman. It was reprinted some times and translated into German and Italian. Even so, Meurdrac's hands-on chemistry was rooted in the same traditions as alchemy. She used distillation, sublimation and other alchemy methods. Her book shows how alchemy was slowly changing into chemistry during the 1600s. It also shows that women contributed to that change, even though their work was often overlooked.

[long-pause]

{img:63-noblewoman-household-still-room-herbs}

Many women practised chemistry in household still rooms, making cures for their families and neighbours. In the 1500s and 1600s, many large households had a room called a still room. There, women of the household distilled herbal waters, prepared cures, made preserves and produced cosmetics. Noblewomen often supervised this work and kept recipe books recording their methods. Many of these old texts survive in libraries and archives. Scholars such as Elaine Leong have studied them. They show that women exchanged recipes, tested cures and recorded results. Some recipe books contain alchemy processes alongside cooking and medicine. Even so, this work was rarely recognised as science at the time. It was seen as part of household management. Yet the knowledge was real and valuable. Women in still rooms provided much of the medical care available to families and local communities, above all in the countryside, where doctors were rare. Their work shows that the hands-on chemistry of the period reached far beyond the labs of alchemists and the courts of princes.

[long-pause]

{img:64-women-alchemists-engraving-mutus-liber}

Some alchemy images show a man and a woman working together, reflecting the role of couples in alchemy. Some alchemy books show men and women working side by side. One famous example is the Mutus Liber, meaning the silent book, published in France in 1677. It contains fifteen engravings with almost no words. Some plates show a man and a woman working together at the furnace and collecting dew from the fields with cloths. Alchemy work was at times carried out by married couples. The legend of Nicolas Flamel included his wife Pernelle as a partner in his work. Real couples also worked together in labs, although records of women's contributions are often limited. Even so, the images had symbolic meanings as well. The union of male and female represented the joining of opposite principles, such as sulphur and mercury, or sun and moon. Scholars suggest that these images reflected both real partnerships and the symbolic language of alchemy. They remind readers that alchemy was not only a solitary pursuit.

[long-pause]

{img:65-woman-alchemist-modern-portrait-museum}

Scholars are increasingly rediscovering the role of women in the history of alchemy and chemistry. For a long time, histories of alchemy focused mainly on famous men. In recent decades, scholars have looked more closely at women's contributions. They have studied recipe books, letters, court records and printed works. They have found evidence of women working as workers, patrons, assistants, writers and collectors. Some, like Anna of Saxony, ran large labs. Others, like Marie Meurdrac, wrote influential books. Many more worked quietly in households and still rooms, leaving few traces. The boundaries between alchemy, medicine and household knowledge were not fixed. Women's work was often placed on the household side and therefore less valued. Even so, this research has changed understanding of early science. It shows that knowledge was produced in many places, not only in universities and courts. Museums and exhibitions now include these stories more often. The women of early chemistry are slowly taking their place in the history of science.

[long-pause]

{img:66-robert-boyle-laboratory-air-pump-1660}

Robert Boyle, often called a founder of modern chemistry, also searched for the philosopher's stone. Robert Boyle was born in Ireland in 1627 and became one of the leading scientists of 1600s England. He was a founding member of the Royal Society, set up in London in 1660. He is famous for Boyle's law, which describes the relationship between the pressure and volume of a gas. In 1661, he published The Sceptical Chymist, in which he criticised the traditional theories of the four elements and the three principles. Scholars once saw this book as a rejection of alchemy. More recent research, above all by Lawrence Principe and William Newman, has shown that Boyle was very keen on making gold. He believed it might be possible and conducted many tests. He even supported the repeal of the old English law against multiplying gold in 1689. Even so, Boyle insisted on careful tests and clear reporting. His work shows that alchemy and early modern chemistry were closely connected rather than opposed.

[long-pause]

{img:67-isaac-newton-manuscripts-candle-cambridge}

Isaac Newton wrote more than a million words about alchemy. Isaac Newton, born in 1643, is famous for his laws of motion, his theory of gravity and his work on light and mathematics. He was also deeply involved in alchemy. Over about thirty years, he wrote more than a million words on the subject, copying texts, making notes and recording tests. He built a lab in his garden at Trinity College, Cambridge. According to his assistant, Newton at times kept his furnace burning for weeks during his tests. Scholars long ignored or downplayed this side of Newton's work. They believed it did not fit his image as a founder of modern science. In recent decades, scholars have studied his alchemy papers closely. The Chymistry of Isaac Newton project at Indiana University has published many of them online. Even so, the meaning of his alchemy is still debated. Newton seems to have hoped that alchemy could reveal hidden forces in nature, like gravity.

[long-pause]

{img:68-sothebys-auction-1936-newton-papers-room}

Newton's alchemy papers were sold at auction in 1936, and the economist John Maynard Keynes bought many of them. After Newton's death in 1727, his papers passed to his family. For many years, his alchemy papers were seen as embarrassing and unfit for publication. In 1936, a large collection of Newton's papers was sold at an auction at Sotheby's in London. The British economist John Maynard Keynes bought many of the alchemy papers. After studying them, Keynes wrote a famous essay called Newton, the Man, published in 1947 after his death. In it, he described Newton as not the first of the age of reason, but the last of the magicians. This phrase changed how people viewed Newton. Keynes left his set to King's College, Cambridge, where it remains today. Other papers were bought by the scholar Abraham Yahuda and are now in the National Library of Israel in Jerusalem. Even so, many scholars now argue that Newton's alchemy was not simply magic. It was part of his real investigation into the nature of matter.

[long-pause]

{img:69-newton-hair-mercury-analysis-modern-lab}

Analysis of Newton's hair has shown high levels of mercury, possibly from his alchemy tests. In 1693, Newton suffered a period of serious illness, with insomnia, loss of appetite and suspicion of his friends. His letters from this time show distress. In 1979, researchers analysed some hairs said to have come from Newton. They found high levels of mercury, lead and other metals. Some scientists suggested that his illness might have been caused by mercury poisoning from his alchemy tests. Newton often heated mercury, which produces toxic vapour. Scholars note, yet, that this explanation is uncertain. The hair samples may not all have been genuine, and Newton's symptoms could have had other causes, such as overwork or emotional stress. Even so, the findings show the real dangers of alchemy work. Many alchemists handled toxic substances without knowing the risks. Newton recovered and went on to become Warden and later Master of the Royal Mint in London, where he worked on coinage and the detection of counterfeit coins, a fitting task for a former alchemist.

[long-pause]

{img:70-royal-society-meeting-seventeenth-century-room}

The Royal Society encouraged tests and open reporting, which slowly changed how chemistry was practised. The Royal Society of London was founded in 1660 to promote knowledge through test. Its motto, Nullius in verba, meaning take nobody's word for it, expressed the idea that claims should be tested by observation. Members met regularly to discuss and demonstrate tests. They published results in the journal Philosophical Transactions, first issued in 1665, which is still published today. This emphasis on sharing results openly contrasted with the secrecy of traditional alchemy. Many members, such as Boyle and Newton, were keen on making gold. Yet they increasingly preferred clear language and reproducible tests. Even so, the change was gradual. Secret and open practices existed side by side for many decades. Other such societies were founded in other countries, such as the French Academy of Sciences in 1666. These institutions helped transform alchemy into the modern science of chemistry. The word alchemy itself slowly came to mean only the search for gold, while chemistry described all else.

[long-pause]

{img:71-cambridge-trinity-college-garden-evening}

Newton's alchemy lab once stood beside his rooms at Trinity College, Cambridge. Newton lived at Trinity College, Cambridge, from 1661 until 1696. His rooms were near the Great Gate of the college. According to accounts by his assistant, Humphrey Newton, who was not related to him, Isaac Newton had a lab in the garden nearby. There he carried out chemical and alchemy tests, often late into the night. Humphrey wrote that Newton rarely went to bed before two or three in the morning and at times worked until dawn. Scholars use these accounts, together with Newton's notebooks, to reconstruct his working life. Even so, the lab itself no longer exists. The garden has changed over the hundreds of years. Today, Trinity College is a quiet place of stone courts, lawns and libraries. Visitors can see an apple tree said to be descended from the tree at Newton's family home that inspired his thoughts on gravity. Few realise that the same scholar spent many hours watching flasks glow over a furnace in search of hidden secrets.

[long-pause]

{img:72-hennig-brand-glowing-phosphorus-flask-dark}

In 1669, the alchemist Hennig Brand found phosphorus while searching for the philosopher's stone. Hennig Brand was a German merchant and alchemist living in Hamburg. Around 1669, he began testing with large amounts of human urine, which he believed might contain the secret of the philosopher's stone. He boiled it down, let it stand and heated the residue strongly. The result was a white, waxy substance that glowed in the dark with a pale green light. Brand had found phosphorus. It was the first chemical element to be found by a known person in recorded history. The name phosphorus comes from Greek words meaning light bearer. Brand kept his method secret for a while and sold some of the substance. Later, other chemists, such as Robert Boyle, found how to make it. Even so, Brand did not find the philosopher's stone. Yet his discovery shows how alchemy searches could lead to real scientific findings. A famous 1700s painting by Joseph Wright of Derby imagines the moment of discovery.

[long-pause]

{img:73-bottger-porcelain-white-cup-meissen-kiln}

An alchemist jailed to make gold helped create Europe's first true porcelain in the early eighteenth century. Johann Friedrich Böttger was a young apothecary's apprentice in Berlin who claimed to be able to make gold. In 1701, he fled to Saxony, where the ruler, Augustus the Strong, held him in custody and ordered him to produce gold. Böttger could not succeed. Instead, he was set to work with the scientist Ehrenfried Walther von Tschirnhaus, who was trying to make porcelain. At that time, fine white porcelain came only from China and Japan and was extremely valuable in Europe. Around 1708 and 1709, the team succeeded in producing true hard-paste porcelain. In 1710, Augustus founded a porcelain factory in the town of Meissen. Porcelain became known as white gold because of its value. Even so, Böttger remained under close watch for the rest of his short life and died in 1719. The Meissen factory still operates today and is famous for its crossed swords mark.

[long-pause]

{img:74-lavoisier-laboratory-precise-balances-1780s}

In the late eighteenth century, Antoine Lavoisier helped establish modern chemistry based on elements and careful measurement. Antoine Lavoisier was a French chemist who lived from 1743 to 1794. Working with his wife, Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier, who assisted with tests, translations and illustrations, he transformed chemistry. He showed that combustion involved combining with a gas, which he named oxygen. He emphasised that mass is conserved in chemical reactions, which he showed by careful weighing. In 1789, he published a textbook listing chemical elements as substances that could not be broken down further. Scholars see this as a major step in the creation of modern chemistry. Lavoisier's system replaced the old ideas of four elements and principles. Even so, his list still contained some errors, such as light and heat. Lavoisier built on the work of many earlier chemists, some of whom had been involved with alchemy. The methods of distillation, weighing and careful observation worked out by alchemists over hundreds of years became tools of the new science.

[long-pause]

{img:75-elements-periodic-table-old-chart-classroom}

Modern chemistry showed that metals are elements that cannot be turned into one another by chemical means. During the 1800s, chemists identified many new elements and studied their properties. In 1869, the Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev published a periodic table, arranging the elements in order of their atomic weights and grouping those with matching properties. He even predicted elements that had not yet been found. This system showed clearly that gold, silver, lead and other metals are distinct elements. Ordinary chemical reactions cannot change one element into one more. This meant that the alchemists' dream of making gold from lead by chemical means was impossible. Even so, the story did not end there. In the early twentieth century, scientists found radioactivity and learned that atoms could change into other atoms through nuclear processes. In 1901, Ernest Rutherford and Frederick Soddy observed that radioactive thorium was changing into one more element. Soddy reportedly exclaimed that it was transmutation, and Rutherford warned him not to call it that.

[long-pause]

{img:76-particle-accelerator-modern-lab-quiet}

In the 1900s, physicists turned tiny amounts of other elements into gold using nuclear reactions. Modern physics has made a form of transmutation possible. In nuclear reactions, the number of protons in an atom's nucleus can change, turning one element into one more. In 1980, a team led by the American chemist Glenn Seaborg at the Lawrence Berkeley Lab produced a tiny amount of gold from bismuth using a particle accelerator. Scholars of science often mention this test as a symbolic fulfilment of the alchemists' dream. Even so, the amount produced was extremely small, only a few thousand atoms. The cost was enormous. The gold was also radioactive in part. Making gold in this way is far more costly than buying it. Other tests have produced gold atoms in particle collisions as well. Yet the dream that inspired alchemists for hundreds of years has been realised only in a form they could never have imagined. The search for the philosopher's stone ended not in riches, but in a deeper understanding of matter.

[long-pause]

{img:77-chemistry-laboratory-glassware-today-flasks}

Many tools and methods of modern chemistry labs have their roots in alchemy. Modern chemistry labs still use methods that alchemists worked out for hundreds of years. Distillation, filtration, crystallisation and sublimation are taught to every chemistry student. The water bath, still called a bain-marie in French, is used to heat substances gently. Flasks, beakers and condensers are descendants of the alembics and retorts of the past. The language of chemistry also contains many words from alchemy and Arabic, such as alkali, alcohol and elixir. Even so, the theories behind modern chemistry are very other. Today, chemists grasp atoms, molecules and chemical bonds. They no longer search for a single substance that can perfect all metals. Yet the curiosity, patience and careful practice of alchemists live on. Scholars such as Lawrence Principe argue that alchemy should be seen as an early stage of chemistry, rather than as a separate or foolish pursuit. In labs around the world, the long tradition goes on in new forms.

[long-pause]

{img:78-golden-lane-prague-today-visitors-evening}

Today, Golden Lane in Prague attracts visitors who come to imagine the world of Rudolf's alchemists. Prague Castle is now the seat of the President of the Czech Republic and one of the largest castle complexes in the world. Every year, millions of visitors explore its courtyards, palaces and cathedral. Golden Lane is one of its most popular streets. The tiny houses have been restored to show how people lived there in other periods, from the sixteenth to the 1900s. Some display furniture, tools and costumes. The association of Golden Lane with alchemists is a legend. Yet it reflects the real importance of alchemy at Rudolf's court. Elsewhere in the city, museums and guided tours tell stories of alchemists, astronomers and emperors. Even so, much of Rudolf's world has disappeared. His great collection is scattered across museums in Vienna, Stockholm and elsewhere. In the evening, when the crowds leave, the lane becomes quiet. The small houses glow softly against the old castle wall.

[long-pause]

{img:79-library-old-alchemical-books-reading-room}

Libraries around the world preserve thousands of alchemy papers and printed books. The writings of alchemists are preserved in many libraries. The British Library and the Wellcome Collection in London hold large collections of alchemy papers. The Ritman Library in Amsterdam, also known as the Embassy of the Free Mind, specialises in Hermetic and alchemy texts. The Science History Institute in Philadelphia holds alchemy books and a famous collection of paintings showing alchemists at work. Scholars use these collections to study how alchemy worked out and spread. Many old texts have now been digitised and can be viewed online. Even so, many texts have never been fully studied. Some are written in hard languages and codes, and others survive only in single copies. Researchers continue to discover new facts in these archives. The quiet reading rooms where these books are kept are very different from the smoky labs where they were once used. Yet the texts still carry the hopes and questions of their authors.

[long-pause]

{img:80-painting-alchemist-at-work-museum-gallery}

Many paintings from the 1500s and 1600s show alchemists at work in their labs. Alchemists were a popular subject for painters, above all in the Netherlands and Flanders in the 1500s and 1600s. Artists such as Pieter Bruegel the Elder, David Teniers the Younger and Adriaen van Ostade painted alchemists in cluttered workshops. Some paintings are humorous and critical. A famous print after Bruegel, from 1558, shows an alchemist who has ruined his family with his obsession, while his wife searches an empty purse. Other paintings are more respectful and show careful scholars at work. Scholars use these images to study lab tools and the public image of alchemy. Many such paintings are displayed in museums, such as the Science History Institute in Philadelphia, which holds a large set. Even so, paintings are not photographs. Artists often exaggerated or combined details for effect. Yet they capture the atmosphere of the period: the furnaces, the vessels, the books, and the mixture of hope and folly that surrounded the search for gold.

[long-pause]

{img:81-philosophers-stone-book-children-reading}

The philosopher's stone remains famous today through novels, films and popular culture. The idea of the philosopher's stone has went on to capture the imagination long after alchemy declined. It appears in many novels, films and games. One of the best-known modern examples is the first Harry Potter book by J. K. Rowling, published in 1997 as Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone. In the story, the stone is created by Nicolas Flamel, the real Parisian scribe whose legend began hundreds of years after his death. Such stories have introduced the history of alchemy to many young readers. Museums have held exhibitions about the real history behind the legends. In 2017, the British Library organised an exhibition marking twenty years of Harry Potter, which included alchemy papers and objects. Even so, popular images of alchemy often show it as pure magic. The real history is more complex. It involved careful work, real thought and hands-on discoveries, alongside dreams of gold and eternal life.

[long-pause]

{img:82-psychologist-study-alchemy-symbols-books}

In the 1900s, the psychologist Carl Jung studied alchemy as a source of symbols of the human mind. The Swiss psychiatrist Carl Gustav Jung, who lived from 1875 to 1961, became fascinated by alchemy. He collected alchemy books and wrote some works on the subject, such as Psychology and Alchemy, published in 1944. Jung believed that alchemy images, such as the union of opposites, the stages of blackness, whiteness and redness, and the transformation of base materials, expressed processes of psychological growth. He saw the search for the philosopher's stone as a symbol of the search for wholeness of the self. Jung's work made alchemy popular among many readers keen on psychology and spirituality. Even so, scholars of science have criticised this reading. They argue that it overlooks the hands-on, chemical side of alchemy, which was central for most workers. Recent research has restored that balance. Yet Jung's books remain influential. His readings show how alchemy goes on to inspire new meanings in other times.

[long-pause]

{img:83-prague-night-river-castle-stars-calm}

The long search for the philosopher's stone left behind a rich legacy of science, art and imagination. For more than fifteen hundred years, alchemists searched for the philosopher's stone. They worked in Alexandria, Baghdad, Toledo, Paris, Prague and London. They built furnaces, invented vessels and worked out methods such as distillation. They wrote books full of symbols and painted books with gold. Kings supported them, frauds deceived, and scholars such as Boyle and Newton took their questions seriously. Scholars now see alchemy as a key part of the history of science. Even so, no alchemist ever made gold from lead. Yet their search led to discoveries in medicine, chemistry and industry, from distilled cures to phosphorus and porcelain. Their questions about the nature of matter were in the end answered by modern chemistry and physics. In Prague, where the emperor Rudolf once welcomed alchemists from all over Europe, the river still flows quietly beneath the castle. On calm nights, the old city glows softly under the stars.

That was the long search for the philosopher's stone: the furnaces of Alexandria and Baghdad, the alembics and the symbols, the emperor's court in Prague, the gilded gallows, the women in their still rooms, Newton's late nights, and the glowing flask of phosphorus.
