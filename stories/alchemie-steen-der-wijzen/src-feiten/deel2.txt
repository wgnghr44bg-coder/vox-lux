TOPIC: Paracelsus and the new medicine
===
IMG: paracelsus-portrait-sword-book-1530s | a sixteenth century Swiss physician with a broad face and cap, holding a book, a sword at his side, standing in a study with flasks
HEAD: The Swiss doctor Paracelsus used alchemy to develop new kinds of medicine in the 1500s.
BODY: Paracelsus was born in 1493 in Einsiedeln, in what is now Switzerland. His real name was Theophrastus von Hohenheim. He trained as a doctor and travelled widely across Europe, learning from doctors, miners, barbers and folk healers. He was known for his fierce temper and his attacks on traditional medicine. In 1527, while teaching at Basel, he reportedly burned a copy of the works of the respected old doctor Avicenna, to show his rejection of old authorities. Paracelsus argued that alchemy should be used mainly to make cures rather than gold. He believed that diseases had specific causes and could be treated with specific chemical cures. Scholars see him as a founder of iatrochemistry, the use of chemistry in medicine. Even so, his writings mixed practical advice with mystical ideas, which makes them hard to read. He died in Salzburg in 1541. But his followers spread his ideas widely in the decades that followed.
===
IMG: dose-makes-poison-apothecary-scale | a small brass apothecary scale weighing a tiny amount of white powder, glass bottles of medicines behind, soft light
HEAD: Paracelsus is remembered for the idea that the dose makes the poison.
BODY: One of the most famous ideas linked with Paracelsus is that all things are poisons, and only the dose determines whether something is harmful or helpful. He wrote this in a work defending his use of strong mineral cures. This principle is still taught in toxicology today, usually summarised as the dose makes the poison. Paracelsus used substances such as mercury, antimony, sulphur and iron in his cures. Some of these were dangerous, and his opponents criticised him for using poisons. Scholars say that mercury was indeed effective against syphilis, a disease that spread far in Europe after the 1490s, although it also caused real side effects. Even so, the idea of carefully controlling doses was key. It encouraged more precise preparation of cures. Paracelsus also wrote about the illnesses of miners, describing diseases caused by breathing harmful dust and fumes. This work is seen as one of the earliest studies of occupational disease.
===
IMG: paracelsian-physician-preparing-mineral-remedy | a physician around 1600 preparing a mineral remedy in a small laboratory, distilling a bright liquid, patients' notes on the table
HEAD: By 1600, followers of Paracelsus were preparing chemical cures across Europe.
BODY: After Paracelsus died, his writings were collected and published, many for the first time in the 1560s and 1570s. His followers, called Paracelsians, promoted chemical cures in universities, courts and towns. They prepared cures by distillation, sublimation and dissolution of minerals and metals. Scholars say that this led to fierce debates. Traditional doctors, following the ancient Greek doctor Galen, relied mainly on herbal cures and the theory of the four humours. Paracelsians argued that chemistry offered stronger and more precise treatments. Gradually, many doctors adopted a mixture of both approaches. In 1618, the London College of Doctors published an official pharmacopoeia, a list of approved cures, which included some chemical cures. Even so, the debate went on for many decades. The rise of chemical medicine shows how alchemy was connected with health and healing. Many people around 1600 who called themselves alchemists were actually making cures rather than searching for gold.
===
IMG: drinkable-gold-elixir-glass-goblet | a glass goblet with a faintly golden liquid on a table beside a small gold leaf and a flask, candlelight
HEAD: Some alchemists tried to make a drinkable form of gold, believing it could heal the body.
BODY: Because gold did not rust or decay, many people believed it had special powers to preserve life and health. Alchemists tried to make a drinkable gold, called aurum potabile. Recipes varied, but many involved dissolving gold or gold leaf in liquids. Some products contained very little real gold. Scholars have found that drinkable gold was sold by doctors and apothecaries and was very costly. It was given to wealthy patients, such as members of royal families. One English doctor, Francis Anthony, became famous in the early 1600s for selling his own version. He was criticised by the College of Doctors. Even so, the idea of drinkable gold remained popular for a long time. Modern research shows that gold has no general health benefits when swallowed, although certain gold compounds were later used to treat some forms of arthritis in the 1900s. The belief in drinkable gold reflected the close link between alchemy, medicine and the dream of long life.
===
IMG: herbal-distillation-spirits-rose-water | copper stills distilling rose petals and herbs in a busy sixteenth century workshop, bottles of coloured herbal waters on shelves
HEAD: Distilled herbal waters and spirits were among the most hands-on products of alchemy methods.
BODY: Distillation was widely used to make herbal waters and medicinal spirits. Rose water, lavender water and other fragrant liquids were made by distilling flowers and herbs. These were used as cures, perfumes and flavourings. Strong alcohol, called aqua vitae, was seen as a powerful medicine and was used to extract the qualities of plants. Printed books on distillation caught on in the 1500s. One of the best known was written by the German doctor Hieronymus Brunschwig, first published in 1500. Scholars say that such books were read by doctors, apothecaries and wealthy households. Many noblewomen ran their own distilleries to make cures for their families and communities. Even so, the effectiveness of many of these cures was limited. Some contained useful substances, while others had little real effect. Yet distillation spread hands-on chemical knowledge widely. The production of spirits such as brandy also worked out from these methods during this period.
===
IMG: anna-of-saxony-distillery-noblewoman | a sixteenth century German noblewoman in a dark gown and white collar supervising a large distillery with many copper stills and assistants
HEAD: Anna of Saxony ran one of the largest distilleries in Europe for making cures in the late sixteenth century.
BODY: Anna of Denmark, Electress of Saxony, lived from 1532 to 1585. She was married to Augustus, the ruler of Saxony. Anna had a strong interest in medicine and plants. She set up a large lab and distillery at her castle, such as at Annaburg, where many assistants prepared cures. She corresponded with other noblewomen and doctors, exchanging recipes and cures. Her cures were sent to family members, courtiers and other rulers. Scholars have studied her letters and recipe collections, which survive in German archives. They show that she was a skilled and knowledgeable worker. Her husband Augustus also supported alchemists and had his own lab in Dresden. Even so, Anna's work was focused on medicine rather than gold. She represents many women who practised chemistry in households and courts, though their work is often less visible in historical records than that of men.

TOPIC: Prague, the city of the alchemists
===
IMG: prague-castle-hradcany-river-mist-1600 | Prague Castle and its cathedral on the hill above the Vltava river in morning mist around 1600, a stone bridge with statues, red roofs
HEAD: Around 1600, the court of Emperor Rudolf the Second in Prague became the most famous centre of alchemy in Europe.
BODY: Rudolf the Second was Holy Roman Emperor from 1576 until 1612. In 1583, he moved his court from Vienna to Prague, in Bohemia. Prague Castle, standing on a hill above the Vltava river, became the centre of his court. Rudolf was very keen on art, science and the secrets of nature. He invited painters, sculptors, astronomers, astrologers and alchemists from across Europe. Scholars describe his court as one of the most remarkable cultural centres of its time. Among those who worked there were the astronomers Tycho Brahe and Johannes Kepler, who made major contributions to astronomy. Alchemists came in large numbers, hoping for the emperor's patronage. Even so, Rudolf's interests distracted him from politics. His reign was marked by growing religious and political tensions. In 1611, he was forced to give up most of his power to his brother Matthias. He died the following year in Prague.
===
IMG: rudolf-ii-emperor-portrait-court-collection | a Holy Roman Emperor around 1600 with a long chin and a dark beard, in rich black clothing and a gold chain, standing in a room full of curiosities
HEAD: Emperor Rudolf the Second was a passionate collector who was fascinated by alchemy and the natural world.
BODY: Rudolf the Second was a quiet and reserved man who preferred his collections and studies to the duties of government. He is said to have suffered from periods of deep melancholy. In Prague Castle, he built a huge collection known as a Kunstkammer, or cabinet of art and curiosities. It contained paintings, sculptures, clocks, scientific instruments, minerals, shells, exotic animals and strange objects from around the world. Scholars say that such collections were meant to represent the whole of nature and human skill. Rudolf personally took part in alchemy tests, according to of the time reports. He supported many alchemists and paid for labs. A famous portrait by the painter Giuseppe Arcimboldo shows Rudolf as Vertumnus, the Roman god of the seasons, with his face made of fruits and vegetables. Even so, much of his collection was later scattered. Swedish troops took large parts of it during the Thirty Years' War in 1648.
===
IMG: golden-lane-tiny-colourful-houses-castle | a narrow lane of tiny colourful houses built against the castle wall in Prague, cobblestones, soft evening light
HEAD: Golden Lane in Prague Castle is linked with alchemists by legend, though scholars say it was home to guards and goldsmiths.
BODY: Golden Lane, or Zlatá ulička in Czech, is a narrow street of tiny houses built into the northern wall of Prague Castle. The houses were built in the late 1500s for the castle guards. Later, goldsmiths and other craftsmen lived there, which may explain the name. According to a popular legend, Emperor Rudolf housed his alchemists in Golden Lane, where they worked to make gold. Scholars have found no evidence for this. The emperor's alchemists worked in other parts of the castle and in houses in the city. Even so, the legend remains popular, and many visitors associate the lane with alchemy. In the early twentieth century, the writer Franz Kafka lived and worked for a short time at house number twenty-two, which belonged to his sister. Today, Golden Lane is a museum street. Some houses show how people lived there in other hundreds of years, and the colourful little buildings attract many visitors.
===
IMG: alchemists-arriving-prague-courtyard-cart | travellers with carts of books and glassware arriving in a cobbled courtyard of an old palace in Prague, guards and courtiers watching
HEAD: Alchemists from all over Europe travelled to Prague hoping to gain the emperor's support.
BODY: News of Rudolf's interest in alchemy spread across Europe. Alchemists from Germany, Italy, Poland, England and elsewhere travelled to Prague. Some were real scholars and skilled workers. Others were adventurers or frauds hoping to make their fortune. Scholars estimate that dozens of alchemists worked in Prague at other times during Rudolf's reign. Many received payments, titles or labs. Others were disappointed. Records from the imperial court show payments to alchemists and purchases of materials. Some alchemists worked for nobles in Bohemia rather than for the emperor directly. One key patron was the nobleman Vilém of Rožmberk, who had his own alchemy labs. Even so, life at court could be not sure. Alchemists who failed to produce results could fall from favour or be jailed. The atmosphere of Prague around 1600 was a mixture of real learning, hope, competition and suspicion, in a city full of new ideas.
===
IMG: sendivogius-polish-alchemist-demonstration | a Polish nobleman alchemist in a fur-lined coat demonstrating an experiment with a crucible before an emperor and courtiers in a candlelit hall
HEAD: The Polish alchemist Michael Sendivogius was one of the most respected alchemists at Rudolf's court.
BODY: Michael Sendivogius was born in 1566 in Poland. He studied at some universities and became a diplomat and alchemist. He worked at the court of Rudolf the Second in Prague and later for King Sigismund the Third of Poland. According to stories told after his death, he performed a successful change of metal into gold in front of the emperor, who rewarded him. Scholars treat these stories with caution, since they come from later sources. Sendivogius wrote a much read book called A New Light of Alchemy, published in 1604. In it, he described a strange substance in the air, which he called the food of life, essential for life and combustion. Some scholars have suggested that this idea anticipated the later discovery of oxygen, although this reading is debated. Even so, his book was translated into many languages and reprinted dozens of times. Sendivogius died in 1636. He was one of the most influential alchemy writers of the 1600s.
===
IMG: tycho-brahe-kepler-observatory-instruments | two astronomers around 1600 with large brass instruments on a balcony at night, stars above, one taking measurements, a castle in the background
HEAD: Rudolf's court also supported astronomers, such as Tycho Brahe and Johannes Kepler.
BODY: Rudolf the Second's interest in the secrets of nature extended to astronomy and astrology. In 1599, the Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe arrived in Prague as imperial mathematician. He had made the most accurate notes of the stars and planets before the invention of the telescope. Tycho was also keen on alchemy and had a lab at his earlier observatory in Denmark. After Tycho died in 1601, his assistant Johannes Kepler succeeded him. Using Tycho's notes, Kepler found that planets move in elliptical orbits. He published these findings in 1609 in a book called New Astronomy. Scholars see this as a major step in the Scientific Revolution. Even so, Kepler also cast horoscopes for the emperor and others, as was expected of an imperial mathematician. Astronomy, astrology and alchemy were closely connected at the time. All of them sought to grasp the hidden order of nature and its influence on human life.
===
IMG: prague-old-town-square-astronomical-clock | the Old Town Square of Prague with the medieval astronomical clock on the town hall tower, people gathered below, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Prague's old astronomical clock reflects the city's long interest in the heavens and in clever mechanisms.
BODY: The Prague astronomical clock, called the Orloj, is mounted on the tower of the Old Town Hall. Its oldest parts date from 1410, making it one of the oldest working astronomical clocks in the world. It shows the positions of the Sun and the Moon, the signs of the zodiac and some systems of time. Later additions include moving figures of the apostles, which appear every hour. In Rudolf's time, the clock was already almost two hundred years old. It represents the long tradition of learning about the heavens in Prague. The city was home to scholars, instrument makers and clockmakers, as well as alchemists. Even so, the clock has been damaged and repaired many times, most recently after the Second World War and in a major restoration completed in 2018. Today, crowds gather in the Old Town Square every hour to watch the figures move, much as people have done for hundreds of years.

TOPIC: John Dee and Edward Kelley
===
IMG: john-dee-scholar-white-beard-study-books | an elderly English scholar with a long white beard and a black skullcap in a study crowded with books, globes and mathematical instruments
HEAD: John Dee was an English mathematician, astronomer and adviser to Queen Elizabeth the First who also studied alchemy.
BODY: John Dee was born in London in 1527. He studied at Cambridge and became one of the most learned men in England. He was an expert in mathematics, navigation, astronomy and geography. He advised English explorers about routes to Asia and promoted the idea of a British empire at sea. He also served as an adviser to Queen Elizabeth the First and is said to have chosen the date of her coronation using astrology. Dee built one of the largest private libraries in England at his house in Mortlake, near London, with thousands of books and old texts. Dee combined science with mystical interests. He studied alchemy, astrology and the possibility of communicating with angels. Even so, he was a real scholar, and his mathematical work was respected. His life shows how science and mysticism could exist side by side in the 1500s. Some of his books survive today in libraries in Britain.
===
IMG: edward-kelley-scrying-mirror-crystal | a man in a dark cap gazing into a polished black obsidian mirror on a table, a scholar with a white beard taking notes beside him, candlelight
HEAD: Edward Kelley worked with John Dee, claiming to receive messages from angels through a crystal and a mirror.
BODY: In 1582, John Dee met Edward Kelley, a younger man who claimed to be able to see and hear angels in a crystal stone or a polished mirror. This practice is called scrying. Kelley would describe what he saw, and Dee recorded the messages in detail. These sessions went on for some years. Dee's records survive and fill many pages of old texts. Scholars debate whether Kelley believed in these visions or was deceiving Dee. Kelley had a doubtful past, and according to some reports, he had been punished earlier in life for forgery. Even so, Dee trusted him for a long time. A black obsidian mirror believed to have belonged to Dee is now in the British Museum in London. It was probably made in Mexico by the Aztecs and brought to Europe after the Spanish conquest. This mirror links Dee's strange studies with the wider world of the 1500s.
===
IMG: travelling-coach-forest-road-central-europe | a horse-drawn coach with luggage travelling along a forest road in Central Europe in autumn, riders beside it, late afternoon light
HEAD: In 1583, Dee and Kelley travelled to Central Europe, where they visited the courts of Poland and Bohemia.
BODY: In 1583, John Dee, Edward Kelley and their families left England for Central Europe. They hoped to find patrons keen on their angelic messages and alchemy. They visited the court of King Stephen Báthory of Poland and then travelled to Prague, where they met Emperor Rudolf the Second. Scholars know about their journey from Dee's diaries and letters. Rudolf received them, but did not become their main patron. They later found support from the Bohemian nobleman Vilém of Rožmberk, who gave them a place to work at his castle in Třeboň. Even so, the relationship between Dee and Kelley became increasingly strained. In 1587, Kelley reported that the angels had instructed the two men to share all, such as their wives. Dee was deeply troubled but reportedly agreed for a time. The partnership broke up soon afterwards. In 1589, Dee returned to England, while Kelley remained in Bohemia.
===
IMG: kelley-knighted-bohemian-castle-hall | a man kneeling before an emperor in a grand hall of a Bohemian castle, courtiers watching, rich tapestries and candles
HEAD: Edward Kelley became wealthy and was given a noble title in Bohemia, but he later fell from favour.
BODY: After Dee left, Edward Kelley stayed in Bohemia and concentrated on alchemy. He claimed to be able to perform transmutation and impressed many people. Emperor Rudolf granted him a title of nobility, and he became known as Sir Edward Kelley. He acquired estates, houses and great wealth. His fame reached England, where Queen Elizabeth's advisers tried to persuade him to return and make gold for England. Even so, Kelley's fortunes changed suddenly. In 1591, Rudolf had him arrested and jailed at Křivoklát Castle. The reasons are not entirely clear. Some sources say he killed an official in a duel, while others suggest he failed to deliver the promised gold. He was released for a time and then jailed again. According to tradition, Kelley died in 1597 after injuring himself while trying to escape from prison. Scholars are uncertain about the exact circumstances of his death.
===
IMG: mortlake-house-thames-library-ruined | a large old house on the bank of the River Thames at Mortlake, its library rooms in disorder with empty shelves and scattered papers, grey light
HEAD: When John Dee returned to England in 1589, he found that much of his library had been damaged or stolen.
BODY: During Dee's absence, his house at Mortlake had been entered. Many of his books, old texts and instruments had been taken or damaged. Scholars believe that some of the losses were caused by people Dee had trusted to look after the house, while others may have been the result of theft. Dee was deeply upset and spent years trying to recover his books. Some later appeared in other collections. He received a position as warden of a college in Manchester in 1595. But he was not happy there and faced financial hardies. Even so, his reputation remained mixed. Some admired his learning, while others suspected him of dealing with spirits. After the death of Queen Elizabeth in 1603, he lost royal support. He died in poverty at Mortlake around 1608 or 1609. Scholars have reassessed Dee in recent decades, recognising his importance as a scholar, mathematician and collector, as well as his interest in mysticism.
===
IMG: dee-manuscript-angelic-alphabet-pages | an old manuscript page covered with a strange alphabet and tables of letters, written in brown ink, lying under soft library light
HEAD: John Dee's records of his sessions with Kelley include a strange language that Kelley said came from angels.
BODY: In his records, John Dee wrote down messages that Kelley said were dictated by angels. Some were written in a language that Dee called the angelic language. Today it is often called Enochian. It has its own alphabet and grammar. Scholars and linguists have studied these texts. Most conclude that the language was invented, probably by Kelley, though it is internally in line in some ways. Dee's old texts recording these sessions are now kept in the British Library and the Bodleian Library in Oxford. Some were printed in 1659, long after Dee's death, by a scholar named Méric Casaubon, who presented them as evidence that Dee had been deceived by evil spirits. Even so, the old texts are key historical documents. They show how a respected scholar of the 1500s searched for knowledge in many directions. The story of Dee and Kelley has inspired many novels, plays and an opera.

TOPIC: Gold, frauds and gilded gallows
===
IMG: fraud-alchemist-hollow-stirring-rod-trick | a sly-looking alchemist secretly slipping a small piece of gold into a crucible with a hollow rod while an onlooker is distracted, candlelit workshop
HEAD: Fraudulent alchemists used clever tricks to make it appear that they had created gold.
BODY: Many people around 1600 claimed to be able to make gold. Some were sincere but mistaken. Others were deliberate frauds. They used many tricks to fool their audiences. One common method used a hollow stirring rod filled with gold dust and sealed with wax. When the rod was used to stir a hot crucible, the wax melted and the gold fell into the mixture. Another trick involved crucibles with false bottoms hiding gold. Some frauds used coins coated with mercury or other substances, which could be changed in appearance. Scholars know about these tricks from books written to warn the public. The English playwright Ben Jonson mocked such deceivers in his comedy The Alchemist, first performed in 1610. Paintings of the period also show fraudulent alchemists. Even so, it was not always easy to tell frauds from sincere workers. Some alchemists believed their own mistaken results. The hardy of testing claims made it easy for deception to flourish.
===
IMG: gilded-gallows-stuttgart-1597-town-square | a town square in a German city around 1600 with a tall gallows painted gold, a crowd gathered at a distance, grey sky
HEAD: In 1597, an alchemist who failed to make gold was hanged on a gilded gallows in Stuttgart.
BODY: Some patrons punished alchemists who deceived them. One famous case took place in Stuttgart, in the German duchy of Württemberg. An alchemist named Georg Honauer had promised Duke Frederick the First that he could turn iron into gold. The duke provided him with large amounts of iron and money. When Honauer failed and tried to flee, he was arrested. In 1597, he was hanged on a gallows covered with gold-coloured metal, reportedly made from the very iron he had promised to transform. Gilded gallows were used in some cases to mock failed alchemists. Records of such punishments survive in German archives. Even so, these cases were not common. Most failed alchemists were simply dismissed or jailed. The duke of Württemberg himself went on to support other alchemists. The story of the gilded gallows became famous as a warning about the dangers of promising gold to powerful patrons.
===
IMG: alchemist-locked-cell-castle-tower | an alchemist sitting alone in a stone cell in a castle tower with a small barred window, papers on a wooden table, dim light
HEAD: Some rulers jailed alchemists to force them to reveal the secret of making gold.
BODY: Alchemists who claimed to know how to make gold could become valuable prisoners. Some rulers held alchemists captive, hoping to force them to work. The case of Edward Kelley in Bohemia is one example. Another famous case involved a Scottish alchemist named Alexander Seton, who was reportedly jailed and tortured in Saxony around 1603 by the elector Christian the Second, who wanted his secret. According to later accounts, Michael Sendivogius helped him escape, but Seton died soon afterwards. Scholars are cautious about these stories, since many details come from later, dramatic accounts. Even so, they reflect real dangers. In the early eighteenth century, the young apothecary Johann Friedrich Böttger was held by Augustus the Strong of Saxony after claiming he could make gold. His captivity would lead to an unexpected discovery. These cases show how alchemy could become entangled with power, greed and the hopes of rulers seeking wealth.
===
IMG: coins-mint-assay-balance-officials | officials at a sixteenth century mint testing gold coins with fine balances and touchstones, stacks of coins and ingots on a table
HEAD: Mints and assayers tested gold and silver carefully, and fake alchemy gold could threaten the currency.
BODY: In the 1500s, coins were made of real gold and silver. Their value depended on the purity of the metal. Governments employed assayers to test metals at mints. They used methods such as the touchstone, where a metal was rubbed on a dark stone and the colour of the streak compared with samples. A more precise method was cupellation, which separated precious metals from other metals by heating. Scholars say that these methods were highly worked out by 1600. A famous treatise on mining and metals, De Re Metallica by Georgius Agricola, published in 1556, described them in detail with many illustrations. Even so, false gold made from alloys could fool people outside the mints. Rulers worried that it might enter circulation. This was one reason why laws against multiplying gold existed. Assayers were therefore some of the main judges of alchemy claims. Many alchemy gold samples failed their tests.
===
IMG: michael-maier-physician-writing-emblems | a German physician around 1610 writing at a desk covered with engraved emblem prints and sheet music, a lute leaning against the wall
HEAD: Michael Maier, doctor to Emperor Rudolf, wrote alchemy books combining images, poems and music.
BODY: Michael Maier was born in 1568 in Holstein, in northern Germany. He studied medicine and became a doctor at the court of Rudolf the Second, who gave him the title of count palatine. After Rudolf's death, Maier travelled and published many books on alchemy. His most famous work, Atalanta Fugiens, was published in 1617. It contains fifty emblems, each combining an engraved image, a short poem, an explanatory text and a piece of music for three voices. The music takes the form of fugues, in which the voices chase one another, like the characters of the Greek myth of Atalanta. Scholars describe it as one of the first multimedia books. In recent years, scholars and musicians have recorded the music. Even so, the meaning of the emblems is complex and symbolic. Maier believed that alchemy contained deep truths hidden in ancient myths. He died in 1622 in Magdeburg, during the early years of the Thirty Years' War.
===
IMG: royal-treasury-empty-chests-chamberlain | a royal treasurer standing in a vaulted treasury room with open wooden chests nearly empty, a ledger in hand, candlelight
HEAD: Many rulers who supported alchemy hoped it would solve their financial problems.
BODY: In the 1500s and 1600s, European rulers faced rising costs for armies, courts and buildings. Wars were above all costly. Taxes were often unpopular and hard to collect. Alchemy offered the hope of a simple solution: making gold to fill the treasury. This hope explains much of the support given to alchemists by princes. Some rulers spent large sums on labs and alchemists. Records from courts in Saxony, Württemberg, Hesse and Bohemia show regular payments. Even so, no ruler ever gained gold from alchemy. Some lost large amounts of money. Yet support for alchemy was not always irrational. Alchemists also produced useful things, such as cures, dyes, metal treatments and knowledge of mining. Scholars such as Tara Nummedal have studied these relationships closely. They found that alchemists often worked under contracts, with promises of hands-on services as well as gold. Alchemy was part of the economy of the courts.
