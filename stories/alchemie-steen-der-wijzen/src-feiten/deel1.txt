TOPIC: The dream of the philosopher's stone
===
IMG: alchemist-workshop-candlelight-glowing-furnace | a candlelit alchemist's workshop around 1600 with a glowing brick furnace, glass flasks, old books and a man in a long robe looking into a crucible, warm golden red light
HEAD: For many hundreds of years, alchemists searched for a substance called the philosopher's stone.
BODY: The philosopher's stone was a legendary substance that alchemists believed could perfect metals and the human body. According to their writings, a small amount could turn common metals, such as lead or copper, into gold or silver. This change was called transmutation, or chrysopoeia, from Greek words meaning making gold. Many alchemists also believed that the stone could cure disease and lengthen life. In this form, it was called the elixir of life. The search for the stone lasted from ancient times until the 1700s. Scholars know about it from thousands of surviving old texts, printed books, letters and lab notes. Many of these are now kept in libraries such as the British Library and the Wellcome Collection in London. Even so, no one ever succeeded in making the stone. Yet the search was not foolish in its own time. It was based on real ideas about nature and led to many hands-on discoveries.
===
IMG: alchemist-holding-red-powder-small-vial | an older alchemist in a fur-trimmed robe holding up a small glass vial of red powder to the window light, workshop shelves behind
HEAD: Alchemists believed that all metals were slowly growing toward gold inside the earth.
BODY: To grasp alchemy, it helps to know how people around 1600 thought about nature. Many educated people believed that metals were formed slowly underground, much like plants growing in soil. According to this view, all metals were striving toward perfection, and gold was the most perfect metal of all. Lead, tin, iron and copper were simply metals that had not yet matured. If nature could turn base metals into gold over thousands of years, alchemists reasoned, perhaps skilled people could speed up the process. The philosopher's stone was the agent that would make this possible. Scholars say that this idea was in line with the science of the time, which drew on the writings of Aristotle. Even so, the theory was wrong. Metals are chemical elements and cannot be changed into one another by common chemical means. Yet within its own framework, the search made logical sense to many thoughtful people.
===
IMG: four-elements-diagram-old-book-candle | an old printed book open to a circular diagram of the four elements, earth, water, air and fire, beside a candle and a quill
HEAD: Alchemy was based on the ancient idea that all was made of four elements.
BODY: Since ancient Greece, many scholars believed that all matter was made of four elements: earth, water, air and fire. This idea was worked out by the philosophers Empedocles and Aristotle. Each element had two qualities. Fire was hot and dry, air was hot and wet, water was cold and wet, and earth was cold and dry. By changing these qualities, one substance could in theory be changed into one more. Alchemists also used a later idea that metals were made from two principles, called sulphur and mercury. These were not the common substances of those names, but philosophical principles representing qualities such as combustibility and fluidity. In the 1500s, the doctor Paracelsus added a third principle, salt. Scholars call this the tria prima. Even so, these theories were slowly replaced by modern chemistry in the seventeenth and eighteenth hundreds of years. Yet they provided a framework for tests for well over a thousand years.
===
IMG: gold-silver-lead-metals-on-wooden-table | pieces of gold, silver, copper, iron, tin and lead laid out on a dark wooden table, each marked with an old planetary symbol on a small card
HEAD: Alchemists linked each of the seven known metals with one of the seven planets known at the time.
BODY: Around 1600, seven metals were known: gold, silver, mercury, copper, iron, tin and lead. Alchemists connected each with one of the seven heavenly bodies that moved across the sky. Gold was linked with the Sun and silver with the Moon. Mercury, the liquid metal, shared its name with the planet Mercury. Copper was linked with Venus, iron with Mars, tin with Jupiter and lead with Saturn. Each metal had its own symbol, which alchemists used in their writings. This system reflected a belief in deep connections between the heavens and the earth. A famous phrase expressed it: as above, so below. Even so, the symbols also had practical uses. They allowed alchemists to write quickly and at times secretly. Some of these symbols survived into early chemistry. The name mercury is still used today for the liquid metal, a reminder of these old connections between planets and metals.
===
IMG: alchemist-reading-manuscript-night-study | an alchemist reading a large handwritten manuscript late at night at a wooden desk, a candle and an hourglass beside him, shelves of jars
HEAD: Alchemists studied old texts carefully, believing that ancient writers held secret knowledge.
BODY: Alchemy was not only hands-on work. It was also a scholarly tradition based on reading. Alchemists collected and studied texts written by earlier masters, some of them over a thousand years old. Many believed that ancient writers had known the secret of the philosopher's stone but had hidden it in symbolic language. Texts were copied by hand, translated and later printed. Large collections of alchemy writings were published in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth hundreds of years. One of the main was the Theatrum Chemicum, first printed in 1602, which gathered dozens of treatises in some volumes. Scholars use these collections to study how ideas spread. Even so, the texts were often confusing and contradictory. Many were attributed to famous names but were written by others. Alchemists spent years trying to read them. Reading and testing went hand in hand. Many hours were spent at the desk as well as at the furnace.
===
IMG: wealthy-patron-visiting-alchemist-lab | a wealthy nobleman in rich clothing visiting an alchemist's laboratory, the alchemist showing him a glowing crucible, assistants working
HEAD: Kings, princes and wealthy patrons often paid alchemists to work for them.
BODY: Alchemy required costly tools, fuel and materials. Many alchemists depended on wealthy patrons, such as kings, princes, nobles and merchants. Patrons hoped that alchemists would produce gold to fill their treasuries, or cures to preserve their health. Some rulers kept alchemists at their courts and provided them with labs. In return, they expected regular reports and results. Scholars have found contracts between patrons and alchemists in archives across Europe. These documents often specify payments, materials and deadlines. Even so, the relationship could be risky. Patrons grew impatient when no gold appeared. Some alchemists were jailed or punished. Others moved from court to court, seeking new support. Patronage also brought prestige. A ruler who supported alchemy showed interest in learning and the secrets of nature. Around 1600, alchemy was fashionable among the powerful, above all in Central Europe, where some courts became famous centres of alchemy work.

TOPIC: Where alchemy began
===
IMG: alexandria-ancient-harbour-lighthouse-sunset | the ancient harbour of Alexandria in Egypt at sunset with the great lighthouse, sailing ships and white buildings, calm sea
HEAD: Alchemy in the Western tradition began in Egypt, in the city of Alexandria, about two thousand years ago.
BODY: The earliest surviving alchemy writings in the Western tradition come from Egypt during the period of Roman rule. They were written in Greek, mainly in the city of Alexandria, between about the first and fourth hundreds of years after Christ. Alexandria was a great centre of learning, with scholars from many cultures. Egyptian craftsmen had long experience in working metals, dyeing cloth and making imitation gemstones. Greek philosophy provided theories about matter. These traditions combined to produce early alchemy. Scholars know about it from papyri and later copied old texts. Two papyri, known as the Leiden and Stockholm papyri, contain recipes for making imitation gold and silver, dyes and gems. Even so, it is not always clear whether early writers wanted to imitate gold or truly transform metals. The word chemistry itself may derive from the Greek word chemeia, though its origins are debated. Some scholars connect it to an ancient name for Egypt.
===
IMG: zosimos-scholar-writing-scroll-lamp | a scholar in late Roman Egypt writing on a papyrus scroll by an oil lamp, simple glass and clay apparatus on the table
HEAD: Zosimos of Panopolis, who lived around the year 300, is one of the earliest known alchemy writers.
BODY: Zosimos came from Panopolis, a town in Upper Egypt, and lived around the end of the third and beginning of the fourth century. He wrote many works on alchemy, of which fragments survive in later Greek old texts. His writings describe lab apparatus, such as devices for distillation and sublimation. They also contain visions and dreams with spiritual meanings. Zosimos wrote to a woman named Theosebeia, who seems to have been his student or colleague. Scholars consider his works a key link between hands-on metalworking and the spiritual side of alchemy. He referred to earlier authorities, such as a woman alchemist known as Maria the Jewess. Even so, much of his work is lost, and what remains is hard to read. His writings were copied by Byzantine scholars and preserved in libraries for hundreds of years. Through them, the ideas of Alexandrian alchemy passed into the later traditions of the Arabic and European worlds.
===
IMG: maria-the-jewess-water-bath-apparatus | an ancient woman in simple robes heating a glass vessel gently in a pot of warm water over a small fire, early distilling apparatus nearby
HEAD: Maria the Jewess, an early alchemist, is remembered for inventing lab tools, such as a gentle water bath.
BODY: Maria the Jewess, also called Maria the Prophetess, lived in Alexandria probably in the first to third hundreds of years after Christ. None of her own writings survive complete, but later authors, above all Zosimos, quoted her and described her inventions. She is credited with devising some types of apparatus. One was the kerotakis, a device for exposing metals to vapours. Another was a still with three arms, called the tribikos. She is also traditionally associated with the water bath, a method of heating substances gently by placing them in a container of hot water. In French, this is still called a bain-marie, after her name. The attribution is based on tradition, and certainty is impossible. Even so, Maria is regarded as one of the first known women in the history of chemistry. Her name survives in kitchens around the world, where cooks melt chocolate gently in a bain-marie.
===
IMG: baghdad-house-of-wisdom-scholars-books | scholars in the medieval Islamic world studying and copying books in a large library hall with arches and lamps
HEAD: Alchemy grew further in the Islamic world, where scholars translated and expanded Greek knowledge.
BODY: After the seventh century, the lands of the Islamic caliphates stretched from Spain to Central Asia. Scholars in cities such as Baghdad, Damascus and Cairo translated many Greek works into Arabic, such as texts on alchemy. They added their own notes and tests. The word alchemy itself comes from the Arabic al-kimiya. Many other words connected with chemistry also come from Arabic, such as alembic, elixir and alcohol, although the meaning of alcohol later changed. Islamic scholars made key advances in lab methods, above all distillation, crystallisation and the classification of substances. Even so, much of this work was later known in Europe only through Latin translations. In some cases, the original Arabic texts are lost. Islamic alchemy was a major bridge between ancient and European science. Without it, many of the ideas that later reached alchemists around 1600 would not have survived.
===
IMG: jabir-scholar-workshop-glass-alembic | a medieval Arabic scholar in a workshop with a glass alembic on a small furnace, liquid dripping into a flask, warm lamplight
HEAD: A large body of Arabic alchemy writings was attributed to a figure named Jabir ibn Hayyan.
BODY: Jabir ibn Hayyan is traditionally said to have lived in the eighth century. Hundreds of Arabic works on alchemy, medicine and other subjects were attributed to him. Scholars now believe that many of these works were written by some authors over a long period, perhaps in the ninth and tenth hundreds of years. The Jabirian writings worked out the theory that metals were formed from sulphur and mercury in other proportions. They also emphasised the importance of balance and number in nature. In Europe, Latin works were later published under the name Geber, a Latin form of Jabir. Some of the main of these, such as the Summa Perfectionis from around 1300, were actually written by a European author, possibly Paul of Taranto. Even so, they were widely believed to be Arabic. The name Geber became one of the greatest authorities in European alchemy. The English word gibberish is at times said to come from his name, though this is uncertain.
===
IMG: al-razi-physician-laboratory-flasks-patients | a medieval Persian physician in a laboratory with labelled flasks, scales and distilling equipment, a hospital courtyard visible through an arch
HEAD: The Persian doctor al-Razi classified chemical substances and described lab tools in detail.
BODY: Abu Bakr al-Razi, known in Europe as Rhazes, lived from about 865 to 925. He was a famous doctor who directed hospitals in Rayy, near modern Tehran, and in Baghdad. He also wrote on alchemy. In his Book of Secrets, he described substances, tools and procedures in a hands-on and orderly way. He divided substances into groups, such as minerals, plants and animal products, and described apparatus such as flasks, furnaces and stills. Scholars regard al-Razi as a key figure in the development of chemical methods. His approach was more hands-on than mystical. He believed in transmutation, but his accounts of lab work were clear and useful. Even so, his medical works were more much read in Europe than his alchemy. His books on smallpox and measles were studied for hundreds of years. His careful notes show how alchemy and medicine were closely linked, a connection that remained strong around 1600.

TOPIC: Alchemy comes to Europe
===
IMG: toledo-translators-manuscripts-twelfth-century | scholars translating Arabic manuscripts into Latin at wooden desks in a medieval Spanish city, sunlight through high windows, stacks of books
HEAD: In the 1100s, European scholars began translating Arabic works on alchemy into Latin.
BODY: During the 1100s, scholars from across Europe travelled to Spain and Sicily, where Christian, Muslim and Jewish cultures met. In cities such as Toledo, they translated Arabic books on science, medicine, mathematics and philosophy into Latin. Alchemy was among these subjects. In 1144, an English scholar named Robert of Chester completed a translation called the Book of the Composition of Alchemy. Scholars often describe it as the first alchemy text translated into Latin. Its preface noted that Latin readers did not yet know what alchemy was. Other translations followed, bringing the works of Jabir, al-Razi and others into Europe. Even so, translations were at times inaccurate, and the meaning of technical terms was often unclear. European scholars had to read hard texts in an unfamiliar subject. Over the following hundreds of years, alchemy became an set up part of European learning, studied by monks, doctors, scholars and craftsmen.
===
IMG: medieval-monk-scholar-roger-bacon-study | a medieval friar in a brown habit writing in a study filled with books and simple glass apparatus, a lens and candle on the table
HEAD: Old scholars such as Roger Bacon and Albertus Magnus wrote about alchemy and the nature of metals.
BODY: In the 1200s, alchemy attracted the interest of some of Europe's leading scholars. Albertus Magnus, a German friar and teacher, wrote about minerals and metals and discussed alchemy cautiously. Roger Bacon, an English Franciscan friar, praised alchemy as a useful science that could improve medicine and prolong life. Both men emphasised the importance of observation and experience. Many later alchemy texts were falsely attributed to famous scholars such as these, to give them authority. Even so, it is clear that alchemy was taken seriously in old universities and religious orders. It was discussed alongside natural philosophy and medicine. At the same time, it attracted suspicion. Some religious authorities worried that alchemy encouraged fraud or forbidden knowledge. The tension between respect and suspicion followed alchemy throughout its history. It was still present at the courts of Europe around 1600.
===
IMG: flamel-house-paris-medieval-stone-street | a narrow medieval stone house with carved figures on its facade in an old Paris street, early morning light, quiet
HEAD: Nicolas Flamel was a real scribe in old Paris, but the legend of his success as an alchemist began long after his death.
BODY: Nicolas Flamel lived in Paris from about 1330 to 1418. He worked as a scribe and bookseller and became fairly wealthy. With his wife Pernelle, he gave money to churches and charities. Records of his property and gifts survive in Paris archives. About two hundred years after his death, books began to appear claiming that Flamel had found the philosopher's stone. The main, published in 1612, contained a story of how he found a strange book and learned its secrets. Scholars have shown that this text was written long after Flamel's lifetime and was falsely attributed to him. Even so, the legend became extremely popular. People searched his houses for hidden gold. A house that Flamel built in 1407 still stands in Paris, on the rue de Montmorency. It is often described as one of the oldest stone houses in the city. It is now a restaurant.
===
IMG: pope-papal-decree-parchment-seal | a parchment document with a heavy wax seal on a wooden table in a medieval church office, candlelight, quill and ink
HEAD: Old authorities passed laws against fraudulent alchemists who claimed to make gold.
BODY: As alchemy spread, so did fraud. Some people claimed to make gold or silver but actually used tricks, such as hidden gold or coloured alloys. In 1317, Pope John the Twenty-Second issued a decree condemning alchemists who promised what they could not deliver and produced false gold. In England, a law passed in 1404 under King Henry the Fourth made it a crime to multiply gold or silver. Scholars say that rulers feared that false gold could damage trade and the currency. Some also worried that if alchemists succeeded, the value of gold would collapse. Even so, these laws did not stop alchemy. Some rulers granted special licences to alchemists they trusted. The English law against multiplying gold remained in force until 1689, when it was repealed by Parliament. The scientist Robert Boyle supported its repeal. Laws like these show that people took the idea of making gold seriously.
===
IMG: printing-press-alchemy-book-woodcut | an early printing press workshop with sheets of paper hanging to dry and a printed alchemical book with woodcut images on a table
HEAD: The invention of printing spread alchemy books widely across Europe in the 1500s.
BODY: After Johannes Gutenberg worked out printing with movable type around 1450, books became much cheaper and more easy to find. Alchemy texts, which had circulated in old texts, began to appear in print. By the 1500s, printers in cities such as Basel, Frankfurt, Strasbourg and Venice were producing many alchemy books. Some were translations of older works, while others were new. Woodcuts and engravings illustrated furnaces, vessels and symbolic images. Scholars say that printing made alchemy accessible to a wider audience, such as doctors, apothecaries, craftsmen and educated townspeople. Large collections, such as the Theatrum Chemicum, helped preserve and spread texts. Even so, print also made it easier for fraudulent or confused works to circulate. Readers had to judge which books to trust. Around 1600, interest in alchemy was at a high point. Printed books fed this interest and connected alchemists across Europe in a shared culture of reading and test.
===
IMG: town-apothecary-shop-1600-jars-shelves | the interior of an apothecary shop around 1600 with rows of painted ceramic jars, wooden drawers, scales and an apothecary preparing medicine
HEAD: Around 1600, alchemy methods were used by apothecaries, metalworkers and makers of dyes and cures.
BODY: Alchemy was not practised only by scholars seeking gold. Many of its methods were used in daily trades. Apothecaries used distillation to prepare cures and herbal waters. Metalworkers used furnaces and crucibles to refine and test metals. Assayers tested the purity of gold and silver in coins and ores. Makers of dyes, pigments, glass, gunpowder and soap all used processes like those described in alchemy texts. Scholars such as Pamela Smith and Lawrence Principe have emphasised these hands-on connections. They show that alchemy was part of a broad world of craft knowledge. Even so, the line between alchemy and common chemistry was not clear. The word chymistry is at times used by scholars to describe this combined field in the 1500s and 1600s. In towns across Europe, apothecary shops were filled with jars, bottles and distilled waters, products of methods worked out by alchemists for hundreds of years.

TOPIC: Inside the alchemist's workshop
===
IMG: athanor-brick-furnace-tower-slow-fire | a tall round brick furnace with a tower for charcoal, glowing gently through a small door, in a dim stone laboratory
HEAD: An alchemist's lab around 1600 was built around a special furnace called the athanor.
BODY: The athanor was a furnace designed to provide steady, gentle heat for long periods, at times for weeks or months. It usually had a tall tower filled with charcoal, which slowly fed the fire below. This design allowed the temperature to remain fairly constant with little attention. Many alchemy processes required slow heating, since alchemists believed they were imitating the gentle warmth of nature. The athanor was at times compared to a hen warming an egg. Scholars know the design of athanors from illustrations in printed books and from surviving accounts. Some labs also had other furnaces for strong heat, used for melting metals. Even so, controlling heat precisely was very hard without thermometers, which were invented only in the 1600s. Alchemists used their experience, watching colours and sounds, to judge the fire. Keeping the athanor burning steadily was one of the main tasks in the lab.
===
IMG: alembic-distillation-glass-copper-drips | a copper and glass alembic on a small furnace with clear liquid dripping slowly from its long spout into a glass flask
HEAD: Distillation with an alembic was one of the main methods in alchemy.
BODY: Distillation separates substances by heating a liquid until it turns into vapour, then cooling the vapour so that it becomes liquid again. Alchemists used a piece of tools called an alembic. It consisted of a lower vessel, called a cucurbit, where the liquid was heated, and an upper head with a long spout. Vapour rose into the head, cooled and ran down the spout into a receiving flask. With this method, alchemists could make strong alcohol, called aqua vitae, or water of life. Many herbal waters and acids. Scholars say that distillation methods improved greatly in the Middle Ages and spread far in the 1500s. Even so, glass vessels often cracked under heat. Copper and pottery vessels were also used. Alembics appear in many paintings and engravings of labs. Distillation remains one of the basic methods of chemistry, and the same principle is still used today.
===
IMG: retort-crucible-pelican-vessels-shelf | a shelf of alchemical glassware including a retort, a round flask with curved arms called a pelican, crucibles and stoppered bottles, soft window light
HEAD: Alchemists used many specialised vessels, such as retorts, crucibles and a looped flask called the pelican.
BODY: Alchemy labs contained a variety of vessels, each with a particular purpose. A retort was a round flask with a long, bent neck, used for distillation of strong substances. A crucible was a small heat-resistant pot used to melt metals in a hot fire. An aludel was used for sublimation, the process of turning solids directly into vapour. One unusual vessel was called the pelican. It had two curved arms leading from the neck back into the body, so that vapours could condense and flow back repeatedly. Its name came from its resemblance to a pelican feeding its young. Scholars and archaeologists have found many such objects. At Oberstockstall in Austria, a 1500s lab was found under a church sacristy in 1980, containing hundreds of crucibles, flasks and other items. Even so, most labs have disappeared. Yet surviving objects and images show a well-equipped working space.
===
IMG: bellows-charcoal-sacks-assistant-working | a young assistant working large leather bellows beside a fierce furnace in an alchemist's laboratory, sacks of charcoal stacked nearby
HEAD: Lab work required assistants, large amounts of fuel and much physical labour.
BODY: Alchemy was hard physical work. Furnaces had to be fed with charcoal, and strong fires needed bellows to blow air into them. Materials had to be ground, washed, filtered and stirred. Many alchemists employed assistants, at times called laborants, who did much of this work. Scholars have found records of payments for charcoal, glass, metals and wages in the accounts of princely labs. These show that alchemy could be very costly. A lab at a royal court might consume large amounts of fuel every month. Even so, the work could also be dangerous. Fumes from mercury, lead, arsenic and other substances were poisonous. Explosions and fires happened. Some alchemists suffered from illnesses caused by their work, although they did not always grasp the cause. The lab was a place of skill and labour, not just mystery. The success of any test depended on careful, patient effort, often over many weeks.
===
IMG: alchemical-symbols-chalk-wall-notes | a wall in an alchemist's workshop covered with chalk drawings of alchemical symbols and notes, a stool and lamp in front, dim light
HEAD: Alchemists recorded their work in notes full of symbols, codes and abbreviations.
BODY: Alchemists kept notebooks recording their tests. Some surviving notebooks are remarkably detailed. They list materials, quantities, temperatures, colours and results. Others are brief and hard to grasp. Alchemists used symbols for metals, salts, processes and times, which made writing faster. Some also used codes to keep their work secret. Scholars have studied notebooks from the period, such as those of the American alchemist George Starkey in the 1600s, who wrote under the name Eirenaeus Philalethes. These notebooks show careful, methodical work. Even so, alchemy writing was often deliberately obscure. Authors used metaphors, such as dragons, kings and queens, to describe chemical processes. They believed that the secrets should be understood only by those who were worthy. Scholars such as Lawrence Principe have reproduced some of these processes in modern labs. By following old recipes, they found that many accounts referred to real chemical reactions.
===
IMG: laboratory-and-oratory-prayer-desk-furnace | a combined room with a prayer desk and open book on one side and a glowing furnace and flasks on the other, warm candlelight
HEAD: Many alchemists saw their work as both a hands-on and a spiritual task.
BODY: A famous engraving from 1595, by the German alchemist Heinrich Khunrath, shows a room divided into two parts. One side is a lab with a furnace and vessels. The other is an oratory, a place of prayer. Khunrath believed that alchemy required both prayer and work. This idea was summed up in the phrase ora et labora, pray and work. Many alchemists around 1600 were devout Christians. They saw the purification of metals as connected with the purification of the soul. Some believed that only a person of good character could succeed. Even so, scholars caution against seeing all alchemy as purely spiritual. Most alchemists were also very keen on real substances and hands-on results. The spiritual and the practical were combined, not separated. The engraving by Khunrath, called the Amphitheatre of Eternal Wisdom, remains one of the most famous images of alchemy and is often reproduced today.

TOPIC: A day in the laboratory
===
IMG: early-morning-lab-fire-rekindled-dawn | an alchemist rekindling the furnace at dawn in a quiet stone laboratory, pale light through a small window, steam rising from a vessel
HEAD: Alchemy processes could last for weeks, so the lab fire had to be tended day and night.
BODY: Many alchemy procedures required continuous heating for long periods. Some recipes called for a vessel to be kept warm for forty days or more. This meant that the fire had to be watched constantly. Alchemists or their assistants took turns tending the furnace, adding charcoal and checking the temperature. Scholars know about these routines from lab notes, letters and instructions in alchemy books. Some accounts mention sleepless nights spent watching colours change inside sealed flasks. Even so, accidents often interrupted the work. A flask might crack, a fire might go out, or a vessel might overheat. Then the process had to begin again. Patience was seen as one of the main qualities of an alchemist. Many texts warned against hurrying. A common saying held that haste was the work of the devil. The slow rhythm of the lab, marked by the steady glow of the furnace, shaped the daily life of alchemists.
===
IMG: grinding-materials-mortar-pestle-table | an alchemist grinding a bright mineral into powder with a heavy stone mortar and pestle, small piles of coloured powders around
HEAD: Preparing materials by grinding, washing and dissolving took up much of an alchemist's time.
BODY: Before any substance could be heated or combined, it had to be prepared. Minerals and metals were ground into fine powders using mortars and pestles. They were washed, dissolved in acids or other liquids, filtered and dried. Alchemists worked with substances such as antimony, sulphur, mercury, saltpetre, vitriol and many kinds of salts and ores. Each required special handling. Scholars say that alchemists worked out key hands-on knowledge about the properties of these materials. They learned how substances dissolved, reacted and crystallised. Some of this knowledge was later used in medicine, mining and industry. Even so, the purpose of these preparations was often based on theories that later proved wrong. Alchemists might spend months purifying a material they believed contained the seed of gold. Yet the careful methods they used were sound. They formed the basis of lab skills that chemists still learn today.
===
IMG: colour-changes-sealed-flask-black-white-red | three sealed glass flasks on a shelf showing black, white and red contents in a row, lit by candlelight
HEAD: Alchemists believed the philosopher's stone formed through a series of colour changes, from black to white to red.
BODY: According to many alchemy texts, the making of the philosopher's stone passed through some stages, each marked by a colour. The first was blackness, called nigredo. It represented decay and dissolution. Next came whiteness, called albedo, a stage of purification. Some texts mentioned a yellow stage, called citrinitas. The final stage was redness, called rubedo, when the stone was believed to be complete. Scholars say that these colour changes were based partly on real notes of chemical reactions. Heating certain mixtures of metals and other substances does produce a series of colours. Even so, alchemists also gave these stages symbolic and spiritual meanings. Blackness was linked with death and redness with resurrection. Many illustrations show these stages as birds, such as a black crow, a white swan and a red phoenix. The colours guided alchemists in judging the progress of their work.
===
IMG: alchemist-weighing-results-scales-careful | an alchemist carefully weighing a tiny amount of metal on fine brass balance scales, notes and a quill beside him
HEAD: Some alchemists made careful measurements and weighed their materials precisely.
BODY: Although alchemy is often imagined as strange, many alchemists worked carefully with measurements. They used balances to weigh materials before and after tests. Assayers, who tested metals for purity, worked out very accurate methods for determining how much gold or silver a sample contained. Some alchemists used these assaying methods to check their results. Scholars have found that the notebooks of 1600s workers, such as George Starkey, contain detailed weights and quantities. These show a methodical approach to test. Even so, alchemists often readed their results according to their beliefs. A slight increase in weight or a change of colour could be seen as a sign of success. The habit of careful measurement later became central to modern chemistry. The French chemist Antoine Lavoisier, in the late eighteenth century, built his new chemistry on precise weighing. In this sense, some alchemy practices prepared the way for later science.
===
IMG: evening-lab-quiet-notes-candle-cat | an alchemist writing notes at the end of the day in a quiet laboratory, the furnace glowing low, a cat sleeping by the warm bricks
HEAD: At the end of the day, alchemists recorded their notes and planned the next steps.
BODY: When the day's work was done, alchemists often wrote down what had happened. They recorded colours, smells, sounds, weights and the state of the fire. They compared their results with the accounts in books and tried to read them. Letters between alchemists show that they shared results and asked each other for advice. Scholars have found extensive correspondence between alchemists across Europe in the 1500s and 1600s. Some letters are full of hope, others of frustration. Even so, the evening was also a time of rest. Many alchemists lived in their labs or in rooms nearby. The furnace, kept burning through the night, provided warmth in winter. The life of an alchemist could be lonely, above all for those who worked in secret. Yet many were part of wider networks of correspondents, patrons and fellow workers. Their notes, letters and books form the main evidence for understanding their work today.
