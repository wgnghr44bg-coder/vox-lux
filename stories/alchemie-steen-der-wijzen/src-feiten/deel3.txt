TOPIC: The secret language of symbols

IMG: emerald-tablet-green-stone-inscription-candle | a dark green stone tablet with an engraved inscription resting on a velvet cloth beside a candle in a quiet study
HEAD: Alchemists honoured a short, strange text called the Emerald Tablet, attributed to Hermes Trismegistus.
BODY: The Emerald Tablet is a brief text that was seen as one of the main in alchemy. It was attributed to Hermes Trismegistus, a legendary wise figure combining the Greek god Hermes and the Egyptian god Thoth. The oldest known versions are in Arabic and date from around the eighth or ninth century. The text was translated into Latin in the 1100s. It contains the famous phrase often summarised as as above, so below, expressing the idea that the heavens and the earth reflect each other. Alchemists read it as a secret description of the making of the philosopher's stone. The legend of an actual emerald tablet, found in a tomb, appears in later stories and has no basis in evidence. Even so, the text inspired many commentaries. In the 1600s, Isaac Newton made his own English translation of the Emerald Tablet, which survives among his papers.

IMG: ouroboros-serpent-eating-tail-manuscript | an old manuscript illustration of a serpent biting its own tail forming a circle, painted in green and gold, on aged parchment
HEAD: The ouroboros, a serpent eating its own tail, was a common alchemy symbol of unity and endless cycles.
BODY: The ouroboros is an image of a serpent or dragon forming a circle by swallowing its own tail. It is one of the oldest symbols associated with alchemy. It appears in a Greek alchemy manuscript from the Byzantine period, with the words one is all. The symbol represented the idea that all matter is one and that nature moves in endless cycles of destruction and renewal. Scholars say that such symbols appear in ancient Egyptian art and in other cultures. In alchemy, it could also represent the repeated distillation of a substance, in which vapour rises and falls back again. Even so, alchemy symbols often had some meanings at once. Their reading depended on the author and the context. The ouroboros remains a popular symbol today. It appears in art, literature and modern designs. In the history of chemistry, it is at times connected with the story of the German chemist August Kekulé, who said in 1890 that a dream of a serpent biting its tail helped him grasp the structure of benzene.

IMG: green-lion-devouring-sun-painting | an alchemical painting of a green lion swallowing a golden sun, bright colours on old parchment, decorative border
HEAD: Alchemists used strange images such as a green lion devouring the sun to describe chemical processes.
BODY: Alchemy books and old texts are full of remarkable pictures. Kings and queens, dragons, lions, eagles, suns and moons appear in many combinations. One famous image shows a green lion devouring the sun. Scholars and chemists have readed many of these images as accounts of real chemical reactions. The green lion may represent a strong solvent or a substance such as impure vitriol, while the sun represents gold. The image may show gold being dissolved. Another common image, a king and queen joined together, often represented the combination of two substances, such as sulphur and mercury. Even so, the meanings were deliberately hidden. Alchemists believed that their knowledge was precious and should not be easily available. The historian Lawrence Principe has recreated some such processes in a lab, following the symbolic instructions. He found that some images corresponded to real reactions that produce striking results, such as a metallic tree growing in a flask.

IMG: splendor-solis-illuminated-manuscript-gold | a page of a richly illuminated sixteenth century manuscript showing an alchemist with a golden flask, framed by flowers and landscapes, gold leaf glowing
HEAD: The Splendor Solis is one of the most beautiful illuminated alchemy papers, made in the 1500s.
BODY: The Splendor Solis, meaning the splendour of the sun, is an illustrated alchemy treatise. The earliest surviving copy was made in Germany around 1532 to 1535 and is now kept in Berlin. A later and above all famous copy, dated 1582, is in the British Library in London. It contains twenty-two full-page paintings, richly decorated with gold. The images show alchemy stages through symbolic scenes, such as a knight standing on two fountains, a king drowning in the sea and a series of flasks containing birds and animals. Around the flasks are scenes of daily life, such as people working, children playing and travellers on roads. Scholars value the manuscript both as a work of art and as a source for alchemy ideas. Even so, its exact author is unknown. The text was once attributed to a legendary alchemist named Salomon Trismosin. The paintings show how alchemy combined science, art and spiritual imagery.

IMG: rosicrucian-manifesto-printed-pages-1614 | a small printed pamphlet from 1614 lying open on a wooden table with a rose and cross emblem on the title page, a candle and seal
HEAD: In 1614, strange pamphlets announced the existence of a secret brotherhood of the Rosy Cross.
BODY: Between 1614 and 1616, three pamphlets were published in Germany describing a secret society called the Brotherhood of the Rosy Cross, or the Rosicrucians. They told the story of a legendary founder, Christian Rosenkreutz, who had supposedly travelled to the East to learn hidden wisdom. The brotherhood was said to work for the reform of knowledge and society. The third pamphlet, The Chymical Wedding of Christian Rosenkreutz, published in 1616, is an allegorical story full of alchemy imagery. Scholars believe it was written by a young Lutheran theologian named Johann Valentin Andreae. The pamphlets caused great excitement across Europe. Many people tried to contact the brotherhood. Even so, there is no evidence that it existed as an organised group at the time. Scholars see the Rosicrucian manifestos as part of a wider movement of religious and intellectual reform. They show how closely alchemy was linked with hopes for a better understanding of the world.

IMG: alchemical-emblem-engraving-garden-figures | an early seventeenth century engraving of a symbolic garden with figures, a fountain, a sun and moon, and a philosopher pointing, fine black lines on paper
HEAD: Printed emblem books made alchemy imagery easy to find in the early seventeenth century.
BODY: In the early seventeenth century, publishers in Germany produced many illustrated alchemy books. Engravers such as Matthäus Merian created detailed images for them. These books presented alchemy ideas through emblems, combinations of pictures and short texts. Readers were invited to contemplate the images and discover their hidden meanings. These books were costly and were bought by educated readers, doctors and nobles. Many of the most famous alchemy images known today come from this period. They include the engravings in Michael Maier's Atalanta Fugiens and in collections such as the Musaeum Hermeticum, published in Frankfurt in 1625. Even so, the purpose of these images was not only decorative. They expressed the belief that nature contained hidden meanings that could be understood through symbols. Today, these engravings are admired as works of art. They appear in museums, books and exhibitions about the history of science and the imagination.

TOPIC: Women who practised alchemy

IMG: isabella-cortese-venetian-book-recipes | a sixteenth century Venetian woman in an elegant gown reading a small printed book of recipes at a window overlooking a canal
HEAD: In 1561, a book of secrets attributed to a woman named Isabella Cortese was published in Venice.
BODY: In 1561, a book called The Secrets, in Italian, was published in Venice under the name of Isabella Cortese. It contained recipes for cures, cosmetics, perfumes, dyes and alchemy processes. The book was much loved and was reprinted many times. In its introduction, the author advised readers to avoid wasting their money on false alchemists and to follow hands-on methods. Scholars are uncertain who Isabella Cortese seriously was. Little is known about her life. Some scholars have suggested that the name may have been used by a publisher. Even so, the book shows that women were recognised as workers of chemical arts. Books of secrets were a popular genre in the 1500s. They combined useful household knowledge with the promise of hidden wonders. Readers used them to make cures and cosmetics at home. Such books helped spread chemical methods beyond universities and courts into common households.

IMG: marie-meurdrac-chemistry-book-1666-women | a seventeenth century Frenchwoman in a lace collar working at a laboratory table with small glass vessels and a printed book beside her, 1666
HEAD: In 1666, the French chemist Marie Meurdrac published a chemistry book written above all for women.
BODY: Marie Meurdrac was a Frenchwoman who lived in the 1600s. In 1666, she published a book called La Chymie charitable et facile, en faveur des dames, meaning Charitable and Easy Chemistry for Ladies. It described lab tools, methods and recipes for cures and cosmetics. In her preface, she wrote that she had hesitated before publishing, since teaching was not usually seen as a woman's role. She concluded that minds have no sex and that knowledge should be shared. Scholars regard her book as one of the first chemistry books written by a woman. It was reprinted some times and translated into German and Italian. Even so, Meurdrac's hands-on chemistry was rooted in the same traditions as alchemy. She used distillation, sublimation and other alchemy methods. Her book shows how alchemy was slowly changing into chemistry during the 1600s. It also shows that women contributed to that change, even though their work was often overlooked.

IMG: noblewoman-household-still-room-herbs | a noblewoman and her maids working in a household still room with copper stills, drying herbs and rows of bottles, morning light
HEAD: Many women practised chemistry in household still rooms, making cures for their families and neighbours.
BODY: In the 1500s and 1600s, many large households had a room called a still room. There, women of the household distilled herbal waters, prepared cures, made preserves and produced cosmetics. Noblewomen often supervised this work and kept recipe books recording their methods. Many of these old texts survive in libraries and archives. Scholars such as Elaine Leong have studied them. They show that women exchanged recipes, tested cures and recorded results. Some recipe books contain alchemy processes alongside cooking and medicine. Even so, this work was rarely recognised as science at the time. It was seen as part of household management. Yet the knowledge was real and valuable. Women in still rooms provided much of the medical care available to families and local communities, above all in the countryside, where doctors were rare. Their work shows that the hands-on chemistry of the period reached far beyond the labs of alchemists and the courts of princes.

IMG: women-alchemists-engraving-mutus-liber | an old engraving showing a man and a woman working together at an alchemical furnace, collecting dew with cloths in a field in the background, 1677
HEAD: Some alchemy images show a man and a woman working together, reflecting the role of couples in alchemy.
BODY: Some alchemy books show men and women working side by side. One famous example is the Mutus Liber, meaning the silent book, published in France in 1677. It contains fifteen engravings with almost no words. Some plates show a man and a woman working together at the furnace and collecting dew from the fields with cloths. Alchemy work was at times carried out by married couples. The legend of Nicolas Flamel included his wife Pernelle as a partner in his work. Real couples also worked together in labs, although records of women's contributions are often limited. Even so, the images had symbolic meanings as well. The union of male and female represented the joining of opposite principles, such as sulphur and mercury, or sun and moon. Scholars suggest that these images reflected both real partnerships and the symbolic language of alchemy. They remind readers that alchemy was not only a solitary pursuit.

IMG: woman-alchemist-modern-portrait-museum | a museum gallery displaying old portraits and books about women in the history of chemistry, a visitor reading a panel, soft lighting, today
HEAD: Scholars are increasingly rediscovering the role of women in the history of alchemy and chemistry.
BODY: For a long time, histories of alchemy focused mainly on famous men. In recent decades, scholars have looked more closely at women's contributions. They have studied recipe books, letters, court records and printed works. They have found evidence of women working as workers, patrons, assistants, writers and collectors. Some, like Anna of Saxony, ran large labs. Others, like Marie Meurdrac, wrote influential books. Many more worked quietly in households and still rooms, leaving few traces. The boundaries between alchemy, medicine and household knowledge were not fixed. Women's work was often placed on the household side and therefore less valued. Even so, this research has changed understanding of early science. It shows that knowledge was produced in many places, not only in universities and courts. Museums and exhibitions now include these stories more often. The women of early chemistry are slowly taking their place in the history of science.

TOPIC: Isaac Newton and Robert Boyle
===
IMG: robert-boyle-laboratory-air-pump-1660 | a seventeenth century English gentleman scientist with a long wig standing beside a large glass air pump in a laboratory, assistants nearby, 1660s
HEAD: Robert Boyle, often called a founder of modern chemistry, also searched for the philosopher's stone.
BODY: Robert Boyle was born in Ireland in 1627 and became one of the leading scientists of 1600s England. He was a founding member of the Royal Society, set up in London in 1660. He is famous for Boyle's law, which describes the relationship between the pressure and volume of a gas. In 1661, he published The Sceptical Chymist, in which he criticised the traditional theories of the four elements and the three principles. Scholars once saw this book as a rejection of alchemy. More recent research, above all by Lawrence Principe and William Newman, has shown that Boyle was very keen on making gold. He believed it might be possible and conducted many tests. He even supported the repeal of the old English law against multiplying gold in 1689. Even so, Boyle insisted on careful tests and clear reporting. His work shows that alchemy and early modern chemistry were closely connected rather than opposed.
===
IMG: isaac-newton-manuscripts-candle-cambridge | a seventeenth century scholar with long grey hair writing late at night in a room at Cambridge, stacks of handwritten pages and a small furnace in the corner, 1680s
HEAD: Isaac Newton wrote more than a million words about alchemy.
BODY: Isaac Newton, born in 1643, is famous for his laws of motion, his theory of gravity and his work on light and mathematics. He was also deeply involved in alchemy. Over about thirty years, he wrote more than a million words on the subject, copying texts, making notes and recording tests. He built a lab in his garden at Trinity College, Cambridge. According to his assistant, Newton at times kept his furnace burning for weeks during his tests. Scholars long ignored or downplayed this side of Newton's work. They believed it did not fit his image as a founder of modern science. In recent decades, scholars have studied his alchemy papers closely. The Chymistry of Isaac Newton project at Indiana University has published many of them online. Even so, the meaning of his alchemy is still debated. Newton seems to have hoped that alchemy could reveal hidden forces in nature, like gravity.

IMG: sothebys-auction-1936-newton-papers-room | an auction room in the 1930s with bidders seated in rows and a table of old handwritten papers and boxes, an auctioneer at the front
HEAD: Newton's alchemy papers were sold at auction in 1936, and the economist John Maynard Keynes bought many of them.
BODY: After Newton's death in 1727, his papers passed to his family. For many years, his alchemy papers were seen as embarrassing and unfit for publication. In 1936, a large collection of Newton's papers was sold at an auction at Sotheby's in London. The British economist John Maynard Keynes bought many of the alchemy papers. After studying them, Keynes wrote a famous essay called Newton, the Man, published in 1947 after his death. In it, he described Newton as not the first of the age of reason, but the last of the magicians. This phrase changed how people viewed Newton. Keynes left his set to King's College, Cambridge, where it remains today. Other papers were bought by the scholar Abraham Yahuda and are now in the National Library of Israel in Jerusalem. Even so, many scholars now argue that Newton's alchemy was not simply magic. It was part of his real investigation into the nature of matter.

IMG: newton-hair-mercury-analysis-modern-lab | a modern laboratory with a scientist examining a small lock of old hair under a microscope, an old portrait print on the bench, today
HEAD: Analysis of Newton's hair has shown high levels of mercury, possibly from his alchemy tests.
BODY: In 1693, Newton suffered a period of serious illness, with insomnia, loss of appetite and suspicion of his friends. His letters from this time show distress. In 1979, researchers analysed some hairs said to have come from Newton. They found high levels of mercury, lead and other metals. Some scientists suggested that his illness might have been caused by mercury poisoning from his alchemy tests. Newton often heated mercury, which produces toxic vapour. Scholars note, yet, that this explanation is uncertain. The hair samples may not all have been genuine, and Newton's symptoms could have had other causes, such as overwork or emotional stress. Even so, the findings show the real dangers of alchemy work. Many alchemists handled toxic substances without knowing the risks. Newton recovered and went on to become Warden and later Master of the Royal Mint in London, where he worked on coinage and the detection of counterfeit coins, a fitting task for a former alchemist.

IMG: royal-society-meeting-seventeenth-century-room | a meeting of seventeenth century gentlemen scientists around a long table with instruments and papers in a wood-panelled room, candles lit, 1660s
HEAD: The Royal Society encouraged tests and open reporting, which slowly changed how chemistry was practised.
BODY: The Royal Society of London was founded in 1660 to promote knowledge through test. Its motto, Nullius in verba, meaning take nobody's word for it, expressed the idea that claims should be tested by observation. Members met regularly to discuss and demonstrate tests. They published results in the journal Philosophical Transactions, first issued in 1665, which is still published today. This emphasis on sharing results openly contrasted with the secrecy of traditional alchemy. Many members, such as Boyle and Newton, were keen on making gold. Yet they increasingly preferred clear language and reproducible tests. Even so, the change was gradual. Secret and open practices existed side by side for many decades. Other such societies were founded in other countries, such as the French Academy of Sciences in 1666. These institutions helped transform alchemy into the modern science of chemistry. The word alchemy itself slowly came to mean only the search for gold, while chemistry described all else.

IMG: cambridge-trinity-college-garden-evening | the courtyard and gardens of an old Cambridge college at evening with stone buildings, a fountain and warm lit windows, today
HEAD: Newton's alchemy lab once stood beside his rooms at Trinity College, Cambridge.
BODY: Newton lived at Trinity College, Cambridge, from 1661 until 1696. His rooms were near the Great Gate of the college. According to accounts by his assistant, Humphrey Newton, who was not related to him, Isaac Newton had a lab in the garden nearby. There he carried out chemical and alchemy tests, often late into the night. Humphrey wrote that Newton rarely went to bed before two or three in the morning and at times worked until dawn. Scholars use these accounts, together with Newton's notebooks, to reconstruct his working life. Even so, the lab itself no longer exists. The garden has changed over the hundreds of years. Today, Trinity College is a quiet place of stone courts, lawns and libraries. Visitors can see an apple tree said to be descended from the tree at Newton's family home that inspired his thoughts on gravity. Few realise that the same scholar spent many hours watching flasks glow over a furnace in search of hidden secrets.

TOPIC: From alchemy to chemistry

IMG: hennig-brand-glowing-phosphorus-flask-dark | a seventeenth century alchemist in a dark laboratory staring in wonder at a glass flask glowing with a soft pale green light, 1669
HEAD: In 1669, the alchemist Hennig Brand found phosphorus while searching for the philosopher's stone.
BODY: Hennig Brand was a German merchant and alchemist living in Hamburg. Around 1669, he began testing with large amounts of human urine, which he believed might contain the secret of the philosopher's stone. He boiled it down, let it stand and heated the residue strongly. The result was a white, waxy substance that glowed in the dark with a pale green light. Brand had found phosphorus. It was the first chemical element to be found by a known person in recorded history. The name phosphorus comes from Greek words meaning light bearer. Brand kept his method secret for a while and sold some of the substance. Later, other chemists, such as Robert Boyle, found how to make it. Even so, Brand did not find the philosopher's stone. Yet his discovery shows how alchemy searches could lead to real scientific findings. A famous 1700s painting by Joseph Wright of Derby imagines the moment of discovery.

IMG: bottger-porcelain-white-cup-meissen-kiln | a young man in early eighteenth century clothing holding up a delicate white porcelain cup beside a glowing kiln, workers in the background, 1700s
HEAD: An alchemist jailed to make gold helped create Europe's first true porcelain in the early eighteenth century.
BODY: Johann Friedrich Böttger was a young apothecary's apprentice in Berlin who claimed to be able to make gold. In 1701, he fled to Saxony, where the ruler, Augustus the Strong, held him in custody and ordered him to produce gold. Böttger could not succeed. Instead, he was set to work with the scientist Ehrenfried Walther von Tschirnhaus, who was trying to make porcelain. At that time, fine white porcelain came only from China and Japan and was extremely valuable in Europe. Around 1708 and 1709, the team succeeded in producing true hard-paste porcelain. In 1710, Augustus founded a porcelain factory in the town of Meissen. Porcelain became known as white gold because of its value. Even so, Böttger remained under close watch for the rest of his short life and died in 1719. The Meissen factory still operates today and is famous for its crossed swords mark.

IMG: lavoisier-laboratory-precise-balances-1780s | an elegant eighteenth century French laboratory with precise balances, glass apparatus and a gas collecting bell jar, a couple working together, 1780s
HEAD: In the late eighteenth century, Antoine Lavoisier helped establish modern chemistry based on elements and careful measurement.
BODY: Antoine Lavoisier was a French chemist who lived from 1743 to 1794. Working with his wife, Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier, who assisted with tests, translations and illustrations, he transformed chemistry. He showed that combustion involved combining with a gas, which he named oxygen. He emphasised that mass is conserved in chemical reactions, which he showed by careful weighing. In 1789, he published a textbook listing chemical elements as substances that could not be broken down further. Scholars see this as a major step in the creation of modern chemistry. Lavoisier's system replaced the old ideas of four elements and principles. Even so, his list still contained some errors, such as light and heat. Lavoisier built on the work of many earlier chemists, some of whom had been involved with alchemy. The methods of distillation, weighing and careful observation worked out by alchemists over hundreds of years became tools of the new science.

IMG: elements-periodic-table-old-chart-classroom | an old printed chart of chemical elements hanging on the wall of a nineteenth century classroom, wooden desks and glass bottles on a shelf, 1870s
HEAD: Modern chemistry showed that metals are elements that cannot be turned into one another by chemical means.
BODY: During the 1800s, chemists identified many new elements and studied their properties. In 1869, the Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev published a periodic table, arranging the elements in order of their atomic weights and grouping those with matching properties. He even predicted elements that had not yet been found. This system showed clearly that gold, silver, lead and other metals are distinct elements. Ordinary chemical reactions cannot change one element into one more. This meant that the alchemists' dream of making gold from lead by chemical means was impossible. Even so, the story did not end there. In the early twentieth century, scientists found radioactivity and learned that atoms could change into other atoms through nuclear processes. In 1901, Ernest Rutherford and Frederick Soddy observed that radioactive thorium was changing into one more element. Soddy reportedly exclaimed that it was transmutation, and Rutherford warned him not to call it that.
===
IMG: particle-accelerator-modern-lab-quiet | a quiet modern particle physics laboratory with large metal equipment, cables and soft blue lights, no people, today
HEAD: In the 1900s, physicists turned tiny amounts of other elements into gold using nuclear reactions.
BODY: Modern physics has made a form of transmutation possible. In nuclear reactions, the number of protons in an atom's nucleus can change, turning one element into one more. In 1980, a team led by the American chemist Glenn Seaborg at the Lawrence Berkeley Lab produced a tiny amount of gold from bismuth using a particle accelerator. Scholars of science often mention this test as a symbolic fulfilment of the alchemists' dream. Even so, the amount produced was extremely small, only a few thousand atoms. The cost was enormous. The gold was also radioactive in part. Making gold in this way is far more costly than buying it. Other tests have produced gold atoms in particle collisions as well. Yet the dream that inspired alchemists for hundreds of years has been realised only in a form they could never have imagined. The search for the philosopher's stone ended not in riches, but in a deeper understanding of matter.
===
IMG: chemistry-laboratory-glassware-today-flasks | a bright modern chemistry laboratory with glass flasks, a distillation apparatus and a fume cupboard, tidy benches and soft daylight, today
HEAD: Many tools and methods of modern chemistry labs have their roots in alchemy.
BODY: Modern chemistry labs still use methods that alchemists worked out for hundreds of years. Distillation, filtration, crystallisation and sublimation are taught to every chemistry student. The water bath, still called a bain-marie in French, is used to heat substances gently. Flasks, beakers and condensers are descendants of the alembics and retorts of the past. The language of chemistry also contains many words from alchemy and Arabic, such as alkali, alcohol and elixir. Even so, the theories behind modern chemistry are very other. Today, chemists grasp atoms, molecules and chemical bonds. They no longer search for a single substance that can perfect all metals. Yet the curiosity, patience and careful practice of alchemists live on. Scholars such as Lawrence Principe argue that alchemy should be seen as an early stage of chemistry, rather than as a separate or foolish pursuit. In labs around the world, the long tradition goes on in new forms.

HEAD: Today, Golden Lane in Prague attracts visitors who come to imagine the world of Rudolf's alchemists.
BODY: Prague Castle is now the seat of the President of the Czech Republic and one of the largest castle complexes in the world. Every year, millions of visitors explore its courtyards, palaces and cathedral. Golden Lane is one of its most popular streets. The tiny houses have been restored to show how people lived there in other periods, from the sixteenth to the 1900s. Some display furniture, tools and costumes. The association of Golden Lane with alchemists is a legend. Yet it reflects the real importance of alchemy at Rudolf's court. Elsewhere in the city, museums and guided tours tell stories of alchemists, astronomers and emperors. Even so, much of Rudolf's world has disappeared. His great collection is scattered across museums in Vienna, Stockholm and elsewhere. In the evening, when the crowds leave, the lane becomes quiet. The small houses glow softly against the old castle wall.

HEAD: Libraries around the world preserve thousands of alchemy papers and printed books.
BODY: The writings of alchemists are preserved in many libraries. The British Library and the Wellcome Collection in London hold large collections of alchemy papers. The Ritman Library in Amsterdam, also known as the Embassy of the Free Mind, specialises in Hermetic and alchemy texts. The Science History Institute in Philadelphia holds alchemy books and a famous collection of paintings showing alchemists at work. Scholars use these collections to study how alchemy worked out and spread. Many old texts have now been digitised and can be viewed online. Even so, many texts have never been fully studied. Some are written in hard languages and codes, and others survive only in single copies. Researchers continue to discover new facts in these archives. The quiet reading rooms where these books are kept are very different from the smoky labs where they were once used. Yet the texts still carry the hopes and questions of their authors.

IMG: painting-alchemist-at-work-museum-gallery | a museum gallery with old Dutch and Flemish paintings of alchemists in cluttered workshops, a visitor sitting on a bench looking at them
HEAD: Many paintings from the 1500s and 1600s show alchemists at work in their labs.
BODY: Alchemists were a popular subject for painters, above all in the Netherlands and Flanders in the 1500s and 1600s. Artists such as Pieter Bruegel the Elder, David Teniers the Younger and Adriaen van Ostade painted alchemists in cluttered workshops. Some paintings are humorous and critical. A famous print after Bruegel, from 1558, shows an alchemist who has ruined his family with his obsession, while his wife searches an empty purse. Other paintings are more respectful and show careful scholars at work. Scholars use these images to study lab tools and the public image of alchemy. Many such paintings are displayed in museums, such as the Science History Institute in Philadelphia, which holds a large set. Even so, paintings are not photographs. Artists often exaggerated or combined details for effect. Yet they capture the atmosphere of the period: the furnaces, the vessels, the books, and the mixture of hope and folly that surrounded the search for gold.

HEAD: The philosopher's stone remains famous today through novels, films and popular culture.
BODY: The idea of the philosopher's stone has went on to capture the imagination long after alchemy declined. It appears in many novels, films and games. One of the best-known modern examples is the first Harry Potter book by J. K. Rowling, published in 1997 as Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone. In the story, the stone is created by Nicolas Flamel, the real Parisian scribe whose legend began hundreds of years after his death. Such stories have introduced the history of alchemy to many young readers. Museums have held exhibitions about the real history behind the legends. In 2017, the British Library organised an exhibition marking twenty years of Harry Potter, which included alchemy papers and objects. Even so, popular images of alchemy often show it as pure magic. The real history is more complex. It involved careful work, real thought and hands-on discoveries, alongside dreams of gold and eternal life.

IMG: psychologist-study-alchemy-symbols-books | a twentieth century study with shelves of old alchemical books, illustrated volumes open on the desk, a pipe and reading glasses, calm afternoon light, 1940s
HEAD: In the 1900s, the psychologist Carl Jung studied alchemy as a source of symbols of the human mind.
BODY: The Swiss psychiatrist Carl Gustav Jung, who lived from 1875 to 1961, became fascinated by alchemy. He collected alchemy books and wrote some works on the subject, such as Psychology and Alchemy, published in 1944. Jung believed that alchemy images, such as the union of opposites, the stages of blackness, whiteness and redness, and the transformation of base materials, expressed processes of psychological growth. He saw the search for the philosopher's stone as a symbol of the search for wholeness of the self. Jung's work made alchemy popular among many readers keen on psychology and spirituality. Even so, scholars of science have criticised this reading. They argue that it overlooks the hands-on, chemical side of alchemy, which was central for most workers. Recent research has restored that balance. Yet Jung's books remain influential. His readings show how alchemy goes on to inspire new meanings in other times.

IMG: prague-night-river-castle-stars-calm | Prague Castle and the Charles Bridge at night reflected in the calm Vltava river, stars above, soft golden lights, today
HEAD: The long search for the philosopher's stone left behind a rich legacy of science, art and imagination.
BODY: For more than fifteen hundred years, alchemists searched for the philosopher's stone. They worked in Alexandria, Baghdad, Toledo, Paris, Prague and London. They built furnaces, invented vessels and worked out methods such as distillation. They wrote books full of symbols and painted books with gold. Kings supported them, frauds deceived, and scholars such as Boyle and Newton took their questions seriously. Scholars now see alchemy as a key part of the history of science. Even so, no alchemist ever made gold from lead. Yet their search led to discoveries in medicine, chemistry and industry, from distilled cures to phosphorus and porcelain. Their questions about the nature of matter were in the end answered by modern chemistry and physics. In Prague, where the emperor Rudolf once welcomed alchemists from all over Europe, the river still flows quietly beneath the castle. On calm nights, the old city glows softly under the stars.
