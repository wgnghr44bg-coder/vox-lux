Karim is thirty-six years old. He is a master inlayer, a parchin kar, as the Persians call it. He sets small pieces of coloured stone into white marble, to make pictures of flowers, and leaves, and vines. It is very fine, very slow, very skilful work. Karim learned it from his father's friend, when he was a boy, and he has done it all his life. He is one of the best inlayers in Agra.

Karim came to work on the great tomb ten years ago, when the work began. Before that, he worked on the palaces in the fort, and on the tombs of nobles, in the gardens along the river. But the great tomb is the most important work of his life. He has worked on it every day, for ten years, except on Fridays, and holy days. He knows every flower he has made, on its walls.

--

{img:mughal-woman-1643-veil-cooking-courtyard-clay-stove-bread-family-home}
His wife Zainab is thirty. She grew up in Agra, in the old city, the daughter of a cloth merchant. She is lively, and warm, and practical, and she manages the house, and the money, and the children, and her husband, who is very good at inlaying stones, and not very good at anything else. She cooks, and sews, and embroiders, and she sells her embroidery in the bazaar, for a little extra money.

Noor is twelve. She is quiet, and clever, and she loves to draw. She draws flowers, and birds, and patterns, on scraps of paper, and on the ground, with a stick, and on the walls, with charcoal, which her mother does not like. Karim says that she has the eye of an artist. Sometimes, he lets her come to the workshop, and watch him work. And sometimes, he lets her draw the designs for his flowers, on paper, before he cuts the stones.

{img:boy-flying-kite-rooftop-agra-dome-in-distance-sunny-afternoon}
Ali is nine. He is full of energy, and noise, and mischief. He loves kites, and flies them from the flat roof of the house, in the afternoons, with the other boys, and tries to cut their strings with his. He loves the elephants, which carry the marble to the building site, and he follows them around, until the elephant men chase him away. He wants to be an elephant driver, when he grows up. Or a soldier. Or a kite maker.

And there is Yusuf, Karim's father. He is sixty-five. He was a stonecutter, for fifty years. He cut the red sandstone for the great fort, in Agra, and the palaces at Fatehpur Sikri, for the emperor Akbar, Shah Jahan's grandfather. Now, his hands are too stiff, and his eyes too weak, to work. So he sits in the courtyard, in the shade of a neem tree, and smokes his hookah, and watches the world go by, and tells stories.

--

The family lives in a new town, which has grown up beside the great tomb, to the south. It is called Mumtazabad, after the empress. It was built for the workers, and the craftsmen, and the merchants who serve them. There are houses, and workshops, and bazaars, and caravanserais, for travelling merchants, and mosques, and wells. Thousands of people live there. It is a busy, noisy, friendly place.

{img:mumtazabad-street-1643-bazaar-shops-workers-houses-taj-dome-above}
Karim's house is small, but comfortable. It is built of brick, and plaster, around a little courtyard, with the neem tree in the middle. There are three rooms, and a kitchen, and a flat roof, where the family sleeps on hot nights, and dries the washing, and flies kites. From the roof, they can see the great white dome of the tomb, rising above the houses, just a few hundred steps away.

Every morning, when Karim goes up to the roof, before dawn, to say his prayers, he looks at the dome. And every morning, it takes his breath away. Even after ten years. He says to Zainab that it is like living next to the moon.

In the evenings, the family often sits together on the roof, after supper, to enjoy the cool air. Zainab embroiders, by the light of a little oil lamp. Karim tells them about his day, and the flowers he is making. Yusuf smokes his hookah, which bubbles softly. The children play, or draw, or listen. And all around them, on the other rooftops, the other families of Mumtazabad are doing the same, talking quietly, under the stars.
