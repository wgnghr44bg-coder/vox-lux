The white marble of the Taj Mahal comes from Makrana, in Rajasthan. It is a small town, on the edge of the desert, about three hundred and seventy kilometres west of Agra. Beneath it, there are great hills of marble. The best white marble in India. It is very pure, and very hard, and it has a special quality. It glows, when the light shines on it. As if there were a light inside it.

The quarries of Makrana are very old. People have been cutting marble there for more than a thousand years. In the time of Shah Jahan, thousands of workers were sent there, to cut the marble for the great tomb. They cut the stone out of the hillside, in great blocks, with iron chisels, and hammers, and wedges. Some of the blocks are enormous, as big as a small room, and weigh many tons.

--

{img:elephant-pulling-marble-block-on-cart-road-mahout-dusty-landscape}
Getting the marble from Makrana to Agra was a huge task. The blocks were loaded onto great wooden carts, with big, solid wheels, pulled by teams of oxen, or buffaloes, or elephants. Some carts needed thirty oxen, or more, to pull them. They travelled slowly, along the dusty roads, across the dry plains of Rajasthan, for weeks, stopping at villages, and wells, and caravanserais, on the way.

The story says that a thousand elephants were used to carry the materials for the Taj Mahal. Nobody knows if that number is true. But there were certainly many elephants. The elephants were very useful. They could pull great weights, and lift heavy stones, with their trunks, and push carts out of the mud. They were patient, and strong, and clever. And each one had its own driver, called a mahout, who sat on its neck, and talked to it, and guided it.

{img:boy-watching-elephant-mahout-bathing-elephant-river-yamuna-evening}
Ali loves the elephants. Every evening, when the work is finished, he goes down to the river, to watch the mahouts washing their elephants. The elephants walk slowly into the water, and lie down, on their sides, and the mahouts scrub them with brushes, and stones, while the elephants spray water over themselves, with their trunks. They seem to enjoy it very much. And so does Ali.

One of the mahouts, an old man called Bhola, has become Ali's friend. He lets Ali sit on the bank, and help, by handing him the brushes, and the stones. His elephant is a big, gentle female, called Champa. She is about forty years old, and she has been carrying marble for the great tomb for ten years. Bhola says she is the cleverest elephant in Agra. Once, she lifted Ali up, with her trunk, very gently, and put him on her back. Ali has never been so happy.

--

When the marble arrives at Agra, it is unloaded, and stored in great yards, near the tomb. The stonecutters look at each block carefully, to find any flaws, or cracks. Then they cut it, with long iron saws, with sand and water, into slabs, and blocks, of the right shapes and sizes. It is slow, hard work. A big block of marble can take days to cut.

{img:stonecutters-sawing-marble-slabs-sand-water-workshop-yard-agra}
The marble is cut very precisely. Every piece has its own place in the building, which the architects have planned. The stonecutters mark each piece, with a little sign, or a number, so that the masons know where it goes. Some of these marks can still be seen today, on the back of the stones, by the people who repair the building.

Old Yusuf sometimes walks to the marble yard, slowly, with his stick, to watch the stonecutters at work. He knows many of them. He gives them advice, which they do not always want. He runs his old hand over the smooth white marble, and shakes his head, in wonder. He says that in fifty years of cutting stone, he never saw marble as beautiful as this. And he wishes he were young again, so that he could work on it too.
