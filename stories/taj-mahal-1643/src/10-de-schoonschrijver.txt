Around the great arched doorways of the tomb, there are words. Long bands of beautiful writing, in black stone, set into the white marble, flowing around the arches, like ribbons. The words are verses from the Quran, the holy book of Islam. They speak of paradise, and of the Day of Judgement, and of God's mercy, and of the gardens of heaven, where the faithful will rest, for ever.

The writing was designed by a great calligrapher, called Amanat Khan. He came from Shiraz, in Persia, the city of poets, and roses, and gardens. He was one of the finest calligraphers in the whole Mughal Empire. Calligraphy, beautiful writing, is one of the highest arts in the Islamic world. Because the words of God are sacred, they must be written as beautifully as possible.

--

{img:persian-calligrapher-reed-pen-ink-paper-low-desk-concentrating-mughal}
Amanat Khan wrote the verses on paper, first, with a pen made of reed, and black ink. Then the designs were traced onto the marble, and the letters were cut out, and filled with black marble, or jasper, in the same way as the flowers. Every letter was cut separately, and fitted into the white stone, so that the writing flows, smoothly, and perfectly, around the arches.

But Amanat Khan did something very clever. The words higher up on the arches are further away from the eye of the person standing below. So they look smaller. To make all the writing look the same size, from the ground, he made the letters at the top bigger than the letters at the bottom. So, when you stand in front of the arch, and look up, the writing seems perfectly even, all the way around. It is a kind of optical trick. And it works perfectly.

{img:looking-up-at-tall-arch-calligraphy-band-sky-perspective-visitor}
Amanat Khan was so respected, and so proud of his work, that he was allowed to sign it. On the inside of the great tomb, near the end of one of the inscriptions, he wrote his name. And the date, 1638 or 1639. He was the only person who worked on the Taj Mahal who was allowed to sign his name on it. All the other workers, the architects, the carvers, the inlayers, the thousands of labourers, remain unknown.

Noor is fascinated by the calligraphy. She tries to copy the letters, at home, with a reed pen, and ink, on scraps of paper. She can read a little, because her mother has taught her, and she knows some of the verses by heart. She loves the shapes of the letters. The long, sweeping lines, and the dots, and the curves. She says that each letter is like a little dancer.

--

One day, an old calligrapher, who worked with Amanat Khan, sees Noor copying the letters, outside the workshop, sitting on the ground, with her paper on her knees. He stops, and looks at her work. He says that it is not bad, for a girl of twelve. He shows her how to hold the pen, at the right angle, and how to make the strokes, smoothly, with one movement. And he gives her a good reed pen, of her own.

{img:old-calligrapher-teaching-young-girl-pen-angle-paper-on-knees-courtyard}
After that, Noor practises every day. She writes the letters of the alphabet, again and again, until they are smooth, and even. Then she writes words. Then whole verses. Her mother is proud of her. Her father says that perhaps she will be a calligrapher, one day. Her grandfather says that girls do not become calligraphers. But Noor's mother says that some of the princesses of the court are very fine calligraphers, and poets. So why not Noor?

The verses around the doors of the great tomb are still there today, black on white, as clear and perfect as the day they were made. Visitors from all over the world look up at them, and admire them, even if they cannot read them. They do not need to read them, to feel their beauty. And the verses at the great gateway still invite visitors, as they did in 1643, to enter the garden of paradise.
