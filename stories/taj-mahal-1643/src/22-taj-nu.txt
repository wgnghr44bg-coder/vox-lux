Today, the Taj Mahal is one of the most visited places on earth. Every year, about seven or eight million people come to see it. From every part of India, and from every country in the world. They come early in the morning, to see it at sunrise, when it is pink, and gold. And in the afternoon, when it is dazzling white. And in the evening, when it glows, in the setting sun.

They come through the great gateway, and see it for the first time, framed in the arch, at the end of the garden, just as the architects planned, almost four hundred years ago. And they gasp, and stop, and stare. Many of them have seen pictures of it, all their lives. But the real thing is different. Bigger, and whiter, and more beautiful, and more peaceful, than any picture.

--

{img:today-visitors-shoe-covers-marble-platform-inlay-close-looking-tourists}
They walk slowly along the paths, beside the long water channel, toward the tomb. They take photographs, at the marble bench, in the middle of the garden, where a famous princess once sat, to be photographed. They climb the steps, to the great white platform. They put covers over their shoes, to protect the marble. And they go inside, into the great hall, where the emperor and the empress lie, side by side, in the soft, dim light.

Inside the hall, the guards ask visitors to be quiet. But sometimes, a guard will make a sound, a call, or a clap, so that visitors can hear the famous echo, going round and round the dome, and slowly fading away. And sometimes, a guard will hold a torch close to one of the inlaid flowers, so that visitors can see the stones glowing, from inside. Just as Karim did, in 1643.

{img:today-agra-inlay-workshop-craftsmen-descendants-marble-tabletop-flowers}
Near the Taj Mahal, in the old streets of Agra, there are workshops, where craftsmen still make inlaid marble, in the old way. Tabletops, and plates, and boxes, and elephants, of white marble, inlaid with flowers of coloured stone. Some of the craftsmen say that their families have done this work for generations, since the time of the great tomb. Visitors can watch them at work, cutting the tiny pieces of stone, and fitting them into the marble, by hand.

The Taj Mahal faces some dangers, today. The air of Agra is polluted, by factories, and traffic, and smoke. And the pollution makes the white marble turn yellow, and brown. To clean it, the workers cover the walls with a special clay, a kind of mud pack, like a beauty treatment. When the clay dries, and is washed off, it takes the dirt with it, and the marble is white again. The government has also closed many factories, near the Taj, and banned cars from coming close to it.

--

{img:today-yamuna-river-low-behind-taj-boats-birds-evening-haze}
The river Yamuna, which flows behind the Taj, is also a worry. It is much lower, and dirtier, than it was in Shah Jahan's time, because so much of its water is taken for farms, and cities. Some people worry that, without the river, the foundations of the tomb could be damaged. Engineers watch the building very carefully, and measure it, every year, to make sure that it is safe. So far, it is.

The Taj Mahal is closed to visitors on Fridays, so that people can pray in the mosque, beside it, as they have done since 1643. And on a few nights every month, around the full moon, visitors can come and see it in the moonlight. In small groups, quietly, for half an hour. They stand in the garden, and look at the white dome, shining, silver, in the moonlight. And they do not say anything. There is nothing to say.

If you stand in the garden today, early in the morning, before the crowds, when the mist is rising from the river, and the birds are singing, and the first light of the sun is touching the top of the dome, you can still feel what Shah Jahan felt, almost four hundred years ago. Love, and loss, and memory, and peace. All made into stone, and light, beside the river.
