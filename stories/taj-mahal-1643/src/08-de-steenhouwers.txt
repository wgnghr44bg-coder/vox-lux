On the walls of the Taj Mahal, there are flowers. Not real flowers, but flowers carved in the white marble. Tulips, and lilies, and irises, and poppies, and narcissus, and roses. They are carved in low relief, standing out a little from the flat surface of the stone, so that they catch the light, and make soft shadows. They grow up the lower walls of the tomb, inside and out, in neat rows, like a garden of stone.

The carvers who made them are among the most skilful craftsmen in the empire. They draw the design on the marble first, with charcoal, or with a fine brush. Then they cut away the marble around the flower, very carefully, little by little, with small, sharp chisels, and files, until the flower stands out from the background. Then they shape the petals, and the leaves, and the stems, so that they look soft, and natural, as if they were growing.

{img:jali-marble-screen-octagonal-lattice-light-patterns-carver-finishing}
The most wonderful work of the carvers is the jali. A jali is a screen, made of marble, carved right through, with holes, in delicate patterns, like lace. Around the tombs of the emperor and the empress, inside the great hall, there is a screen of jali, made of eight panels, each carved from a single piece of marble. The patterns are so fine, and so delicate, that it seems impossible that they were made from stone.

To carve a jali, the carver starts with a thin slab of marble. He draws the pattern on it. Then he drills holes, and cuts, and files, very slowly, very carefully, through the stone. One wrong cut, and the whole slab can crack, and months of work are lost. A single jali panel can take a team of carvers more than a year. The light shines through the holes, and makes patterns on the floor, which move slowly, as the sun moves across the sky.

{img:light-through-jali-screen-patterns-on-marble-floor-inside-tomb-soft}
The carvers work in a long workshop, beside Karim's. Karim's best friend is one of them. His name is Ram Lal. He is a Hindu, from a family of stone carvers in Rajasthan. His family has carved the temples, and the palaces, of the Rajput kings, for generations. Now, he carves the flowers of the great tomb. Karim and Ram Lal have worked side by side for ten years. They eat their lunch together, every day, under a tree, and talk about their work, and their families.

Many of the workers on the great tomb are Hindus. Others are Muslims. Some are from Persia, or Central Asia. They work together, side by side, each with his own skills. The building is a mixture of many traditions. The Persian tradition of domes, and gardens. The Indian tradition of carving, and stonework. The Islamic tradition of calligraphy, and geometric patterns. Together, they make something new, that is neither Persian, nor Indian, but Mughal.

--

Ram Lal is carving a panel of flowers, for the outer wall of the tomb. Irises, with tall, curving leaves, and petals that seem to be moving in the wind. He has been working on it for three months. He shows it to Karim, proudly. Karim says it is the most beautiful iris he has ever seen. Ram Lal says it is not finished yet. There is a leaf that is not quite right. He will work on it for another week.

{img:two-craftsmen-friends-eating-lunch-under-tree-hindu-muslim-laughing}
Noor sometimes comes to watch Ram Lal carve. She sits on a stool beside him, and watches, for hours. She loves the way the flowers seem to grow out of the stone, under his chisel. He shows her how to hold the chisel, and how to tap it, very lightly, with the little hammer. He gives her a small piece of broken marble, to practise on. She carves a little flower on it, very carefully, over many days. It is not very good. But Ram Lal says it is a good beginning.

She keeps her little marble flower for the rest of her life. And many years later, when she is an old woman, she gives it to her granddaughter, and tells her about Ram Lal, and the irises, and the great white tomb, and the light coming through the jali, like the sun through leaves.
