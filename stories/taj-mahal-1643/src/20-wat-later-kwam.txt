After Shah Jahan, the Mughal Empire slowly grew weaker. Aurangzeb ruled for almost fifty years, and made the empire bigger than ever. But he spent most of his life fighting wars, and the empire grew tired, and poor. After his death, in 1707, the emperors who followed were weaker, and the empire began to break apart. Other powers rose up, in India. And the great tomb was slowly neglected.

The money for its care ran out. The gardens became overgrown. The fountains stopped. Some of the precious stones were dug out of the walls, by thieves, and sold. Soldiers, and robbers, and invaders, took away the silver doors, and the golden decorations, and the rich carpets. The great tomb was still beautiful. But it was sad, and neglected, and a little forgotten.

--

{img:british-colonial-officers-1850s-visiting-taj-mahal-overgrown-garden}
In the 1800s, the British came to rule most of India. At first, they did not treat the great tomb with much respect. Some British visitors held picnics, and parties, and even dances, on the platform. Some chipped off pieces of the stone, as souvenirs. There were even stories, which may or may not be true, that a British official had thought of taking it apart, and selling the marble. But that never happened.

Then, slowly, people began to realise what a treasure it was. Artists came, and painted it. Writers came, and wrote about it. Photographers came, with the new invention of photography, and took pictures of it, which were seen all over the world. The great tomb became famous, in Europe, and America. People began to call it one of the wonders of the world.

{img:workers-restoring-taj-garden-early-1900s-lord-curzon-era-lamps}
At the beginning of the twentieth century, the British viceroy of India, Lord Curzon, ordered a great restoration of the tomb. The garden was replanted, in the English style, with lawns, and fewer trees. The water channels were repaired. The fountains were made to work again. The damaged stones were replaced. And a great lamp was hung in the hall, inside the tomb, a gift from Lord Curzon, copied from a lamp in a mosque in Cairo. It still hangs there today.

In 1947, India became independent. The great tomb became one of the most precious treasures of the new nation. A symbol of India's history, and its art, and its beauty. In 1983, it was made a World Heritage Site. And in 2007, it was chosen as one of the New Seven Wonders of the World, in a vote by millions of people around the globe.

And what about Karim, and his family? When the great tomb was finished, Karim went to Delhi, where Shah Jahan was building a new capital city, with a great new fort, and a great mosque. He worked there for many years, inlaying flowers in the palaces of the new fort. He became a master of masters, and taught many young inlayers. Ram Lal went with him, and they stayed friends, all their lives.

{img:old-woman-calligrapher-teaching-girls-writing-mughal-courtyard-later}
Noor became a fine calligrapher, as she had dreamed. She copied books, of poetry, and prayers, for the ladies of the court, and taught the daughters of nobles to write beautifully. Ali did not become an elephant driver. He became an inlayer, like his father, and his grandson's grandson was an inlayer too. And old Yusuf lived to see the great tomb finished, and passed away peacefully, in his courtyard, under the neem tree, at the age of seventy-six.

The families of Agra who made the inlay, and carved the marble, kept their skills, and passed them on, from father to son, for generations. And some of their descendants still do the same work, in Agra, today. In little workshops, in the narrow streets near the great tomb, they still cut the coloured stones, and set them into white marble, in the shape of flowers, just as their ancestors did, four hundred years ago.
