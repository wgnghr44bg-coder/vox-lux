Building the great tomb was one of the largest building projects the world had ever seen. About twenty thousand workers were employed, at the height of the work. They came from all over the Mughal Empire, and beyond. From Agra, and Delhi, and Lahore, and Multan. From Persia, and Central Asia, and even, some people say, from Europe. Each group brought its own special skills.

There were brick makers, and lime burners, and stonecutters, and masons. Carvers, and inlayers, and calligraphers, and painters. Carpenters, and blacksmiths, and rope makers. Gardeners, and water engineers. Labourers, who carried the stones, and the bricks, and the mortar. And the elephant drivers, and the ox cart drivers, who brought the materials from far away. A whole army of workers.

--

{img:workers-digging-deep-foundation-wells-river-bank-baskets-ropes}
The first job was the foundation. The tomb stands on the bank of a river, where the ground is soft, and wet. A building as heavy as the Taj Mahal, on soft ground, could sink, or crack, or fall into the river. So the builders dug deep wells, down into the earth, below the level of the river, and filled them with stone, and rubble, and mortar. On top of these wells, they built great arches, and on top of the arches, the platform. The foundations were so good that the building has stood, safely, for almost four hundred years.

The tomb is not made of solid marble, as many people think. Its walls are made of brick, and rubble, held together with a strong mortar, made from lime. The marble is only on the outside, a thick skin of white stone, fixed to the brick, with iron clamps, and mortar. Inside, too, the walls are covered with marble. But the strength of the building is in the brick.

{img:brick-kilns-smoke-workers-stacking-bricks-mughal-agra-plain}
Millions of bricks were made, in kilns, near Agra. And millions of tons of lime, for the mortar, and the plaster, were burned in lime kilns, from limestone. Great quantities of red sandstone came from the quarries near Fatehpur Sikri, about forty kilometres away, for the gateway, the mosque, and the walls. And the white marble came from much further away. From the quarries of Makrana, in Rajasthan, more than three hundred kilometres to the west.

To build the high walls, and the great dome, the builders needed scaffolding. The story says that they built a huge scaffolding of bricks, around the tomb, as big as the tomb itself. And a long ramp, of earth, more than a kilometre long, up which the elephants and oxen pulled the heavy stones, to the top. The workers used great wooden pulleys, and ropes, and wheels, turned by oxen, to lift the stones into place.

--

The work went on for years. The platform, and the tomb, and the dome, were finished first, by about 1643. Then the work continued, on the gateways, and the mosque, and the garden, and the walls, and the other buildings, for another ten years, until about 1653. Altogether, more than twenty years of work. It cost an enormous amount of money. About thirty-two million rupees, in the money of the time. A fortune beyond counting.

{img:paymaster-counting-silver-coins-workers-line-mughal-office-ledger}
The workers were paid, every week, by the emperor's officials. Skilled craftsmen, like Karim, were paid well. Labourers were paid less. The work was hard, especially in the hot season, when the temperature in Agra can rise to more than forty-five degrees. Work began at dawn, and stopped in the heat of the middle of the day, and began again in the late afternoon, until sunset. On Fridays, the Muslim workers rested. On their own holy days, the Hindu workers rested.

Karim's workshop is in a long, low building, beside the tomb, with a roof of thatch, to keep out the sun. There are dozens of inlayers working there, at long tables. Each has his own tools, and his own boxes of coloured stones. It is quiet, in the workshop. There is only the soft sound of the tools, scraping and tapping, and the murmur of voices, and sometimes, someone humming a song. It is a peaceful place, to work.
