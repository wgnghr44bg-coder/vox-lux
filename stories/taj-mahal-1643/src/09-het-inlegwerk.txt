Karim's work is the most delicate of all. He is an inlayer. He makes pictures in the white marble, with tiny pieces of coloured stone. Flowers, and leaves, and vines, in red, and blue, and green, and yellow, and black. The stones are so carefully cut, and so perfectly fitted, that you cannot see the joins, even if you look very closely. It looks as if the flowers have been painted on the marble. But they are stone, set into stone.

This kind of work is called parchin kari, in Persian. In Europe, it is called pietra dura, which means hard stone in Italian. Some people say that the Mughal craftsmen learned it from Italian craftsmen, who came to India. Others say it grew from older Indian and Persian traditions. Whatever its origin, the craftsmen of Agra made it their own, and made it more beautiful than anywhere else in the world.

--

{img:precious-stones-sorted-in-boxes-turquoise-lapis-jade-coral-workshop-table}
The stones that Karim uses come from all over the world. Red carnelian, from Arabia, and from Gujarat. Blue lapis lazuli, from the mountains of Afghanistan. Green jade, from China. Turquoise, from Tibet, and Persia. Yellow amber, from Burma. Black onyx, and coral, and mother of pearl, and agate, and jasper, and malachite. Altogether, about twenty eight different kinds of precious and semi-precious stones are used in the Taj Mahal.

The stones arrive at the workshop in little bags, and boxes. Karim sorts them, by colour, and quality. He keeps them in little wooden boxes, with many compartments, on his workbench. When he makes a flower, he chooses the stones very carefully, to get exactly the right colours. A red poppy might use five or six different shades of carnelian, from dark red, to pale pink, to show the light, and the shadow, on the petals.

{img:inlayer-cutting-tiny-stone-piece-bow-saw-emery-thin-slice-concentrating}
First, Karim draws the design of the flower on paper. Then he cuts out each part of the design, each petal, each leaf, and traces it onto the stones. Then he cuts the stones, into thin slices, and then into the exact shapes, with a small bow saw, made of wire, and a paste of emery powder, and water. Then he grinds and polishes the edges, on a stone wheel, until each piece fits perfectly.

At the same time, another craftsman carves the shape of the flower into the marble, cutting out a shallow hollow, exactly the same shape as the design. Then Karim fits the little pieces of stone into the hollow, one by one, like a jigsaw puzzle, and fixes them in place, with a special glue, made from resin, and wax, and lime. When it is all fitted, the surface is polished, until it is perfectly smooth, and shining.

--

A single flower, the size of a hand, may have fifty or sixty separate pieces of stone. Some of the pieces are smaller than a grain of rice. It can take Karim a week, or more, to make one flower. And there are thousands of flowers, on the walls of the great tomb, inside and out. On the cenotaphs of the emperor and the empress, inside the great hall, the inlay is the finest of all. Some single flowers there have more than sixty pieces of stone.

{img:lamp-held-to-inlaid-flower-glowing-translucent-stones-dark-tomb-chamber}
If you hold a lamp close to one of the flowers, in the dark, the stones glow, from inside, like stained glass, because some of them are translucent. The light shines through them, and they glow red, and orange, and gold. Karim loves to show this to visitors. He takes them into the dark hall, and holds up his little oil lamp, to a flower. And they gasp, in wonder, as the stone flower comes to life, glowing, in the dark.

Noor often draws the designs for her father. She has a good eye, and a steady hand. She draws flowers, and leaves, and he uses some of her designs. There is a little red poppy, on one of the panels inside the tomb, near the floor, which Noor designed, when she was eleven. Nobody knows that a girl designed it. But Karim knows. And Noor knows. And every time she sees it, she smiles.
