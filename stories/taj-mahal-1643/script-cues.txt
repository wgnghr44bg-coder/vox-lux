{img:taj-mahal-1643-white-marble-dome-nearly-finished-yamuna-river-dawn-mist}
Tonight, we travel back almost four hundred years, to the year 1643. We go to India, to the city of Agra, on the banks of a wide, slow river, called the Yamuna. It is one of the greatest cities in the world, in those days. The capital of the Mughal Empire, which rules most of India. A city of palaces, and gardens, and mosques, and bazaars, and nearly half a million people.

[pause]

And on the bank of the river, a little way downstream from the great red fort of the emperor, something extraordinary is rising. A building of pure white marble, shining in the sun. A great dome, like a bubble, or a pearl, or a cloud, resting on a high white platform, with four slender towers around it. It has taken more than ten years to build. And it is almost finished.

[long-pause]

It is a tomb. A tomb for a woman. The emperor, Shah Jahan, built it for his wife, Mumtaz Mahal, whom he loved more than anything in the world, and who passed away twelve years ago. He wanted to build her the most beautiful tomb that had ever been made. A tomb as beautiful as she was. A tomb that would last for ever, and tell the whole world how much he loved her.

[pause]

{img:mughal-workers-thousands-building-site-scaffolding-marble-blocks-agra}
Today, we call it the Taj Mahal. It is one of the most famous buildings in the world. Millions of people come every year to see it. But in 1643, it is still a building site. Thousands of workers are still busy, around it. Stonecutters, and masons, and inlayers, and carvers, and gardeners, and labourers. They have come from all over India, and beyond, to work on it. And they are putting the final touches to the most beautiful building on earth.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:mughal-craftsman-family-courtyard-agra-father-mother-girl-boy-grandfather}
In this story, we will follow one family of craftsmen, who work on the great tomb. The family of Karim, a master inlayer, who sets precious stones into the white marble, in the shape of flowers. His wife Zainab, who keeps their home, in the new town beside the tomb. Their daughter Noor, who is twelve, and loves to draw. Their son Ali, who is nine. And Karim's father, old Yusuf, who was a stonecutter all his life, and now sits in the shade, and watches.

[pause]

We will see how the great tomb was built. How the white marble was brought from far away, on the backs of elephants, and in great carts pulled by oxen. How the stonecutters carved it, and the inlayers decorated it, with flowers of precious stone. How the gardeners laid out the great garden, with its fountains, and its water channels. We will walk through the bazaars of Agra, and visit the emperor's fort. And we will be there, at the great ceremony, on the anniversary of the empress's death.

[pause]

{img:taj-mahal-moonlight-reflection-long-pool-cypress-trees-silent-night}
It is a story about love, and loss, and memory. And about the skill, and patience, and devotion of thousands of ordinary people, whose names we mostly do not know, but whose work has amazed the world for almost four hundred years. The emperor gave the order. But it was their hands that made the Taj Mahal.

[pause]

So let us go to Agra, in the year 1643. Let us walk along the river bank, in the early morning, as the mist rises from the water. And let us see the great white dome, shining in the first light of the sun, as the workers begin another day.

[long-pause]

{img:young-mughal-prince-khurram-meeting-arjumand-meena-bazaar-silk-stalls}
The story of the Taj Mahal begins with a love story. More than thirty years before our story, in the year 1607, a young prince called Khurram went to the royal bazaar, in Agra. It was a special bazaar, held inside the palace, where the ladies of the court sold silk, and jewels, and little trinkets, and the princes and nobles came to buy them, and to joke, and to flirt.

[pause]

At one of the stalls, there was a girl selling silk, and glass beads. She was fourteen years old. Her name was Arjumand Banu Begum. She was the granddaughter of an important minister, and the niece of a woman who would later become empress. The story says that the prince saw her, and fell in love with her, at first sight. And that he went to his father, the emperor, the very same day, and asked to marry her.

[long-pause]

{img:mughal-wedding-procession-1612-elephants-lanterns-prince-on-horse-night}
They had to wait five years. They were married in 1612, when she was nineteen, and he was twenty. It was a great royal wedding, with feasts, and music, and fireworks, and processions of elephants. And the prince gave his bride a new name, Mumtaz Mahal. It means the chosen one of the palace. Or the jewel of the palace. From that day on, they were never apart.

[pause]

Mumtaz Mahal was beautiful, and clever, and kind. She was the prince's closest friend, and his wisest adviser. She went everywhere with him. On his journeys, and on his military campaigns, even into war, riding in a covered howdah on the back of an elephant. She helped him with his letters, and his decisions. She gave him advice, about the empire, and the court. And she looked after the poor, and the widows, and the orphans, and asked him to be merciful to his enemies.

[pause]

{img:shah-jahan-coronation-peacock-court-agra-fort-1628-jewels-nobles}
In 1628, the prince became emperor. He took the name Shah Jahan, which means the king of the world. He was the fifth emperor of the great Mughal dynasty, which had ruled India for a hundred years. He was rich beyond imagination. He loved beautiful things. Jewels, and fine buildings, and gardens, and music, and poetry. He would become the greatest builder of all the Mughal emperors.

[pause]

And Mumtaz Mahal became his empress. He gave her the royal seal, so that she could sign orders in his name. He gave her a great palace, inside the fort at Agra. He gave her jewels, and gardens, and an enormous income. But she did not care much for riches. She cared most for him, and for their children. Over nineteen years of marriage, she gave birth to fourteen children. Seven of them lived to grow up.

[long-pause]

The court poets wrote poems about their love. They said that the emperor and the empress were like two bodies with one soul. Shah Jahan had other wives, as Mughal emperors did. But they were wives only in name. He spent all his time with Mumtaz Mahal. The court historians wrote that his love for her was a thousand times greater than for any other.

[pause]

{img:mughal-miniature-painting-emperor-and-empress-garden-pavilion-roses}
The Mughal painters made pictures of them, in the small, detailed, colourful style of the Mughal court. Pictures of Shah Jahan, with his dark beard, and his jewelled turban, and a halo of gold around his head. And pictures of Mumtaz Mahal, holding a rose, gentle and graceful. Some of these little paintings still exist today, in museums. They show two people who look very much in love.

[pause]

For three years after he became emperor, Shah Jahan and Mumtaz Mahal were very happy. They lived in the palaces of Agra, and travelled across the empire, and planned great buildings, and gardens. And then, in the year 1631, everything changed.

[long-pause]

{img:mughal-army-camp-burhanpur-1631-royal-tents-river-tapti-evening}
In 1631, Shah Jahan went south, with his army, to the city of Burhanpur, in the Deccan, far from Agra. There was a rebellion there, and he went to put it down. As always, Mumtaz Mahal went with him, although she was expecting a baby. They lived in the royal camp, in great tents of silk and velvet, beside the river, in the hot, dry country of the south.

[pause]

In June 1631, the baby was born. A little girl, the empress's fourteenth child. But the birth was very long, and very difficult. And afterward, Mumtaz Mahal grew weaker and weaker. The doctors could do nothing. She knew that she was dying. She called her husband to her side, and they talked, quietly, for the last time.

[long-pause]

The story says that she asked him for two things. That he would never marry again. And that he would build her a tomb, so beautiful that the world had never seen anything like it. Some historians are not sure if this story is true. But Shah Jahan did build the tomb. And whatever she asked of him, he never forgot her. That night, in the early hours of the morning, Mumtaz Mahal passed away, peacefully, in his arms. She was thirty-eight years old.

[pause]

{img:empty-royal-tent-single-lamp-silk-cushions-grief-night-quiet}
Shah Jahan's grief was terrible. The court historians wrote that he shut himself away, in his tent, for a week, and would not eat, or drink, or see anyone. When he came out again, his beard had turned white. He wore plain white clothes, the colour of mourning, for two years. He gave up music, and jewels, and fine food, and perfume. He wept so much, they said, that he had to wear spectacles. He said that the empire had lost all its sweetness for him.

[pause]

The whole empire mourned with him. The court wore white, and there were no celebrations, no music, no festivals, for two years. In the mosques and the temples, people prayed for the empress. She had been loved by many people, rich and poor, for her kindness, and her charity. The poor of Agra remembered how she had given them food, and money, and help.

[pause]

{img:funeral-procession-golden-coffin-elephants-mourners-white-desert-road}
At first, Mumtaz Mahal was buried in a garden in Burhanpur, beside the river. But Shah Jahan had already decided that she must lie in Agra, in the most beautiful tomb in the world. So, six months later, in December 1631, her body was taken out of the garden, and carried north, in a golden coffin, on the long road to Agra. Her son, Prince Shah Shuja, went with her, and a great procession of nobles, and soldiers, and servants.

[pause]

All along the way, the procession stopped, and gave food, and money, to the poor, in her memory. It took many weeks to reach Agra. When it arrived, in January 1632, the empress was buried again, for a while, in a small building in a garden, on the bank of the river. In the place where the great tomb would be built, around her.

[long-pause]

{img:riverside-garden-agra-1632-small-temporary-tomb-river-yamuna-morning}
The land on the river bank belonged to a Hindu prince, the Raja Jai Singh of Amber. It was a beautiful garden, with a palace in it, on a bend of the river, where the water was wide, and calm. Shah Jahan asked for it. The raja gave it to him, as a gift, and the emperor gave him four great houses in Agra, in exchange. It was the perfect place. From the fort, where the emperor lived, he could look down the river, and see it.

[pause]

And so, early in 1632, the work began. The emperor gathered the greatest architects, and builders, and craftsmen of his empire, and told them what he wanted. A tomb in a garden. A garden of paradise. With a building in the middle, of white marble, more beautiful than anything the world had ever seen. And he told them that money did not matter. They could have whatever they needed.

[pause]

Later, people would say that the Taj Mahal was the greatest monument to love ever built. A husband's love for his wife, made into stone. And when you stand in front of it, and see it shining, white and perfect, in the sunlight, it is easy to believe. Shah Jahan could not bring Mumtaz Mahal back. But he could make sure that the world would never forget her. And it never has.

[long-pause]

{img:mughal-inlay-craftsman-1643-turban-white-jama-working-marble-panel-courtyard}
Karim is thirty-six years old. He is a master inlayer, a parchin kar, as the Persians call it. He sets small pieces of coloured stone into white marble, to make pictures of flowers, and leaves, and vines. It is very fine, very slow, very skilful work. Karim learned it from his father's friend, when he was a boy, and he has done it all his life. He is one of the best inlayers in Agra.

[pause]

Karim came to work on the great tomb ten years ago, when the work began. Before that, he worked on the palaces in the fort, and on the tombs of nobles, in the gardens along the river. But the great tomb is the most important work of his life. He has worked on it every day, for ten years, except on Fridays, and holy days. He knows every flower he has made, on its walls.

[long-pause]

{img:mughal-woman-1643-veil-cooking-courtyard-clay-stove-bread-family-home}
His wife Zainab is thirty. She grew up in Agra, in the old city, the daughter of a cloth merchant. She is lively, and warm, and practical, and she manages the house, and the money, and the children, and her husband, who is very good at inlaying stones, and not very good at anything else. She cooks, and sews, and embroiders, and she sells her embroidery in the bazaar, for a little extra money.

[pause]

Noor is twelve. She is quiet, and clever, and she loves to draw. She draws flowers, and birds, and patterns, on scraps of paper, and on the ground, with a stick, and on the walls, with charcoal, which her mother does not like. Karim says that she has the eye of an artist. Sometimes, he lets her come to the workshop, and watch him work. And sometimes, he lets her draw the designs for his flowers, on paper, before he cuts the stones.

[pause]

{img:boy-flying-kite-rooftop-agra-dome-in-distance-sunny-afternoon}
Ali is nine. He is full of energy, and noise, and mischief. He loves kites, and flies them from the flat roof of the house, in the afternoons, with the other boys, and tries to cut their strings with his. He loves the elephants, which carry the marble to the building site, and he follows them around, until the elephant men chase him away. He wants to be an elephant driver, when he grows up. Or a soldier. Or a kite maker.

[pause]

And there is Yusuf, Karim's father. He is sixty-five. He was a stonecutter, for fifty years. He cut the red sandstone for the great fort, in Agra, and the palaces at Fatehpur Sikri, for the emperor Akbar, Shah Jahan's grandfather. Now, his hands are too stiff, and his eyes too weak, to work. So he sits in the courtyard, in the shade of a neem tree, and smokes his hookah, and watches the world go by, and tells stories.

[long-pause]

The family lives in a new town, which has grown up beside the great tomb, to the south. It is called Mumtazabad, after the empress. It was built for the workers, and the craftsmen, and the merchants who serve them. There are houses, and workshops, and bazaars, and caravanserais, for travelling merchants, and mosques, and wells. Thousands of people live there. It is a busy, noisy, friendly place.

[pause]

{img:mumtazabad-street-1643-bazaar-shops-workers-houses-taj-dome-above}
Karim's house is small, but comfortable. It is built of brick, and plaster, around a little courtyard, with the neem tree in the middle. There are three rooms, and a kitchen, and a flat roof, where the family sleeps on hot nights, and dries the washing, and flies kites. From the roof, they can see the great white dome of the tomb, rising above the houses, just a few hundred steps away.

[pause]

Every morning, when Karim goes up to the roof, before dawn, to say his prayers, he looks at the dome. And every morning, it takes his breath away. Even after ten years. He says to Zainab that it is like living next to the moon.

[pause]

In the evenings, the family often sits together on the roof, after supper, to enjoy the cool air. Zainab embroiders, by the light of a little oil lamp. Karim tells them about his day, and the flowers he is making. Yusuf smokes his hookah, which bubbles softly. The children play, or draw, or listen. And all around them, on the other rooftops, the other families of Mumtazabad are doing the same, talking quietly, under the stars.

[long-pause]

{img:taj-mahal-plan-aerial-view-charbagh-garden-four-parts-river-mughal}
The great tomb was designed by a team of the best architects and builders of the empire. The chief architect, people believe, was a man called Ustad Ahmad Lahauri, from the city of Lahore. Ustad means master. He was a mathematician, and an astronomer, as well as an architect. He worked with the emperor himself, who loved architecture, and took a great interest in every detail of the design.

[pause]

They did not design just a building. They designed a whole world. A great walled enclosure, about six hundred metres long, and three hundred metres wide, beside the river. With a garden, and gateways, and courtyards, and a mosque, and a guest house, and the tomb itself. Everything in perfect balance. What is on the left is exactly the same as what is on the right. Like the two wings of a bird, or the two halves of a face.

[long-pause]

{img:great-gate-darwaza-red-sandstone-white-marble-arch-glimpse-of-dome}
The visitor comes in through a great gateway, of red sandstone, decorated with white marble, and inscriptions. It is huge, and dark inside. And through the archway, framed by it, like a picture, you see the tomb for the first time. Far away, at the end of the garden, white and shining, floating above the trees. It is a moment that the architects planned very carefully, so that it would take your breath away.

[pause]

Between the gateway and the tomb, there is the garden. It is a paradise garden, called a charbagh, which means four gardens. It is divided into four equal parts, by two wide water channels, crossing in the middle. In the Islamic tradition, paradise is described as a garden, with four rivers, flowing with water, and milk, and wine, and honey. The garden of the tomb is a picture of paradise, on earth. A place where Mumtaz Mahal could rest, for ever.

[pause]

{img:white-marble-mausoleum-on-platform-four-minarets-symmetry-blue-sky}
At the far end of the garden, close to the river, there is the tomb. It stands on a great square platform, of white marble, about seven metres high. At each corner of the platform, there is a tall, slender tower, called a minaret. In the middle, there is the tomb itself. A building of white marble, with great arched doorways, on every side, and smaller domes at the corners. And above it all, the great dome, round and full, like an onion, or a teardrop, rising up to a golden point, high in the sky.

[pause]

On each side of the tomb, there are two buildings of red sandstone. On the west, a mosque, where people can pray. On the east, an exactly matching building, called the answer, which is there only to keep the balance. It looks just like the mosque, but it cannot be used as one, because it faces the wrong way. It is there because the architects wanted everything to be perfectly balanced.

[long-pause]

The tomb sits at the end of the garden, beside the river, not in the middle of the garden, as most Mughal tombs do. This is unusual. And very clever. Behind the tomb, there is nothing but the river, and the sky. So the tomb seems to float, against the open sky, with nothing behind it. It changes colour, with the light. Pink in the morning. Dazzling white at midday. Golden in the evening. And silver, and blue, in the moonlight.

[pause]

{img:architect-master-ustad-holding-drawings-overseeing-workers-tomb-platform}
Everything in the design is based on careful measurements, and proportions. The height of the dome, the width of the arches, the size of the garden, the length of the water channels. All of them are related to each other, by simple numbers. The architects used a basic unit of measurement, called a gaz, of about eighty centimetres. And every part of the building is a certain number of gaz. That is one of the reasons why it looks so perfect, and so calm.

[pause]

Karim has seen the plans, once, when he was called to the architect's office, to discuss the design of the flowers. He was amazed. Every flower, every leaf, every line of the building was drawn, on the plans. He said to Zainab that the architect must have the whole building in his head, all at once. Like the plan of a whole city. Or the whole sky.

[long-pause]

{img:vast-mughal-construction-site-1630s-brick-scaffold-around-dome-workers-ramps}
Building the great tomb was one of the largest building projects the world had ever seen. About twenty thousand workers were employed, at the height of the work. They came from all over the Mughal Empire, and beyond. From Agra, and Delhi, and Lahore, and Multan. From Persia, and Central Asia, and even, some people say, from Europe. Each group brought its own special skills.

[pause]

There were brick makers, and lime burners, and stonecutters, and masons. Carvers, and inlayers, and calligraphers, and painters. Carpenters, and blacksmiths, and rope makers. Gardeners, and water engineers. Labourers, who carried the stones, and the bricks, and the mortar. And the elephant drivers, and the ox cart drivers, who brought the materials from far away. A whole army of workers.

[long-pause]

{img:workers-digging-deep-foundation-wells-river-bank-baskets-ropes}
The first job was the foundation. The tomb stands on the bank of a river, where the ground is soft, and wet. A building as heavy as the Taj Mahal, on soft ground, could sink, or crack, or fall into the river. So the builders dug deep wells, down into the earth, below the level of the river, and filled them with stone, and rubble, and mortar. On top of these wells, they built great arches, and on top of the arches, the platform. The foundations were so good that the building has stood, safely, for almost four hundred years.

[pause]

The tomb is not made of solid marble, as many people think. Its walls are made of brick, and rubble, held together with a strong mortar, made from lime. The marble is only on the outside, a thick skin of white stone, fixed to the brick, with iron clamps, and mortar. Inside, too, the walls are covered with marble. But the strength of the building is in the brick.

[pause]

{img:brick-kilns-smoke-workers-stacking-bricks-mughal-agra-plain}
Millions of bricks were made, in kilns, near Agra. And millions of tons of lime, for the mortar, and the plaster, were burned in lime kilns, from limestone. Great quantities of red sandstone came from the quarries near Fatehpur Sikri, about forty kilometres away, for the gateway, the mosque, and the walls. And the white marble came from much further away. From the quarries of Makrana, in Rajasthan, more than three hundred kilometres to the west.

[pause]

To build the high walls, and the great dome, the builders needed scaffolding. The story says that they built a huge scaffolding of bricks, around the tomb, as big as the tomb itself. And a long ramp, of earth, more than a kilometre long, up which the elephants and oxen pulled the heavy stones, to the top. The workers used great wooden pulleys, and ropes, and wheels, turned by oxen, to lift the stones into place.

[long-pause]

The work went on for years. The platform, and the tomb, and the dome, were finished first, by about 1643. Then the work continued, on the gateways, and the mosque, and the garden, and the walls, and the other buildings, for another ten years, until about 1653. Altogether, more than twenty years of work. It cost an enormous amount of money. About thirty-two million rupees, in the money of the time. A fortune beyond counting.

[pause]

{img:paymaster-counting-silver-coins-workers-line-mughal-office-ledger}
The workers were paid, every week, by the emperor's officials. Skilled craftsmen, like Karim, were paid well. Labourers were paid less. The work was hard, especially in the hot season, when the temperature in Agra can rise to more than forty-five degrees. Work began at dawn, and stopped in the heat of the middle of the day, and began again in the late afternoon, until sunset. On Fridays, the Muslim workers rested. On their own holy days, the Hindu workers rested.

[pause]

Karim's workshop is in a long, low building, beside the tomb, with a roof of thatch, to keep out the sun. There are dozens of inlayers working there, at long tables. Each has his own tools, and his own boxes of coloured stones. It is quiet, in the workshop. There is only the soft sound of the tools, scraping and tapping, and the murmur of voices, and sometimes, someone humming a song. It is a peaceful place, to work.

[long-pause]

{img:makrana-marble-quarry-rajasthan-1630s-workers-cutting-white-blocks-sun}
The white marble of the Taj Mahal comes from Makrana, in Rajasthan. It is a small town, on the edge of the desert, about three hundred and seventy kilometres west of Agra. Beneath it, there are great hills of marble. The best white marble in India. It is very pure, and very hard, and it has a special quality. It glows, when the light shines on it. As if there were a light inside it.

[pause]

The quarries of Makrana are very old. People have been cutting marble there for more than a thousand years. In the time of Shah Jahan, thousands of workers were sent there, to cut the marble for the great tomb. They cut the stone out of the hillside, in great blocks, with iron chisels, and hammers, and wedges. Some of the blocks are enormous, as big as a small room, and weigh many tons.

[long-pause]

{img:elephant-pulling-marble-block-on-cart-road-mahout-dusty-landscape}
Getting the marble from Makrana to Agra was a huge task. The blocks were loaded onto great wooden carts, with big, solid wheels, pulled by teams of oxen, or buffaloes, or elephants. Some carts needed thirty oxen, or more, to pull them. They travelled slowly, along the dusty roads, across the dry plains of Rajasthan, for weeks, stopping at villages, and wells, and caravanserais, on the way.

[pause]

The story says that a thousand elephants were used to carry the materials for the Taj Mahal. Nobody knows if that number is true. But there were certainly many elephants. The elephants were very useful. They could pull great weights, and lift heavy stones, with their trunks, and push carts out of the mud. They were patient, and strong, and clever. And each one had its own driver, called a mahout, who sat on its neck, and talked to it, and guided it.

[pause]

{img:boy-watching-elephant-mahout-bathing-elephant-river-yamuna-evening}
Ali loves the elephants. Every evening, when the work is finished, he goes down to the river, to watch the mahouts washing their elephants. The elephants walk slowly into the water, and lie down, on their sides, and the mahouts scrub them with brushes, and stones, while the elephants spray water over themselves, with their trunks. They seem to enjoy it very much. And so does Ali.

[pause]

One of the mahouts, an old man called Bhola, has become Ali's friend. He lets Ali sit on the bank, and help, by handing him the brushes, and the stones. His elephant is a big, gentle female, called Champa. She is about forty years old, and she has been carrying marble for the great tomb for ten years. Bhola says she is the cleverest elephant in Agra. Once, she lifted Ali up, with her trunk, very gently, and put him on her back. Ali has never been so happy.

[long-pause]

When the marble arrives at Agra, it is unloaded, and stored in great yards, near the tomb. The stonecutters look at each block carefully, to find any flaws, or cracks. Then they cut it, with long iron saws, with sand and water, into slabs, and blocks, of the right shapes and sizes. It is slow, hard work. A big block of marble can take days to cut.

[pause]

{img:stonecutters-sawing-marble-slabs-sand-water-workshop-yard-agra}
The marble is cut very precisely. Every piece has its own place in the building, which the architects have planned. The stonecutters mark each piece, with a little sign, or a number, so that the masons know where it goes. Some of these marks can still be seen today, on the back of the stones, by the people who repair the building.

[pause]

Old Yusuf sometimes walks to the marble yard, slowly, with his stick, to watch the stonecutters at work. He knows many of them. He gives them advice, which they do not always want. He runs his old hand over the smooth white marble, and shakes his head, in wonder. He says that in fifty years of cutting stone, he never saw marble as beautiful as this. And he wishes he were young again, so that he could work on it too.

[long-pause]

{img:marble-carvers-relief-flowers-dado-panel-chisels-mughal-workshop}
On the walls of the Taj Mahal, there are flowers. Not real flowers, but flowers carved in the white marble. Tulips, and lilies, and irises, and poppies, and narcissus, and roses. They are carved in low relief, standing out a little from the flat surface of the stone, so that they catch the light, and make soft shadows. They grow up the lower walls of the tomb, inside and out, in neat rows, like a garden of stone.

[pause]

The carvers who made them are among the most skilful craftsmen in the empire. They draw the design on the marble first, with charcoal, or with a fine brush. Then they cut away the marble around the flower, very carefully, little by little, with small, sharp chisels, and files, until the flower stands out from the background. Then they shape the petals, and the leaves, and the stems, so that they look soft, and natural, as if they were growing.

[long-pause]

{img:jali-marble-screen-octagonal-lattice-light-patterns-carver-finishing}
The most wonderful work of the carvers is the jali. A jali is a screen, made of marble, carved right through, with holes, in delicate patterns, like lace. Around the tombs of the emperor and the empress, inside the great hall, there is a screen of jali, made of eight panels, each carved from a single piece of marble. The patterns are so fine, and so delicate, that it seems impossible that they were made from stone.

[pause]

To carve a jali, the carver starts with a thin slab of marble. He draws the pattern on it. Then he drills holes, and cuts, and files, very slowly, very carefully, through the stone. One wrong cut, and the whole slab can crack, and months of work are lost. A single jali panel can take a team of carvers more than a year. The light shines through the holes, and makes patterns on the floor, which move slowly, as the sun moves across the sky.

[pause]

{img:light-through-jali-screen-patterns-on-marble-floor-inside-tomb-soft}
The carvers work in a long workshop, beside Karim's. Karim's best friend is one of them. His name is Ram Lal. He is a Hindu, from a family of stone carvers in Rajasthan. His family has carved the temples, and the palaces, of the Rajput kings, for generations. Now, he carves the flowers of the great tomb. Karim and Ram Lal have worked side by side for ten years. They eat their lunch together, every day, under a tree, and talk about their work, and their families.

[pause]

Many of the workers on the great tomb are Hindus. Others are Muslims. Some are from Persia, or Central Asia. They work together, side by side, each with his own skills. The building is a mixture of many traditions. The Persian tradition of domes, and gardens. The Indian tradition of carving, and stonework. The Islamic tradition of calligraphy, and geometric patterns. Together, they make something new, that is neither Persian, nor Indian, but Mughal.

[long-pause]

Ram Lal is carving a panel of flowers, for the outer wall of the tomb. Irises, with tall, curving leaves, and petals that seem to be moving in the wind. He has been working on it for three months. He shows it to Karim, proudly. Karim says it is the most beautiful iris he has ever seen. Ram Lal says it is not finished yet. There is a leaf that is not quite right. He will work on it for another week.

[pause]

{img:two-craftsmen-friends-eating-lunch-under-tree-hindu-muslim-laughing}
Noor sometimes comes to watch Ram Lal carve. She sits on a stool beside him, and watches, for hours. She loves the way the flowers seem to grow out of the stone, under his chisel. He shows her how to hold the chisel, and how to tap it, very lightly, with the little hammer. He gives her a small piece of broken marble, to practise on. She carves a little flower on it, very carefully, over many days. It is not very good. But Ram Lal says it is a good beginning.

[pause]

She keeps her little marble flower for the rest of her life. And many years later, when she is an old woman, she gives it to her granddaughter, and tells her about Ram Lal, and the irises, and the great white tomb, and the light coming through the jali, like the sun through leaves.

[long-pause]

{img:pietra-dura-inlay-flowers-carnelian-lapis-jade-white-marble-close-detail}
Karim's work is the most delicate of all. He is an inlayer. He makes pictures in the white marble, with tiny pieces of coloured stone. Flowers, and leaves, and vines, in red, and blue, and green, and yellow, and black. The stones are so carefully cut, and so perfectly fitted, that you cannot see the joins, even if you look very closely. It looks as if the flowers have been painted on the marble. But they are stone, set into stone.

[pause]

This kind of work is called parchin kari, in Persian. In Europe, it is called pietra dura, which means hard stone in Italian. Some people say that the Mughal craftsmen learned it from Italian craftsmen, who came to India. Others say it grew from older Indian and Persian traditions. Whatever its origin, the craftsmen of Agra made it their own, and made it more beautiful than anywhere else in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:precious-stones-sorted-in-boxes-turquoise-lapis-jade-coral-workshop-table}
The stones that Karim uses come from all over the world. Red carnelian, from Arabia, and from Gujarat. Blue lapis lazuli, from the mountains of Afghanistan. Green jade, from China. Turquoise, from Tibet, and Persia. Yellow amber, from Burma. Black onyx, and coral, and mother of pearl, and agate, and jasper, and malachite. Altogether, about twenty eight different kinds of precious and semi-precious stones are used in the Taj Mahal.

[pause]

The stones arrive at the workshop in little bags, and boxes. Karim sorts them, by colour, and quality. He keeps them in little wooden boxes, with many compartments, on his workbench. When he makes a flower, he chooses the stones very carefully, to get exactly the right colours. A red poppy might use five or six different shades of carnelian, from dark red, to pale pink, to show the light, and the shadow, on the petals.

[pause]

{img:inlayer-cutting-tiny-stone-piece-bow-saw-emery-thin-slice-concentrating}
First, Karim draws the design of the flower on paper. Then he cuts out each part of the design, each petal, each leaf, and traces it onto the stones. Then he cuts the stones, into thin slices, and then into the exact shapes, with a small bow saw, made of wire, and a paste of emery powder, and water. Then he grinds and polishes the edges, on a stone wheel, until each piece fits perfectly.

[pause]

At the same time, another craftsman carves the shape of the flower into the marble, cutting out a shallow hollow, exactly the same shape as the design. Then Karim fits the little pieces of stone into the hollow, one by one, like a jigsaw puzzle, and fixes them in place, with a special glue, made from resin, and wax, and lime. When it is all fitted, the surface is polished, until it is perfectly smooth, and shining.

[long-pause]

A single flower, the size of a hand, may have fifty or sixty separate pieces of stone. Some of the pieces are smaller than a grain of rice. It can take Karim a week, or more, to make one flower. And there are thousands of flowers, on the walls of the great tomb, inside and out. On the cenotaphs of the emperor and the empress, inside the great hall, the inlay is the finest of all. Some single flowers there have more than sixty pieces of stone.

[pause]

{img:lamp-held-to-inlaid-flower-glowing-translucent-stones-dark-tomb-chamber}
If you hold a lamp close to one of the flowers, in the dark, the stones glow, from inside, like stained glass, because some of them are translucent. The light shines through them, and they glow red, and orange, and gold. Karim loves to show this to visitors. He takes them into the dark hall, and holds up his little oil lamp, to a flower. And they gasp, in wonder, as the stone flower comes to life, glowing, in the dark.

[pause]

Noor often draws the designs for her father. She has a good eye, and a steady hand. She draws flowers, and leaves, and he uses some of her designs. There is a little red poppy, on one of the panels inside the tomb, near the floor, which Noor designed, when she was eleven. Nobody knows that a girl designed it. But Karim knows. And Noor knows. And every time she sees it, she smiles.

[long-pause]

{img:black-marble-calligraphy-inlaid-white-arch-frame-quranic-verses-taj}
Around the great arched doorways of the tomb, there are words. Long bands of beautiful writing, in black stone, set into the white marble, flowing around the arches, like ribbons. The words are verses from the Quran, the holy book of Islam. They speak of paradise, and of the Day of Judgement, and of God's mercy, and of the gardens of heaven, where the faithful will rest, for ever.

[pause]

The writing was designed by a great calligrapher, called Amanat Khan. He came from Shiraz, in Persia, the city of poets, and roses, and gardens. He was one of the finest calligraphers in the whole Mughal Empire. Calligraphy, beautiful writing, is one of the highest arts in the Islamic world. Because the words of God are sacred, they must be written as beautifully as possible.

[long-pause]

{img:persian-calligrapher-reed-pen-ink-paper-low-desk-concentrating-mughal}
Amanat Khan wrote the verses on paper, first, with a pen made of reed, and black ink. Then the designs were traced onto the marble, and the letters were cut out, and filled with black marble, or jasper, in the same way as the flowers. Every letter was cut separately, and fitted into the white stone, so that the writing flows, smoothly, and perfectly, around the arches.

[pause]

But Amanat Khan did something very clever. The words higher up on the arches are further away from the eye of the person standing below. So they look smaller. To make all the writing look the same size, from the ground, he made the letters at the top bigger than the letters at the bottom. So, when you stand in front of the arch, and look up, the writing seems perfectly even, all the way around. It is a kind of optical trick. And it works perfectly.

[pause]

{img:looking-up-at-tall-arch-calligraphy-band-sky-perspective-visitor}
Amanat Khan was so respected, and so proud of his work, that he was allowed to sign it. On the inside of the great tomb, near the end of one of the inscriptions, he wrote his name. And the date, 1638 or 1639. He was the only person who worked on the Taj Mahal who was allowed to sign his name on it. All the other workers, the architects, the carvers, the inlayers, the thousands of labourers, remain unknown.

[pause]

Noor is fascinated by the calligraphy. She tries to copy the letters, at home, with a reed pen, and ink, on scraps of paper. She can read a little, because her mother has taught her, and she knows some of the verses by heart. She loves the shapes of the letters. The long, sweeping lines, and the dots, and the curves. She says that each letter is like a little dancer.

[long-pause]

One day, an old calligrapher, who worked with Amanat Khan, sees Noor copying the letters, outside the workshop, sitting on the ground, with her paper on her knees. He stops, and looks at her work. He says that it is not bad, for a girl of twelve. He shows her how to hold the pen, at the right angle, and how to make the strokes, smoothly, with one movement. And he gives her a good reed pen, of her own.

[pause]

{img:old-calligrapher-teaching-young-girl-pen-angle-paper-on-knees-courtyard}
After that, Noor practises every day. She writes the letters of the alphabet, again and again, until they are smooth, and even. Then she writes words. Then whole verses. Her mother is proud of her. Her father says that perhaps she will be a calligrapher, one day. Her grandfather says that girls do not become calligraphers. But Noor's mother says that some of the princesses of the court are very fine calligraphers, and poets. So why not Noor?

[pause]

The verses around the doors of the great tomb are still there today, black on white, as clear and perfect as the day they were made. Visitors from all over the world look up at them, and admire them, even if they cannot read them. They do not need to read them, to feel their beauty. And the verses at the great gateway still invite visitors, as they did in 1643, to enter the garden of paradise.

[long-pause]

{img:great-white-onion-dome-taj-mahal-lotus-top-gilded-finial-sky}
The great dome of the Taj Mahal is one of the most beautiful domes in the world. It is shaped like an onion, or a bud, or a raindrop. It rises from a round base, called a drum, swells out, gently, and then curves in again, to a point at the top. It is about thirty-five metres high, by itself. And with the building beneath it, the top of the dome is about seventy-three metres above the ground. As high as a twenty-storey building.

[pause]

On the very top of the dome, there is a tall finial. A pointed ornament, made of metal, and covered with gold. It rises from a carved marble lotus flower, at the top of the dome. At the very top of the finial, there is a crescent moon, the symbol of Islam. People say that the original finial was made of solid gold. But it was replaced, much later, by one of bronze, covered with gold leaf.

[long-pause]

{img:inner-dome-ceiling-hall-cenotaphs-octagonal-chamber-soft-light-marble}
There are actually two domes. The great outer dome, which you see from outside. And a smaller inner dome, inside it, which forms the ceiling of the great hall, where the cenotaphs of the emperor and the empress stand. Between the two domes, there is a large, empty space. The architects did this so that the outer dome could be very tall, and impressive, from the outside, while the inner hall had a ceiling of a more pleasing height, from the inside.

[pause]

Building the dome was the most difficult and dangerous part of the whole project. The workers had to build it, ring by ring, of brick, and mortar, high above the ground, on the great brick scaffolding. Each ring was slightly smaller than the one below, so that the dome curved inward, until it met at the top. Then it was covered with marble, slab by slab, each one cut to exactly the right curve.

[pause]

{img:workers-on-high-scaffold-placing-marble-on-dome-ropes-pulleys-sky}
Karim remembers watching the dome being built, when he was younger, about five years ago. Every day, it grew a little higher. The workers on the scaffolding looked like tiny ants, against the sky. There were ropes, and pulleys, and baskets, going up and down. And the shouts of the workers, calling to each other, high above. Everyone held their breath, when the last stones were placed at the top. And when the dome was finished, there was a great celebration.

[pause]

The four minarets, at the corners of the platform, are tall, slender towers, about forty metres high, with little balconies, and little domes on top. They are not quite straight. They lean a little, outward, away from the tomb. This was done on purpose. If there is ever an earthquake, and the minarets fall, they will fall outward, away from the tomb, and not onto it. And from below, the slight lean makes them look perfectly straight.

[long-pause]

{img:minaret-slight-outward-lean-platform-corner-evening-light-white-marble}
Inside the great hall, beneath the dome, there is a strange and beautiful echo. If you make a sound, in the hall, it echoes around the dome, and comes back to you, again and again, slowly fading. A single note, sung in the hall, can be heard for many seconds. The echo is soft, and gentle, and sounds a little like the sea, or like a prayer. People say that the hall was designed to echo the prayers of the faithful, up to heaven.

[pause]

Ali loves the echo. When nobody is looking, he goes into the hall, and claps his hands, once, and listens to the sound, going round and round, up into the dome, and slowly fading away. Then he sings a note, and listens again. Once, an official catches him, and scolds him, and sends him out. But the next week, Ali does it again. He cannot resist.

[pause]

From the roof of his house, at night, Ali can see the dome, pale against the stars. He tries to imagine how big it is inside. Big enough, his father says, for a whole house to fit in, with room to spare. Ali tries to count how many elephants could stand inside it. He falls asleep before he finishes counting.

[long-pause]

{img:charbagh-garden-water-channel-fountains-cypress-fruit-trees-flowers-mughal}
While the builders finish the tomb, the gardeners are making the garden. The paradise garden, between the gateway and the tomb. It is a huge square, divided into four parts, by two wide water channels, which cross in the middle. And each of the four parts is divided again, into four smaller parts, by paths. And each of those into four again. Sixteen flower beds, in each quarter, sixty-four altogether.

[pause]

In the middle of the garden, where the two water channels cross, there is a raised platform, of white marble, with a pool, and fountains. From here, looking along the long water channel, you can see the tomb, at the end, reflected in the still water. It is one of the most famous views in the world. The tomb, and its reflection, and the cypress trees on each side, standing like guards.

[long-pause]

{img:mughal-gardeners-planting-cypress-trees-rows-water-channel-workers}
The water for the garden comes from the river. But the garden is higher than the river. So the water must be lifted up. Behind the garden walls, beside the river, there are great water wheels, turned by oxen, walking round and round in circles. The wheels lift the water, in buckets, up into a channel, which carries it into big tanks, above the garden. From the tanks, the water flows down, through pipes, into the water channels, and the fountains, and the flower beds.

[pause]

The fountains are very clever. Each fountain has its own little pot, of copper, underneath it, which fills with water first. So that the water comes out of every fountain at the same pressure, at the same time, and the fountains all rise to the same height. The gardeners turn them on in the evenings, and on special days. They make a soft, splashing, musical sound, and fill the air with a fine, cool mist.

[pause]

{img:fountains-playing-marble-pool-tomb-reflection-evening-gardeners-resting}
The gardeners plant trees, and flowers, in the garden beds. Cypress trees, along the water channels, tall and dark, and pointing up to heaven. They are a symbol of death, and of eternal life. And fruit trees, in the flower beds. Mangoes, and oranges, and lemons, and pomegranates, and apples, and cherries. The fruit trees are a symbol of life, and of the gifts of paradise. And there are flowers everywhere. Roses, and tulips, and lilies, and jasmine, and marigolds.

[pause]

In the Mughal garden, the flowers are planted in beds, which are lower than the paths. So that, when you walk along the paths, the flowers are at the level of your feet, like a carpet. And the fruit trees give shade. In the old days, the garden was much fuller, and more crowded with trees, and flowers, than it is today. It was a real garden of paradise. Shady, and green, and full of the scent of flowers, and the sound of water, and birds.

[long-pause]

The head gardener is a Persian, called Mirza. He comes from Isfahan, where the gardens are famous. He is a small, quiet man, who loves plants more than people. He walks through the garden every morning, at dawn, checking every tree, and every flower, and every water channel. He talks to the plants, in Persian. The other gardeners laugh at him, a little. But the plants grow beautifully.

[pause]

{img:persian-head-gardener-dawn-inspecting-roses-dew-mist-garden-paths}
Zainab sometimes takes the children to the garden, in the late afternoon, when the work is finished, and the officials let the families of the workers in. They walk along the paths, between the flower beds, and smell the roses, and sit by the fountains, in the cool of the evening. Ali dips his feet in the water channel, until his mother tells him to stop. Noor draws the flowers, in her little book.

[pause]

Zainab sits on a marble bench, and looks at the tomb, at the end of the garden, glowing pink and gold, in the evening light. She thinks about the empress, who lies there. A woman who had everything, and lost it, so young. And about the emperor, who loved her so much. She thinks that she is lucky. She has her husband, and her children, and her home. And she says a little prayer, for the empress, and for herself.

[long-pause]

{img:agra-bazaar-1643-crowded-street-spice-stalls-silk-camels-elephants-dust}
Agra, in 1643, is one of the greatest cities in the world. It stretches for miles, along both banks of the river Yamuna. It is full of palaces, and mansions, and gardens, belonging to the nobles of the court. And mosques, and temples, and markets, and caravanserais, and narrow, crowded streets, full of people, and animals, and noise, and colour, and smells.

[pause]

The bazaars of Agra are famous. There are bazaars for everything. A bazaar for cloth, where you can buy silks, and cottons, and muslins, so fine that a whole length of cloth can be pulled through a ring. A bazaar for spices, with great sacks and baskets of pepper, and cardamom, and cloves, and cinnamon, and turmeric, and saffron. A bazaar for jewels, and gold, and silver. A bazaar for horses, and camels, and elephants.

[long-pause]

{img:spice-merchant-stall-heaps-colourful-spices-baskets-scales-agra-bazaar}
Zainab goes to the bazaar once a week, with Noor, to buy what the family needs, and to sell her embroidery. They walk from Mumtazabad, along the river, into the city. It takes about an hour. The streets are crowded with people, from every part of the empire, and beyond. Persians, and Afghans, and Turks, and Armenians, and Arabs. Hindus, and Muslims, and Jains, and Christians. And even, sometimes, a European, in strange clothes, with a pale face, and a beard.

[pause]

In the cloth bazaar, Zainab sells her embroidery to a merchant, an old friend of her father's. She embroiders flowers, on cotton and silk, with coloured silk threads. Little shawls, and caps, and cushion covers. The merchant pays her well. He says that her flowers are almost as beautiful as the ones on the great tomb. Zainab laughs, and says that her husband would not agree.

[pause]

{img:mother-and-daughter-buying-sweets-halwai-shop-jalebi-frying-agra}
Then they go shopping. They buy rice, and lentils, and flour, and ghee, and spices, and vegetables, and fruit. Mangoes, in the summer, and oranges, in the winter. And, if there is a little money left over, a treat. Sweets, from the sweet shop, where a fat, cheerful man fries spirals of sweet batter, in hot oil, and dips them in syrup. Noor loves them. She eats them on the way home, very slowly, licking the syrup from her fingers.

[pause]

The city is full of sounds. The calls of the sellers, in the bazaars. The bells of the temples. The calls to prayer, from the minarets of the mosques, five times a day. The music of drums, and flutes, from the houses of the rich. The trumpeting of elephants. The creak of the carts. The bells on the necks of the camels. And always, everywhere, the murmur of thousands of voices, talking, laughing, arguing, bargaining.

[long-pause]

At midday, the city slows down. It is too hot to do anything. The shops close, and the streets empty, and everyone rests, in the shade, until the heat is less. In the late afternoon, the city wakes up again. And in the evening, it is at its busiest, and most beautiful. Lamps are lit, in the shops, and the houses. People stroll along the streets, and visit their friends, and eat, and listen to music.

[pause]

{img:agra-street-evening-oil-lamps-shops-people-strolling-mosque-dome}
On the way home, in the evening, Zainab and Noor walk along the river bank. The river is wide, and calm, and golden, in the evening light. Boats glide along it, with their sails, carrying goods, and passengers. Women wash clothes on the steps, and children swim, and splash. On the other side of the river, the sun is setting, red and gold, behind the trees. And ahead of them, the great white dome rises above the trees, glowing pink, in the last of the light.

[pause]

Noor says that it looks like it is made of rose petals. Zainab says that it looks like it is made of light. They stand together, for a moment, and look at it. Then they walk on, home, through the dusk, as the lamps are lit, and the first stars come out, over the river.

[pause]

At home, Zainab unpacks the shopping, and puts it away, in the cool storeroom. Noor shows her grandfather the drawings she made in the bazaar. A camel, and a spice seller, and a merchant from Persia, with a tall hat. Yusuf looks at them for a long time, holding them close to his eyes. Then he nods, and says that she has a good hand. From Yusuf, that is great praise.

[long-pause]

{img:agra-fort-red-sandstone-walls-river-yamuna-1643-gate-banners-morning}
Upstream from the great tomb, on the same bank of the river, there stands the Red Fort of Agra. It is a huge fortress, with high walls of red sandstone, more than two kilometres around, and a deep moat. It was built by Shah Jahan's grandfather, the great emperor Akbar, about eighty years before our story. Inside the walls, there are palaces, and halls, and courtyards, and gardens, and mosques. It is the home of the emperor, and the centre of the empire.

[pause]

Shah Jahan has rebuilt many of the palaces inside the fort, in white marble, in his own style. Light, and elegant, with slender columns, and carved arches, and inlaid flowers, like those on the great tomb. There is a pavilion, high on the wall, overlooking the river, from which the emperor can see the tomb of his wife, downstream, shining white, in the distance.

[long-pause]

{img:emperor-at-jharokha-window-fort-wall-crowd-below-sunrise-darshan}
Every morning, at sunrise, the emperor appears at a window, high on the wall of the fort, above the river. Crowds of people gather below, on the river bank, to see him. This is called darshan, which means seeing. The people believe that seeing the emperor brings good luck, and blessings. Some of them come every day. They will not eat their breakfast, until they have seen him. The emperor stands at the window for a little while, and looks down at them, and they bow, and cheer.

[pause]

Then, every day, the emperor holds court, in the Hall of Public Audience. It is a great hall, with rows of white columns, and arches, open on three sides. The emperor sits on a raised throne, in an alcove, at the back of the hall. The nobles stand in front of him, in rows, according to their rank. Ordinary people can come, and bring their complaints, and their requests, to the emperor himself. He listens, and decides, and gives orders.

[pause]

{img:hall-of-public-audience-columns-nobles-in-rows-throne-alcove-mughal-court}
In the afternoon, the emperor meets his most important ministers, and generals, in the Hall of Private Audience, a smaller, more beautiful hall, decorated with inlay, and gold. Here, they discuss the affairs of the empire. War, and peace, and taxes, and buildings, and the reports from the provinces. And here, too, the emperor discusses the great tomb, with his architects, and looks at their plans, and their drawings.

[pause]

Shah Jahan is famous for his jewels. He has the greatest collection of jewels in the world. Diamonds, and rubies, and emeralds, and pearls. In 1635, a new throne was finished for him. It had taken seven years to make. It is made of gold, and covered with precious stones. On its canopy, there are two peacocks, made of jewels, with their tails spread. People call it the Peacock Throne. It is the most valuable object in the world.

[long-pause]

Twice a year, on his birthdays, the emperor is weighed, in a great ceremony, in the fort. He sits on one side of a big pair of scales, and on the other side, the officials put gold, and silver, and silk, and precious things, until the scales balance. Then all of it is given away, to the poor. The people of Agra wait eagerly for these days. Karim once received a silver coin, from the emperor's weighing, when he was a boy. He still keeps it.

[pause]

{img:weighing-ceremony-emperor-on-golden-scales-gold-coins-courtiers-watching}
The emperor does not live in the fort alone. There are his children, and his other wives, and the ladies of the court, and their servants, in the harem, the private part of the palace. Thousands of people. Since Mumtaz Mahal passed away, their eldest daughter, Princess Jahanara, has become the most important lady at court. She is clever, and kind, and generous, and a fine poet. She looks after her father, and runs the household, and gives great sums of money to the poor, and to build mosques, and caravanserais.

[pause]

Shah Jahan is getting older. He is fifty-one, in 1643. His beard is white. He still wears white, on the anniversaries of his wife's death. He still visits her tomb, often, by boat, along the river. And in the evenings, people say, he sits in his pavilion, high on the wall of the fort, and looks down the river, at the great white dome, glowing in the sunset. And he does not say anything, for a long time.

[long-pause]

{img:monsoon-clouds-gathering-over-agra-dark-sky-white-dome-first-rain}
In June, after the terrible heat of the summer, the monsoon comes. For weeks, the people of Agra have been waiting for it. The heat has been unbearable. The sun has beaten down, from a white sky. The earth is cracked, and dry. The river is low, and slow. The leaves on the trees are grey with dust. Everyone is tired, and irritable, and longing for the rain.

[pause]

Then, one afternoon, the sky in the south-east grows dark. Great clouds pile up, black, and purple, and grey, towering into the sky. The wind rises, suddenly, and blows the dust around in clouds. The birds fly low, and the animals grow restless. There is a rumble of thunder, far away. And then, the first big drops of rain fall, on the dusty ground, making little craters, and a wonderful smell of wet earth rises up.

[long-pause]

{img:children-dancing-in-first-monsoon-rain-rooftop-agra-joy-wet}
And then the rain comes down, in torrents. A great roaring curtain of water, pouring from the sky. In a few minutes, the streets are rivers. Everyone runs outside, to stand in the rain, and feel it on their faces. The children dance on the rooftops, and shout, and laugh. The grown-ups smile, and lift their faces to the sky. Old Yusuf sits in the courtyard, under the neem tree, and lets the rain soak him, and says that it is the best feeling in the world.

[pause]

The monsoon lasts for about three months. It does not rain all the time. There are sunny days, and cloudy days, and days of heavy rain. But everything changes. The air is cooler, and fresher. The dust is washed away. The trees turn bright green again. The fields fill with water, and the farmers plant their rice, and their millet. Frogs croak, all night, in the pools. And peacocks dance, and call, in the gardens, spreading their great tails.

[pause]

{img:peacock-dancing-monsoon-garden-rain-drops-green-lush-taj-background}
The river rises, too. The Yamuna, which was low and slow in the summer, becomes wide, and fast, and brown, with the mud from the hills. It rises up the bank, almost to the walls of the great tomb. The builders watch it carefully. But the foundations are strong, and the river does not reach the tomb. It flows past, wide and powerful, carrying branches, and leaves, and sometimes whole trees.

[pause]

For the workers on the great tomb, the monsoon is a difficult time. Some of the work must stop. The mortar does not dry properly, in the wet. The scaffolding becomes slippery and dangerous. The roads are muddy, and the carts carrying the marble get stuck. So some of the workers rest, during the heaviest rains. Others work inside, in the workshops, under the thatched roofs, carving, and cutting, and inlaying.

[long-pause]

{img:inlay-workshop-rain-outside-craftsmen-working-oil-lamps-cozy-dim}
Karim likes working in the monsoon. The workshop is cool, and quiet, and the sound of the rain on the thatched roof is soothing. He works on his flowers, by the light of oil lamps, on the dark days. The other inlayers talk, and tell stories, and sing, as they work. Sometimes, someone plays a flute. And outside, the rain falls, and falls, softly, on the great white dome.

[pause]

The tomb is beautiful, in the rain. The white marble turns grey, and soft, and shining, when it is wet. The rain runs down the dome, in little streams. And when the sun comes out, after a storm, the marble glows, and steams, and sparkles, as if it were covered in diamonds. And sometimes, there is a rainbow, over the river, and the dome stands beneath it, white against the dark clouds.

[pause]

In the evenings, during the monsoon, the family sits on the covered veranda of their house, and watches the rain, and listens to it. Zainab makes hot milk, with ginger, and spices, and fried snacks, which everyone loves in the rainy season. Yusuf tells stories, of monsoons long ago. And Noor draws the rain, falling on the flowers, in the courtyard.

[long-pause]

{img:urs-anniversary-ceremony-1643-taj-mahal-platform-tents-carpets-crowds}
Every year, on the anniversary of Mumtaz Mahal's death, there is a great ceremony at her tomb. It is called the Urs. In the Islamic calendar, the anniversary falls at a different time each year, because the Islamic calendar follows the moon. In 1643, it fell in February, in the cool season, before the hot summer and the monsoon. Let us go back to that day. And this year, the Urs is especially important. It is the twelfth anniversary of her death. And the great tomb is, at last, almost finished.

[pause]

For weeks before the ceremony, everyone is busy. The workers clean the tomb, and the platform, and the garden, until everything shines. Great tents are put up, on the platform, and in the garden, made of silk, and velvet, embroidered with gold. Carpets are spread on the ground, from Persia, and Kashmir. Lamps are hung everywhere. The fountains are turned on. The garden is full of flowers.

[long-pause]

{img:emperor-arriving-by-royal-barge-river-yamuna-tomb-ghat-nobles-boats}
On the day of the Urs, the emperor comes from the fort, by boat, along the river, with his family, and his nobles. A great procession of boats, with silk awnings, and banners, gliding down the river, in the early morning. The emperor steps ashore, at the steps below the tomb, and walks slowly up to the platform. He is dressed all in white. His face is calm, and sad.

[pause]

Inside the great hall of the tomb, beneath the dome, the emperor kneels beside the cenotaph of his wife, and prays. A cenotaph is a memorial, which stands above the real grave. The real grave is in a quiet chamber below, under the floor. The cenotaph is carved from white marble, and covered with the finest inlaid flowers in the whole building. Around it, there is the new screen of carved marble jali, finished this very year. It replaced an earlier screen, of gold, set with jewels.

[pause]

{img:prayers-in-garden-mullahs-reciting-quran-carpets-lamps-dawn-crowd}
Outside, on the platform, and in the garden, thousands of people have gathered. Nobles, and officials, and scholars, and holy men. And the ordinary people of Agra, and the workers of the tomb, and their families. Learned men recite the Quran, all day, and all night, in turns. There are prayers, for the soul of the empress. And there are speeches, and poems, in her honour, by the court poets.

[pause]

And there is charity. A great deal of charity. The emperor gives away enormous sums of money, to the poor, in memory of his wife, as she would have wished. Food is given to everyone who comes. Rice, and bread, and meat, and sweets. Thousands of people are fed. Karim and his family receive food too, as workers on the tomb. And each worker receives a gift of money, from the emperor.

[long-pause]

{img:night-garden-thousands-oil-lamps-tomb-glowing-reflections-urs}
In the evening, the whole garden is lit with thousands of little oil lamps. Along the water channels, and the paths, and the edges of the platform, and the arches of the tomb. The lamps flicker, and glow, and are reflected in the water. The great white tomb glows softly, in the lamplight, against the dark sky. It is so beautiful that people stand, and stare, in silence.

[pause]

Karim and his family stand in the garden, with the other workers, and look at the tomb. It is the first time that Karim has seen it like this, finished, and lit up, at night. He thinks about the ten years of work. The thousands of flowers. The millions of tiny pieces of stone. And he feels proud, and humble, at the same time. He has been part of something that will last for centuries.

[pause]

Noor holds her father's hand. She asks him if the empress can see it. Karim thinks for a moment. Then he says that he hopes so. He says that the emperor built it for her, but the workers built it for her too, with their hands, and their hearts. And then he says, very quietly, that he hopes she likes the flowers.

[long-pause]

{img:diwali-1643-agra-rooftops-clay-oil-lamps-rows-night-glow-festival}
In the autumn, after the monsoon, when the rains have stopped, and the air is clear, and cool, and the skies are blue, there is a festival in Agra, which everyone loves. It is Diwali, the festival of lights. It is a Hindu festival, but in Agra, under the Mughal emperors, people of every faith enjoy it, and join in. Even the emperors have celebrated it, at court, with lamps, and fireworks.

[pause]

Diwali celebrates the victory of light over darkness, and good over evil. It remembers the return of the god-king Rama, to his city of Ayodhya, after many years of exile. The people of the city lit lamps, along the streets, and on the rooftops, to welcome him home. And so, every year, at Diwali, people light lamps, thousands of them, on their houses, and in the streets, and on the river.

[long-pause]

{img:hindu-family-preparing-diwali-sweets-rangoli-pattern-doorstep-colours}
Ram Lal, Karim's friend, invites Karim's family to his house, for Diwali. His house is in Mumtazabad too, a few streets away. For days before the festival, Ram Lal's family has been cleaning the house, from top to bottom, and painting the walls, and decorating them. His wife and daughters have been making sweets. Laddoos, round balls of sweet flour, and sugar, and ghee. And many other kinds, in every colour.

[pause]

On the doorstep, Ram Lal's daughters make a beautiful pattern on the ground, with coloured powders. Red, and yellow, and white, and green. Flowers, and lotus shapes, and circles, and lamps. It is called rangoli. It is meant to welcome the goddess Lakshmi, the goddess of good fortune, into the house. Noor watches them, fascinated. They let her help. She makes a little flower, with red powder, at the edge of the pattern. It looks a little like her poppy, in the great tomb.

[pause]

{img:families-lighting-clay-lamps-together-courtyard-girls-children-smiling-dusk}
In the evening, when it gets dark, everyone lights the lamps. Little clay lamps, called diyas, filled with oil, with a cotton wick. Hundreds of them, placed in rows, along the edges of the roof, and the walls, and the windows, and the doorstep. All over Mumtazabad, and all over Agra, the lamps are lit, one by one, until the whole city is glowing, with thousands and thousands of tiny flames.

[pause]

The two families celebrate together. Ram Lal's family, and Karim's family. They eat together, in Ram Lal's courtyard. Rice, and lentils, and vegetables, and breads, and the wonderful sweets. Ali eats far too many laddoos, and feels sick. The children play with little fireworks, sparklers, and little rockets, which whizz up into the sky. And the grown-ups sit and talk, and laugh, late into the night.

[long-pause]

{img:river-yamuna-floating-lamps-diwali-night-reflections-dome-distant}
Later, the two families walk down to the river. Many people are there, putting little lamps on the water, in small boats made of leaves, and letting them float away, down the river. The river is covered with tiny lights, drifting slowly, in the dark. And in the distance, the great white dome of the tomb rises above the trees, faintly glowing, in the starlight. It is a magical sight.

[pause]

Noor puts a little lamp on the water too, and makes a wish, and watches it float away. She does not tell anyone what she wished for. But she thinks it was something about becoming a calligrapher, or an artist. Ali makes a wish too. He tells everyone. He wished for an elephant of his own.

[pause]

Karim and Ram Lal stand together, by the river, and watch the lamps. They do not say much. They have been friends for ten years. They have worked side by side, on the most beautiful building in the world. They know that the work will end soon, and they may go to different places, to work on other buildings. But they promise each other that they will always be friends. And they always are.

[long-pause]

{img:european-traveller-17th-century-hat-cloak-on-horseback-agra-road-dome}
In the time of Shah Jahan, travellers came to Agra from all over the world. Merchants, and diplomats, and soldiers, and adventurers. From Persia, and Turkey, and Arabia, and Central Asia. And from Europe, too. English, and French, and Dutch, and Portuguese, and Italian. They came to trade, in cloth, and spices, and indigo, and jewels. And they wrote about what they saw, in letters, and books, which were read by people back home.

[pause]

One of the first Europeans to see the great tomb was an Englishman, called Peter Mundy. He worked for the English East India Company, and lived in Agra in 1632 and 1633, when the building had just begun. He wrote in his diary that the emperor was building a tomb for his wife, with excessive labour and cost, and that gold and silver were used as commonly as other metals, and marble as ordinary stone.

[long-pause]

{img:french-jewel-merchant-tavernier-showing-gems-mughal-official-agra}
Another traveller was a Frenchman, called Jean-Baptiste Tavernier. He was a jewel merchant, who travelled to India six times, and bought and sold diamonds, and other precious stones. He was in Agra in the 1640s, and he saw the great tomb, as it was being finished. He wrote that he had seen the beginning and the end of this great work, and that twenty thousand men had worked on it, without stopping.

[pause]

Tavernier was amazed by the tomb. But he was also amazed by the wealth of the emperor, and the court. He saw the Peacock Throne, and the great jewels of the emperor, and wrote about them, in detail. He said that the Mughal emperor was the richest ruler in the world. Richer than the king of France, or the sultan of Turkey, or the shah of Persia.

[pause]

{img:european-merchants-and-mughal-traders-cloth-bales-caravanserai-courtyard}
Later, in the 1660s, a French doctor, called François Bernier, lived in India for many years, as the doctor of a Mughal noble. He visited the great tomb, and wrote a long, careful description of it. He wrote that it was a building far more worthy of being in the list of the wonders of the world than the pyramids of Egypt, which he called unshapely masses of stone. He spent hours walking in the garden, admiring it.

[pause]

These travellers were the first people to tell Europe about the great tomb. Their books were translated into many languages, and read all over Europe. People in London, and Paris, and Amsterdam, read about the white marble tomb, beside the river, in India, built by an emperor for his wife. They could hardly believe it. It sounded like something from a fairy tale.

[long-pause]

One day in the autumn of 1643, an English merchant visits the tomb. He is a young man, with a red face, and a big hat, and a sword. He comes into the workshop, where Karim is working, with an interpreter. He looks at Karim's work, for a long time. He asks how long it takes to make one flower. Karim tells him. He asks where the stones come from. Karim tells him. He asks how much Karim is paid. Karim tells him that too.

[pause]

{img:english-merchant-watching-inlay-craftsman-workshop-interpreter-amazed}
The Englishman shakes his head, in amazement. He says that in England, there is nothing like this. No building like this, and no craftsmen like this. He says that he will write about it, to his family, in London. He gives Karim a coin, an English silver coin, with the head of the English king on it. Karim has never seen an English coin before. He gives it to Ali, who keeps it, as a treasure.

[pause]

The English merchant could not know it. But in about two hundred years, the English would rule India. And the great tomb would become one of the most famous sights of the British Empire. But in 1643, the English in India are just a few small merchants, in a few small trading posts, on the coast. And the Mughal emperor is one of the greatest rulers on earth.

[long-pause]

{img:old-shah-jahan-white-beard-looking-from-marble-pavilion-at-distant-taj}
The great tomb was finished in about 1653, with all its gardens, and gateways, and buildings. Shah Jahan was very proud of it. He wrote a poem about it. He said that it was like the tomb of a sinner who has been forgiven, and that its sight made the sun and the moon shed tears. But his happiness did not last.

[pause]

In 1657, Shah Jahan fell seriously ill. His four sons, who were grown men now, each ruling a part of the empire, began to fight each other, for the throne. It was a long and bitter struggle. In the end, the third son, Aurangzeb, won. He was a stern, serious, very religious man. He did not approve of his father's love of luxury, and beautiful buildings.

[long-pause]

{img:musamman-burj-octagonal-marble-tower-agra-fort-river-view-evening}
In 1658, Aurangzeb took his father prisoner, in the fort at Agra. He did not harm him. But he kept him there, in the palace, for the rest of his life. Shah Jahan was never allowed to leave. He lived in his marble rooms, inside the fort, with his daughter, Jahanara, who chose to stay with him, and look after him, and a few servants. He was no longer the emperor. But he was still the father of the emperor.

[pause]

From the windows of his rooms, in a beautiful octagonal tower, of white marble, on the wall of the fort, Shah Jahan could see the great tomb of his wife, down the river. People say that he spent his days sitting at the window, looking at it. In the morning, when it was pink, and at midday, when it was white, and in the evening, when it was golden. And at night, when it was silver, in the moonlight. He looked at it for eight years.

[pause]

{img:princess-jahanara-reading-to-old-father-marble-room-lamplight-gentle}
Jahanara looked after him lovingly, all those years. She read to him, and talked to him, and comforted him. She was a poet, and a scholar, and a deeply spiritual woman. Her father loved her very much. She was the one person who stayed with him, through everything. Without her, his last years would have been very lonely.

[pause]

In January 1666, Shah Jahan fell ill, and knew that he was dying. He asked to be carried to the window, so that he could see the great tomb, one last time. And there, the story says, looking at the white dome of his wife's tomb, in the distance, he passed away, peacefully, with his daughter beside him, reciting prayers. He was seventy-four years old.

[long-pause]

Shah Jahan was buried in the great tomb, beside his wife. His cenotaph was placed next to hers, in the great hall, beneath the dome. It is the only thing in the whole building that is not perfectly symmetrical. Her cenotaph is in the exact centre of the hall. His is beside it, to the west, a little larger, and a little higher. The perfect balance of the building was broken, at last, so that he could lie beside her.

[pause]

{img:two-cenotaphs-side-by-side-inside-taj-jali-screen-soft-lamplight}
Jahanara lived on for many more years, in Delhi. She passed away in 1681. She did not want a grand tomb, like her mother. She asked for a simple grave, open to the sky, with grass growing on it. On her gravestone, she wrote that nothing should cover her grave but the green grass, because grass is the best covering for the grave of the humble. Her grave can still be seen, in Delhi, today, with the grass still growing.

[pause]

So the emperor and the empress were together again, after thirty-five years apart. Side by side, in the great hall, beneath the dome, in the soft light that comes through the marble screens. And the river flows past, outside, as it did then. And the garden blooms. And the great white dome rises above them both, as it has for almost four hundred years.

[long-pause]

{img:taj-mahal-1800s-overgrown-garden-british-visitors-picnic-neglect}
After Shah Jahan, the Mughal Empire slowly grew weaker. Aurangzeb ruled for almost fifty years, and made the empire bigger than ever. But he spent most of his life fighting wars, and the empire grew tired, and poor. After his death, in 1707, the emperors who followed were weaker, and the empire began to break apart. Other powers rose up, in India. And the great tomb was slowly neglected.

[pause]

The money for its care ran out. The gardens became overgrown. The fountains stopped. Some of the precious stones were dug out of the walls, by thieves, and sold. Soldiers, and robbers, and invaders, took away the silver doors, and the golden decorations, and the rich carpets. The great tomb was still beautiful. But it was sad, and neglected, and a little forgotten.

[long-pause]

{img:british-colonial-officers-1850s-visiting-taj-mahal-overgrown-garden}
In the 1800s, the British came to rule most of India. At first, they did not treat the great tomb with much respect. Some British visitors held picnics, and parties, and even dances, on the platform. Some chipped off pieces of the stone, as souvenirs. There were even stories, which may or may not be true, that a British official had thought of taking it apart, and selling the marble. But that never happened.

[pause]

Then, slowly, people began to realise what a treasure it was. Artists came, and painted it. Writers came, and wrote about it. Photographers came, with the new invention of photography, and took pictures of it, which were seen all over the world. The great tomb became famous, in Europe, and America. People began to call it one of the wonders of the world.

[pause]

{img:workers-restoring-taj-garden-early-1900s-lord-curzon-era-lamps}
At the beginning of the twentieth century, the British viceroy of India, Lord Curzon, ordered a great restoration of the tomb. The garden was replanted, in the English style, with lawns, and fewer trees. The water channels were repaired. The fountains were made to work again. The damaged stones were replaced. And a great lamp was hung in the hall, inside the tomb, a gift from Lord Curzon, copied from a lamp in a mosque in Cairo. It still hangs there today.

[pause]

In 1947, India became independent. The great tomb became one of the most precious treasures of the new nation. A symbol of India's history, and its art, and its beauty. In 1983, it was made a World Heritage Site. And in 2007, it was chosen as one of the New Seven Wonders of the World, in a vote by millions of people around the globe.

[long-pause]

And what about Karim, and his family? When the great tomb was finished, Karim went to Delhi, where Shah Jahan was building a new capital city, with a great new fort, and a great mosque. He worked there for many years, inlaying flowers in the palaces of the new fort. He became a master of masters, and taught many young inlayers. Ram Lal went with him, and they stayed friends, all their lives.

[pause]

{img:old-woman-calligrapher-teaching-girls-writing-mughal-courtyard-later}
Noor became a fine calligrapher, as she had dreamed. She copied books, of poetry, and prayers, for the ladies of the court, and taught the daughters of nobles to write beautifully. Ali did not become an elephant driver. He became an inlayer, like his father, and his grandson's grandson was an inlayer too. And old Yusuf lived to see the great tomb finished, and passed away peacefully, in his courtyard, under the neem tree, at the age of seventy-six.

[pause]

The families of Agra who made the inlay, and carved the marble, kept their skills, and passed them on, from father to son, for generations. And some of their descendants still do the same work, in Agra, today. In little workshops, in the narrow streets near the great tomb, they still cut the coloured stones, and set them into white marble, in the shape of flowers, just as their ancestors did, four hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:black-taj-legend-mehtab-bagh-moonlight-garden-across-river-ruins}
Like every great building, the Taj Mahal has many legends. Some of them are beautiful, and some of them are strange, and some of them are sad. And most of them are not true. But people have told them, for hundreds of years, and they are part of the story of the great tomb.

[pause]

The most famous legend is the story of the Black Taj. People say that Shah Jahan planned to build a second tomb, for himself, on the other side of the river, exactly like the first, but of black marble. And that the two tombs would be joined by a bridge, of silver. One white, for the empress, and one black, for the emperor, facing each other, across the river, for ever. It is a beautiful story. But there is no evidence that it was ever planned.

[long-pause]

{img:moonlight-garden-mehtab-bagh-pool-reflection-taj-across-river-night}
On the other side of the river, opposite the tomb, there is a garden. It is called the Moonlight Garden, Mehtab Bagh. For a long time, people thought it might be the place where the Black Taj was to be built. Archaeologists dug there, in the 1990s. But they found no foundations for a black tomb. Instead, they found the remains of a beautiful garden, with a great octagonal pool. And when the pool was full, at night, the white tomb was reflected in it, perfectly, across the river.

[pause]

So the Moonlight Garden was probably made for looking at the tomb, in the moonlight, reflected in the water. A place where the emperor could sit, at night, and see his wife's tomb, shining white, and its reflection, shining in the pool. Today, the Moonlight Garden has been restored, and visitors can go there, in the evening, and see the great tomb, across the river, as the emperor once did.

[pause]

{img:craftsmen-hands-at-work-old-legend-sad-story-marble-dust-gentle}
Another legend is a sad one. People say that, when the great tomb was finished, Shah Jahan ordered that the hands of the workers should be cut off, so that they could never build anything as beautiful again. This story is told to many visitors, even today. But it is almost certainly not true. There is no record of it, anywhere. And we know that many of the workers went on to work on other great buildings, for Shah Jahan, in Delhi. Including the Red Fort, and the great mosque.

[pause]

There are other legends too. That the marble changes colour, according to the mood of the empress. That on nights of the full moon, the tomb glows by itself. That there are secret rooms, beneath the platform, full of treasure. That the workers were paid in gold, by weight. That the emperor wept so much, while it was being built, that the river rose.

[long-pause]

These stories are not true. But they show how much the great tomb has touched people's hearts, over the centuries. People need stories, to explain things that are too beautiful to understand. And the Taj Mahal is one of those things. It is so perfect, so calm, so lovely, that people cannot quite believe that it was made by human hands.

[pause]

{img:taj-mahal-full-moon-night-dome-silvery-glow-garden-dark-cypress}
But it was. It was made by human hands. By twenty thousand pairs of hands. Hands that cut, and carried, and carved, and inlaid, and polished, for more than twenty years. The hands of Hindus, and Muslims, and Persians, and Indians. Of master craftsmen, and humble labourers. Most of their names are forgotten. But their work is not. It is still there, shining, by the river, for everyone to see.

[pause]

And on nights of the full moon, even if it does not really glow by itself, the great tomb does look as if it is made of light. The moonlight falls on the white marble, and it shines, softly, silver, and blue, against the dark sky. And many people who see it say that it is the most beautiful thing they have ever seen.

[long-pause]

{img:today-taj-mahal-sunrise-visitors-crowd-reflecting-pool-photos}
Today, the Taj Mahal is one of the most visited places on earth. Every year, about seven or eight million people come to see it. From every part of India, and from every country in the world. They come early in the morning, to see it at sunrise, when it is pink, and gold. And in the afternoon, when it is dazzling white. And in the evening, when it glows, in the setting sun.

[pause]

They come through the great gateway, and see it for the first time, framed in the arch, at the end of the garden, just as the architects planned, almost four hundred years ago. And they gasp, and stop, and stare. Many of them have seen pictures of it, all their lives. But the real thing is different. Bigger, and whiter, and more beautiful, and more peaceful, than any picture.

[long-pause]

{img:today-visitors-shoe-covers-marble-platform-inlay-close-looking-tourists}
They walk slowly along the paths, beside the long water channel, toward the tomb. They take photographs, at the marble bench, in the middle of the garden, where a famous princess once sat, to be photographed. They climb the steps, to the great white platform. They put covers over their shoes, to protect the marble. And they go inside, into the great hall, where the emperor and the empress lie, side by side, in the soft, dim light.

[pause]

Inside the hall, the guards ask visitors to be quiet. But sometimes, a guard will make a sound, a call, or a clap, so that visitors can hear the famous echo, going round and round the dome, and slowly fading away. And sometimes, a guard will hold a torch close to one of the inlaid flowers, so that visitors can see the stones glowing, from inside. Just as Karim did, in 1643.

[pause]

{img:today-agra-inlay-workshop-craftsmen-descendants-marble-tabletop-flowers}
Near the Taj Mahal, in the old streets of Agra, there are workshops, where craftsmen still make inlaid marble, in the old way. Tabletops, and plates, and boxes, and elephants, of white marble, inlaid with flowers of coloured stone. Some of the craftsmen say that their families have done this work for generations, since the time of the great tomb. Visitors can watch them at work, cutting the tiny pieces of stone, and fitting them into the marble, by hand.

[pause]

The Taj Mahal faces some dangers, today. The air of Agra is polluted, by factories, and traffic, and smoke. And the pollution makes the white marble turn yellow, and brown. To clean it, the workers cover the walls with a special clay, a kind of mud pack, like a beauty treatment. When the clay dries, and is washed off, it takes the dirt with it, and the marble is white again. The government has also closed many factories, near the Taj, and banned cars from coming close to it.

[long-pause]

{img:today-yamuna-river-low-behind-taj-boats-birds-evening-haze}
The river Yamuna, which flows behind the Taj, is also a worry. It is much lower, and dirtier, than it was in Shah Jahan's time, because so much of its water is taken for farms, and cities. Some people worry that, without the river, the foundations of the tomb could be damaged. Engineers watch the building very carefully, and measure it, every year, to make sure that it is safe. So far, it is.

[pause]

The Taj Mahal is closed to visitors on Fridays, so that people can pray in the mosque, beside it, as they have done since 1643. And on a few nights every month, around the full moon, visitors can come and see it in the moonlight. In small groups, quietly, for half an hour. They stand in the garden, and look at the white dome, shining, silver, in the moonlight. And they do not say anything. There is nothing to say.

[pause]

If you stand in the garden today, early in the morning, before the crowds, when the mist is rising from the river, and the birds are singing, and the first light of the sun is touching the top of the dome, you can still feel what Shah Jahan felt, almost four hundred years ago. Love, and loss, and memory, and peace. All made into stone, and light, beside the river.

[long-pause]

So we leave Agra tonight, in the year 1643, as the evening falls. The sun is setting over the river, in a blaze of orange, and pink, and gold. And the great white tomb is glowing. First golden, then rose pink, then soft violet, as the light fades. The workers are putting down their tools, and leaving the building site, in long lines, walking home to Mumtazabad, tired, and dusty, and content.

[pause]

In the garden, the gardeners are turning on the fountains, for the evening. The water rises, and falls, and splashes, in the marble pools. The cypress trees stand dark, and still. The scent of the roses, and the jasmine, fills the warm evening air. And the parakeets, and the mynah birds, are settling noisily into the trees, for the night.

[long-pause]

{img:courtyard-neem-tree-family-evening-meal-oil-lamp-rooftop-dome-view}
In Karim's house, the family is gathering in the courtyard, under the neem tree. Zainab has cooked the evening meal. Rice, and lentils, and spiced vegetables, and fresh flat bread, hot from the oven. They sit on a mat, on the ground, and eat together, with their hands, from the shared dishes. Old Yusuf tells a story about the old emperor Akbar. Ali tells everyone about Champa, the elephant. And Noor shows her father the letters she wrote today.

[pause]

After supper, they go up to the flat roof, to sit in the cool evening air. Zainab spreads mats, and cushions, and they lie down, and look at the sky. The stars are coming out, one by one, over the river. And just a few hundred steps away, above the dark rooftops, the great white dome rises up, pale, and glowing, in the starlight.

[long-pause]

{img:family-on-rooftop-night-looking-at-moonlit-dome-stars-quiet-agra}
Then the moon rises, over the river. A big, round, golden moon, climbing slowly into the sky. And as it rises, it shines on the great white dome, and the dome begins to glow. Silver, and blue, and white. As if it were made of light, or of the moon itself. The whole family is silent. They have seen it many times. But it is always new.

[pause]

Karim lies on his back, with his hands behind his head, and looks at the dome. He thinks about all the flowers he has made, over ten years. And all the hands that have worked beside him. And the emperor, in his fort, looking down the river at the same dome, in the same moonlight, thinking of his wife. Then Karim closes his eyes. And the children are already asleep, curled up on their cushions, under the stars.
