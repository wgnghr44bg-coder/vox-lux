Tonight we travel back about a hundred and seventy-five years, to the year 1850. We go to a busy harbour town on the east coast of America. It is called New Bedford. And in its harbour, dozens of tall wooden ships are getting ready for long voyages across the oceans of the world.

[long-pause]

It is early morning in autumn, and a soft mist lies over the water. The masts of the ships rise up through the mist, like a forest of bare trees. Seagulls cry and circle over the wharves. On the long wooden piers, there are rows and rows of barrels, thousands of them, stacked in the sun. They are full of oil. And everywhere, there is a strong smell of tar, salt and fish.

[pause]

Men are already at work on the wharves. Coopers are hammering barrels together. Riggers are climbing up the masts, fixing ropes. Carts are rolling along the cobbled streets, loaded with food and supplies. A bell rings on a ship. A sailor calls out to another. And a boy of fourteen stands at the end of a wharf, looking up at a big ship, with a small bag over his shoulder, and his heart beating fast.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:whaling-ship-1850-three-masts-whaleboats-hanging-davits-wharf-new-bedford}
The ships in this harbour are whaling ships. They sail to every ocean on Earth, looking for whales. In those days, the oil from whales was very important. It was used in lamps, to light houses and streets and lighthouses, all over the world. It was used to oil machines in the new factories. Whaling was a big and important business. And New Bedford was the most important whaling town in the world.

[pause]

A whaling voyage was very long. A ship might be away for three or four years, sailing around the whole world. The sailors lived on board all that time, far from home, in a small wooden world. They worked hard and saw amazing things. They visited faraway islands and met people from every corner of the Earth. And they waited, sometimes for weeks, for a whale to be seen.

[pause]

{img:boy-fourteen-1850-standing-wharf-small-bag-looking-up-at-ship-nervous}
Tonight we will sail on a whaling ship, with that boy of fourteen. His name is Will. It is his first voyage. We will meet the captain and his wife, the cook and the old harpooner, and sailors from many lands. We will learn how they lived and worked, what they ate, and how they passed the long evenings. We will sail around Cape Horn, to the Galápagos Islands and to Hawaii.

[long-pause]

We will not follow the hunting of the whales closely. It was hard and dangerous work, and today, we see it very differently. Whales are now protected in most of the world, and people go to sea to watch them, not to hunt them. But tonight, we will try to understand the lives of the people who sailed on these ships, long ago. And we will meet the great whales too, gently, as they swim through the oceans.

[pause]

{img:sperm-whale-gentle-surfacing-calm-ocean-blow-spout-morning-light}
So let us begin on a wharf in New Bedford, on an autumn morning in 1850. The mist is lifting from the harbour. The sun is shining on the water. And the boy with the small bag takes a deep breath, and walks up the gangplank onto the ship that will be his home for the next three years.

[pause]

Along the waterfront, the shops are already opening. There are shops that sell ropes and sails, and shops that sell compasses, telescopes and charts of the oceans. There are shops for sea boots and oilskin coats. There are bakeries that make hard ship's bread by the thousand. Almost everything in this town has something to do with ships and the sea. Even the children play at being whalemen, running along the wharves with sticks for harpoons.

[long-pause]

{img:new-bedford-1850-cobbled-street-whaling-merchants-houses-candle-shops-carts}
New Bedford in 1850 is the richest town in America, for its size. And almost all of its wealth comes from whales.

[pause]

The town lies on a hill, sloping down to a wide harbour on the Atlantic coast of Massachusetts. Near the water, there are wharves and warehouses, rope walks and shipyards. Further up the hill, there are shops and banks, churches and taverns. And at the top of the hill, there are the great houses of the rich whaling merchants, with gardens and fine furniture, and windows looking out to sea.

[long-pause]

In 1850, more than two hundred and fifty whaling ships sail from New Bedford. That is more than from any other port in the world. Thousands of people in the town work for the whaling business. There are sailors and captains, shipbuilders and coopers, rope makers and sail makers, blacksmiths and carpenters. There are men who make candles and men who refine the oil. Almost every family in New Bedford has someone at sea.

[pause]

{img:candle-factory-1850-workers-spermaceti-candles-moulds-racks-bright}
Why was whale oil so important? In 1850, there is no electricity. People light their homes with candles and oil lamps. The best oil for lamps comes from whales, especially from the sperm whale. It burns with a bright, clean light, without much smoke or smell. The finest candles are made from a special wax, also from the sperm whale. They are called spermaceti candles, and they burn very brightly and evenly.

[long-pause]

Whale oil also oils the machines in the new factories, which are spreading across America and Europe. It is used to make soap and paint and leather. And there is another product, called whalebone. It is not really bone, but long, flexible plates from the mouths of some kinds of whales. It is used to make things that need to be strong and springy, like umbrellas, hoops for skirts and stiff corsets for ladies' dresses.

[pause]

{img:lamplighter-1850-lighting-whale-oil-street-lamp-dusk-new-bedford}
In the evenings, the street lamps of New Bedford burn with whale oil. A lamplighter walks along the streets with a ladder, lighting them one by one. The windows of the houses glow with the soft yellow light of oil lamps. People say that the whole world reads by the light of New Bedford. And in a way, it is true.

[long-pause]

The whaling merchants of New Bedford are rich, but many of them are Quakers. Quakers are Christians who believe in simple living and plain clothes. They do not like to show off. So even the richest merchants often wear plain grey and black clothes. Many Quakers also believe strongly that slavery is wrong. New Bedford is a place where people who have escaped from slavery in the South can find work and safety. One of them, a young man called Frederick Douglass, lived and worked in New Bedford, and later became one of the most famous speakers in America.

[pause]

{img:quaker-merchants-plain-dress-counting-house-ledgers-harbour-window-1850}
The people of New Bedford are used to saying goodbye. Every week, a ship sails out of the harbour, on a voyage of three or four years. Wives and mothers and children stand on the wharves and wave. And every week, a ship comes home, after years at sea. Then there are cheers and tears and hugs on the wharves. Families walk up to the hill above the harbour, to watch for the ships. Some houses have a little platform on the roof, where wives can look out to sea. People call it a widow's walk. But mostly, they are places of hope, where people wait for their loved ones to come home.

[pause]

In the evenings, the streets of the rich part of town are lit by lamps that burn whale oil. Their soft yellow light shines on the cobblestones and the white fences. Through the windows of the big houses, you can see families sitting at their tables, reading or sewing by the light of spermaceti candles. All that gentle light, in a way, comes from the sea. It was carried home in barrels, from the other side of the world.

[pause]

The streets near the harbour are much busier and much rougher. There are boarding houses for sailors, where men sleep while they wait for a ship. There are clothing shops, where a greenhand can buy everything he needs for a voyage. And there are men called outfitters, who sell the sailors their gear on credit. The cost is taken from the sailor's share at the end of the voyage. Often, the prices are much too high.

[long-pause]

{img:farm-boy-massachusetts-1850-fields-stone-walls-barn-autumn-family}
Let us meet the boy who is going to sea. His name is William Coffin, and everyone calls him Will.

[pause]

Will is fourteen years old. He was born on a small farm in the countryside of Massachusetts, about thirty kilometres from New Bedford. His father is a farmer, and his mother looks after the house and the animals. He has three younger sisters and a little brother. The farm is small, with stony fields, a few cows, some sheep and an apple orchard. Life is not easy, and there is not always enough money.

[long-pause]

Will has always dreamed of the sea. His grandfather was a whaleman, long ago. When Will was little, his grandfather told him stories of his voyages. He told him about the great whales, as big as houses. He told him about the islands of the Pacific, with palm trees and warm blue water. He told him about storms at Cape Horn and the strange creatures of the deep. Will listened with wide eyes. And he decided that one day, he would go to sea too.

[pause]

{img:grandfather-old-whaleman-telling-stories-boy-fireside-farmhouse-scrimshaw}
His grandfather passed away last winter. Before he died, he gave Will a little present. It was a piece of whale tooth, carved and polished, with a picture of a ship scratched into it with ink. It is called scrimshaw. Whalemen make it during the long evenings at sea. Will keeps it in his pocket all the time. It is his greatest treasure.

[long-pause]

This autumn, Will told his parents that he wanted to go to sea, on a whaling ship. His mother cried. His father was quiet for a long time. But they knew that many boys from the farms went to sea. And they knew that the money Will would earn could help the family. So in the end, they said yes. His father took him to New Bedford, to find a ship.

[pause]

{img:father-son-shipping-office-1850-agent-desk-signing-papers-new-bedford}
In New Bedford, they went to the office of a whaling company. A man at a desk looked at Will and asked him some questions. Was he strong? Was he healthy? Had he ever been to sea? Will said he had not, but he was strong and willing to learn. The man nodded. A boy with no experience is called a greenhand, he said. Then he wrote Will's name on a paper, and Will signed it. He was now a member of the crew of a whaling ship.

[long-pause]

Sailors on whaling ships are not paid wages, like most workers. Instead, each man gets a share of the oil that the ship brings home. This share is called a lay. The captain gets the biggest lay. The experienced sailors get smaller ones. And a greenhand like Will gets a very small one. If the voyage goes well, and the ship comes home full of oil, he will earn some money. If not, he will earn very little.

[pause]

{img:mother-packing-sea-chest-knitted-socks-bible-letters-farmhouse-lamp}
Before he leaves, Will's mother packs a little sea chest for him. She puts in warm clothes, thick socks she has knitted, a Bible, some paper and pencils, and a little bag of dried apples from the orchard. She gives him a letter, and tells him to read it on his first night at sea. His little sisters give him drawings of the farm. His father shakes his hand, and then hugs him tightly. Write to us, his mother says. And come home safe. Will promises.

[pause]

On the evening before he leaves, Will walks once more through the orchard. The apples have been picked, and the leaves are turning yellow and red. He touches the rough bark of the old tree that his grandfather planted. He tries to remember everything. The smell of the barn, the sound of the cows, the light in the kitchen window. He wants to take it all with him, in his memory, for the long years at sea.

[long-pause]

{img:whaling-bark-1850-side-view-three-masts-whaleboats-try-works-deck-harbour}
The ship that Will is joining is called the Hannah Grey. She is a whaling ship of New Bedford, about thirty-five metres long, with three tall masts. She is not new. She has already made five long voyages around the world. But she is strong and well built, and her captain knows her well.

[long-pause]

A whaling ship looks a little different from other sailing ships. Along her sides, hanging from wooden arms called davits, there are four small boats. These are the whaleboats. They are long, narrow and light, pointed at both ends, made of thin cedar planks. When a whale is seen, the whaleboats are lowered into the sea, and the men row out in them. A fifth boat is kept on deck, as a spare.

[pause]

{img:whaleboats-hanging-davits-ship-side-oars-coiled-rope-tubs-close}
In the middle of the deck, there is something very strange, for a wooden ship. It is a big brick oven, with two huge iron pots set into the top. This is called the try-works. It is where the oil is boiled out of the whale blubber, at sea. Under the bricks, there is a shallow tank of water, to protect the wooden deck from the heat. When the try-works are not in use, they are covered with a wooden lid.

[long-pause]

Below the deck, there are several parts of the ship. At the front, in the forecastle, the ordinary sailors live and sleep. It is a small, dark, triangular room, with bunks along the walls, one above the other. There is a table in the middle, and a little lamp hanging from the ceiling. About twenty men live there, with their sea chests. It is crowded and stuffy, and it smells of tar and wet wool and old tobacco.

[pause]

{img:forecastle-whaling-ship-bunks-sea-chests-sailors-lamp-dim-crowded}
In the middle of the ship, below the deck, there is the steerage, where the harpooners and the boatsteerers live. They have a little more space than the ordinary sailors. And at the back of the ship, there are the cabins of the officers. The captain has the biggest cabin, with windows in the stern looking out over the sea. There is also a little dining room, where the officers eat together.

[long-pause]

At the very bottom of the ship is the hold. This is where the barrels of oil are stored, thousands of them. At the start of the voyage, the hold is full of barrels of food and water, and empty barrels waiting to be filled with oil. There are also spare sails and ropes, wood and iron, tools and everything else the ship will need for three or four years. A whaling ship is like a little floating town, carrying everything it needs.

[pause]

{img:ship-hold-barrels-stacked-lantern-cooper-working-casks-dim-light}
When Will first goes on board, he is amazed by how small everything is. The ship looked so big from the wharf. But inside, every space is full. He finds his bunk in the forecastle, a narrow wooden shelf just long enough for him to lie down. He puts his sea chest beside it. He takes out his grandfather's scrimshaw and holds it in his hand. Then he goes up on deck, to look at the harbour, and the town, and the ship that is now his home.

[pause]

On deck, there are other things Will has never seen before. Near the back of the ship, there is a little wooden house, with a door and windows. It is the entrance to the captain's cabin. There is a large wheel, for steering the ship, and a compass in a wooden box, called the binnacle. And everywhere, there are coils of rope, neatly laid down on the deck in round spirals, so that they can run out freely without tangling.

[pause]

The ship has a figurehead too, at the very front, under the long pole called the bowsprit. It is a carved wooden woman in a blue dress, with her hair blowing back, as if she is looking out over the sea. Sailors believe a figurehead watches over the ship and keeps her safe. Every few years, someone repaints her dress and her face, so she always looks fresh and bright.

[long-pause]

{img:whaling-crew-1850-gathered-deck-diverse-sailors-captain-mates-morning}
About thirty men sail on the Hannah Grey. They come from all over the world. A whaling ship is one of the most mixed places of its time.

[pause]

At the top is the captain, Captain Benjamin Howland. He is about forty-five years old, with a grey beard and calm blue eyes. He has been at sea since he was a boy, like Will. He has commanded whaling ships for fifteen years. He is strict but fair. The men respect him. When he gives an order, it is obeyed at once.

[long-pause]

Below the captain, there are the mates, the officers. The first mate, Mr. Swift, is a tall, quiet man from Nantucket, an island near New Bedford famous for its whalemen. He is in charge of the crew and the daily work of the ship. The second and third mates help him. Each mate is also in charge of one of the whaleboats.

[pause]

{img:harpooner-cape-verdean-1850-strong-calm-coiling-rope-deck-sunlight}
Then there are the harpooners, who are also called boatsteerers. They are the most skilled men in the whaleboats. One of them is called Joaquim. He comes from the Cape Verde Islands, a group of islands off the coast of Africa. Many fine whalemen come from those islands. Joaquim is about thirty, strong and calm, with a gentle smile. He has sailed on six voyages. He speaks Portuguese and a little English.

[long-pause]

The ordinary sailors come from everywhere. There are young men from the farms and towns of New England, like Will. There are sailors from Ireland and England, from Portugal and the Azores. There are men from the Pacific islands, especially from Hawaii, who are wonderful swimmers and sailors. There are Native Americans, like the Wampanoag men from Martha's Vineyard, near New Bedford, who have been whalemen for generations. And there are African American sailors, some of whom have escaped from slavery in the South.

[pause]

{img:sailors-many-backgrounds-mending-sails-deck-talking-laughing-sun}
On a whaling ship, what matters most is whether a man can do his job. In many ways, the ship is fairer than the land. African American sailors can become harpooners and mates, and a few even become captains. The men eat together, sleep together and work together. They depend on each other in the whaleboats. Of course, not everything is equal. But many sailors say they found more respect at sea than on land.

[long-pause]

There is also a cook, an old African American man called Isaiah. He has been cooking on ships for thirty years. He is round and cheerful, and he sings hymns while he works. He cooks in a little kitchen on deck, called the galley. And there is a cooper, who makes and mends the barrels. There is a carpenter, a blacksmith and a steward, who looks after the captain's cabin.

[pause]

{img:old-ships-cook-galley-singing-pot-stove-cheerful-1850}
On his first day, Will makes friends with another greenhand, a boy of sixteen called Patrick. Patrick comes from Ireland. His family came to America a few years before, during a terrible famine in Ireland. He has a quick smile and a lot of red hair. He is as nervous as Will. The two boys find their bunks next to each other in the forecastle. And they promise to look after each other, for the whole long voyage.

[pause]

Every man on board has a nickname, sooner or later. The cook is called the Doctor, which is an old joke on ships. The carpenter is called Chips, because of the wood shavings. The tallest sailor is called Shorty. And Will, because he comes from a farm, is soon called Farmer. At first, he does not like it. But the men say it kindly, and after a while, he starts to feel rather proud of it.

[pause]

The cabin boy is the youngest person on board, apart from the captain's daughter. He is only twelve years old, a thin boy from Boston called Sam. He serves the captain's meals, cleans the cabin and runs errands all day long. He is shy at first. But Will and Patrick take him under their wing. Before long, the three of them are always together, whenever there is time.

[long-pause]

{img:whaling-ship-leaving-new-bedford-harbour-sails-filling-families-waving-wharf}
On a bright autumn morning, the Hannah Grey sets sail. The whole crew is on board. The barrels are loaded. The whaleboats are hanging in their davits. And on the wharf, a crowd of people has gathered, to say goodbye.

[pause]

The sailors climb up the masts and loosen the great sails. The anchor is raised, with a long rattling of chains. The sails fill with wind. And slowly, the ship moves away from the wharf. On the wharf, wives and mothers and children wave their handkerchiefs. Some of them are crying. They will not see their loved ones for three or four years.

[long-pause]

Will stands at the rail, looking back at the town. He knows his family is not there. They could not come to New Bedford to see him off. But he waves anyway, at everyone on the wharf. Patrick stands beside him, waving too. The town gets smaller and smaller. The church spires and the houses on the hill become little shapes. And then the ship sails out of the harbour, past the lighthouse, into the open sea.

[pause]

{img:greenhand-seasick-leaning-rail-ship-rolling-grey-waves-first-day}
In the first days at sea, Will is very seasick. The ship rolls and pitches in the Atlantic waves. His head spins and his stomach turns over. He can hardly eat. He lies in his bunk, feeling miserable, and wonders why he ever wanted to go to sea. Many of the other greenhands are sick too. The old sailors laugh at them, but kindly. Everyone is seasick at first, they say. In a few days, you will be fine.

[long-pause]

On the first night, Will remembers his mother's letter. He takes it out of his sea chest and reads it by the light of the little lamp in the forecastle. She writes that she is proud of him. She writes that she will pray for him every night. She writes that the apple trees will be waiting for him when he comes home. And she reminds him to change his socks. Will smiles, and holds the letter for a long time. Then he folds it carefully and puts it under his pillow.

[pause]

{img:boy-reading-letter-bunk-forecastle-lamp-night-homesick-smile}
After four days, Will wakes up and feels better. The ship is still rolling. But his stomach is calm. He is hungry for the first time since they left. He eats a big bowl of Isaiah's porridge and a piece of hard bread, and it tastes wonderful. Then he goes up on deck. The sea is blue and sparkling, and the sun is shining. The ship is sailing fast, with all her sails filled. And Will feels, for the first time, that he is really a sailor.

[long-pause]

In the first weeks, the greenhands have a lot to learn. They must learn the names of all the sails and ropes, and there are hundreds of them. They must learn to climb the rigging, up to the tops of the masts. They must learn to tie knots, and to steer the ship, and to row the whaleboats. The mates teach them, patiently and sometimes not so patiently. Joaquim, the harpooner, helps them most. He shows them the knots slowly, again and again, until they get them right.

[pause]

{img:harpooner-teaching-boys-knots-deck-ropes-patient-sunny-afternoon}
Every day, the greenhands practise rowing in the whaleboats. The boats are lowered into the sea, and the crews row around the ship, fast, in rhythm. The mate stands at the back, steering with a long oar, and calling out the stroke. At first, the greenhands are clumsy. Their oars bump into each other, and they splash everyone. But slowly, they learn to row together, smoothly and fast. Will's hands get blisters, and then hard skin. He is getting stronger every day.

[pause]

In the evenings of those first weeks, Will practises his knots by the lamp in the forecastle. The bowline, the reef knot, the clove hitch, the sheet bend. He ties them and unties them, again and again, until his fingers know them by heart. The old sailors watch him and nod. A sailor who knows his knots, they say, is a sailor who will come home. Will does not know if it is true. But he keeps practising anyway.

[long-pause]

{img:whaling-ship-deck-early-morning-sailors-scrubbing-planks-sunrise-calm-sea}
Life on a whaling ship follows a steady rhythm. The crew is divided into two groups, called watches. One watch works while the other rests. Every four hours, a bell rings, and the watches change. Day and night, there are always men on deck, steering the ship and looking after the sails.

[long-pause]

The day begins early. Just before sunrise, the men of the morning watch scrub the deck. They pour buckets of sea water over the planks. Then they scrub them with sand and a soft stone, which the sailors call a holystone. They kneel in rows and push the stones back and forth. The wood becomes clean and pale. Will does not like scrubbing. But he likes watching the sun come up over the sea while he works.

[pause]

{img:sailors-breakfast-forecastle-tin-plates-hard-bread-coffee-mugs}
Breakfast is simple. There is coffee, sweetened with molasses, because sugar is too expensive. There is hard bread, called hardtack, which is so dry that the men soak it in their coffee. Sometimes there is a kind of stew, made from salted beef or pork and potatoes. The ordinary sailors eat in the forecastle, sitting on their sea chests. The food is the same, day after day. But after a morning of work, everything tastes good.

[long-pause]

During the day, there is always work to do. Ropes wear out and must be replaced. Sails tear in the wind and must be mended. The wood of the ship must be painted and tarred, to protect it from the salt water. The cooper checks the barrels. The carpenter repairs anything that is broken. And the greenhands are sent up the masts again and again, to learn the rigging, until they can climb as quickly as monkeys.

[pause]

{img:sailor-high-in-rigging-mast-ocean-below-blue-sky-wind}
Climbing the masts frightens Will at first. The main mast is as tall as a church tower. The ropes swing with every movement of the ship. When he looks down, the deck looks tiny, and the sea seems very far below. But Joaquim tells him to look up, never down, and to always hold on with one hand. After a few weeks, Will climbs without fear. And from the top, he can see the whole wide ocean, shining all around him.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, when the main work is done, the men have some free time. Some of them sleep. Some of them mend their clothes, sitting cross-legged on deck with needle and thread. Some of them play cards or tell stories. Some of them read, if they can read. And some of them sit quietly at the rail, smoking their pipes, and watching the sea go by.

[pause]

{img:sailors-afternoon-rest-deck-mending-clothes-pipes-sun-sails-shadows}
Supper is eaten in the late afternoon. Then the sun sets, and the evening comes. Lanterns are lit. The night watch takes over. The other men go down to the forecastle to sleep. Will lies in his narrow bunk and listens to the sounds of the ship. The creak of the wood. The splash of the waves against the sides. The quiet footsteps of the men on deck above. The ship rocks gently, like a cradle. And Will falls asleep almost at once.

[pause]

Every day at noon, the captain and the first mate measure the height of the sun with a brass instrument called a sextant. From that, they can work out how far north or south the ship is. With a good clock, called a chronometer, they can also work out how far east or west. Then the captain marks the ship's position on the chart with a small pencil cross. Day after day, the little crosses make a long, slow line across the ocean.

[pause]

On some evenings, the men fish from the side of the ship. They catch bonito and mackerel, and sometimes a flying fish lands right on the deck by itself. Isaiah fries the fresh fish for supper, with a little salt and pepper. After weeks of salted meat, it tastes like a feast. The sailors call this a fresh mess, and they talk about it for days afterwards.

[long-pause]

{img:lookout-hoops-masthead-whaling-ship-sailor-watching-horizon-bright-sea}
On a whaling ship, the most important job of the day is the lookout. At the top of each mast, high above the deck, there are two iron hoops, like a little cage. A sailor stands inside the hoops, holding on, and looks out over the sea. His job is to find whales. There are always lookouts at the mastheads, from sunrise to sunset, every single day of the voyage.

[long-pause]

Each lookout stands for two hours, and then another man takes his place. Everyone takes their turn, even the greenhands. The first time Will climbs to the masthead for lookout duty, his heart is beating fast. He stands in the hoops, holding on tightly, and looks out. The sea stretches away in every direction, blue and empty. The ship is like a little toy far below. The wind is cool, and the sun is warm on his face.

[pause]

{img:boy-lookout-masthead-hoops-holding-on-wind-in-hair-wide-ocean}
At first, Will does not know what to look for. The sea is full of movement. Waves, white foam, birds, shadows of clouds on the water. But Joaquim has explained it to him. You look for the spout, he said. When a whale comes up to breathe, it blows out a cloud of mist and air, high above the water. On a calm day, you can see a spout from many kilometres away. It looks like a little puff of white smoke, rising from the sea.

[long-pause]

Different whales have different spouts. The sperm whale, which the whalemen hunt most of all, has a spout that leans forward, at an angle, because its blowhole is at the front of its head. The right whale has a spout shaped like a V. The great blue whale has a tall, straight spout. An experienced lookout can tell which whale it is, just from the shape of the spout, even from very far away.

[pause]

{img:distant-whale-spout-mist-on-horizon-calm-sea-morning-light}
Hours pass, and days, and sometimes weeks. The lookouts see nothing but sea and sky. They see flying fish leaping from the waves, and dolphins swimming beside the ship. They see albatrosses, huge white birds with long wings, gliding over the water without moving their wings at all. They see distant ships, and once, far away, the coast of Africa. But they do not see any whales. And the captain grows quiet, and walks the deck with his hands behind his back.

[long-pause]

And then, one bright morning, many weeks after leaving New Bedford, the lookout on the main mast suddenly shouts. It is not Will. It is an older sailor, called Tom. He shouts at the top of his voice, the old cry of the whalemen. There she blows! There she blows! Everyone on deck looks up. The captain comes running out of his cabin. Where away? he calls. And Tom points with his arm, out to the side of the ship. Two points off the bow, sir! Sperm whales! A whole family of them!

[pause]

{img:sailors-rushing-deck-pointing-sea-captain-telescope-excitement-morning}
The ship comes alive at once. Men come running up from below. The captain climbs into the rigging with his telescope. Far away, near the horizon, Will sees them too. Little puffs of white mist, rising one after another from the sea, leaning forward in the sunlight. One, two, five, ten. A whole group of whales, swimming slowly together, breathing in the morning air. Will holds his breath. He has never seen anything so wonderful.

[pause]

When a sailor finally sees a whale, he often receives a small reward. On many ships, the captain promises a pound of tobacco, or a few dollars, to the man who first raises a whale that is caught. So the lookouts watch very carefully. Some of them stand in the hoops for two hours without blinking, it seems. Will dreams of being the first to call out. But on this voyage, it takes him a long time before his turn comes.

[pause]

Up at the masthead, time passes slowly. The lookout can hear the wind in the sails below him, and the creak of the masts. He can feel the whole ship swaying under him, like a tall tree in a breeze. Some men sing softly to themselves to pass the time. Others think about home. Will counts the waves, and the birds, and the little white clouds. And he keeps his eyes on the far, bright edge of the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:sperm-whale-family-swimming-calm-blue-sea-mother-calf-sunlight}
Before we go on, let us stop for a moment, and think about the whales themselves. Because they are some of the most remarkable animals that have ever lived on Earth.

[pause]

The whale that the New Bedford ships hunted most was the sperm whale. It is the largest animal with teeth in the world. A full-grown male can be eighteen metres long, as long as a big bus and a half. It has an enormous square head, which takes up about a third of its body. Inside that head, there is a huge chamber, filled with a clear, waxy oil. This is the spermaceti, the most valuable oil of all.

[long-pause]

Sperm whales are deep divers. They can dive down more than a kilometre into the dark ocean, and stay under the water for more than an hour. Down there, in the cold and darkness, they hunt giant squid. Sometimes, when whalemen caught a sperm whale, they found round scars on its skin, from the suckers of the squid. Nobody in 1850 had ever seen a giant squid alive. They were creatures of legend, hidden in the deep sea.

[pause]

{img:sperm-whale-diving-tail-flukes-raised-above-water-ocean-spray}
When a sperm whale dives, it lifts its great tail high out of the water, and then slides down into the deep. The tail is called the flukes. It can be four metres wide. The whalemen loved to watch this moment, even though it meant the whale was gone for an hour. The tail rose up, dripping with water, shining in the sun, and then slipped silently beneath the waves.

[long-pause]

Sperm whales live in families, much like people. The females and the young whales stay together in groups, with grandmothers, mothers, aunts and children. They look after each other's young. When a baby whale is born, the other females help it to the surface, to take its first breath. The young males leave the family when they grow up, and roam the oceans alone or with other young males.

[pause]

{img:sperm-whales-resting-vertical-near-surface-sleeping-blue-water}
Whales also talk to each other. Sperm whales make clicking sounds, like the ticking of a clock, which travel far through the water. Today, scientists believe that each family has its own pattern of clicks, like a dialect. In 1850, the whalemen knew none of this. But sometimes, through the wooden hull of the ship, at night, they heard strange sounds coming from the sea. Clicks and creaks and long, low songs. Some sailors thought they were ghosts. Others knew they were whales.

[long-pause]

There were other whales too. The right whale, slow and gentle, was called the right whale because it was the right whale to hunt. It swam slowly, close to shore, and it floated after it died. The bowhead whale lived in the cold Arctic seas. It has the thickest blubber of any whale, and some bowheads live for more than two hundred years. A bowhead swimming in the Arctic today may have been born before 1850.

[pause]

{img:bowhead-whale-arctic-water-ice-floes-calm-grey-light}
The whalemen of 1850 saw the whales as a kind of harvest from the sea. To them, whales were like the forests to a lumberjack, or the fields to a farmer. They did not know how many whales there were. They did not know how slowly whales have their young. And they did not know that, one day, many kinds of whales would almost disappear. Today, we look at whales very differently. But that is a story for later in our journey.

[pause]

Some sailors told stories about famous whales, which they said had been seen in many parts of the ocean. Whales that were very large, or very old, or that had a white patch on their heads. Sailors gave them names, and talked about them in the forecastle at night. A few of these whales were real. One of these stories helped to inspire a famous novel, published just one year after our story. It was called Moby-Dick.

[long-pause]

{img:whaleboats-lowered-sea-crews-rowing-away-from-ship-morning-distance}
When the whales are sighted, the captain gives the order to lower the boats. The whaleboats swing out from the davits and drop into the sea. The crews climb down into them. Six men in each boat. Four oarsmen, the harpooner at the front, and the mate at the back, steering. Will is one of the oarsmen in Mr. Swift's boat. Joaquim is his harpooner.

[long-pause]

As we said at the beginning, we will not follow the hunt closely. It was hard and dangerous work, and it ended the lives of many whales. Instead, let us watch from a distance, from the deck of the ship, the way the cook and the carpenter and the cabin boy watched it.

[pause]

{img:distant-whaleboats-small-on-wide-sea-sails-up-ship-deck-watching}
The boats spread out over the sea, rowing quietly toward the whales. Sometimes the men put up small sails, so they can move without making a sound. From the ship, the boats look tiny, like little beetles on a wide blue table. The whales are still far away. Their spouts rise and fall in the sunlight. The men in the boats row for a long time. The sun climbs higher. The ship follows slowly behind.

[long-pause]

In the boat, Will rows with all his strength. He sits with his back to the whales, as all oarsmen do. So he cannot see where they are going. He can only see the face of Mr. Swift, at the back of the boat, watching the sea ahead. Mr. Swift speaks quietly, encouraging them. Row, lads, row softly. Steady now. Will's heart is pounding. His arms are aching. And somewhere behind him, very close now, he hears a great, deep breath, like the sigh of a giant.

[pause]

{img:whale-surfacing-near-small-boat-mist-of-breath-calm-sea-awe}
Will looks over his shoulder, just for a second. And he sees the whale. It is so close, he could almost touch it. A vast, dark, wrinkled back, rising from the water like a small island. A cloud of warm mist drifts over the boat, smelling of the sea. For a moment, everything is perfectly still. The whale, the boat, the men. And Will feels something he will remember all his life. Not fear, exactly. Something more like wonder.

[long-pause]

What happens next, we will leave to the whalemen. Some whales escaped that day, diving deep into the ocean and swimming away. One did not. In the late afternoon, the boats come back to the ship, slowly, towing the whale behind them. The men are exhausted. Their hands are bleeding from the ropes. They are wet through, and some of them are shaking. Nobody says very much.

[pause]

{img:tired-whalemen-climbing-back-aboard-ship-evening-light-quiet}
That evening, Will sits alone at the rail, looking at the sunset. He is glad that everyone in his boat is safe. But he also feels strange and quiet inside. Joaquim comes and sits beside him. He does not say anything for a while. Then he says, softly, that he always feels this way too, after a hunt. That the whale is a great creature, and that one should always respect it. Will nods. And they sit together, watching the last light fade from the sky.

[pause]

Later, the old sailors tell Will that this feeling never quite goes away. Joaquim has been whaling for many years, and still he says a quiet prayer for the whale afterwards, in Portuguese. Many whalemen respected the whales deeply. They admired their size and strength, and their care for each other. They did their work because it was their living. But many of them, in their hearts, loved the whales too.

[long-pause]

{img:whale-alongside-ship-cutting-stage-planks-evening-calm-sea}
After a whale is caught, there is a great deal of work to do. The whale is tied to the side of the ship with heavy chains. Over the side, the men lower a narrow platform of planks, called the cutting stage. The mates stand on it, with long, sharp spades on wooden poles. And they begin to cut away the thick layer of blubber that covers the whale's body.

[long-pause]

Blubber is the fat under a whale's skin. It keeps the whale warm in the cold sea. On a sperm whale, it is about thirty centimetres thick. The men cut it off in one long strip, which winds around the whale like the peel of an orange. The strip is lifted up onto the deck with a great hook and a system of ropes. The pieces are so heavy that the whole ship leans to one side.

[pause]

{img:try-works-fire-glowing-night-deck-iron-pots-smoke-sailors-silhouettes}
On deck, the blubber is cut into smaller pieces, and then into thin slices, like the pages of a book. The sailors call these slices bible leaves. Then the pieces go into the two great iron pots of the try-works. A fire is lit in the brick oven below. And slowly, the blubber begins to melt, and turns into clear, golden oil.

[long-pause]

The fire of the try-works is fed with pieces of the blubber itself, once the oil has been cooked out of them. They burn very hot and very bright. So the whale, in a strange way, provides its own fuel. The try-works burn day and night, without stopping, until all the blubber is boiled down. For a big whale, this can take three days.

[pause]

{img:whaling-ship-at-night-glowing-red-try-works-black-smoke-dark-sea}
At night, a whaling ship with the try-works burning is an unforgettable sight. The fire glows red and orange in the darkness. Thick black smoke rises from the chimney and drifts away over the sea. The faces of the men are lit up by the flames, shining with sweat. The smell is very strong, like burning fat, and it gets into everything. The clothes, the hair, the wood of the ship. Sailors said you could smell a whaling ship long before you could see it.

[long-pause]

Meanwhile, the men also open the head of the sperm whale. Inside, there is the spermaceti, the clear, precious oil. It is so pure that the men simply scoop it out with buckets. When it cools, it turns into a soft white wax. This is the oil that will become the finest candles in New Bedford. One sperm whale can give more than a thousand litres of spermaceti.

[pause]

{img:cooper-filling-barrels-golden-oil-ship-deck-lantern-light}
The hot oil is poured into big copper tanks to cool. Then it is poured into barrels. The cooper checks every barrel carefully, to make sure it does not leak. The full barrels are rolled across the deck and lowered into the hold. One large sperm whale can fill about eighty barrels. And every barrel is worth a good deal of money, back in New Bedford.

[long-pause]

When the work is finally done, the whole ship is covered in oil and soot. The decks are slippery and black. So the men clean everything. They scrub the deck with lye and sand, and they wash their clothes and themselves. Strangely, the whale oil is good for the wood. After a good cleaning, the decks are whiter than ever. And the men, tired but satisfied, can rest at last. Will sleeps for twelve hours, without dreaming at all.

[pause]

While the try-works are burning, the cook sometimes does something the sailors love. He takes some dough and fries little doughnuts in the hot whale oil. They are crisp and golden on the outside and soft on the inside. The men eat them by the light of the fire, in the middle of the night. It is a strange meal, perhaps. But after hours of hard work, nothing tastes better.

[pause]

By the end of the whole voyage, the cooper will have filled and sealed more than two thousand barrels. Each one is marked with the name of the ship, and stored carefully in the hold, packed tightly so it cannot roll. As the hold slowly fills, the ship sits lower and lower in the water. The sailors like to watch this. A low ship, they say, is a happy ship, because it means she is nearly ready to go home.

[long-pause]

{img:captains-wife-1850-bonnet-shawl-standing-quarterdeck-sea-breeze}
There is something on the Hannah Grey that Will did not expect. In the captain's cabin, at the back of the ship, live two passengers. The captain's wife, Mary Howland, and their little daughter, Lucy, who is five years old.

[long-pause]

In 1850, it was becoming more common for whaling captains to take their wives along on the long voyages. Being apart for four years was very hard for a family. So some wives chose to sail with their husbands, and sometimes their children came too. The sailors called these ships hen frigates. They joked about them a little. But secretly, many of them liked having a woman and a child on board. It made the ship feel more like home.

[pause]

{img:little-girl-1850-playing-with-doll-captains-cabin-stern-windows-light}
Mary Howland is about thirty-five years old. She is a calm, sensible woman, with brown hair pinned up under a white cap. She grew up in New Bedford, the daughter of a ship's carpenter. She has already sailed on one voyage with her husband. She keeps a journal every day, writing about the weather, the whales, the ports they visit and the life on board. Her journal is full of small details, and sometimes funny remarks.

[long-pause]

Life for the captain's wife is lonely in some ways. She is the only grown woman on the ship. She cannot really be friends with the sailors. She spends most of her time in the cabin, sewing, reading, writing letters and teaching Lucy. She has brought a little library of books, and some flower seeds, which she plants in boxes near the stern windows. She grows a few herbs and a pot of geraniums, which flower bravely in the middle of the ocean.

[pause]

{img:geraniums-flower-box-cabin-window-sea-outside-soft-light}
Little Lucy is the darling of the whole ship. She has blond curls and a loud laugh. She runs around the deck, as far as she is allowed, and watches everything the sailors do. The carpenter makes her a little wooden doll. Isaiah, the cook, saves her the best biscuits. The sailors teach her songs and knots. And Joaquim carves her a little whale from a piece of whale tooth, which she carries everywhere.

[long-pause]

Will and Patrick become friends with Lucy too. Sometimes, on calm afternoons, they are allowed to play with her on deck. They tell her stories about their homes. Will tells her about the farm, and the apple trees, and the cows. Patrick tells her about Ireland, and about fairies and giants. Lucy listens with wide eyes. She has spent half her young life at sea. She has never seen a cow.

[pause]

{img:boys-telling-stories-to-little-girl-on-deck-sails-afternoon-calm}
Mary Howland also helps when the men are sick or hurt. There is no doctor on board. The captain has a medicine chest, with a book that explains how to use it. But Mary is gentler and more patient. When a sailor has a fever, she brings him soup and cool water. When a man cuts his hand, she cleans and bandages it. The sailors are grateful. They call her the captain's lady, and they take off their caps when she passes.

[long-pause]

Many years later, the journals of the whaling wives became very precious. Some of them are kept today in the museums and libraries of New England. They tell us what life was really like on the ships, in the words of the people who lived it. The small joys, the long days, the homesickness, the beauty of the sea. Through their pages, we can still hear the quiet voices of these women, writing by lamplight in their little cabins, so far from home.

[pause]

Mary also teaches little Lucy her letters and her numbers, every morning, at the table in the cabin. They use a small slate and a piece of chalk. Lucy learns to count by counting the sails, and the barrels, and the birds following the ship. She learns geography in the best way possible, by sailing around the world. By the time she is seven, she can point to Cape Horn and Hawaii on her father's charts.

[long-pause]

{img:sailor-carving-scrimshaw-whale-tooth-knife-needle-lamp-evening}
A whaling voyage is very long, and much of it is quiet. There can be weeks, or even months, without seeing a single whale. The sailors have a lot of free time in the evenings, and they find many ways to fill it. The most famous of these is scrimshaw.

[long-pause]

Scrimshaw is the art of carving pictures into whale teeth and whale bone. A sperm whale has about forty large teeth in its lower jaw, each as big as a man's hand. The sailors polish the teeth until they are smooth and shiny. Then, with a sailor's knife or a sharp needle, they scratch fine lines into the surface. They draw ships, whales, flowers, women in long dresses, eagles and flags.

[pause]

{img:scrimshaw-whale-tooth-close-detail-ship-engraving-ink-lines}
When the picture is finished, the sailor rubs ink or soot into the scratched lines. Then he wipes the surface clean. And the picture appears, black on white, sharp and clear. Some sailors are very skilled. Their scrimshaw is so fine that it looks like a printed picture from a book. Others are clumsy, and their ships look a little like ducks. But everyone tries.

[long-pause]

Will has his grandfather's scrimshaw tooth with him. Now he wants to make one of his own. Joaquim gives him a tooth and shows him how. Will practises for weeks. He scratches and scratches, in the evenings, by the light of the lamp. He decides to carve a picture of his family's farm, with the house, the barn and the apple trees. It takes him months. But when it is finished, he is very proud of it. He will give it to his mother when he comes home.

[pause]

{img:carved-whalebone-objects-pie-crimper-swift-yarn-winder-busk}
Sailors also carve useful things from whale bone, as gifts for the people at home. They make pie crimpers, little wheels for cutting pastry. They make knitting needles, buttons, napkin rings and boxes. They make a strange folding thing called a swift, for winding wool. And they make busks, long flat pieces of bone that women wore in the front of their dresses. Often, the sailors carved little love messages on the busks, for their sweethearts.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, there is also music. One of the sailors has a fiddle, and another has a concertina. They play the old sea songs, and the men sing along. They sing about the girls they left behind, about the whales and about the long way home. Some of the songs are sad, and some are cheerful. When the music is lively, the men dance on the deck, stamping their feet, while the others clap.

[pause]

{img:sailors-singing-evening-deck-fiddle-concertina-lantern-dancing}
Sometimes, the men tell stories. The old sailors tell tales of ships they have sailed on, of strange islands, of storms and whales and sea serpents. Some of the stories are true, and some are not. Everyone knows the difference, but nobody minds. Isaiah, the cook, tells stories from the Bible, in his deep, warm voice. And Joaquim tells stories from the Cape Verde Islands, about his mother's house by the sea, and the music of his village.

[long-pause]

Will sometimes thinks these evenings are the best part of the voyage. The work is done. The lamp is swinging gently. The ship is creaking softly. Someone is playing a slow tune on the fiddle. And the men sit together, carving and talking and singing, far from everything they know. They come from all over the world. But here, in this little wooden room in the middle of the ocean, they are almost like a family.

[pause]

Some sailors also keep a journal of their own, like the captain's wife. They write down the weather, the whales, and the things that happen on board. In the margins, they sometimes draw little pictures of whales. A whole whale means one was caught. A whale's tail means one was seen, but it got away. Turning the pages of these old journals today, you can see the little tails, swimming along the edges of the paper.

[long-pause]

{img:cape-horn-grey-stormy-sea-huge-waves-whaling-ship-small-sails}
To reach the great whaling grounds of the Pacific Ocean, the Hannah Grey must sail around the southern tip of South America. This is Cape Horn. It is one of the most famous and feared places in the world of sailing.

[pause]

At Cape Horn, two great oceans meet. The Atlantic and the Pacific. The winds blow almost always from the west, very strong and very cold. The waves are enormous, the biggest in the world. Sometimes there are icebergs, drifting up from the Antarctic. Sailors who have rounded the Horn are allowed to wear a gold earring, as a sign of their courage.

[long-pause]

As the ship sails south, the weather gets colder and colder. The sky turns grey. The wind howls in the rigging. The sailors put on all their warm clothes, thick woollen sweaters, oilskin coats and heavy boots. Even so, they are always cold and wet. In the forecastle, water drips from the ceiling, and nothing is ever dry. Will wraps himself in his blanket and dreams of the warm kitchen at home.

[pause]

{img:sailors-oilskins-hauling-ropes-icy-deck-spray-cold-grey-light}
For three weeks, the Hannah Grey struggles to get around the Horn. The wind blows against her, again and again. She sails forward for a day, and then she is pushed back. The waves crash over the deck. The men must tie ropes around their waists, so they are not washed overboard. The sails freeze stiff with ice. The sailors climb the masts with frozen hands, to take in the sails before the wind tears them away.

[long-pause]

One night, there is a great storm. The ship rolls so far over that the men think she will not come back up. The wind screams. Everything that is not tied down crashes across the deck. In the forecastle, the sea chests slide back and forth. Will holds on to his bunk and prays. Patrick, beside him, is very pale. But the old sailors are calm. She is a good ship, says Tom. She has been around the Horn five times. She will do it again.

[pause]

{img:captain-at-wheel-storm-night-lantern-rain-wind-calm-face}
All through the night, Captain Howland stands on deck, beside the man at the wheel. He does not go to his cabin once. He watches the waves and the sails and the sky, and he gives his orders calmly. In the cabin, Mary holds little Lucy in her arms, and sings to her, so that she will not be afraid. And slowly, very slowly, the night passes.

[long-pause]

In the morning, the storm is over. The wind has turned. The sky is still grey, but the waves are smaller, and the ship is sailing west at last. Everyone is tired and wet, but nobody has been hurt. A few sails are torn, and one of the whaleboats has been damaged. But the Hannah Grey has come through.

[pause]

{img:albatross-gliding-over-calm-southern-ocean-morning-break-in-clouds}
A few days later, the captain announces that they have rounded the Horn. They are in the Pacific Ocean. The men give a great cheer. That evening, the cook makes a special supper, with a plum duff, a pudding made with flour and raisins, which the sailors love. And then the ship turns north, toward warmer seas. Every day, the air gets a little softer, and the sun a little brighter. Will takes off his heavy sweater, and lets the sun warm his back.

[pause]

Many years later, a canal was dug across Panama, in Central America. It opened in 1914. After that, ships no longer needed to sail around Cape Horn. They could simply pass through the canal, from one ocean to the other, in a single day. But in 1850, the canal was still a dream. Every ship going to the Pacific had to face the long, cold journey around the Horn.

[long-pause]

{img:galapagos-islands-volcanic-shore-whaling-ship-anchored-blue-bay}
After many weeks of sailing north along the coast of South America, the Hannah Grey reaches the Galapagos Islands. They lie in the Pacific Ocean, right on the equator, about a thousand kilometres from the coast. They are volcanic islands, made of black rock and grey lava. Some of them are dry and bare. Others are green, with forests on the hills.

[long-pause]

The whaling ships often stopped at the Galapagos. The waters around the islands were full of sperm whales. And on the islands, the ships could find fresh water, firewood and food. Sometimes, the men also hunted the giant tortoises that lived there, for their meat. Sadly, this was one of the reasons why some kinds of tortoise almost disappeared. Today, the islands are a protected national park, and the tortoises are safe.

[pause]

{img:giant-tortoise-galapagos-slow-walking-green-grass-gentle-old}
Will has never seen anything like the Galapagos. When he goes ashore with the boat crew, the animals are not afraid of people at all. Sea lions lie on the beach and barely look up. Strange black lizards, called marine iguanas, sit on the rocks in the sun, like little dragons. Birds with bright blue feet stand quietly and watch him pass. And a giant tortoise, as big as a wheelbarrow, walks slowly past, chewing on a leaf.

[long-pause]

On one of the islands, called Floreana, there is a very special place. It is a wooden barrel, standing on a pole on the beach. It is called the post office barrel. Whalemen have been using it since the end of the seventeen hundreds. When a ship is leaving the Pacific for home, the sailors put their letters in the barrel. When a ship arrives from the other direction, the sailors look through the letters. If any of them are addressed to their home town, they take them along, and deliver them by hand.

[pause]

{img:post-office-barrel-floreana-beach-letters-sailors-sorting-sunlight}
Will has written a letter to his mother. He has been writing it for months, a little every evening. He tells her about the ship, the crew, the whales, the storms of Cape Horn and the strange animals of the Galapagos. He tells her that he is well, and that he thinks of home every day. He puts it into the barrel, with a little prayer that someone will carry it to New Bedford. Then he looks through the other letters. There is nothing for anyone on the Hannah Grey.

[long-pause]

The post office barrel still exists today. There is still a barrel on that beach on Floreana. Visitors to the Galapagos still leave postcards in it. And other visitors still look through them, and take home the ones addressed near where they live, and deliver them by hand. It is a lovely old tradition, which has lasted for more than two hundred years.

[pause]

{img:sea-lions-sleeping-galapagos-beach-sunset-calm-quiet}
Before they sail, the crew fills the water barrels from a spring on one of the islands. They gather wood for the galley stove. Mary Howland and little Lucy come ashore for an afternoon, to walk on the sand and look at the animals. Lucy laughs at the sea lions, and wants to take one home. Then the boats row back to the ship, and the anchor is raised. The Hannah Grey sails away, out into the wide Pacific, toward the whaling grounds near the equator.

[pause]

Fifteen years earlier, in 1835, a young English scientist had visited these same islands, on a ship called the Beagle. His name was Charles Darwin. He noticed that the birds and the tortoises were a little different on each island. Years later, these observations helped him to develop his famous ideas about how living things change over time. In 1850, his great book had not been written yet. But the islands were already quietly waiting to become famous.

[pause]

Some of the old sailors leave small signs of their visit on the islands. They carve the name of their ship and the date into the rocks near the beach. Some of these carvings can still be seen today, worn smooth by almost two centuries of wind and rain. They are like little messages from the past, saying quietly that we were here, and we sailed on.

[long-pause]

{img:two-whaling-ships-side-by-side-calm-sea-boat-rowing-between-visit}
In the middle of the vast Pacific Ocean, a whaling ship may sail for months without seeing another ship. So when two whaling ships meet, it is a happy event. And the whalemen have a special word for it. They call it a gam.

[long-pause]

When a lookout sees the sails of another ship, he calls down to the deck. The captain looks through his telescope. If it is a whaling ship, he steers toward it. As the two ships come closer, the sailors crowd along the rails, waving and shouting. They raise their flags, so they can see where the other ship comes from. Often, it is another ship from New Bedford, or from Nantucket. Sometimes they even know people on board.

[pause]

{img:sailors-crowding-rail-waving-at-passing-ship-flags-flying}
The two ships stop, close to each other, in the middle of the ocean. Then the captain of one ship is rowed over to the other ship, to visit. And at the same time, the first mate of the other ship is rowed back, to visit the first ship. So both ships have a visitor, and both have someone to talk to. The captain's wife sometimes goes along too, if there is another captain's wife on the other ship.

[long-pause]

A gam can last for hours, or even for a day or two, if there are no whales nearby. The sailors visit each other's forecastles. They exchange news. Where are the whales this season? Which ships have done well, and which have had bad luck? What has happened back home? Some ships have newspapers that are only a few months old, and the men read them over and over.

[pause]

{img:sailors-two-crews-forecastle-sharing-news-letters-laughing-lamp}
The men also trade things. Tobacco, books, clothes, pieces of scrimshaw. And most important of all, they exchange letters. If one ship is going home, the other ship gives it letters to carry. If one ship has come from home more recently, it may have letters for the other ship's crew. This is how news travels across the oceans in 1850. Slowly, from ship to ship, like a message passed from hand to hand.

[long-pause]

On the Hannah Grey's first gam, Will is lucky. The other ship has left New Bedford six months after the Hannah Grey. And in its mail bag, there is a letter for Will Coffin. It is from his mother. He takes it with trembling hands and reads it right there on deck. Everyone at home is well. His little sister has learned to read. The cow has had a calf. And the apple trees have given a wonderful harvest.

[pause]

{img:boy-reading-letter-from-home-on-deck-smiling-tears-ships-behind}
Will reads the letter five times. Then he folds it carefully and puts it in his sea chest, next to the first letter from his mother. Patrick does not get a letter. So Will reads his aloud to him, in the evening, and they talk about home for a long time. Patrick says that one day, when they are old, he will come and visit Will's farm, and see the apple trees for himself. Will says he would like that very much.

[long-pause]

When the gam is over, the visitors are rowed back to their own ships. The sailors wave goodbye. The two ships set their sails, and turn away from each other, each to its own part of the ocean. Soon, the other ship is only a little white shape on the horizon. And then it is gone, and the Hannah Grey is alone again, on the endless blue sea.

[pause]

Sometimes a gam even ends in a little party. The two crews gather on one ship, and the fiddlers from both ships play together. The men sing the old songs, and dance on the deck. The captains sit in the cabin with their wives, drinking tea and talking about home. For a few hours, the lonely ocean feels like a village square. And when the visitors leave in the evening, everyone waves until the boats are out of sight.

[long-pause]

{img:lahaina-maui-1850-harbour-whaling-ships-anchored-palm-trees-mountains}
After more than a year at sea, the Hannah Grey sails north, to the Hawaiian Islands. In 1850, Hawaii was an independent kingdom, ruled by its own king, Kamehameha the Third. And it was the most important stopping place for whaling ships in the whole Pacific.

[long-pause]

The Hannah Grey anchors at Lahaina, on the island of Maui. Lahaina is a small town, with white houses, a stone fort, a church and a long row of shops along the shore. Behind it, green mountains rise up into the clouds. In the harbour, there are dozens of whaling ships, from New Bedford, Nantucket, England and France. Sometimes there are more than a hundred ships at once. The whole harbour is a forest of masts.

[pause]

{img:lahaina-street-1850-shops-sailors-hawaiian-people-breadfruit-trees}
The whaling ships come to Hawaii to rest and to resupply. They buy fresh food. Potatoes, sweet potatoes, oranges, bananas, pigs, chickens and fresh water. They repair their ships. They send their oil home on other ships. And they hire new sailors, because many Hawaiian men are excellent seamen, and they want to see the world. Some of them sail all the way back to New Bedford, and some of them settle there.

[long-pause]

For the sailors, the stop in Hawaii is the best part of the whole voyage. They are allowed to go ashore. After so many months at sea, the land feels strange under their feet. They walk a little unsteadily, as if the ground is still rolling. Will and Patrick walk through the town together. They eat fresh fruit until they can eat no more. Will tastes a banana for the first time in his life. He thinks it is the most delicious thing in the world.

[pause]

{img:hawaiian-beach-outrigger-canoes-boys-swimming-turquoise-water}
The Hawaiian people are friendly and curious. Children follow the sailors through the streets, laughing. Men paddle long canoes with outriggers across the bay. Women sell flowers and fruit at the market. Will watches some young men riding the waves on long wooden boards, standing up, as the waves carry them toward the shore. It is called surfing. Nobody in New England has ever seen anything like it.

[long-pause]

Mary Howland and little Lucy stay in a house on shore, with other captains' wives. Some whaling wives spent many months in Lahaina, while their husbands went hunting in the north. They visited each other, went to church together and had little tea parties. Lucy plays with the children of the other captains. For a few weeks, she has friends her own age. She is very sad when it is time to go back to sea.

[pause]

{img:missionary-house-lahaina-captains-wives-tea-veranda-afternoon}
In Lahaina, there are also American missionaries, who have come to teach the Christian faith. They have built schools and a printing press. Many Hawaiians can read and write in their own language. In fact, there are more people who can read in Hawaii than in many parts of America. Not everyone in Lahaina gets along. The missionaries do not like the sailors' wild behaviour on shore. But in general, the town is peaceful and busy, and Will remembers it all his life as a kind of paradise.

[long-pause]

After three weeks, the Hannah Grey is ready to sail again. The hold is full of fresh food. The water barrels are full. Some new Hawaiian sailors have joined the crew. One of them is a young man called Kalani, who becomes good friends with Will and Patrick. And on a bright morning, the ship sails out of Lahaina, heading north, toward the cold seas of the Arctic.

[pause]

On Sundays in Lahaina, the bells of the stone church ring out across the bay. Many sailors go to the service, in their clean shirts. The hymns are sung in Hawaiian and in English, one after the other. Will does not understand the Hawaiian words. But the tunes are the same ones he knows from his church at home. He sings along quietly, and for a moment he feels very close to his mother.

[pause]

In the evenings, Will and Patrick sit on the beach and watch the sun go down behind the island of Lanai, across the water. The sky turns orange and pink and purple. The palm trees rustle in the warm wind. Out in the bay, the lanterns of the ships come on, one by one, like stars on the water. Patrick says he never knew the world could be so beautiful. Will agrees. He wishes his mother could see it too.

[long-pause]

{img:arctic-sea-ice-floes-fog-whaling-ship-grey-cold-light}
In 1848, just two years before our story, a whaling captain from New York sailed through the Bering Strait, the narrow sea between Alaska and Russia. On the other side, in the Arctic Ocean, he found great numbers of bowhead whales. The news spread quickly. And soon, many whaling ships were sailing north every summer, into the icy waters of the Arctic.

[long-pause]

The Hannah Grey sails north for many weeks. The weather gets colder again. The sea turns grey, and then dark green. Fog rolls over the water, thick and white. One morning, the lookout shouts that there is ice ahead. Will climbs up to see. And there, stretching away into the fog, is a vast field of white ice, floating on the sea, cracked into pieces like a broken plate.

[pause]

{img:polar-bear-on-ice-floe-distance-fog-quiet-arctic-morning}
The Arctic is a strange and beautiful world. In summer, the sun hardly sets at all. At midnight, it hangs low over the horizon, golden and pale. The ice glows blue and white. Walruses lie on the ice floes, huge and brown, with long white tusks. Seals pop their heads out of the water and look at the ship with big dark eyes. And once, far away on the ice, Will sees a polar bear, walking slowly, all alone.

[long-pause]

But the Arctic is also dangerous. The ice is always moving. The wind can push the ice floes together, and a ship can be trapped between them. Then the ice squeezes the ship, harder and harder, until the wood cracks. The captain must be very careful. He must keep watching the ice and the wind, and always leave a way out, back to open water.

[pause]

{img:ship-navigating-narrow-channel-between-ice-floes-sailors-watching-tense}
One afternoon, the fog comes down suddenly, thicker than ever. The sailors can barely see the front of the ship from the back. Then the wind changes. And the ice begins to close in around them. The men can hear it, in the fog. A grinding, creaking sound, getting closer and closer. Captain Howland orders all the boats to be lowered. The men row ahead of the ship, towing it with ropes, through the narrowing channels between the ice.

[long-pause]

For hours, they row and pull, in the cold and the fog. Will's hands are frozen. His breath makes clouds in the air. The ice is so close now that the men can touch it with their oars. Sometimes they push the ice floes away with long poles. The captain stands at the bow, peering into the fog, calling out directions. Left a little. Now right. Steady. Everyone is silent, except for the sound of the oars and the creaking of the ice.

[pause]

{img:fog-lifting-open-water-ahead-ship-free-of-ice-pale-sun}
And then, slowly, the fog begins to lift. A pale sun shines through. And ahead of them, there is open water, dark and calm. The ship slides out of the ice and into the open sea. The men in the boats give a great shout of joy. They climb back on board, exhausted and frozen. Isaiah has hot coffee waiting for everyone. And Mary Howland writes in her journal that evening, in a small, neat hand, a single line. Thank God, we are free.

[long-pause]

Not all ships were so lucky. Some years later, in 1871, more than thirty whaling ships were trapped in the Arctic ice and crushed. But the sailors had time to leave the ships, and they all escaped in their whaleboats, and were rescued by other ships. More than a thousand people, including the captains' wives and children, sailed away safely. Nobody was lost. But almost all of the ships were lost to the ice.

[pause]

Sometimes the whaling ships meet the people who live along the cold Arctic coasts. They are the Inupiat of Alaska and the Yupik and Chukchi people of the Russian shore. They paddle out to the ships in boats made of skin stretched over wooden frames. They trade warm furs and fresh meat for tools, cloth and needles. These people have hunted bowhead whales for thousands of years, in small numbers, with great care and respect. They still do today.

[long-pause]

{img:sailor-sitting-alone-bow-evening-looking-at-sea-homesick-sunset}
The Hannah Grey stays at sea for a long time. After the Arctic, she sails back to Hawaii for the winter. Then she goes hunting near the equator, and near Japan, and then back to the Arctic for a second summer. Month after month, the hold slowly fills with barrels of oil. And month after month, the men grow a little older, and a little more tired, and think a little more about home.

[long-pause]

Homesickness is the great sickness of the whaling voyage. Some days, Will hardly thinks about home at all. He is busy, and the sea is beautiful, and he is happy with his friends. But on other days, he misses home so much that it hurts. He misses his mother's voice. He misses the smell of the barn and the taste of fresh milk. He misses sleeping in a bed that does not move.

[pause]

{img:old-letters-tied-with-string-sea-chest-lamp-light-close}
Letters are precious. They arrive only a few times a year, from passing ships or in ports. Each letter is many months old when it arrives. The news in it has already happened long ago. A baby has been born. A grandmother has passed away. A friend has married. The sailors read the letters again and again, until the paper is soft and torn along the folds. Then they tie them with string and keep them in their sea chests.

[long-pause]

Will writes letters too, whenever he can. He writes about the Galapagos, and Hawaii, and the Arctic. He writes about Joaquim and Patrick and Kalani, and little Lucy. He tries not to write about the dangers, so his mother will not worry. He never knows if his letters will arrive. Some of them are carried home by other ships. Some of them are lost forever, somewhere on the long way across the oceans.

[pause]

{img:sailor-writing-letter-forecastle-quill-ink-bottle-lantern-swaying}
On Sundays, there is no work on board, unless there are whales. The men wash their clothes and rest. Captain Howland reads from the Bible on deck, for anyone who wants to listen. Isaiah leads the singing of hymns, in his deep voice. Many of the sailors are not very religious. But they come and stand and listen anyway. The old words remind them of home, and of the churches where their families are sitting, on the same Sunday, on the other side of the world.

[long-pause]

During the second year, one of the older sailors, a quiet man from Maine, falls ill with a fever. Mary Howland nurses him for many days. But one night, he passes away peacefully in his sleep. The next morning, the whole crew gathers on deck. The captain reads a prayer. And the sailor is given to the sea, wrapped in his hammock, with the flag over him. It is a very quiet day on the Hannah Grey. That evening, nobody plays the fiddle.

[pause]

{img:calm-sea-at-dawn-ship-sails-soft-pink-light-peaceful}
But life goes on, as it always does. The sun rises the next morning, and the work begins again. Will turns sixteen at sea, and then seventeen. He grows taller and stronger. His voice gets deeper. His hands are hard from the ropes. He is no longer a greenhand. He has become a real sailor, and a good oarsman. Joaquim tells him he could be a harpooner one day, if he wants. Will thinks about it. But in his heart, he is not sure. He keeps thinking about the apple trees.

[long-pause]

And then, one day, in the third year of the voyage, Captain Howland comes out on deck and calls all hands together. The hold is full, he says. Every barrel. We are going home. For a moment, there is silence. Then the men cheer so loudly that little Lucy covers her ears. Patrick throws his cap into the air, and it blows overboard. Nobody cares. They are going home.

[pause]

On clear nights, Will likes to lie on the deck and look at the stars. In the southern seas, the stars are different from the ones at home. There is a group of four bright stars, in the shape of a cross, called the Southern Cross. Joaquim teaches him to find it. And when the ship sails north again, Will sees the old familiar stars come back, one by one, like friends returning from a long journey.

[pause]

Patrick helps Will through the hard days, and Will helps Patrick. When one of them is sad, the other tells a story or a joke. They talk about what they will do when they get home. Patrick wants to open a small shop one day, with his name painted over the door. Will wants to plant more apple trees, and perhaps buy a horse. They make many plans, lying in their bunks at night, while the ship sails on.

[long-pause]

{img:whaling-ship-heavy-laden-sailing-home-atlantic-full-sails-bright-day}
The journey home is long, but it feels shorter than the journey out. The ship sails south across the Pacific, around Cape Horn again, and then north through the whole length of the Atlantic Ocean. This time, the Horn is kinder. The wind blows from behind, and the ship flies around the cape in only a few days. Every day, the men count the days until they will reach New Bedford.

[long-pause]

The Hannah Grey is heavy now. Her hold is full of oil. More than two thousand barrels of whale oil and spermaceti. It is a very good voyage. The captain is pleased. The owners will be pleased. And every sailor will have his share. Even Will, with his small lay, will bring home some money for his family. Not a fortune. But enough to help with the farm.

[pause]

{img:sailors-painting-ship-rail-cleaning-preparing-for-homecoming}
As they come closer to home, the men start to get the ship ready. They scrub the decks. They paint the sides. They mend the sails, so the ship will look her best when she arrives. The sailors trim their beards and cut each other's hair. They take out their best clothes, which have been folded in their sea chests for three years. Many of them no longer fit. Will has grown so much that his good jacket is now far too small.

[long-pause]

At last, one bright spring morning, the lookout shouts again. But this time, he is not shouting about whales. Land ho! Land! Everyone rushes to the rail. And there, low on the horizon, is a grey line. It is the coast of America. The coast of home. Some of the old sailors have tears in their eyes. They have sailed around the whole world, and now they are back.

[pause]

{img:new-bedford-harbour-spring-morning-crowd-on-wharf-ship-arriving}
The Hannah Grey sails slowly into the harbour of New Bedford. The news has spread through the town. A whaling ship is coming in. People hurry down to the wharf. Wives, mothers, children, friends. They have been waiting for more than three years. They shade their eyes with their hands, and look for faces at the rail of the ship. And on the ship, the sailors look for faces on the wharf.

[long-pause]

Will does not expect anyone to be there. But as the ship comes closer, he sees a small figure in a grey shawl, standing at the front of the crowd. It is his mother. She has heard the ship was due, and she has come all the way to New Bedford, to wait for him. Beside her is his sister Ruth, who was nine when he left. Now she is almost thirteen, and not so little any more. When the ship touches the wharf, Will jumps down before anyone can stop him. And his mother holds him so tightly that he can hardly breathe.

[pause]

{img:mother-embracing-tall-young-sailor-wharf-sister-smiling-crowd}
On the wharf, there are many happy reunions. Captain Howland's parents are there, to welcome Mary and little Lucy, who is now eight years old and talks without stopping. Patrick's family is there too, and they take Will and his mother home for supper. Joaquim is staying in New Bedford for a few weeks, before his next voyage. He shakes Will's hand and gives him a little scrimshaw, of a ship and a whale, swimming side by side.

[long-pause]

A few days later, Will goes home to the farm. He has brought presents for everyone. Shells from the Pacific, a piece of Hawaiian cloth, and for his mother, the scrimshaw tooth with the picture of the farm. She looks at it for a long time, without saying anything. Then she puts it on the shelf by the window. That evening, Will walks out into the orchard. The apple trees are in blossom, white and pink, just as he remembered them.

[pause]

When the oil has been sold, the sailors receive their pay. For the old sailors and the harpooners, it is a good sum. For the greenhands, it is much less, after the cost of their clothes and tobacco has been taken off. But Will still brings home more money than his family has ever seen at once. His father uses it to buy two more cows and to mend the roof of the barn.

[pause]

Will never goes whaling again. He stays on the farm, and many years later, it becomes his own. He plants a whole new orchard, with rows and rows of apple trees. He marries, and has children of his own. And on winter evenings, by the fire, he tells them stories about the Hannah Grey. About Joaquim and Patrick and little Lucy, about Cape Horn and the Galapagos, and about the first whale he ever saw, so close he could almost touch it.

[long-pause]

{img:oil-derrick-pennsylvania-1859-wooden-tower-fields-early-petroleum}
In 1850, when Will sailed away, the whaling industry of New Bedford was at its very peak. More than three hundred ships sailed from the town. Thousands of men worked on them. And New Bedford was one of the richest towns in America. It seemed as if it would go on forever. But in fact, the end was already coming.

[long-pause]

In 1859, in the state of Pennsylvania, a man called Edwin Drake drilled a hole deep into the ground. And from the hole came a thick, dark liquid. It was petroleum, oil from under the earth. From petroleum, people could make a new kind of lamp oil, called kerosene. It burned brightly, and it was much cheaper than whale oil. Within a few years, most lamps in America were burning kerosene instead.

[pause]

{img:kerosene-lamp-glowing-parlour-table-1870-family-evening}
At about the same time, other things also changed. In 1861, the American Civil War began. Many whaling ships were captured or burned by Confederate ships during the war. Others were sunk on purpose, filled with stones, to block the harbours of the South. And in 1871, as we heard, more than thirty ships were lost in the Arctic ice. Each of these events made the whaling fleet smaller.

[long-pause]

Whales were also getting harder to find. After so many years of hunting, there were far fewer of them than before. The ships had to sail further and further, and stay away longer and longer, to fill their holds. The voyages became four or five years long. And they brought home less oil than before. Slowly, the owners stopped building new whaling ships.

[pause]

{img:old-whaling-ship-moored-quiet-wharf-sails-furled-late-afternoon}
New Bedford did not die. The rich families of the town put their money into new businesses, especially cotton mills. Great brick factories were built along the river, where thousands of people worked, making cloth. And many of the families who had come to New Bedford for the whaling stayed in the town. Especially the Cape Verdeans and the Portuguese, whose descendants still live there today.

[long-pause]

The last whaling ship sailed from New Bedford in 1924. She was called the Wanderer. On her very first day, before she had even left the coast, she was caught by a storm and wrecked on the rocks. Nobody was hurt. But it was the end of an era. After almost two hundred years, the whaling days of New Bedford were over.

[pause]

{img:humpback-whale-breaching-joyful-blue-ocean-sunlight-spray}
Meanwhile, the whales were in trouble. In the twentieth century, other countries hunted whales with fast steam ships and explosive harpoons. Many kinds of whales almost disappeared. But slowly, people began to understand how special whales are. They learned about their families, their songs and their long lives. And in 1986, most countries in the world agreed to stop hunting whales commercially.

[long-pause]

Since then, many whale populations have slowly recovered. Humpback whales, which were once very rare, are now seen in large numbers in many oceans again. Their songs fill the sea once more. Sperm whales still swim in the deep waters near the Galapagos and the Azores, where the whalemen once hunted them. Today, they are protected. And people go out on boats, not to hunt them, but simply to see them, and to wonder at them, just as Will did on that first morning long ago.

[pause]

Some of the old whaling ships found new work after the whaling ended. A few became cargo ships, or training ships for young sailors. One of them, the Charles W. Morgan, built in New Bedford in 1841, was carefully kept. She is the last wooden whaling ship left in the whole world. Today, she lies at the museum in Mystic, Connecticut, freshly restored. In 2014, she even sailed again, back to New Bedford, to visit her old home.

[long-pause]

{img:new-bedford-today-cobblestone-street-old-brick-buildings-harbour-later}
Today, New Bedford is a city of about a hundred thousand people. It is still a fishing port. In fact, it is one of the most valuable fishing ports in the whole United States, famous for its scallops. Every day, fishing boats come and go from the harbour, just as the whaling ships did long ago.

[long-pause]

But the old part of the town, near the harbour, has been carefully preserved. It is called the New Bedford Whaling National Historical Park. There are cobblestone streets, old brick warehouses and banks, and the houses of the ship owners and captains. Walking through these streets, it is easy to imagine what the town looked like in 1850, with the smell of oil in the air and the masts of ships rising over the rooftops.

[pause]

{img:whaling-museum-new-bedford-whale-skeleton-hanging-hall-today}
In the middle of the old town is the New Bedford Whaling Museum. It is one of the most wonderful museums in America. When you walk in, you see the enormous skeletons of whales, hanging from the ceiling. There is a blue whale, a humpback whale, a sperm whale and a right whale. They are so big that you have to look up and up, to see the whole of them.

[long-pause]

The museum also has a whaling ship. Not a real one, but a model, built at half size. It is called the Lagoda, and it is the largest model ship in the world. Children can climb on board, and walk on the deck, and look at the try-works and the whaleboats. It gives a very good idea of how small and crowded the real ships were.

[pause]

{img:scrimshaw-collection-museum-glass-case-carved-teeth-soft-light-today}
And the museum has the largest collection of scrimshaw in the world. Thousands of carved whale teeth and bones, made by sailors like Will, on long quiet evenings at sea. There are ships and whales, flowers and ladies, and little love messages. There are also logbooks and journals, kept by captains and their wives. Each one tells the story of a long voyage, day by day, in faded ink.

[long-pause]

Across the street from the museum, there is a small white church, called the Seamen's Bethel. It was built in 1832, for the sailors of New Bedford. On its walls, there are marble plaques, remembering sailors who were lost at sea. Many whalemen went to a service here before they sailed. The pulpit inside is shaped like the front of a ship. Visitors still come every day to sit quietly in the old wooden pews.

[pause]

{img:whale-watching-boat-people-at-rail-humpback-tail-cape-cod-today}
Not far from New Bedford, in the waters near Cape Cod, whales still swim every summer. Humpbacks, fin whales and even the rare right whales come there to feed. And every day, boats full of people go out to watch them. When a whale rises from the water, or lifts its great tail into the air, everyone on the boat gasps and points and smiles.

[long-pause]

Many of the people on those boats have ancestors who were whalemen. Their great-great-grandfathers sailed from New Bedford to hunt the whales. Now their great-great-grandchildren sail out to watch them, and to protect them. It is a gentle ending to a long story. The town that once lived from the whales now loves them. And in the museum, the old harpoons hang quietly on the walls, never to be used again.

[pause]

Every year, in the winter, people come together at the Whaling Museum for a special event. They read the whole of the novel Moby-Dick aloud, from beginning to end, without stopping. It takes about twenty-five hours. Hundreds of readers take turns, each reading a few pages. Some of them read in the middle of the night, to a sleepy audience wrapped in blankets. It is a lovely, cosy tradition, in a town that still loves its old stories.

[pause]

In the museum, there is also a room about the people of New Bedford. The Cape Verdeans, the Azoreans, the Wampanoag, the African American sailors and the Hawaiians. Their stories are told with photographs and letters, and with objects their families have kept. Visitors can learn how many different people worked together on the whaling ships. And how the town became, in the end, a home for all of them.

[long-pause]

{img:new-bedford-harbour-1850-evening-sunset-masts-silhouettes-calm-water}
So we leave New Bedford tonight, in the year 1850, as the evening falls. The sun is setting over the harbour. The masts of the ships stand dark against the golden sky. The wharves are quiet now. The barrels of oil are standing in long rows, cooling in the evening air. And the seagulls have settled on the rooftops for the night.

[long-pause]

Somewhere far away, on the wide Pacific Ocean, the Hannah Grey is sailing through the night. The lanterns are lit. The watch is on deck. The man at the wheel steers by the stars. And down in the forecastle, a boy from a small farm in Massachusetts is asleep in his narrow bunk, with two letters from his mother under his pillow.

[pause]

{img:ship-at-night-on-calm-ocean-stars-lantern-glow-gentle-waves}
The ship rocks gently on the long, slow waves. The wood creaks softly. The water whispers along her sides. Far below, in the deep, dark sea, the whales are swimming too. A family of sperm whales, travelling slowly through the night, clicking softly to each other. Mothers and grandmothers and little ones, close together, as they have always been.

[long-pause]

In the captain's cabin, Mary Howland closes her journal and blows out the lamp. Little Lucy is asleep in her small bed, holding the carved whale that Joaquim made for her. Out on deck, Joaquim himself is standing at the rail, looking up at the stars, and humming a quiet song from his island, far away.

[pause]

{img:farmhouse-massachusetts-night-lamp-in-window-apple-trees-moonlight}
And far away, on a small farm in Massachusetts, a lamp is burning in a kitchen window. A mother is sitting at the table, writing a letter that will take many months to arrive. Outside, the apple trees stand quietly in the moonlight. The cows are asleep in the barn. And the whole farm is waiting, patiently, for the boy to come home.

[long-pause]

The sea goes on, as it always has. The waves rise and fall. The stars turn slowly overhead. Ships come and go, and the years go by. But the sea remains, wide and deep and calm, holding all its stories.

[pause]

Perhaps, tonight, you can imagine yourself there too. Lying in a narrow wooden bunk, under a warm wool blanket, while the ship rocks you slowly from side to side. Above you, on deck, the watch is walking quietly back and forth. The lantern swings gently. The sea murmurs against the planks, just a few centimetres from your ear. There is nothing you need to do, and nowhere you need to be. The ship will sail on through the night, and carry you safely toward the morning.

[pause]

If you enjoyed this journey to life aboard a whaling ship in 1850, you are warmly invited to subscribe, so that another calm story from the past is always close by, whenever you need one.

[pause]

{img:calm-night-ocean-moon-path-on-water-distant-ship-sails-peaceful}
There should be another quiet story waiting on the screen now, if you are still awake.

[pause]

Sleep well, and good night.
