Life on a whaling ship follows a steady rhythm. The crew is divided into two groups, called watches. One watch works while the other rests. Every four hours, a bell rings, and the watches change. Day and night, there are always men on deck, steering the ship and looking after the sails.

--

The day begins early. Just before sunrise, the men of the morning watch scrub the deck. They pour buckets of sea water over the planks. Then they scrub them with sand and a soft stone, which the sailors call a holystone. They kneel in rows and push the stones back and forth. The wood becomes clean and pale. Will does not like scrubbing. But he likes watching the sun come up over the sea while he works.

{img:sailors-breakfast-forecastle-tin-plates-hard-bread-coffee-mugs}
Breakfast is simple. There is coffee, sweetened with molasses, because sugar is too expensive. There is hard bread, called hardtack, which is so dry that the men soak it in their coffee. Sometimes there is a kind of stew, made from salted beef or pork and potatoes. The ordinary sailors eat in the forecastle, sitting on their sea chests. The food is the same, day after day. But after a morning of work, everything tastes good.

--

During the day, there is always work to do. Ropes wear out and must be replaced. Sails tear in the wind and must be mended. The wood of the ship must be painted and tarred, to protect it from the salt water. The cooper checks the barrels. The carpenter repairs anything that is broken. And the greenhands are sent up the masts again and again, to learn the rigging, until they can climb as quickly as monkeys.

{img:sailor-high-in-rigging-mast-ocean-below-blue-sky-wind}
Climbing the masts frightens Will at first. The main mast is as tall as a church tower. The ropes swing with every movement of the ship. When he looks down, the deck looks tiny, and the sea seems very far below. But Joaquim tells him to look up, never down, and to always hold on with one hand. After a few weeks, Will climbs without fear. And from the top, he can see the whole wide ocean, shining all around him.

--

In the afternoon, when the main work is done, the men have some free time. Some of them sleep. Some of them mend their clothes, sitting cross-legged on deck with needle and thread. Some of them play cards or tell stories. Some of them read, if they can read. And some of them sit quietly at the rail, smoking their pipes, and watching the sea go by.

{img:sailors-afternoon-rest-deck-mending-clothes-pipes-sun-sails-shadows}
Supper is eaten in the late afternoon. Then the sun sets, and the evening comes. Lanterns are lit. The night watch takes over. The other men go down to the forecastle to sleep. Will lies in his narrow bunk and listens to the sounds of the ship. The creak of the wood. The splash of the waves against the sides. The quiet footsteps of the men on deck above. The ship rocks gently, like a cradle. And Will falls asleep almost at once.

Every day at noon, the captain and the first mate measure the height of the sun with a brass instrument called a sextant. From that, they can work out how far north or south the ship is. With a good clock, called a chronometer, they can also work out how far east or west. Then the captain marks the ship's position on the chart with a small pencil cross. Day after day, the little crosses make a long, slow line across the ocean.

On some evenings, the men fish from the side of the ship. They catch bonito and mackerel, and sometimes a flying fish lands right on the deck by itself. Isaiah fries the fresh fish for supper, with a little salt and pepper. After weeks of salted meat, it tastes like a feast. The sailors call this a fresh mess, and they talk about it for days afterwards.
