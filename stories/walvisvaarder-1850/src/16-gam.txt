In the middle of the vast Pacific Ocean, a whaling ship may sail for months without seeing another ship. So when two whaling ships meet, it is a happy event. And the whalemen have a special word for it. They call it a gam.

--

When a lookout sees the sails of another ship, he calls down to the deck. The captain looks through his telescope. If it is a whaling ship, he steers toward it. As the two ships come closer, the sailors crowd along the rails, waving and shouting. They raise their flags, so they can see where the other ship comes from. Often, it is another ship from New Bedford, or from Nantucket. Sometimes they even know people on board.

{img:sailors-crowding-rail-waving-at-passing-ship-flags-flying}
The two ships stop, close to each other, in the middle of the ocean. Then the captain of one ship is rowed over to the other ship, to visit. And at the same time, the first mate of the other ship is rowed back, to visit the first ship. So both ships have a visitor, and both have someone to talk to. The captain's wife sometimes goes along too, if there is another captain's wife on the other ship.

--

A gam can last for hours, or even for a day or two, if there are no whales nearby. The sailors visit each other's forecastles. They exchange news. Where are the whales this season? Which ships have done well, and which have had bad luck? What has happened back home? Some ships have newspapers that are only a few months old, and the men read them over and over.

{img:sailors-two-crews-forecastle-sharing-news-letters-laughing-lamp}
The men also trade things. Tobacco, books, clothes, pieces of scrimshaw. And most important of all, they exchange letters. If one ship is going home, the other ship gives it letters to carry. If one ship has come from home more recently, it may have letters for the other ship's crew. This is how news travels across the oceans in 1850. Slowly, from ship to ship, like a message passed from hand to hand.

--

On the Hannah Grey's first gam, Will is lucky. The other ship has left New Bedford six months after the Hannah Grey. And in its mail bag, there is a letter for Will Coffin. It is from his mother. He takes it with trembling hands and reads it right there on deck. Everyone at home is well. His little sister has learned to read. The cow has had a calf. And the apple trees have given a wonderful harvest.

{img:boy-reading-letter-from-home-on-deck-smiling-tears-ships-behind}
Will reads the letter five times. Then he folds it carefully and puts it in his sea chest, next to the first letter from his mother. Patrick does not get a letter. So Will reads his aloud to him, in the evening, and they talk about home for a long time. Patrick says that one day, when they are old, he will come and visit Will's farm, and see the apple trees for himself. Will says he would like that very much.

--

When the gam is over, the visitors are rowed back to their own ships. The sailors wave goodbye. The two ships set their sails, and turn away from each other, each to its own part of the ocean. Soon, the other ship is only a little white shape on the horizon. And then it is gone, and the Hannah Grey is alone again, on the endless blue sea.

Sometimes a gam even ends in a little party. The two crews gather on one ship, and the fiddlers from both ships play together. The men sing the old songs, and dance on the deck. The captains sit in the cabin with their wives, drinking tea and talking about home. For a few hours, the lonely ocean feels like a village square. And when the visitors leave in the evening, everyone waves until the boats are out of sight.
