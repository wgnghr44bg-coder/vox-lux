The journey home is long, but it feels shorter than the journey out. The ship sails south across the Pacific, around Cape Horn again, and then north through the whole length of the Atlantic Ocean. This time, the Horn is kinder. The wind blows from behind, and the ship flies around the cape in only a few days. Every day, the men count the days until they will reach New Bedford.

--

The Hannah Grey is heavy now. Her hold is full of oil. More than two thousand barrels of whale oil and spermaceti. It is a very good voyage. The captain is pleased. The owners will be pleased. And every sailor will have his share. Even Will, with his small lay, will bring home some money for his family. Not a fortune. But enough to help with the farm.

{img:sailors-painting-ship-rail-cleaning-preparing-for-homecoming}
As they come closer to home, the men start to get the ship ready. They scrub the decks. They paint the sides. They mend the sails, so the ship will look her best when she arrives. The sailors trim their beards and cut each other's hair. They take out their best clothes, which have been folded in their sea chests for three years. Many of them no longer fit. Will has grown so much that his good jacket is now far too small.

--

At last, one bright spring morning, the lookout shouts again. But this time, he is not shouting about whales. Land ho! Land! Everyone rushes to the rail. And there, low on the horizon, is a grey line. It is the coast of America. The coast of home. Some of the old sailors have tears in their eyes. They have sailed around the whole world, and now they are back.

{img:new-bedford-harbour-spring-morning-crowd-on-wharf-ship-arriving}
The Hannah Grey sails slowly into the harbour of New Bedford. The news has spread through the town. A whaling ship is coming in. People hurry down to the wharf. Wives, mothers, children, friends. They have been waiting for more than three years. They shade their eyes with their hands, and look for faces at the rail of the ship. And on the ship, the sailors look for faces on the wharf.

--

Will does not expect anyone to be there. But as the ship comes closer, he sees a small figure in a grey shawl, standing at the front of the crowd. It is his mother. She has heard the ship was due, and she has come all the way to New Bedford, to wait for him. Beside her is his sister Ruth, who was nine when he left. Now she is almost thirteen, and not so little any more. When the ship touches the wharf, Will jumps down before anyone can stop him. And his mother holds him so tightly that he can hardly breathe.

{img:mother-embracing-tall-young-sailor-wharf-sister-smiling-crowd}
On the wharf, there are many happy reunions. Captain Howland's parents are there, to welcome Mary and little Lucy, who is now eight years old and talks without stopping. Patrick's family is there too, and they take Will and his mother home for supper. Joaquim is staying in New Bedford for a few weeks, before his next voyage. He shakes Will's hand and gives him a little scrimshaw, of a ship and a whale, swimming side by side.

--

A few days later, Will goes home to the farm. He has brought presents for everyone. Shells from the Pacific, a piece of Hawaiian cloth, and for his mother, the scrimshaw tooth with the picture of the farm. She looks at it for a long time, without saying anything. Then she puts it on the shelf by the window. That evening, Will walks out into the orchard. The apple trees are in blossom, white and pink, just as he remembered them.

When the oil has been sold, the sailors receive their pay. For the old sailors and the harpooners, it is a good sum. For the greenhands, it is much less, after the cost of their clothes and tobacco has been taken off. But Will still brings home more money than his family has ever seen at once. His father uses it to buy two more cows and to mend the roof of the barn.

Will never goes whaling again. He stays on the farm, and many years later, it becomes his own. He plants a whole new orchard, with rows and rows of apple trees. He marries, and has children of his own. And on winter evenings, by the fire, he tells them stories about the Hannah Grey. About Joaquim and Patrick and little Lucy, about Cape Horn and the Galapagos, and about the first whale he ever saw, so close he could almost touch it.
