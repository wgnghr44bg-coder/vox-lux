So we leave New Bedford tonight, in the year 1850, as the evening falls. The sun is setting over the harbour. The masts of the ships stand dark against the golden sky. The wharves are quiet now. The barrels of oil are standing in long rows, cooling in the evening air. And the seagulls have settled on the rooftops for the night.

--

Somewhere far away, on the wide Pacific Ocean, the Hannah Grey is sailing through the night. The lanterns are lit. The watch is on deck. The man at the wheel steers by the stars. And down in the forecastle, a boy from a small farm in Massachusetts is asleep in his narrow bunk, with two letters from his mother under his pillow.

{img:ship-at-night-on-calm-ocean-stars-lantern-glow-gentle-waves}
The ship rocks gently on the long, slow waves. The wood creaks softly. The water whispers along her sides. Far below, in the deep, dark sea, the whales are swimming too. A family of sperm whales, travelling slowly through the night, clicking softly to each other. Mothers and grandmothers and little ones, close together, as they have always been.

--

In the captain's cabin, Mary Howland closes her journal and blows out the lamp. Little Lucy is asleep in her small bed, holding the carved whale that Joaquim made for her. Out on deck, Joaquim himself is standing at the rail, looking up at the stars, and humming a quiet song from his island, far away.

{img:farmhouse-massachusetts-night-lamp-in-window-apple-trees-moonlight}
And far away, on a small farm in Massachusetts, a lamp is burning in a kitchen window. A mother is sitting at the table, writing a letter that will take many months to arrive. Outside, the apple trees stand quietly in the moonlight. The cows are asleep in the barn. And the whole farm is waiting, patiently, for the boy to come home.

--

The sea goes on, as it always has. The waves rise and fall. The stars turn slowly overhead. Ships come and go, and the years go by. But the sea remains, wide and deep and calm, holding all its stories.

Perhaps, tonight, you can imagine yourself there too. Lying in a narrow wooden bunk, under a warm wool blanket, while the ship rocks you slowly from side to side. Above you, on deck, the watch is walking quietly back and forth. The lantern swings gently. The sea murmurs against the planks, just a few centimetres from your ear. There is nothing you need to do, and nowhere you need to be. The ship will sail on through the night, and carry you safely toward the morning.
