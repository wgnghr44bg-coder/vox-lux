New Bedford in 1850 is the richest town in America, for its size. And almost all of its wealth comes from whales.

The town lies on a hill, sloping down to a wide harbour on the Atlantic coast of Massachusetts. Near the water, there are wharves and warehouses, rope walks and shipyards. Further up the hill, there are shops and banks, churches and taverns. And at the top of the hill, there are the great houses of the rich whaling merchants, with gardens and fine furniture, and windows looking out to sea.

--

In 1850, more than two hundred and fifty whaling ships sail from New Bedford. That is more than from any other port in the world. Thousands of people in the town work for the whaling business. There are sailors and captains, shipbuilders and coopers, rope makers and sail makers, blacksmiths and carpenters. There are men who make candles and men who refine the oil. Almost every family in New Bedford has someone at sea.

{img:candle-factory-1850-workers-spermaceti-candles-moulds-racks-bright}
Why was whale oil so important? In 1850, there is no electricity. People light their homes with candles and oil lamps. The best oil for lamps comes from whales, especially from the sperm whale. It burns with a bright, clean light, without much smoke or smell. The finest candles are made from a special wax, also from the sperm whale. They are called spermaceti candles, and they burn very brightly and evenly.

--

Whale oil also oils the machines in the new factories, which are spreading across America and Europe. It is used to make soap and paint and leather. And there is another product, called whalebone. It is not really bone, but long, flexible plates from the mouths of some kinds of whales. It is used to make things that need to be strong and springy, like umbrellas, hoops for skirts and stiff corsets for ladies' dresses.

{img:lamplighter-1850-lighting-whale-oil-street-lamp-dusk-new-bedford}
In the evenings, the street lamps of New Bedford burn with whale oil. A lamplighter walks along the streets with a ladder, lighting them one by one. The windows of the houses glow with the soft yellow light of oil lamps. People say that the whole world reads by the light of New Bedford. And in a way, it is true.

--

The whaling merchants of New Bedford are rich, but many of them are Quakers. Quakers are Christians who believe in simple living and plain clothes. They do not like to show off. So even the richest merchants often wear plain grey and black clothes. Many Quakers also believe strongly that slavery is wrong. New Bedford is a place where people who have escaped from slavery in the South can find work and safety. One of them, a young man called Frederick Douglass, lived and worked in New Bedford, and later became one of the most famous speakers in America.

{img:quaker-merchants-plain-dress-counting-house-ledgers-harbour-window-1850}
The people of New Bedford are used to saying goodbye. Every week, a ship sails out of the harbour, on a voyage of three or four years. Wives and mothers and children stand on the wharves and wave. And every week, a ship comes home, after years at sea. Then there are cheers and tears and hugs on the wharves. Families walk up to the hill above the harbour, to watch for the ships. Some houses have a little platform on the roof, where wives can look out to sea. People call it a widow's walk. But mostly, they are places of hope, where people wait for their loved ones to come home.

In the evenings, the streets of the rich part of town are lit by lamps that burn whale oil. Their soft yellow light shines on the cobblestones and the white fences. Through the windows of the big houses, you can see families sitting at their tables, reading or sewing by the light of spermaceti candles. All that gentle light, in a way, comes from the sea. It was carried home in barrels, from the other side of the world.

The streets near the harbour are much busier and much rougher. There are boarding houses for sailors, where men sleep while they wait for a ship. There are clothing shops, where a greenhand can buy everything he needs for a voyage. And there are men called outfitters, who sell the sailors their gear on credit. The cost is taken from the sailor's share at the end of the voyage. Often, the prices are much too high.
