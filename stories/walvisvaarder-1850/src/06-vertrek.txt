On a bright autumn morning, the Hannah Grey sets sail. The whole crew is on board. The barrels are loaded. The whaleboats are hanging in their davits. And on the wharf, a crowd of people has gathered, to say goodbye.

The sailors climb up the masts and loosen the great sails. The anchor is raised, with a long rattling of chains. The sails fill with wind. And slowly, the ship moves away from the wharf. On the wharf, wives and mothers and children wave their handkerchiefs. Some of them are crying. They will not see their loved ones for three or four years.

--

Will stands at the rail, looking back at the town. He knows his family is not there. They could not come to New Bedford to see him off. But he waves anyway, at everyone on the wharf. Patrick stands beside him, waving too. The town gets smaller and smaller. The church spires and the houses on the hill become little shapes. And then the ship sails out of the harbour, past the lighthouse, into the open sea.

{img:greenhand-seasick-leaning-rail-ship-rolling-grey-waves-first-day}
In the first days at sea, Will is very seasick. The ship rolls and pitches in the Atlantic waves. His head spins and his stomach turns over. He can hardly eat. He lies in his bunk, feeling miserable, and wonders why he ever wanted to go to sea. Many of the other greenhands are sick too. The old sailors laugh at them, but kindly. Everyone is seasick at first, they say. In a few days, you will be fine.

--

On the first night, Will remembers his mother's letter. He takes it out of his sea chest and reads it by the light of the little lamp in the forecastle. She writes that she is proud of him. She writes that she will pray for him every night. She writes that the apple trees will be waiting for him when he comes home. And she reminds him to change his socks. Will smiles, and holds the letter for a long time. Then he folds it carefully and puts it under his pillow.

{img:boy-reading-letter-bunk-forecastle-lamp-night-homesick-smile}
After four days, Will wakes up and feels better. The ship is still rolling. But his stomach is calm. He is hungry for the first time since they left. He eats a big bowl of Isaiah's porridge and a piece of hard bread, and it tastes wonderful. Then he goes up on deck. The sea is blue and sparkling, and the sun is shining. The ship is sailing fast, with all her sails filled. And Will feels, for the first time, that he is really a sailor.

--

In the first weeks, the greenhands have a lot to learn. They must learn the names of all the sails and ropes, and there are hundreds of them. They must learn to climb the rigging, up to the tops of the masts. They must learn to tie knots, and to steer the ship, and to row the whaleboats. The mates teach them, patiently and sometimes not so patiently. Joaquim, the harpooner, helps them most. He shows them the knots slowly, again and again, until they get them right.

{img:harpooner-teaching-boys-knots-deck-ropes-patient-sunny-afternoon}
Every day, the greenhands practise rowing in the whaleboats. The boats are lowered into the sea, and the crews row around the ship, fast, in rhythm. The mate stands at the back, steering with a long oar, and calling out the stroke. At first, the greenhands are clumsy. Their oars bump into each other, and they splash everyone. But slowly, they learn to row together, smoothly and fast. Will's hands get blisters, and then hard skin. He is getting stronger every day.

In the evenings of those first weeks, Will practises his knots by the lamp in the forecastle. The bowline, the reef knot, the clove hitch, the sheet bend. He ties them and unties them, again and again, until his fingers know them by heart. The old sailors watch him and nod. A sailor who knows his knots, they say, is a sailor who will come home. Will does not know if it is true. But he keeps practising anyway.
