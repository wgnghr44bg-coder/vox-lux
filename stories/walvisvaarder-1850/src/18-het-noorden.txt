In 1848, just two years before our story, a whaling captain from New York sailed through the Bering Strait, the narrow sea between Alaska and Russia. On the other side, in the Arctic Ocean, he found great numbers of bowhead whales. The news spread quickly. And soon, many whaling ships were sailing north every summer, into the icy waters of the Arctic.

--

The Hannah Grey sails north for many weeks. The weather gets colder again. The sea turns grey, and then dark green. Fog rolls over the water, thick and white. One morning, the lookout shouts that there is ice ahead. Will climbs up to see. And there, stretching away into the fog, is a vast field of white ice, floating on the sea, cracked into pieces like a broken plate.

{img:polar-bear-on-ice-floe-distance-fog-quiet-arctic-morning}
The Arctic is a strange and beautiful world. In summer, the sun hardly sets at all. At midnight, it hangs low over the horizon, golden and pale. The ice glows blue and white. Walruses lie on the ice floes, huge and brown, with long white tusks. Seals pop their heads out of the water and look at the ship with big dark eyes. And once, far away on the ice, Will sees a polar bear, walking slowly, all alone.

--

But the Arctic is also dangerous. The ice is always moving. The wind can push the ice floes together, and a ship can be trapped between them. Then the ice squeezes the ship, harder and harder, until the wood cracks. The captain must be very careful. He must keep watching the ice and the wind, and always leave a way out, back to open water.

{img:ship-navigating-narrow-channel-between-ice-floes-sailors-watching-tense}
One afternoon, the fog comes down suddenly, thicker than ever. The sailors can barely see the front of the ship from the back. Then the wind changes. And the ice begins to close in around them. The men can hear it, in the fog. A grinding, creaking sound, getting closer and closer. Captain Howland orders all the boats to be lowered. The men row ahead of the ship, towing it with ropes, through the narrowing channels between the ice.

--

For hours, they row and pull, in the cold and the fog. Will's hands are frozen. His breath makes clouds in the air. The ice is so close now that the men can touch it with their oars. Sometimes they push the ice floes away with long poles. The captain stands at the bow, peering into the fog, calling out directions. Left a little. Now right. Steady. Everyone is silent, except for the sound of the oars and the creaking of the ice.

{img:fog-lifting-open-water-ahead-ship-free-of-ice-pale-sun}
And then, slowly, the fog begins to lift. A pale sun shines through. And ahead of them, there is open water, dark and calm. The ship slides out of the ice and into the open sea. The men in the boats give a great shout of joy. They climb back on board, exhausted and frozen. Isaiah has hot coffee waiting for everyone. And Mary Howland writes in her journal that evening, in a small, neat hand, a single line. Thank God, we are free.

--

Not all ships were so lucky. Some years later, in 1871, more than thirty whaling ships were trapped in the Arctic ice and crushed. But the sailors had time to leave the ships, and they all escaped in their whaleboats, and were rescued by other ships. More than a thousand people, including the captains' wives and children, sailed away safely. Nobody was lost. But almost all of the ships were lost to the ice.

Sometimes the whaling ships meet the people who live along the cold Arctic coasts. They are the Inupiat of Alaska and the Yupik and Chukchi people of the Russian shore. They paddle out to the ships in boats made of skin stretched over wooden frames. They trade warm furs and fresh meat for tools, cloth and needles. These people have hunted bowhead whales for thousands of years, in small numbers, with great care and respect. They still do today.
