Tonight we travel back about a hundred and seventy-five years, to the year 1850. We go to a busy harbour town on the east coast of America. It is called New Bedford. And in its harbour, dozens of tall wooden ships are getting ready for long voyages across the oceans of the world.

--

It is early morning in autumn, and a soft mist lies over the water. The masts of the ships rise up through the mist, like a forest of bare trees. Seagulls cry and circle over the wharves. On the long wooden piers, there are rows and rows of barrels, thousands of them, stacked in the sun. They are full of oil. And everywhere, there is a strong smell of tar, salt and fish.

Men are already at work on the wharves. Coopers are hammering barrels together. Riggers are climbing up the masts, fixing ropes. Carts are rolling along the cobbled streets, loaded with food and supplies. A bell rings on a ship. A sailor calls out to another. And a boy of fourteen stands at the end of a wharf, looking up at a big ship, with a small bag over his shoulder, and his heart beating fast.

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

--

{img:whaling-ship-1850-three-masts-whaleboats-hanging-davits-wharf-new-bedford}
The ships in this harbour are whaling ships. They sail to every ocean on Earth, looking for whales. In those days, the oil from whales was very important. It was used in lamps, to light houses and streets and lighthouses, all over the world. It was used to oil machines in the new factories. Whaling was a big and important business. And New Bedford was the most important whaling town in the world.

A whaling voyage was very long. A ship might be away for three or four years, sailing around the whole world. The sailors lived on board all that time, far from home, in a small wooden world. They worked hard and saw amazing things. They visited faraway islands and met people from every corner of the Earth. And they waited, sometimes for weeks, for a whale to be seen.

{img:boy-fourteen-1850-standing-wharf-small-bag-looking-up-at-ship-nervous}
Tonight we will sail on a whaling ship, with that boy of fourteen. His name is Will. It is his first voyage. We will meet the captain and his wife, the cook and the old harpooner, and sailors from many lands. We will learn how they lived and worked, what they ate, and how they passed the long evenings. We will sail around Cape Horn, to the Galápagos Islands and to Hawaii.

--

We will not follow the hunting of the whales closely. It was hard and dangerous work, and today, we see it very differently. Whales are now protected in most of the world, and people go to sea to watch them, not to hunt them. But tonight, we will try to understand the lives of the people who sailed on these ships, long ago. And we will meet the great whales too, gently, as they swim through the oceans.

{img:sperm-whale-gentle-surfacing-calm-ocean-blow-spout-morning-light}
So let us begin on a wharf in New Bedford, on an autumn morning in 1850. The mist is lifting from the harbour. The sun is shining on the water. And the boy with the small bag takes a deep breath, and walks up the gangplank onto the ship that will be his home for the next three years.

Along the waterfront, the shops are already opening. There are shops that sell ropes and sails, and shops that sell compasses, telescopes and charts of the oceans. There are shops for sea boots and oilskin coats. There are bakeries that make hard ship's bread by the thousand. Almost everything in this town has something to do with ships and the sea. Even the children play at being whalemen, running along the wharves with sticks for harpoons.
