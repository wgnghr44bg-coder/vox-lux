About thirty men sail on the Hannah Grey. They come from all over the world. A whaling ship is one of the most mixed places of its time.

At the top is the captain, Captain Benjamin Howland. He is about forty-five years old, with a grey beard and calm blue eyes. He has been at sea since he was a boy, like Will. He has commanded whaling ships for fifteen years. He is strict but fair. The men respect him. When he gives an order, it is obeyed at once.

--

Below the captain, there are the mates, the officers. The first mate, Mr. Swift, is a tall, quiet man from Nantucket, an island near New Bedford famous for its whalemen. He is in charge of the crew and the daily work of the ship. The second and third mates help him. Each mate is also in charge of one of the whaleboats.

{img:harpooner-cape-verdean-1850-strong-calm-coiling-rope-deck-sunlight}
Then there are the harpooners, who are also called boatsteerers. They are the most skilled men in the whaleboats. One of them is called Joaquim. He comes from the Cape Verde Islands, a group of islands off the coast of Africa. Many fine whalemen come from those islands. Joaquim is about thirty, strong and calm, with a gentle smile. He has sailed on six voyages. He speaks Portuguese and a little English.

--

The ordinary sailors come from everywhere. There are young men from the farms and towns of New England, like Will. There are sailors from Ireland and England, from Portugal and the Azores. There are men from the Pacific islands, especially from Hawaii, who are wonderful swimmers and sailors. There are Native Americans, like the Wampanoag men from Martha's Vineyard, near New Bedford, who have been whalemen for generations. And there are African American sailors, some of whom have escaped from slavery in the South.

{img:sailors-many-backgrounds-mending-sails-deck-talking-laughing-sun}
On a whaling ship, what matters most is whether a man can do his job. In many ways, the ship is fairer than the land. African American sailors can become harpooners and mates, and a few even become captains. The men eat together, sleep together and work together. They depend on each other in the whaleboats. Of course, not everything is equal. But many sailors say they found more respect at sea than on land.

--

There is also a cook, an old African American man called Isaiah. He has been cooking on ships for thirty years. He is round and cheerful, and he sings hymns while he works. He cooks in a little kitchen on deck, called the galley. And there is a cooper, who makes and mends the barrels. There is a carpenter, a blacksmith and a steward, who looks after the captain's cabin.

{img:old-ships-cook-galley-singing-pot-stove-cheerful-1850}
On his first day, Will makes friends with another greenhand, a boy of sixteen called Patrick. Patrick comes from Ireland. His family came to America a few years before, during a terrible famine in Ireland. He has a quick smile and a lot of red hair. He is as nervous as Will. The two boys find their bunks next to each other in the forecastle. And they promise to look after each other, for the whole long voyage.

Every man on board has a nickname, sooner or later. The cook is called the Doctor, which is an old joke on ships. The carpenter is called Chips, because of the wood shavings. The tallest sailor is called Shorty. And Will, because he comes from a farm, is soon called Farmer. At first, he does not like it. But the men say it kindly, and after a while, he starts to feel rather proud of it.

The cabin boy is the youngest person on board, apart from the captain's daughter. He is only twelve years old, a thin boy from Boston called Sam. He serves the captain's meals, cleans the cabin and runs errands all day long. He is shy at first. But Will and Patrick take him under their wing. Before long, the three of them are always together, whenever there is time.
