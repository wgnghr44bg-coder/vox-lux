After a whale is caught, there is a great deal of work to do. The whale is tied to the side of the ship with heavy chains. Over the side, the men lower a narrow platform of planks, called the cutting stage. The mates stand on it, with long, sharp spades on wooden poles. And they begin to cut away the thick layer of blubber that covers the whale's body.

--

Blubber is the fat under a whale's skin. It keeps the whale warm in the cold sea. On a sperm whale, it is about thirty centimetres thick. The men cut it off in one long strip, which winds around the whale like the peel of an orange. The strip is lifted up onto the deck with a great hook and a system of ropes. The pieces are so heavy that the whole ship leans to one side.

{img:try-works-fire-glowing-night-deck-iron-pots-smoke-sailors-silhouettes}
On deck, the blubber is cut into smaller pieces, and then into thin slices, like the pages of a book. The sailors call these slices bible leaves. Then the pieces go into the two great iron pots of the try-works. A fire is lit in the brick oven below. And slowly, the blubber begins to melt, and turns into clear, golden oil.

--

The fire of the try-works is fed with pieces of the blubber itself, once the oil has been cooked out of them. They burn very hot and very bright. So the whale, in a strange way, provides its own fuel. The try-works burn day and night, without stopping, until all the blubber is boiled down. For a big whale, this can take three days.

{img:whaling-ship-at-night-glowing-red-try-works-black-smoke-dark-sea}
At night, a whaling ship with the try-works burning is an unforgettable sight. The fire glows red and orange in the darkness. Thick black smoke rises from the chimney and drifts away over the sea. The faces of the men are lit up by the flames, shining with sweat. The smell is very strong, like burning fat, and it gets into everything. The clothes, the hair, the wood of the ship. Sailors said you could smell a whaling ship long before you could see it.

--

Meanwhile, the men also open the head of the sperm whale. Inside, there is the spermaceti, the clear, precious oil. It is so pure that the men simply scoop it out with buckets. When it cools, it turns into a soft white wax. This is the oil that will become the finest candles in New Bedford. One sperm whale can give more than a thousand litres of spermaceti.

{img:cooper-filling-barrels-golden-oil-ship-deck-lantern-light}
The hot oil is poured into big copper tanks to cool. Then it is poured into barrels. The cooper checks every barrel carefully, to make sure it does not leak. The full barrels are rolled across the deck and lowered into the hold. One large sperm whale can fill about eighty barrels. And every barrel is worth a good deal of money, back in New Bedford.

--

When the work is finally done, the whole ship is covered in oil and soot. The decks are slippery and black. So the men clean everything. They scrub the deck with lye and sand, and they wash their clothes and themselves. Strangely, the whale oil is good for the wood. After a good cleaning, the decks are whiter than ever. And the men, tired but satisfied, can rest at last. Will sleeps for twelve hours, without dreaming at all.

While the try-works are burning, the cook sometimes does something the sailors love. He takes some dough and fries little doughnuts in the hot whale oil. They are crisp and golden on the outside and soft on the inside. The men eat them by the light of the fire, in the middle of the night. It is a strange meal, perhaps. But after hours of hard work, nothing tastes better.

By the end of the whole voyage, the cooper will have filled and sealed more than two thousand barrels. Each one is marked with the name of the ship, and stored carefully in the hold, packed tightly so it cannot roll. As the hold slowly fills, the ship sits lower and lower in the water. The sailors like to watch this. A low ship, they say, is a happy ship, because it means she is nearly ready to go home.
