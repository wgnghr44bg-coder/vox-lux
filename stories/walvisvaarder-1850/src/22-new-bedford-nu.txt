Today, New Bedford is a city of about a hundred thousand people. It is still a fishing port. In fact, it is one of the most valuable fishing ports in the whole United States, famous for its scallops. Every day, fishing boats come and go from the harbour, just as the whaling ships did long ago.

--

But the old part of the town, near the harbour, has been carefully preserved. It is called the New Bedford Whaling National Historical Park. There are cobblestone streets, old brick warehouses and banks, and the houses of the ship owners and captains. Walking through these streets, it is easy to imagine what the town looked like in 1850, with the smell of oil in the air and the masts of ships rising over the rooftops.

{img:whaling-museum-new-bedford-whale-skeleton-hanging-hall-today}
In the middle of the old town is the New Bedford Whaling Museum. It is one of the most wonderful museums in America. When you walk in, you see the enormous skeletons of whales, hanging from the ceiling. There is a blue whale, a humpback whale, a sperm whale and a right whale. They are so big that you have to look up and up, to see the whole of them.

--

The museum also has a whaling ship. Not a real one, but a model, built at half size. It is called the Lagoda, and it is the largest model ship in the world. Children can climb on board, and walk on the deck, and look at the try-works and the whaleboats. It gives a very good idea of how small and crowded the real ships were.

{img:scrimshaw-collection-museum-glass-case-carved-teeth-soft-light-today}
And the museum has the largest collection of scrimshaw in the world. Thousands of carved whale teeth and bones, made by sailors like Will, on long quiet evenings at sea. There are ships and whales, flowers and ladies, and little love messages. There are also logbooks and journals, kept by captains and their wives. Each one tells the story of a long voyage, day by day, in faded ink.

--

Across the street from the museum, there is a small white church, called the Seamen's Bethel. It was built in 1832, for the sailors of New Bedford. On its walls, there are marble plaques, remembering sailors who were lost at sea. Many whalemen went to a service here before they sailed. The pulpit inside is shaped like the front of a ship. Visitors still come every day to sit quietly in the old wooden pews.

{img:whale-watching-boat-people-at-rail-humpback-tail-cape-cod-today}
Not far from New Bedford, in the waters near Cape Cod, whales still swim every summer. Humpbacks, fin whales and even the rare right whales come there to feed. And every day, boats full of people go out to watch them. When a whale rises from the water, or lifts its great tail into the air, everyone on the boat gasps and points and smiles.

--

Many of the people on those boats have ancestors who were whalemen. Their great-great-grandfathers sailed from New Bedford to hunt the whales. Now their great-great-grandchildren sail out to watch them, and to protect them. It is a gentle ending to a long story. The town that once lived from the whales now loves them. And in the museum, the old harpoons hang quietly on the walls, never to be used again.

Every year, in the winter, people come together at the Whaling Museum for a special event. They read the whole of the novel Moby-Dick aloud, from beginning to end, without stopping. It takes about twenty-five hours. Hundreds of readers take turns, each reading a few pages. Some of them read in the middle of the night, to a sleepy audience wrapped in blankets. It is a lovely, cosy tradition, in a town that still loves its old stories.

In the museum, there is also a room about the people of New Bedford. The Cape Verdeans, the Azoreans, the Wampanoag, the African American sailors and the Hawaiians. Their stories are told with photographs and letters, and with objects their families have kept. Visitors can learn how many different people worked together on the whaling ships. And how the town became, in the end, a home for all of them.
