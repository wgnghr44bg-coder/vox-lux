There is something on the Hannah Grey that Will did not expect. In the captain's cabin, at the back of the ship, live two passengers. The captain's wife, Mary Howland, and their little daughter, Lucy, who is five years old.

--

In 1850, it was becoming more common for whaling captains to take their wives along on the long voyages. Being apart for four years was very hard for a family. So some wives chose to sail with their husbands, and sometimes their children came too. The sailors called these ships hen frigates. They joked about them a little. But secretly, many of them liked having a woman and a child on board. It made the ship feel more like home.

{img:little-girl-1850-playing-with-doll-captains-cabin-stern-windows-light}
Mary Howland is about thirty-five years old. She is a calm, sensible woman, with brown hair pinned up under a white cap. She grew up in New Bedford, the daughter of a ship's carpenter. She has already sailed on one voyage with her husband. She keeps a journal every day, writing about the weather, the whales, the ports they visit and the life on board. Her journal is full of small details, and sometimes funny remarks.

--

Life for the captain's wife is lonely in some ways. She is the only grown woman on the ship. She cannot really be friends with the sailors. She spends most of her time in the cabin, sewing, reading, writing letters and teaching Lucy. She has brought a little library of books, and some flower seeds, which she plants in boxes near the stern windows. She grows a few herbs and a pot of geraniums, which flower bravely in the middle of the ocean.

{img:geraniums-flower-box-cabin-window-sea-outside-soft-light}
Little Lucy is the darling of the whole ship. She has blond curls and a loud laugh. She runs around the deck, as far as she is allowed, and watches everything the sailors do. The carpenter makes her a little wooden doll. Isaiah, the cook, saves her the best biscuits. The sailors teach her songs and knots. And Joaquim carves her a little whale from a piece of whale tooth, which she carries everywhere.

--

Will and Patrick become friends with Lucy too. Sometimes, on calm afternoons, they are allowed to play with her on deck. They tell her stories about their homes. Will tells her about the farm, and the apple trees, and the cows. Patrick tells her about Ireland, and about fairies and giants. Lucy listens with wide eyes. She has spent half her young life at sea. She has never seen a cow.

{img:boys-telling-stories-to-little-girl-on-deck-sails-afternoon-calm}
Mary Howland also helps when the men are sick or hurt. There is no doctor on board. The captain has a medicine chest, with a book that explains how to use it. But Mary is gentler and more patient. When a sailor has a fever, she brings him soup and cool water. When a man cuts his hand, she cleans and bandages it. The sailors are grateful. They call her the captain's lady, and they take off their caps when she passes.

--

Many years later, the journals of the whaling wives became very precious. Some of them are kept today in the museums and libraries of New England. They tell us what life was really like on the ships, in the words of the people who lived it. The small joys, the long days, the homesickness, the beauty of the sea. Through their pages, we can still hear the quiet voices of these women, writing by lamplight in their little cabins, so far from home.

Mary also teaches little Lucy her letters and her numbers, every morning, at the table in the cabin. They use a small slate and a piece of chalk. Lucy learns to count by counting the sails, and the barrels, and the birds following the ship. She learns geography in the best way possible, by sailing around the world. By the time she is seven, she can point to Cape Horn and Hawaii on her father's charts.
