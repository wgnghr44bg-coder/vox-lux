On the eighteenth of September 1698, a prisoner wearing a black mask arrived at the Bastille in Paris. In the afternoon of the eighteenth of September 1698, a closed carriage drove into the Bastille, the great royal fortress and prison on the eastern edge of Paris. Inside was the new governor of the Bastille, Bénigne Dauvergne de Saint-Mars, and a prisoner whose face was hidden. The prisoner wore a mask of black velvet. Historians know about his arrival from the journal of Étienne du Junca, the king's lieutenant at the Bastille, who wrote down the arrival of every prisoner. Du Junca noted that the governor had brought an old prisoner with him, whom he had guarded for many years, and that the prisoner's face was always covered by a mask. The prisoner's name was not written down. Even so, du Junca recorded that a room had been prepared for him in a tower of the fortress. From that day on, the masked prisoner lived in the Bastille, quietly and carefully watched, until his death five years later.

[long-pause]

{img:02-bastille-fortress-eight-towers-paris-1700}

The Bastille was a grey stone fortress with eight round towers, standing at the eastern gate of Paris. The Bastille was built in the fourteenth century to protect the eastern entrance to Paris. Its full name was the Bastille Saint-Antoine, because it stood next to the gate of Saint-Antoine. It had eight round towers, joined by thick walls about twenty-four metres high, and was surrounded by a deep moat. By the seventeenth century, it was no longer needed as a defence. Instead, it was used as a state prison. Historians note that it held prisoners sent there on the direct order of the king, through a sealed letter called a lettre de cachet. Many prisoners were nobles, writers or people involved in political matters. Even so, the Bastille was not a large prison. It usually held between twenty and fifty prisoners at a time. Each tower had a name, such as the Liberty Tower, the Well Tower and the Bertaudière Tower. Its grim towers became a famous symbol of royal power.

Most of what is known about the masked prisoner comes from a lieutenant's careful journal. Étienne du Junca was the king's lieutenant at the Bastille from 1690 until his death in 1706. He kept a detailed journal of the prisoners who entered and left the fortress. He wrote down their names, the dates of their arrival, the rooms they were given and, when they died, the dates of their deaths and burials. Historians consider his journal one of the most important sources for the story of the masked prisoner. It is kept today in the Arsenal Library in Paris. Du Junca's entries about the masked prisoner are short. He wrote about his arrival in 1698 and his death in 1703. Even so, these few lines are among the only direct records of the prisoner's life in the Bastille. Du Junca did not seem to know who the prisoner was. He never wrote the name, and he described him only as the old prisoner of Monsieur de Saint-Mars.

Although he became famous as the man in the iron mask, the prisoner's mask was actually made of black velvet. The prisoner became famous around the world as the Man in the Iron Mask. Yet historians note that the records from his own time describe a mask of black velvet, not iron. Du Junca wrote in his journal that the prisoner always wore a mask of black velvet. Velvet is a soft, thick fabric, often used for fine clothing. In the seventeenth century, wearing a velvet mask outdoors was not unusual. Some ladies wore them to protect their skin from the sun. Even so, it was very unusual for a prisoner to wear one. The idea of an iron mask came later. It appeared in stories told in the eighteenth century, especially by the writer Voltaire. An iron mask made the tale more dramatic and mysterious. Today, historians believe the prisoner probably wore the mask only when others might see him, such as when he travelled or when visitors were present.

The masked prisoner was held on the orders of King Louis the Fourteenth. Louis the Fourteenth was king of France from 1643 until 1715, one of the longest reigns in European history. He became king at the age of four and ruled personally from 1661. He was known as the Sun King, because he chose the sun as his symbol. During his reign, France became the most powerful country in Europe. Historians know that the masked prisoner was held on the direct orders of the king and his ministers. Letters about the prisoner were written by the king's ministers to the jailer, Saint-Mars. Even so, the letters never explained why the prisoner was being held. They only gave strict instructions about keeping him secret. Some letters suggest that the king himself took an interest in the prisoner's care. Louis the Fourteenth died in 1715, twelve years after the prisoner. Whatever he knew about the prisoner's identity, he took to his grave.

The prisoner had been held in secret for nearly thirty years before he reached the Bastille. When the masked prisoner arrived at the Bastille in 1698, he was not a new prisoner. According to the surviving letters, he had first been arrested in 1669, almost thirty years earlier. Since then, he had been held in several different fortresses, always under the guard of the same jailer, Saint-Mars. Each time Saint-Mars was given a new post, the prisoner went with him. Historians have traced his journey through letters written by the ministers of Louis the Fourteenth. These letters were kept in the French state archives. Even so, they rarely mention the prisoner by name. He is usually described in vague terms, such as the prisoner, or the one from Pignerol. Over these long years, the prisoner grew from a young man into an old one. By the time he reached Paris, he was probably in his fifties or sixties. He had spent most of his adult life behind walls.

Outside the walls of the Bastille, Paris was one of the largest cities in Europe. Around the year 1700, Paris had about half a million inhabitants, making it one of the largest cities in Europe. Its narrow streets were crowded with carriages, carts, street sellers and craftspeople. Houses were built on the bridges over the river Seine. Historians note that the city was full of churches, markets and workshops, and its people worked as bakers, weavers, water carriers and booksellers. The king and his court no longer lived in Paris. In 1682, Louis the Fourteenth had moved his court to his great palace at Versailles, about twenty kilometres away. Even so, Paris remained the heart of France. It was a centre of trade, learning and fashion. Its people loved news and gossip, and stories about the court spread quickly through the city. The secret prisoner in the Bastille would soon become one of the most popular subjects of rumour in all of Paris.

[long-pause]

{img:08-versailles-palace-gardens-fountains-1690}

Louis the Fourteenth built the Palace of Versailles, one of the grandest palaces in the world. Versailles began as a small hunting lodge built by Louis the Thirteenth, the father of Louis the Fourteenth. From the 1660s, Louis the Fourteenth turned it into an enormous palace. Thousands of workers built new wings, halls and gardens. The famous Hall of Mirrors was completed in 1684. Historians note that the gardens were as important as the palace. They were designed by André Le Nôtre, with long avenues, fountains, statues and a great canal. In 1682, the king made Versailles the official home of the royal court and the government. Even so, life at Versailles was not always comfortable. The palace was crowded, cold in winter and noisy with visitors. Thousands of courtiers lived there, hoping to win the king's favour. Every part of the king's day, from waking to going to bed, was a public ceremony. Versailles became a model for royal palaces across Europe.

[long-pause]

{img:09-royal-ministers-council-table-papers-1680}

Louis the Fourteenth ruled with the help of a small group of powerful ministers. Louis the Fourteenth wanted to rule France himself, without a chief minister. He met regularly with a small council of trusted advisers. Two of the most important were Jean-Baptiste Colbert, who managed the kingdom's finances and trade, and the Marquis de Louvois, who was in charge of the army and war. Historians note that Louvois was also responsible for the state prisons and fortresses. That is why many of the letters about the masked prisoner were written by him. Louvois was hardworking, strict and feared by many. He built up the French army into the largest in Europe. Even so, he was also known for his harshness. After his death in 1691, his son, the Marquis de Barbezieux, took over his duties and continued to write letters about the prisoner. These ministers' letters, written by secretaries and signed by the ministers, are now among the most important sources for the mystery.

{img:10-royal-letter-wax-seal-quill-desk}

With a sealed royal letter, the king could send anyone to prison without a trial. In France under Louis the Fourteenth, the king had the power to imprison people without a trial. He did this through a document called a lettre de cachet, which means a letter with a seal. It was signed by the king and a minister and sealed with the royal seal. Historians note that these letters were used for many reasons. Some were used against people who had criticised the government or caused public scandal. Others were requested by families who wanted to stop a relative from wasting the family fortune or bringing shame on their name. Even so, the system was often criticised as unfair, because prisoners had no chance to defend themselves. Some were held for a few weeks, while others were held for many years. The masked prisoner was held by the king's order. The lettres de cachet became a symbol of royal power that was later attacked during the French Revolution.

The king's musketeers were an elite company of soldiers who guarded the king. The musketeers of the guard were an elite company of soldiers who served the king of France. They were formed in 1622 under Louis the Thirteenth. They wore blue tunics decorated with a silver cross, and they fought both on horseback and on foot. Many young nobles joined them, hoping to win the king's notice and begin a career. Historians note that one of the most famous musketeers was Charles de Batz de Castelmore, known as d'Artagnan. He became captain-lieutenant of the musketeers and served Louis the Fourteenth loyally. He later became famous through the novels of Alexandre Dumas. Even so, the real d'Artagnan was quite different from the character in the books. He was a careful and trusted officer. In 1661, the king gave him an important secret task. The jailer Saint-Mars had served under him in the musketeers, and this would shape the rest of his career.

{img:12-french-army-camp-tents-flags-1670}

During the reign of Louis the Fourteenth, France was often at war with its neighbours. Louis the Fourteenth wanted to make France safer and more powerful by expanding its borders. During his reign, France fought several major wars. These included the War of Devolution against Spain, the Dutch War against the Dutch Republic and its allies, and later the Nine Years' War and the War of the Spanish Succession. Historians note that these wars were very expensive and cost many lives. Yet they also added new territories to France, including parts of Flanders and the region of Franche-Comté. Even so, war affected the lives of prisoners too. Fortresses on the borders, such as Pignerol in the Alps, were important military places, and some became prisons for people the king wished to keep far from Paris. The secret letters about the masked prisoner were written during these years of war, when the king's ministers were busy with armies, sieges and treaties across Europe.

For ordinary people in France, life was mostly about work, bread and the changing seasons. While the king and his court lived in splendour, most people in France lived simple lives. About eight out of ten people were peasants, working the land in small villages. They grew wheat, rye and vegetables, kept animals and paid taxes to the king, the church and their local lords. In towns, people worked as craftspeople, shopkeepers and labourers. Historians note that bread was the most important food, and its price mattered greatly to ordinary families. A bad harvest could cause hunger. Even so, life also had its pleasures. People enjoyed festivals, fairs, markets and religious holidays. They gathered in taverns and churches and shared news and rumours. Stories about the king, his court and mysterious events travelled quickly by word of mouth. The tale of a masked prisoner was exactly the kind of story that people loved to tell, and it spread from town to town.

Hundreds of letters from the king's ministers about the state prisons have survived in the archives. The French government of the seventeenth century kept many written records. Letters from ministers to governors, officers and jailers were copied into registers before they were sent. Many of these copies survive today in French archives, including the archives of the Ministry of War. Historians have searched through them for clues about the masked prisoner. Among them are letters from Louvois and Barbezieux to Saint-Mars, giving orders about his prisoners. Some replies from Saint-Mars survive as well. Even so, the letters do not answer the main question. They show how carefully the prisoner was guarded, but never clearly say who he was or why he was held. Some historians believe that the letters were written in a deliberately vague way. Others think that everyone involved already knew the reasons, so there was no need to write them down. The archives keep their silence on the most important point.

In July 1669, a man was arrested at the port of Dunkirk, on the northern coast of France. The story of the masked prisoner begins in July 1669. On the nineteenth of that month, the minister Louvois wrote a letter to Saint-Mars, then governor of the prison fortress of Pignerol. He told him to prepare a cell for a prisoner who was being sent to him. The prisoner was arrested near Dunkirk, a port on the northern coast of France, which Louis the Fourteenth had bought from England a few years earlier, in 1662. Historians know about the arrest from this letter and from others written at the same time. The arrest was made by Captain Alexandre de Vauroy, an officer of the garrison at Dunkirk. Even so, the letters do not explain why the man was arrested. He was taken quickly and quietly, and then escorted all the way across France to the Alps. The journey took about a month. The prisoner arrived at Pignerol in August 1669.

In his letter, Louvois gave the prisoner a name: Eustache Dauger. In his letter of July 1669, Louvois gave the prisoner a name. He called him Eustache Dauger. This is the only name that appears in the official letters. Historians have spent a great deal of time trying to discover who Eustache Dauger was. Some believe the name was false, chosen to hide his real identity. The handwriting of the letter suggests the name may have been added by a different hand. Others believe Dauger was a real person, and have searched for families with that name. Even so, no one has been able to prove who he was with certainty. Louvois described him as only a valet, a servant. This suggests he was not a nobleman or a person of high rank. Yet the care taken to hide him has made many people doubt this description. The question of how a simple valet could be so dangerous to the king has fascinated historians ever since.

The prisoner was taken across France under escort, and no one was allowed to speak with him. After his arrest, Eustache Dauger was taken south across France to Pignerol. The journey was long, more than a thousand kilometres, through towns, fields and forests and then into the Alps. Historians know from the letters that strict orders were given. No one was to know where the prisoner was being taken, and no one was allowed to speak with him. The escort was led by Captain de Vauroy, who had made the arrest. He was told to deliver the prisoner directly to Saint-Mars. Even so, little is known about the journey itself. The prisoner probably travelled by carriage or on horseback, with soldiers riding beside him. They stopped at inns and military posts along the way. In August 1669, the group reached Pignerol. Saint-Mars took charge of the prisoner. From that moment, he was cut off from the outside world, and he would remain so for the rest of his life.

Louvois ordered a cell with several doors, so that no one could hear the prisoner speak. Louvois gave Saint-Mars precise instructions about the prisoner's cell. It had to be secure, with several doors, one closing behind another, so that the guards outside could not hear anything the prisoner might say. Historians know these orders from the letter of July 1669. Louvois also ordered that Saint-Mars himself should bring the prisoner his food once a day. He was to ask the prisoner only what he needed. Even so, the instructions were even stricter than this. Louvois wrote that if the prisoner ever spoke about anything other than his daily needs, Saint-Mars was to threaten him with death. These orders show how important it was to keep the prisoner silent. Historians believe that he must have known something that the king and his ministers wanted to keep secret. What that secret was has never been discovered. The prisoner's silence was guarded for more than thirty years.

The prisoner was given good food, decent clothing and a few comforts, despite the strict secrecy. Although the prisoner was kept in strict secrecy, he does not seem to have been treated cruelly. The letters show that he was given good food, decent clothing and furniture. Historians note that prisoners in royal fortresses were often fed according to their social rank. The king paid a sum each day for each prisoner's keep. For the masked prisoner, the amount was generous. He was given meals with meat, bread and wine. He was allowed books, especially religious books, and it is said that he played the guitar. Even so, he lived a lonely life. He could not speak freely with anyone, and he had no contact with his family or friends. Later accounts say he was treated with a certain respect by his jailers, who spoke politely to him. This careful treatment has made some people believe that he was a person of importance, perhaps even of noble or royal birth.

Some historians link the arrest to secret talks between France and England in 1669. Some historians have tried to understand the prisoner by looking at what was happening in 1669. In that year, Louis the Fourteenth was holding secret talks with King Charles the Second of England. These talks led, in 1670, to the Secret Treaty of Dover. In this treaty, Charles agreed to support France against the Dutch, and he promised to announce himself a Catholic when the moment was right, in return for French money. Historians note that this promise was a dangerous secret, since most English people were Protestant. Some have suggested that Eustache Dauger may have been a servant who carried letters or knew something about these negotiations. Dunkirk was close to England, and messages passed through it often. Even so, this theory cannot be proven. It is one of many ideas put forward over the centuries. Yet the timing of the arrest, during these secret talks, remains interesting to historians.

Bénigne Dauvergne de Saint-Mars guarded the masked prisoner for more than thirty years. Bénigne Dauvergne de Saint-Mars was born in 1626 in Burgundy, in eastern France. He came from a modest noble family and joined the army as a young man. He served in the musketeers under d'Artagnan and rose through the ranks. Historians describe him as loyal, careful and strict. He followed his orders exactly and was trusted by the king and his ministers. In 1664, he was appointed governor of the prison at Pignerol. Even so, guarding prisoners was not glamorous work. Saint-Mars spent most of his life in isolated fortresses, far from Paris and the court. In return, he was well paid, and he became wealthy. He bought land and an estate in Burgundy. For more than thirty years, he guarded the masked prisoner, moving with him from one fortress to another. He became governor of the Bastille in 1698 and died there in 1708.

[long-pause]

{img:22-officer-checking-keys-ring-stone-corridor}

Saint-Mars checked every detail of his prisoners' lives himself. Saint-Mars was known for his attention to detail. He checked the locks, the doors and the windows of the cells himself. He read his prisoners' letters, when they were allowed to write any, and he watched who came and went from the fortress. Historians know from his letters to the ministers that he reported regularly on his prisoners' health, behaviour and needs. He asked permission for even small changes, such as giving a prisoner new clothes or a book. Even so, his care was also a way of protecting himself. If a prisoner escaped or spoke to the wrong person, Saint-Mars would be blamed. So he was always cautious. Some historians think that he may have exaggerated the importance of his prisoners, to make his own role seem more important. Others believe he was simply doing his duty well. Either way, his letters give a detailed picture of life in a seventeenth century state prison.

Saint-Mars lived with his family inside the fortresses where he guarded his prisoners. Like many fortress governors, Saint-Mars lived with his family inside the walls of the fortress he commanded. He married Marie-Antoinette Collot, the sister-in-law of the Marquis de Louvois's chief clerk, which helped his career. They had two sons, who later joined the army. Historians note that a governor's house was usually more comfortable than the rest of the fortress, with proper rooms, a kitchen and servants. Even so, life could be lonely and dull. Pignerol, Exilles and the island of Sainte-Marguerite were far from big cities. Visitors were few, and there was little entertainment. The family's life revolved around the routine of the fortress and the needs of the prisoners. Saint-Mars' sons grew up among soldiers, guards and prisoners. Sadly, both sons were later killed in battle, one in 1692 and the other in 1693. Saint-Mars and his wife were deeply saddened by their loss.

A small company of soldiers helped Saint-Mars guard his prisoners day and night. Saint-Mars commanded a small company of soldiers, who guarded the fortress and its prisoners. They stood watch on the walls, at the gates and outside the prisoners' rooms. Historians know from the letters that Saint-Mars chose his soldiers and servants carefully. Only a few trusted men were allowed near the most secret prisoners. Saint-Mars' nephew, Guillaume de Formanoir, and an officer named Jacques de Rosarges served with him for many years. Rosarges followed Saint-Mars to the Bastille and was present when the masked prisoner died. Even so, the soldiers' life was often boring. They spent long hours on guard duty, with little to do. In their free time, they played cards and dice, drank in the canteen and wrote letters home. Some came from nearby villages, while others came from far across France. Most of them never knew the name of the prisoner they guarded.

Saint-Mars often asked to be moved to a better post, but he was always sent with his prisoner. Over the years, Saint-Mars wrote many letters asking for promotion or a better position. He hoped for a post closer to Paris or to his estates in Burgundy. Historians note that the ministers often agreed, but each time Saint-Mars moved, he was ordered to take his most important prisoners with him. In 1681, he became governor of Exilles, a fortress in the Alps. In 1687, he became governor of the islands of Sainte-Marguerite and Saint-Honorat, near Cannes. In 1698, he was finally appointed governor of the Bastille. Even so, his life always remained tied to the masked prisoner. Some historians believe that the prisoner was kept with Saint-Mars because no one else could be trusted with the secret. Others think that the ministers simply found it convenient. Either way, the jailer and the prisoner spent more than thirty years together, closer than many families, yet never truly free to speak.

With his earnings as a jailer, Saint-Mars bought a country estate in Burgundy. Saint-Mars earned a good income from his work as a governor and jailer. He was paid for each prisoner he guarded, as well as receiving his salary. Historians note that by the end of his career, he had become a wealthy man. He bought land and a country house at Palteau, near the town of Villeneuve-sur-Yonne in Burgundy. The house still stands today. Even so, Saint-Mars rarely had time to enjoy it. His duties kept him in his fortresses most of the time. The estate at Palteau later became important in the story of the masked prisoner. In 1698, on the way from Sainte-Marguerite to Paris, Saint-Mars stopped there with his prisoner for a few days. A later owner of the estate, a relative of Saint-Mars, wrote down what the local peasants had seen. Their account became one of the few descriptions of the masked prisoner from people who had seen him.

Pignerol was a fortress town at the foot of the Alps, on the border between France and Italy. Pignerol, called Pinerolo in Italian, is a town in the region of Piedmont, at the foot of the Alps. In the seventeenth century, it belonged to France. It had been captured by French troops in 1630 and was officially given to France in 1631. In 1696, France gave Pignerol back to the Duke of Savoy as part of a peace treaty. Its fortress was then demolished.

It stood inside the citadel, surrounded by its own walls and guarded by soldiers. Historians know from letters and plans that it had several floors, with rooms for prisoners of different ranks. The most important prisoners had larger rooms with furniture, fireplaces and windows, while others had smaller cells. Saint-Mars and his family lived in a house nearby. Even so, life in the donjon was hard. In winter, snow covered the mountains, and the cold was severe. In summer, the rooms could be hot and stuffy. The windows were barred and partly covered, so that prisoners could see only the sky. Saint-Mars added extra doors and walls to make the cells more secure. Over the years, the donjon held several famous prisoners. The masked prisoner lived there for about twelve years, from 1669 to 1681.

Around the fortress of Pignerol lay green valleys, chestnut forests and small mountain villages. The land around Pignerol was beautiful. Green valleys stretched up into the Alps, with rivers, forests of chestnut trees and small villages. Farmers grew grapes, wheat and vegetables, and herded sheep and cows on the mountain pastures. Historians note that many people in the nearby valleys were Waldensians, members of an old Protestant community that had lived in the Alps for centuries. They spoke a local language and kept their own traditions. Even so, life in the region was not always peaceful. Armies often passed through, and the Waldensians suffered religious persecution at times. The people of Pignerol lived alongside the French soldiers of the fortress. They sold them food and wine and provided services. Some of them must have wondered about the mysterious prisoners in the tower. Yet they knew little about them. The prisoners themselves could see almost nothing of the green valleys around them.

The prisoner spent his days reading, praying and looking at the sky through a barred window. Little is known about the daily life of Eustache Dauger at Pignerol. However, the priest was chosen carefully, and the prisoner was warned not to speak about his past or his identity. Louvois ordered that the confessor should be someone who would not ask questions. If the prisoner did speak, his words remained secret. The prisoner's faith may have been a comfort during his long years of imprisonment. Later accounts describe him as a quiet and pious man, who spent much time reading religious books and praying.

[long-pause]

{img:33-winter-alps-fortress-snow-smoke-chimneys}

Winters at Pignerol were long and cold, and the prisoners spent them close to the fire. Pignerol lies at the foot of the Alps, and its winters were cold and snowy. From December to March, the mountains were covered with snow, and icy winds blew down the valleys. Historians note that the fortress needed large amounts of firewood to keep its rooms warm. Soldiers and servants carried wood to the prisoners' fireplaces every day. The prisoners spent much of the winter close to the fire, wrapped in warm clothes and blankets. Even so, some rooms were draughty, and the cold could be hard to bear. Letters mention requests for warmer clothes and extra blankets for the prisoners. In spring, the snow melted, and the rivers filled with water from the mountains. Flowers appeared in the valleys, and the days grew longer. For the prisoners, the changing seasons were one of the few ways to mark the passing of time. Each winter was much like the last one.

[long-pause]

{img:34-vaux-le-vicomte-chateau-gardens-fete-1661}

In 1661, the king's finance minister, Nicolas Fouquet, held a magnificent party at his château. Nicolas Fouquet was superintendent of finances, one of the most powerful positions in France. He was rich, clever and a great patron of the arts. He built a magnificent château called Vaux-le-Vicomte, south of Paris, with gardens by André Le Nôtre and paintings by Charles Le Brun. On the seventeenth of August 1661, Fouquet held a lavish party there for the young King Louis the Fourteenth. Historians describe it as one of the most splendid celebrations of the century. There was a feast, a play by Molière, music and fireworks. Even so, the party was a mistake. The king was angered by the display of wealth, which seemed greater than his own. He also suspected that Fouquet had grown rich by misusing public money. Advised by Colbert, Louis decided to remove him. The magnificent party was the beginning of Fouquet's downfall. Today, Vaux-le-Vicomte is open to visitors.

[long-pause]

{img:35-arrest-fouquet-nantes-musketeers-carriage}

Three weeks after the party, Fouquet was arrested by d'Artagnan and his musketeers. On the fifth of September 1661, during a royal visit to the city of Nantes, Fouquet was arrested. The arrest was carried out by d'Artagnan, captain of the king's musketeers. Historians know that the king planned it carefully and in secret, fearing that Fouquet might resist or escape. Fouquet was taken to prison, and his papers were seized. His trial lasted three years. He was accused of misusing public money and of plotting against the king. Even so, many people sympathised with him. Writers such as Madame de Sévigné and Jean de La Fontaine followed the trial closely and spoke up for him. In December 1664, the judges sentenced him to banishment from France. The king was not satisfied. He changed the sentence to life imprisonment. This was very unusual, since kings normally reduced sentences rather than making them harsher. Fouquet was sent to Pignerol, under the guard of Saint-Mars.

[long-pause]

{img:36-fallen-minister-writing-prison-room-books}

Fouquet spent fifteen years in the fortress of Pignerol, reading, writing and praying. Fouquet arrived at Pignerol in January 1665. He was given rooms in the donjon, where he was guarded by Saint-Mars. Historians note that his life in prison was strict at first. He was not allowed to write letters, receive visitors or have news of the outside world. Over the years, the rules became gentler. He was allowed books, writing materials and, later, letters to and from his family. He wrote religious works and translations. He also suffered from poor health, and he grew old in prison. Even so, his spirit did not break. He remained intelligent and dignified, and he spent much time in prayer. In 1679, his wife and children were finally allowed to visit him. Fouquet died at Pignerol in March 1680, after fifteen years in prison. Some people later suggested that Fouquet himself was the masked prisoner, but the letters show clearly that he died before the masked prisoner did.

[long-pause]

{img:37-two-valets-serving-master-prison-room}

In 1675, the masked prisoner was allowed to work as a valet for Fouquet. Fouquet was allowed to have servants in prison to help him dress, clean and prepare his meals. One of his valets was a man named La Rivière. In 1675, Fouquet's other servant fell ill, and Louvois gave permission for Eustache Dauger to serve him as a second valet. Historians find this decision very strange. It seemed odd that a man with a dangerous secret should spend time with another prisoner. Louvois gave strict conditions. Dauger was never to be in the presence of anyone except Fouquet and La Rivière. Even so, the arrangement shows that Dauger was not kept in total isolation. For several years, he lived and worked alongside Fouquet. Some historians believe that Dauger was chosen because he was already a servant by profession. Others think it was simply a convenient way to keep him busy. Either way, the two prisoners came to know each other well.

[long-pause]

{img:38-two-prisoners-talking-quietly-fireside}

Louvois forbade Fouquet to tell anyone what the valet had done before his arrest. In 1679, the king allowed Fouquet more freedom. He could walk in the fortress and meet another prisoner, the Count of Lauzun. However, Louvois wrote to Saint-Mars that Eustache Dauger must never be in the same room as Lauzun. He also asked Fouquet directly whether Dauger had told him about his past life. Historians consider this letter one of the most interesting clues. Louvois wanted to know if Dauger had talked about what he had done before coming to Pignerol. This suggests that the secret concerned something Dauger had seen or done before 1669. Even so, the letter does not say what it was. Fouquet's answer has not survived. Historians have wondered what the two men talked about during their long years together. Fouquet had known many secrets of the state. Dauger seems to have known at least one. Their conversations by the fireside will never be known.

[long-pause]

{img:39-hole-in-wall-chimney-prison-room-candle}

After Fouquet died, Saint-Mars found a secret hole in the wall between two prisoners' rooms. When Fouquet died in March 1680, Saint-Mars searched his rooms. He discovered a hole in the wall, near the chimney, which connected Fouquet's rooms to those of Lauzun. Historians know about this discovery from Saint-Mars' letters. The two prisoners had been secretly visiting each other for some time. Saint-Mars was worried. Lauzun might have met Dauger through the hole and learned his secret. Even so, Saint-Mars and Louvois took immediate action. They moved Dauger and La Rivière into a separate room in a lower tower and told Lauzun that both valets had been set free. Historians note that this was a lie, designed to make Lauzun believe that they were no longer important. In fact, the two valets were now held in even greater secrecy than before. From this moment, they were called the two gentlemen of the lower tower in Saint-Mars' letters.

[long-pause]

{img:40-pignerol-funeral-procession-church-1680}

Fouquet's body was later taken to Paris and buried in his family's chapel. After Fouquet's death in March 1680, his body was first placed in a church in Pignerol. About a year later, in 1681, it was taken to Paris and buried in the family chapel of the Convent of the Visitation of Sainte-Marie. His family had asked for this, and the king gave permission. Historians note that Fouquet's death was a great sadness for his family and friends, who had hoped he might one day be released. Even so, his memory lived on. His château at Vaux-le-Vicomte remained one of the most beautiful in France. Writers remembered him as a generous patron of the arts. Some later writers suggested that he had not really died, and that he was the masked prisoner. Historians have rejected this idea, since the letters clearly describe both men at the same time. Yet Fouquet's story is closely connected to the mystery. His valet became one of the most famous prisoners in history.

[long-pause]

{img:41-court-nobleman-elegant-hall-versailles}

The Count of Lauzun was a charming courtier who fell from the king's favour and was sent to Pignerol. Antoine Nompar de Caumont, Count of Lauzun, was a nobleman from Gascony, in southwestern France. He was small, witty and ambitious, and he became a favourite of Louis the Fourteenth. Historians note that he caused a great scandal in 1670, when he planned to marry the king's cousin, Anne Marie Louise of Orléans, known as La Grande Mademoiselle. She was one of the richest women in Europe. The king first gave permission and then changed his mind. Even so, Lauzun continued to annoy the king and his ministers. In November 1671, he was arrested and sent to Pignerol, where he was guarded by Saint-Mars. He stayed there for ten years. He was a difficult prisoner, often angry and demanding. He was finally released in 1681, partly because La Grande Mademoiselle gave some of her lands to the king's son. He lived until 1723 and had many more adventures.

[long-pause]

{img:42-prisoner-digging-tunnel-candle-stones}

Lauzun once tried to escape from Pignerol by digging a tunnel through the walls. Lauzun was not a patient prisoner. He wanted to escape, and he spent months making plans. Historians know from Saint-Mars' letters that he secretly dug a passage through the walls and floor of his room, using whatever tools he could find. He hid the rubble and covered the hole. He also made a rope from strips of cloth. In 1676, he made his attempt. He climbed out through the passage and reached the fortress walls. Even so, he was caught before he could get away. A guard saw him, and he was taken back to his room. Saint-Mars was embarrassed and alarmed, and he made the fortress even more secure. Lauzun's attempt made Saint-Mars more careful with all his prisoners. Later, Lauzun found a way to visit Fouquet secretly, through the hole near the chimney. His restless energy was a constant worry for his jailer, who wished for a quieter prisoner.

[long-pause]

{img:43-italian-diplomat-portrait-turin-1678}

In 1679, an Italian diplomat named Ercole Antonio Mattioli was secretly kidnapped and taken to Pignerol. Ercole Antonio Mattioli was a minister of the Duke of Mantua, a small state in northern Italy. In 1678, he helped arrange a secret agreement to sell the fortress of Casale to France. Casale was an important fortress, and the sale would give Louis the Fourteenth great power in northern Italy. Historians note that Mattioli then betrayed the agreement. He revealed it to other courts, including Spain and Savoy, in return for money. Louis the Fourteenth was furious. In May 1679, Mattioli was lured to a meeting near Turin and kidnapped by French agents. He was taken secretly to Pignerol and placed under the guard of Saint-Mars. Even so, his disappearance was not completely secret. Some people at the courts of Italy suspected what had happened. Mattioli was held under a false name, and he was said to have become ill and confused in prison. He later became one of the main suspects in the mystery.

[long-pause]

{img:44-casale-fortress-river-po-italy}

The fortress of Casale was at the centre of the diplomatic scandal that cost Mattioli his freedom. Casale Monferrato is a town on the river Po, in the region of Piedmont in northern Italy. In the seventeenth century, its fortress was one of the strongest in Italy. It belonged to the Duke of Mantua, who needed money. Historians note that Louis the Fourteenth wanted Casale because it would allow France to control the region and threaten Spanish lands nearby. After Mattioli's betrayal, the deal was delayed. Even so, France took control of Casale in 1681, after new negotiations. French troops occupied the fortress, and it remained in French hands until 1695. Then it was returned and its fortifications were destroyed. The story of Casale shows how dangerous diplomacy could be in that age. Secret agreements, betrayals and kidnappings were part of the competition between European states. For Mattioli, his involvement in the affair led to a long imprisonment, far from his home and family.

[long-pause]

{img:45-moody-prisoner-scratching-wall-cell}

Mattioli was a troubled prisoner, who wrote on his walls and argued with his guards. According to Saint-Mars' letters, Mattioli did not accept his imprisonment calmly. He often argued with his guards and complained loudly about his treatment. Historians know that he sometimes wrote on the walls of his cell, and that he was said to have become confused and unwell. At one point, he was moved to share a room with a monk who was also mentally ill. Saint-Mars described him as difficult. Even so, Mattioli's fate was not forgotten by everyone. His family in Italy searched for news of him, and rumours of his kidnapping spread. He remained at Pignerol until 1694, when he was moved to the island of Sainte-Marguerite. He died there in April 1694, shortly after his arrival. Historians note that his death is recorded in the letters, which is important for the mystery. Since he died nine years before the masked prisoner, he could not have been the man buried in Paris in 1703.

[long-pause]

{img:46-two-lists-names-ledger-candle-quill}

Because the masked prisoner was buried under a name like Mattioli, many people believed they were the same man. When the masked prisoner died in 1703, he was buried under the name Marchioly. This name looks similar to Mattioli. Historians note that for many years, people believed the masked prisoner must have been Mattioli. The idea was supported by some writers in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. It seemed to explain the mask, since Mattioli was a foreign diplomat whose kidnapping had broken international law. Even so, most historians today reject this theory. Mattioli was a prisoner of the king for a known reason, and his kidnapping was not a deep secret. More importantly, the letters suggest that Mattioli died in 1694, while the masked prisoner lived until 1703. The use of a similar name may have been a coincidence, or a deliberate way of hiding the prisoner's identity. Some think the burial clerk simply wrote a name that was going around the prison. The puzzle of the name remains unsolved.

[long-pause]

{img:47-exilles-fort-mountain-valley-1681}

In 1681, Saint-Mars was made governor of Exilles, a small fort high in the Alps. In 1681, Saint-Mars was given a new post. He became governor of the fort of Exilles, in the Susa Valley in the Alps, west of Turin. Exilles was a small fortress on a rocky hill, guarding the road between France and Italy. Historians note that Saint-Mars took two prisoners with him: Eustache Dauger and La Rivière, the two valets of the lower tower. The other prisoners, including Mattioli, stayed behind at Pignerol. This choice suggests that the two valets were considered especially important. Even so, Exilles was a lonely place. It was far from any large town, and the winters were long and harsh. The fort was surrounded by high mountains, and snow could block the roads for months. Saint-Mars complained about the cold and the isolation. The two prisoners were kept in a tower, with their own guards. Today, the fort of Exilles has been rebuilt and is open to visitors.

[long-pause]

{img:48-alpine-valley-road-mules-snow}

Supplies for the fortress of Exilles had to be carried up narrow mountain roads by mules. Exilles was difficult to reach. Supplies of food, firewood, wine and other goods had to be carried up from the valley along narrow mountain roads. Historians note that mules were the main form of transport in the Alps, since they were sure-footed on steep and rocky paths. Mule drivers led long trains of animals, each carrying heavy loads. In winter, the roads were often blocked by snow, and the fort had to be stocked with supplies in advance. Even so, life in the valley continued around the fort. Villages along the river grew grapes, kept cows and goats and made cheese. Traders and soldiers passed through on their way between France and Italy. The fortress depended on these local people for its food and supplies. Few of them would have known anything about the two prisoners in the tower. For them, Exilles was simply the king's fort on the hill.

[long-pause]

{img:49-two-prisoners-tower-room-mountain-light}

At Exilles, the two valets lived together in one tower room, guarded day and night. At Exilles, Dauger and La Rivière shared a room in a tower of the fort. Saint-Mars arranged it so that no one could hear them or see them. Historians know from his letters that the guards who watched them were not allowed to speak to them. Saint-Mars brought them their meals himself, as he had done at Pignerol. Even so, the two prisoners had each other for company. They had lived and worked together for years as Fouquet's valets. They may have talked, prayed and kept each other's spirits up. La Rivière was not in good health. He suffered from an illness that caused swelling, which the letters called dropsy. In January 1687, La Rivière died at Exilles. Dauger was now alone again. Historians note that his companion's death must have been a great loss. After almost twenty years of imprisonment, Dauger was left with only his jailer and his guards for company.

[long-pause]

{img:50-fort-exilles-winter-storm-snowdrifts}

Saint-Mars complained in his letters that the winters at Exilles were bitter and long. The winters at Exilles were especially hard. The fort stood more than eight hundred metres above sea level, and the mountains around it rose much higher. Heavy snow fell for months, and freezing winds blew through the valley. Historians know from Saint-Mars' letters that he found the climate difficult. He complained about the cold and about his own health. He asked to be moved to a warmer place. Even so, the prisoners suffered more than he did. Their tower room was cold, and they depended on firewood brought by the guards. La Rivière's illness may have been made worse by the harsh conditions. Saint-Mars wrote to Louvois about the prisoners' health and the difficulties of the fort. In 1687, after six years at Exilles, his request was granted. He was appointed governor of the islands of Sainte-Marguerite and Saint-Honorat, off the warm Mediterranean coast near Cannes. His prisoner would come with him.

[long-pause]

{img:51-sedan-chair-covered-oilcloth-mountain-road}

In 1687, the prisoner was carried to the coast in a closed chair covered with oiled cloth. In the spring of 1687, Saint-Mars moved from Exilles to the island of Sainte-Marguerite. He took Dauger with him. Historians know from his letters that the prisoner was carried in a sedan chair, a closed seat carried on poles by porters. The chair was covered with oiled cloth, so that no one could see inside. Saint-Mars wrote that this would keep the prisoner hidden. Even so, the arrangement nearly killed him. The chair was so tightly covered that the prisoner could hardly breathe. The journey took twelve days, through the mountains and down to the coast of Provence. When they arrived, the prisoner was very ill. Saint-Mars reported that he had suffered greatly. Historians note that this is one of the few times that the letters describe the prisoner's suffering. After his arrival, he slowly recovered. Some writers later said that he wore a mask on this journey, but the letters mention only the covered chair.

[long-pause]

{img:52-provence-coast-road-olive-trees-sea}

The journey to the coast took the prisoner down from the cold Alps to the warm shores of Provence. The route from Exilles to Sainte-Marguerite led through the mountains and down into Provence, in the south of France. Historians note that the travellers passed from snowy peaks and pine forests into warmer valleys with olive groves, vineyards and fields of lavender. They reached the coast near Cannes, which was then a small fishing village. Even so, the prisoner saw none of this beauty, hidden as he was inside the covered chair. The journey was long and exhausting for everyone. The porters had to carry the heavy chair over steep roads, and the soldiers rode alongside. They stopped at night in inns or military posts, where the prisoner was kept apart from other travellers. In late April 1687, the group reached the coast. From there, a boat carried them across the water to the island of Sainte-Marguerite. The prisoner's new home would be very different from the cold mountain fortresses.

[long-pause]

{img:53-sainte-marguerite-island-fort-blue-sea}

Sainte-Marguerite is a small island covered with pine trees, just off the coast near Cannes. The Lérins Islands lie in the Mediterranean Sea, about a kilometre from the coast near Cannes. The largest is Sainte-Marguerite, a long island covered with pine and eucalyptus trees today, with rocky shores and clear water. In the seventeenth century, it had a fortress called the Fort Royal, built to protect the coast from enemy fleets. The smaller island nearby, Saint-Honorat, was home to a monastery that had been founded in the fifth century. Historians note that Saint-Mars became governor of both islands in 1687. Even so, the island was not a paradise for prisoners. They were kept inside the fortress and saw little of its beauty. Yet the climate was much warmer than in the Alps. The sea breezes were pleasant, and the winters were mild. Today, the Fort Royal is home to a museum, and visitors can walk through its old prison cells.

[long-pause]

{img:54-prison-cell-thick-walls-sea-window-bars}

Saint-Mars built a new prison on the island, with thick walls and windows facing the sea. When Saint-Mars arrived on Sainte-Marguerite, he found the existing prison unsuitable. He ordered the construction of a new prison building inside the fortress. Historians know from his letters that he designed it carefully. Each cell had very thick walls, and its window looked out over the sea, protected by three sets of iron bars. The cells were placed so that prisoners could not communicate with each other or with anyone outside. Even so, the cells were bright and airy, with views of the water and the sky. The masked prisoner was placed in one of these cells. Today, visitors to the Fort Royal can see the cell that tradition says was his. It is a large vaulted room with a barred window looking out towards the open sea. Many people visit it every year. Standing there, they can imagine the prisoner looking out at the endless blue water, year after year.

[long-pause]

{img:55-prisoner-mask-walking-fort-terrace-sea}

On the island, the prisoner was sometimes allowed to walk, but his face had to be hidden. Some accounts say that on Sainte-Marguerite, the prisoner was allowed to walk in the fortress, always in the company of Saint-Mars. On these occasions, he wore his mask, so that no one could see his face. Historians note that these stories were written down long afterwards, and they may have been exaggerated. Even so, they suggest that the people of the island and the soldiers of the garrison were aware of a mysterious prisoner. Rumours began to spread along the coast of Provence. People said that a prisoner of great importance was held on the island, and that Saint-Mars treated him with unusual respect. Some said that the governor stood while the prisoner was seated, or that he spoke to him with his hat in his hand. These stories cannot be confirmed. Yet they show how the mystery grew. The masked prisoner was becoming a legend even during his own lifetime.

[long-pause]

{img:56-fisherman-silver-plate-rocks-shore}

According to legend, the prisoner once scratched a message on a silver plate and threw it from his window. One of the most famous stories about the masked prisoner was told by Voltaire. According to him, the prisoner once wrote a message on a silver plate with a knife and threw it out of his window. A fisherman found the plate on the shore and brought it to Saint-Mars. The governor asked him if he had read what was written on it. The fisherman said that he could not read, and Saint-Mars let him go, saying he was lucky. Historians do not believe that this story is true. There is no record of it in the letters, and similar stories were told about other prisoners. Even so, it became one of the best known parts of the legend. The silver plate suggested that the prisoner was used to fine things, perhaps a person of high birth. Voltaire also said that the prisoner wore fine linen and lace. These details made the mystery even more exciting for readers.

[long-pause]

{img:57-protestant-pastors-prison-psalm-cell}

Later, several Protestant pastors were imprisoned in the cells next to the masked prisoner. In 1685, Louis the Fourteenth cancelled the Edict of Nantes. This edict, signed by his grandfather Henry the Fourth in 1598, had given French Protestants, called Huguenots, the right to practise their religion. After 1685, Protestant worship was forbidden in France. Historians note that many Protestants fled abroad, while others worshipped secretly. Protestant pastors who continued to preach were arrested. Several of them were sent to Sainte-Marguerite and held in cells in the same prison as the masked prisoner. Even so, they were not allowed to meet him or speak with him. Some of the pastors were held for many years, and some went mad or died in prison. Their names are remembered today in the museum of the Fort Royal. Historians note that their faith and suffering are part of the history of the island. They sang psalms in their cells, and some of their songs may have been heard by the masked prisoner.

[long-pause]

{img:58-lerins-monastery-saint-honorat-monks-sea}

Across a narrow channel, the monks of Saint-Honorat continued their quiet life of prayer. Just across a narrow channel from Sainte-Marguerite lies the island of Saint-Honorat. A monastery was founded there around the year 410 by Saint Honoratus, and monks have lived on the island almost continuously since then. Historians note that the monastery of Lérins was one of the most important religious centres of early Christian Europe. In the Middle Ages, the monks built a fortified tower to protect themselves from pirates. In the seventeenth century, the monks lived a quiet life of prayer and work, tending vineyards and gardens. Even so, they lived very close to the fortress and its prisoners. Some historians have wondered whether the monks knew anything about the masked prisoner. There is no evidence that they did. Today, monks still live on Saint-Honorat. They make wine and liqueurs and welcome visitors. The bells of their monastery can be heard across the water, just as they could be heard by the prisoners long ago.

[long-pause]

{img:59-sunset-sea-from-fort-sainte-marguerite}

The masked prisoner lived on Sainte-Marguerite for eleven years, from 1687 to 1698. The masked prisoner spent eleven years on the island of Sainte-Marguerite. Historians know little about his daily life there, but the letters show that he was well cared for. He continued to receive good food, clothing and books. He was visited regularly by Saint-Mars and sometimes by a doctor or a priest. Even so, his life remained one of total isolation. He could not speak freely, could not write letters and could not leave his cell without permission. Year after year, he looked out at the same sea and sky from behind his iron bars. The seasons changed, ships sailed past, and the sun rose and set over the water. In 1698, everything changed again. Saint-Mars was appointed governor of the Bastille in Paris. As always, the minister ordered that the old prisoner should go with him. After almost thirty years in the south, the prisoner was going to Paris.

[long-pause]

{img:60-closed-carriage-road-provence-1698}

In the late summer of 1698, Saint-Mars and his prisoner set out on the long journey to Paris. In 1698, the minister Barbezieux, the son of Louvois, wrote to Saint-Mars about his new appointment as governor of the Bastille. He told him to bring his old prisoner with him, taking every precaution so that no one would see him or know who he was. Historians know about the journey from these letters and from later accounts. Saint-Mars and the prisoner left Sainte-Marguerite in late summer, crossing to the mainland by boat. They travelled north through Provence and Burgundy towards Paris, a journey of about nine hundred kilometres. Even so, the details of the journey are uncertain. The prisoner probably travelled in a closed carriage or litter, with Saint-Mars and a small escort of soldiers. They stopped at inns and houses along the way. The journey took several weeks. It was the prisoner's longest journey since his arrest almost thirty years earlier, and his last.

[long-pause]

{img:61-french-village-inn-courtyard-horses-1698}

At the inns along the road, the prisoner was kept apart from other travellers. Travel in France in the late seventeenth century was slow and often uncomfortable. Roads were rough, and carriages bumped over stones and holes. Travellers usually covered between thirty and fifty kilometres a day. Historians note that they spent their nights at inns, where they could eat, sleep and change horses. At each stop, Saint-Mars made sure that his prisoner was kept away from the other guests. He was given a separate room, and his meals were brought to him. Even so, the arrival of a governor with a masked prisoner must have attracted attention. Innkeepers, servants and other travellers would have noticed the soldiers and the closed carriage. Stories about the mysterious prisoner probably spread along the road. By the time they reached Paris, rumours had already begun to circulate. People wondered who could be so important that his face had to be hidden from everyone he met.

[long-pause]

{img:62-chateau-palteau-courtyard-carriage-arrives}

On the way to Paris, Saint-Mars stopped for a few days at his own château of Palteau. On the journey to Paris, Saint-Mars and his prisoner stopped at Palteau, Saint-Mars' estate in Burgundy. Historians know about this stop from a letter written many years later, in 1768, by a relative of Saint-Mars who had inherited the estate. He had heard the story from old peasants who had seen the prisoner. According to them, the prisoner arrived in a litter, followed by Saint-Mars on horseback. He wore a black mask, and the peasants noticed that his teeth and lips could be seen. Even so, they could not see his face. They said that he was tall and had white hair. The peasants remembered the visit for the rest of their lives. Historians note that this is one of the few descriptions of the prisoner by people who saw him. It must be treated with caution, since it was told many years later. Yet it gives a vivid picture of the mysterious traveller.

[long-pause]

{img:63-dining-room-pistols-on-table-candles}

At Palteau, Saint-Mars ate with his prisoner, with two loaded pistols on the table. The peasants of Palteau told another story about the prisoner's visit. According to them, Saint-Mars ate his meals with the prisoner in the dining room of the château. The prisoner sat with his back to the window, so that no one outside could see his face. Saint-Mars sat opposite him, with two loaded pistols on the table beside his plate. Historians note that the peasants watched through the windows and could see these details. Even so, the story may have been improved over time. Pistols on the table would have been an unusual sight, and the story became one of the most dramatic parts of the legend. The peasants also said that the prisoner slept in a bed next to Saint-Mars' room. The governor watched over him day and night. After a few days, the travellers continued their journey. The prisoner left Palteau as mysteriously as he had arrived, and the peasants never saw him again.

[long-pause]

{img:64-road-through-forest-fontainebleau-autumn}

The last part of the journey led through the great forests south of Paris. After leaving Burgundy, Saint-Mars and his prisoner travelled north towards Paris. The road passed through the great forests south of the capital, including the forest of Fontainebleau, where the kings of France had a famous palace and liked to hunt. Historians note that the forests were wide and quiet, with tall oak and beech trees. Travellers had to be careful of robbers, who sometimes attacked carriages on lonely roads. Even so, a governor travelling with soldiers would have been safe. In September, the leaves were beginning to turn gold. The air was cooler than in the south. For the prisoner, who had spent most of his life in mountain fortresses and on an island, the countryside of northern France must have been a strange sight, if he could see it at all. As they came closer to Paris, the villages grew larger, and the roads became busier with carts, coaches and travellers.

[long-pause]

{img:65-entering-paris-gate-saint-antoine-crowd}

On the eighteenth of September 1698, the carriage entered Paris and drove into the Bastille. On the eighteenth of September 1698, Saint-Mars and his prisoner arrived in Paris. They entered the city through the gate of Saint-Antoine, next to the Bastille. Historians know the exact date from du Junca's journal. He wrote that the new governor arrived at three o'clock in the afternoon, bringing with him an old prisoner he had guarded at Pignerol. The prisoner was taken first to a room in the Bassinière tower, and later moved to the third room of the Bertaudière tower. Even so, du Junca did not learn his name. He wrote only that the prisoner was always masked and that his name was not to be spoken. The streets around the Bastille were busy with people going about their daily lives. Few would have noticed one more carriage entering the fortress. Yet the arrival of the masked prisoner was the beginning of one of the most famous mysteries in the history of Paris.

[long-pause]

{img:66-bastille-tower-room-furniture-fireplace}

The rooms of the Bastille were often larger and more comfortable than people later imagined. In later years, the Bastille became a symbol of cruelty and injustice. Yet historians note that many of its rooms were fairly comfortable, especially for prisoners of high rank. The rooms in the towers were large and octagonal, with thick walls, a fireplace and a window. Prisoners could bring their own furniture, books and clothing, and some had servants. Even so, conditions varied greatly. The worst cells were the dungeons at the bottom of the towers, which were damp and dark, and the rooms at the top, which were very cold in winter and hot in summer. Most prisoners lived in the middle floors. The masked prisoner was placed in the third room of the Bertaudière tower. It was a decent room, with a fireplace and furniture. Historians believe that he was treated well, as he had been in his earlier prisons. His life in the Bastille was quiet and orderly.

[long-pause]

{img:67-bastille-kitchen-cooks-preparing-meals}

Prisoners in the Bastille were fed at the king's expense, and the food was often good. The king paid for the food of prisoners in the Bastille. The amount depended on their social rank. Historians note that ordinary prisoners received a modest sum each day, while nobles and important prisoners received much more. The governor was responsible for feeding them, and he could make a profit if he spent less than he received. Even so, many prisoners ate well. Meals often included soup, meat, bread, vegetables, fruit and wine. On religious fast days, fish and eggs were served instead of meat. Some prisoners were even invited to dine with the governor. The masked prisoner was given generous meals, as he had been in his earlier prisons. According to some accounts, he was fond of fine linen and good food. The kitchens of the Bastille were busy places, where cooks prepared meals for the prisoners, the governor's family, the officers and the soldiers of the garrison.

[long-pause]

{img:68-chapel-curtained-mass-fortress-candles}

Prisoners attended mass in the Bastille chapel, hidden from each other behind curtains. The Bastille had its own chapel, where mass was said for the prisoners and the garrison. Prisoners who were allowed to attend were placed in small booths or behind curtains, so that they could see the altar but could not see each other. Historians note that this arrangement allowed the governor to keep prisoners apart, even during religious services. The masked prisoner attended mass regularly. Du Junca wrote that he went to mass on the day of his death. Even so, he was always masked when he left his room. The chaplain of the Bastille also visited prisoners in their rooms. He heard their confessions and gave them communion. For many prisoners, religion was one of their few comforts. The masked prisoner seems to have been a religious man, who found consolation in prayer during his long years of imprisonment. The chapel was one of the few places where he could leave his room.

[long-pause]

{img:69-prisoners-walking-tower-top-paris-view}

Some prisoners were allowed to walk on the tops of the towers, from where they could see Paris. Prisoners in the Bastille were sometimes allowed to take exercise. Historians note that some walked in a small courtyard, while others were allowed onto the tops of the towers. From there, they could see the rooftops of Paris, the river Seine and the countryside beyond. For prisoners who had spent months or years indoors, it was a precious moment of freedom. Even so, they were always accompanied by guards, and they were not allowed to signal to anyone outside. Whether the masked prisoner was allowed to walk on the towers is not known. If he was, he would have worn his mask. From the top of the Bertaudière tower, he could have seen the busy streets below, the church towers and the gardens of the nearby faubourg Saint-Antoine. After so many years in remote fortresses, Paris must have seemed enormous. Yet he remained as isolated as ever, a stranger in the heart of a great city.

[long-pause]

{img:70-bastille-library-books-shelves-prisoner}

The Bastille had a library, and many prisoners spent their days reading. Reading was one of the main ways prisoners passed the time in the Bastille. Many brought their own books, and over the years, a library was built up from books left by former prisoners. Historians note that religious books, histories and works of literature were common. Some prisoners also wrote, producing letters, poems, memoirs and even books. Even so, everything they wrote was checked by the governor. Prisoners were not allowed to write about the prison or to send secret messages. The masked prisoner was probably given books, as he had been in his earlier prisons. Some accounts say that he enjoyed reading and that he played the guitar. Historians cannot confirm these details. Yet it is likely that books were his companions during the quiet days and long evenings in the tower. In a life without conversation, reading allowed him to travel in his mind far beyond the stone walls.

[long-pause]

{img:71-guards-night-watch-bastille-ramparts-lantern}

Every night, soldiers kept watch on the walls and in the courtyards of the Bastille. The Bastille was guarded by a small garrison of soldiers, called invalides, who were often older soldiers no longer fit for active service. Historians note that they kept watch day and night, on the walls, at the gates and in the courtyards. At night, the gates were locked, and the drawbridges were raised. The governor and his officers made regular rounds to check that everything was secure. Even so, life in the Bastille at night was usually quiet. The prisoners slept in their rooms, and the guards walked the walls under the stars. The bells of nearby churches marked the hours. Occasionally, prisoners called out or made noise, and the guards had to respond. The masked prisoner was a calm and quiet prisoner, who seems to have caused no trouble. In the silence of the night, the great fortress stood dark and still, its towers rising above the sleeping streets of the faubourg Saint-Antoine.

[long-pause]

{img:72-governor-office-registers-officers-1700}

As governor of the Bastille, Saint-Mars continued to guard his old prisoner personally. At the Bastille, Saint-Mars was responsible for all the prisoners, the soldiers and the buildings. He had a staff of officers, including du Junca and Rosarges, as well as a doctor, a surgeon, a chaplain and servants. Historians note that he was now in his seventies, and his health was not always good. Yet he continued to take a personal interest in the masked prisoner. He made sure that his orders were followed exactly and that the prisoner's identity remained secret. Even so, he had many other prisoners to manage. The Bastille held people accused of all sorts of crimes, from political plots to writing forbidden books. Saint-Mars dealt with each of them according to the king's orders. He lived in the governor's house inside the Bastille with his family. He remained governor until his death in September 1708, five years after the masked prisoner. He was buried in the church of Saint-Paul.

[long-pause]

{img:73-bastille-november-grey-sky-towers-rain}

On Monday, the nineteenth of November 1703, the masked prisoner died in the Bastille. On Sunday, the eighteenth of November 1703, the masked prisoner went to mass in the chapel of the Bastille as usual. Later that day, he felt ill. Historians know about his last days from du Junca's journal. The next day, Monday, the nineteenth of November, the prisoner died in his room in the Bertaudière tower, at about ten o'clock in the evening. Du Junca wrote that he died after a short illness, without having had the time to receive the last rites. He had confessed to the chaplain the day before. Even so, the cause of his death is unknown. He was probably in his sixties, and he had spent about thirty-four years in prison. Du Junca recorded the death in his journal with his usual brief style. He again described him as the unknown prisoner, who had always been masked with a mask of black velvet. Outside, Paris was cold and grey in the November rain.

[long-pause]

{img:74-priest-candle-deathbed-stone-room}

The chaplain of the Bastille was called to the prisoner's room on the night he died. When the masked prisoner fell ill, the chaplain of the Bastille was called. His name was Abbé Giraut. Historians know from du Junca's journal that the chaplain had heard the prisoner's confession on the day before his death. When the prisoner's condition worsened, the chaplain came again, but the prisoner died before he could receive the last sacraments. Even so, the chaplain prayed for him. Historians have wondered whether the prisoner revealed his identity in his final confession. If he did, the chaplain was bound to keep it secret. Some later writers claimed that the prisoner had told his secret to the chaplain, but there is no evidence for this. The death of a prisoner in the Bastille was not unusual. Many prisoners died there over the years, from illness or old age. Yet this death would be remembered more than most. It was the end of a life spent almost entirely in silence.

[long-pause]

{img:75-saint-paul-church-cemetery-paris-1703}

The prisoner was buried the next day in the cemetery of the church of Saint-Paul, near the Bastille. On Tuesday, the twentieth of November 1703, the masked prisoner was buried in the cemetery of the parish church of Saint-Paul, not far from the Bastille. Historians know about the burial from du Junca's journal and from the church's burial register. The funeral took place in the afternoon. It was attended by a small number of officials from the Bastille, including Rosarges, the major of the fortress, and Reilhe, its surgeon. They signed the burial register. Even so, the funeral was quiet and simple. There were no family members or friends. The cost of the burial, forty livres, was paid by the Bastille. The cemetery of Saint-Paul was closed in the late eighteenth century, and its graves were moved. The prisoner's remains were lost. The church of Saint-Paul itself was destroyed during the French Revolution. Today, nothing remains to show where the most famous prisoner of the Bastille was buried.

[long-pause]

{img:76-burial-register-name-marchioly-quill}

In the burial register, the prisoner's name was written as Marchioly, aged about forty-five. The burial register of the church of Saint-Paul recorded the masked prisoner under the name Marchioly. It gave his age as about forty-five years. Historians have studied this entry closely. The name resembles Mattioli, the Italian diplomat who had been held at Pignerol. However, Mattioli seems to have died in 1694. The age is also puzzling. If the prisoner had been arrested in 1669 as a grown man, he would have been at least in his fifties in 1703. Most historians believe that both the name and the age were deliberately false, chosen to hide the prisoner's real identity. Even so, the register is one of the few official documents about the prisoner. The original register was destroyed in a fire in 1871, during the Paris Commune. Fortunately, the entry had been copied by historians before the fire, so its words survive. They are the last official trace of the prisoner's life.

[long-pause]

{img:77-servants-burning-furniture-courtyard-fire}

According to later accounts, everything the prisoner had used was burned after his death. According to stories told later in the eighteenth century, the prisoner's belongings were destroyed after his death. His furniture, bedding and clothes were said to have been burned. The walls of his room were scraped and whitewashed, and even the floor tiles were replaced, so that no trace of him or any message he might have left would remain. Historians note that these stories cannot be confirmed by the documents. They may be exaggerations added to the legend. Even so, it was a common practice to burn the bedding and clothes of people who had died, to prevent the spread of disease. The story of the scraped walls made the mystery more dramatic. It suggested that the prisoner might have written his secret somewhere in his room. Over time, the legend grew. Readers imagined secret messages, hidden letters and clues that had been destroyed forever. Yet the real prisoner left almost nothing behind except a few lines in a journal.

[long-pause]

{img:78-paris-evening-lamplight-rooftops-1703}

After his death, the prisoner was soon forgotten by the officials, but not by the people of Paris. After the burial, life in the Bastille continued as before. Du Junca returned to his journal, and Saint-Mars to his duties. Historians note that the officials of the Bastille did not write about the masked prisoner again. For them, the matter was closed. Even so, the story did not disappear. Soldiers, servants and other prisoners had seen the masked man, and they talked about him. Rumours spread through Paris. People said that a prisoner of royal blood had been kept in the Bastille with his face hidden. They wondered who he could have been. Over the following decades, the story was repeated in letters, memoirs and books. It became one of the great mysteries of the age. In 1711, the king's own sister-in-law, the Princess Palatine, wrote in a letter about a man who had been kept masked in the Bastille for many years. The legend of the Man in the Mask had begun.

[long-pause]

{img:79-voltaire-writing-desk-quill-bastille-room}

The writer Voltaire, who was himself imprisoned in the Bastille, made the masked prisoner famous. François-Marie Arouet, known as Voltaire, was one of the most famous writers of the eighteenth century. In 1717, as a young man, he was imprisoned in the Bastille for eleven months, for writing verses that mocked the government. Historians note that during his imprisonment, he heard stories about the masked prisoner from the guards and officers. Later, in 1751, he wrote about the prisoner in his book The Age of Louis the Fourteenth. He described a young, tall and handsome prisoner who wore a mask with an iron chin piece, held by steel springs. Even so, Voltaire did not reveal who he thought the prisoner was. Later, in 1771, he suggested that the prisoner was an older brother of Louis the Fourteenth, born to the queen Anne of Austria. Voltaire's writings made the story famous throughout Europe. They also gave it its most famous detail, the iron mask, which most likely never existed.

[long-pause]

{img:80-twin-brothers-portraits-royal-palace}

In his novel, Alexandre Dumas made the masked prisoner the secret twin brother of the king. In the nineteenth century, the French novelist Alexandre Dumas wrote a series of adventure novels about the musketeers. The last of these, The Vicomte de Bragelonne, was published between 1847 and 1850. Part of it tells the story of the Man in the Iron Mask. In Dumas's story, the prisoner is Philippe, the secret twin brother of Louis the Fourteenth, hidden away at birth to prevent a struggle for the throne. The musketeer Aramis tries to replace the king with his twin. Historians note that this idea was not invented by Dumas. Earlier writers had suggested it. Even so, Dumas made it famous. His story has been told again and again in plays and films, including a well known film from 1998. Historians do not believe the twin theory is true. There is no evidence that Louis the Fourteenth had a twin. Yet the idea remains the best known explanation of the mystery.

[long-pause]

{img:81-historian-desk-old-letters-magnifying-glass}

Over the centuries, historians have suggested dozens of different people as the masked prisoner. Since the eighteenth century, historians and writers have suggested more than fifty different people as the masked prisoner. Some believed he was a royal brother or son. Others believed he was Fouquet, the Duke of Beaufort, the Italian count Mattioli or a son of King Charles the Second of England. Historians note that most of these theories have been rejected, because the dates do not fit or the evidence is too weak. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, historians began studying the letters in the archives more carefully. Many came to agree that the masked prisoner was the man called Eustache Dauger in the letters. In 1903, the Scottish writer Andrew Lang wrote a book called The Valet's Tragedy, arguing that the prisoner was indeed a simple valet. Even so, two questions remained: the true identity of Eustache Dauger, and what he knew. The search for answers continues in the archives today.

[long-pause]

{img:82-nobleman-cavoye-family-house-paris-1660}

Some historians believe Eustache Dauger was a nobleman who had fallen into disgrace. One theory connects the prisoner to a real nobleman named Eustache Dauger de Cavoye. He came from a respected family, and his brother Louis was a friend of Louis the Fourteenth. Historians note that Eustache de Cavoye led a troubled life. He was involved in a scandal in 1659, when he and other young nobles held a wild party during Holy Week, which was considered a great offence. He later killed a young page in a quarrel and fell into debt. His family disowned him. Even so, documents suggest that he was imprisoned elsewhere, in a different prison, around the same time. Some historians believe that he cannot be the masked prisoner, while others think that the evidence is unclear. The theory remains popular, because the name is the same. Yet it does not explain why a disgraced nobleman would be guarded so secretly for so many years, or why he would serve as a valet.

[long-pause]

{img:83-cardinal-mazarin-portrait-palace-study}

A recent theory links the prisoner to the secret finances of Cardinal Mazarin. Cardinal Jules Mazarin was the chief minister of France during the childhood of Louis the Fourteenth. He died in 1661, leaving an enormous fortune. Historians note that much of his wealth had been gained in ways that were not entirely honest. In 2016, the American historian Paul Sonnino published a book suggesting a new explanation. He argued that Eustache Dauger was a valet who had worked for the treasurer of Mazarin, and who may have known how the cardinal's fortune was made. If this knowledge had become public, it could have embarrassed the king and his family. Even so, this theory, like the others, cannot be proven with certainty. Other historians have offered different explanations. Yet many agree on one point. The prisoner was probably not a prince or a twin, but an ordinary man who knew something dangerous. His secret, whatever it was, was more important than his name.

[long-pause]

{img:84-storming-bastille-1789-crowd-towers-smoke}

When the Bastille was stormed in 1789, people searched its records for the name of the masked prisoner. On the fourteenth of July 1789, a crowd of Parisians attacked and captured the Bastille. It was one of the first great events of the French Revolution. Historians note that, at the time, the fortress held only seven prisoners. Even so, it was a powerful symbol of royal power, and its fall was celebrated throughout France. In the days that followed, people searched through the papers of the Bastille. Some hoped to find the name of the masked prisoner. According to some accounts, the page of the register for the year of his arrival was missing. This only added to the mystery. The Bastille was soon demolished, and its stones were used for building, including a bridge over the Seine. Today, the Place de la Bastille stands where the fortress once was. A column rises in the middle of the square, and lines on the ground show where some of the old walls stood. The fourteenth of July is France's national day.

[long-pause]

{img:85-museum-cell-sainte-marguerite-visitors-window}

Today, visitors to the island of Sainte-Marguerite can stand in the cell where the masked prisoner lived. Today, the island of Sainte-Marguerite can be reached by a short boat trip from Cannes. Visitors walk along its shady paths, swim in its clear water and visit the Fort Royal. The fort houses the Musée de la Mer, a museum of the sea, with objects from ancient shipwrecks found nearby. Historians note that the museum also preserves the old prison. Visitors can enter the cell where, according to tradition, the masked prisoner spent eleven years. It is a large vaulted room with thick walls and a window with iron bars looking out to sea. Even so, it is quiet and peaceful today, filled with sunlight and the sound of the waves. Nearby, the cells of the Protestant pastors are also open, with paintings and memorials. Many visitors stop at the barred window and look out at the sea, imagining the life of the prisoner who stood there more than three hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:86-black-velvet-mask-museum-glass-soft-light}

More than three hundred years after his death, the identity of the masked prisoner is still unknown. The masked prisoner died in 1703, more than three centuries ago. Yet his identity remains one of the great unsolved mysteries of history. Historians have studied every surviving letter, register and journal, and they have built many careful theories. Most now agree that he was the man called Eustache Dauger, arrested near Dunkirk in 1669. They agree that his mask was made of velvet, not iron, and that he was probably not a royal twin. Even so, they still do not know who he really was, or why he was kept hidden for so long. Perhaps the answer was never written down. Perhaps it was in papers that were lost or destroyed. The story continues to inspire books, films and visits to the places where he was held. From Pignerol to Exilles, from Sainte-Marguerite to the Bastille, the prisoner's long journey can still be followed. His secret, it seems, will stay with him forever.
