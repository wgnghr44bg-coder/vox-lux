TOPIC: The masked prisoner of the Bastille
===
IMG: masked-prisoner-arrives-bastille-courtyard-1698 | a prisoner in a long dark cloak and a black velvet mask led by guards with lanterns across the stone courtyard of the Bastille fortress in Paris, 1698, evening, warm lantern light
HEAD: On the eighteenth of September 1698, a prisoner wearing a black mask arrived at the Bastille in Paris.
BODY: In the afternoon of the eighteenth of September 1698, a closed carriage drove into the Bastille, the great royal fortress and prison on the eastern edge of Paris. Inside was the new governor of the Bastille, Bénigne Dauvergne de Saint-Mars, and a prisoner whose face was hidden. The prisoner wore a mask of black velvet. Historians know about his arrival from the journal of Étienne du Junca, the king's lieutenant at the Bastille, who wrote down the arrival of every prisoner. Du Junca noted that the governor had brought an old prisoner with him, whom he had guarded for many years, and that the prisoner's face was always covered by a mask. The prisoner's name was not written down. Even so, du Junca recorded that a room had been prepared for him in a tower of the fortress. From that day on, the masked prisoner lived in the Bastille, quietly and carefully watched, until his death five years later.
===
IMG: bastille-fortress-eight-towers-paris-1700 | the massive stone Bastille fortress with eight round towers and high walls beside a moat in Paris around 1700, horse-drawn carts and townspeople in the street, soft morning light
HEAD: The Bastille was a grey stone fortress with eight round towers, standing at the eastern gate of Paris.
BODY: The Bastille was built in the fourteenth century to protect the eastern entrance to Paris. Its full name was the Bastille Saint-Antoine, because it stood next to the gate of Saint-Antoine. It had eight round towers, joined by thick walls about twenty-four metres high, and was surrounded by a deep moat. By the seventeenth century, it was no longer needed as a defence. Instead, it was used as a state prison. Historians note that it held prisoners sent there on the direct order of the king, through a sealed letter called a lettre de cachet. Many prisoners were nobles, writers or people involved in political matters. Even so, the Bastille was not a large prison. It usually held between twenty and fifty prisoners at a time. Each tower had a name, such as the Liberty Tower, the Well Tower and the Bertaudière Tower. Its grim towers became a famous symbol of royal power.
===
IMG: lieutenant-writing-journal-candle-bastille-office | an officer in a long coat and wig writing in a leather-bound journal by candlelight in a stone-walled office of a fortress around 1700, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: Most of what is known about the masked prisoner comes from a lieutenant's careful journal.
BODY: Étienne du Junca was the king's lieutenant at the Bastille from 1690 until his death in 1706. He kept a detailed journal of the prisoners who entered and left the fortress. He wrote down their names, the dates of their arrival, the rooms they were given and, when they died, the dates of their deaths and burials. Historians consider his journal one of the most important sources for the story of the masked prisoner. It is kept today in the Arsenal Library in Paris. Du Junca's entries about the masked prisoner are short. He wrote about his arrival in 1698 and his death in 1703. Even so, these few lines are among the only direct records of the prisoner's life in the Bastille. Du Junca did not seem to know who the prisoner was. He never wrote the name, and he described him only as the old prisoner of Monsieur de Saint-Mars.
===
IMG: black-velvet-mask-on-wooden-table-candle | a black velvet mask lying on a rough wooden table beside a pewter cup and a candle in a stone prison cell around 1700, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: Although he became famous as the man in the iron mask, the prisoner's mask was actually made of black velvet.
BODY: The prisoner became famous around the world as the Man in the Iron Mask. Yet historians note that the records from his own time describe a mask of black velvet, not iron. Du Junca wrote in his journal that the prisoner always wore a mask of black velvet. Velvet is a soft, thick fabric, often used for fine clothing. In the seventeenth century, wearing a velvet mask outdoors was not unusual. Some ladies wore them to protect their skin from the sun. Even so, it was very unusual for a prisoner to wear one. The idea of an iron mask came later. It appeared in stories told in the eighteenth century, especially by the writer Voltaire. An iron mask made the tale more dramatic and mysterious. Today, historians believe the prisoner probably wore the mask only when others might see him, such as when he travelled or when visitors were present.
===
IMG: louis-xiv-portrait-versailles-hall-1690 | King Louis XIV of France in his later years wearing a long dark curled wig and a richly embroidered coat, standing in a gilded hall of the Palace of Versailles, warm golden light
HEAD: The masked prisoner was held on the orders of King Louis the Fourteenth.
BODY: Louis the Fourteenth was king of France from 1643 until 1715, one of the longest reigns in European history. He became king at the age of four and ruled personally from 1661. He was known as the Sun King, because he chose the sun as his symbol. During his reign, France became the most powerful country in Europe. Historians know that the masked prisoner was held on the direct orders of the king and his ministers. Letters about the prisoner were written by the king's ministers to the jailer, Saint-Mars. Even so, the letters never explained why the prisoner was being held. They only gave strict instructions about keeping him secret. Some letters suggest that the king himself took an interest in the prisoner's care. Louis the Fourteenth died in 1715, twelve years after the prisoner. Whatever he knew about the prisoner's identity, he took to his grave.
===
IMG: guards-lanterns-stone-stairway-tower-night | two guards in long coats and tricorn hats carrying lanterns up a narrow spiral stone stairway inside an old fortress tower around 1700, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lantern light
HEAD: The prisoner had been held in secret for nearly thirty years before he reached the Bastille.
BODY: When the masked prisoner arrived at the Bastille in 1698, he was not a new prisoner. According to the surviving letters, he had first been arrested in 1669, almost thirty years earlier. Since then, he had been held in several different fortresses, always under the guard of the same jailer, Saint-Mars. Each time Saint-Mars was given a new post, the prisoner went with him. Historians have traced his journey through letters written by the ministers of Louis the Fourteenth. These letters were kept in the French state archives. Even so, they rarely mention the prisoner by name. He is usually described in vague terms, such as the prisoner, or the one from Pignerol. Over these long years, the prisoner grew from a young man into an old one. By the time he reached Paris, he was probably in his fifties or sixties. He had spent most of his adult life behind walls.
===
IMG: paris-1700-rooftops-seine-bridges-dawn | a view over the rooftops of Paris around 1700 with church towers, the river Seine and old stone bridges lined with houses, small boats on the river, soft dawn light
HEAD: Outside the walls of the Bastille, Paris was one of the largest cities in Europe.
BODY: Around the year 1700, Paris had about half a million inhabitants, making it one of the largest cities in Europe. Its narrow streets were crowded with carriages, carts, street sellers and craftspeople. Houses were built on the bridges over the river Seine. Historians note that the city was full of churches, markets and workshops, and its people worked as bakers, weavers, water carriers and booksellers. The king and his court no longer lived in Paris. In 1682, Louis the Fourteenth had moved his court to his great palace at Versailles, about twenty kilometres away. Even so, Paris remained the heart of France. It was a centre of trade, learning and fashion. Its people loved news and gossip, and stories about the court spread quickly through the city. The secret prisoner in the Bastille would soon become one of the most popular subjects of rumour in all of Paris.

TOPIC: France in the age of the Sun King
===
IMG: versailles-palace-gardens-fountains-1690 | the great Palace of Versailles around 1690 with long formal gardens, fountains and clipped hedges, courtiers in long coats and gowns walking along gravel paths, bright morning light
HEAD: Louis the Fourteenth built the Palace of Versailles, one of the grandest palaces in the world.
BODY: Versailles began as a small hunting lodge built by Louis the Thirteenth, the father of Louis the Fourteenth. From the 1660s, Louis the Fourteenth turned it into an enormous palace. Thousands of workers built new wings, halls and gardens. The famous Hall of Mirrors was completed in 1684. Historians note that the gardens were as important as the palace. They were designed by André Le Nôtre, with long avenues, fountains, statues and a great canal. In 1682, the king made Versailles the official home of the royal court and the government. Even so, life at Versailles was not always comfortable. The palace was crowded, cold in winter and noisy with visitors. Thousands of courtiers lived there, hoping to win the king's favour. Every part of the king's day, from waking to going to bed, was a public ceremony. Versailles became a model for royal palaces across Europe.
===
IMG: royal-ministers-council-table-papers-1680 | royal ministers in long curled wigs and dark coats seated around a council table covered with papers, quills and sealing wax in a panelled room around 1680, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm light
HEAD: Louis the Fourteenth ruled with the help of a small group of powerful ministers.
BODY: Louis the Fourteenth wanted to rule France himself, without a chief minister. He met regularly with a small council of trusted advisers. Two of the most important were Jean-Baptiste Colbert, who managed the kingdom's finances and trade, and the Marquis de Louvois, who was in charge of the army and war. Historians note that Louvois was also responsible for the state prisons and fortresses. That is why many of the letters about the masked prisoner were written by him. Louvois was hardworking, strict and feared by many. He built up the French army into the largest in Europe. Even so, he was also known for his harshness. After his death in 1691, his son, the Marquis de Barbezieux, took over his duties and continued to write letters about the prisoner. These ministers' letters, written by secretaries and signed by the ministers, are now among the most important sources for the mystery.
===
IMG: royal-letter-wax-seal-quill-desk | a folded letter sealed with red wax and a royal seal lying on a polished wooden desk beside a quill, an inkpot and a candle around 1680, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: With a sealed royal letter, the king could send anyone to prison without a trial.
BODY: In France under Louis the Fourteenth, the king had the power to imprison people without a trial. He did this through a document called a lettre de cachet, which means a letter with a seal. It was signed by the king and a minister and sealed with the royal seal. Historians note that these letters were used for many reasons. Some were used against people who had criticised the government or caused public scandal. Others were requested by families who wanted to stop a relative from wasting the family fortune or bringing shame on their name. Even so, the system was often criticised as unfair, because prisoners had no chance to defend themselves. Some were held for a few weeks, while others were held for many years. The masked prisoner was held by the king's order. The lettres de cachet became a symbol of royal power that was later attacked during the French Revolution.
===
IMG: musketeers-blue-tunics-silver-crosses-courtyard | French royal musketeers in blue tunics with silver crosses, wide-brimmed feathered hats and long boots standing beside their horses in a palace courtyard around 1670, warm afternoon light
HEAD: The king's musketeers were an elite company of soldiers who guarded the king.
BODY: The musketeers of the guard were an elite company of soldiers who served the king of France. They were formed in 1622 under Louis the Thirteenth. They wore blue tunics decorated with a silver cross, and they fought both on horseback and on foot. Many young nobles joined them, hoping to win the king's notice and begin a career. Historians note that one of the most famous musketeers was Charles de Batz de Castelmore, known as d'Artagnan. He became captain-lieutenant of the musketeers and served Louis the Fourteenth loyally. He later became famous through the novels of Alexandre Dumas. Even so, the real d'Artagnan was quite different from the character in the books. He was a careful and trusted officer. In 1661, the king gave him an important secret task. The jailer Saint-Mars had served under him in the musketeers, and this would shape the rest of his career.
===
IMG: french-army-camp-tents-flags-1670 | a French army camp around 1670 with rows of white canvas tents, flags with fleurs-de-lis, soldiers in long coats cooking over fires and horses tethered nearby, soft evening light
HEAD: During the reign of Louis the Fourteenth, France was often at war with its neighbours.
BODY: Louis the Fourteenth wanted to make France safer and more powerful by expanding its borders. During his reign, France fought several major wars. These included the War of Devolution against Spain, the Dutch War against the Dutch Republic and its allies, and later the Nine Years' War and the War of the Spanish Succession. Historians note that these wars were very expensive and cost many lives. Yet they also added new territories to France, including parts of Flanders and the region of Franche-Comté. Even so, war affected the lives of prisoners too. Fortresses on the borders, such as Pignerol in the Alps, were important military places, and some became prisons for people the king wished to keep far from Paris. The secret letters about the masked prisoner were written during these years of war, when the king's ministers were busy with armies, sieges and treaties across Europe.
===
IMG: paris-street-life-bakers-water-carriers-1680 | a narrow Paris street around 1680 with a baker selling bread from his shop, a water carrier with buckets, women in long skirts and bonnets, children and a horse-drawn cart, timber-framed houses, soft morning light
HEAD: For ordinary people in France, life was mostly about work, bread and the changing seasons.
BODY: While the king and his court lived in splendour, most people in France lived simple lives. About eight out of ten people were peasants, working the land in small villages. They grew wheat, rye and vegetables, kept animals and paid taxes to the king, the church and their local lords. In towns, people worked as craftspeople, shopkeepers and labourers. Historians note that bread was the most important food, and its price mattered greatly to ordinary families. A bad harvest could cause hunger. Even so, life also had its pleasures. People enjoyed festivals, fairs, markets and religious holidays. They gathered in taverns and churches and shared news and rumours. Stories about the king, his court and mysterious events travelled quickly by word of mouth. The tale of a masked prisoner was exactly the kind of story that people loved to tell, and it spread from town to town.
===
IMG: royal-archives-shelves-bundles-letters | tall wooden shelves filled with tied bundles of old letters and leather-bound registers in a quiet archive room with a reading table and a candle, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft warm light
HEAD: Hundreds of letters from the king's ministers about the state prisons have survived in the archives.
BODY: The French government of the seventeenth century kept many written records. Letters from ministers to governors, officers and jailers were copied into registers before they were sent. Many of these copies survive today in French archives, including the archives of the Ministry of War. Historians have searched through them for clues about the masked prisoner. Among them are letters from Louvois and Barbezieux to Saint-Mars, giving orders about his prisoners. Some replies from Saint-Mars survive as well. Even so, the letters do not answer the main question. They show how carefully the prisoner was guarded, but never clearly say who he was or why he was held. Some historians believe that the letters were written in a deliberately vague way. Others think that everyone involved already knew the reasons, so there was no need to write them down. The archives keep their silence on the most important point.

TOPIC: An arrest at Dunkirk, 1669
===
IMG: dunkirk-harbour-ships-1669-grey-sky | the busy harbour of Dunkirk in 1669 with wooden sailing ships at anchor, fishermen unloading nets, stone fortifications and church towers behind, grey sky and soft light
HEAD: In July 1669, a man was arrested at the port of Dunkirk, on the northern coast of France.
BODY: The story of the masked prisoner begins in July 1669. On the nineteenth of that month, the minister Louvois wrote a letter to Saint-Mars, then governor of the prison fortress of Pignerol. He told him to prepare a cell for a prisoner who was being sent to him. The prisoner was arrested near Dunkirk, a port on the northern coast of France, which Louis the Fourteenth had bought from England a few years earlier, in 1662. Historians know about the arrest from this letter and from others written at the same time. The arrest was made by Captain Alexandre de Vauroy, an officer of the garrison at Dunkirk. Even so, the letters do not explain why the man was arrested. He was taken quickly and quietly, and then escorted all the way across France to the Alps. The journey took about a month. The prisoner arrived at Pignerol in August 1669.
===
IMG: louvois-minister-writing-letter-desk | a stern French minister in a long curled wig and dark velvet coat writing a letter with a quill at a heavy wooden desk around 1669, papers and maps beside him, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm light
HEAD: In his letter, Louvois gave the prisoner a name: Eustache Dauger.
BODY: In his letter of July 1669, Louvois gave the prisoner a name. He called him Eustache Dauger. This is the only name that appears in the official letters. Historians have spent a great deal of time trying to discover who Eustache Dauger was. Some believe the name was false, chosen to hide his real identity. The handwriting of the letter suggests the name may have been added by a different hand. Others believe Dauger was a real person, and have searched for families with that name. Even so, no one has been able to prove who he was with certainty. Louvois described him as only a valet, a servant. This suggests he was not a nobleman or a person of high rank. Yet the care taken to hide him has made many people doubt this description. The question of how a simple valet could be so dangerous to the king has fascinated historians ever since.
===
IMG: prisoner-escorted-carriage-country-road | a closed black carriage with drawn curtains escorted by mounted soldiers in long coats along a dusty country road through French farmland in 1669, poplar trees, soft summer light
HEAD: The prisoner was taken across France under escort, and no one was allowed to speak with him.
BODY: After his arrest, Eustache Dauger was taken south across France to Pignerol. The journey was long, more than a thousand kilometres, through towns, fields and forests and then into the Alps. Historians know from the letters that strict orders were given. No one was to know where the prisoner was being taken, and no one was allowed to speak with him. The escort was led by Captain de Vauroy, who had made the arrest. He was told to deliver the prisoner directly to Saint-Mars. Even so, little is known about the journey itself. The prisoner probably travelled by carriage or on horseback, with soldiers riding beside him. They stopped at inns and military posts along the way. In August 1669, the group reached Pignerol. Saint-Mars took charge of the prisoner. From that moment, he was cut off from the outside world, and he would remain so for the rest of his life.
===
IMG: stone-cell-two-doors-small-window | a bare stone prison cell around 1670 with a small high barred window, a heavy wooden door inside another door, a narrow bed and a wooden stool, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft daylight
HEAD: Louvois ordered a cell with several doors, so that no one could hear the prisoner speak.
BODY: Louvois gave Saint-Mars precise instructions about the prisoner's cell. It had to be secure, with several doors, one closing behind another, so that the guards outside could not hear anything the prisoner might say. Historians know these orders from the letter of July 1669. Louvois also ordered that Saint-Mars himself should bring the prisoner his food once a day. He was to ask the prisoner only what he needed. Even so, the instructions were even stricter than this. Louvois wrote that if the prisoner ever spoke about anything other than his daily needs, Saint-Mars was to threaten him with death. These orders show how important it was to keep the prisoner silent. Historians believe that he must have known something that the king and his ministers wanted to keep secret. What that secret was has never been discovered. The prisoner's silence was guarded for more than thirty years.
===
IMG: prisoner-meal-tray-bread-wine-cell-door | a jailer in a long coat handing a wooden tray with bread, a bowl of soup and a small jug of wine through a heavy cell door in an old stone fortress, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lantern light
HEAD: The prisoner was given good food, decent clothing and a few comforts, despite the strict secrecy.
BODY: Although the prisoner was kept in strict secrecy, he does not seem to have been treated cruelly. The letters show that he was given good food, decent clothing and furniture. Historians note that prisoners in royal fortresses were often fed according to their social rank. The king paid a sum each day for each prisoner's keep. For the masked prisoner, the amount was generous. He was given meals with meat, bread and wine. He was allowed books, especially religious books, and it is said that he played the guitar. Even so, he lived a lonely life. He could not speak freely with anyone, and he had no contact with his family or friends. Later accounts say he was treated with a certain respect by his jailers, who spoke politely to him. This careful treatment has made some people believe that he was a person of importance, perhaps even of noble or royal birth.
===
IMG: dunkirk-dunes-fishing-boats-evening | the sandy dunes and long beach near Dunkirk around 1669 with small fishing boats pulled up on the sand, fishermen mending nets and a distant windmill, soft golden evening light
HEAD: Some historians link the arrest to secret talks between France and England in 1669.
BODY: Some historians have tried to understand the prisoner by looking at what was happening in 1669. In that year, Louis the Fourteenth was holding secret talks with King Charles the Second of England. These talks led, in 1670, to the Secret Treaty of Dover. In this treaty, Charles agreed to support France against the Dutch, and he promised to announce himself a Catholic when the moment was right, in return for French money. Historians note that this promise was a dangerous secret, since most English people were Protestant. Some have suggested that Eustache Dauger may have been a servant who carried letters or knew something about these negotiations. Dunkirk was close to England, and messages passed through it often. Even so, this theory cannot be proven. It is one of many ideas put forward over the centuries. Yet the timing of the arrest, during these secret talks, remains interesting to historians.

TOPIC: Saint-Mars, the faithful jailer
===
IMG: saint-mars-jailer-portrait-fortress-office | a middle-aged French officer in a long curled wig and a dark military coat seated at a desk in a fortress office around 1670, keys and papers beside him, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm light
HEAD: Bénigne Dauvergne de Saint-Mars guarded the masked prisoner for more than thirty years.
BODY: Bénigne Dauvergne de Saint-Mars was born in 1626 in Burgundy, in eastern France. He came from a modest noble family and joined the army as a young man. He served in the musketeers under d'Artagnan and rose through the ranks. Historians describe him as loyal, careful and strict. He followed his orders exactly and was trusted by the king and his ministers. In 1664, he was appointed governor of the prison at Pignerol. Even so, guarding prisoners was not glamorous work. Saint-Mars spent most of his life in isolated fortresses, far from Paris and the court. In return, he was well paid, and he became wealthy. He bought land and an estate in Burgundy. For more than thirty years, he guarded the masked prisoner, moving with him from one fortress to another. He became governor of the Bastille in 1698 and died there in 1708.
===
IMG: officer-checking-keys-ring-stone-corridor | an officer in a long coat holding a large ring of iron keys while checking a heavy locked door in a dim stone corridor of a fortress, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lantern light
HEAD: Saint-Mars checked every detail of his prisoners' lives himself.
BODY: Saint-Mars was known for his attention to detail. He checked the locks, the doors and the windows of the cells himself. He read his prisoners' letters, when they were allowed to write any, and he watched who came and went from the fortress. Historians know from his letters to the ministers that he reported regularly on his prisoners' health, behaviour and needs. He asked permission for even small changes, such as giving a prisoner new clothes or a book. Even so, his care was also a way of protecting himself. If a prisoner escaped or spoke to the wrong person, Saint-Mars would be blamed. So he was always cautious. Some historians think that he may have exaggerated the importance of his prisoners, to make his own role seem more important. Others believe he was simply doing his duty well. Either way, his letters give a detailed picture of life in a seventeenth century state prison.
===
IMG: jailer-family-dinner-fortress-house | a family dinner in the warm house of a fortress governor around 1675, a man in a wig, his wife in a silk gown and children at a long table with candles and pewter dishes, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: Saint-Mars lived with his family inside the fortresses where he guarded his prisoners.
BODY: Like many fortress governors, Saint-Mars lived with his family inside the walls of the fortress he commanded. He married Marie-Antoinette Collot, the sister-in-law of the Marquis de Louvois's chief clerk, which helped his career. They had two sons, who later joined the army. Historians note that a governor's house was usually more comfortable than the rest of the fortress, with proper rooms, a kitchen and servants. Even so, life could be lonely and dull. Pignerol, Exilles and the island of Sainte-Marguerite were far from big cities. Visitors were few, and there was little entertainment. The family's life revolved around the routine of the fortress and the needs of the prisoners. Saint-Mars' sons grew up among soldiers, guards and prisoners. Sadly, both sons were later killed in battle, one in 1692 and the other in 1693. Saint-Mars and his wife were deeply saddened by their loss.
===
IMG: fortress-soldiers-changing-guard-ramparts | French soldiers in long grey-white coats and tricorn hats changing the guard on the stone ramparts of a mountain fortress around 1670, muskets on their shoulders, mountains behind, soft morning light
HEAD: A small company of soldiers helped Saint-Mars guard his prisoners day and night.
BODY: Saint-Mars commanded a small company of soldiers, who guarded the fortress and its prisoners. They stood watch on the walls, at the gates and outside the prisoners' rooms. Historians know from the letters that Saint-Mars chose his soldiers and servants carefully. Only a few trusted men were allowed near the most secret prisoners. Saint-Mars' nephew, Guillaume de Formanoir, and an officer named Jacques de Rosarges served with him for many years. Rosarges followed Saint-Mars to the Bastille and was present when the masked prisoner died. Even so, the soldiers' life was often boring. They spent long hours on guard duty, with little to do. In their free time, they played cards and dice, drank in the canteen and wrote letters home. Some came from nearby villages, while others came from far across France. Most of them never knew the name of the prisoner they guarded.
===
IMG: officer-reading-orders-candle-lonely-room | a lonely officer reading a letter by candlelight at a small table in a plain stone room of a fortress at night, a sword hanging on the wall, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: Saint-Mars often asked to be moved to a better post, but he was always sent with his prisoner.
BODY: Over the years, Saint-Mars wrote many letters asking for promotion or a better position. He hoped for a post closer to Paris or to his estates in Burgundy. Historians note that the ministers often agreed, but each time Saint-Mars moved, he was ordered to take his most important prisoners with him. In 1681, he became governor of Exilles, a fortress in the Alps. In 1687, he became governor of the islands of Sainte-Marguerite and Saint-Honorat, near Cannes. In 1698, he was finally appointed governor of the Bastille. Even so, his life always remained tied to the masked prisoner. Some historians believe that the prisoner was kept with Saint-Mars because no one else could be trusted with the secret. Others think that the ministers simply found it convenient. Either way, the jailer and the prisoner spent more than thirty years together, closer than many families, yet never truly free to speak.
===
IMG: burgundy-chateau-palteau-vineyards | a modest French country château with a slate roof and stone walls surrounded by fields and vineyards in Burgundy around 1690, farm workers in the fields, soft golden afternoon light
HEAD: With his earnings as a jailer, Saint-Mars bought a country estate in Burgundy.
BODY: Saint-Mars earned a good income from his work as a governor and jailer. He was paid for each prisoner he guarded, as well as receiving his salary. Historians note that by the end of his career, he had become a wealthy man. He bought land and a country house at Palteau, near the town of Villeneuve-sur-Yonne in Burgundy. The house still stands today. Even so, Saint-Mars rarely had time to enjoy it. His duties kept him in his fortresses most of the time. The estate at Palteau later became important in the story of the masked prisoner. In 1698, on the way from Sainte-Marguerite to Paris, Saint-Mars stopped there with his prisoner for a few days. A later owner of the estate, a relative of Saint-Mars, wrote down what the local peasants had seen. Their account became one of the few descriptions of the masked prisoner from people who had seen him.
===
