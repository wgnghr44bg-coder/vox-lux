TOPIC: The long road to Paris, 1698
===
IMG: closed-carriage-road-provence-1698 | a closed carriage with drawn leather curtains travelling along a tree-lined road through the French countryside in 1698, escorted by riders in long coats and tricorn hats, soft late summer light
HEAD: In the late summer of 1698, Saint-Mars and his prisoner set out on the long journey to Paris.
BODY: In 1698, the minister Barbezieux, the son of Louvois, wrote to Saint-Mars about his new appointment as governor of the Bastille. He told him to bring his old prisoner with him, taking every precaution so that no one would see him or know who he was. Historians know about the journey from these letters and from later accounts. Saint-Mars and the prisoner left Sainte-Marguerite in late summer, crossing to the mainland by boat. They travelled north through Provence and Burgundy towards Paris, a journey of about nine hundred kilometres. Even so, the details of the journey are uncertain. The prisoner probably travelled in a closed carriage or litter, with Saint-Mars and a small escort of soldiers. They stopped at inns and houses along the way. The journey took several weeks. It was the prisoner's longest journey since his arrest almost thirty years earlier, and his last.
===
IMG: french-village-inn-courtyard-horses-1698 | the courtyard of a village inn in France in 1698 with horses being watered, a stable boy, an innkeeper in an apron and a closed carriage waiting, stone buildings with wooden shutters, warm evening light
HEAD: At the inns along the road, the prisoner was kept apart from other travellers.
BODY: Travel in France in the late seventeenth century was slow and often uncomfortable. Roads were rough, and carriages bumped over stones and holes. Travellers usually covered between thirty and fifty kilometres a day. Historians note that they spent their nights at inns, where they could eat, sleep and change horses. At each stop, Saint-Mars made sure that his prisoner was kept away from the other guests. He was given a separate room, and his meals were brought to him. Even so, the arrival of a governor with a masked prisoner must have attracted attention. Innkeepers, servants and other travellers would have noticed the soldiers and the closed carriage. Stories about the mysterious prisoner probably spread along the road. By the time they reached Paris, rumours had already begun to circulate. People wondered who could be so important that his face had to be hidden from everyone he met.
===
IMG: chateau-palteau-courtyard-carriage-arrives | a closed carriage arriving in the courtyard of a modest stone château in Burgundy in 1698, servants and peasants watching from a distance, a man in a dark cloak stepping down, warm late afternoon light
HEAD: On the way to Paris, Saint-Mars stopped for a few days at his own château of Palteau.
BODY: On the journey to Paris, Saint-Mars and his prisoner stopped at Palteau, Saint-Mars' estate in Burgundy. Historians know about this stop from a letter written many years later, in 1768, by a relative of Saint-Mars who had inherited the estate. He had heard the story from old peasants who had seen the prisoner. According to them, the prisoner arrived in a litter, followed by Saint-Mars on horseback. He wore a black mask, and the peasants noticed that his teeth and lips could be seen. Even so, they could not see his face. They said that he was tall and had white hair. The peasants remembered the visit for the rest of their lives. Historians note that this is one of the few descriptions of the prisoner by people who saw him. It must be treated with caution, since it was told many years later. Yet it gives a vivid picture of the mysterious traveller.
===
IMG: dining-room-pistols-on-table-candles | two men dining at a table in a château dining room with their backs to a tall window, one wearing a black mask, a pair of pistols lying on the table beside the plates, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: At Palteau, Saint-Mars ate with his prisoner, with two loaded pistols on the table.
BODY: The peasants of Palteau told another story about the prisoner's visit. According to them, Saint-Mars ate his meals with the prisoner in the dining room of the château. The prisoner sat with his back to the window, so that no one outside could see his face. Saint-Mars sat opposite him, with two loaded pistols on the table beside his plate. Historians note that the peasants watched through the windows and could see these details. Even so, the story may have been improved over time. Pistols on the table would have been an unusual sight, and the story became one of the most dramatic parts of the legend. The peasants also said that the prisoner slept in a bed next to Saint-Mars' room. The governor watched over him day and night. After a few days, the travellers continued their journey. The prisoner left Palteau as mysteriously as he had arrived, and the peasants never saw him again.
===
IMG: road-through-forest-fontainebleau-autumn | a dirt road through a large forest of tall oaks and beeches in early autumn with golden leaves, a closed carriage and riders far in the distance, soft misty light
HEAD: The last part of the journey led through the great forests south of Paris.
BODY: After leaving Burgundy, Saint-Mars and his prisoner travelled north towards Paris. The road passed through the great forests south of the capital, including the forest of Fontainebleau, where the kings of France had a famous palace and liked to hunt. Historians note that the forests were wide and quiet, with tall oak and beech trees. Travellers had to be careful of robbers, who sometimes attacked carriages on lonely roads. Even so, a governor travelling with soldiers would have been safe. In September, the leaves were beginning to turn gold. The air was cooler than in the south. For the prisoner, who had spent most of his life in mountain fortresses and on an island, the countryside of northern France must have been a strange sight, if he could see it at all. As they came closer to Paris, the villages grew larger, and the roads became busier with carts, coaches and travellers.
===
IMG: entering-paris-gate-saint-antoine-crowd | a closed carriage escorted by riders entering Paris through an old stone city gate in 1698, crowds of townspeople, carts and street sellers around it, the towers of the Bastille rising beside the gate, afternoon light
HEAD: On the eighteenth of September 1698, the carriage entered Paris and drove into the Bastille.
BODY: On the eighteenth of September 1698, Saint-Mars and his prisoner arrived in Paris. They entered the city through the gate of Saint-Antoine, next to the Bastille. Historians know the exact date from du Junca's journal. He wrote that the new governor arrived at three o'clock in the afternoon, bringing with him an old prisoner he had guarded at Pignerol. The prisoner was taken first to a room in the Bassinière tower, and later moved to the third room of the Bertaudière tower. Even so, du Junca did not learn his name. He wrote only that the prisoner was always masked and that his name was not to be spoken. The streets around the Bastille were busy with people going about their daily lives. Few would have noticed one more carriage entering the fortress. Yet the arrival of the masked prisoner was the beginning of one of the most famous mysteries in the history of Paris.
===
TOPIC: Life inside the Bastille
===
IMG: bastille-tower-room-furniture-fireplace | a large octagonal room inside a stone fortress tower with a fireplace, a bed with curtains, a wooden table, chairs and a small high window, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: The rooms of the Bastille were often larger and more comfortable than people later imagined.
BODY: In later years, the Bastille became a symbol of cruelty and injustice. Yet historians note that many of its rooms were fairly comfortable, especially for prisoners of high rank. The rooms in the towers were large and octagonal, with thick walls, a fireplace and a window. Prisoners could bring their own furniture, books and clothing, and some had servants. Even so, conditions varied greatly. The worst cells were the dungeons at the bottom of the towers, which were damp and dark, and the rooms at the top, which were very cold in winter and hot in summer. Most prisoners lived in the middle floors. The masked prisoner was placed in the third room of the Bertaudière tower. It was a decent room, with a fireplace and furniture. Historians believe that he was treated well, as he had been in his earlier prisons. His life in the Bastille was quiet and orderly.
===
IMG: bastille-kitchen-cooks-preparing-meals | cooks in white aprons preparing meals in a large stone kitchen of a fortress around 1700, copper pots over an open fire, bread on a table and a servant carrying trays, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: Prisoners in the Bastille were fed at the king's expense, and the food was often good.
BODY: The king paid for the food of prisoners in the Bastille. The amount depended on their social rank. Historians note that ordinary prisoners received a modest sum each day, while nobles and important prisoners received much more. The governor was responsible for feeding them, and he could make a profit if he spent less than he received. Even so, many prisoners ate well. Meals often included soup, meat, bread, vegetables, fruit and wine. On religious fast days, fish and eggs were served instead of meat. Some prisoners were even invited to dine with the governor. The masked prisoner was given generous meals, as he had been in his earlier prisons. According to some accounts, he was fond of fine linen and good food. The kitchens of the Bastille were busy places, where cooks prepared meals for the prisoners, the governor's family, the officers and the soldiers of the garrison.
===
IMG: chapel-curtained-mass-fortress-candles | a small stone chapel inside a fortress with a priest in vestments celebrating mass at a candlelit altar, small curtained wooden boxes for prisoners along the side wall, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: Prisoners attended mass in the Bastille chapel, hidden from each other behind curtains.
BODY: The Bastille had its own chapel, where mass was said for the prisoners and the garrison. Prisoners who were allowed to attend were placed in small booths or behind curtains, so that they could see the altar but could not see each other. Historians note that this arrangement allowed the governor to keep prisoners apart, even during religious services. The masked prisoner attended mass regularly. Du Junca wrote that he went to mass on the day of his death. Even so, he was always masked when he left his room. The chaplain of the Bastille also visited prisoners in their rooms. He heard their confessions and gave them communion. For many prisoners, religion was one of their few comforts. The masked prisoner seems to have been a religious man, who found consolation in prayer during his long years of imprisonment. The chapel was one of the few places where he could leave his room.
===
IMG: prisoners-walking-tower-top-paris-view | a prisoner in a long coat walking on the flat stone roof of a fortress tower beside a guard, the rooftops and church towers of Paris spread out below, soft morning light
HEAD: Some prisoners were allowed to walk on the tops of the towers, from where they could see Paris.
BODY: Prisoners in the Bastille were sometimes allowed to take exercise. Historians note that some walked in a small courtyard, while others were allowed onto the tops of the towers. From there, they could see the rooftops of Paris, the river Seine and the countryside beyond. For prisoners who had spent months or years indoors, it was a precious moment of freedom. Even so, they were always accompanied by guards, and they were not allowed to signal to anyone outside. Whether the masked prisoner was allowed to walk on the towers is not known. If he was, he would have worn his mask. From the top of the Bertaudière tower, he could have seen the busy streets below, the church towers and the gardens of the nearby faubourg Saint-Antoine. After so many years in remote fortresses, Paris must have seemed enormous. Yet he remained as isolated as ever, a stranger in the heart of a great city.
===
IMG: bastille-library-books-shelves-prisoner | a prisoner in a plain coat reading a leather-bound book in a chair by a small window in a stone tower room, a shelf of books and a candle beside him, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft daylight
HEAD: The Bastille had a library, and many prisoners spent their days reading.
BODY: Reading was one of the main ways prisoners passed the time in the Bastille. Many brought their own books, and over the years, a library was built up from books left by former prisoners. Historians note that religious books, histories and works of literature were common. Some prisoners also wrote, producing letters, poems, memoirs and even books. Even so, everything they wrote was checked by the governor. Prisoners were not allowed to write about the prison or to send secret messages. The masked prisoner was probably given books, as he had been in his earlier prisons. Some accounts say that he enjoyed reading and that he played the guitar. Historians cannot confirm these details. Yet it is likely that books were his companions during the quiet days and long evenings in the tower. In a life without conversation, reading allowed him to travel in his mind far beyond the stone walls.
===
IMG: guards-night-watch-bastille-ramparts-lantern | two soldiers in long coats and tricorn hats keeping night watch on the ramparts of a great stone fortress with round towers, a lantern glowing, the dark city with a few lit windows below, night
HEAD: Every night, soldiers kept watch on the walls and in the courtyards of the Bastille.
BODY: The Bastille was guarded by a small garrison of soldiers, called invalides, who were often older soldiers no longer fit for active service. Historians note that they kept watch day and night, on the walls, at the gates and in the courtyards. At night, the gates were locked, and the drawbridges were raised. The governor and his officers made regular rounds to check that everything was secure. Even so, life in the Bastille at night was usually quiet. The prisoners slept in their rooms, and the guards walked the walls under the stars. The bells of nearby churches marked the hours. Occasionally, prisoners called out or made noise, and the guards had to respond. The masked prisoner was a calm and quiet prisoner, who seems to have caused no trouble. In the silence of the night, the great fortress stood dark and still, its towers rising above the sleeping streets of the faubourg Saint-Antoine.
===
IMG: governor-office-registers-officers-1700 | a governor in a long wig and an officer in a military coat examining registers and papers at a large table in a fortress office around 1700, keys hanging on the wall, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm light
HEAD: As governor of the Bastille, Saint-Mars continued to guard his old prisoner personally.
BODY: At the Bastille, Saint-Mars was responsible for all the prisoners, the soldiers and the buildings. He had a staff of officers, including du Junca and Rosarges, as well as a doctor, a surgeon, a chaplain and servants. Historians note that he was now in his seventies, and his health was not always good. Yet he continued to take a personal interest in the masked prisoner. He made sure that his orders were followed exactly and that the prisoner's identity remained secret. Even so, he had many other prisoners to manage. The Bastille held people accused of all sorts of crimes, from political plots to writing forbidden books. Saint-Mars dealt with each of them according to the king's orders. He lived in the governor's house inside the Bastille with his family. He remained governor until his death in September 1708, five years after the masked prisoner. He was buried in the church of Saint-Paul.

TOPIC: November 1703
===
IMG: bastille-november-grey-sky-towers-rain | the grey stone towers of the Bastille under a low grey November sky with light rain falling, wet cobblestones and a few townspeople in cloaks hurrying past, soft dim light
HEAD: On Monday, the nineteenth of November 1703, the masked prisoner died in the Bastille.
BODY: On Sunday, the eighteenth of November 1703, the masked prisoner went to mass in the chapel of the Bastille as usual. Later that day, he felt ill. Historians know about his last days from du Junca's journal. The next day, Monday, the nineteenth of November, the prisoner died in his room in the Bertaudière tower, at about ten o'clock in the evening. Du Junca wrote that he died after a short illness, without having had the time to receive the last rites. He had confessed to the chaplain the day before. Even so, the cause of his death is unknown. He was probably in his sixties, and he had spent about thirty-four years in prison. Du Junca recorded the death in his journal with his usual brief style. He again described him as the unknown prisoner, who had always been masked with a mask of black velvet. Outside, Paris was cold and grey in the November rain.
===
IMG: priest-candle-deathbed-stone-room | a priest in a black cassock holding a candle beside a simple bed in a stone tower room, a figure lying still under a blanket, a crucifix on the wall, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft candlelight
HEAD: The chaplain of the Bastille was called to the prisoner's room on the night he died.
BODY: When the masked prisoner fell ill, the chaplain of the Bastille was called. His name was Abbé Giraut. Historians know from du Junca's journal that the chaplain had heard the prisoner's confession on the day before his death. When the prisoner's condition worsened, the chaplain came again, but the prisoner died before he could receive the last sacraments. Even so, the chaplain prayed for him. Historians have wondered whether the prisoner revealed his identity in his final confession. If he did, the chaplain was bound to keep it secret. Some later writers claimed that the prisoner had told his secret to the chaplain, but there is no evidence for this. The death of a prisoner in the Bastille was not unusual. Many prisoners died there over the years, from illness or old age. Yet this death would be remembered more than most. It was the end of a life spent almost entirely in silence.
===
IMG: saint-paul-church-cemetery-paris-1703 | the old Gothic church of Saint-Paul in the Marais district of Paris with a small walled cemetery beside it in 1703, a few gravestones and bare autumn trees, a small group in dark cloaks, grey morning light
HEAD: The prisoner was buried the next day in the cemetery of the church of Saint-Paul, near the Bastille.
BODY: On Tuesday, the twentieth of November 1703, the masked prisoner was buried in the cemetery of the parish church of Saint-Paul, not far from the Bastille. Historians know about the burial from du Junca's journal and from the church's burial register. The funeral took place in the afternoon. It was attended by a small number of officials from the Bastille, including Rosarges, the major of the fortress, and Reilhe, its surgeon. They signed the burial register. Even so, the funeral was quiet and simple. There were no family members or friends. The cost of the burial, forty livres, was paid by the Bastille. The cemetery of Saint-Paul was closed in the late eighteenth century, and its graves were moved. The prisoner's remains were lost. The church of Saint-Paul itself was destroyed during the French Revolution. Today, nothing remains to show where the most famous prisoner of the Bastille was buried.
===
IMG: burial-register-name-marchioly-quill | an old parish burial register open on a wooden church desk showing handwritten entries in faded brown ink, a quill and a candle beside it, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft candlelight
HEAD: In the burial register, the prisoner's name was written as Marchioly, aged about forty-five.
BODY: The burial register of the church of Saint-Paul recorded the masked prisoner under the name Marchioly. It gave his age as about forty-five years. Historians have studied this entry closely. The name resembles Mattioli, the Italian diplomat who had been held at Pignerol. However, Mattioli seems to have died in 1694. The age is also puzzling. If the prisoner had been arrested in 1669 as a grown man, he would have been at least in his fifties in 1703. Most historians believe that both the name and the age were deliberately false, chosen to hide the prisoner's real identity. Even so, the register is one of the few official documents about the prisoner. The original register was destroyed in a fire in 1871, during the Paris Commune. Fortunately, the entry had been copied by historians before the fire, so its words survive. They are the last official trace of the prisoner's life.
===
IMG: servants-burning-furniture-courtyard-fire | servants in plain clothes burning old wooden furniture, a mattress and bedding on a fire in a stone courtyard of a fortress, smoke rising into a grey sky, soldiers watching, dim afternoon light
HEAD: According to later accounts, everything the prisoner had used was burned after his death.
BODY: According to stories told later in the eighteenth century, the prisoner's belongings were destroyed after his death. His furniture, bedding and clothes were said to have been burned. The walls of his room were scraped and whitewashed, and even the floor tiles were replaced, so that no trace of him or any message he might have left would remain. Historians note that these stories cannot be confirmed by the documents. They may be exaggerations added to the legend. Even so, it was a common practice to burn the bedding and clothes of people who had died, to prevent the spread of disease. The story of the scraped walls made the mystery more dramatic. It suggested that the prisoner might have written his secret somewhere in his room. Over time, the legend grew. Readers imagined secret messages, hidden letters and clues that had been destroyed forever. Yet the real prisoner left almost nothing behind except a few lines in a journal.
===
IMG: paris-evening-lamplight-rooftops-1703 | the rooftops and church towers of Paris on a quiet November evening in 1703, smoke rising from chimneys, a few windows glowing with candlelight and the river Seine reflecting the last light
HEAD: After his death, the prisoner was soon forgotten by the officials, but not by the people of Paris.
BODY: After the burial, life in the Bastille continued as before. Du Junca returned to his journal, and Saint-Mars to his duties. Historians note that the officials of the Bastille did not write about the masked prisoner again. For them, the matter was closed. Even so, the story did not disappear. Soldiers, servants and other prisoners had seen the masked man, and they talked about him. Rumours spread through Paris. People said that a prisoner of royal blood had been kept in the Bastille with his face hidden. They wondered who he could have been. Over the following decades, the story was repeated in letters, memoirs and books. It became one of the great mysteries of the age. In 1711, the king's own sister-in-law, the Princess Palatine, wrote in a letter about a man who had been kept masked in the Bastille for many years. The legend of the Man in the Mask had begun.

TOPIC: Legends, guesses and the mask today
===
IMG: voltaire-writing-desk-quill-bastille-room | a thin young writer in a long wig and simple coat writing with a quill at a small table in a stone tower room in 1717, papers spread around him, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: The writer Voltaire, who was himself imprisoned in the Bastille, made the masked prisoner famous.
BODY: François-Marie Arouet, known as Voltaire, was one of the most famous writers of the eighteenth century. In 1717, as a young man, he was imprisoned in the Bastille for eleven months, for writing verses that mocked the government. Historians note that during his imprisonment, he heard stories about the masked prisoner from the guards and officers. Later, in 1751, he wrote about the prisoner in his book The Age of Louis the Fourteenth. He described a young, tall and handsome prisoner who wore a mask with an iron chin piece, held by steel springs. Even so, Voltaire did not reveal who he thought the prisoner was. Later, in 1771, he suggested that the prisoner was an older brother of Louis the Fourteenth, born to the queen Anne of Austria. Voltaire's writings made the story famous throughout Europe. They also gave it its most famous detail, the iron mask, which most likely never existed.
===
IMG: twin-brothers-portraits-royal-palace | two identical young noblemen in rich seventeenth century clothes and long dark wigs standing face to face in a gilded palace room, tall windows behind them, warm light
HEAD: In his novel, Alexandre Dumas made the masked prisoner the secret twin brother of the king.
BODY: In the nineteenth century, the French novelist Alexandre Dumas wrote a series of adventure novels about the musketeers. The last of these, The Vicomte de Bragelonne, was published between 1847 and 1850. Part of it tells the story of the Man in the Iron Mask. In Dumas's story, the prisoner is Philippe, the secret twin brother of Louis the Fourteenth, hidden away at birth to prevent a struggle for the throne. The musketeer Aramis tries to replace the king with his twin. Historians note that this idea was not invented by Dumas. Earlier writers had suggested it. Even so, Dumas made it famous. His story has been told again and again in plays and films, including a well known film from 1998. Historians do not believe the twin theory is true. There is no evidence that Louis the Fourteenth had a twin. Yet the idea remains the best known explanation of the mystery.
===
IMG: historian-desk-old-letters-magnifying-glass | a historian in a nineteenth century frock coat examining old handwritten letters with a magnifying glass at a desk piled with documents in a quiet library, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: Over the centuries, historians have suggested dozens of different people as the masked prisoner.
BODY: Since the eighteenth century, historians and writers have suggested more than fifty different people as the masked prisoner. Some believed he was a royal brother or son. Others believed he was Fouquet, the Duke of Beaufort, the Italian count Mattioli or a son of King Charles the Second of England. Historians note that most of these theories have been rejected, because the dates do not fit or the evidence is too weak. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, historians began studying the letters in the archives more carefully. Many came to agree that the masked prisoner was the man called Eustache Dauger in the letters. In 1903, the Scottish writer Andrew Lang wrote a book called The Valet's Tragedy, arguing that the prisoner was indeed a simple valet. Even so, two questions remained: the true identity of Eustache Dauger, and what he knew. The search for answers continues in the archives today.
===
IMG: nobleman-cavoye-family-house-paris-1660 | a fashionable young nobleman in an embroidered coat and lace collar leaving an elegant Paris town house in the 1660s, a carriage waiting, servants by the door, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Some historians believe Eustache Dauger was a nobleman who had fallen into disgrace.
BODY: One theory connects the prisoner to a real nobleman named Eustache Dauger de Cavoye. He came from a respected family, and his brother Louis was a friend of Louis the Fourteenth. Historians note that Eustache de Cavoye led a troubled life. He was involved in a scandal in 1659, when he and other young nobles held a wild party during Holy Week, which was considered a great offence. He later killed a young page in a quarrel and fell into debt. His family disowned him. Even so, documents suggest that he was imprisoned elsewhere, in a different prison, around the same time. Some historians believe that he cannot be the masked prisoner, while others think that the evidence is unclear. The theory remains popular, because the name is the same. Yet it does not explain why a disgraced nobleman would be guarded so secretly for so many years, or why he would serve as a valet.
===
IMG: cardinal-mazarin-portrait-palace-study | an Italian cardinal in red robes and a red skullcap seated in a richly furnished palace study with paintings and books around 1660, papers and a chest of coins on the table, warm golden light
HEAD: A recent theory links the prisoner to the secret finances of Cardinal Mazarin.
BODY: Cardinal Jules Mazarin was the chief minister of France during the childhood of Louis the Fourteenth. He died in 1661, leaving an enormous fortune. Historians note that much of his wealth had been gained in ways that were not entirely honest. In 2016, the American historian Paul Sonnino published a book suggesting a new explanation. He argued that Eustache Dauger was a valet who had worked for the treasurer of Mazarin, and who may have known how the cardinal's fortune was made. If this knowledge had become public, it could have embarrassed the king and his family. Even so, this theory, like the others, cannot be proven with certainty. Other historians have offered different explanations. Yet many agree on one point. The prisoner was probably not a prince or a twin, but an ordinary man who knew something dangerous. His secret, whatever it was, was more important than his name.
===
IMG: storming-bastille-1789-crowd-towers-smoke | a great crowd of Parisians in 1789 with muskets, pikes and tricolour cockades gathered before the stone towers of the Bastille, smoke drifting in the summer sky, dramatic afternoon light
HEAD: When the Bastille was stormed in 1789, people searched its records for the name of the masked prisoner.
BODY: On the fourteenth of July 1789, a crowd of Parisians attacked and captured the Bastille. It was one of the first great events of the French Revolution. Historians note that, at the time, the fortress held only seven prisoners. Even so, it was a powerful symbol of royal power, and its fall was celebrated throughout France. In the days that followed, people searched through the papers of the Bastille. Some hoped to find the name of the masked prisoner. According to some accounts, the page of the register for the year of his arrival was missing. This only added to the mystery. The Bastille was soon demolished, and its stones were used for building, including a bridge over the Seine. Today, the Place de la Bastille stands where the fortress once was. A column rises in the middle of the square, and lines on the ground show where some of the old walls stood. The fourteenth of July is France's national day.
===
IMG: museum-cell-sainte-marguerite-visitors-window | a vaulted stone prison cell inside an old island fortress with a barred window looking out over the blue sea, a small plaque on the wall and soft daylight falling across the empty floor, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a vaulted ceiling
HEAD: Today, visitors to the island of Sainte-Marguerite can stand in the cell where the masked prisoner lived.
BODY: Today, the island of Sainte-Marguerite can be reached by a short boat trip from Cannes. Visitors walk along its shady paths, swim in its clear water and visit the Fort Royal. The fort houses the Musée de la Mer, a museum of the sea, with objects from ancient shipwrecks found nearby. Historians note that the museum also preserves the old prison. Visitors can enter the cell where, according to tradition, the masked prisoner spent eleven years. It is a large vaulted room with thick walls and a window with iron bars looking out to sea. Even so, it is quiet and peaceful today, filled with sunlight and the sound of the waves. Nearby, the cells of the Protestant pastors are also open, with paintings and memorials. Many visitors stop at the barred window and look out at the sea, imagining the life of the prisoner who stood there more than three hundred years ago.
===
IMG: black-velvet-mask-museum-glass-soft-light | a black velvet mask resting on a cushion inside a glass museum display case in a quiet gallery with old stone walls, soft warm spotlight, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: More than three hundred years after his death, the identity of the masked prisoner is still unknown.
BODY: The masked prisoner died in 1703, more than three centuries ago. Yet his identity remains one of the great unsolved mysteries of history. Historians have studied every surviving letter, register and journal, and they have built many careful theories. Most now agree that he was the man called Eustache Dauger, arrested near Dunkirk in 1669. They agree that his mask was made of velvet, not iron, and that he was probably not a royal twin. Even so, they still do not know who he really was, or why he was kept hidden for so long. Perhaps the answer was never written down. Perhaps it was in papers that were lost or destroyed. The story continues to inspire books, films and visits to the places where he was held. From Pignerol to Exilles, from Sainte-Marguerite to the Bastille, the prisoner's long journey can still be followed. His secret, it seems, will stay with him forever.
