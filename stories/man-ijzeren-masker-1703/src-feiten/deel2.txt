TOPIC: The fortress of Pignerol
===
IMG: pignerol-fortress-alps-town-1670 | the stone fortress and keep of Pignerol on a hill above a small town with red roofs at the foot of the snowy Alps around 1670, soldiers at the gate, soft morning light
HEAD: Pignerol was a fortress town at the foot of the Alps, on the border between France and Italy.
BODY: Pignerol, called Pinerolo in Italian, is a town in the region of Piedmont, at the foot of the Alps. In the seventeenth century, it belonged to France. It had been captured by French troops in 1630 and was officially given to France in 1631. Historians note that it was an important military base, because it guarded a route through the mountains into Italy. The town was protected by strong walls and a citadel on a hill. Inside the citadel stood an old stone tower called the donjon, which was used as a state prison. Even so, Pignerol was far from Paris, more than seven hundred kilometres away. This made it a good place to hide important prisoners. Few people knew who was held there, and escape across the mountains would be very difficult. In 1696, France gave Pignerol back to the Duke of Savoy as part of a peace treaty. Its fortress was then demolished.
===
IMG: donjon-tower-pignerol-stone-walls-snow | an old square stone prison tower with small barred windows inside a fortress courtyard, snow on the roofs and the white Alps rising behind, soldiers in long coats below, pale winter light
HEAD: The prisoners at Pignerol were kept in the donjon, an old stone tower inside the citadel.
BODY: The prison at Pignerol was a tall stone tower called the donjon. It stood inside the citadel, surrounded by its own walls and guarded by soldiers. Historians know from letters and plans that it had several floors, with rooms for prisoners of different ranks. The most important prisoners had larger rooms with furniture, fireplaces and windows, while others had smaller cells. Saint-Mars and his family lived in a house nearby. Even so, life in the donjon was hard. In winter, snow covered the mountains, and the cold was severe. In summer, the rooms could be hot and stuffy. The windows were barred and partly covered, so that prisoners could see only the sky. Saint-Mars added extra doors and walls to make the cells more secure. Over the years, the donjon held several famous prisoners. The masked prisoner lived there for about twelve years, from 1669 to 1681.
===
IMG: mountain-valley-river-pignerol-summer | a green mountain valley with a river, chestnut trees, small farms and a stone bridge near Pignerol around 1670, peasants with mules on the road, the high Alps behind, bright summer light
HEAD: Around the fortress of Pignerol lay green valleys, chestnut forests and small mountain villages.
BODY: The land around Pignerol was beautiful. Green valleys stretched up into the Alps, with rivers, forests of chestnut trees and small villages. Farmers grew grapes, wheat and vegetables, and herded sheep and cows on the mountain pastures. Historians note that many people in the nearby valleys were Waldensians, members of an old Protestant community that had lived in the Alps for centuries. They spoke a local language and kept their own traditions. Even so, life in the region was not always peaceful. Armies often passed through, and the Waldensians suffered religious persecution at times. The people of Pignerol lived alongside the French soldiers of the fortress. They sold them food and wine and provided services. Some of them must have wondered about the mysterious prisoners in the tower. Yet they knew little about them. The prisoners themselves could see almost nothing of the green valleys around them.
===
IMG: prisoner-window-bars-looking-at-sky | a man in a plain shirt and waistcoat standing in a stone prison room looking up at the sky through a high barred window, a simple bed and table behind him, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft daylight
HEAD: The prisoner spent his days reading, praying and looking at the sky through a barred window.
BODY: Little is known about the daily life of Eustache Dauger at Pignerol. Historians can only guess from the letters and from what is known about other prisoners. He probably woke early, dressed and ate a simple breakfast. Saint-Mars brought him his meals and asked if he needed anything. He may have spent his days reading, praying and sitting by his window. Religious books were usually allowed, and the fortress chaplain could visit to hear confession. Even so, he had few ways to pass the time. He could not talk freely with his guards, and he had no companions. The letters mention that Saint-Mars once reported that the prisoner was quiet and patient, and that he asked for nothing. Louvois wrote back that he should be given what he needed. Year after year, the seasons changed outside his window, while his own life remained the same, enclosed by thick stone walls.
===
IMG: fortress-chaplain-hearing-confession-cell | an elderly priest in a black cassock sitting with a prisoner on a wooden bench in a plain stone room, a crucifix on the wall and a small candle, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: The prisoner was allowed to see a priest, but he was forbidden to talk about himself.
BODY: Religion was an important part of life in seventeenth century France, even for prisoners. Most prisoners were allowed to attend mass and confess their sins to a priest. Historians note that at Pignerol, the prisoner was allowed to confess a few times a year. However, the priest was chosen carefully, and the prisoner was warned not to speak about his past or his identity. Louvois ordered that the confessor should be someone who would not ask questions. Even so, some people later wondered whether the prisoner told his secret to a priest. Priests were bound by the seal of confession, which forbade them to reveal what they heard. If the prisoner did speak, his words remained secret. The prisoner's faith may have been a comfort during his long years of imprisonment. Later accounts describe him as a quiet and pious man, who spent much time reading religious books and praying.
===
IMG: fortress-doctor-examining-prisoner-bed | a doctor in a long dark coat and wig examining a pale prisoner lying in a simple wooden bed in a stone room of a fortress, a basin and bottles on a table, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft daylight
HEAD: When the prisoner fell ill, a doctor was called, but even he was watched carefully.
BODY: Over the years, the masked prisoner sometimes fell ill. When this happened, Saint-Mars wrote to the minister asking permission to call a doctor. Historians note from the letters that the prisoner's health was taken seriously. He was given medicine and care, and Saint-Mars reported regularly on his recovery. Even so, the doctor was not allowed to speak freely with him. Saint-Mars stayed in the room during the visits. Medicine in the seventeenth century was very different from today. Doctors treated many illnesses with bloodletting, which meant removing blood from the patient, and with herbal remedies, rest and special diets. They had little understanding of the causes of disease. Yet some treatments helped, and the prisoner survived many illnesses. The care shown to him suggests that the king wished him to stay alive. He was not meant to die in prison quickly. He was meant to stay hidden, silent and alive.
===
IMG: winter-alps-fortress-snow-smoke-chimneys | a snow-covered mountain fortress around 1675 with smoke rising from chimneys, soldiers clearing snow from the courtyard and the white peaks of the Alps under a pale winter sky
HEAD: Winters at Pignerol were long and cold, and the prisoners spent them close to the fire.
BODY: Pignerol lies at the foot of the Alps, and its winters were cold and snowy. From December to March, the mountains were covered with snow, and icy winds blew down the valleys. Historians note that the fortress needed large amounts of firewood to keep its rooms warm. Soldiers and servants carried wood to the prisoners' fireplaces every day. The prisoners spent much of the winter close to the fire, wrapped in warm clothes and blankets. Even so, some rooms were draughty, and the cold could be hard to bear. Letters mention requests for warmer clothes and extra blankets for the prisoners. In spring, the snow melted, and the rivers filled with water from the mountains. Flowers appeared in the valleys, and the days grew longer. For the prisoners, the changing seasons were one of the few ways to mark the passing of time. Each winter was much like the last one.
===
TOPIC: Fouquet and the valet
===
IMG: vaux-le-vicomte-chateau-gardens-fete-1661 | the grand French château of Vaux-le-Vicomte with formal gardens, fountains and statues during a summer evening festivity in 1661, elegant guests in silk, lanterns and fireworks in the sky
HEAD: In 1661, the king's finance minister, Nicolas Fouquet, held a magnificent party at his château.
BODY: Nicolas Fouquet was superintendent of finances, one of the most powerful positions in France. He was rich, clever and a great patron of the arts. He built a magnificent château called Vaux-le-Vicomte, south of Paris, with gardens by André Le Nôtre and paintings by Charles Le Brun. On the seventeenth of August 1661, Fouquet held a lavish party there for the young King Louis the Fourteenth. Historians describe it as one of the most splendid celebrations of the century. There was a feast, a play by Molière, music and fireworks. Even so, the party was a mistake. The king was angered by the display of wealth, which seemed greater than his own. He also suspected that Fouquet had grown rich by misusing public money. Advised by Colbert, Louis decided to remove him. The magnificent party was the beginning of Fouquet's downfall. Today, Vaux-le-Vicomte is open to visitors.
===
IMG: arrest-fouquet-nantes-musketeers-carriage | royal musketeers in blue tunics surrounding a closed carriage in a cobbled street of the old town of Nantes in 1661, a man in a dark coat being escorted to it, stone houses, morning light
HEAD: Three weeks after the party, Fouquet was arrested by d'Artagnan and his musketeers.
BODY: On the fifth of September 1661, during a royal visit to the city of Nantes, Fouquet was arrested. The arrest was carried out by d'Artagnan, captain of the king's musketeers. Historians know that the king planned it carefully and in secret, fearing that Fouquet might resist or escape. Fouquet was taken to prison, and his papers were seized. His trial lasted three years. He was accused of misusing public money and of plotting against the king. Even so, many people sympathised with him. Writers such as Madame de Sévigné and Jean de La Fontaine followed the trial closely and spoke up for him. In December 1664, the judges sentenced him to banishment from France. The king was not satisfied. He changed the sentence to life imprisonment. This was very unusual, since kings normally reduced sentences rather than making them harsher. Fouquet was sent to Pignerol, under the guard of Saint-Mars.
===
IMG: fallen-minister-writing-prison-room-books | an older man in a plain dark coat writing at a table covered with books and papers in a furnished stone prison room with a fireplace, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: Fouquet spent fifteen years in the fortress of Pignerol, reading, writing and praying.
BODY: Fouquet arrived at Pignerol in January 1665. He was given rooms in the donjon, where he was guarded by Saint-Mars. Historians note that his life in prison was strict at first. He was not allowed to write letters, receive visitors or have news of the outside world. Over the years, the rules became gentler. He was allowed books, writing materials and, later, letters to and from his family. He wrote religious works and translations. He also suffered from poor health, and he grew old in prison. Even so, his spirit did not break. He remained intelligent and dignified, and he spent much time in prayer. In 1679, his wife and children were finally allowed to visit him. Fouquet died at Pignerol in March 1680, after fifteen years in prison. Some people later suggested that Fouquet himself was the masked prisoner, but the letters show clearly that he died before the masked prisoner did.
===
IMG: two-valets-serving-master-prison-room | two servants in plain coats setting a table and folding clothes for an older gentleman in a furnished stone prison room, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm light
HEAD: In 1675, the masked prisoner was allowed to work as a valet for Fouquet.
BODY: Fouquet was allowed to have servants in prison to help him dress, clean and prepare his meals. One of his valets was a man named La Rivière. In 1675, Fouquet's other servant fell ill, and Louvois gave permission for Eustache Dauger to serve him as a second valet. Historians find this decision very strange. It seemed odd that a man with a dangerous secret should spend time with another prisoner. Louvois gave strict conditions. Dauger was never to be in the presence of anyone except Fouquet and La Rivière. Even so, the arrangement shows that Dauger was not kept in total isolation. For several years, he lived and worked alongside Fouquet. Some historians believe that Dauger was chosen because he was already a servant by profession. Others think it was simply a convenient way to keep him busy. Either way, the two prisoners came to know each other well.
===
IMG: two-prisoners-talking-quietly-fireside | two men in plain coats sitting by a fireplace in a stone room talking quietly, one older and grey, one younger, wooden chairs and a simple table, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: Louvois forbade Fouquet to tell anyone what the valet had done before his arrest.
BODY: In 1679, the king allowed Fouquet more freedom. He could walk in the fortress and meet another prisoner, the Count of Lauzun. However, Louvois wrote to Saint-Mars that Eustache Dauger must never be in the same room as Lauzun. He also asked Fouquet directly whether Dauger had told him about his past life. Historians consider this letter one of the most interesting clues. Louvois wanted to know if Dauger had talked about what he had done before coming to Pignerol. This suggests that the secret concerned something Dauger had seen or done before 1669. Even so, the letter does not say what it was. Fouquet's answer has not survived. Historians have wondered what the two men talked about during their long years together. Fouquet had known many secrets of the state. Dauger seems to have known at least one. Their conversations by the fireside will never be known.
===
IMG: hole-in-wall-chimney-prison-room-candle | a small hole broken through the stone wall beside a fireplace in an old prison room, loose stones on the floor and a candle on the hearth, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: After Fouquet died, Saint-Mars found a secret hole in the wall between two prisoners' rooms.
BODY: When Fouquet died in March 1680, Saint-Mars searched his rooms. He discovered a hole in the wall, near the chimney, which connected Fouquet's rooms to those of Lauzun. Historians know about this discovery from Saint-Mars' letters. The two prisoners had been secretly visiting each other for some time. Saint-Mars was worried. Lauzun might have met Dauger through the hole and learned his secret. Even so, Saint-Mars and Louvois took immediate action. They moved Dauger and La Rivière into a separate room in a lower tower and told Lauzun that both valets had been set free. Historians note that this was a lie, designed to make Lauzun believe that they were no longer important. In fact, the two valets were now held in even greater secrecy than before. From this moment, they were called the two gentlemen of the lower tower in Saint-Mars' letters.
===
IMG: pignerol-funeral-procession-church-1680 | a small funeral procession with priests in white and black, candles and a wooden coffin carried through a narrow street of a hillside town to a stone church in 1680, mourners in dark cloaks, grey morning light
HEAD: Fouquet's body was later taken to Paris and buried in his family's chapel.
BODY: After Fouquet's death in March 1680, his body was first placed in a church in Pignerol. About a year later, in 1681, it was taken to Paris and buried in the family chapel of the Convent of the Visitation of Sainte-Marie. His family had asked for this, and the king gave permission. Historians note that Fouquet's death was a great sadness for his family and friends, who had hoped he might one day be released. Even so, his memory lived on. His château at Vaux-le-Vicomte remained one of the most beautiful in France. Writers remembered him as a generous patron of the arts. Some later writers suggested that he had not really died, and that he was the masked prisoner. Historians have rejected this idea, since the letters clearly describe both men at the same time. Yet Fouquet's story is closely connected to the mystery. His valet became one of the most famous prisoners in history.

TOPIC: Lauzun and the Italian count
===
IMG: court-nobleman-elegant-hall-versailles | a small, elegant French nobleman in a rich embroidered coat and long wig bowing in a gilded hall of a palace around 1670, courtiers watching, warm candlelight
HEAD: The Count of Lauzun was a charming courtier who fell from the king's favour and was sent to Pignerol.
BODY: Antoine Nompar de Caumont, Count of Lauzun, was a nobleman from Gascony, in southwestern France. He was small, witty and ambitious, and he became a favourite of Louis the Fourteenth. Historians note that he caused a great scandal in 1670, when he planned to marry the king's cousin, Anne Marie Louise of Orléans, known as La Grande Mademoiselle. She was one of the richest women in Europe. The king first gave permission and then changed his mind. Even so, Lauzun continued to annoy the king and his ministers. In November 1671, he was arrested and sent to Pignerol, where he was guarded by Saint-Mars. He stayed there for ten years. He was a difficult prisoner, often angry and demanding. He was finally released in 1681, partly because La Grande Mademoiselle gave some of her lands to the king's son. He lived until 1723 and had many more adventures.
===
IMG: prisoner-digging-tunnel-candle-stones | a man in a shirt with rolled sleeves secretly digging through the stone floor of a prison room by the light of a small candle, loose stones and a hidden tool, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, dim candlelight
HEAD: Lauzun once tried to escape from Pignerol by digging a tunnel through the walls.
BODY: Lauzun was not a patient prisoner. He wanted to escape, and he spent months making plans. Historians know from Saint-Mars' letters that he secretly dug a passage through the walls and floor of his room, using whatever tools he could find. He hid the rubble and covered the hole. He also made a rope from strips of cloth. In 1676, he made his attempt. He climbed out through the passage and reached the fortress walls. Even so, he was caught before he could get away. A guard saw him, and he was taken back to his room. Saint-Mars was embarrassed and alarmed, and he made the fortress even more secure. Lauzun's attempt made Saint-Mars more careful with all his prisoners. Later, Lauzun found a way to visit Fouquet secretly, through the hole near the chimney. His restless energy was a constant worry for his jailer, who wished for a quieter prisoner.
===
IMG: italian-diplomat-portrait-turin-1678 | an Italian diplomat in a dark silk coat, white lace collar and long wig standing in a palace room with tall windows and maps on the wall in Turin around 1678, warm afternoon light
HEAD: In 1679, an Italian diplomat named Ercole Antonio Mattioli was secretly kidnapped and taken to Pignerol.
BODY: Ercole Antonio Mattioli was a minister of the Duke of Mantua, a small state in northern Italy. In 1678, he helped arrange a secret agreement to sell the fortress of Casale to France. Casale was an important fortress, and the sale would give Louis the Fourteenth great power in northern Italy. Historians note that Mattioli then betrayed the agreement. He revealed it to other courts, including Spain and Savoy, in return for money. Louis the Fourteenth was furious. In May 1679, Mattioli was lured to a meeting near Turin and kidnapped by French agents. He was taken secretly to Pignerol and placed under the guard of Saint-Mars. Even so, his disappearance was not completely secret. Some people at the courts of Italy suspected what had happened. Mattioli was held under a false name, and he was said to have become ill and confused in prison. He later became one of the main suspects in the mystery.
===
IMG: casale-fortress-river-po-italy | a large star-shaped fortress with stone walls beside a wide river in the green plains of northern Italy around 1680, soldiers on the ramparts and boats on the river, soft morning light
HEAD: The fortress of Casale was at the centre of the diplomatic scandal that cost Mattioli his freedom.
BODY: Casale Monferrato is a town on the river Po, in the region of Piedmont in northern Italy. In the seventeenth century, its fortress was one of the strongest in Italy. It belonged to the Duke of Mantua, who needed money. Historians note that Louis the Fourteenth wanted Casale because it would allow France to control the region and threaten Spanish lands nearby. After Mattioli's betrayal, the deal was delayed. Even so, France took control of Casale in 1681, after new negotiations. French troops occupied the fortress, and it remained in French hands until 1695. Then it was returned and its fortifications were destroyed. The story of Casale shows how dangerous diplomacy could be in that age. Secret agreements, betrayals and kidnappings were part of the competition between European states. For Mattioli, his involvement in the affair led to a long imprisonment, far from his home and family.
===
IMG: moody-prisoner-scratching-wall-cell | a thin, unshaven man in a worn coat scratching marks on the stone wall of a dim prison cell with a nail, a straw mattress on the floor, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, dim light
HEAD: Mattioli was a troubled prisoner, who wrote on his walls and argued with his guards.
BODY: According to Saint-Mars' letters, Mattioli did not accept his imprisonment calmly. He often argued with his guards and complained loudly about his treatment. Historians know that he sometimes wrote on the walls of his cell, and that he was said to have become confused and unwell. At one point, he was moved to share a room with a monk who was also mentally ill. Saint-Mars described him as difficult. Even so, Mattioli's fate was not forgotten by everyone. His family in Italy searched for news of him, and rumours of his kidnapping spread. He remained at Pignerol until 1694, when he was moved to the island of Sainte-Marguerite. He died there in April 1694, shortly after his arrival. Historians note that his death is recorded in the letters, which is important for the mystery. Since he died nine years before the masked prisoner, he could not have been the man buried in Paris in 1703.
===
IMG: two-lists-names-ledger-candle-quill | an open leather-bound ledger with handwritten names and dates beside a quill, an inkpot and a candle on a wooden desk in a dim fortress office, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: Because the masked prisoner was buried under a name like Mattioli, many people believed they were the same man.
BODY: When the masked prisoner died in 1703, he was buried under the name Marchioly. This name looks similar to Mattioli. Historians note that for many years, people believed the masked prisoner must have been Mattioli. The idea was supported by some writers in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. It seemed to explain the mask, since Mattioli was a foreign diplomat whose kidnapping had broken international law. Even so, most historians today reject this theory. Mattioli was a prisoner of the king for a known reason, and his kidnapping was not a deep secret. More importantly, the letters suggest that Mattioli died in 1694, while the masked prisoner lived until 1703. The use of a similar name may have been a coincidence, or a deliberate way of hiding the prisoner's identity. Some think the burial clerk simply wrote a name that was going around the prison. The puzzle of the name remains unsolved.

TOPIC: Exilles in the mountains
===
IMG: exilles-fort-mountain-valley-1681 | a stone mountain fort perched on a rocky hill above a narrow green Alpine valley with a river below, snowy peaks around it, around 1681, soft morning light
HEAD: In 1681, Saint-Mars was made governor of Exilles, a small fort high in the Alps.
BODY: In 1681, Saint-Mars was given a new post. He became governor of the fort of Exilles, in the Susa Valley in the Alps, west of Turin. Exilles was a small fortress on a rocky hill, guarding the road between France and Italy. Historians note that Saint-Mars took two prisoners with him: Eustache Dauger and La Rivière, the two valets of the lower tower. The other prisoners, including Mattioli, stayed behind at Pignerol. This choice suggests that the two valets were considered especially important. Even so, Exilles was a lonely place. It was far from any large town, and the winters were long and harsh. The fort was surrounded by high mountains, and snow could block the roads for months. Saint-Mars complained about the cold and the isolation. The two prisoners were kept in a tower, with their own guards. Today, the fort of Exilles has been rebuilt and is open to visitors.
===
IMG: alpine-valley-road-mules-snow | a long line of mules carrying supplies along a narrow mountain road in a deep Alpine valley around 1682, drivers in cloaks and hats, snow on the peaks above and pine trees below, cold clear light
HEAD: Supplies for the fortress of Exilles had to be carried up narrow mountain roads by mules.
BODY: Exilles was difficult to reach. Supplies of food, firewood, wine and other goods had to be carried up from the valley along narrow mountain roads. Historians note that mules were the main form of transport in the Alps, since they were sure-footed on steep and rocky paths. Mule drivers led long trains of animals, each carrying heavy loads. In winter, the roads were often blocked by snow, and the fort had to be stocked with supplies in advance. Even so, life in the valley continued around the fort. Villages along the river grew grapes, kept cows and goats and made cheese. Traders and soldiers passed through on their way between France and Italy. The fortress depended on these local people for its food and supplies. Few of them would have known anything about the two prisoners in the tower. For them, Exilles was simply the king's fort on the hill.
===
IMG: two-prisoners-tower-room-mountain-light | two men in plain coats in a small round stone tower room with narrow windows looking out at snowy mountains, two simple beds and a table, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, cold clear light
HEAD: At Exilles, the two valets lived together in one tower room, guarded day and night.
BODY: At Exilles, Dauger and La Rivière shared a room in a tower of the fort. Saint-Mars arranged it so that no one could hear them or see them. Historians know from his letters that the guards who watched them were not allowed to speak to them. Saint-Mars brought them their meals himself, as he had done at Pignerol. Even so, the two prisoners had each other for company. They had lived and worked together for years as Fouquet's valets. They may have talked, prayed and kept each other's spirits up. La Rivière was not in good health. He suffered from an illness that caused swelling, which the letters called dropsy. In January 1687, La Rivière died at Exilles. Dauger was now alone again. Historians note that his companion's death must have been a great loss. After almost twenty years of imprisonment, Dauger was left with only his jailer and his guards for company.
===
IMG: fort-exilles-winter-storm-snowdrifts | an old stone fort in the Alps buried in snowdrifts during a winter storm, a single soldier with a lantern at the gate, swirling snow and dark grey sky
HEAD: Saint-Mars complained in his letters that the winters at Exilles were bitter and long.
BODY: The winters at Exilles were especially hard. The fort stood more than eight hundred metres above sea level, and the mountains around it rose much higher. Heavy snow fell for months, and freezing winds blew through the valley. Historians know from Saint-Mars' letters that he found the climate difficult. He complained about the cold and about his own health. He asked to be moved to a warmer place. Even so, the prisoners suffered more than he did. Their tower room was cold, and they depended on firewood brought by the guards. La Rivière's illness may have been made worse by the harsh conditions. Saint-Mars wrote to Louvois about the prisoners' health and the difficulties of the fort. In 1687, after six years at Exilles, his request was granted. He was appointed governor of the islands of Sainte-Marguerite and Saint-Honorat, off the warm Mediterranean coast near Cannes. His prisoner would come with him.
===
IMG: sedan-chair-covered-oilcloth-mountain-road | a closed wooden sedan chair covered with dark oiled canvas carried by porters along a mountain road, escorted by mounted soldiers in cloaks, spring snow on the peaks, soft morning light
HEAD: In 1687, the prisoner was carried to the coast in a closed chair covered with oiled cloth.
BODY: In the spring of 1687, Saint-Mars moved from Exilles to the island of Sainte-Marguerite. He took Dauger with him. Historians know from his letters that the prisoner was carried in a sedan chair, a closed seat carried on poles by porters. The chair was covered with oiled cloth, so that no one could see inside. Saint-Mars wrote that this would keep the prisoner hidden. Even so, the arrangement nearly killed him. The chair was so tightly covered that the prisoner could hardly breathe. The journey took twelve days, through the mountains and down to the coast of Provence. When they arrived, the prisoner was very ill. Saint-Mars reported that he had suffered greatly. Historians note that this is one of the few times that the letters describe the prisoner's suffering. After his arrival, he slowly recovered. Some writers later said that he wore a mask on this journey, but the letters mention only the covered chair.
===
IMG: provence-coast-road-olive-trees-sea | a dusty road winding down through olive groves and pine trees towards the blue Mediterranean sea on the coast of Provence around 1687, a small group of riders far below, warm spring light
HEAD: The journey to the coast took the prisoner down from the cold Alps to the warm shores of Provence.
BODY: The route from Exilles to Sainte-Marguerite led through the mountains and down into Provence, in the south of France. Historians note that the travellers passed from snowy peaks and pine forests into warmer valleys with olive groves, vineyards and fields of lavender. They reached the coast near Cannes, which was then a small fishing village. Even so, the prisoner saw none of this beauty, hidden as he was inside the covered chair. The journey was long and exhausting for everyone. The porters had to carry the heavy chair over steep roads, and the soldiers rode alongside. They stopped at night in inns or military posts, where the prisoner was kept apart from other travellers. In late April 1687, the group reached the coast. From there, a boat carried them across the water to the island of Sainte-Marguerite. The prisoner's new home would be very different from the cold mountain fortresses.
===
TOPIC: The island of Sainte-Marguerite
===
IMG: sainte-marguerite-island-fort-blue-sea | a stone fort on the rocky shore of a pine-covered island in a calm blue Mediterranean sea near Cannes around 1690, small sailing boats nearby, the hills of the coast in the distance, bright sunlight
HEAD: Sainte-Marguerite is a small island covered with pine trees, just off the coast near Cannes.
BODY: The Lérins Islands lie in the Mediterranean Sea, about a kilometre from the coast near Cannes. The largest is Sainte-Marguerite, a long island covered with pine and eucalyptus trees today, with rocky shores and clear water. In the seventeenth century, it had a fortress called the Fort Royal, built to protect the coast from enemy fleets. The smaller island nearby, Saint-Honorat, was home to a monastery that had been founded in the fifth century. Historians note that Saint-Mars became governor of both islands in 1687. Even so, the island was not a paradise for prisoners. They were kept inside the fortress and saw little of its beauty. Yet the climate was much warmer than in the Alps. The sea breezes were pleasant, and the winters were mild. Today, the Fort Royal is home to a museum, and visitors can walk through its old prison cells.

HEAD: Saint-Mars built a new prison on the island, with thick walls and windows facing the sea.
BODY: When Saint-Mars arrived on Sainte-Marguerite, he found the existing prison unsuitable. He ordered the construction of a new prison building inside the fortress. Historians know from his letters that he designed it carefully. Each cell had very thick walls, and its window looked out over the sea, protected by three sets of iron bars. The cells were placed so that prisoners could not communicate with each other or with anyone outside. Even so, the cells were bright and airy, with views of the water and the sky. The masked prisoner was placed in one of these cells. Today, visitors to the Fort Royal can see the cell that tradition says was his. It is a large vaulted room with a barred window looking out towards the open sea. Many people visit it every year. Standing there, they can imagine the prisoner looking out at the endless blue water, year after year.
===
IMG: prisoner-mask-walking-fort-terrace-sea | a man in a dark cloak and black mask walking slowly on a stone terrace of an island fortress beside an officer, the blue sea and distant hills behind, warm afternoon light
HEAD: On the island, the prisoner was sometimes allowed to walk, but his face had to be hidden.
BODY: Some accounts say that on Sainte-Marguerite, the prisoner was allowed to walk in the fortress, always in the company of Saint-Mars. On these occasions, he wore his mask, so that no one could see his face. Historians note that these stories were written down long afterwards, and they may have been exaggerated. Even so, they suggest that the people of the island and the soldiers of the garrison were aware of a mysterious prisoner. Rumours began to spread along the coast of Provence. People said that a prisoner of great importance was held on the island, and that Saint-Mars treated him with unusual respect. Some said that the governor stood while the prisoner was seated, or that he spoke to him with his hat in his hand. These stories cannot be confirmed. Yet they show how the mystery grew. The masked prisoner was becoming a legend even during his own lifetime.
===
IMG: fisherman-silver-plate-rocks-shore | a fisherman in rough clothes and a cap standing on the rocky shore of an island holding up a silver plate he has found, his small wooden boat behind him, calm blue sea, soft morning light
HEAD: According to legend, the prisoner once scratched a message on a silver plate and threw it from his window.
BODY: One of the most famous stories about the masked prisoner was told by Voltaire. According to him, the prisoner once wrote a message on a silver plate with a knife and threw it out of his window. A fisherman found the plate on the shore and brought it to Saint-Mars. The governor asked him if he had read what was written on it. The fisherman said that he could not read, and Saint-Mars let him go, saying he was lucky. Historians do not believe that this story is true. There is no record of it in the letters, and similar stories were told about other prisoners. Even so, it became one of the best known parts of the legend. The silver plate suggested that the prisoner was used to fine things, perhaps a person of high birth. Voltaire also said that the prisoner wore fine linen and lace. These details made the mystery even more exciting for readers.

IMG: protestant-pastors-prison-psalm-cell | a Protestant pastor in a plain black coat sitting on a bench in a vaulted stone prison cell reading a small book, a window with iron bars and the sea outside, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a vaulted ceiling, soft light
HEAD: Later, several Protestant pastors were imprisoned in the cells next to the masked prisoner.
BODY: In 1685, Louis the Fourteenth cancelled the Edict of Nantes. This edict, signed by his grandfather Henry the Fourth in 1598, had given French Protestants, called Huguenots, the right to practise their religion. After 1685, Protestant worship was forbidden in France. Historians note that many Protestants fled abroad, while others worshipped secretly. Protestant pastors who continued to preach were arrested. Several of them were sent to Sainte-Marguerite and held in cells in the same prison as the masked prisoner. Even so, they were not allowed to meet him or speak with him. Some of the pastors were held for many years, and some went mad or died in prison. Their names are remembered today in the museum of the Fort Royal. Historians note that their faith and suffering are part of the history of the island. They sang psalms in their cells, and some of their songs may have been heard by the masked prisoner.
===
IMG: lerins-monastery-saint-honorat-monks-sea | an old stone monastery with a square fortified tower on the shore of a small island, monks in white robes walking among vineyards and pine trees, the blue sea around, soft evening light
HEAD: Across a narrow channel, the monks of Saint-Honorat continued their quiet life of prayer.
BODY: Just across a narrow channel from Sainte-Marguerite lies the island of Saint-Honorat. A monastery was founded there around the year 410 by Saint Honoratus, and monks have lived on the island almost continuously since then. Historians note that the monastery of Lérins was one of the most important religious centres of early Christian Europe. In the Middle Ages, the monks built a fortified tower to protect themselves from pirates. In the seventeenth century, the monks lived a quiet life of prayer and work, tending vineyards and gardens. Even so, they lived very close to the fortress and its prisoners. Some historians have wondered whether the monks knew anything about the masked prisoner. There is no evidence that they did. Today, monks still live on Saint-Honorat. They make wine and liqueurs and welcome visitors. The bells of their monastery can be heard across the water, just as they could be heard by the prisoners long ago.
===
IMG: sunset-sea-from-fort-sainte-marguerite | the sun setting over the calm Mediterranean sea seen from the stone walls of an island fortress, pine trees and a small fishing boat silhouetted in golden and rose light
HEAD: The masked prisoner lived on Sainte-Marguerite for eleven years, from 1687 to 1698.
BODY: The masked prisoner spent eleven years on the island of Sainte-Marguerite. Historians know little about his daily life there, but the letters show that he was well cared for. He continued to receive good food, clothing and books. He was visited regularly by Saint-Mars and sometimes by a doctor or a priest. Even so, his life remained one of total isolation. He could not speak freely, could not write letters and could not leave his cell without permission. Year after year, he looked out at the same sea and sky from behind his iron bars. The seasons changed, ships sailed past, and the sun rose and set over the water. In 1698, everything changed again. Saint-Mars was appointed governor of the Bastille in Paris. As always, the minister ordered that the old prisoner should go with him. After almost thirty years in the south, the prisoner was going to Paris.
