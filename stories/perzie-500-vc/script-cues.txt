{img:persepolis-500bc-terrace-palaces-columns-mountain-plain-morning}
Tonight we are going two and a half thousand years back, to the heart of the Persian Empire, in the year 500 before Christ.

[long-pause]

On a great stone terrace at the foot of a mountain, on a wide plain in the south of what is now Iran, a new city of palaces is rising. There are forests of tall stone columns. There are great staircases carved with long processions of people. And there are gateways guarded by winged stone bulls with human faces. The place is called Persepolis. It belongs to Darius, the King of Kings, ruler of the largest empire the world has ever known.

[pause]

Tonight we will walk slowly through his world. His palaces and gardens, his roads and messengers, and the ordinary people who built it all.

[pause]

If you like these slow journeys into the distant past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, and to come back for the next one.

[long-pause]

In the year 500 before Christ, the Persian Empire stretches from the borders of Greece in the west to the river Indus, in what is now Pakistan, in the east. It reaches from the deserts of Egypt in the south to the grasslands of Central Asia in the north.

[pause]

It is enormous. Some historians think almost half of all the people in the world live inside it. It includes what is now Iran, Iraq, Turkey, Syria, Egypt and Afghanistan, and much more besides.

[long-pause]

Inside it live dozens of peoples speaking dozens of languages. Persians and Babylonians, Arabs and Jews, Egyptians and Greeks, Indians and many more.

[pause]

And over all of them rules one man. The Great King, the King of Kings, Darius.

[pause]

{img:fars-province-green-valley-mountains-shepherds-flocks-spring}
The Persians come from a region in the southwest of Iran called Persis. That is where their name comes from. It is a land of high mountains, green valleys and wide plains. Summers are hot and winters are snowy. There are orchards and fields of wheat, and flocks of sheep and herds of horses.

[long-pause]

Only about seventy years before our story, the Persians were a small people, ruled by their neighbours. Then, in one man's lifetime, under a great king called Cyrus, they conquered one kingdom after another, and built an empire.

[pause]

How they did it, and how they ruled it, is one of the most remarkable stories of the ancient world.

[pause]

{img:persian-nobles-long-robes-cylindrical-hats-walking-colonnade-sun}
Even their enemies, the Greeks, remembered the Persians not only for their power and wealth. They remembered their good roads, their fair laws and their respect for other peoples and religions. They remembered their love of truth, of gardens, of horses and of beautiful things. But in the year 500, far to the west, among the Greek cities, trouble is quietly beginning to stir.

[long-pause]

{img:cyrus-the-great-king-portrait-calm-wise-robes-crown-mountains}
The founder of the Persian Empire was Cyrus the Great.

[pause]

He was born as a prince of a small kingdom in Persis. The Greeks later told many stories about his childhood. One story says his grandfather dreamed that the baby would one day take his throne. So he ordered the baby to be killed. But the servant who was told to do it gave the baby to a shepherd instead. The shepherd's wife had just lost her own baby. They raised Cyrus as their own son, in the mountains.

[long-pause]

When the boy was ten, the story goes, he was playing with the village children. They chose him as king in their game. He played the part so well and so seriously, giving orders and punishing a boy who disobeyed, that the truth came out. He was recognised, and sent back to his real parents.

[pause]

It is probably only a story. But it shows how people remembered him, as a born king.

[pause]

When he grew up, Cyrus rebelled against the people who ruled over the Persians, and won. Then he conquered the rich kingdom of Lydia in the west, whose king was famous for his gold. And in 539 before Christ he took Babylon, the greatest city in the world, almost without a fight.

[long-pause]

Cyrus was a different kind of conqueror.

[pause]

He did not destroy the cities he conquered, or carry off their people as slaves, as earlier kings had done. He respected their gods, their temples and their customs. He let them keep their own laws and languages, as long as they obeyed him and paid him tribute.

[pause]

{img:clay-cylinder-cuneiform-script-museum-glass-close-soft-light}
In Babylon, he had a message written on a clay cylinder, which still exists today in a museum in London. It says he entered Babylon in peace, and that the people welcomed him. And it says he allowed the peoples who had been carried away to Babylon to go home, and rebuild their temples.

[long-pause]

Among those peoples were the Jews, who had been taken to Babylon nearly fifty years before. Cyrus let them go home to Jerusalem and rebuild their temple. In the Bible, Cyrus is called the anointed one of the Lord. He is the only foreign king ever given that title.

[pause]

Cyrus died in 530 before Christ, fighting far away in Central Asia. He was buried in a simple, beautiful tomb of white stone. It looks like a little house with a pointed roof, standing on six great steps.

[pause]

{img:tomb-of-cyrus-pasargadae-white-stone-steps-plain-dawn}
The Greek writers say there was a message on the tomb: O man, I am Cyrus, who founded the empire of the Persians. Do not begrudge me this monument.

[pause]

Two hundred years later, Alexander the Great found the tomb robbed. He was saddened, and ordered it repaired. It still stands today on the empty plain, among the grass and wildflowers.

[long-pause]

{img:babylon-ishtar-gate-blue-glazed-bricks-procession-way-539bc}
In the autumn of 539 before Christ, the armies of Cyrus came to Babylon.

[pause]

Babylon was the greatest city in the world. It lay on the river Euphrates, with enormous double walls. They were so thick, the Greeks said, that a chariot with four horses could turn around on top of them. Its great gate was covered with blue glazed bricks, decorated with golden lions, dragons and bulls. And its huge stepped temple tower rose high above the city. Perhaps it was the Tower of Babel from the Bible.

[long-pause]

The king of Babylon was not popular with his people. He had spent ten years away in the Arabian desert, and missed the great yearly festival of the city's god. The priests and many people were unhappy.

[pause]

So when Cyrus came, there was little fighting. A Greek historian tells a famous story. The river Euphrates flowed right through the middle of the city. The Persians dug a channel and turned the river into a lake, so the water level dropped. Then, at night, during a great festival, the soldiers walked along the riverbed, under the walls, into the city. The Babylonians were dancing and feasting. They did not even know their city had been taken until it was all over.

[pause]

{img:euphrates-river-low-water-night-city-walls-quiet-festival-lights}
Cyrus's own clay cylinder tells it more simply. It says the god of Babylon chose Cyrus, and he entered the city without a battle. The people welcomed him and spread green branches before him.

[long-pause]

Cyrus treated Babylon with respect. He did not destroy it or rob it. He honoured its gods, its temples and its priests. He let the people go on living as before, and let the captives in the city go home. Babylon stayed one of the great royal cities of the Persian Empire.

[pause]

The Bible tells a famous story about Babylon's last night. The king's son held a great feast. Suddenly a mysterious hand appeared and wrote strange words on the palace wall. Nobody could read them, except a wise young man called Daniel. He explained them to the king. Your days are numbered, the words said. You have been weighed and found wanting. Your kingdom will be given to the Medes and the Persians.

[pause]

{img:feast-hall-babylon-lamps-wall-mysterious-writing-calm-guests}
Even today, when something is about to come to an end, people say: the writing is on the wall.

[long-pause]

{img:darius-the-great-king-throne-relief-beard-crown-sceptre-calm}
In the year 500 before Christ, the King of Kings is Darius. He is about fifty years old, and he has ruled for twenty-two years.

[pause]

He was not the son of a king. When Cyrus died, his son became king and conquered Egypt. When that son died, there was confusion and rebellion. A man claiming to be the dead king's brother seized the throne. Darius, a young nobleman and officer of the royal guard, joined six other Persian nobles. Together they overthrew the man, and Darius became king.

[long-pause]

A Greek writer tells a charming story about how Darius was chosen. The seven nobles agreed to ride out of the city at sunrise. Whoever's horse neighed first would be king. The night before, Darius's clever groom took his master's horse to the meeting place, together with a mare it was fond of. So in the morning, when they reached that spot, Darius's horse neighed at once. And Darius became king.

[pause]

It is probably only a story.

[pause]

In his first years, Darius had to fight many rebellions all over the empire. He won them all. He recorded his victories in a great carving high on a cliff in western Iran, written in three languages. Many centuries later, a British officer climbed that cliff on ladders and ropes to copy it. That carving helped scholars learn to read the ancient wedge-shaped writing of the Middle East.

[long-pause]

Darius is a great organiser. He divides the empire into provinces, each run by a governor, usually a Persian nobleman. He sets the tribute each province must pay, fairly, according to how rich it is. He brings in the same weights and measures everywhere. And he makes the empire's first gold coins. They show the king running with a bow and a spear.

[pause]

He builds roads and canals. One canal links the Nile to the Red Sea, so ships can sail from the Mediterranean to the Indian Ocean.

[pause]

{img:gold-daric-coins-king-archer-running-hand-close-light}
The Greeks called him the shopkeeper, because he cared so much about money and taxes and accounts. But it shows how well he ran his great empire.

[long-pause]

In his carved messages, Darius tells us what he believes. He says he is a friend of what is right, and not of what is wrong. He does not want the weak to be wronged by the strong, or the strong by the weak. He says he controls his anger.

[pause]

And above all, he says he hates the Lie and loves the Truth. For the Persians, the worst of all wrongs is to tell a lie.

[pause]

{img:king-darius-listening-petitioners-audience-hall-courtiers-calm}
And he says everything he has done, he has done by the will of the great god Ahura Mazda, the Wise Lord, who made the earth and the sky and people, and happiness for people. In the year 500, Darius feels secure. He does not yet know that a small rebellion in the far west is about to grow into a war that will last for years.

[long-pause]

{img:greek-doctor-democedes-treating-king-ankle-palace-chamber}
A Greek writer tells a story about a Greek doctor at the court of Darius.

[pause]

One day, while hunting, Darius jumped down from his horse and badly twisted his ankle. The Egyptian doctors of the court were thought to be the best in the world. They tried to put it right, but they twisted it so roughly that they made it worse. For seven days and nights, the king could not sleep because of the pain.

[long-pause]

Then someone remembered that among the prisoners there was a Greek doctor called Democedes. They found him and brought him to the king, still in his chains and rags.

[pause]

At first, Democedes said he was not a doctor. He was afraid that if he cured the king, he would never be allowed to go home. But Darius would not accept that, and he admitted it. He treated the ankle gently, the Greek way, with rest and soft bandages. The king slept at last, and in a few days he was well again.

[pause]

{img:king-giving-golden-chains-to-doctor-women-of-palace-gifts}
Darius was so grateful that, as a joke, he gave Democedes two pairs of golden chains, because he had come in chains. Democedes asked whether the king meant to double his misery. Darius laughed, and sent him to visit the queens. Each queen filled a golden bowl with gold coins and gave it to him. So many coins, the story says, that the servant following behind him, picking up the coins that fell, became rich too.

[long-pause]

Democedes became very rich. He lived in a fine house and ate at the king's table. But he still longed to go home to his city in southern Italy.

[pause]

So, the story says, he persuaded one of the queens to ask Darius to send ships to spy out Greece, with Democedes as a guide. When the ships reached Italy, Democedes slipped away and escaped home. He married the daughter of a famous wrestler, and never came back.

[pause]

{img:ship-sailing-west-italian-coast-greek-doctor-looking-shore}
It is a good story, and perhaps partly true. It shows that the Persian court welcomed clever people from everywhere and rewarded them richly. And it shows that the Greeks, even when they were treated very well, longed for their own homes and their own freedom. That longing for freedom will soon cause the Great King a great deal of trouble.

[long-pause]

{img:persepolis-great-staircase-gentle-steps-terrace-gate-of-all-nations}
About twenty years before our story, Darius began to build a new royal city in the heart of Persis, at the foot of a mountain.

[pause]

The Greeks later called it Persepolis, the city of the Persians. It was not a city where ordinary people lived. It was a great palace for ceremonies. Here the king received his people and celebrated the great festivals of the empire.

[long-pause]

First the builders made a great terrace, a huge stone platform. Part of it was cut out of the mountain, and part of it built up with enormous stone blocks, fitted together without mortar and held by iron clamps. It is about twelve metres high, and longer than four football fields.

[pause]

To reach the top, there is a great double staircase with a hundred and eleven steps. The steps are so wide and so shallow that nobles in long robes could climb them slowly and with dignity. Some say even horses could walk up.

[pause]

At the top stands the Gate of All Nations, built a little later by Darius's son. Its doorways are guarded by huge stone bulls with wings and the heads of bearded men wearing crowns. They are taller than two men, and they look out over the plain.

[long-pause]

Beyond the gate are the palaces.

[pause]

The greatest is the great audience hall of Darius, where he receives his people. It is a huge square hall with thirty-six columns, each nearly twenty metres tall. They are slim and grooved. On top of each column sit two carved bulls, or lions, or griffins, back to back. They hold up the great cedar beams of the roof. More columns stand in porches around the hall.

[pause]

Seventy-two columns in all. Today only a few are still standing. But they still rise tall and slender against the blue sky of Iran.

[long-pause]

The walls are made of mud brick, plastered, painted and hung with rich cloth. The doorways are of stone, polished so smooth they shine like mirrors. The roofs are of cedar from Lebanon. Everything is decorated with gold, silver, ivory and bright paint.

[pause]

There are other palaces too. There is the private palace of Darius, a hall of a hundred columns, and a treasury where the riches of the empire are kept. There are palaces for the queens, storerooms and rooms for the guards.

[pause]

{img:persepolis-treasury-courtyards-columns-guards-storerooms-light}
In the year 500, much of it is still being built. Thousands of workers from all over the empire are working on it. Stone cutters and carpenters, sculptors, goldsmiths and painters. It will take three generations and nearly a hundred years to finish.

[long-pause]

A few kilometres away, Darius has already chosen the place for his own tomb. It is cut high into the face of a great cliff. Its front is carved like the front of a palace, in the shape of a cross, with a door leading to a burial room inside the rock. Above the door, a carving shows the king standing before a fire altar, worshipping his god, held up on a great throne by all the peoples of the empire.

[pause]

{img:naqsh-e-rostam-royal-tombs-cliff-cross-shaped-facades-sunset}
Darius chose this place himself and had it carved years before his death. That way he would always look out over the plain, toward his city. Even a king who rules half the world knows his time will one day end.

[long-pause]

{img:behistun-cliff-inscription-three-languages-relief-high-rock}
How do the Persians write?

[pause]

The Persian Empire is full of languages and kinds of writing. In his great carved messages, Darius uses three languages side by side. Old Persian, the language of the Persians themselves. Elamite, the language of an old kingdom nearby. And Babylonian, the ancient language of Mesopotamia. All three are written in wedge-shaped signs, pressed into clay with a reed or carved into stone.

[long-pause]

Old Persian writing is special. It may have been invented in the time of Darius himself, just for royal messages. It is simple, with only about thirty-six signs for sounds, and a few signs for whole words, like king and country. It is used almost only for royal carvings.

[pause]

But for everyday business, the empire uses another language and another way of writing. That language is Aramaic, from Syria. It is written with an alphabet, in ink, on leather or papyrus. It is quick and easy to write.

[pause]

{img:scribes-writing-aramaic-ink-parchment-and-clay-tablets-office}
Aramaic becomes the common language of the empire, used from Egypt to India by officials, merchants and travellers. Five hundred years later, in the time of Jesus, it will still be the everyday language of his land. Jesus himself will speak it.

[long-pause]

In the government offices there are many writers, called scribes. They write the king's orders, letters and accounts in all these languages, and translate between them. Every order of the king is copied and sent out by royal messengers to every province, in every language.

[pause]

The Bible says that the king's letters were sent to every province in its own script, and to every people in its own language.

[pause]

{img:clay-tablet-cuneiform-wedges-reed-stylus-close-lamplight}
For two thousand years after the Persian Empire ended, nobody could read this wedge-shaped writing. The knowledge was completely lost. Then, two hundred years ago, scholars began to crack it. They started with Old Persian, the simplest, using the carvings at Persepolis and the great cliff carving in three languages.

[long-pause]

A young German schoolteacher made the first breakthrough, as a bet with a friend. He guessed that the carvings on the palaces would begin with the names and titles of kings. Something like: so-and-so, great king, king of kings, son of so-and-so. He guessed which kings they might be, Darius and his son. And he was right.

[pause]

And so, after two thousand years of silence, the voices of the Persian kings could be heard again.

[long-pause]

{img:apadana-staircase-relief-delegations-gifts-procession-carved}
Many historians believe that every spring there was a great festival at Persepolis. It was the festival of the New Year, at the spring equinox around the twenty-first of March, when day and night are equal and the sun begins to win over the dark. The Persians call it Nowruz, which means new day.

[long-pause]

On the great staircases of the audience hall there are carvings that may show this festival. Long processions of people, carved in stone, walk up the stairs toward the king.

[pause]

On one side are the Persians and their neighbours the Medes. There are nobles and guards in long flowing robes or riding clothes, with bows and spears and tall hats. They chat with each other. Some turn around to talk to the man behind. Some hold flowers. Some hold hands, or put a hand on a friend's shoulder. They look relaxed and friendly and happy.

[pause]

On the other side come the peoples of the empire. Twenty-three groups, each led by a Persian guide who holds the hand of the group's leader. Each group wears its own clothes, and brings gifts from its own land.

[long-pause]

The Babylonians bring a bull with a hump, and cloth. The Lydians from Turkey bring golden bowls and a little chariot. The Arabs bring a camel. The Indians bring baskets of gold dust and a donkey. The Ethiopians bring an elephant's tusk and a strange animal like a giraffe. People from Afghanistan bring a camel with two humps. The Egyptians bring a bull. The Greeks bring cloth and bowls.

[pause]

Every person in the procession is carved in fine detail. Their faces and beards, their clothes and shoes, the folds of their robes. It is one of the greatest works of art of the ancient world.

[pause]

{img:carved-ethiopian-delegation-tusk-okapi-relief-stone-afternoon}
And what is striking is how peaceful it is. Nobody is in chains. Nobody is being beaten. The people are not shown as defeated prisoners, as on many other ancient monuments. They walk calmly and with dignity, side by side, all the peoples of the world coming together to honour the king at the New Year.

[pause]

But not every people in the empire feels as happy as the carvings suggest. On the Greek coast, some cities are growing tired of foreign rule.

[long-pause]

At the top of the stairs, in the great hall, the king sits on his throne with a lotus flower in his hand. The crown prince stands behind him, with officials and guards and smoking incense. He receives the gifts and the greetings of his peoples.

[pause]

And afterwards, perhaps, there is a great feast with thousands of guests, music and wine, in the spring sunshine.

[pause]

{img:nowruz-table-spring-sprouts-apples-garlic-coins-goldfish-candles}
The festival of Nowruz is still celebrated today, two and a half thousand years later, by hundreds of millions of people in Iran, Afghanistan, Central Asia and far beyond. People clean their houses, wear new clothes and visit their families. They set a special table with seven things whose names begin with the letter S in Persian, all symbols of spring and new life. There is a bowl of goldfish, a mirror, candles, painted eggs and green shoots of wheat.

[long-pause]

{img:royal-road-persian-messenger-galloping-relay-station-plain-dust}
To hold his great empire together, Darius needs roads. And the Persians build the best roads of the ancient world.

[pause]

The most famous is the Royal Road. It runs for about two thousand seven hundred kilometres, from the old city of Sardis near the coast of the Aegean Sea in the west, all the way across Turkey and Mesopotamia, to the royal city of Susa in Iran.

[long-pause]

Along the road, about every day's walk, there are royal stations. They are inns with stables, food, water and guards. A Greek writer counted a hundred and eleven of them. Ordinary travellers on foot need about three months to walk the whole road.

[pause]

But the king's messengers are much faster.

[pause]

At each station, fresh horses and fresh riders are waiting, day and night. A messenger gallops to the next station and hands the message to a new rider, who gallops on to the next. It is like a relay race. In this way, a message from the king can travel the whole Royal Road in about seven days.

[pause]

The Greek writer admired this very much. Nothing in the world travels faster than these messengers, he wrote. Neither snow nor rain, nor heat nor the dark of night, stops them from finishing their journey as fast as they can.

[long-pause]

Two and a half thousand years later, the post office of New York carved those words above the door of its great main building. Many people think of them as the motto of the American postman.

[pause]

The messengers carry the king's orders, letters and reports, sealed with the royal seal.

[pause]

{img:scribe-writing-aramaic-parchment-seal-clay-office-lamp}
Travellers on the royal roads carry a travel pass, sealed by an official. It says who they are, where they are going and how much food they should get at each station. Some of these passes have survived. They say things like: give him each day so much flour, so much wine and so much beer, and food for his horses, until he reaches the king.

[long-pause]

There are other royal roads too, all over the empire, to the north, to Babylon, to India and to Egypt.

[pause]

Along them travel not only the king's messengers, but soldiers and officials, merchants with caravans of donkeys and camels, pilgrims, craftsmen on their way to build the king's palaces, and families moving house. Even the whole royal court travels along them, moving from one capital to another with the seasons.

[pause]

{img:caravan-on-persian-road-mules-camels-mountains-poplar-trees}
The roads bring the empire together. Goods and ideas, people and religions travel more easily than ever before. But roads carry bad news too. And soon, the fastest messengers in the world will be galloping east with news the king does not want to hear.

[long-pause]

{img:satrap-palace-sardis-governor-receiving-officials-courtyard}
How does Darius rule an empire so big that it takes three months to walk from one end to the other?

[pause]

He divides it into about twenty great provinces. Each one is ruled by a governor, usually a Persian nobleman, often a relative of the king. The governor lives in a palace in the capital of his province, almost like a little king, with his own court, his own guards and his own garden.

[long-pause]

The governor collects the taxes and sends them to the king. He keeps order, settles quarrels and looks after the roads. He makes sure the king's orders are carried out.

[pause]

But the king does not trust anyone completely. So in each province there is also a royal secretary and an army commander, who report directly to the king and keep an eye on the governor. And there are inspectors who travel around the empire for the king. They visit the provinces, see what is happening and report back. The Greeks call them the Eyes of the King and the Ears of the King.

[pause]

{img:royal-inspector-arriving-provincial-town-escort-seal-officials}
In this way the king knows what is happening everywhere. No governor can grow too powerful or rebel without the king hearing about it. At least, that is the idea.

[long-pause]

The peoples of the empire keep their own laws, customs, languages and gods, as long as they obey the king, pay their tribute and send soldiers when needed.

[pause]

Darius even collects the laws of the different peoples and has them written down. In Egypt, he orders the wise men to write down the old Egyptian laws, so the Persian officials can judge the Egyptians by their own laws. Later, the Egyptians remember Darius as one of their wise lawgivers.

[pause]

{img:egyptian-scribes-copying-laws-persian-official-listening-temple}
And the king himself is the highest judge. Anyone in the empire can ask the king for justice. A Greek writer tells the story of a judge who took a bribe. He was punished severely. Then the king made the judge's son a judge in his place, and told him to remember his father every time he judged a case.

[long-pause]

It is a system that works. For two hundred years, the Persian Empire holds together, with all its peoples, languages and religions, more peacefully than most empires before or after it. Later empires, like the Romans, will learn from it and copy it.

[long-pause]

{img:clay-tablets-elamite-cuneiform-ration-records-archive-room}
Who built Persepolis? And how did they live?

[pause]

About ninety years ago, archaeologists digging at Persepolis found thousands of little clay tablets inside a wall. They were written in wedge-shaped signs. They turned out to be the records of the royal offices, from exactly the time of our story.

[long-pause]

They are not grand carvings about kings and battles. They are ordinary records. Receipts, lists and accounts of the food handed out from the royal storehouses to workers, officials, travellers and animals.

[pause]

And they tell us, in wonderful detail, about the ordinary people who built the palaces.

[pause]

The workers come from all over the empire. Persians, Babylonians, Egyptians, Greeks, Indians and many others. Some are skilled craftsmen. Stone cutters, carpenters, goldsmiths and weavers. Others are labourers. They work in groups, sometimes hundreds strong, under a foreman.

[long-pause]

They are paid not with money, but with food. Each month they get rations of barley or wheat, and wine or beer, and sometimes sheep and fruit, depending on their work and skill.

[pause]

And here is something remarkable. Women work too, as craftsmen, and they are paid. In some jobs, skilled women are paid the same as men, or even more. Some women lead work groups, and earn more than the men in their group.

[pause]

When a woman worker has a baby, she gets an extra ration of grain, wine and meat to help her. And for some months, while she is not working, she still gets a ration. It is a kind of maternity pay, two and a half thousand years ago. The workers' children get small rations too.

[long-pause]

The tablets also record people travelling across the empire. A group of workers coming from Egypt. An official on his way to India with his servants and a sealed travel pass. One of the king's wives, who has her own lands and her own workers, sending orders. A man taking horses to the king.

[pause]

They record the feeding of the king's horses, camels and cattle, and the grain given to priests for offerings to the gods.

[pause]

{img:stone-cutters-workshop-carving-column-capital-persepolis-dust}
So thanks to these little tablets, we know the names of thousands of ordinary people from two and a half thousand years ago. We know how much bread they ate and how much wine they drank, where they came from and where they were going.

[long-pause]

In a great carving about building his palace at Susa, Darius tells us where everything came from.

[pause]

The cedar wood came from Lebanon. The gold from Lydia and Afghanistan. Blue and red precious stones from Central Asia. Silver and ebony from Egypt. Ivory from Ethiopia and India.

[pause]

{img:craftsmen-many-nations-working-palace-susa-gold-ivory-cedar}
The stone cutters were Greeks and Lydians. The goldsmiths were Medes and Egyptians. The brick makers were Babylonians.

[pause]

It seems the whole world came together to build the palaces of the King of Kings. The question is whether the whole world will stay together.

[long-pause]

{img:pazyryk-style-carpet-red-borders-horsemen-deer-knotted-wool}
The Persians are famous all over the world for their carpets. And the oldest carpet in the world may come from the time of our story.

[pause]

In 1949, a Russian archaeologist was digging in frozen tombs high in the mountains of Siberia. In one tomb, which had been filled with ice for nearly two and a half thousand years, he found a carpet, almost perfectly preserved.

[long-pause]

It is a knotted carpet, just like a modern Persian carpet. It is about two metres square, made of wool, with more than a million knots. Its colours are deep red, blue, yellow and green. In the middle there is a pattern of squares with stars. Around it run borders. In one border, deer are grazing. In another, horsemen ride and walk beside their horses, like the processions on the walls of Persepolis.

[pause]

Nobody knows exactly where it was made. Some think the nomads of the north made it themselves. But many experts think it was made in Persia, and traded far to the north.

[pause]

{img:weaver-women-carpet-loom-knotting-wool-courtyard-sunlight}
To make a knotted carpet, weavers stretch long threads on a loom. Then they tie little knots of coloured wool around them, one by one, row after row, and cut the ends short to make the soft surface. A good carpet can have hundreds of knots in every square centimetre. A family of weavers working together may need months or even years to finish one.

[long-pause]

The wool comes from mountain sheep and is spun by hand. The colours come from plants and insects. Red comes from the root of a plant, and from tiny insects. Blue comes from indigo. Yellow comes from saffron. Brown comes from walnut shells.

[pause]

The nomads, moving with their flocks, make carpets too. They use little looms they can roll up and carry on their camels. They make carpets for the floors of their tents, and bags and covers for their animals, with bold patterns that show where they come from.

[pause]

{img:nomad-tent-interior-carpets-cushions-family-tea-evening}
In the palaces of Persepolis, the floors are covered with carpets and the walls with hangings of wool, silk and gold thread. The king sits on carpets, walks on carpets and gives carpets as gifts.

[pause]

Two and a half thousand years later, Persian carpets are still among the most beautiful things made by human hands, and they are found in homes all over the world.

[long-pause]

{img:persian-paradise-garden-four-water-channels-fruit-trees-pavilion}
The Persians love gardens more than anything.

[pause]

All over the empire, the king, the governors and the nobles make great gardens. They are walled parks with trees and flowers, streams and fountains, little garden houses and wild animals. The Persians call such a garden a pairidaeza, which means a walled place. The Greeks heard the word and turned it into paradeisos. And from that comes our word paradise.

[long-pause]

The most famous kind of Persian garden is a square garden divided into four parts. Two water channels cross each other in the middle, where there is a pool or a fountain or a little pavilion. It is a picture of the world with its four quarters, and of paradise itself.

[pause]

At the old capital of Cyrus, archaeologists have found the remains of such a garden. There are stone water channels with little basins along them, where cool, clear water once flowed among the trees and flowers. It is the oldest garden of this kind we know of.

[pause]

The gardens are full of trees. Tall, dark cypresses and broad, shady plane trees. Pomegranates and apricots, peaches and figs, almonds and walnuts and vines. And flowers. Above all roses, which the Persians love, and lilies, tulips and jasmine.

[long-pause]

A Greek writer tells a story about a Spartan general who visited a Persian prince in his garden, about a hundred years after our story. The Spartan admired the trees, planted in perfect rows, and the beauty and the scent of the garden. He asked who had planned it. I planned it all myself, said the prince, and I planted some of the trees with my own hands. The Spartan looked at the prince's fine clothes and jewels, and was astonished. And the prince swore that he never sat down to dinner without first working in his garden until he sweated.

[pause]

{img:prince-planting-tree-garden-rows-cypress-visitor-admiring}
The Persian kings plant trees as a duty and a pleasure. Darius even writes to one of his governors to praise him for planting fruit trees from far away in his province.

[long-pause]

The great parks are also hunting parks, full of deer, wild boar and even lions. There the king and his nobles hunt on horseback with bows and spears.

[pause]

And in the summer heat, the king, his family and his court sit in the shade of the garden houses by the cool water. They eat fruit and drink wine, and listen to music and poetry, to the birds singing, the fountains splashing and the wind in the poplar leaves.

[pause]

{img:shaded-pavilion-garden-fountain-courtiers-fruit-afternoon-calm}
For the Persians, a garden is the most beautiful thing in the world. It is a little piece of paradise on earth, made by human hands, in the middle of a dry and dusty land.

[long-pause]

{img:persian-king-hunting-chariot-bow-lions-paradise-park-relief}
The favourite pastime of the Persian kings and nobles is hunting.

[pause]

In the great hunting parks, in the mountains and on the plains, they hunt deer and gazelles, wild boar, bears, leopards and lions. They hunt on horseback with bows and spears at full gallop, and in chariots. A Persian seal from the time of Darius shows the king in his chariot, shooting arrows at a lion rearing up in front of his horses.

[long-pause]

Hunting is training for war. It teaches young men to ride hard over rough ground, to shoot straight from a galloping horse, and to be brave and quick and work together.

[pause]

After their gardens, the Persians love horses more than anything. The finest horses in the world come from a great plain in the west of Iran. They are tall, strong, fast and beautiful, often white or golden or dappled. A Greek writer says fifty thousand mares graze on that plain, to breed them.

[pause]

{img:nisaean-horses-white-golden-grazing-green-plain-mountains}
Sacred white horses of this breed pull an empty chariot for the god Ahura Mazda in the royal processions, just ahead of the king. The king's own chariot is always pulled by these horses. And the king gives them as the most precious of gifts to his friends and to foreign kings.

[long-pause]

Every Persian boy learns to ride from the age of five. Persian horsemen ride without stirrups, which have not been invented yet. They grip the horse with their knees. Their trousers protect their legs. They can even shoot backwards while galloping away.

[pause]

The Persians also love polo, the game played on horseback with sticks and a ball. It may have been invented in Persia a little later. Persian kings, princes and even princesses play it for centuries. Two thousand years later, the great square of the city of Isfahan will have stone goalposts for polo at each end.

[pause]

{img:early-polo-game-riders-sticks-ball-plain-persian-dust}
In the evenings after the hunt, the king and the nobles feast on the game they have caught, roasted over the fire. They drink wine and tell stories of the day, of the great hunts of the past, and of the great horses they have known.

[long-pause]

{img:lion-attacking-bull-relief-persepolis-stone-carving-sunlight}
On the walls and staircases of Persepolis, one carving appears again and again. A lion leaps onto the back of a bull and bites it.

[pause]

Nobody knows exactly what it means. Some think it is a sign of the New Year. The lion stands for the sun and summer. The bull stands for winter. At the spring festival, the lion wins. Others think it shows the power of the king. Either way, it is carved so beautifully and so full of life that it is one of the most famous pictures of ancient Persia.

[long-pause]

The Persians love animals in their art, both real and imagined.

[pause]

Lions are everywhere, on walls, jewellery, seals and cups. In those days there were still real lions in Persia, and the kings hunted them. There are bulls and horses, wild goats with great curling horns, eagles and rams.

[pause]

{img:griffin-capital-persepolis-column-eagle-head-lion-body-carved}
And there are imaginary animals. Griffins have the body of a lion and the head and wings of an eagle. Winged bulls with the heads of men guard the gates. And there is a great magical bird that brings good luck to anyone it flies over. On top of some columns at Persepolis sit two griffins or two bulls, back to back, holding up the roof beams.

[long-pause]

In the palace gardens, the kings keep real animals from all over the empire. Deer and gazelles, ostriches and peacocks, camels and elephants from India. There are even cheetahs, trained to hunt.

[pause]

And out in the Persian countryside live wolves and bears, foxes and hares, hedgehogs and porcupines. Storks nest on the rooftops. Little hoopoes hop about with their crests. And at night, nightingales sing in the gardens. Persian poets will write about those nightingales for thousands of years.

[long-pause]

{img:fire-altar-mountain-top-priests-white-robes-mouth-veils-dawn}
The Persians worship a great god called Ahura Mazda, the Wise Lord, the creator of all good things.

[pause]

Their religion comes from a prophet called Zarathustra. The Greeks called him Zoroaster. Nobody knows exactly when he lived. Perhaps around a thousand years before Christ, somewhere in the east of Iran or in Central Asia. He was a priest and a poet who had visions of the Wise Lord. He taught people about him in hymns that are still recited today.

[long-pause]

Zarathustra taught that the world is a battlefield between good and evil. On one side stands the Wise Lord, the god of light, truth and goodness. On the other side stands a destroying spirit, the spirit of darkness and lies.

[pause]

Every person must choose which side to be on. And the way to choose good, Zarathustra said, is simple. Good thoughts, good words, good deeds.

[pause]

At the end of time, he taught, good will win. The dead will rise and be judged, and the world will be made new and perfect. Evil will be no more.

[pause]

Some of these ideas, about heaven and hell, a last judgement and angels, may later have influenced the Jews who lived in the Persian Empire. And through them, perhaps, Christianity and Islam too.

[long-pause]

For the Persians, the most sacred things are fire, water, earth and air. They are pure, and must not be made dirty.

[pause]

Fire is the sign of the Wise Lord, of truth and light. In their temples, the Persian priests keep sacred fires burning day and night, for centuries. They never let them go out. When they tend the fire, they cover their mouths with a white cloth, so their breath will not make it impure. The Persians also worship in the open air, on mountaintops, under the sky.

[pause]

{img:sacred-flame-fire-temple-dim-interior-priest-veil-tending}
A Greek writer says the Persians do not make statues of their gods, as the Greeks do. Instead, they climb the highest mountains and make offerings to the whole circle of the sky, to the sun and the moon, to fire and water and the winds.

[long-pause]

The Persian priests are called the Magi. They are wise men who know the old prayers and rituals, study the stars and explain dreams. Five hundred years later, the Bible says, wise men from the East will follow a star to Bethlehem to see the baby Jesus. They are called Magi too.

[pause]

Rivers are sacred as well. The Persians never wash their hands in a river or spit in it.

[pause]

{img:clear-mountain-river-persian-landscape-poplars-sacred-calm}
And truth is sacred. A Greek writer says Persian boys learn three things from the age of five to twenty. To ride a horse, to shoot a bow and to tell the truth. The most shameful thing of all, the Persians think, is to tell a lie. The next most shameful is to owe money. Because someone who owes money will be tempted to lie.

[long-pause]

The religion of Zarathustra is still alive today. There are still Zoroastrians in Iran, around the city of Yazd, where they say a sacred fire has been burning for fifteen hundred years. And there are many in India, where they are called Parsis. Their ancestors fled from Persia more than a thousand years ago. They still keep the sacred fires, say the old prayers, and try to live by the old rule. Good thoughts, good words, good deeds.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonian-astronomers-ziggurat-roof-night-stars-clay-tablets}
In Babylon, which is part of the Persian Empire, live the best star-watchers in the world.

[pause]

For hundreds of years, every night, the priests of Babylon have climbed to the top of the great temple tower and watched the sky. They wrote down what they saw on clay tablets. The positions of the moon, the planets and the stars. Eclipses and the weather. And even the level of the river and the prices of barley and wool in the market.

[long-pause]

These records were kept night after night for six hundred years. It is the longest scientific record kept by anyone before modern times.

[pause]

From them, the Babylonians learned to predict eclipses of the moon and the movements of the planets. They did their sums with numbers based on sixty. That is why we still divide an hour into sixty minutes, a minute into sixty seconds, and a circle into three hundred and sixty degrees.

[pause]

They also divided the sun's path across the sky into twelve parts, each with its own group of stars. The bull, the twins, the lion, the scorpion and the others. These are the signs of the zodiac, still known all over the world today.

[long-pause]

Under the Persian kings, the star-watchers of Babylon carried on their work in peace. Their knowledge spread through the empire to the Greeks, who learned from them, and later to India, the Arabs and Europe.

[pause]

The Persian priests, the Magi, watched the stars too, and read them as signs of the will of the gods. Our word magic comes from their name.

[pause]

{img:persian-plain-night-sky-milky-way-shepherd-fire-mountains}
And on clear nights all over the empire, ordinary people looked up at the same stars. Shepherds on the mountains, farmers on their roofs, travellers in the inns and soldiers on the walls. They knew the stars' names, told stories about them, and found their way by them, in the great, dark, silent night of the ancient world.

[long-pause]

{img:persian-boys-learning-horseback-archery-plain-instructor-morning}
How are the sons of Persian nobles raised?

[pause]

The Greek writers describe it with admiration. Until the age of five, a Persian boy lives with his mother and the women of the household. His father hardly sees him. A Greek writer says this is so that, if the child dies young, the father will not grieve too much.

[long-pause]

From five until twenty, the boy is taught together with other noble boys, often at the court of the king or a governor.

[pause]

They get up before dawn. A brass trumpet calls them together, and they go out to exercise. They run and swim and ride. They throw spears and shoot with the bow. They learn to bear heat and cold, rain, hunger, thirst and tiredness. They learn to hunt on horseback in the parks and the mountains, to care for their horses and to make camp.

[pause]

They learn the hymns of the gods and the stories of the heroes and the great kings. And they learn about justice. When boys quarrel, they bring the quarrel to their teachers. The teachers judge, and punish any boy who has been unfair or dishonest.

[long-pause]

And above all, as the Greek writer says, they learn three things. To ride, to shoot and to tell the truth.

[pause]

Darius himself boasts about these things on his tomb. I am trained in my hands and my feet, he says. As a horseman I am a good horseman. As a bowman I am a good bowman, both on foot and on horseback.

[pause]

{img:young-persian-noble-bow-quiver-horse-riding-clothes-proud}
The Persians are famous horsemen. Their best horses, the tall and beautiful horses of the western plains, may only be ridden by the king and the greatest nobles.

[long-pause]

The Persians wear trousers. The Greeks find that very strange and funny, because they themselves wear tunics with nothing on their legs. But trousers are practical for riding. The Persians also wear long-sleeved coats and soft felt hats that cover their ears and chins against the wind and dust. At court they wear long flowing robes with wide sleeves, and tall hats.

[pause]

The nobles wear gold jewellery. Necklaces and bracelets, often ending in the heads of lions or wild goats or griffins. The king gives them as rewards for good service.

[pause]

{img:golden-torque-bracelet-ibex-heads-oxus-treasure-close-light}
Many beautiful golden pieces like these were found in Central Asia, about a hundred and fifty years ago, beside a great river. Today they are in a museum in London. There are tiny golden chariots with horses, golden bracelets with griffins, golden fish and rings. Each one was once given, perhaps, to a young nobleman who had served his king well.

[long-pause]

{img:susa-market-street-merchants-silver-weights-goods-many-peoples}
Let us visit the market of a great Persian city in the year 500 before Christ.

[pause]

It is crowded, noisy and colourful. Merchants from every part of the empire are here. Babylonians with long hair and long robes sell cloth, dates and grain. Phoenicians from the coast sell purple cloth and glass. Egyptians sell linen and papyrus. Lydians sell gold and perfume. Arabs bring sweet-smelling incense from the far south. Indians sell cotton, spices and ivory.

[long-pause]

They pay with silver, weighed on scales, and with the new coins of Darius. One golden coin is worth about a month's pay for a soldier.

[pause]

There are bankers too. In Babylon there is a great family of bankers and businessmen whose clay records survive for five generations. They list loans and rents, the buying and selling of houses and land, marriage contracts and wills.

[pause]

{img:babylonian-banker-clay-tablets-scales-silver-client-office}
In the market, craftsmen are at work. Potters and weavers, leather workers and jewellers, bakers and butchers and wine sellers.

[long-pause]

There are food stalls selling flat bread baked on hot stones, dates, figs, nuts, cheese, yoghurt, roast meat, beer and wine.

[pause]

There are storytellers and musicians, and fortune tellers who read the future in the stars or the flight of birds. There are beggars and children, dogs, donkeys and camels.

[pause]

{img:storyteller-marketplace-crowd-listening-shade-awning-afternoon}
And everywhere people are talking in Persian, Aramaic, Babylonian, Greek, Egyptian and many other languages. They bargain, gossip and greet each other. The Persians greet each other in different ways, a Greek writer says, depending on rank. Equals kiss each other on the lips. Someone a little lower kisses the other on the cheek. And someone much lower bows down to the ground.

[pause]

Lately there is new gossip in the market too. Traders from the west speak of unrest among the Greek cities on the coast. Some say there may be trouble. Others shrug and say the Great King will deal with it.

[long-pause]

As the sun goes down, the market closes. The merchants pack up their goods and go home, or to the inn. The donkeys are led away. Lamps are lit in the houses. And the city settles down for the night under the stars, which the star-watchers of Babylon have watched for hundreds of years.

[long-pause]

{img:royal-banquet-persian-king-behind-curtain-guests-hall-feast}
The king of Persia eats like no one else in the world.

[pause]

Every day, the Greek writers say, thousands of people eat at the king's table. His family and nobles, his guards and officials, his servants and guests. The food comes from every part of the empire, in huge amounts and of wonderful quality.

[long-pause]

A Greek writer copied a list of the food needed for the king's dinner every day. He said it was carved on a bronze pillar in the palace. Hundreds of measures of fine flour. Hundreds of oxen, sheep and geese, and pigeons and little birds. Milk and cheese, garlic and onions, dates and raisins, almonds and pistachios. Apples, quinces and pomegranates. Wine and oil and honey. Saffron and cumin and mustard, and much more.

[pause]

The king eats alone, or with his queen and a few relatives, behind a curtain. He can see his guests, but they cannot see him. Only on great feast days do they see the king eat.

[long-pause]

The Persians love sweet things, fruit and desserts. A Greek writer says the Persians eat only a few main dishes, but many desserts, one after another. They laugh at the Greeks, who, they say, leave the table still hungry, because they have no proper desserts.

[pause]

And they love wine. The same writer tells a famous story. The Persians, he says, make every important decision twice. First they discuss it when they are drunk, at a feast. The next day, sober, they discuss it again. If they still agree, they do it.

[pause]

{img:persian-nobles-reclining-feast-wine-cups-musicians-evening}
It is probably a joke. But the Persians do enjoy their feasts. They drink wine from golden cups shaped like animal heads. There is music, singing and dancing, poetry and long talks late into the night.

[long-pause]

Many foods we eat today first came from Persia, or travelled through Persia to the West. Peaches, which the Romans called Persian apples. Pistachios, spinach, saffron and pomegranates. Even chickens came from India through Persia. The Greeks called the rooster the Persian bird.

[pause]

Persian cooks are famous for their sauces, sweet and sour together, made with fruit and nuts and spices. Some are still eaten in Iran today. Chicken with pomegranates and walnuts. Lamb with dried apricots. Rice with saffron.

[pause]

{img:pomegranates-pistachios-apricots-saffron-market-baskets-sunlight}
The king rewards his friends and faithful servants with food from his own table. A dish sent from the king's own plate is a great honour. Everyone at court envies the person who receives it. And everyone at court watches carefully to see who receives it, and who does not.

[long-pause]

{img:royal-court-travelling-caravan-wagons-horses-mountain-pass-persian}
The King of Kings does not live in one place. He has several capitals, and the whole royal court moves between them with the seasons.

[pause]

In winter, the court lives at Susa, in the warm lowlands near the border of Iraq. Winters there are mild, though summers are burning hot. Darius has built a great palace there. Its walls are covered with glazed bricks in blue, yellow, white and green. They show lions and griffins, and rows of royal guards with spears and bows.

[long-pause]

In spring, the court moves to Persepolis for the New Year festival, and to Babylon, the richest and biggest city of the empire.

[pause]

And in summer, when the lowlands are too hot, the court moves north, up into the mountains, to an old capital where the air is cool and fresh among green valleys and snowy peaks. A Greek writer says that city had seven walls, one inside the other, each painted a different colour. White, black, purple, blue, orange, silver and gold.

[pause]

When the court moves, it is like a whole city on the move. The king, his family, his nobles and officials, guards and servants, cooks and musicians. Horses and chariots, wagons, camels and mules, carrying tents and furniture, treasure and food.

[long-pause]

On the road, the king travels in a chariot, or a covered carriage with curtains, or on horseback. Guards ride in front, and behind him the long procession stretches for kilometres.

[pause]

A Greek writer says the king drinks water from only one river, the river that flows past Susa. The water is boiled, stored in silver jars and carried in mule wagons wherever the king goes, so he never has to drink anything else.

[pause]

{img:silver-water-jars-mule-wagons-royal-road-escort-sunlight}
At night the court camps in great tents like palaces of cloth, with carpets, hangings and lamps. The king's tent has columns of golden wood and hangings of embroidered cloth.

[long-pause]

At Susa, about a hundred and twenty years ago, French archaeologists found a tall black stone pillar carved with very old laws. They were the laws of a king of Babylon who lived twelve hundred years before Darius. The stone had been carried to Susa long ago as a war prize. Today it stands in a museum in Paris.

[pause]

And at Susa, the Bible says, lived Esther, the Jewish girl who became queen of Persia and saved her people. Her story is still remembered every year by Jews all over the world at the festival of Purim, with costumes, noise and gifts of food.

[pause]

{img:susa-mound-citadel-plain-river-palms-ancient-town-evening}
At Susa, as in all the royal cities, the palaces are surrounded by gardens and orchards. Every morning, people gather at the gates to watch the officials come and go, to hand in their requests, and to hear the news of the empire. And one morning, a messenger will come galloping through those gates with news from the west that changes everything.

[long-pause]

{img:jews-returning-jerusalem-road-caravan-donkeys-hills-dawn}
One people of the Persian Empire remembered the Persian kings with gratitude for thousands of years. They were the Jews.

[pause]

When Cyrus took Babylon, many Jews were living there. They were the children and grandchildren of the people of Jerusalem. Fifty years earlier, a king of Babylon had destroyed Jerusalem and its temple and carried them away.

[long-pause]

Cyrus let them go home. Many of them did, in groups, over the years that followed. It was a long journey on foot, with donkeys and camels, across the desert from Babylon to Jerusalem, which lay in ruins. Nobody knew what they would find when they got there.

[pause]

In the time of Darius, just a few years before our story, they rebuilt the temple of Jerusalem. The Persian king gave them permission and help. He ordered his officials to give them money, timber and supplies for the building. The Bible keeps a copy of Darius's letter ordering this.

[pause]

{img:second-temple-jerusalem-building-workers-stones-hills-morning}
It is called the Second Temple. It stood for nearly six hundred years.

[long-pause]

But many Jews stayed in Babylon, where they had houses, families and businesses. A great Jewish community lived there for more than two thousand years, right up to modern times.

[pause]

There were Jews in other parts of the empire too. On an island in the Nile, in the far south of Egypt, there was a group of Jewish soldiers serving the Persian king, with their families and their own temple. Their letters and contracts, written on papyrus, have survived. Marriage contracts and loans, the sale of houses, letters about the Passover festival. Through them we can see the daily life of Jewish families in Egypt two and a half thousand years ago.

[pause]

{img:elephantine-island-nile-houses-palms-boats-calm-afternoon}
In the Bible, the Persian kings are remembered kindly. Cyrus, Darius, and later kings who sent leaders to Jerusalem to rebuild its walls and restore its laws.

[pause]

And the story of Esther, the Jewish queen of Persia, is still told every year. In the Iranian city that was once a royal capital, there is a tomb said to be the tomb of Esther and her cousin Mordecai. Jewish pilgrims have visited it for centuries.

[long-pause]

{img:qanat-shaft-mounds-line-across-desert-plain-village-green}
Most people in the Persian Empire are farmers, as in all ancient lands. And in Iran, a farmer's biggest problem is water.

[pause]

Much of Iran is dry, with high plains and deserts and little rain, especially in summer. There are few rivers. But in the mountains there is snow and rain. The water sinks into the ground and flows slowly, deep underground, toward the plains.

[long-pause]

So the Persians invented a wonderful way to bring that water to their fields. It is called a qanat.

[pause]

A qanat is a tunnel under the ground. It starts at the foot of the mountains, where there is water deep down. From there it slopes very gently downward for many kilometres, until it comes out on the plain. There the water flows into a channel, and on to the fields, the gardens and the village.

[pause]

To dig a qanat, the diggers first dig a deep well at the foot of the mountains, down to the water. Then they dig a line of other shafts across the plain, every few dozen metres. Underground, they join the shafts with a tunnel just big enough for a man to crawl through. The slope must be exactly right. Too steep and the water rushes and wears the tunnel away. Too flat and it stops flowing. It is hard, skilled and dangerous work. The diggers are greatly respected.

[long-pause]

From above, a qanat looks like a long line of little round mounds of earth, like giant molehills, stretching across the desert. Each mound has a hole in the middle, with the dug-out earth piled around it.

[pause]

The water in a qanat is cool and clean. It flows all year round, even in the hottest summer, because it stays underground, out of the sun.

[pause]

{img:village-garden-qanat-water-emerging-channel-pomegranate-trees}
The Persian kings encourage the building of qanats. A Greek writer says that if a man brought water to land that had never had water before, the king let his family farm it without paying tax, for five generations.

[pause]

There are still tens of thousands of qanats in Iran today. Some are thousands of years old and still flowing.

[long-pause]

With this water, the farmers grow wheat and barley, cotton and vegetables, and grass for the horses. They grow orchards of apples and pomegranates, apricots and grapes, and dates in the hot south. And nuts, like almonds, walnuts and pistachios.

[pause]

They keep sheep, goats and cattle. The nomads move their flocks with the seasons, from the warm plains in winter to the cool mountain meadows in summer, as they still do today.

[pause]

{img:nomads-moving-flocks-mountain-pasture-black-tents-summer}
The villages are built of mud brick, with flat roofs or domes, and courtyards with a pool, a fruit tree and a little garden. In the hottest places, houses have wind catchers. These are tall towers on the roof, open to the wind. They catch the breeze and send it down into the house, over a pool of water, to cool it. It is the first air conditioning.

[long-pause]

{img:yakhchal-ice-house-dome-mud-brick-desert-shade-wall-winter}
In the hot, dry lands of Persia, people found a clever way to have ice in summer, right in the middle of the desert.

[pause]

They built ice houses. These are great domes of thick mud brick, like huge beehives, rising from the desert over a deep pit in the ground. The oldest ones may go back to the time of the Persian kings, or a little later.

[long-pause]

In winter, on cold, clear nights, people let water flow from a qanat into long, shallow pools beside the ice house. A tall wall on the south side keeps off the sun. Overnight the water freezes. In the morning, workers break the ice, carry it into the pit under the dome, and pack it with straw to keep it cold.

[pause]

The walls of the dome are very thick, two metres or more, made of a special mix of sand, clay, lime and goat hair. It keeps out the heat. And the shape of the dome lets warm air rise and escape through a hole at the top, while cold air sinks down into the pit.

[pause]

{img:workers-carrying-ice-blocks-into-dome-pit-straw-cold-morning}
So in the middle of the burning summer, when the desert air shimmers with heat, people can take ice from the ice house. They cool their water and make cold drinks. They even make a frozen sweet of ice, rose water and thin noodles, which Persians still love today.

[long-pause]

Together with the qanats that bring water under the desert, and the wind catchers that cool the houses, the ice houses show how the people of Persia learned to live comfortably in one of the hottest, driest lands on earth. They used only mud and water, wind, and the cold of winter nights.

[pause]

Some of these old ice houses still stand today in the deserts of Iran, with their great brown domes against the blue sky.

[long-pause]

{img:persian-queen-atossa-royal-robes-veil-palace-garden-dignified}
The women of the Persian royal family are much more powerful and important than the Greeks expected.

[pause]

Darius has several wives. The most important is Atossa, the daughter of Cyrus the Great. By marrying her, Darius showed that he belonged to the family of Cyrus. She is clever, proud and very influential. A Greek writer says it was thanks to her that her son Xerxes was chosen as the next king, rather than Darius's older sons by another wife.

[long-pause]

His favourite wife, the same writer says, is another daughter of Cyrus. Darius loved her so much that he had a golden statue made of her. The clay tablets from Persepolis mention her too. They show she owned her own lands and workers, had her own steward, gave her own orders and travelled with her own household. She received huge amounts of wine, grain and sheep for her feasts.

[pause]

The royal women of Persia are rich. They own land, villages and workshops, and run them through their own officials. They travel and give banquets. They have their own seals to sign their orders. They are not shut away, as Greek women often are.

[pause]

{img:royal-woman-seal-stamping-order-clay-tablet-steward-waiting}
The tablets mention many other women too. Princesses and noblewomen, and ordinary working women with their own names, rations and jobs. Weavers, wine makers and leaders of work groups.

[long-pause]

The Persian queens and princesses wear beautiful clothes. Long robes of fine wool or linen, dyed in rich purple, blue and red, and embroidered. They wear veils and crowns and golden jewellery.

[pause]

They ride horses and travel in covered carriages. They keep their own court, with their own servants and guards.

[pause]

{img:noblewomen-riding-horses-garden-paradise-attendants-spring}
Later, Greek writers will tell many dramatic stories about Persian queens, about their power and their schemes in the palace. Some of these stories are probably exaggerated. But they show how much the royal women mattered. In a palace, the right word from the right queen could decide who would be the next king.

[long-pause]

In Persian art, and later in Persian poetry, women are honoured, loved and celebrated. The mother, the wife and the beloved, in a garden among roses and nightingales. Two thousand years later, those will be the great themes of the famous Persian poets.

[long-pause]

{img:persian-immortals-guard-spears-golden-apples-robes-palace-gate}
The king of Persia is protected by his guard. They are ten thousand chosen soldiers, the best in the Persian army. The Greeks called them the Immortals.

[pause]

A Greek writer says they got that name because their number was always kept at exactly ten thousand. If one of them fell ill or died, another man took his place at once. So there were never more and never fewer.

[long-pause]

A thousand of them, the most noble, carry spears with golden pomegranates on the end. They are the king's personal bodyguard, always close to him. The others carry spears with silver pomegranates.

[pause]

On the palace walls at Susa, they are shown in glazed bricks of bright colours. Tall, bearded men in long, patterned robes, with spears held upright and bows on their shoulders. They walk in a long, calm line, one behind the other, against a blue background. They are among the most beautiful works of ancient art. Today they can be seen in a museum in Paris.

[pause]

{img:glazed-brick-archers-frieze-susa-blue-background-museum-light}
The Immortals get the best food and the best equipment. When they travel, their families and servants come along in covered carriages, and camels carry their food.

[long-pause]

The Persian army itself is enormous. It has soldiers from every part of the empire. Persian foot soldiers with wicker shields, spears and bows. Persian horsemen on fine horses. Riders from Central Asia. Indian soldiers in cotton clothes with bows of cane. Ethiopians in leopard skins. Arabs on camels. And sailors from Phoenicia, Egypt and Greece in the navy.

[pause]

A Greek writer describes this great army many years later, listing all the peoples, their weapons and their clothes, one after another, like a great parade.

[pause]

{img:persian-cavalry-riders-fine-horses-plain-banners-dust-calm}
But in the year 500 before Christ, the empire is mostly at peace. The soldiers guard the palaces, the cities, the roads and the borders. They train and hunt, and they wait. For how much longer?

[long-pause]

At Persepolis, the guards live in barracks near the palaces. They train and eat together, play games and talk. On the great staircases they are carved standing in long lines with their spears, as the guard of honour for the New Year festival.

[pause]

And every evening, as the sun goes down behind the mountains and the long shadows of the columns stretch across the terrace, the guard changes at the palace gates. The new guards take their places for the night, with their spears and their bows, under the stars.

[long-pause]

{img:ionian-greek-city-coast-asia-minor-temple-harbour-500bc}
In the year 500 before Christ, on the western edge of the Persian Empire, trouble is brewing.

[pause]

Along the coast of what is now Turkey there are many Greek cities, like Miletus and Ephesus. They are rich and busy and clever, with their temples, their thinkers, their harbours and their ships. The Persians have ruled them for about forty years.

[long-pause]

The Persians let them keep their own customs and gods. But they rule them through local Greek strongmen, backed by Persia. And the Greeks, who love their freedom, do not like it.

[pause]

In 499 before Christ, just one year after our story, the Greek cities on this coast rise up against Persia. They ask the cities of mainland Greece for help. Sparta refuses. But Athens and one other city send ships and soldiers.

[pause]

{img:ionian-ships-harbour-miletus-rebels-gathering-evening-calm}
The rebellion lasts six years. In the end the Persians win, and take Miletus. We will not watch it.

[pause]

A Greek writer says that when Darius heard the Athenians had helped the rebels and burned a Persian city, he asked: who are the Athenians? When he was told, he took his bow and shot an arrow into the sky. O god, he said, let me punish the Athenians. And he ordered a servant to say to him three times every day at dinner: Master, remember the Athenians.

[long-pause]

Ten years after our story, Darius sends an army across the sea to punish Athens. It lands at a bay called Marathon, about forty kilometres from Athens. And there the Athenian army, with help from one small city, defeats the Persians and drives them back to their ships.

[pause]

The story goes that a runner ran all the way from Marathon to Athens with the news. He shouted: rejoice, we have won! And then he fell down, worn out. The modern marathon race is named after this story.

[pause]

{img:marathon-plain-bay-hills-greek-coast-distant-quiet-dawn}
Darius planned a much bigger invasion of Greece. But he died before he could carry it out. He was buried in his great tomb in the cliff, looking out over the plain.

[long-pause]

His son Xerxes will lead that great invasion. It will become the most famous war of the ancient world, with great battles on land and sea. And Xerxes will be defeated, and go home.

[pause]

But that is the Greek side of the story. For the Persians, it was a war on the far western edge of a vast empire. The empire itself went on, peaceful, rich and powerful, for another hundred and fifty years.

[long-pause]

{img:ephesus-temple-of-artemis-columns-ionian-city-philosopher-walking}
In the year 500 before Christ, in the Greek cities on the western edge of the Persian Empire, something new is happening. It is called philosophy, which means love of wisdom.

[pause]

A hundred years before, in Miletus, a man called Thales began to ask a new kind of question. Not which god made the world, but what is the world made of? He answered: water. Everything is made of water in different forms. It was the wrong answer. But it was a new way of thinking. He was looking for natural causes, instead of stories about gods.

[long-pause]

Thales, it was said, even predicted an eclipse of the sun. The story goes that it happened in the middle of a battle. The soldiers on both sides were so frightened that they made peace on the spot. Perhaps Thales learned about eclipses from the star-watchers of Babylon.

[pause]

After Thales, other thinkers in Miletus kept asking questions. One drew the first map of the world. He said the earth floats in space, held up by nothing. He even said the first living things came from the sea. Another said everything is made of air.

[pause]

{img:philosopher-drawing-world-map-sand-students-miletus-harbour}
And in the year 500, in the great city of Ephesus, lives a strange, proud, lonely thinker called Heraclitus.

[long-pause]

Heraclitus says that everything is always changing. You cannot step into the same river twice, he says, because new water is always flowing past. Everything flows. The world is like a fire that never goes out, always changing, and yet always the same.

[pause]

He writes his thoughts in short, mysterious sayings that are hard to understand. People called him Heraclitus the Obscure. He gave his book to the great temple of his city for safekeeping.

[pause]

{img:river-flowing-over-stones-ephesus-countryside-reflections-calm}
Some think Heraclitus was influenced by Persian ideas about fire and about the struggle between opposites. We do not know. But in these cities on the edge of the Persian Empire, where Greeks and Persians and Babylonians met and traded and talked, new ideas were born. They would change the way people think forever.

[long-pause]

{img:xerxes-gate-of-all-nations-completed-persepolis-procession}
After Darius, his son Xerxes became king. He was about thirty-two.

[pause]

Xerxes finished many of his father's buildings at Persepolis, like the Gate of All Nations with its great winged bulls. He built his own palace, and began the biggest hall of all, with a hundred columns in ten rows of ten. There the king received his army officers.

[long-pause]

On the Gate of All Nations, Xerxes carved a message in three languages that can still be read today. I am Xerxes, it says, the Great King, King of Kings, king of lands with all kinds of people. By the favour of the Wise Lord I built this gateway. Whatever good building is seen here, my father and I built it.

[pause]

After Xerxes came other kings. For a hundred and fifty years more, the Persian Empire went on, rich and powerful and mostly at peace. It was the greatest power in the world.

[pause]

{img:hall-of-hundred-columns-forest-of-columns-persepolis-afternoon}
The kings kept building at Persepolis and Susa and Babylon. The roads kept carrying messengers and merchants. The farmers kept digging qanats and planting orchards. The priests kept tending the sacred fires. And every spring, the peoples of the empire came to celebrate the New Year.

[long-pause]

There were troubles too. Rebellions in Egypt and elsewhere, quarrels in the royal family and plots in the palace. But the empire held together.

[pause]

And many Greeks came to Persia to work for the Persian kings, as doctors, soldiers, craftsmen and advisers. Even the Athenian general who had beaten Xerxes at sea later fled from Athens when the Athenians turned against him. He went to Persia. The Persian king welcomed him, and gave him three cities to rule, where he lived out his life in peace.

[pause]

{img:greek-exile-received-persian-court-king-friendly-gesture}
And Persian ideas, art, gardens and ways of governing spread across the world. The Greeks copied them, and later Alexander and the Romans and many others too. But far in the future, one young man from the west will come not to copy Persia, but to conquer it.

[long-pause]

{img:persepolis-evening-terrace-lamps-columns-guards-mountain-dusk}
Let us end our visit with an evening at Persepolis, in the spring of the year 500 before Christ, after the New Year festival.

[pause]

The guests have gone home along the royal roads, with their gifts from the king. The great hall is quiet. The sun is going down over the plain. Its last light turns the stone columns gold and then pink. The shadows of the columns stretch long across the terrace.

[long-pause]

From the workers' village below the terrace comes the smell of bread baking. Smoke rises, children play, and voices call out in many languages. The stone cutters, carpenters and weavers are finishing work for the day and going home to their families, with their rations of grain and wine.

[pause]

On the terrace, the guards change at the Gate of All Nations. Little oil lamps are lit in the palaces and glow in the windows.

[pause]

{img:workers-village-below-terrace-evening-smoke-children-families}
In the gardens below, water flows in the stone channels. The nightingales begin to sing in the trees. The scent of roses drifts up on the evening air.

[long-pause]

In the royal rooms, the king sits with his family and listens to music. A harp, a flute and a singer sing the old songs of the Persians about the heroes of long ago.

[pause]

And behind the terrace, the stars come out one by one over the mountain, over the great dark plain and the far mountains, and over the whole vast sleeping empire, from the river Indus to the shores of the Aegean Sea. Tonight all is calm. Far to the west, the Greek cities are restless. But that is a worry for another day.

[long-pause]

{img:alexander-army-approaching-persepolis-plain-330bc-distant}
In 334 before Christ, a young king from Macedonia, called Alexander, crossed into Asia with an army, to conquer the Persian Empire.

[pause]

He was twenty-two. In four years and three great battles, he defeated the Persian king and conquered the whole empire. Turkey, Syria, Egypt, Babylon and Susa. And in the winter of 331, Persepolis.

[long-pause]

We will not watch the battles.

[pause]

At Persepolis, Alexander took the treasure of the Persian kings. There was so much gold and silver, the old writers say, that it took thousands of mules and camels to carry it away. And then, in the spring of 330, the palaces of Persepolis burned.

[pause]

Nobody knows exactly why. Some say it was revenge for the burning of Athens by Xerxes, a hundred and fifty years before. Others say it happened at a feast, when Alexander and his friends had drunk too much, and that he was sorry afterwards. We will see it only from very far away, as a faint glow on the distant plain under the mountain, on a spring night.

[long-pause]

The great cedar roofs burned and fell. The mud-brick walls crumbled. The palaces became ruins. The stone columns, doorways, staircases and carvings were left standing among the ashes, under the open sky.

[pause]

And yet the fire also saved something. When the roofs fell, they buried the clay tablets of the record office. The fire baked them hard, like pottery. So they survived for more than two thousand years, until archaeologists found them.

[pause]

{img:ruins-persepolis-columns-standing-empty-terrace-dawn-quiet}
Alexander respected the Persians and their ways. He visited the tomb of Cyrus and ordered it repaired. He married a princess from Central Asia, and later two Persian princesses. He sometimes wore Persian clothes and took up Persian court customs. He took Persians into his army. He even held a great wedding where many of his officers married Persian noblewomen, hoping to join the two peoples together.

[long-pause]

He died in Babylon only seven years after the fall of Persepolis, at just thirty-two. His empire was divided among his generals.

[pause]

But Persia did not disappear. Five hundred years later, a new Persian royal family rose up in the same region, near Persepolis. They built a new Persian empire that lasted four hundred years more. They carved pictures of their own kings on horseback in the same cliff, right below the tombs of Darius and his family.

[pause]

{img:sasanian-relief-king-on-horseback-cliff-below-achaemenid-tombs}
And the Persian language, Persian poetry, Persian gardens and the festival of Nowruz lived on. They still live today.

[long-pause]

{img:persepolis-ruins-today-columns-terrace-blue-sky-visitors}
Today, the ruins of Persepolis still stand on their great stone terrace at the foot of the mountain, in southern Iran, not far from the city of Shiraz.

[pause]

It is one of the most impressive ruins in the world.

[long-pause]

You climb the great double staircase with its shallow steps, as the visitors from all the peoples of the empire did two and a half thousand years ago. You pass through the Gate of All Nations, between the great winged bulls with human heads. They are battered, but still magnificent, still looking out over the plain.

[pause]

On the walls of the gate, travellers over the centuries have scratched their names. Explorers and soldiers from Europe and America, three hundred, two hundred and a hundred and fifty years ago.

[pause]

Of the seventy-two columns of the great hall, thirteen still stand, tall and slim against the blue sky. On the great staircases, the carved processions are still there, wonderfully preserved, because they lay buried under earth for centuries. The Persians and Medes chatting and holding hands. And the twenty-three peoples with their gifts, walking up the stairs forever.

[long-pause]

A few kilometres away, the four great tombs of Darius, Xerxes and later kings are still carved high in the golden cliff, in the shape of a cross. Below them are the later carvings of kings on horseback.

[pause]

The tomb of Cyrus the Great still stands alone on its wide plain. It is a simple house of white stone on six steps, among the grass and the spring wildflowers. Nearby lie the remains of his palaces and the stone water channels of his garden.

[pause]

And in the city of Shiraz there are famous Persian gardens with cypresses, pools and roses. There are the tombs of great Persian poets too. Every evening, people come there to read the poems aloud and lay flowers on the tombs.

[long-pause]

And every year, around the twenty-first of March, millions of people in Iran celebrate Nowruz, the New Year, as perhaps their ancestors did at Persepolis. They clean their houses, buy new clothes, set out the special table and visit their families. On the thirteenth day of the New Year, everyone goes out into the countryside for a picnic in the fresh green spring.

[pause]

Some families even go to Persepolis itself, and have their New Year picnic there, among the ancient columns, under the mountain.

[pause]

{img:families-picnic-spring-grass-near-ancient-columns-nowruz}
So we leave the King of Kings for tonight, on his great stone terrace above the plain, with the last light falling across the columns, and the carved processions climbing the stairs, as they have for two and a half thousand years.

[pause]

The messengers on their fast horses, the gardeners planting cypresses in long rows, the women weavers of Persepolis receiving their rations, and the peoples of the world walking side by side, with their gifts, in the spring.

[pause]

{img:persepolis-sunset-columns-long-shadows-golden-plain-calm}
If you enjoyed this evening in ancient Persia, you are very welcome to subscribe to the channel, and to come back for another quiet story.
