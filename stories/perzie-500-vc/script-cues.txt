Tonight we are going two and a half thousand years back, to the heart of the Persian Empire, in the year 500 before Christ.

[long-pause]

On a great stone terrace, at the foot of a mountain, on a wide plain in the south of what is now Iran, a new city of palaces is rising: forests of tall stone columns, great staircases carved with endless processions of people, and gateways guarded by winged bulls with human faces. It is called Persepolis, and it belongs to Darius, the King of Kings, ruler of the largest empire the world has ever known.

[pause]

Tonight, we will walk slowly through his world: his palaces and gardens, his roads and messengers, and the lives of the ordinary people who built it all.

[pause]

If you like these slow journeys into the distant past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, and to come back for the next one.

[long-pause]

In the year 500 before Christ, the Persian Empire stretches from the borders of Greece in the west, to the river Indus, in what is now Pakistan, in the east. From the deserts of Egypt and Libya in the south, to the steppes of Central Asia in the north.

[pause]

It covers perhaps five and a half million square kilometres, and rules over perhaps fifty million people: almost half of all the people in the world, at that time, some historians think. It includes what is now Iran, and Iraq, and Turkey, and Syria, and Lebanon, and Israel, and Egypt, and Afghanistan, and parts of Pakistan, and Central Asia, and the Caucasus, and the Greek cities of the coast of Asia, and even, for a while, Thrace, in Europe.

[long-pause]

Within it live dozens of peoples, speaking dozens of languages: Persians, and Medes, and Elamites, and Babylonians, and Assyrians, and Arabs, and Jews, and Phoenicians, and Egyptians, and Lydians, and Ionian Greeks, and Armenians, and Bactrians, and Sogdians, and Scythians, and Indians, and many more.

[pause]

And over all of them rules one man: the Great King, the King of Kings, Darius, of the family of the Achaemenids.

[pause]

{img:fars-province-green-valley-mountains-shepherds-flocks-spring}
The Persians themselves come from a region in the south west of Iran, called Parsa, or Persis, from which the Greeks gave them their name. It is a land of high mountains, and green valleys, and wide plains, hot in summer, and cold and snowy in winter, with rivers running down from the mountains, and orchards, and fields of wheat and barley, and flocks of sheep and goats, and herds of horses.

[long-pause]

Only about seventy years before our story, the Persians were a small people, ruled by their neighbours, the Medes. Then, in a single lifetime, under a great king called Cyrus, they conquered the Medes, and the rich kingdom of Lydia, in Turkey, and the great city of Babylon, and built an empire.

[pause]

How they did it, and how they ruled it, is one of the most remarkable stories of the ancient world.

[pause]

{img:persian-nobles-long-robes-cylindrical-hats-walking-colonnade-sun}
And unlike most of the empires of the ancient world, the Persians are remembered, even by their enemies, the Greeks, not only for their power, and their wealth, but also for their good roads, their fair laws, their tolerance of other peoples and other religions, and their love of truth, and gardens, and horses, and beautiful things.

[long-pause]

{img:cyrus-the-great-king-portrait-calm-wise-robes-crown-mountains}
The founder of the Persian Empire was Cyrus the Great.

[pause]

He was born, around 590 before Christ, a prince of the little kingdom of Anshan, in Persis. There are many stories about his childhood, told later by the Greeks. Herodotus says that his grandfather, the king of the Medes, had a dream that his grandson would take his throne, and ordered the baby to be killed. But the servant who was told to do it gave the baby to a shepherd, whose wife had just lost her own baby, and they brought him up as their own son, in the mountains.

[long-pause]

When the boy was ten, Herodotus says, he was playing with the other village children, and they chose him to be their king, in their game. And he played the part so well, and so seriously, giving orders, and punishing a boy who disobeyed, that the true story came out, and he was recognized, and sent back to his real parents.

[pause]

It is probably only a story. But it shows how the people remembered him: as a born king.

[pause]

In 550 before Christ, Cyrus rebelled against the Medes, and won, and became king of both peoples. Then, he conquered Lydia, in the west, the kingdom of the famously rich king Croesus. And in 539, he took Babylon, the greatest city in the world, almost without fighting.

[long-pause]

Cyrus was a different kind of conqueror.

[pause]

He did not destroy the cities he conquered, or kill their people, or drag them off as slaves, as the Assyrians and Babylonians had done before him. He respected their gods, and their temples, and their customs, and let them keep their own laws, and their own languages, as long as they obeyed him, and paid tribute.

[pause]

{img:clay-cylinder-cuneiform-script-museum-glass-close-soft-light}
In Babylon, he had a declaration written, in cuneiform, on a clay cylinder, which still exists, and is in the British Museum, in London. It says that he entered Babylon in peace, and that the people welcomed him, and that he allowed the peoples who had been carried away to Babylon to go home, to their own lands, and to rebuild their temples, and take their gods home with them.

[long-pause]

Among those peoples were the Jews, who had been taken to Babylon, by King Nebuchadnezzar, nearly fifty years before. Cyrus allowed them to go home, to Jerusalem, and to rebuild their temple. In the Bible, in the book of Isaiah, Cyrus is called the anointed one of the Lord, the only foreign king ever to be given that title.

[pause]

Cyrus passed away in 530 before Christ, fighting in Central Asia. He was buried at his capital, Pasargadae, in Persis, in a simple, beautiful tomb, of white stone, like a little house, with a gabled roof, on top of six great steps.

[pause]

{img:tomb-of-cyrus-pasargadae-white-stone-steps-plain-dawn}
The Greek writers say that on the tomb there was an inscription: O man, I am Cyrus, who founded the empire of the Persians, and was king of Asia. Do not grudge me this monument.

[pause]

When Alexander the Great came to Pasargadae, two hundred years later, he found the tomb robbed, and was saddened, and ordered it to be restored. The tomb still stands today, on the empty plain, among the grass and the wildflowers.

[long-pause]

{img:babylon-ishtar-gate-blue-glazed-bricks-procession-way-539bc}
In the autumn of 539 before Christ, the armies of Cyrus came to Babylon.

[pause]

Babylon was the greatest city in the world: a vast city, on the river Euphrates, with enormous double walls, so thick that, the Greek writers said, a four-horse chariot could turn on the top of them. Its great gate, the Ishtar Gate, was covered with blue glazed bricks, with golden lions, and dragons, and bulls, in relief. Its great temple tower, the ziggurat, called Etemenanki, the house of the foundation of heaven and earth, rose high above the city, in seven stages, perhaps the Tower of Babel of the Bible.

[long-pause]

The king of Babylon, Nabonidus, was unpopular with his people. He had spent ten years away, in the Arabian desert, at the oasis of Tayma, and neglected the great festival of Marduk, the god of Babylon, which the king had to lead every year. The priests, and many of the people, were unhappy.

[pause]

So when Cyrus came, there was little fighting. Herodotus says that the Persians diverted the river Euphrates, which flowed through the middle of the city, into a lake, so that the water level fell, and the soldiers walked along the river bed, under the walls, into the city, at night, during a great festival, while the Babylonians were dancing and feasting, and did not even know that their city had been taken until it was over.

[pause]

{img:euphrates-river-low-water-night-city-walls-quiet-festival-lights}
The Cyrus Cylinder says, more simply, that Marduk, the god of Babylon, chose Cyrus, and that he entered the city without battle, and the people welcomed him, and spread green branches before him.

[long-pause]

Cyrus treated Babylon with respect. He did not destroy it, or loot it. He honoured its gods, and its temples, and its priests, and took part in the festival of Marduk, as the kings of Babylon had always done. He let the people go on living as they always had. He let the peoples who had been brought to Babylon as captives go home. And Babylon remained one of the greatest cities of the Persian Empire, and one of the royal capitals, where the Persian kings lived for part of every year.

[pause]

In the Bible, in the book of Daniel, there is a famous story about the last night of Babylon: the feast of Belshazzar, the son of Nabonidus, when a mysterious hand appeared, and wrote on the wall of the palace, mene, mene, tekel, upharsin. And Daniel explained it to the king: God has numbered the days of your kingdom, and brought it to an end. You have been weighed in the balance, and found wanting. Your kingdom is divided, and given to the Medes and the Persians.

[pause]

{img:feast-hall-babylon-lamps-wall-mysterious-writing-calm-guests}
Today, we still say that the writing is on the wall, when something is about to end.

[long-pause]

{img:darius-the-great-king-throne-relief-beard-crown-sceptre-calm}
In the year 500 before Christ, the King of Kings is Darius, the first of that name. He is about fifty years old. He has ruled for twenty-two years.

[pause]

He was not the son of a king. When Cyrus died, his son Cambyses became king, and conquered Egypt. And when Cambyses died, in 522, there was confusion, and rebellion, and a man claiming to be Cambyses's brother seized the throne. Darius, a young nobleman of the royal family, an officer of the guard, with six other Persian nobles, overthrew him, and became king.

[long-pause]

Herodotus tells a charming story about how Darius was chosen. The seven nobles agreed that they would all ride out of the city, at sunrise, and the one whose horse neighed first would be king. And Darius's clever groom, the night before, took his master's horse to the place, with a mare it was fond of. So in the morning, when they came to the place, Darius's horse neighed at once. And Darius became king.

[pause]

It is probably only a story.

[pause]

In his first years, Darius had to fight many rebellions, all over the empire, and won them all. He recorded his victories in a great inscription, carved high on a cliff, at a place called Behistun, in the west of Iran, in three languages, Old Persian, and Elamite, and Babylonian, with a great relief, showing him, with his foot on the defeated rebel, and the other rebels, roped together, before him, and above, the winged symbol of his god. In the nineteenth century, a British officer, Henry Rawlinson, climbed the cliff, on ladders, and ropes, to copy it, and it helped scholars to decipher the cuneiform writing of the ancient world.

[long-pause]

Darius is a great organizer. He divides the empire into provinces, called satrapies, each governed by a satrap, a governor, usually a Persian nobleman. He sets the tribute that each province must pay, in silver, or gold, or goods, fairly, according to its wealth. He introduces a common system of weights and measures. And he issues the first gold coins of the empire, called darics, with a picture of the king, running, with a bow and a spear.

[pause]

He builds roads, and canals, including a canal from the Nile to the Red Sea, so that ships can sail from the Mediterranean to the Indian Ocean. He sends explorers, to sail down the river Indus, and around Arabia.

[pause]

{img:gold-daric-coins-king-archer-running-hand-close-light}
The Greeks called him Darius the shopkeeper, because he was so interested in money, and taxes, and accounts. But it is a sign of how well he ran his great empire.

[long-pause]

In his inscriptions, Darius speaks about himself, and his beliefs. He says that he is a friend of what is right, and not a friend of what is wrong. That he does not wish the weak to be wronged by the strong, or the strong to be wronged by the weak. That he rewards the man who cooperates, and punishes the man who does harm. That he is not hot-tempered, and controls his anger.

[pause]

And above all, that he hates the Lie, and loves the Truth. Because for the Persians, the greatest of all sins is to tell a lie.

[pause]

{img:king-darius-listening-petitioners-audience-hall-courtiers-calm}
And he says that everything he has done, he has done by the will of the great god, Ahura Mazda, the Wise Lord, who made the earth, and the sky, and man, and happiness for man.

[long-pause]

{img:greek-doctor-democedes-treating-king-ankle-palace-chamber}
There is a story, told by Herodotus, about a Greek doctor at the court of Darius.

[pause]

One day, when Darius was out hunting, he jumped down from his horse, and twisted his foot, badly, and dislocated his ankle. The Egyptian doctors of the court, who were thought to be the best in the world, tried to put it right, but they twisted it so roughly that they made it worse. And for seven days and seven nights, the king could not sleep, because of the pain.

[long-pause]

Then someone remembered that, among the prisoners, and slaves, captured at Samos, there was a Greek doctor, called Democedes, from the city of Croton, in the south of Italy. They found him, and brought him to the king, still in his chains, and in rags.

[pause]

At first, Democedes said that he was not a doctor, because he was afraid that if he cured the king, he would never be allowed to go home. But Darius threatened him, and he admitted it. And he treated the king's ankle, gently, in the Greek way, with soft treatments, and rest, and soothing bandages, and the king slept, and in a few days, he was well again.

[pause]

{img:king-giving-golden-chains-to-doctor-women-of-palace-gifts}
Darius was so grateful that he gave Democedes two pairs of golden chains, as a joke, because he had come to him in chains. And Democedes asked if the king meant to double his misery. Darius laughed, and sent him to visit the queens, in the women's palace. And each of the queens took a golden bowl, and filled it with gold coins, and gave it to him. So many coins, Herodotus says, that the servant who followed him, picking up the coins that fell, became rich.

[long-pause]

Democedes became very rich, and lived in a great house, in Susa, and ate at the king's table, and was a favourite of the king. Later, he also cured Queen Atossa of an illness.

[pause]

But he still wanted to go home, to Croton. And so, Herodotus says, he persuaded Atossa to ask Darius to send an expedition to Greece, to spy out the land, with Democedes as a guide. And when the ships reached Italy, Democedes escaped, and went home, to Croton, and married the daughter of a famous wrestler, and never came back.

[pause]

{img:ship-sailing-west-italian-coast-greek-doctor-looking-shore}
It is a good story, and perhaps partly true. It shows that the Persian court welcomed clever people, from everywhere, and rewarded them richly. And that the Greeks, even when they were treated very well, longed for their own homes, and their own freedom.

[long-pause]

{img:persepolis-great-staircase-gentle-steps-terrace-gate-of-all-nations}
About twenty years before our story, Darius began to build a new royal city, in the heart of Persis, on the plain of Marvdasht, at the foot of a mountain, called the Mountain of Mercy.

[pause]

The Persians called it Parsa. The Greeks, later, called it Persepolis, the city of the Persians. It was not a city where ordinary people lived. It was a great ceremonial palace, a place for the king to receive his subjects, and celebrate the great festivals of the empire.

[long-pause]

First, the builders made a great terrace, a huge platform of stone, partly cut out of the mountain, and partly built up, with enormous blocks of stone, fitted together without mortar, held with iron clamps, about twelve metres high, and more than four hundred metres long, and three hundred metres wide.

[pause]

To reach the top, there is a great double staircase, with a hundred and eleven steps, so wide, and so shallow, that it is said horses could walk up it, and that the nobles, in their long robes, could climb it with dignity, without hurrying.

[pause]

At the top of the staircase, there is the Gate of All Nations, built a little later, by Darius's son, Xerxes: a great hall, with doorways guarded by huge stone bulls, and winged bulls, with the heads of bearded men, wearing crowns, taller than two men, looking out over the plain.

[long-pause]

Beyond the gate, there are the palaces.

[pause]

The greatest is the Apadana, the great audience hall of Darius, where he receives his subjects. It is a huge square hall, sixty metres on each side, with thirty-six columns, each nearly twenty metres tall, slender, and fluted, and topped with great capitals, carved in the shape of two bulls, or two lions, or two griffins, back to back, which hold up the huge cedar beams of the roof. Around it, on three sides, there are porches, with more columns.

[pause]

Seventy-two columns in all. Today, only a few are still standing. But they still rise, slender and tall, against the blue sky of Iran.

[long-pause]

The walls are of mud brick, plastered, and painted, and hung with rich hangings. The doorways and windows are of stone, carved and polished, so smooth that they shine like mirrors. The roofs are of cedar wood, from Lebanon. And everything is decorated with gold, and silver, and ivory, and lapis lazuli, and paint, in bright colours.

[pause]

There are other palaces too: the private palace of Darius, called the Tachara, the house of winter, with its polished stone, and the Hall of a Hundred Columns, and the treasury, where the treasures of the empire are kept, and the palaces of the queens, and storerooms, and barracks for the guards.

[pause]

{img:persepolis-treasury-courtyards-columns-guards-storerooms-light}
In the year 500 before Christ, much of it is still being built. Thousands of workers, from all over the empire, are working on it: stone cutters, and masons, and carpenters, and sculptors, and goldsmiths, and painters. It will take three generations, and nearly a hundred years, to finish.

[long-pause]

And behind the terrace, cut into the side of the Mountain of Mercy, there are the beginnings of the royal tombs.

[pause]

Darius's own tomb is not here, but a few kilometres away, at a place called Naqsh-e Rostam, cut high into the face of a great cliff: a great cross-shaped façade, carved like the front of a palace, with a door, leading to the burial chamber, inside the rock, and above, a carving of the king, standing before a fire altar, worshipping Ahura Mazda, held up on a great throne, by the peoples of the empire.

[pause]

{img:naqsh-e-rostam-royal-tombs-cliff-cross-shaped-facades-sunset}
Darius chose it himself, and had it carved, years before his death, so that he would always be able to look out, over the plain, toward his city.

[long-pause]

{img:behistun-cliff-inscription-three-languages-relief-high-rock}
How do the Persians write?

[pause]

The Persian Empire is full of languages, and scripts. And Darius, in his inscriptions, uses three of them, side by side: Old Persian, the language of the Persians themselves. Elamite, the language of the old kingdom of Elam, around Susa. And Babylonian, the ancient language of Mesopotamia. All three are written in cuneiform: wedge-shaped signs, pressed into clay with a reed, or carved into stone.

[long-pause]

Old Persian cuneiform is special. It is a new script, which may have been invented in the time of Darius himself, for his royal inscriptions. It is simple, with only about thirty-six signs for sounds, and a few signs for whole words, like king, and country, and Ahura Mazda. It is used almost only for royal inscriptions, carved on stone, and on gold and silver plates.

[pause]

But for everyday business, letters, and accounts, and orders, the empire uses another language, and another script: Aramaic, the language of Syria, written with an alphabet, in ink, on parchment, or papyrus, which is quick, and easy to write.

[pause]

{img:scribes-writing-aramaic-ink-parchment-and-clay-tablets-office}
Aramaic becomes the common language of the empire, used from Egypt to India, by officials, and merchants, and travellers, everywhere. Five hundred years later, in the time of Jesus, it will still be the everyday language of Palestine, and Jesus himself will speak it.

[long-pause]

In the government offices, there are scribes, many of them, who write the king's orders, and the letters, and the accounts, in all these languages, and translate between them. Every order of the king is written down, and copied, and sent out, by the royal messengers, to every province, in every language.

[pause]

In the book of Esther, in the Bible, it says that the king's letters were sent to every province, in its own script, and to every people, in its own language.

[pause]

{img:clay-tablet-cuneiform-wedges-reed-stylus-close-lamplight}
For two thousand years after the end of the Persian Empire, nobody could read cuneiform. The knowledge was lost. Then, in the nineteenth century, scholars began to decipher it, starting with Old Persian, the simplest, using the royal inscriptions of Persepolis, and the great inscription of Behistun, with its three languages, side by side, like the Rosetta Stone of Egypt.

[long-pause]

A young German schoolteacher, Georg Friedrich Grotefend, made the first breakthrough, in 1802, as a bet with a friend. He guessed that the inscriptions on the palaces of Persepolis would begin with the names and titles of kings, like the later Persian inscriptions: X, great king, king of kings, son of Y, great king. And he guessed which kings they might be, Darius, and Xerxes. And he was right.

[pause]

And so, after two thousand years, the voices of the Persian kings could be heard again.

[long-pause]

{img:apadana-staircase-relief-delegations-gifts-procession-carved}
Every spring, at Persepolis, there was a great festival, many historians believe: the festival of the New Year, at the spring equinox, on the twenty-first of March, when day and night are equal, and the sun begins to win over the darkness. The Persians call it Nowruz, new day.

[long-pause]

On the great staircases of the Apadana, there are carvings, which show what may be this festival: endless processions of people, carved in stone, walking up the stairs, toward the king.

[pause]

On one side, there are the Persians and the Medes: the nobles, and the guards, in their long, flowing robes, or their riding clothes, with their bows, and their spears, and their tall hats, chatting with each other, turning around to talk to the man behind, holding flowers, some of them holding hands, or putting a hand on a friend's shoulder. They look relaxed, and friendly, and happy.

[pause]

On the other side, there are the delegations of the peoples of the empire: twenty-three of them, each led by a Persian or Median usher, who holds the hand of the leader of the delegation. Each delegation wears its own costume, and brings its own gifts, from its own land.

[long-pause]

The Babylonians bring a humped bull, and cloth. The Lydians, from Turkey, bring golden bowls, and bracelets, and a chariot, with little horses. The Arabs bring a camel. The Indians bring baskets of gold dust, and a donkey. The Ethiopians bring an elephant's tusk, and an okapi, or a giraffe. The Bactrians, from Afghanistan, bring a two-humped camel. The Scythians bring horses, and bracelets. The Egyptians bring a bull. The Ionian Greeks bring cloth, and bowls. The Armenians bring a horse, and a wine jar.

[pause]

Each person in the procession is carved in fine detail: their faces, their beards, their hair, their clothes, their shoes, their gifts, the folds of their garments, the muscles of the animals. It is one of the greatest works of art of the ancient world.

[pause]

{img:carved-ethiopian-delegation-tusk-okapi-relief-stone-afternoon}
And what is striking is the peace in it. Nobody is in chains. Nobody is being beaten. The people are not shown as conquered prisoners, as they would be on the monuments of Egypt, or Assyria. They walk calmly, with dignity, with their gifts, side by side, all the peoples of the world, coming together, to honour the king, at the New Year.

[long-pause]

At the top of the stairs, in the great hall, the king sits on his throne, with his crown, and his sceptre, and a lotus flower in his hand, with the crown prince standing behind him, and his officials, and guards, and incense burners, receiving the gifts, and the greetings, of his peoples.

[pause]

And then, perhaps, there is a great feast, with thousands of guests, and music, and wine, and celebration, in the spring sunshine.

[pause]

{img:nowruz-table-spring-sprouts-apples-garlic-coins-goldfish-candles}
The festival of Nowruz is still celebrated today, two and a half thousand years later, by hundreds of millions of people, in Iran, and Afghanistan, and Central Asia, and the Caucasus, and among Kurds, and Persians all over the world: with spring cleaning, and new clothes, and visits to family, and a special table, with seven things whose names begin with the letter S, in Persian, symbols of the spring, and new life, and a bowl of goldfish, and a mirror, and candles, and painted eggs, and green sprouts of wheat.

[long-pause]

{img:royal-road-persian-messenger-galloping-relay-station-plain-dust}
To hold his great empire together, Darius needs roads. And the Persians build the best roads of the ancient world.

[pause]

The most famous is the Royal Road, which runs for about two thousand seven hundred kilometres, from Sardis, the old capital of Lydia, near the coast of the Aegean Sea, in the west, across the whole of Anatolia, and Mesopotamia, to Susa, one of the royal capitals, in the south west of Iran.

[long-pause]

Along the road, every day's journey, about twenty-five kilometres apart, there are royal stations: inns, with stables, and food, and water, and guards. Herodotus says there were a hundred and eleven of them, between Sardis and Susa. Ordinary travellers, walking, take about three months to make the whole journey.

[pause]

But the king's messengers are much faster.

[pause]

At each station, there are fresh horses, and fresh riders, waiting, day and night. A messenger gallops from one station to the next, and hands the message to the next rider, who gallops on, and hands it to the next. Like a relay race. In this way, a message from the king can travel the whole length of the Royal Road in about seven days.

[pause]

Herodotus admired this system very much. He wrote: there is nothing in the world that travels faster than these Persian messengers. Neither snow, nor rain, nor heat, nor darkness of night, prevents them from accomplishing the task proposed to them with the utmost speed.

[long-pause]

Two and a half thousand years later, the post office of New York, in the United States, carved Herodotus's words over the door of its great main building: Neither snow nor rain nor heat nor gloom of night stays these couriers from the swift completion of their appointed rounds. And many people think of them as the motto of the postmen of America.

[pause]

The messengers carry the king's orders, and letters, and reports, written on clay tablets, or on parchment, or on papyrus, in the Aramaic language, which is the common language of the empire, sealed with the royal seal.

[pause]

{img:scribe-writing-aramaic-parchment-seal-clay-office-lamp}
Travellers on the royal roads carry a document, a travel pass, sealed by an official, which says who they are, where they are going, and how much food they should be given, at each station along the way. Some of these travel passes have survived, and say things like: give him, each day, so much flour, so much wine, so much beer, and so much for his horses, from the stores of the king, at each station, until he reaches the king.

[long-pause]

There are other royal roads too, all over the empire: to Ecbatana, in the north, and Babylon, and Persepolis, and Bactria, and India, and Egypt.

[pause]

Along the roads travel not only the king's messengers, but soldiers, and officials, and merchants, with their caravans of donkeys, and mules, and camels, and pilgrims, and craftsmen, going to work on the king's palaces, and ambassadors, and families moving house, and the great court of the king himself, moving from one capital to another, with the seasons.

[pause]

{img:caravan-on-persian-road-mules-camels-mountains-poplar-trees}
The roads bring the empire together. Goods, and ideas, and people, and languages, and religions, travel along them, from one end of the world to the other, more easily than ever before.

[long-pause]

{img:satrap-palace-sardis-governor-receiving-officials-courtyard}
How does Darius rule an empire so large that it takes three months to walk from one end to the other?

[pause]

He divides it into about twenty great provinces, called satrapies. Each one is ruled by a satrap, a governor, usually a Persian nobleman, often a relative of the king, who lives in a palace, in the capital of the province, like a little king, with his own court, and his own guards, and his own paradise garden.

[long-pause]

The satrap collects the taxes, and the tribute, and sends them to the king. He keeps order, and judges disputes, and looks after the roads, and the royal stations, and the land. He makes sure that the king's orders are carried out.

[pause]

But the king does not trust anyone completely. So, in each province, there is also a royal secretary, and a commander of the soldiers, who report directly to the king, and keep an eye on the satrap. And there are inspectors, who travel around the empire, on behalf of the king, and visit the provinces, and see what is happening, and report back to the king. The Greeks call them the Eyes of the King, and the Ears of the King.

[pause]

{img:royal-inspector-arriving-provincial-town-escort-seal-officials}
In this way, the king knows what is happening everywhere in his empire, and no satrap can become too powerful, or rebel, without the king hearing about it.

[long-pause]

The peoples of the empire keep their own laws, and their own customs, and their own languages, and their own gods, and their own local rulers, as long as they obey the king, and pay their tribute, and send soldiers when they are needed.

[pause]

Darius also collects the laws of the different peoples, and has them written down. In Egypt, he orders the wise men to write down the old laws of Egypt, so that the Persian governors can judge the Egyptians by their own laws. Later, the Egyptians remember Darius as one of their wise lawgivers.

[pause]

{img:egyptian-scribes-copying-laws-persian-official-listening-temple}
And the king himself is the highest judge. Anyone in the empire can appeal to the king, if they believe they have been treated unjustly. And the Persian judges, Herodotus says, are chosen for life, unless they are found to be unjust. He tells the story of a judge who took a bribe, and was punished severely, by King Cambyses, who then made the judge's son a judge in his place, and told him to remember his father, every time he sat in judgment.

[long-pause]

It is a system that works. For two hundred years, the Persian Empire holds together, with its many peoples, and languages, and religions, more peacefully than most empires before it, or after it. Many later empires, the empire of Alexander, and the Romans, and many others, will learn from it, and copy it.

[long-pause]

{img:clay-tablets-elamite-cuneiform-ration-records-archive-room}
Who built Persepolis? And how did they live?

[pause]

In the 1930s, archaeologists digging at Persepolis found, in the fortification wall, thousands of little clay tablets, written in cuneiform, mostly in the Elamite language. They are the records of the royal administration, from the years 509 to 493 before Christ: exactly the time of our story.

[long-pause]

They are not great inscriptions about kings and battles. They are ordinary records: receipts, and lists, and accounts, of the food given out to the workers, and officials, and travellers, and animals, from the royal storehouses, in the region around Persepolis.

[pause]

And they tell us, in wonderful detail, about the lives of the ordinary people who built the palaces.

[pause]

The workers come from all over the empire: Persians, and Elamites, and Babylonians, and Egyptians, and Lydians, and Ionian Greeks, and Bactrians, and Indians, and many others. They are called kurtash. Some are skilled craftsmen: stone cutters, and sculptors, and carpenters, and goldsmiths, and weavers. Others are labourers. They work in groups, sometimes hundreds strong, under a foreman.

[long-pause]

They are paid, not with money, but with food: rations of grain, barley, or wheat, every month, and wine, or beer, and sometimes sheep, and fruit, according to their work, and their skill.

[pause]

And here is something remarkable. Women work too, as craftsmen, and are paid. And in some jobs, skilled women are paid the same as men, or even more. There are women who are the leaders of work groups, and are paid more than the men in their groups.

[pause]

And when a woman worker gives birth to a baby, she receives an extra ration, of grain, and wine, and meat, to help her: more for a boy, and a little less for a girl. And she receives a reduced ration, for some months, while she is not working, a kind of maternity pay, two and a half thousand years ago. The children of the workers receive small rations too.

[long-pause]

The tablets also record the travels of people across the empire: a group of workers, travelling from Egypt to Persepolis. An official, going to India, with his servants, and a sealed travel document. A woman, Irtashduna, a wife of King Darius, with her own estates, and her own workers, and her own steward, sending orders. A group of Lydian stone cutters. A man, taking horses to the king.

[pause]

They record the feeding of the king's horses, and camels, and cattle, and the grain given to the priests, for the sacrifices to the gods.

[pause]

{img:stone-cutters-workshop-carving-column-capital-persepolis-dust}
So, thanks to these little tablets, we know the names of thousands of ordinary people who lived two and a half thousand years ago, and how much bread they ate, and how much wine they drank, and where they came from, and where they were going.

[long-pause]

And in the great inscription that Darius carved at Susa, about the building of his palace there, he tells us where the materials came from, and who worked on them.

[pause]

The cedar wood came from Lebanon. The gold from Sardis and Bactria. The lapis lazuli and carnelian from Sogdiana. The turquoise from Chorasmia. The silver and ebony from Egypt. The ivory from Ethiopia, and India, and Arachosia. The stone columns from a village in Elam.

[pause]

{img:craftsmen-many-nations-working-palace-susa-gold-ivory-cedar}
The stone cutters were Ionians and Sardians. The goldsmiths were Medes and Egyptians. The men who worked the wood were Sardians and Egyptians. The men who made the baked bricks were Babylonians. The men who decorated the walls were Medes and Egyptians.

[pause]

The whole world, it seems, came together, to build the palaces of the King of Kings.

[long-pause]

{img:pazyryk-style-carpet-red-borders-horsemen-deer-knotted-wool}
The Persians are famous, all over the world, for their carpets. And the oldest carpet in the world may come from the time of our story.

[pause]

In 1949, a Russian archaeologist, Sergei Rudenko, was digging in the frozen tombs of the Scythian nomads, in the Altai mountains, in Siberia, at a place called Pazyryk. In one of the tombs, which had been filled with ice, for nearly two and a half thousand years, he found a carpet, almost perfectly preserved.

[long-pause]

It is a knotted pile carpet, like a modern Persian carpet, about two metres square, of wool, with more than a million knots, beautifully made, in deep red, and blue, and yellow, and green. In the middle, there is a pattern of squares, with stars. Around it, there are borders: one with a procession of deer, grazing, and another with horsemen, riding, and walking beside their horses, like the processions on the walls of Persepolis.

[pause]

It was made around the fourth or fifth century before Christ. Nobody knows exactly where. Some think it was made by the Scythians themselves. But many experts think that it was made in Persia, or by Persian craftsmen, and traded far to the north, along the routes of the steppes.

[pause]

{img:weaver-women-carpet-loom-knotting-wool-courtyard-sunlight}
To make a knotted carpet, the weavers set up the warp, long threads, on a loom, upright, or flat on the ground. Then they tie little knots of coloured wool, one by one, around the warp threads, row after row, and cut the ends short, to make the soft pile. A good carpet may have hundreds of knots in every square centimetre, and take months, or years, to make, by a family of weavers, working together.

[long-pause]

The wool comes from the sheep of the mountains, and the plains, spun by hand. The colours come from plants and insects: red from the root of the madder plant, and from tiny insects, called kermes. Blue from indigo. Yellow from saffron, and other plants. Brown and black from walnut husks, and oak galls.

[pause]

The nomads, moving with their flocks, make carpets too, on little looms that they can roll up, and carry on their camels: carpets for the floors of their tents, and bags, and covers for their animals, with bold patterns, and bright colours, that tell where they come from.

[pause]

{img:nomad-tent-interior-carpets-cushions-family-tea-evening}
In the palaces of Persepolis, and Susa, the floors are covered with carpets, and the walls with hangings, of wool, and silk, and gold thread. And the king sits on carpets, and walks on carpets, and gives carpets as gifts.

[pause]

Two and a half thousand years later, Persian carpets are still among the most beautiful things made by human hands, and are found in homes all over the world.

[long-pause]

{img:persian-paradise-garden-four-water-channels-fruit-trees-pavilion}
The Persians love gardens more than anything.

[pause]

All over the empire, the king, and the satraps, and the nobles, make great gardens: walled parks, with trees, and flowers, and streams, and fountains, and pavilions, and wild animals, for hunting. The Persians call them pairidaeza, which means a walled enclosure. The Greeks heard the word, and made it paradeisos. And from it comes our word paradise.

[long-pause]

The most famous kind of Persian garden is the chahar bagh, the fourfold garden: a square garden, divided into four parts, by two water channels, crossing each other in the middle, where there is a pool, or a fountain, or a pavilion. It is a picture of the world, with its four rivers, and its four quarters. And of paradise.

[pause]

At Pasargadae, the old capital of Cyrus, archaeologists have found the remains of such a garden: the stone water channels, with little basins, at regular intervals, where the water flowed, cool and clear, among the trees and flowers. It is the oldest garden of this kind that we know of.

[pause]

In the gardens, there are trees of every kind: cypresses, tall and dark, and plane trees, broad and shady, and pomegranates, and apricots, and peaches, and pears, and figs, and almonds, and pistachios, and walnuts, and vines. And flowers: roses, above all, which the Persians love, and lilies, and tulips, and irises, and jasmine, and narcissus.

[long-pause]

The Greek writer Xenophon tells a story about a Spartan general, Lysander, who visited the young Persian prince, Cyrus the Younger, a hundred years after our story, in his garden, at Sardis. Lysander admired the trees, planted in perfect rows, and the beauty, and the scent, of the garden, and asked who had planned it. And Cyrus said: I planned it all myself, and planted some of the trees with my own hands. Lysander was astonished, looking at the prince's fine clothes, and jewellery. And Cyrus swore that he never sat down to dinner, if he was well, without first working in his garden, or exercising, until he sweated.

[pause]

{img:prince-planting-tree-garden-rows-cypress-visitor-admiring}
The Persian kings plant trees, as a duty, and a pleasure. And Darius writes to one of his satraps, in Asia Minor, praising him for planting fruit trees, from beyond the Euphrates, in his province.

[long-pause]

The great parks, called paradises, are also hunting parks, full of deer, and wild boar, and lions, where the king and his nobles hunt on horseback, with bows, and spears.

[pause]

And in the heat of the summer, the king and his family, and his court, sit in the shade of the pavilions, in the gardens, by the cool water, and eat fruit, and drink wine, and listen to music, and poetry, and the singing of the birds, and the splashing of the fountains, and the wind in the leaves of the poplars.

[pause]

{img:shaded-pavilion-garden-fountain-courtiers-fruit-afternoon-calm}
For the Persians, a garden is the most beautiful thing in the world: a little piece of paradise on earth, made by human hands, in the middle of a dry and dusty land.

[long-pause]

{img:persian-king-hunting-chariot-bow-lions-paradise-park-relief}
The favourite pastime of the Persian kings and nobles is hunting.

[pause]

In the great hunting parks, the paradises, and in the mountains, and the plains, they hunt deer, and gazelles, and wild boar, and wild asses, and bears, and leopards, and lions. They hunt on horseback, with bows, and spears, and javelins, at full gallop, and in chariots. A Persian seal, from the time of Darius, shows the king in his chariot, shooting arrows at a lion, which is rearing up in front of the horses.

[long-pause]

Hunting is training for war: it teaches the young men to ride hard, over rough ground, and to shoot straight, from a galloping horse, and to be brave, and quick, and to work together.

[pause]

The Persians love horses more than anything, after their gardens. The finest horses in the world are the Nisaean horses, bred on the great Nisaean plain, in Media, in the west of Iran: tall, strong, swift, and beautiful, often white, or golden, or dappled. Herodotus says that the Medes keep fifty thousand mares on the Nisaean plain, to breed them.

[pause]

{img:nisaean-horses-white-golden-grazing-green-plain-mountains}
Sacred white horses, of the Nisaean breed, pull the empty chariot of Ahura Mazda, in the royal processions, ahead of the king. The horses of the king's chariot are always Nisaean horses. And the king gives Nisaean horses, as the most precious of gifts, to his friends, and to foreign kings.

[long-pause]

Every Persian boy learns to ride, from the age of five. Persian horsemen ride without stirrups, which have not been invented yet, gripping the horse with their knees, and their trousers protect their legs. They can shoot backwards, while galloping away, a skill that the peoples of the steppes, the Scythians, and later the Parthians, are famous for.

[pause]

The Persians also love polo, the game of horsemen, with sticks, and a ball, which may have been invented in Persia, a little later, and is played by Persian kings, and princes, and princesses, for centuries. The great square of the city of Isfahan, built two thousand years later, will have stone goalposts for polo, at each end.

[pause]

{img:early-polo-game-riders-sticks-ball-plain-persian-dust}
In the evenings, after the hunt, the king and the nobles feast, on the game they have caught, roasted over the fire, and drink wine, and tell stories of the day, and of the great hunts of the past, and of the great horses they have known.

[long-pause]

{img:lion-attacking-bull-relief-persepolis-stone-carving-sunlight}
On the walls and staircases of Persepolis, there is one carving that appears again and again: a lion, leaping onto the back of a bull, and biting it.

[pause]

Nobody knows exactly what it means. Some scholars think it is a symbol of the New Year: the lion, the sign of the sun, and summer, overcoming the bull, the sign of winter, at the spring equinox, when the constellation of the lion is high in the sky, and the bull disappears. Others think it is a symbol of the power of the king. It is carved so beautifully, with such life, that it is one of the most famous images of ancient Persia.

[long-pause]

The Persians love animals, real and imaginary, in their art.

[pause]

There are lions, everywhere: on the walls, and on the jewellery, and the seals, and the cups. There were still real lions in Persia, and in Mesopotamia, in those days, and the kings hunted them. There are bulls, and horses, and ibexes, with great curling horns, and eagles, and rams.

[pause]

{img:griffin-capital-persepolis-column-eagle-head-lion-body-carved}
And there are imaginary animals: griffins, with the body of a lion, and the head and wings of an eagle. Winged bulls, with the heads of men, which guard the gates. And the homa, a great mythical bird, which brings good luck to anyone it flies over. On the tops of some columns at Persepolis, there are great double capitals, in the shape of two griffins, or two bulls, back to back, holding up the roof beams.

[long-pause]

In the paradise gardens, the kings keep real animals, too, from all over the empire: deer, and gazelles, and wild asses, and ostriches, and peacocks, and camels, and elephants, from India, and lions, and leopards, and cheetahs, which are trained to hunt.

[pause]

And in the Persian countryside, there are wolves, and bears, and wild boar, and foxes, and jackals, and hares, and hedgehogs, and porcupines, and storks, and eagles, and vultures, and the little hoopoes, with their crests, and the nightingales, singing in the gardens, at night, which the Persian poets will love, for thousands of years.

[long-pause]

{img:fire-altar-mountain-top-priests-white-robes-mouth-veils-dawn}
The Persians worship a great god, Ahura Mazda, the Wise Lord, the creator of all good things.

[pause]

Their religion comes from a prophet, called Zarathustra, whom the Greeks called Zoroaster. Nobody knows exactly when he lived: perhaps around a thousand years before Christ, perhaps a little later, somewhere in the east of Iran, or in Central Asia. He was a priest, and a poet, who had visions of Ahura Mazda, and taught the people about him, in hymns, called the Gathas, which are still recited today.

[long-pause]

Zarathustra taught that the world is a battlefield, between good and evil. On one side, there is Ahura Mazda, the Wise Lord, the god of light, and truth, and order, and goodness. On the other side, there is the destructive spirit, the spirit of darkness, and lies, and chaos.

[pause]

Every person must choose, freely, which side to be on. And the way to choose good is simple, Zarathustra said: good thoughts, good words, good deeds. Humata, hukhta, hvarshta.

[pause]

At the end of time, he taught, good will win. The dead will rise, and be judged, and the world will be made new, and perfect, and evil will be no more.

[pause]

Some of these ideas, about heaven, and hell, and the last judgment, and the struggle between good and evil, and angels, may have influenced the Jews, who lived in the Persian Empire, and through them, Christianity, and Islam.

[long-pause]

For the Persians, the most sacred things are fire, and water, and earth, and air, which are pure, and must not be polluted.

[pause]

Fire is the symbol of Ahura Mazda, and of truth, and light. In their temples, the Persian priests, called the Magi, keep sacred fires burning, day and night, for centuries, never letting them go out. When they tend the fire, they cover their mouths with a white cloth, so that their breath will not pollute it. The Persians worship in the open air, too, on the tops of mountains, under the sky.

[pause]

{img:sacred-flame-fire-temple-dim-interior-priest-veil-tending}
Herodotus says that the Persians do not build temples, or statues, of the gods, as the Greeks do, but go up to the highest mountains, and make sacrifices to the whole circle of the sky, and to the sun, and the moon, and the earth, and fire, and water, and the winds.

[long-pause]

The Magi are the priests of Persia, a tribe, or a class, of wise men, who know the rituals, and the prayers, and the stars, and interpret dreams. Five hundred years later, according to the Gospel of Matthew, three wise men, Magi, will come from the East, following a star, to Bethlehem, to see the baby Jesus.

[pause]

Rivers, too, are sacred. Herodotus says that the Persians never wash their hands in a river, or spit in it, or pollute it in any way, and respect rivers very much.

[pause]

{img:clear-mountain-river-persian-landscape-poplars-sacred-calm}
And truth is sacred. Herodotus says that Persian boys are taught three things, from the age of five to twenty: to ride a horse, to shoot a bow, and to tell the truth. The Persians think that the most shameful thing of all is to tell a lie, and the next most shameful, to owe money, because a man who owes money will be tempted to lie.

[long-pause]

The religion of Zarathustra is still alive today. There are still Zoroastrians, in Iran, around the city of Yazd, where a sacred fire has been burning, they say, for fifteen hundred years. And in India, where they are called Parsis, the Persians, because their ancestors fled from Persia, more than a thousand years ago. They still keep the sacred fires, and say the old prayers, and try to live by the old rule: good thoughts, good words, good deeds.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonian-astronomers-ziggurat-roof-night-stars-clay-tablets}
In Babylon, which is part of the Persian Empire, there are the best astronomers in the world.

[pause]

For hundreds of years, every night, the priests of Babylon have climbed to the top of the great temple tower, the ziggurat, or the roofs of the temples, and watched the sky, and written down what they saw, on clay tablets: the positions of the moon, and the planets, and the stars, and eclipses, and the weather, and the level of the river, and the prices of barley, and dates, and wool, in the market.

[long-pause]

These records are called the Astronomical Diaries. They were kept, night after night, from about 650 before Christ, until the time of the Romans, for six hundred years: the longest scientific record ever kept, before modern times.

[pause]

From them, the Babylonian astronomers learned to predict eclipses of the moon, and the movements of the planets, using mathematics, with numbers based on sixty. It is because of the Babylonians that we still divide an hour into sixty minutes, and a minute into sixty seconds, and a circle into three hundred and sixty degrees.

[pause]

They divided the path of the sun and the planets through the sky into twelve parts, each with its own group of stars, its constellation: the bull, the twins, the lion, the scales, the scorpion, and so on. The signs of the zodiac, which are still known all over the world today.

[long-pause]

Under the Persian kings, the astronomers of Babylon went on with their work, undisturbed. And their knowledge spread, through the Persian Empire, to the Greeks, who learned from them, and later to India, and the Arabs, and Europe.

[pause]

The Magi of Persia, too, watched the stars, and studied them, and interpreted them, as signs of the will of the gods. The word magic comes from their name.

[pause]

{img:persian-plain-night-sky-milky-way-shepherd-fire-mountains}
And on clear nights, all over the empire, ordinary people too, the shepherds on the mountains, and the farmers on their roofs, and the travellers in the caravanserais, and the soldiers on the walls, looked up at the same stars, and knew their names, and told stories about them, and found their way by them, in the great, dark, silent night of the ancient world.

[long-pause]

{img:persian-boys-learning-horseback-archery-plain-instructor-morning}
How are the sons of the Persian nobles brought up?

[pause]

The Greek writers, Herodotus, and Xenophon, and later Strabo, describe it with admiration. Until the age of five, a Persian boy lives with his mother, and the women of the household, and his father hardly sees him. Herodotus says that this is so that, if the child should die young, his father will not grieve too much.

[long-pause]

From five, until twenty, the boy is educated, together with the other boys of noble families, often at the court of the king, or the satrap.

[pause]

They get up before dawn, and are gathered together, by the sound of a brass trumpet, and taken out to exercise. They run, and swim, and ride, and throw the javelin, and shoot with the bow. They learn to endure heat, and cold, and rain, and hunger, and thirst, and tiredness. They learn to hunt, on horseback, in the hunting parks, and in the mountains, and to look after their horses, and to make camp.

[pause]

They learn to sing the hymns of the gods, and to tell the stories of the heroes, and the great deeds of the kings. They learn justice, Xenophon says, in their schools, as Greek boys learn to read and write: they bring disputes to their teachers, and the teachers judge them, and punish the boys who are ungrateful, or dishonest, or unfair.

[long-pause]

And above all, they learn three things, as Herodotus says: to ride, to shoot, and to tell the truth.

[pause]

Darius himself, in the inscription on his tomb, boasts of these things. He says: I am trained in my hands and my feet. As a horseman, I am a good horseman. As a bowman, I am a good bowman, both on foot and on horseback. As a spearman, I am a good spearman, both on foot and on horseback.

[pause]

{img:young-persian-noble-bow-quiver-horse-riding-clothes-proud}
The Persians are famous horsemen. Their horses are the finest in the world: the great Nisaean horses, bred on the plains of Media, tall, and strong, and beautiful, and fast, which only the king, and the greatest nobles, may ride.

[long-pause]

The Persians wear trousers, which the Greeks find very strange, and funny, because the Greeks wear tunics, and cloaks, and nothing on their legs. Persian trousers are practical, for riding horses. They wear long-sleeved tunics, and coats, and soft felt caps, or hoods, which cover their ears and chins, against the wind and the dust. And at court, long, flowing robes, with wide sleeves, and tall, fluted hats.

[pause]

The nobles wear gold jewellery: necklaces, called torques, and bracelets, often with the heads of animals, lions, and ibexes, and griffins, at the ends, which the king gives them, as rewards for their service.

[pause]

{img:golden-torque-bracelet-ibex-heads-oxus-treasure-close-light}
Many of these beautiful golden ornaments were found, in the nineteenth century, by the river Oxus, in Central Asia, in a great hoard, called the Oxus Treasure, which is now in the British Museum, in London: little golden chariots, with horses, and golden bracelets, with griffins, and golden plaques, with figures of priests, and golden fish, and rings, and coins.

[long-pause]

{img:susa-market-street-merchants-silver-weights-goods-many-peoples}
Let us visit the market of a great Persian city, in the year 500 before Christ.

[pause]

It is crowded, and noisy, and colourful. Merchants from every part of the empire are there: Babylonians, with their long hair, and their long robes, and their seals, selling cloth, and dates, and grain. Phoenicians, from the coast of Lebanon, selling purple cloth, and glass, and cedar wood. Egyptians, selling linen, and papyrus. Lydians, selling gold, and perfumes. Arabs, with frankincense, and myrrh, from the far south of Arabia. Indians, selling cotton, and spices, and ivory. Bactrians, with lapis lazuli, and horses.

[long-pause]

They buy and sell with silver, weighed out on scales, in pieces, and with the new coins of Darius: the golden daric, and the silver siglos. A golden daric is worth about a month's wages for a soldier.

[pause]

And there are bankers, too. In Babylon, there is a great family of bankers and businessmen, called the Egibi family, whose records, on clay tablets, survive, for five generations: loans, and rents, and the buying and selling of houses, and land, and slaves, and dates, and barley, and the marriage contracts, and the wills, of the family members.

[pause]

{img:babylonian-banker-clay-tablets-scales-silver-client-office}
In the market, there are craftsmen at work: potters, and weavers, and dyers, and leather workers, and metalworkers, and jewellers, and bakers, and butchers, and wine sellers.

[long-pause]

There are food stalls, selling bread, flat and round, baked on hot stones, and dates, and figs, and raisins, and nuts, and cheese, and yoghurt, and fruit, and roasted meat, and beer, and wine.

[pause]

There are storytellers, and musicians, and fortune tellers, who read the future in the stars, or in the flight of birds, or in oil poured on water. There are beggars, and children, and dogs, and donkeys, and camels.

[pause]

{img:storyteller-marketplace-crowd-listening-shade-awning-afternoon}
And everywhere, there are people talking: in Persian, and Aramaic, and Elamite, and Babylonian, and Greek, and Egyptian, and many other languages. Bargaining, and gossiping, and greeting each other. The Persians greet each other, Herodotus says, in different ways, according to their rank: equals kiss each other on the lips. A man of slightly lower rank kisses the other on the cheek. And a man of much lower rank bows down to the ground.

[long-pause]

As the sun goes down, the market closes. The merchants pack up their goods, and go home, or to the caravanserai. The donkeys are led away, with their loads. The lamps are lit in the houses. And the city settles down for the night, under the stars, which the Babylonian astronomers have watched, and recorded, every night, for hundreds of years.

[long-pause]

{img:royal-banquet-persian-king-behind-curtain-guests-hall-feast}
The king of Persia eats like no one else in the world.

[pause]

Every day, the Greek writers say, thousands of people eat at the king's table: his family, his nobles, his guards, his officials, his servants, his guests. And the food is enormous in quantity, and wonderful in quality, brought from every part of the empire.

[long-pause]

A Greek writer, Polyaenus, preserved a list, which he said was inscribed on a bronze pillar, in the palace, of the food needed for the king's dinner, every day: hundreds of measures of fine wheat flour, and barley flour, and hundreds of oxen, and horses, and sheep, and geese, and pigeons, and little birds, and gazelles. And milk, and cheese, and garlic, and onions, and dates, and raisins, and almonds, and pistachios, and apples, and quinces, and pomegranates, and wine, and oil, and honey, and salt, and saffron, and cardamom, and cumin, and mustard, and many other things.

[pause]

The Persian king eats alone, or with his queen, and a few of his family, behind a curtain, so that he can see his guests, but they cannot see him. Only on great feast days do they see the king eat.

[long-pause]

The Persians love sweet things, and fruit, and desserts. Herodotus says that the Persians eat few main dishes, but many desserts, one after another, and that they laugh at the Greeks, who, they say, leave the table still hungry, because they have no proper desserts.

[pause]

And they love wine. Herodotus tells a famous story: that the Persians make all their important decisions twice. First, they discuss the question when they are drunk, at a feast. And the next day, when they are sober, they discuss it again. If they still agree, they do it. And if they first discuss something when sober, they discuss it again when drunk.

[pause]

{img:persian-nobles-reclining-feast-wine-cups-musicians-evening}
It is probably a joke. But the Persians do enjoy their feasts, with wine, in golden cups, shaped like the heads of animals, called rhytons, and music, and singing, and dancing, and poetry, and long conversations, late into the night.

[long-pause]

The Persians are also great inventors of food. Many of the foods that we eat today first came from Persia, or travelled through Persia, to the West: peaches, which the Romans called Persian apples. Pistachios. Spinach. Saffron. Pomegranates. Apricots. And chickens, which came from India, through Persia, to the Greeks, who called the rooster the Persian bird.

[pause]

Persian cooks are famous for their sauces, of fruit and nuts and herbs and spices, sweet and sour together, which are still eaten in Iran today: chicken, with pomegranates and walnuts. Lamb, with dried apricots. Rice, with saffron.

[pause]

{img:pomegranates-pistachios-apricots-saffron-market-baskets-sunlight}
And the king rewards his friends, and his faithful servants, with gifts from his own table: a dish of food, sent from the king's own plate, is a great honour, and the person who receives it is the envy of the whole court.

[long-pause]

{img:royal-court-travelling-caravan-wagons-horses-mountain-pass-persian}
The King of Kings does not live in one place. He has several capitals, and the whole royal court moves between them, with the seasons.

[pause]

In the winter, the court lives at Susa, in the warm lowlands of Elam, in the south west of Iran, near the border of Iraq, where the winters are mild, and the summers burning hot. Darius has built a great palace there, with walls of glazed bricks, in bright colours, blue, and yellow, and white, and green, showing lions, and griffins, and rows of archers, the royal guard, in their long robes, with their spears and their bows.

[long-pause]

In the spring, the court moves to Persepolis, for the New Year festival, and to Babylon, the ancient city, on the Euphrates, the richest and largest city of the empire.

[pause]

And in the summer, when the lowlands are too hot, the court moves north, up into the mountains, to Ecbatana, the old capital of the Medes, where the air is cool, and fresh, among the green valleys, and the snow-capped mountains. Herodotus says that Ecbatana had seven walls, one inside the other, each painted a different colour: white, black, purple, blue, orange, silver, and gold.

[pause]

When the court moves, it is like a whole city moving. The king, and his family, and his wives, and his children, and the nobles, and the officials, and the guards, and the servants, and the cooks, and the musicians, and the horses, and the chariots, and the wagons, and the camels, and the mules, carrying tents, and furniture, and treasure, and food, and everything needed for the royal life.

[long-pause]

On the road, the king travels in a chariot, or in a covered carriage, with curtains, or on horseback. In front of him, there are the guards, and behind him, the long procession, stretching for kilometres along the road.

[pause]

And Herodotus says that, wherever the king goes, he drinks only water from one river, the Choaspes, which flows past Susa. The water is boiled, and stored in silver jars, and carried with the king, in wagons pulled by mules, wherever he goes, so that he never has to drink any other water.

[pause]

{img:silver-water-jars-mule-wagons-royal-road-escort-sunlight}
At night, the court camps, in great tents, like palaces of cloth, with carpets, and hangings, and couches, and lamps. The king's tent is magnificent, with columns of gilded wood, and hangings of embroidered cloth.

[long-pause]

At Susa, in 1901, French archaeologists found, written on a clay tablet, an ancient code of laws, carved on a tall black stone pillar: the laws of Hammurabi, king of Babylon, who lived twelve hundred years before Darius. It had been carried to Susa as war booty, by an Elamite king, long before. And it is now in the Louvre museum, in Paris.

[pause]

And at Susa, too, according to the Bible, lived Esther, the Jewish girl who became queen of Persia, and saved her people, in the time of a Persian king called Ahasuerus, which may be Xerxes, the son of Darius. The story of Esther is still remembered every year, by Jews all over the world, at the festival of Purim, with costumes, and noise, and gifts of food.

[pause]

{img:susa-mound-citadel-plain-river-palms-ancient-town-evening}
And at Susa, as in all the royal capitals, the king's palaces are surrounded by gardens, and parks, and orchards, and the people of the town gather at the gates, every morning, to see the king's officials go in and out, and to present their petitions, and to hear the news of the empire.

[long-pause]

{img:jews-returning-jerusalem-road-caravan-donkeys-hills-dawn}
One of the peoples of the Persian Empire, who remembered the Persian kings with gratitude for thousands of years, are the Jews.

[pause]

When Cyrus conquered Babylon, in 539 before Christ, there were many Jews living there, the descendants of the people of Jerusalem, who had been carried away to Babylon by King Nebuchadnezzar, fifty years before, when he destroyed Jerusalem, and its temple.

[long-pause]

Cyrus allowed them to go home. And many of them did, in groups, over the following years: a long journey, on foot, and with donkeys, and camels, across the desert, from Babylon, to Jerusalem, which was in ruins.

[pause]

In the time of Darius, around 520 to 515 before Christ, just a few years before our story, they rebuilt the temple of Jerusalem, with the permission, and the help, of the Persian king, who ordered his officials in the province to give them money, and timber, and supplies, for the building. The book of Ezra, in the Bible, preserves the letter of Darius, ordering this.

[pause]

{img:second-temple-jerusalem-building-workers-stones-hills-morning}
It is called the Second Temple. And it stood for nearly six hundred years.

[long-pause]

But many Jews stayed in Babylon, where they had houses, and families, and businesses, and lived there, as a great community, for more than two thousand years, until modern times.

[pause]

And there were Jews in other parts of the empire too. On the island of Elephantine, in the Nile, in the far south of Egypt, at the border with Nubia, there was a garrison of Jewish soldiers, serving the Persian king, with their families, and their own temple. Their letters, and contracts, and accounts, written in Aramaic, on papyrus, have survived: marriage contracts, and loans, and the sale of houses, and letters about the festival of Passover, and a letter asking the Persian governor for permission to rebuild their temple. Through them, we can see the everyday life of a Jewish family, in Egypt, under the Persian kings, two and a half thousand years ago.

[pause]

{img:elephantine-island-nile-houses-palms-boats-calm-afternoon}
In the Bible, the Persian kings are remembered kindly: Cyrus, and Darius, and Artaxerxes, who sent the scholar Ezra, and the governor Nehemiah, to Jerusalem, to rebuild its walls, and restore its laws.

[pause]

And the story of Esther, the Jewish queen of Persia, at Susa, is still told every year, at Purim. And in the Iranian city of Hamadan, which was once Ecbatana, there is a tomb, which is said to be the tomb of Esther and her cousin Mordecai, and Jewish pilgrims have visited it for centuries.

[long-pause]

{img:qanat-shaft-mounds-line-across-desert-plain-village-green}
Most of the people of the Persian Empire are farmers, as in all ancient countries. And in Iran, the greatest problem for farmers is water.

[pause]

Much of Iran is dry: high plateaus, and deserts, with little rain, especially in summer. The rivers are few. But in the mountains, there is snow, and rain, and the water sinks into the ground, and flows, deep underground, toward the plains.

[long-pause]

So the Persians invented a wonderful way of bringing this water to their fields: the qanat.

[pause]

A qanat is an underground canal, a tunnel, dug from the foot of the mountains, where there is water deep under the ground, sloping very gently downward, for many kilometres, sometimes tens of kilometres, until it comes out at the surface, in the plain, where the water flows out, into a channel, and into the fields, and the gardens, and the village.

[pause]

To dig a qanat, the diggers first dig a deep well, a mother well, at the foot of the mountains, down to the water. Then they dig a line of other wells, or shafts, every few dozen metres, across the plain, and join them, underground, by a tunnel, just big enough for a man to crawl through, with exactly the right slope, so that the water flows gently, but not too fast. It is very difficult, and skilled, and dangerous work. The men who do it, the muqannis, are greatly respected.

[long-pause]

From above, a qanat looks like a long line of little round mounds of earth, like giant molehills, stretching across the desert, each one with a hole in the middle: the shafts, with the earth that was dug out piled around them.

[pause]

The water in the qanat is cool, and clean, and flows all year round, even in the hottest summer, because it comes from deep underground, and does not evaporate in the sun.

[pause]

{img:village-garden-qanat-water-emerging-channel-pomegranate-trees}
The Persian kings encourage the building of qanats. The Greek historian Polybius says that if a man brought water to land that had never been watered before, the king would let him, and his family, farm it, without paying tax, for five generations.

[pause]

There are still tens of thousands of qanats in Iran today, and some of them are thousands of years old, and still flowing. They are a World Heritage Site.

[long-pause]

With the water of the qanats, and the rivers, the farmers grow wheat, and barley, and rice, in some places, and sesame, and cotton, and vegetables, and alfalfa, for the horses. They grow orchards of fruit: apples, and pears, and pomegranates, and apricots, and quinces, and grapes, and figs, and dates, in the hot south. And nuts: almonds, and walnuts, and pistachios.

[pause]

They keep sheep, and goats, and cattle, and donkeys, and camels, and the nomads move their flocks, with the seasons, from the warm plains in winter, to the cool mountain pastures in summer, as they have done for thousands of years, and still do today.

[pause]

{img:nomads-moving-flocks-mountain-pasture-black-tents-summer}
The villages are built of mud brick, with flat roofs, or domes, and courtyards, with a pool, and a fruit tree, and a little garden. And in the hottest places, the houses have wind catchers: tall towers on the roof, open to the wind, which catch the breeze, and send it down into the house, over a pool of water, to cool it. The first air conditioning.

[long-pause]

{img:yakhchal-ice-house-dome-mud-brick-desert-shade-wall-winter}
In the hot, dry lands of Persia, the people found a clever way to have ice in summer, in the middle of the desert.

[pause]

They built ice houses, called yakhchals, which means ice pits: great domes of thick mud brick, like huge beehives, rising from the desert, over a deep pit, dug into the ground. The oldest ones may go back to the time of the Persian kings, or a little later.

[long-pause]

In the winter, on cold, clear nights, the people let water flow, from a qanat, into long, shallow channels, or pools, beside the ice house, in the shade of a tall wall, built on the south side, to keep off the sun. The water freezes in the night. In the morning, the workers break the ice, and carry it into the pit, under the dome, and pack it with straw, and sawdust, to keep it cold.

[pause]

The walls of the dome are very thick, two metres or more, made of a special mortar, of sand, and clay, and egg whites, and lime, and goat hair, and ash, which keeps out the heat. And the shape of the dome lets the warm air rise, and escape, through a hole at the top, while the cold air sinks down into the pit.

[pause]

{img:workers-carrying-ice-blocks-into-dome-pit-straw-cold-morning}
And so, in the middle of the burning summer, when the desert is so hot that the air shimmers, the people can take ice from the yakhchal, and cool their water, and make cold drinks, and even a kind of frozen dessert, of ice, and rose water, and fruit, and noodles, which the Persians still love today, called faloodeh.

[long-pause]

Together with the qanats, which bring water under the desert, and the wind catchers, which cool the houses, the ice houses show how the people of Persia learned to live, comfortably, in one of the hottest and driest lands on earth, using only mud, and water, and wind, and the cold of the winter nights.

[pause]

Some of these old ice houses still stand today, in the deserts of Iran, around the cities of Yazd, and Kerman, with their great brown domes, like giant beehives, against the blue sky.

[long-pause]

{img:persian-queen-atossa-royal-robes-veil-palace-garden-dignified}
The women of the Persian royal family are powerful and important, much more than the Greeks expected.

[pause]

Darius has several wives. The most important is Atossa, the daughter of Cyrus the Great. She was married before to her brother, Cambyses, and then to the usurper who took the throne, and then to Darius, who married her, to show that he belonged to the family of Cyrus. She is clever, and proud, and very influential. Herodotus says that she had great power in the palace, and that it was through her influence that her son, Xerxes, became the heir, rather than Darius's older sons by another wife.

[long-pause]

His favourite wife, Herodotus says, is Artystone, another daughter of Cyrus. Darius loved her so much that he had a golden statue made of her. The Persepolis tablets record her as Irtashduna, and show that she owned her own estates, and her own workers, and her own steward, and gave her own orders, and travelled with her own household, and received huge amounts of wine, and grain, and sheep, for her household and her feasts.

[pause]

The royal women of Persia are rich. They own land, and villages, and workshops, and manage them through their own officials. They travel. They give banquets. They have their own seals, to sign their orders. They are not shut away, as Greek women often are.

[pause]

{img:royal-woman-seal-stamping-order-clay-tablet-steward-waiting}
The tablets mention many other women too: princesses, and noblewomen, and ordinary working women, with their own names, and their own rations, and their own jobs: weavers, and wine makers, and supervisors of work groups.

[long-pause]

The Persian queens and princesses wear beautiful clothes: long robes, of fine wool, or linen, or silk, dyed in rich colours, purple, and blue, and red, and embroidered, with veils, and crowns, and golden jewellery.

[pause]

They ride horses, and travel in covered carriages. They keep their own court, with their own servants, and their own guards. And their children are brought up in their households, until the age of five, as Herodotus says.

[pause]

{img:noblewomen-riding-horses-garden-paradise-attendants-spring}
In later centuries, the Greek writers will tell many dramatic stories about the Persian queens, about their power, and their jealousy, and their schemes, in the palace. Some of these stories are probably exaggerated. But they show how important the royal women of Persia were.

[long-pause]

And in Persian art, and later Persian poetry, women are honoured, and loved, and celebrated: the mother, the wife, the beloved, in the garden, among the roses, and the nightingales, which, two thousand years later, will become the great themes of the poetry of Persia, of Hafez, and Saadi, and Rumi.

[long-pause]

{img:persian-immortals-guard-spears-golden-apples-robes-palace-gate}
The king of Persia is protected by his guard: ten thousand chosen soldiers, the best of the Persian army, called by the Greeks the Immortals.

[pause]

They were called that, Herodotus says, because their number was always kept at exactly ten thousand. If one of them fell ill, or passed away, another man was chosen, at once, to take his place, so that there were never more, and never fewer, than ten thousand.

[long-pause]

A thousand of them, the most noble, carry spears with golden pomegranates on the end, instead of the usual metal butt, and are the king's personal bodyguard, always close to him. The others carry spears with silver pomegranates.

[pause]

On the walls of the palace at Susa, they are shown in glazed bricks, in bright colours: tall, bearded men, in long, flowing robes, richly patterned, yellow and white and brown, with their spears held upright, and their bows, and quivers, on their shoulders, walking in a long, calm, dignified line, one behind the other, on a background of blue. They are among the most beautiful works of ancient art, and can be seen today, in the Louvre, in Paris.

[pause]

{img:glazed-brick-archers-frieze-susa-blue-background-museum-light}
The Immortals are the elite of the army. They are given the best food, and the best equipment, and travel with their families, and their servants, in covered carriages, and camels carry their food.

[long-pause]

The Persian army itself is enormous, and made of soldiers from every part of the empire: Persian and Median infantry, with their wicker shields, and their spears, and their bows. Persian cavalry, on their fine horses. Archers from many lands. Bactrian and Scythian horsemen, from Central Asia. Indian soldiers, in cotton clothes, with bows of cane. Ethiopians, in leopard skins and lion skins, with long bows, of palm wood. Arabs, on camels. Phoenician and Egyptian and Greek sailors, in the navy.

[pause]

Herodotus, describing the great army that Xerxes led into Greece, twenty years after our story, lists all these peoples, and their weapons, and their clothes, one after another, like a great procession.

[pause]

{img:persian-cavalry-riders-fine-horses-plain-banners-dust-calm}
But in the year 500 before Christ, the empire is mostly at peace. The soldiers spend their time guarding the palaces, and the cities, and the roads, and the frontiers, and training, and hunting, and waiting.

[long-pause]

At Persepolis, in the barracks, near the palaces, the guards live, and train, and eat together, and play games, and talk. On the great staircases of the Apadana, they are carved, standing in long lines, with their spears, or walking up the stairs, as the guard of honour, for the New Year festival.

[pause]

And every evening, as the sun goes down, behind the mountains, and the long shadows of the columns stretch across the great terrace, the guards change, at the gates of the palace, and the new guards take their places, for the night, with their spears, and their bows, under the stars.

[long-pause]

{img:ionian-greek-city-coast-asia-minor-temple-harbour-500bc}
In the year 500 before Christ, on the western edge of the Persian Empire, there is trouble brewing.

[pause]

Along the coast of what is now Turkey, there are many Greek cities: Miletus, and Ephesus, and Smyrna, and Phocaea, and many others, the cities of the Ionian Greeks, rich, and busy, and clever, with their temples, and their philosophers, and their harbours, and their ships. They have been ruled by the Persians for about forty years, since Cyrus conquered Lydia.

[long-pause]

The Persians let them keep their own customs, and their own gods, and their own temples, but they rule them through local tyrants, Greek rulers, whom the Persians support. And the Greeks, who love their freedom, do not like it.

[pause]

In 499 before Christ, a year after our story, the Ionian cities rebel against Persia, led by the tyrant of Miletus, Aristagoras. They ask the cities of mainland Greece for help. Sparta refuses. But Athens, and Eretria, send ships, and soldiers.

[pause]

{img:ionian-ships-harbour-miletus-rebels-gathering-evening-calm}
The rebellion lasts for six years, and in the end, the Persians win, and take Miletus, in 494. We will not watch it.

[pause]

Herodotus says that when Darius heard that the Athenians had helped the rebels, and burned the city of Sardis, he asked: who are the Athenians? And when he was told, he took his bow, and shot an arrow into the sky, and said: O god, let me punish the Athenians. And he ordered a servant to say to him three times, every day, at dinner: Master, remember the Athenians.

[long-pause]

In 490 before Christ, ten years after our story, Darius sends an army, by sea, across the Aegean, to punish Athens. It lands at Marathon, a bay on the coast, about forty kilometres from Athens. And there, the Athenian army, with the help of the little city of Plataea, defeats the Persians, and drives them back to their ships.

[pause]

The story goes that a runner, Pheidippides, ran from Marathon to Athens, to bring the news of the victory, and shouted: rejoice, we have won! And then fell down, exhausted. The modern Olympic marathon race, of forty-two kilometres, is named after this story.

[pause]

{img:marathon-plain-bay-hills-greek-coast-distant-quiet-dawn}
Darius planned another, much greater invasion of Greece. But he passed away, in 486 before Christ, before he could carry it out. He was about sixty-four. He was buried in his great tomb, in the cliff, at Naqsh-e Rostam.

[long-pause]

His son, Xerxes, will lead the great invasion, in 480: the most famous war of the ancient world, with Thermopylae, and Salamis, and the burning of Athens, and the great Persian army crossing the Hellespont on bridges of boats. And Xerxes will be defeated, and go home.

[pause]

But that is the Greek story, told by the Greeks. For the Persians, it was a war on the far western edge of a vast empire, which went on, peaceful, and rich, and powerful, for another hundred and fifty years.

[long-pause]

{img:ephesus-temple-of-artemis-columns-ionian-city-philosopher-walking}
In the year 500 before Christ, in the Greek cities of Ionia, on the western edge of the Persian Empire, something new is happening: philosophy.

[pause]

A hundred years before, in Miletus, a man called Thales, a merchant, and an engineer, and an astronomer, began to ask a new kind of question: not which god made the world, but what is the world made of? And he answered: water. Everything is made of water, in different forms. It was the wrong answer. But it was a new way of thinking: looking for natural causes, for things, instead of stories about gods.

[long-pause]

Thales, it was said, predicted an eclipse of the sun, in 585 before Christ, which stopped a battle between the Medes and the Lydians, because the soldiers on both sides were so frightened that they made peace. Perhaps he had learned about eclipses from the Babylonians.

[pause]

After Thales, other thinkers in Miletus went on asking questions. Anaximander drew the first map of the world, and said that the earth floats in space, held by nothing. He said that living things first came from the sea, and that human beings came from fish. Anaximenes said that everything is made of air.

[pause]

{img:philosopher-drawing-world-map-sand-students-miletus-harbour}
And in the year 500 before Christ, in Ephesus, the great city of the temple of Artemis, there lives a strange, proud, lonely philosopher, called Heraclitus.

[long-pause]

Heraclitus says that everything is always changing: that you cannot step into the same river twice, because new water is always flowing past. That everything flows. That the world is like an ever-living fire, always burning, always changing, and always the same. That the way up and the way down are one and the same.

[pause]

He writes his thoughts in short, mysterious sayings, which are hard to understand, so that people called him Heraclitus the Obscure. And he gives his book to the temple of Artemis, to keep.

[pause]

{img:river-flowing-over-stones-ephesus-countryside-reflections-calm}
Some people think that Heraclitus was influenced by the ideas of the Persians, about fire, and about the struggle between opposites. We do not know. But in the cities of Ionia, on the edge of the Persian Empire, where Greeks and Persians and Lydians and Babylonians met, and traded, and talked, new ideas were born, which would change the way people thought, for ever.

[long-pause]

{img:xerxes-gate-of-all-nations-completed-persepolis-procession}
After Darius, his son Xerxes became king, in 486 before Christ. He was about thirty-two.

[pause]

Xerxes finished many of his father's buildings at Persepolis: the Gate of All Nations, with its great winged bulls, and the Apadana, and he built his own palace, and began the great Hall of a Hundred Columns, the largest hall at Persepolis, with a hundred columns, in ten rows of ten, where the king received his army officers.

[long-pause]

On the Gate of All Nations, Xerxes carved an inscription, in three languages, which can still be read today: I am Xerxes, the Great King, King of Kings, King of countries containing all kinds of men, King of this great earth far and wide. By the favour of Ahura Mazda, I built this gateway of All Nations. Much other good construction was built in Persepolis, which I built, and which my father built. Whatever good construction is seen, all that, by the favour of Ahura Mazda, we built.

[pause]

After Xerxes, there were other kings: Artaxerxes, and Darius, and Artaxerxes again, and others. For a hundred and fifty years more, the Persian Empire went on, rich, and powerful, and mostly at peace, the greatest power in the world.

[pause]

{img:hall-of-hundred-columns-forest-of-columns-persepolis-afternoon}
The kings went on building at Persepolis, and Susa, and Babylon. The roads went on carrying messengers, and merchants, and travellers. The satraps went on governing the provinces. The farmers went on digging qanats, and planting orchards. The Magi went on tending the sacred fires. And every spring, the peoples of the empire came to celebrate the New Year.

[long-pause]

There were troubles too: rebellions, in Egypt, and elsewhere, and quarrels in the royal family, and plots in the palace. But the empire held together.

[pause]

And many Greeks came to Persia, and worked for the Persian kings, as doctors, and soldiers, and craftsmen, and advisers. Even Themistocles, the Athenian general who had defeated Xerxes at Salamis, later fled from Athens, when the Athenians turned against him, and went to Persia, and the Persian king welcomed him, and gave him three cities to rule, in Asia Minor, where he lived out his life in peace.

[pause]

{img:greek-exile-received-persian-court-king-friendly-gesture}
And Persian ideas, and Persian art, and Persian gardens, and Persian ways of governing, spread across the world, and were copied, by the Greeks, and later by Alexander, and the Romans, and many others.

[long-pause]

{img:persepolis-evening-terrace-lamps-columns-guards-mountain-dusk}
Let us end our visit with an evening at Persepolis, in the spring of the year 500 before Christ, after the New Year festival.

[pause]

The guests have gone home, to their provinces, along the royal roads, with their gifts from the king. The great hall is quiet. The sun is going down behind the plain, and its last light turns the stone columns gold, and then pink, and the shadows of the columns stretch long across the terrace.

[long-pause]

From the workers' village, below the terrace, there is the smell of bread baking, and smoke rising, and the sound of children playing, and voices, in many languages, as the stone cutters, and the carpenters, and the weavers, finish their work for the day, and go home to their families, with their rations of grain, and wine.

[pause]

On the terrace, the guards change, at the Gate of All Nations, and lamps are lit, in the palaces, little clay and bronze lamps, with olive oil, or sesame oil, glowing in the windows.

[pause]

{img:workers-village-below-terrace-evening-smoke-children-families}
In the gardens, below the terrace, the water flows in the stone channels, and the nightingales begin to sing, in the trees, and the scent of the roses, and the orange blossom, drifts up on the evening air.

[long-pause]

In the royal apartments, the king sits with his family, and listens to music, a harp, and a flute, and a singer, singing the old songs of the Persians, about the heroes of long ago.

[pause]

And on the mountain, behind the terrace, the stars come out, one by one, over the Mountain of Mercy, and the great dark plain, and the far mountains, and the whole, vast, sleeping empire, from the river Indus, to the shores of the Aegean Sea.

[long-pause]

{img:alexander-army-approaching-persepolis-plain-330bc-distant}
In 334 before Christ, a young Macedonian king, Alexander, crossed into Asia, with an army, to conquer the Persian Empire.

[pause]

He was twenty-two. In four years, in three great battles, he defeated the Persian king, Darius the Third, and conquered the whole empire: Asia Minor, and Syria, and Egypt, and Babylon, and Susa, and, in the winter of 331 to 330, Persepolis.

[long-pause]

We will not watch the battles.

[pause]

At Persepolis, Alexander took the treasure of the Persian kings: so much gold and silver, the ancient writers say, that it took thousands of mules and camels to carry it away. And then, in the spring of 330, the palaces of Persepolis burned.

[pause]

Nobody knows exactly why. Some ancient writers say that it was revenge, for the burning of Athens by Xerxes, a hundred and fifty years before. Others say that it happened at a feast, when Alexander and his friends had drunk too much, and that he regretted it, afterwards. We will watch it only from very far away, as a faint glow, on the distant plain, under the mountain, on a spring night.

[long-pause]

The great cedar roofs burned, and fell, and the mud-brick walls crumbled, and the palaces were left as ruins: the stone columns, and doorways, and staircases, and carvings, standing among the ashes, under the open sky.

[pause]

And yet, the fire also preserved something. Because when the roofs fell, they buried the clay tablets of the archives, and baked them hard, like pottery, so that they survived, for two thousand three hundred years, until the archaeologists found them.

[pause]

{img:ruins-persepolis-columns-standing-empty-terrace-dawn-quiet}
Alexander respected the Persians, and their ways. He visited the tomb of Cyrus, at Pasargadae, and ordered it to be restored. He married a Persian princess, Roxana, from Bactria, and later two Persian royal princesses. He dressed, sometimes, in Persian clothes, and adopted Persian court customs, and took Persians into his service, and his army, and arranged mass marriages, between his Macedonian officers and Persian noblewomen, at Susa, in the hope of joining the two peoples together.

[long-pause]

He passed away, in Babylon, in 323, at thirty-two, only seven years after the fall of Persepolis. And his empire was divided among his generals.

[pause]

But Persia did not disappear. Five hundred years later, a new Persian dynasty, the Sasanians, rose up, in the same region of Persis, near Persepolis, and built a new Persian empire, which lasted for four hundred years more, until the coming of Islam, in the seventh century after Christ. They carved their own reliefs, of their kings on horseback, on the same cliff, at Naqsh-e Rostam, beneath the tombs of Darius and his family.

[pause]

{img:sasanian-relief-king-on-horseback-cliff-below-achaemenid-tombs}
And the Persian language, and Persian culture, and Persian poetry, and Persian gardens, and the festival of Nowruz, lived on, and still live, today.

[long-pause]

{img:persepolis-ruins-today-columns-terrace-blue-sky-visitors}
Today, the ruins of Persepolis still stand, on their great stone terrace, at the foot of the Mountain of Mercy, on the plain of Marvdasht, in the province of Fars, in southern Iran, about sixty kilometres from the city of Shiraz.

[pause]

It is a World Heritage Site, and one of the most impressive ruins in the world.

[long-pause]

You climb the great double staircase, with its shallow steps, as the ambassadors of the peoples of the empire did, two and a half thousand years ago, and pass through the Gate of All Nations, between the great winged bulls with human heads, which still stand, battered, but still magnificent, looking out over the plain.

[pause]

On the walls of the gate, there are names, scratched by visitors over the centuries: travellers, and explorers, and soldiers, from Europe, and America, from the seventeenth, eighteenth, and nineteenth centuries, including the famous explorer Henry Morton Stanley, who carved his name there in 1870.

[pause]

Of the seventy-two columns of the Apadana, thirteen still stand, tall and slender, against the blue sky. And on the great staircases, the carvings of the processions are still there, wonderfully preserved, because they were buried under earth for many centuries: the Persians, and the Medes, chatting, and holding hands, and the delegations of the twenty-three peoples, with their gifts, walking up the stairs, forever.

[long-pause]

At Naqsh-e Rostam, a few kilometres away, the four great tombs of Darius, and Xerxes, and the kings who came after, are still carved high in the golden cliff, in the shape of a cross, with the carvings of the kings, worshipping before the fire, held up by the peoples of the empire. And below them, the Sasanian reliefs, of kings on horseback.

[pause]

At Pasargadae, the tomb of Cyrus the Great still stands, alone on the wide plain, a simple house of white stone, on its six steps, among the grass, and the wildflowers, in the spring, with the remains of his palaces, and the stone water channels of his garden, nearby.

[pause]

And in the city of Shiraz, nearby, there are the famous Persian gardens, with their cypresses, and their pools, and their roses, and the tombs of the great Persian poets, Hafez, and Saadi, where people come, every evening, to read their poems, and recite them, and put flowers on their tombs.

[long-pause]

And every year, at the spring equinox, on the twenty-first of March, millions of people in Iran celebrate Nowruz, the New Year, as perhaps their ancestors did at Persepolis, two and a half thousand years ago. They clean their houses, and buy new clothes, and set out the table of the seven S's, and visit their families, and eat special foods. And on the thirteenth day of the New Year, everyone goes out into the countryside, to have a picnic, in the open air, in the fresh green spring.

[pause]

Some families even go to Persepolis itself, and have their New Year picnic there, among the ancient columns, under the Mountain of Mercy.

[pause]

{img:families-picnic-spring-grass-near-ancient-columns-nowruz}
So we leave the King of Kings for tonight, on his great stone terrace above the plain, with the last light falling across the columns, and the carved processions climbing the stairs, as they have for two and a half thousand years.

[pause]

The messengers on their fast horses, the gardeners planting cypresses in long rows, the women weavers of Persepolis receiving their rations, and the peoples of the world walking side by side, with their gifts, in the spring.
