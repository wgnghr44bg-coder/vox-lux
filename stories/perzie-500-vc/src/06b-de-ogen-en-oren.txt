How does Darius rule an empire so large that it takes three months to walk from one end to the other?

He divides it into about twenty great provinces, called satrapies. Each one is ruled by a satrap, a governor, usually a Persian nobleman, often a relative of the king, who lives in a palace, in the capital of the province, like a little king, with his own court, and his own guards, and his own paradise garden.

--

The satrap collects the taxes, and the tribute, and sends them to the king. He keeps order, and judges disputes, and looks after the roads, and the royal stations, and the land. He makes sure that the king's orders are carried out.

But the king does not trust anyone completely. So, in each province, there is also a royal secretary, and a commander of the soldiers, who report directly to the king, and keep an eye on the satrap. And there are inspectors, who travel around the empire, on behalf of the king, and visit the provinces, and see what is happening, and report back to the king. The Greeks call them the Eyes of the King, and the Ears of the King.

{img:royal-inspector-arriving-provincial-town-escort-seal-officials}
In this way, the king knows what is happening everywhere in his empire, and no satrap can become too powerful, or rebel, without the king hearing about it.

--

The peoples of the empire keep their own laws, and their own customs, and their own languages, and their own gods, and their own local rulers, as long as they obey the king, and pay their tribute, and send soldiers when they are needed.

Darius also collects the laws of the different peoples, and has them written down. In Egypt, he orders the wise men to write down the old laws of Egypt, so that the Persian governors can judge the Egyptians by their own laws. Later, the Egyptians remember Darius as one of their wise lawgivers.

{img:egyptian-scribes-copying-laws-persian-official-listening-temple}
And the king himself is the highest judge. Anyone in the empire can appeal to the king, if they believe they have been treated unjustly. And the Persian judges, Herodotus says, are chosen for life, unless they are found to be unjust. He tells the story of a judge who took a bribe, and was punished severely, by King Cambyses, who then made the judge's son a judge in his place, and told him to remember his father, every time he sat in judgment.

--

It is a system that works. For two hundred years, the Persian Empire holds together, with its many peoples, and languages, and religions, more peacefully than most empires before it, or after it. Many later empires, the empire of Alexander, and the Romans, and many others, will learn from it, and copy it.
