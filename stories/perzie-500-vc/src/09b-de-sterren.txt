In Babylon, which is part of the Persian Empire, there are the best astronomers in the world.

For hundreds of years, every night, the priests of Babylon have climbed to the top of the great temple tower, the ziggurat, or the roofs of the temples, and watched the sky, and written down what they saw, on clay tablets: the positions of the moon, and the planets, and the stars, and eclipses, and the weather, and the level of the river, and the prices of barley, and dates, and wool, in the market.

--

These records are called the Astronomical Diaries. They were kept, night after night, from about 650 before Christ, until the time of the Romans, for six hundred years: the longest scientific record ever kept, before modern times.

From them, the Babylonian astronomers learned to predict eclipses of the moon, and the movements of the planets, using mathematics, with numbers based on sixty. It is because of the Babylonians that we still divide an hour into sixty minutes, and a minute into sixty seconds, and a circle into three hundred and sixty degrees.

They divided the path of the sun and the planets through the sky into twelve parts, each with its own group of stars, its constellation: the bull, the twins, the lion, the scales, the scorpion, and so on. The signs of the zodiac, which are still known all over the world today.

--

Under the Persian kings, the astronomers of Babylon went on with their work, undisturbed. And their knowledge spread, through the Persian Empire, to the Greeks, who learned from them, and later to India, and the Arabs, and Europe.

The Magi of Persia, too, watched the stars, and studied them, and interpreted them, as signs of the will of the gods. The word magic comes from their name.

{img:persian-plain-night-sky-milky-way-shepherd-fire-mountains}
And on clear nights, all over the empire, ordinary people too, the shepherds on the mountains, and the farmers on their roofs, and the travellers in the caravanserais, and the soldiers on the walls, looked up at the same stars, and knew their names, and told stories about them, and found their way by them, in the great, dark, silent night of the ancient world.
