Today, the ruins of Persepolis still stand, on their great stone terrace, at the foot of the Mountain of Mercy, on the plain of Marvdasht, in the province of Fars, in southern Iran, about sixty kilometres from the city of Shiraz.

It is a World Heritage Site, and one of the most impressive ruins in the world.

--

You climb the great double staircase, with its shallow steps, as the ambassadors of the peoples of the empire did, two and a half thousand years ago, and pass through the Gate of All Nations, between the great winged bulls with human heads, which still stand, battered, but still magnificent, looking out over the plain.

On the walls of the gate, there are names, scratched by visitors over the centuries: travellers, and explorers, and soldiers, from Europe, and America, from the seventeenth, eighteenth, and nineteenth centuries, including the famous explorer Henry Morton Stanley, who carved his name there in 1870.

Of the seventy-two columns of the Apadana, thirteen still stand, tall and slender, against the blue sky. And on the great staircases, the carvings of the processions are still there, wonderfully preserved, because they were buried under earth for many centuries: the Persians, and the Medes, chatting, and holding hands, and the delegations of the twenty-three peoples, with their gifts, walking up the stairs, forever.

--

At Naqsh-e Rostam, a few kilometres away, the four great tombs of Darius, and Xerxes, and the kings who came after, are still carved high in the golden cliff, in the shape of a cross, with the carvings of the kings, worshipping before the fire, held up by the peoples of the empire. And below them, the Sasanian reliefs, of kings on horseback.

At Pasargadae, the tomb of Cyrus the Great still stands, alone on the wide plain, a simple house of white stone, on its six steps, among the grass, and the wildflowers, in the spring, with the remains of his palaces, and the stone water channels of his garden, nearby.

And in the city of Shiraz, nearby, there are the famous Persian gardens, with their cypresses, and their pools, and their roses, and the tombs of the great Persian poets, Hafez, and Saadi, where people come, every evening, to read their poems, and recite them, and put flowers on their tombs.

--

And every year, at the spring equinox, on the twenty-first of March, millions of people in Iran celebrate Nowruz, the New Year, as perhaps their ancestors did at Persepolis, two and a half thousand years ago. They clean their houses, and buy new clothes, and set out the table of the seven S's, and visit their families, and eat special foods. And on the thirteenth day of the New Year, everyone goes out into the countryside, to have a picnic, in the open air, in the fresh green spring.

Some families even go to Persepolis itself, and have their New Year picnic there, among the ancient columns, under the Mountain of Mercy.

{img:families-picnic-spring-grass-near-ancient-columns-nowruz}
So we leave the King of Kings for tonight, on his great stone terrace above the plain, with the last light falling across the columns, and the carved processions climbing the stairs, as they have for two and a half thousand years.

The messengers on their fast horses, the gardeners planting cypresses in long rows, the women weavers of Persepolis receiving their rations, and the peoples of the world walking side by side, with their gifts, in the spring.
