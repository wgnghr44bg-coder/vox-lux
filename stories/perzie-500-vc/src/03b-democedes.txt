There is a story, told by Herodotus, about a Greek doctor at the court of Darius.

One day, when Darius was out hunting, he jumped down from his horse, and twisted his foot, badly, and dislocated his ankle. The Egyptian doctors of the court, who were thought to be the best in the world, tried to put it right, but they twisted it so roughly that they made it worse. And for seven days and seven nights, the king could not sleep, because of the pain.

--

Then someone remembered that, among the prisoners, and slaves, captured at Samos, there was a Greek doctor, called Democedes, from the city of Croton, in the south of Italy. They found him, and brought him to the king, still in his chains, and in rags.

At first, Democedes said that he was not a doctor, because he was afraid that if he cured the king, he would never be allowed to go home. But Darius threatened him, and he admitted it. And he treated the king's ankle, gently, in the Greek way, with soft treatments, and rest, and soothing bandages, and the king slept, and in a few days, he was well again.

{img:king-giving-golden-chains-to-doctor-women-of-palace-gifts}
Darius was so grateful that he gave Democedes two pairs of golden chains, as a joke, because he had come to him in chains. And Democedes asked if the king meant to double his misery. Darius laughed, and sent him to visit the queens, in the women's palace. And each of the queens took a golden bowl, and filled it with gold coins, and gave it to him. So many coins, Herodotus says, that the servant who followed him, picking up the coins that fell, became rich.

--

Democedes became very rich, and lived in a great house, in Susa, and ate at the king's table, and was a favourite of the king. Later, he also cured Queen Atossa of an illness.

But he still wanted to go home, to Croton. And so, Herodotus says, he persuaded Atossa to ask Darius to send an expedition to Greece, to spy out the land, with Democedes as a guide. And when the ships reached Italy, Democedes escaped, and went home, to Croton, and married the daughter of a famous wrestler, and never came back.

{img:ship-sailing-west-italian-coast-greek-doctor-looking-shore}
It is a good story, and perhaps partly true. It shows that the Persian court welcomed clever people, from everywhere, and rewarded them richly. And that the Greeks, even when they were treated very well, longed for their own homes, and their own freedom.
