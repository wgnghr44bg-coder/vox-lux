The Persians love gardens more than anything.

All over the empire, the king, and the satraps, and the nobles, make great gardens: walled parks, with trees, and flowers, and streams, and fountains, and pavilions, and wild animals, for hunting. The Persians call them pairidaeza, which means a walled enclosure. The Greeks heard the word, and made it paradeisos. And from it comes our word paradise.

--

The most famous kind of Persian garden is the chahar bagh, the fourfold garden: a square garden, divided into four parts, by two water channels, crossing each other in the middle, where there is a pool, or a fountain, or a pavilion. It is a picture of the world, with its four rivers, and its four quarters. And of paradise.

At Pasargadae, the old capital of Cyrus, archaeologists have found the remains of such a garden: the stone water channels, with little basins, at regular intervals, where the water flowed, cool and clear, among the trees and flowers. It is the oldest garden of this kind that we know of.

In the gardens, there are trees of every kind: cypresses, tall and dark, and plane trees, broad and shady, and pomegranates, and apricots, and peaches, and pears, and figs, and almonds, and pistachios, and walnuts, and vines. And flowers: roses, above all, which the Persians love, and lilies, and tulips, and irises, and jasmine, and narcissus.

--

The Greek writer Xenophon tells a story about a Spartan general, Lysander, who visited the young Persian prince, Cyrus the Younger, a hundred years after our story, in his garden, at Sardis. Lysander admired the trees, planted in perfect rows, and the beauty, and the scent, of the garden, and asked who had planned it. And Cyrus said: I planned it all myself, and planted some of the trees with my own hands. Lysander was astonished, looking at the prince's fine clothes, and jewellery. And Cyrus swore that he never sat down to dinner, if he was well, without first working in his garden, or exercising, until he sweated.

{img:prince-planting-tree-garden-rows-cypress-visitor-admiring}
The Persian kings plant trees, as a duty, and a pleasure. And Darius writes to one of his satraps, in Asia Minor, praising him for planting fruit trees, from beyond the Euphrates, in his province.

--

The great parks, called paradises, are also hunting parks, full of deer, and wild boar, and lions, where the king and his nobles hunt on horseback, with bows, and spears.

And in the heat of the summer, the king and his family, and his court, sit in the shade of the pavilions, in the gardens, by the cool water, and eat fruit, and drink wine, and listen to music, and poetry, and the singing of the birds, and the splashing of the fountains, and the wind in the leaves of the poplars.

{img:shaded-pavilion-garden-fountain-courtiers-fruit-afternoon-calm}
For the Persians, a garden is the most beautiful thing in the world: a little piece of paradise on earth, made by human hands, in the middle of a dry and dusty land.
