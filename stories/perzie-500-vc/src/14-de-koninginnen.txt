The women of the Persian royal family are powerful and important, much more than the Greeks expected.

Darius has several wives. The most important is Atossa, the daughter of Cyrus the Great. She was married before to her brother, Cambyses, and then to the usurper who took the throne, and then to Darius, who married her, to show that he belonged to the family of Cyrus. She is clever, and proud, and very influential. Herodotus says that she had great power in the palace, and that it was through her influence that her son, Xerxes, became the heir, rather than Darius's older sons by another wife.

--

His favourite wife, Herodotus says, is Artystone, another daughter of Cyrus. Darius loved her so much that he had a golden statue made of her. The Persepolis tablets record her as Irtashduna, and show that she owned her own estates, and her own workers, and her own steward, and gave her own orders, and travelled with her own household, and received huge amounts of wine, and grain, and sheep, for her household and her feasts.

The royal women of Persia are rich. They own land, and villages, and workshops, and manage them through their own officials. They travel. They give banquets. They have their own seals, to sign their orders. They are not shut away, as Greek women often are.

{img:royal-woman-seal-stamping-order-clay-tablet-steward-waiting}
The tablets mention many other women too: princesses, and noblewomen, and ordinary working women, with their own names, and their own rations, and their own jobs: weavers, and wine makers, and supervisors of work groups.

--

The Persian queens and princesses wear beautiful clothes: long robes, of fine wool, or linen, or silk, dyed in rich colours, purple, and blue, and red, and embroidered, with veils, and crowns, and golden jewellery.

They ride horses, and travel in covered carriages. They keep their own court, with their own servants, and their own guards. And their children are brought up in their households, until the age of five, as Herodotus says.

{img:noblewomen-riding-horses-garden-paradise-attendants-spring}
In later centuries, the Greek writers will tell many dramatic stories about the Persian queens, about their power, and their jealousy, and their schemes, in the palace. Some of these stories are probably exaggerated. But they show how important the royal women of Persia were.

--

And in Persian art, and later Persian poetry, women are honoured, and loved, and celebrated: the mother, the wife, the beloved, in the garden, among the roses, and the nightingales, which, two thousand years later, will become the great themes of the poetry of Persia, of Hafez, and Saadi, and Rumi.
