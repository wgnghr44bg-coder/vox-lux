Who built Persepolis? And how did they live?

In the 1930s, archaeologists digging at Persepolis found, in the fortification wall, thousands of little clay tablets, written in cuneiform, mostly in the Elamite language. They are the records of the royal administration, from the years 509 to 493 before Christ: exactly the time of our story.

--

They are not great inscriptions about kings and battles. They are ordinary records: receipts, and lists, and accounts, of the food given out to the workers, and officials, and travellers, and animals, from the royal storehouses, in the region around Persepolis.

And they tell us, in wonderful detail, about the lives of the ordinary people who built the palaces.

The workers come from all over the empire: Persians, and Elamites, and Babylonians, and Egyptians, and Lydians, and Ionian Greeks, and Bactrians, and Indians, and many others. They are called kurtash. Some are skilled craftsmen: stone cutters, and sculptors, and carpenters, and goldsmiths, and weavers. Others are labourers. They work in groups, sometimes hundreds strong, under a foreman.

--

They are paid, not with money, but with food: rations of grain, barley, or wheat, every month, and wine, or beer, and sometimes sheep, and fruit, according to their work, and their skill.

And here is something remarkable. Women work too, as craftsmen, and are paid. And in some jobs, skilled women are paid the same as men, or even more. There are women who are the leaders of work groups, and are paid more than the men in their groups.

And when a woman worker gives birth to a baby, she receives an extra ration, of grain, and wine, and meat, to help her: more for a boy, and a little less for a girl. And she receives a reduced ration, for some months, while she is not working, a kind of maternity pay, two and a half thousand years ago. The children of the workers receive small rations too.

--

The tablets also record the travels of people across the empire: a group of workers, travelling from Egypt to Persepolis. An official, going to India, with his servants, and a sealed travel document. A woman, Irtashduna, a wife of King Darius, with her own estates, and her own workers, and her own steward, sending orders. A group of Lydian stone cutters. A man, taking horses to the king.

They record the feeding of the king's horses, and camels, and cattle, and the grain given to the priests, for the sacrifices to the gods.

{img:stone-cutters-workshop-carving-column-capital-persepolis-dust}
So, thanks to these little tablets, we know the names of thousands of ordinary people who lived two and a half thousand years ago, and how much bread they ate, and how much wine they drank, and where they came from, and where they were going.

--

And in the great inscription that Darius carved at Susa, about the building of his palace there, he tells us where the materials came from, and who worked on them.

The cedar wood came from Lebanon. The gold from Sardis and Bactria. The lapis lazuli and carnelian from Sogdiana. The turquoise from Chorasmia. The silver and ebony from Egypt. The ivory from Ethiopia, and India, and Arachosia. The stone columns from a village in Elam.

{img:craftsmen-many-nations-working-palace-susa-gold-ivory-cedar}
The stone cutters were Ionians and Sardians. The goldsmiths were Medes and Egyptians. The men who worked the wood were Sardians and Egyptians. The men who made the baked bricks were Babylonians. The men who decorated the walls were Medes and Egyptians.

The whole world, it seems, came together, to build the palaces of the King of Kings.
