How do the Persians write?

The Persian Empire is full of languages, and scripts. And Darius, in his inscriptions, uses three of them, side by side: Old Persian, the language of the Persians themselves. Elamite, the language of the old kingdom of Elam, around Susa. And Babylonian, the ancient language of Mesopotamia. All three are written in cuneiform: wedge-shaped signs, pressed into clay with a reed, or carved into stone.

--

Old Persian cuneiform is special. It is a new script, which may have been invented in the time of Darius himself, for his royal inscriptions. It is simple, with only about thirty-six signs for sounds, and a few signs for whole words, like king, and country, and Ahura Mazda. It is used almost only for royal inscriptions, carved on stone, and on gold and silver plates.

But for everyday business, letters, and accounts, and orders, the empire uses another language, and another script: Aramaic, the language of Syria, written with an alphabet, in ink, on parchment, or papyrus, which is quick, and easy to write.

{img:scribes-writing-aramaic-ink-parchment-and-clay-tablets-office}
Aramaic becomes the common language of the empire, used from Egypt to India, by officials, and merchants, and travellers, everywhere. Five hundred years later, in the time of Jesus, it will still be the everyday language of Palestine, and Jesus himself will speak it.

--

In the government offices, there are scribes, many of them, who write the king's orders, and the letters, and the accounts, in all these languages, and translate between them. Every order of the king is written down, and copied, and sent out, by the royal messengers, to every province, in every language.

In the book of Esther, in the Bible, it says that the king's letters were sent to every province, in its own script, and to every people, in its own language.

{img:clay-tablet-cuneiform-wedges-reed-stylus-close-lamplight}
For two thousand years after the end of the Persian Empire, nobody could read cuneiform. The knowledge was lost. Then, in the nineteenth century, scholars began to decipher it, starting with Old Persian, the simplest, using the royal inscriptions of Persepolis, and the great inscription of Behistun, with its three languages, side by side, like the Rosetta Stone of Egypt.

--

A young German schoolteacher, Georg Friedrich Grotefend, made the first breakthrough, in 1802, as a bet with a friend. He guessed that the inscriptions on the palaces of Persepolis would begin with the names and titles of kings, like the later Persian inscriptions: X, great king, king of kings, son of Y, great king. And he guessed which kings they might be, Darius, and Xerxes. And he was right.

And so, after two thousand years, the voices of the Persian kings could be heard again.
