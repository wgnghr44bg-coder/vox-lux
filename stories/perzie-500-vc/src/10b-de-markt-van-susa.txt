Let us visit the market of a great Persian city in the year 500 before Christ.

It is crowded, noisy and colourful. Merchants from every part of the empire are here. Babylonians with long hair and long robes sell cloth, dates and grain. Phoenicians from the coast sell purple cloth and glass. Egyptians sell linen and papyrus. Lydians sell gold and perfume. Arabs bring sweet-smelling incense from the far south. Indians sell cotton, spices and ivory.

--

They pay with silver, weighed on scales, and with the new coins of Darius. One golden coin is worth about a month's pay for a soldier.

There are bankers too. In Babylon there is a great family of bankers and businessmen whose clay records survive for five generations. They list loans and rents, the buying and selling of houses and land, marriage contracts and wills.

{img:babylonian-banker-clay-tablets-scales-silver-client-office}
In the market, craftsmen are at work. Potters and weavers, leather workers and jewellers, bakers and butchers and wine sellers.

--

There are food stalls selling flat bread baked on hot stones, dates, figs, nuts, cheese, yoghurt, roast meat, beer and wine.

There are storytellers and musicians, and fortune tellers who read the future in the stars or the flight of birds. There are beggars and children, dogs, donkeys and camels.

{img:storyteller-marketplace-crowd-listening-shade-awning-afternoon}
And everywhere people are talking in Persian, Aramaic, Babylonian, Greek, Egyptian and many other languages. They bargain, gossip and greet each other. The Persians greet each other in different ways, a Greek writer says, depending on rank. Equals kiss each other on the lips. Someone a little lower kisses the other on the cheek. And someone much lower bows down to the ground.

Lately there is new gossip in the market too. Traders from the west speak of unrest among the Greek cities on the coast. Some say there may be trouble. Others shrug and say the Great King will deal with it.

--

As the sun goes down, the market closes. The merchants pack up their goods and go home, or to the inn. The donkeys are led away. Lamps are lit in the houses. And the city settles down for the night under the stars, which the star-watchers of Babylon have watched for hundreds of years.
