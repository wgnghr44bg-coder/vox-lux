Let us visit the market of a great Persian city, in the year 500 before Christ.

It is crowded, and noisy, and colourful. Merchants from every part of the empire are there: Babylonians, with their long hair, and their long robes, and their seals, selling cloth, and dates, and grain. Phoenicians, from the coast of Lebanon, selling purple cloth, and glass, and cedar wood. Egyptians, selling linen, and papyrus. Lydians, selling gold, and perfumes. Arabs, with frankincense, and myrrh, from the far south of Arabia. Indians, selling cotton, and spices, and ivory. Bactrians, with lapis lazuli, and horses.

--

They buy and sell with silver, weighed out on scales, in pieces, and with the new coins of Darius: the golden daric, and the silver siglos. A golden daric is worth about a month's wages for a soldier.

And there are bankers, too. In Babylon, there is a great family of bankers and businessmen, called the Egibi family, whose records, on clay tablets, survive, for five generations: loans, and rents, and the buying and selling of houses, and land, and slaves, and dates, and barley, and the marriage contracts, and the wills, of the family members.

{img:babylonian-banker-clay-tablets-scales-silver-client-office}
In the market, there are craftsmen at work: potters, and weavers, and dyers, and leather workers, and metalworkers, and jewellers, and bakers, and butchers, and wine sellers.

--

There are food stalls, selling bread, flat and round, baked on hot stones, and dates, and figs, and raisins, and nuts, and cheese, and yoghurt, and fruit, and roasted meat, and beer, and wine.

There are storytellers, and musicians, and fortune tellers, who read the future in the stars, or in the flight of birds, or in oil poured on water. There are beggars, and children, and dogs, and donkeys, and camels.

{img:storyteller-marketplace-crowd-listening-shade-awning-afternoon}
And everywhere, there are people talking: in Persian, and Aramaic, and Elamite, and Babylonian, and Greek, and Egyptian, and many other languages. Bargaining, and gossiping, and greeting each other. The Persians greet each other, Herodotus says, in different ways, according to their rank: equals kiss each other on the lips. A man of slightly lower rank kisses the other on the cheek. And a man of much lower rank bows down to the ground.

--

As the sun goes down, the market closes. The merchants pack up their goods, and go home, or to the caravanserai. The donkeys are led away, with their loads. The lamps are lit in the houses. And the city settles down for the night, under the stars, which the Babylonian astronomers have watched, and recorded, every night, for hundreds of years.
