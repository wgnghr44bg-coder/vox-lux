One of the peoples of the Persian Empire, who remembered the Persian kings with gratitude for thousands of years, are the Jews.

When Cyrus conquered Babylon, in 539 before Christ, there were many Jews living there, the descendants of the people of Jerusalem, who had been carried away to Babylon by King Nebuchadnezzar, fifty years before, when he destroyed Jerusalem, and its temple.

--

Cyrus allowed them to go home. And many of them did, in groups, over the following years: a long journey, on foot, and with donkeys, and camels, across the desert, from Babylon, to Jerusalem, which was in ruins.

In the time of Darius, around 520 to 515 before Christ, just a few years before our story, they rebuilt the temple of Jerusalem, with the permission, and the help, of the Persian king, who ordered his officials in the province to give them money, and timber, and supplies, for the building. The book of Ezra, in the Bible, preserves the letter of Darius, ordering this.

{img:second-temple-jerusalem-building-workers-stones-hills-morning}
It is called the Second Temple. And it stood for nearly six hundred years.

--

But many Jews stayed in Babylon, where they had houses, and families, and businesses, and lived there, as a great community, for more than two thousand years, until modern times.

And there were Jews in other parts of the empire too. On the island of Elephantine, in the Nile, in the far south of Egypt, at the border with Nubia, there was a garrison of Jewish soldiers, serving the Persian king, with their families, and their own temple. Their letters, and contracts, and accounts, written in Aramaic, on papyrus, have survived: marriage contracts, and loans, and the sale of houses, and letters about the festival of Passover, and a letter asking the Persian governor for permission to rebuild their temple. Through them, we can see the everyday life of a Jewish family, in Egypt, under the Persian kings, two and a half thousand years ago.

{img:elephantine-island-nile-houses-palms-boats-calm-afternoon}
In the Bible, the Persian kings are remembered kindly: Cyrus, and Darius, and Artaxerxes, who sent the scholar Ezra, and the governor Nehemiah, to Jerusalem, to rebuild its walls, and restore its laws.

And the story of Esther, the Jewish queen of Persia, at Susa, is still told every year, at Purim. And in the Iranian city of Hamadan, which was once Ecbatana, there is a tomb, which is said to be the tomb of Esther and her cousin Mordecai, and Jewish pilgrims have visited it for centuries.
