Every spring, at Persepolis, there was a great festival, many historians believe: the festival of the New Year, at the spring equinox, on the twenty-first of March, when day and night are equal, and the sun begins to win over the darkness. The Persians call it Nowruz, new day.

--

On the great staircases of the Apadana, there are carvings, which show what may be this festival: endless processions of people, carved in stone, walking up the stairs, toward the king.

On one side, there are the Persians and the Medes: the nobles, and the guards, in their long, flowing robes, or their riding clothes, with their bows, and their spears, and their tall hats, chatting with each other, turning around to talk to the man behind, holding flowers, some of them holding hands, or putting a hand on a friend's shoulder. They look relaxed, and friendly, and happy.

On the other side, there are the delegations of the peoples of the empire: twenty-three of them, each led by a Persian or Median usher, who holds the hand of the leader of the delegation. Each delegation wears its own costume, and brings its own gifts, from its own land.

--

The Babylonians bring a humped bull, and cloth. The Lydians, from Turkey, bring golden bowls, and bracelets, and a chariot, with little horses. The Arabs bring a camel. The Indians bring baskets of gold dust, and a donkey. The Ethiopians bring an elephant's tusk, and an okapi, or a giraffe. The Bactrians, from Afghanistan, bring a two-humped camel. The Scythians bring horses, and bracelets. The Egyptians bring a bull. The Ionian Greeks bring cloth, and bowls. The Armenians bring a horse, and a wine jar.

Each person in the procession is carved in fine detail: their faces, their beards, their hair, their clothes, their shoes, their gifts, the folds of their garments, the muscles of the animals. It is one of the greatest works of art of the ancient world.

{img:carved-ethiopian-delegation-tusk-okapi-relief-stone-afternoon}
And what is striking is the peace in it. Nobody is in chains. Nobody is being beaten. The people are not shown as conquered prisoners, as they would be on the monuments of Egypt, or Assyria. They walk calmly, with dignity, with their gifts, side by side, all the peoples of the world, coming together, to honour the king, at the New Year.

--

At the top of the stairs, in the great hall, the king sits on his throne, with his crown, and his sceptre, and a lotus flower in his hand, with the crown prince standing behind him, and his officials, and guards, and incense burners, receiving the gifts, and the greetings, of his peoples.

And then, perhaps, there is a great feast, with thousands of guests, and music, and wine, and celebration, in the spring sunshine.

{img:nowruz-table-spring-sprouts-apples-garlic-coins-goldfish-candles}
The festival of Nowruz is still celebrated today, two and a half thousand years later, by hundreds of millions of people, in Iran, and Afghanistan, and Central Asia, and the Caucasus, and among Kurds, and Persians all over the world: with spring cleaning, and new clothes, and visits to family, and a special table, with seven things whose names begin with the letter S, in Persian, symbols of the spring, and new life, and a bowl of goldfish, and a mirror, and candles, and painted eggs, and green sprouts of wheat.
