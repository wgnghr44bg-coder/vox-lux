In 334 before Christ, a young Macedonian king, Alexander, crossed into Asia, with an army, to conquer the Persian Empire.

He was twenty-two. In four years, in three great battles, he defeated the Persian king, Darius the Third, and conquered the whole empire: Asia Minor, and Syria, and Egypt, and Babylon, and Susa, and, in the winter of 331 to 330, Persepolis.

--

We will not watch the battles.

At Persepolis, Alexander took the treasure of the Persian kings: so much gold and silver, the ancient writers say, that it took thousands of mules and camels to carry it away. And then, in the spring of 330, the palaces of Persepolis burned.

Nobody knows exactly why. Some ancient writers say that it was revenge, for the burning of Athens by Xerxes, a hundred and fifty years before. Others say that it happened at a feast, when Alexander and his friends had drunk too much, and that he regretted it, afterwards. We will watch it only from very far away, as a faint glow, on the distant plain, under the mountain, on a spring night.

--

The great cedar roofs burned, and fell, and the mud-brick walls crumbled, and the palaces were left as ruins: the stone columns, and doorways, and staircases, and carvings, standing among the ashes, under the open sky.

And yet, the fire also preserved something. Because when the roofs fell, they buried the clay tablets of the archives, and baked them hard, like pottery, so that they survived, for two thousand three hundred years, until the archaeologists found them.

{img:ruins-persepolis-columns-standing-empty-terrace-dawn-quiet}
Alexander respected the Persians, and their ways. He visited the tomb of Cyrus, at Pasargadae, and ordered it to be restored. He married a Persian princess, Roxana, from Bactria, and later two Persian royal princesses. He dressed, sometimes, in Persian clothes, and adopted Persian court customs, and took Persians into his service, and his army, and arranged mass marriages, between his Macedonian officers and Persian noblewomen, at Susa, in the hope of joining the two peoples together.

--

He passed away, in Babylon, in 323, at thirty-two, only seven years after the fall of Persepolis. And his empire was divided among his generals.

But Persia did not disappear. Five hundred years later, a new Persian dynasty, the Sasanians, rose up, in the same region of Persis, near Persepolis, and built a new Persian empire, which lasted for four hundred years more, until the coming of Islam, in the seventh century after Christ. They carved their own reliefs, of their kings on horseback, on the same cliff, at Naqsh-e Rostam, beneath the tombs of Darius and his family.

{img:sasanian-relief-king-on-horseback-cliff-below-achaemenid-tombs}
And the Persian language, and Persian culture, and Persian poetry, and Persian gardens, and the festival of Nowruz, lived on, and still live, today.
