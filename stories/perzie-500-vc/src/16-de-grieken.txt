In the year 500 before Christ, on the western edge of the Persian Empire, there is trouble brewing.

Along the coast of what is now Turkey, there are many Greek cities: Miletus, and Ephesus, and Smyrna, and Phocaea, and many others, the cities of the Ionian Greeks, rich, and busy, and clever, with their temples, and their philosophers, and their harbours, and their ships. They have been ruled by the Persians for about forty years, since Cyrus conquered Lydia.

--

The Persians let them keep their own customs, and their own gods, and their own temples, but they rule them through local tyrants, Greek rulers, whom the Persians support. And the Greeks, who love their freedom, do not like it.

In 499 before Christ, a year after our story, the Ionian cities rebel against Persia, led by the tyrant of Miletus, Aristagoras. They ask the cities of mainland Greece for help. Sparta refuses. But Athens, and Eretria, send ships, and soldiers.

{img:ionian-ships-harbour-miletus-rebels-gathering-evening-calm}
The rebellion lasts for six years, and in the end, the Persians win, and take Miletus, in 494. We will not watch it.

Herodotus says that when Darius heard that the Athenians had helped the rebels, and burned the city of Sardis, he asked: who are the Athenians? And when he was told, he took his bow, and shot an arrow into the sky, and said: O god, let me punish the Athenians. And he ordered a servant to say to him three times, every day, at dinner: Master, remember the Athenians.

--

In 490 before Christ, ten years after our story, Darius sends an army, by sea, across the Aegean, to punish Athens. It lands at Marathon, a bay on the coast, about forty kilometres from Athens. And there, the Athenian army, with the help of the little city of Plataea, defeats the Persians, and drives them back to their ships.

The story goes that a runner, Pheidippides, ran from Marathon to Athens, to bring the news of the victory, and shouted: rejoice, we have won! And then fell down, exhausted. The modern Olympic marathon race, of forty-two kilometres, is named after this story.

{img:marathon-plain-bay-hills-greek-coast-distant-quiet-dawn}
Darius planned another, much greater invasion of Greece. But he passed away, in 486 before Christ, before he could carry it out. He was about sixty-four. He was buried in his great tomb, in the cliff, at Naqsh-e Rostam.

--

His son, Xerxes, will lead the great invasion, in 480: the most famous war of the ancient world, with Thermopylae, and Salamis, and the burning of Athens, and the great Persian army crossing the Hellespont on bridges of boats. And Xerxes will be defeated, and go home.

But that is the Greek story, told by the Greeks. For the Persians, it was a war on the far western edge of a vast empire, which went on, peaceful, and rich, and powerful, for another hundred and fifty years.
