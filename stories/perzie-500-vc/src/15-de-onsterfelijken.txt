The king of Persia is protected by his guard: ten thousand chosen soldiers, the best of the Persian army, called by the Greeks the Immortals.

They were called that, Herodotus says, because their number was always kept at exactly ten thousand. If one of them fell ill, or passed away, another man was chosen, at once, to take his place, so that there were never more, and never fewer, than ten thousand.

--

A thousand of them, the most noble, carry spears with golden pomegranates on the end, instead of the usual metal butt, and are the king's personal bodyguard, always close to him. The others carry spears with silver pomegranates.

On the walls of the palace at Susa, they are shown in glazed bricks, in bright colours: tall, bearded men, in long, flowing robes, richly patterned, yellow and white and brown, with their spears held upright, and their bows, and quivers, on their shoulders, walking in a long, calm, dignified line, one behind the other, on a background of blue. They are among the most beautiful works of ancient art, and can be seen today, in the Louvre, in Paris.

{img:glazed-brick-archers-frieze-susa-blue-background-museum-light}
The Immortals are the elite of the army. They are given the best food, and the best equipment, and travel with their families, and their servants, in covered carriages, and camels carry their food.

--

The Persian army itself is enormous, and made of soldiers from every part of the empire: Persian and Median infantry, with their wicker shields, and their spears, and their bows. Persian cavalry, on their fine horses. Archers from many lands. Bactrian and Scythian horsemen, from Central Asia. Indian soldiers, in cotton clothes, with bows of cane. Ethiopians, in leopard skins and lion skins, with long bows, of palm wood. Arabs, on camels. Phoenician and Egyptian and Greek sailors, in the navy.

Herodotus, describing the great army that Xerxes led into Greece, twenty years after our story, lists all these peoples, and their weapons, and their clothes, one after another, like a great procession.

{img:persian-cavalry-riders-fine-horses-plain-banners-dust-calm}
But in the year 500 before Christ, the empire is mostly at peace. The soldiers spend their time guarding the palaces, and the cities, and the roads, and the frontiers, and training, and hunting, and waiting.

--

At Persepolis, in the barracks, near the palaces, the guards live, and train, and eat together, and play games, and talk. On the great staircases of the Apadana, they are carved, standing in long lines, with their spears, or walking up the stairs, as the guard of honour, for the New Year festival.

And every evening, as the sun goes down, behind the mountains, and the long shadows of the columns stretch across the great terrace, the guards change, at the gates of the palace, and the new guards take their places, for the night, with their spears, and their bows, under the stars.
