Most of the people of the Persian Empire are farmers, as in all ancient countries. And in Iran, the greatest problem for farmers is water.

Much of Iran is dry: high plateaus, and deserts, with little rain, especially in summer. The rivers are few. But in the mountains, there is snow, and rain, and the water sinks into the ground, and flows, deep underground, toward the plains.

--

So the Persians invented a wonderful way of bringing this water to their fields: the qanat.

A qanat is an underground canal, a tunnel, dug from the foot of the mountains, where there is water deep under the ground, sloping very gently downward, for many kilometres, sometimes tens of kilometres, until it comes out at the surface, in the plain, where the water flows out, into a channel, and into the fields, and the gardens, and the village.

To dig a qanat, the diggers first dig a deep well, a mother well, at the foot of the mountains, down to the water. Then they dig a line of other wells, or shafts, every few dozen metres, across the plain, and join them, underground, by a tunnel, just big enough for a man to crawl through, with exactly the right slope, so that the water flows gently, but not too fast. It is very difficult, and skilled, and dangerous work. The men who do it, the muqannis, are greatly respected.

--

From above, a qanat looks like a long line of little round mounds of earth, like giant molehills, stretching across the desert, each one with a hole in the middle: the shafts, with the earth that was dug out piled around them.

The water in the qanat is cool, and clean, and flows all year round, even in the hottest summer, because it comes from deep underground, and does not evaporate in the sun.

{img:village-garden-qanat-water-emerging-channel-pomegranate-trees}
The Persian kings encourage the building of qanats. The Greek historian Polybius says that if a man brought water to land that had never been watered before, the king would let him, and his family, farm it, without paying tax, for five generations.

There are still tens of thousands of qanats in Iran today, and some of them are thousands of years old, and still flowing. They are a World Heritage Site.

--

With the water of the qanats, and the rivers, the farmers grow wheat, and barley, and rice, in some places, and sesame, and cotton, and vegetables, and alfalfa, for the horses. They grow orchards of fruit: apples, and pears, and pomegranates, and apricots, and quinces, and grapes, and figs, and dates, in the hot south. And nuts: almonds, and walnuts, and pistachios.

They keep sheep, and goats, and cattle, and donkeys, and camels, and the nomads move their flocks, with the seasons, from the warm plains in winter, to the cool mountain pastures in summer, as they have done for thousands of years, and still do today.

{img:nomads-moving-flocks-mountain-pasture-black-tents-summer}
The villages are built of mud brick, with flat roofs, or domes, and courtyards, with a pool, and a fruit tree, and a little garden. And in the hottest places, the houses have wind catchers: tall towers on the roof, open to the wind, which catch the breeze, and send it down into the house, over a pool of water, to cool it. The first air conditioning.
