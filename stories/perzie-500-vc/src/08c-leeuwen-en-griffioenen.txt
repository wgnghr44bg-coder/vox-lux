On the walls and staircases of Persepolis, there is one carving that appears again and again: a lion, leaping onto the back of a bull, and biting it.

Nobody knows exactly what it means. Some scholars think it is a symbol of the New Year: the lion, the sign of the sun, and summer, overcoming the bull, the sign of winter, at the spring equinox, when the constellation of the lion is high in the sky, and the bull disappears. Others think it is a symbol of the power of the king. It is carved so beautifully, with such life, that it is one of the most famous images of ancient Persia.

--

The Persians love animals, real and imaginary, in their art.

There are lions, everywhere: on the walls, and on the jewellery, and the seals, and the cups. There were still real lions in Persia, and in Mesopotamia, in those days, and the kings hunted them. There are bulls, and horses, and ibexes, with great curling horns, and eagles, and rams.

{img:griffin-capital-persepolis-column-eagle-head-lion-body-carved}
And there are imaginary animals: griffins, with the body of a lion, and the head and wings of an eagle. Winged bulls, with the heads of men, which guard the gates. And the homa, a great mythical bird, which brings good luck to anyone it flies over. On the tops of some columns at Persepolis, there are great double capitals, in the shape of two griffins, or two bulls, back to back, holding up the roof beams.

--

In the paradise gardens, the kings keep real animals, too, from all over the empire: deer, and gazelles, and wild asses, and ostriches, and peacocks, and camels, and elephants, from India, and lions, and leopards, and cheetahs, which are trained to hunt.

And in the Persian countryside, there are wolves, and bears, and wild boar, and foxes, and jackals, and hares, and hedgehogs, and porcupines, and storks, and eagles, and vultures, and the little hoopoes, with their crests, and the nightingales, singing in the gardens, at night, which the Persian poets will love, for thousands of years.
