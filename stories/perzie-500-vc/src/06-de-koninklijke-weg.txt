To hold his great empire together, Darius needs roads. And the Persians build the best roads of the ancient world.

The most famous is the Royal Road, which runs for about two thousand seven hundred kilometres, from Sardis, the old capital of Lydia, near the coast of the Aegean Sea, in the west, across the whole of Anatolia, and Mesopotamia, to Susa, one of the royal capitals, in the south west of Iran.

--

Along the road, every day's journey, about twenty-five kilometres apart, there are royal stations: inns, with stables, and food, and water, and guards. Herodotus says there were a hundred and eleven of them, between Sardis and Susa. Ordinary travellers, walking, take about three months to make the whole journey.

But the king's messengers are much faster.

At each station, there are fresh horses, and fresh riders, waiting, day and night. A messenger gallops from one station to the next, and hands the message to the next rider, who gallops on, and hands it to the next. Like a relay race. In this way, a message from the king can travel the whole length of the Royal Road in about seven days.

Herodotus admired this system very much. He wrote: there is nothing in the world that travels faster than these Persian messengers. Neither snow, nor rain, nor heat, nor darkness of night, prevents them from accomplishing the task proposed to them with the utmost speed.

--

Two and a half thousand years later, the post office of New York, in the United States, carved Herodotus's words over the door of its great main building: Neither snow nor rain nor heat nor gloom of night stays these couriers from the swift completion of their appointed rounds. And many people think of them as the motto of the postmen of America.

The messengers carry the king's orders, and letters, and reports, written on clay tablets, or on parchment, or on papyrus, in the Aramaic language, which is the common language of the empire, sealed with the royal seal.

{img:scribe-writing-aramaic-parchment-seal-clay-office-lamp}
Travellers on the royal roads carry a document, a travel pass, sealed by an official, which says who they are, where they are going, and how much food they should be given, at each station along the way. Some of these travel passes have survived, and say things like: give him, each day, so much flour, so much wine, so much beer, and so much for his horses, from the stores of the king, at each station, until he reaches the king.

--

There are other royal roads too, all over the empire: to Ecbatana, in the north, and Babylon, and Persepolis, and Bactria, and India, and Egypt.

Along the roads travel not only the king's messengers, but soldiers, and officials, and merchants, with their caravans of donkeys, and mules, and camels, and pilgrims, and craftsmen, going to work on the king's palaces, and ambassadors, and families moving house, and the great court of the king himself, moving from one capital to another, with the seasons.

{img:caravan-on-persian-road-mules-camels-mountains-poplar-trees}
The roads bring the empire together. Goods, and ideas, and people, and languages, and religions, travel along them, from one end of the world to the other, more easily than ever before.
