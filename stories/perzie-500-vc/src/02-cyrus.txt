The founder of the Persian Empire was Cyrus the Great.

He was born, around 590 before Christ, a prince of the little kingdom of Anshan, in Persis. There are many stories about his childhood, told later by the Greeks. Herodotus says that his grandfather, the king of the Medes, had a dream that his grandson would take his throne, and ordered the baby to be killed. But the servant who was told to do it gave the baby to a shepherd, whose wife had just lost her own baby, and they brought him up as their own son, in the mountains.

--

When the boy was ten, Herodotus says, he was playing with the other village children, and they chose him to be their king, in their game. And he played the part so well, and so seriously, giving orders, and punishing a boy who disobeyed, that the true story came out, and he was recognized, and sent back to his real parents.

It is probably only a story. But it shows how the people remembered him: as a born king.

In 550 before Christ, Cyrus rebelled against the Medes, and won, and became king of both peoples. Then, he conquered Lydia, in the west, the kingdom of the famously rich king Croesus. And in 539, he took Babylon, the greatest city in the world, almost without fighting.

--

Cyrus was a different kind of conqueror.

He did not destroy the cities he conquered, or kill their people, or drag them off as slaves, as the Assyrians and Babylonians had done before him. He respected their gods, and their temples, and their customs, and let them keep their own laws, and their own languages, as long as they obeyed him, and paid tribute.

{img:clay-cylinder-cuneiform-script-museum-glass-close-soft-light}
In Babylon, he had a declaration written, in cuneiform, on a clay cylinder, which still exists, and is in the British Museum, in London. It says that he entered Babylon in peace, and that the people welcomed him, and that he allowed the peoples who had been carried away to Babylon to go home, to their own lands, and to rebuild their temples, and take their gods home with them.

--

Among those peoples were the Jews, who had been taken to Babylon, by King Nebuchadnezzar, nearly fifty years before. Cyrus allowed them to go home, to Jerusalem, and to rebuild their temple. In the Bible, in the book of Isaiah, Cyrus is called the anointed one of the Lord, the only foreign king ever to be given that title.

Cyrus passed away in 530 before Christ, fighting in Central Asia. He was buried at his capital, Pasargadae, in Persis, in a simple, beautiful tomb, of white stone, like a little house, with a gabled roof, on top of six great steps.

{img:tomb-of-cyrus-pasargadae-white-stone-steps-plain-dawn}
The Greek writers say that on the tomb there was an inscription: O man, I am Cyrus, who founded the empire of the Persians, and was king of Asia. Do not grudge me this monument.

When Alexander the Great came to Pasargadae, two hundred years later, he found the tomb robbed, and was saddened, and ordered it to be restored. The tomb still stands today, on the empty plain, among the grass and the wildflowers.
