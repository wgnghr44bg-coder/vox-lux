In the year 500 before Christ, in the Greek cities of Ionia, on the western edge of the Persian Empire, something new is happening: philosophy.

A hundred years before, in Miletus, a man called Thales, a merchant, and an engineer, and an astronomer, began to ask a new kind of question: not which god made the world, but what is the world made of? And he answered: water. Everything is made of water, in different forms. It was the wrong answer. But it was a new way of thinking: looking for natural causes, for things, instead of stories about gods.

--

Thales, it was said, predicted an eclipse of the sun, in 585 before Christ, which stopped a battle between the Medes and the Lydians, because the soldiers on both sides were so frightened that they made peace. Perhaps he had learned about eclipses from the Babylonians.

After Thales, other thinkers in Miletus went on asking questions. Anaximander drew the first map of the world, and said that the earth floats in space, held by nothing. He said that living things first came from the sea, and that human beings came from fish. Anaximenes said that everything is made of air.

{img:philosopher-drawing-world-map-sand-students-miletus-harbour}
And in the year 500 before Christ, in Ephesus, the great city of the temple of Artemis, there lives a strange, proud, lonely philosopher, called Heraclitus.

--

Heraclitus says that everything is always changing: that you cannot step into the same river twice, because new water is always flowing past. That everything flows. That the world is like an ever-living fire, always burning, always changing, and always the same. That the way up and the way down are one and the same.

He writes his thoughts in short, mysterious sayings, which are hard to understand, so that people called him Heraclitus the Obscure. And he gives his book to the temple of Artemis, to keep.

{img:river-flowing-over-stones-ephesus-countryside-reflections-calm}
Some people think that Heraclitus was influenced by the ideas of the Persians, about fire, and about the struggle between opposites. We do not know. But in the cities of Ionia, on the edge of the Persian Empire, where Greeks and Persians and Lydians and Babylonians met, and traded, and talked, new ideas were born, which would change the way people thought, for ever.
