How are the sons of the Persian nobles brought up?

The Greek writers, Herodotus, and Xenophon, and later Strabo, describe it with admiration. Until the age of five, a Persian boy lives with his mother, and the women of the household, and his father hardly sees him. Herodotus says that this is so that, if the child should die young, his father will not grieve too much.

--

From five, until twenty, the boy is educated, together with the other boys of noble families, often at the court of the king, or the satrap.

They get up before dawn, and are gathered together, by the sound of a brass trumpet, and taken out to exercise. They run, and swim, and ride, and throw the javelin, and shoot with the bow. They learn to endure heat, and cold, and rain, and hunger, and thirst, and tiredness. They learn to hunt, on horseback, in the hunting parks, and in the mountains, and to look after their horses, and to make camp.

They learn to sing the hymns of the gods, and to tell the stories of the heroes, and the great deeds of the kings. They learn justice, Xenophon says, in their schools, as Greek boys learn to read and write: they bring disputes to their teachers, and the teachers judge them, and punish the boys who are ungrateful, or dishonest, or unfair.

--

And above all, they learn three things, as Herodotus says: to ride, to shoot, and to tell the truth.

Darius himself, in the inscription on his tomb, boasts of these things. He says: I am trained in my hands and my feet. As a horseman, I am a good horseman. As a bowman, I am a good bowman, both on foot and on horseback. As a spearman, I am a good spearman, both on foot and on horseback.

{img:young-persian-noble-bow-quiver-horse-riding-clothes-proud}
The Persians are famous horsemen. Their horses are the finest in the world: the great Nisaean horses, bred on the plains of Media, tall, and strong, and beautiful, and fast, which only the king, and the greatest nobles, may ride.

--

The Persians wear trousers, which the Greeks find very strange, and funny, because the Greeks wear tunics, and cloaks, and nothing on their legs. Persian trousers are practical, for riding horses. They wear long-sleeved tunics, and coats, and soft felt caps, or hoods, which cover their ears and chins, against the wind and the dust. And at court, long, flowing robes, with wide sleeves, and tall, fluted hats.

The nobles wear gold jewellery: necklaces, called torques, and bracelets, often with the heads of animals, lions, and ibexes, and griffins, at the ends, which the king gives them, as rewards for their service.

{img:golden-torque-bracelet-ibex-heads-oxus-treasure-close-light}
Many of these beautiful golden ornaments were found, in the nineteenth century, by the river Oxus, in Central Asia, in a great hoard, called the Oxus Treasure, which is now in the British Museum, in London: little golden chariots, with horses, and golden bracelets, with griffins, and golden plaques, with figures of priests, and golden fish, and rings, and coins.
