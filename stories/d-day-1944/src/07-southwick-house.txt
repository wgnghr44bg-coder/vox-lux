In the hills just north of Portsmouth, there is a large white country house. It has tall windows and white columns beside the front door. Woods and parkland surround it on every side. It is called Southwick House.

In the spring of 1944, it has become the headquarters of the landing. Here, in the library and the drawing room, the generals and admirals meet. They sit among the tall windows and the old bookshelves, to plan and to decide.

--

The top commander is the American general Dwight Eisenhower, whom people call Ike. He is a tall, friendly, smiling man from Kansas. He is very good at getting people from many countries to work together. With him are the British general Montgomery, the admiral Ramsay, and the chiefs of the air forces.

In one room there is a huge map of the Channel and the coast of Normandy. It covers a whole wall, from the floor to the ceiling. A toy company made it out of wood. Officers move little markers on it, to show where every ship is. As the reports come in, the markers move slowly across the sea. That map is still there today, on the same wall. It looks exactly as it did on the morning of D-Day.

{img:southwick-house-wall-map-channel-normandy-ships-markers-officers}
The house is busy at every hour of the day and night. Officers hurry along the corridors with papers in their hands. Telephones ring in every room. In the kitchen, cooks make endless sandwiches and huge pots of tea and coffee. Outside, army cars come and go on the gravel drive, their tyres crunching.

On the evening of the fourth of June, at half past nine, the commanders meet in the library. Rain beats against the tall windows. The wind howls in the trees outside. And James Stagg, the weatherman, comes in. He tells them about the break in the weather he expects on the sixth.

--

The generals ask him many questions, and he answers each one carefully. He tells them honestly that he cannot be certain. Then everyone in the room turns and looks at Eisenhower. It is his decision, and his alone.

He sits silently for a long time, thinking. The rain beats on the glass, and nobody says a word. Then at last he speaks, quietly. I am quite sure we must give the order, he says. I don't like it, but there it is.

{img:library-southwick-house-generals-armchairs-night-lamplight-rain-windows}
The orders go out. But there is still a little time to change them if needed. So at four o'clock in the morning on the fifth of June, the commanders meet again in the library. They want to hear the very latest forecast. The rain is still beating on the windows. But Stagg says the break in the weather is still coming. Eisenhower thinks for a few moments more. And then he says, OK, let's go.

--

The room empties very quickly. The commanders hurry out into the dark and the rain, to their cars, to give their orders. And Eisenhower is left alone in the quiet library. The hardest decision of the war has been made. Now there is nothing more he can do but wait.

Outside, as dawn comes on the fifth of June, the rain begins to ease a little. And slowly, the wind begins to drop.
