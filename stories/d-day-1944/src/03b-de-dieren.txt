It is not only people who take part in D-Day. There are animals too.

Among the most important of them are the pigeons. In 1944, there are no mobile phones, of course. Radios are heavy, and they can break easily. And the enemy can listen in to radio messages. So the armies and the newspapers use carrier pigeons. The pigeons carry messages home across the sea, from France to England.

--

Pigeons have a wonderful gift. Wherever they are taken, they can always find their way home to their own loft. They can do it even across hundreds of kilometres of land and sea. Nobody knows exactly how they do it, even today. Perhaps they follow the sun. Perhaps they can feel the pull of the earth itself. Perhaps they use smells and landmarks along the way.

So the pigeons are carried across the sea in wicker baskets, with the soldiers and the reporters. When there is a message to send, it is written on a tiny piece of thin paper. The paper is rolled up and put into a little tube on the pigeon's leg. Then the pigeon is let go. It flies up into the sky, circles once, and heads straight for home.

The pigeons are looked after very carefully by their keepers. In their lofts in England, they have clean straw and fresh water and good seed. Their keepers know each bird by name. They talk to them softly and stroke their grey feathers.

{img:pigeon-flying-over-sea-channel-grey-clouds-wind-alone}
One of the most famous of these pigeons was called Gustav. On the morning of D-Day, a reporter on a ship off the French coast wrote a short message. He tied it to Gustav's leg and let him go into the grey sky. Gustav flew back across the sea, against a strong wind. Would he make it all the way home? He flew for more than five hours, nearly three hundred kilometres. And at last he arrived at his loft near Portsmouth. He brought some of the very first news of the landings.

--

For this, Gustav was given a medal for bravery. It is a special medal, given only to animals. And he lived happily in his loft for many years afterwards.

There were dogs as well. Some soldiers had dogs that were trained to jump by parachute with them. The dogs could hear and smell things long before people could. On the night before D-Day, a few of these brave dogs jumped into Normandy with their soldiers, in the dark.

{img:paratrooper-with-alsatian-dog-airfield-1944-gentle-companionship}
One of these dogs was a big Alsatian called Bing. He was gentle and calm, and his soldier loved him very much. Before the flight, they sat together on the grass by the planes. The soldier scratched Bing behind the ears and shared his biscuits with him.

And then there were the cows of Normandy. They stood in their fields in amazement, as gliders landed around them in the night. Parachutes drifted down softly into their meadows. The next morning, the farmers came out to find their fields full of parachutes and gliders and strangers.

--

Later, the farmers' wives gathered up the parachutes left lying in the fields. They were made of white and green silk. There was no other cloth to be had in those days. So the women made them into dresses and blouses and shirts. They even made wedding dresses from them. Some of these parachute wedding dresses are still kept today, in the museums of Normandy. They are white and soft and beautiful.
