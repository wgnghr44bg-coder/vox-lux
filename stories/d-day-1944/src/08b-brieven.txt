In the last days and hours before D-Day, many soldiers sit down to write letters.

They write to their mothers and fathers. They write to their wives and children and sweethearts, and to their brothers and sisters. They cannot say where they are, or what is about to happen. The letters will be checked by an officer and posted later, perhaps after the landing.

--

So they write about other things, ordinary everyday things. They write about the weather and the food. They write about their friends in the camp, and the jokes they tell. They write about the English countryside and the pretty villages. They write about the cows and the hedges and the birds singing in the woods. And they ask about home. How is the garden, they ask, and the dog, and the neighbours?

And they write, as simply as they can, how much they love their families. They write how much they miss them. They write that they will come home soon, when it is all over. And they write that nobody should worry about them.

{img:mother-reading-letter-from-son-kitchen-window-light-1944}
Some of them write a second letter as well. They give it to a friend to keep, just in case. In these letters, they say the things that are hard to say out loud. Thank you for everything, they write. I love you. Look after each other, and live a good life.

--

Many of these letters have survived until today. Families have kept them for eighty years. They kept them in boxes and drawers and in old family Bibles. Now many of them are in museums and in books. So we can still read them. And we can hear the voices of the young men, writing by lamplight on the night before D-Day.

They are often very ordinary letters, and that is what makes them so touching. A young American tells his mother that the English food is terrible. Please send cookies, he writes, lots of them. A young British soldier tells his wife to plant the beans and to kiss the baby for him. A young Canadian tells his girl he has seen the prettiest village in the world. One day, he writes, I will bring you here to see it.

{img:bundle-old-letters-ribbon-photographs-medals-family-box-today}
And they end the way letters always do. All my love, they write. God bless you. See you soon. Don't worry about me.

--

At home, in America and Canada, in Britain and many other countries, the families read the letters. They read them again and again, until the paper grows soft. They keep them in their pockets, or under their pillows at night. They wait for the next one to come. They listen to the news on the radio every evening. And they wonder where their sons and husbands and brothers are tonight.

In a small kitchen somewhere, a mother sits by the window with a letter in her lap. The kettle is singing on the stove, but she does not get up. She reads the last lines once more, slowly. Don't worry about me, Mum, it says. She smiles, and folds the letter carefully, and puts it back in its envelope. Then she gets up and makes the tea.
