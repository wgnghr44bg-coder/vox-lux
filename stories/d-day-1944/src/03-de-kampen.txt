At the end of May, the soldiers who will cross the sea first are moved into special camps near the coast. And the gates are closed behind them.

The camps are sealed, which means nobody may go in or out without special permission. There are wire fences all around and guards standing at the gates. The soldiers may not send letters or make telephone calls. They may not talk to anyone outside the fence. That is because now, at last, they are going to be told the great secret.

--

The soldiers give these camps a funny name. They call them the sausage machines. The men go in at one end, they say, and come out at the other end, onto the ships.

Inside, the soldiers live in tents among the trees and in the fields. And for the first time, they are told where they are going.

{img:briefing-tent-soldiers-sand-table-model-coast-officer-pointing}
In big tents, the officers gather their men around long tables. There are maps, and photographs taken from planes flying low over the coast. There are models made of plaster and sand, painted carefully in green and brown. They show the coast where the men will land. They show the beaches and the dunes and the cliffs. They show the villages and the roads and the little churches. Every house, every hedge and every path is there.

--

But the names written on the maps are not the real names. They are code names, so the real place stays secret. Even now, if anyone talked, the enemy would not learn the real place. The beaches have names like Utah and Omaha, Gold and Juno and Sword.

The men study the maps and models again and again, until they know them by heart. Some of them recognise the coast from seaside holidays before the war. And then they realise where they are going. It is Normandy.

{img:soldiers-waiting-camp-playing-cards-reading-writing-letters-sunshine}
Then there is the waiting, which takes up most of the days. The men clean their rifles again and again. They check their equipment and pack it and unpack it. They are given French money and little books of useful French words. They practise saying good morning and thank you, and they laugh at each other's accents. They are given pills for seasickness, and paper bags in case the pills do not work. They play cards for the French money, and lose it, and win it back again. They read, and they write letters that will not be posted until later. They sleep and talk and play football in the clearings. And they eat better food than they have had in years. There is steak and white bread, and even ice cream.

--

On Sunday morning, the chaplains hold services in the open air. Many men go who have not been to church for years. They kneel on the grass in the sunshine among the trees. They sing the old hymns they learned as boys, and they pray quietly.

{img:chaplain-field-service-soldiers-kneeling-woods-morning-quiet}
And in the evenings, as the sun sets behind the trees, they sit outside their tents. They look up at the sky, which is turning pink and gold. Birds are singing in the woods, as if nothing at all were different. The men talk quietly about home and their families. They talk about the girls they hope to marry and the jobs they hope to have. And they talk about what they will do when the war is over.
