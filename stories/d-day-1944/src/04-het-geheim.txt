The greatest secret of the war is a simple question. Where, and when, will the Allies land?

The enemy knows that the landing is coming. They have built great defences all along the coast of France. There is concrete and steel, and there are guns and obstacles on the beaches. But the coast is very long. They cannot be strong everywhere at once. If they knew where the landing would be, they could gather their armies there and wait.

--

So the Allies have made a huge plan full of tricks. They want the enemy to believe that the landing will come somewhere else. Perhaps at Calais, further north, where the sea is narrowest. Or perhaps even far away in Norway.

In the east of England, across the sea from Calais, they create a whole army that does not exist. There are camps full of empty tents, with smoke rising from a few cooking fires. There are tanks and lorries made of rubber, blown up like big balloons. Four men can carry one of these rubber tanks and set it up in a field. From the air, it looks just like a real tank.

The men who look after this pretend army have a strange job. They move the rubber tanks around every few days, so they look busy. They make tracks in the mud with a special roller, as if real tanks had driven there. They hang washing on lines between the empty tents. Sometimes they laugh about it in the evenings. But they know it is very important work.

{img:dummy-landing-craft-canvas-wood-estuary-moored-rows-dawn}
There are pretend landing boats made of wood and canvas, floating in rows on the rivers. There are radio operators sending thousands of messages every day, from one empty camp to another. It sounds as if a great army were there, talking to itself. And a famous American general is put in charge of this ghost army. That way, the enemy will think it must be real.

--

And the plan works very well. The enemy believes it. They keep a large army waiting near Calais for many weeks. They wait there for a landing that never comes.

But there are also some funny moments, when the secret nearly seems to slip out.

{img:schoolmaster-crossword-puzzle-newspaper-armchair-pipe-1944}
In the weeks before D-Day, the crossword puzzle in a big English newspaper had some very strange answers. One day, one of the answers was Utah. Another day, it was Omaha. Then came Overlord, which was the secret code name for the whole landing. Then Mulberry, and then Neptune. All of them were secret code names.

--

The security officers were very alarmed. They went to see the man who made the crosswords. He was a quiet schoolmaster, who liked his pipe and his garden. They asked him questions for hours. But he had no idea at all what they were talking about.

Many years later, one of his old pupils explained what had happened. The boys at the school spent a lot of time with the soldiers camped nearby. They heard these strange words and remembered them. And the schoolmaster sometimes asked the boys to fill in empty crossword grids with any words they knew. Then he wrote clues for those words. So the great secrets went innocently from the soldiers to the schoolboys, and into the newspaper. And luckily, nobody on the other side noticed.

{img:quiet-country-road-signposts-removed-1944-hedges-afternoon}
All over Britain, the signposts have been taken down from the roads. If an enemy ever landed, he would not know where he was. Even the names of the railway stations have been painted over. Travellers have to ask the porters where they are. And posters everywhere remind people not to talk about what they see. Careless talk costs lives, the posters say. And people do not talk.
