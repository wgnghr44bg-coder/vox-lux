The greatest secret of the war is this. Where, and when, will the Allies land?

The enemy knows the landing is coming. They have built great defences all along the coast of France. Concrete and steel and guns. Obstacles on the beaches. But the coast is very long. They cannot be strong everywhere. If they knew where the landing would be, they could gather their armies there and wait.

--

So the Allies have made a huge plan of tricks. They want the enemy to believe the landing will come somewhere else. At Calais, further north, where the sea is narrowest. Or even in Norway.

In the east of England, across the sea from Calais, they create a whole army that does not exist. There are camps with empty tents. There are tanks and lorries made of rubber, blown up like balloons. Four men can carry one and set it up in a field. From the air, it looks like a real tank.

{img:dummy-landing-craft-canvas-wood-estuary-moored-rows-dawn}
There are fake landing boats made of wood and canvas, floating in rows on the rivers. There are radio operators sending thousands of messages every day, from one empty camp to another. As if a great army were there, talking to itself. And a famous American general is put in charge of this ghost army, so the enemy will think it must be real.

--

And it works. The enemy believes it. They keep a large army waiting near Calais for weeks. For a landing that never comes.

But there are also funny moments, when the secret nearly seems to slip out.

{img:schoolmaster-crossword-puzzle-newspaper-armchair-pipe-1944}
In the weeks before D-Day, the crossword puzzle in a big English newspaper had some very strange answers. One day, the answer was Utah. Another day, Omaha. Then Overlord, the secret code name for the whole landing. Then Mulberry. Then Neptune. All secret code names.

--

The security officers were alarmed. They went to see the man who made the crosswords. He was a quiet schoolmaster. They questioned him for hours. But he had no idea what they were talking about.

Many years later, one of his old pupils explained. The boys at the school spent a lot of time with the soldiers camped nearby. They heard these strange words. And the schoolmaster sometimes asked the boys to fill in empty crossword grids with words. Then he wrote clues for them. So the great secrets went innocently from the soldiers, to the schoolboys, into the newspaper. And luckily, nobody on the other side noticed.

{img:quiet-country-road-signposts-removed-1944-hedges-afternoon}
All over Britain, the signposts have been taken down from the roads. If an enemy ever landed, he would not know where he was. And posters everywhere remind people not to talk about what they see. Careless talk costs lives, they say. And people do not talk.
