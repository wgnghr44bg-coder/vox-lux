In the spring of 1944, the south of England has become one huge army camp.

Nearly two million soldiers, sailors and airmen are gathered in Britain, getting ready. There are British and Americans and Canadians. There are Free French and Poles and Norwegians. There are Dutch and Belgians and Czechs. And there are men from Australia and New Zealand and many other countries too.

--

They live in camps in fields and woods and parks, all over the south of England. Their tanks and lorries and guns are parked along the roads. Their supplies are stacked in great piles beside the roads, covered with canvas. The airfields are full of planes and gliders, lined up in long rows on the grass.

In the mornings, the camps wake up early. Soldiers wash in tin basins outside their tents, splashing cold water on their faces. They shave with little mirrors hung on the tent poles. They queue for breakfast with their tin plates and mugs. There is porridge and bread and sweet tea, and sometimes a fried egg. Then the sergeants call out the orders for the day.

People joked that the island was so full of soldiers and equipment that it might sink. Only the big silver balloons floating above it were holding it up, they said.

{img:barrage-balloons-silver-sky-over-harbour-town-1944-afternoon}
For months, the soldiers have been training for what lies ahead. They practised landing from boats on beaches in the west and in Scotland. They climbed cliffs with ropes and crossed rivers on little bridges. They marched for miles along the country lanes, singing to keep in step. They jumped from planes by parachute over the green fields. In the evenings they came back to their camps tired and hungry, with mud on their boots.

--

The people of the villages have grown used to having them around. The soldiers buy cups of tea and buns in the village cafés. They go to the pubs and play darts with the local men. They go to the dances in the village halls on Saturday nights. And they walk along the lanes with the local girls in the long summer evenings.

The children follow them everywhere they go. They collect badges and buttons from the soldiers' uniforms. They ask for chewing gum and chocolate. They climb on the tanks when they are allowed to. They watch the planes flying over, and they know the names of all of them.

{img:children-watching-tanks-convoy-village-street-waving-1944}
And then, at the end of May, everything changes. The soldiers are told to pack their kit. The long convoys begin to move toward the coast, day and night. There are long slow lines of lorries and tanks and jeeps, rumbling through the villages. The soldiers sit in the back of the lorries and wave to the people by the road.

--

The people of the villages stand at their garden gates and watch them go. Some of the women hand up cups of tea and little bunches of flowers. An old man takes off his cap as the lorries pass. The children run beside the lorries for as long as they can, shouting goodbye. And everyone knows, without being told, what it means. It will be soon now, very soon. When the last lorry has gone, the lanes are strangely quiet. Only the swallows are left, flying low over the hedges.
