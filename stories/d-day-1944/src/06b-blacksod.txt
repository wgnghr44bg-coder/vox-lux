Far away, on the wild west coast of Ireland, there is a little lighthouse and a post office. They stand at the very end of a long, narrow piece of land. The place is called Blacksod Point.

It looks out over the Atlantic Ocean toward America. There is nothing between Blacksod and America but the wide grey sea. The grass is short and tough there, bent by the wind. Sheep graze among the stones, and the waves boom against the rocks all day and all night.

--

Ireland is not fighting in the war. But for years, the little weather station at Blacksod has sent weather reports every hour to Dublin. From there, the reports are quietly passed on to the forecasters in Britain. That is because Blacksod is one of the first places in Europe that the Atlantic weather reaches. Whatever weather comes to Blacksod will reach England and France a day or so later.

The lighthouse keeper, Ted Sweeney, and his young assistant, Maureen Flavin, take the readings. They do it every hour, day and night. They read the air pressure on the barometer. They measure the temperature. And they note the wind and the clouds and the rain.

Between the readings, there is plenty of ordinary work to do. Maureen sorts the letters in the little post office and sells stamps to the neighbours. She makes tea on the stove and listens to the radio. In the evenings she walks along the shore, with her coat wrapped tightly around her. She knows every mood of the sea.

{img:young-irish-woman-reading-barometer-post-office-lighthouse-dawn}
On the third of June, 1944, it is Maureen's twenty-first birthday. Very early that morning, at one o'clock, she goes to read the barometer. She holds up her lamp and looks closely. The pressure is falling fast. She knows what that means. A storm is coming.

--

She sends the report by telephone, as she always does. Soon afterwards, the telephone rings. A voice with an English accent asks her a question. Please check that reading, and repeat it. So she checks it again carefully. The reading is the same. Later they ask her again, and again she checks. Why do they keep asking, she wonders? She has no idea.

She does not know that her readings are being carried across the sea to a house in the south of England. There, the generals and the forecasters are making the most important decision of the war.

{img:atlantic-waves-rocky-shore-mayo-storm-clouds-lighthouse-beam}
Her reports helped to warn of the storm on the fifth of June. That is why the landing was put off by a day. And her later reports helped them to see the break in the weather coming just behind it.

--

Maureen later married Ted Sweeney, the lighthouse keeper. She lived at Blacksod for the rest of her long life, and she raised her family there. For many years, she did not know how important her birthday reading had been. Only much later did she learn the whole story. When she was very old, she was honoured by the American government for her part in D-Day. She passed away peacefully at the age of a hundred, in her home on the edge of the Atlantic. She had watched the weather and the waves there all her life.
