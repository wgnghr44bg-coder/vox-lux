A few kilometres inland from the beaches lies the old town of Bayeux. On the seventh of June, the day after D-Day, it becomes one of the first towns in France to be freed.

And by great good luck, Bayeux is hardly damaged at all. Its old stone houses are still standing. Its narrow streets and its little river with old water mills are still there. And its great cathedral, with its tall grey spires, still rises above the roofs.

--

The people of Bayeux come out into the streets in the morning sunshine. They hang out the French flag, which has been hidden away for four years. They give flowers to the soldiers and offer them glasses of cider. The cathedral bells ring out over the town. Old women weep with joy, and children climb onto the jeeps. And everyone can hardly believe that it is true.

In the bakery, the baker makes extra bread that day, and he gives some away for free. In the café on the square, the owner brings out his best bottles from the cellar. People who have not smiled for years are laughing in the streets.

A week later, on the fourteenth of June, a tall French general arrives in Bayeux. His name is Charles de Gaulle. For four years, he has led the Free French from London. He has spoken to the French people on the radio, giving them hope. Now he sets foot in France again for the first time in four years. In the square of Bayeux, he speaks to the people. They crowd around him and cheer, and many of them cry. France is coming back, they feel.

{img:general-de-gaulle-speaking-crowd-bayeux-square-june-1944-flags}
Bayeux is also famous for something much older. It has a great embroidery, about seventy metres long and nearly a thousand years old. It tells the story of another crossing of the sea. In the year 1066, Duke William of Normandy sailed the other way, from Normandy to England. He took his ships and his horses and his knights with him. The embroidery shows it all, stitched in coloured wool on linen. There are ships and horses, castles and feasts. And there is a comet, shining in the sky.

--

During the war, the embroidery was hidden away to keep it safe. Now it is back in Bayeux, in its own quiet museum. Visitors walk slowly past it, from one end to the other. It is like a very long picture book. You can see the men building the ships, cutting down trees with their axes. You can see the horses being led on board, and the sails filling with wind. You can even see the cooks preparing a feast, with meat on spits.

{img:bayeux-tapestry-panel-ships-horses-sail-embroidered-wool-museum-light}
So in Bayeux, two great sea crossings meet. One was in 1066, from Normandy to England. And one was in 1944, from England to Normandy. They are nearly nine hundred years apart. And the old town has seen them both.

Today, the streets of Bayeux are quiet and peaceful. Flowers hang in baskets from the windows. People sit at café tables in the sun, drinking coffee. And the cathedral bells still ring out every hour, over the old grey roofs.
