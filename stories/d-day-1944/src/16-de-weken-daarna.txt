In the weeks after D-Day, the great floating harbours are towed across the sea. They are put together off the coast of Normandy.

The huge concrete blocks, as big as houses, are sunk in a long line out at sea. Old ships are sunk beside them to make a wall. Behind this wall, the sea becomes calm. Inside, there are floating piers and long floating roads. They rise and fall with the tide. They stretch from the beach right out to the ships. So lorries and supplies can drive straight off the ships and onto the land.

--

At Arromanches, a little seaside town, the great harbour is built in only a few weeks. For months afterwards, millions of tons of supplies come through it. Hundreds of thousands of soldiers come ashore there too.

In the middle of June, a great storm strikes the coast. It is the worst summer storm in forty years, and it lasts for three days. It wrecks one of the two harbours and damages the other badly. For three days, nobody knows if the harbour at Arromanches will survive. But it holds. It is repaired quickly. And it goes on working for many months.

{img:normandy-village-liberated-1944-families-flags-soldiers-flowers-street}
Slowly, through the summer, the armies move forward through the hedges and the fields and the orchards of Normandy. It is a long, hard summer. Many towns and villages are damaged, and the people of Normandy go through a great deal. We remember them gently, and with sorrow. But in village after village, the flags come out of hiding. People stand in the streets with flowers and wine and bread. The children run alongside the jeeps, just as Tom did in England.

--

At the end of August, the armies break out of Normandy and move quickly across France. On the twenty-fifth of August, Paris is free again. The great bells of Notre-Dame ring out over the city. The people of Paris pour into the streets, singing and dancing. Then Belgium is freed, and part of the Netherlands. And after a long, hard winter, the war in Europe finally ends, in May 1945. It ends eleven months after D-Day.

{img:normandy-farm-family-rebuilding-1945-orchard-blossom-son-returned}
And what about the farming family in Normandy, with their orchard and their cows? All through the summer and autumn, they work to repair their farm. The father mends the barn roof and the broken fences. The mother and the children plant new vegetables and look after the animals. The grandmother sits by the fire and knits warm socks for the winter. They wait for news of their son, and the months pass slowly.

Their son, the prisoner, comes home at last in the spring of 1945. One evening in May, he walks slowly up the lane to the farmhouse. He is thin and tired, after five long years away. The apple trees are in full blossom, pink and white. His mother sees him from the kitchen window. She drops what she is holding and runs out to meet him. And the whole family cries and laughs and holds him in the farmyard, under the white blossom.

That evening, they eat together at the big kitchen table, all of them at last. The grandmother lights a candle and says a prayer of thanks. The old horse Pompon looks in through the stable door. And outside, the blossom falls softly in the orchard, like snow.
