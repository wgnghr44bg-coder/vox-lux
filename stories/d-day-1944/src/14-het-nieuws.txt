In Britain, on the morning of the sixth of June, everyone is listening to the radio.

All night long, they heard the planes going over. Since dawn, there have been rumours everywhere. But people are waiting for their own news, from the BBC.

--

At nine thirty-two in the morning, the voice of the BBC announcer comes over the radio. It is calm and clear. It reaches into every home and factory, every office and school in Britain. This is the BBC Home Service, he says. Here is a special bulletin. D-Day has come. Early this morning, the Allies began landing on the north-western coast of Europe.

{img:factory-workers-stopped-listening-loudspeaker-d-day-news-1944}
All over Britain, people stop what they are doing and listen. In the factories, the machines are switched off. The workers gather around the loudspeakers in their overalls. In the offices, the clerks put down their pens. In the schools, the teachers stop the lessons, and the children sit very still. In the shops, the shopkeepers and the customers stand together and listen.

--

There is no great celebration. People are quiet and serious. They know this is not the end, but only the beginning. And they know that many young men they love are there now, on the beaches and in the fields of France. So they go quietly back to their work. They think of those young men all day long. And they wait for more news.

{img:london-street-people-reading-newspapers-headlines-invasion-crowd-1944}
In London, people buy every new newspaper as soon as it comes out. The newspaper boys can hardly keep up. People stand in the streets reading the headlines. They talk quietly to strangers, which Londoners do not usually do. On the buses, people share their newspapers with each other.

In America, it is still the middle of the night when the news comes. People are woken up by the news on their radios. Church bells ring out in the dark towns. And people go to the churches in the night, to pray.

--

At midday, Mr Churchill tells Parliament that the landings have begun. So far, he says, everything is going according to plan. And in the evening, at nine o'clock, the King speaks to the nation on the radio.

The King does not speak of victory. Instead, he asks the people to pray. He asks for one great prayer, rising up from the whole nation, for the young men who are far away. And all over Britain that evening, people bow their heads. In their homes and in the churches, they pray together.

{img:family-evening-listening-king-radio-heads-bowed-living-room-1944}
In a small living room, a family sits around the wireless set. The grandmother sits in the armchair, with her knitting still in her lap. The father stands by the fireplace, and the mother sits on the sofa with the children. When the King asks them to pray, they all bow their heads together. The clock on the mantelpiece ticks softly. Outside, the long summer evening is still light.

In America too, the President speaks on the radio that evening. Instead of a speech, he reads a prayer that he wrote himself. It is a prayer for the soldiers and for the whole nation. Millions of Americans pray along with him, in their homes. Almighty God, he begins. Our sons, the pride of our nation, have this day set out upon a mighty task.
