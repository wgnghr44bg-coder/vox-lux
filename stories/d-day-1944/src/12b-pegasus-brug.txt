Shortly after midnight on the sixth of June, the first soldiers arrive in France. They come silently, out of the night sky.

They come in gliders, which are big wooden planes without any engines. The gliders are towed across the sea behind bombers, on long ropes. Then, high above the coast, the ropes are let go. And the gliders float down in silence through the dark, to land in the fields.

--

Six of these gliders are heading for two bridges near a little village, a few kilometres from the sea. One bridge crosses a canal, and the other crosses a river right beside it. The soldiers must take the bridges quickly and quietly, before they can be damaged. Then the soldiers landing on the beaches in the morning will be able to cross them.

The gliders come down in the dark, and the pilots can hardly see the ground. Will they find the right field? They land at sixteen minutes past midnight, in a little field right beside the canal bridge. They land so close and so exactly that the pilots are praised for it ever after. People call it some of the finest flying of the whole war. Within minutes, the bridge is in the hands of the British soldiers. We will leave that brief, sharp moment there, in the dark and the distance.

{img:canal-bridge-normandy-cafe-house-beside-dawn-quiet-1944}
Beside the bridge there is a little café. It belongs to a French family called Gondrée. There is a husband and wife, and their two little daughters. They have been hiding in their cellar all night, frightened, listening to the noise outside.

--

In the early hours of the morning, there is a knock at the door. The family hears voices outside, speaking English. The father goes up the stairs and opens the door carefully. The soldiers come in, tired and dirty. And then he realises who they are. They are British. They have come at last.

The Gondrées' home is one of the very first houses in France to be freed. The father goes out into his garden with a spade. He digs up bottles of champagne that he buried there four years earlier. He had been saving them for exactly this day. He opens them and pours them out for the soldiers. His wife and daughters come up from the cellar. They hug the soldiers and kiss them on both cheeks. Afterwards, their faces are covered with the black paint from the soldiers' faces. Everyone laughs and laughs.

{img:cafe-gondree-today-pegasus-bridge-flags-visitors-flowers}
The bridge was later named Pegasus Bridge. It was named after the flying horse that was the badge of the British airborne soldiers.

--

The café is still there today, beside the canal, with its flags and photographs and memories. For many years it was run by the youngest daughter. She was the little girl of four who had hidden in the cellar that night. Every year she welcomed the old soldiers and their families back to her café. She poured them drinks and listened to their stories. And the original bridge stands nearby, in the garden of a museum. Visitors can walk across it slowly and remember.
