Of all the soldiers in England in 1944, the ones who make the biggest impression are the Americans.

They began to arrive about two years earlier. By the spring of 1944, there are more than one and a half million of them all over Britain. For many English villagers, these are the first Americans they have ever met. And for many of the Americans, it is the very first time they have ever left home.

--

The Americans seem very different to the English villagers. They are tall and well fed and cheerful. They talk loudly in their unfamiliar accents. They chew gum all the time. Their uniforms are smarter than the British ones. And they are paid much more money.

They bring things that the British have not seen for years, because of the war. They bring chewing gum and chocolate, oranges and tinned peaches. They bring nylon stockings, which the young women love. And they give these things away generously. They give them to the children, to the girls, and to their new friends.

{img:gis-children-candy-gum-english-lane-laughing-jeep-1944}
Got any gum, chum, the English children shout, running after the jeeps. And the Americans laugh and throw them chewing gum and sweets. The children love them for it. Some of the children have never tasted an orange before. They hold it in their hands and smell it for a long time before they eat it.

--

The Americans bring their music with them as well. They bring jazz and swing and the big band songs. They teach the English girls fast new dances in the village halls. They whirl them round and swing them through the air. The old people sitting at the side of the hall shake their heads and laugh.

There are a few small misunderstandings, of course. The Americans find English beer too warm and English weather too wet. They find English food rather dull and English trains rather small. The English find the Americans loud and a bit boastful at first. But slowly, they get to know each other better. And many of them become good friends.

{img:american-soldiers-thanksgiving-english-family-kitchen-table-shared-meal}
Many English families invite American soldiers into their homes for Sunday tea. They share their small wartime rations with them. In return, the Americans bring tins of meat and fruit and sugar from their stores. For the first time in years, the families have more than enough on the table. In the autumn, some of the Americans even cook a Thanksgiving dinner for their English friends. They explain what Thanksgiving is, and everyone sits together around the kitchen table. The children ask a hundred questions about America, and the soldiers answer every one.

--

Thousands of English girls fall in love with American soldiers, and many of them marry. After the war, these young brides will cross the ocean to America. There they will begin new lives, far from their families and their villages.

And in the first days of June, the Americans too are in their camps by the sea, waiting. They write letters home to their mothers and their sweethearts, far away across the ocean. They write about the pretty villages and the green fields and the rain. They write about the English family who gave them Sunday tea. And they do not say a word about what is about to happen.
