Of all the soldiers in England in 1944, the ones who have made the biggest impression are the Americans.

They began to arrive two years ago. By the spring of 1944, there are more than one and a half million of them all over Britain. For many English villagers, they are the first Americans they have ever met. And for many of the Americans, it is the first time they have ever left home.

--

The Americans seem very different. They are tall and well fed and cheerful. They talk loudly in their strange accents. They chew gum. Their uniforms are smarter than the British ones. And they are paid much more.

They bring things the British have not seen for years. Chewing gum and chocolate. Oranges and tinned peaches. Nylon stockings. And they give them away generously. To the children, to the girls, to their new friends.

{img:gis-children-candy-gum-english-lane-laughing-jeep-1944}
Got any gum, chum? the English children shout, running after the jeeps. And the Americans laugh and throw them chewing gum and sweets. The children love them.

--

The Americans bring their music too. Jazz and swing and the big bands. And fast dances that they teach the English girls in the village halls. They whirl them round and swing them through the air. The old people watching shake their heads and laugh.

There are misunderstandings. The Americans find English beer warm and English weather wet. They find English food dull and English trains small. The English find the Americans loud and boastful at first. But slowly, they get to know each other. And they become friends.

{img:american-soldiers-thanksgiving-english-family-kitchen-table-shared-meal}
Many English families invite American soldiers into their homes for Sunday tea. They share their small rations. The Americans bring tins of meat and fruit and sugar from their stores. For the first time in years, the families have more than enough.

--

Thousands of English girls fall in love with American soldiers and marry them. After the war, they will cross the ocean to America to begin new lives.

And in the first days of June, the Americans too are in their camps by the sea, waiting. They write letters home to their mothers and girlfriends far away. They write about the pretty villages and the green fields and the rain. And they do not say a word about what is about to happen.
