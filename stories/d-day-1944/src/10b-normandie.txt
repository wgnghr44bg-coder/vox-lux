Now let us go across the sea to Normandy, on the evening of the fifth of June, 1944.

Normandy is a green and gentle and beautiful land. It has small fields surrounded by thick, high hedges, growing on banks of earth. Narrow sunken lanes wind between them, shady and cool. There are apple orchards and grey stone farmhouses with steep roofs. There are little villages, each with its own stone church. There are market towns, and the old city of Bayeux with its tall spires. And along the coast there are long sandy beaches and cliffs.

--

Let us visit a farming family in a stone farmhouse, a few kilometres from the sea.

They have farmed this land for many generations. There is a father and a mother and three children, and a grandmother who remembers the last war. They keep brown and white Normandy cows, with dark patches around their eyes like spectacles. They make butter and cream and soft round cheeses. They grow apples for cider in their orchard. And they keep chickens and pigs, and an old brown horse called Pompon.

{img:normandy-farm-kitchen-1944-family-supper-lamp-soup-bread-cider}
The kitchen is the heart of the farmhouse. There is a big wooden table, worn smooth by many years of meals. There is a great open fireplace, with copper pans hanging beside it. Strings of onions hang from the beams, and a cat sleeps on a chair by the fire.

For four years, the family has lived under the occupation. There have been foreign soldiers in the village, and rules and curfews and shortages. Life has been hard and full of worry. Their eldest son is far away, a prisoner of war. They have not seen him for four years. Every night, his mother says his name in her prayers.

--

Lately, there have been more and more planes flying overhead. The family has been told to stay away from the beaches. They are full of mines and obstacles now. Everyone knows that a landing will come one day. But everyone thinks it will come far to the north, near Calais.

Tonight the weather is bad. The wind blows hard from the sea. Rain falls in heavy showers, and the sea is rough. Nobody, the father says, will cross the sea on a night like this.

{img:normandy-family-going-to-bed-candle-stairs-old-farmhouse-wind}
They eat their supper in the kitchen, by the light of an oil lamp. There is soup and bread, butter and cheese, and a little cider. The grandmother says her prayers in her chair by the fire. The children go up the creaking wooden stairs to bed. The father goes out with a lantern to check the cows in the barn. He stands for a moment and looks at the sky. He listens to the wind in the orchard. Then he comes in and locks the door. They all go to bed and blow out their candles. And they lie listening to the wind and the rain on the roof, until they fall asleep.

--

They do not know that out at sea, beyond the horizon, the greatest fleet in history is coming toward them. It is coming through the rain and the wind. They do not know that in a few hours, their lives and their village and their country will change forever.

And in the middle of the night, they will be woken by a sound they have never heard before. It is the roar of hundreds and hundreds of planes, flying low over the farmhouse and the orchard, in the dark.
