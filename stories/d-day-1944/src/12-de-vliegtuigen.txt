In the villages and towns of the south of England, on the night of the fifth of June, people are woken by a sound. It is a sound they will never forget.

It begins at around eleven o'clock in the evening. At first it is a low hum, far away. It grows and grows into a great, deep, steady roar. It fills the whole sky. And it goes on for hours and hours.

--

It is the sound of planes, hundreds and then thousands of them. They are flying south over the houses and the fields and the coast, toward France. There are transport planes carrying paratroopers. There are planes towing gliders behind them on long ropes. There are bombers and fighters, flying high above the rest. There are more planes than anyone has ever seen or heard in a single night.

People get out of bed and go to their windows. They open them and look up into the night. Then they go out into their gardens in their dressing gowns and slippers. They stand on the lawns and in the lanes and on the village greens. And they look up at the sky.

{img:night-sky-formations-aircraft-navigation-lights-over-english-village}
In the moonlight, between the clouds, they can see them. There are great groups of planes, coming wave after wave. Their little lights glow red and green and white, in long rows across the sky. They look like moving stars. Their dark shapes pass across the face of the moon. And all of them are flying the same way, south.

--

The neighbours come out of their houses too. They stand together at their garden gates and talk in low voices. This is it, they say to each other. It has started. And nobody goes back to bed for a long time.

Somebody brings out a pot of tea and some cups on a tray. They stand by the gate and drink it together, still looking up. Nobody quite knows what to say. So they just stand there, side by side, in the summer night.

Some people go to the churches, which have been opened in the night. They kneel and pray for the young men flying over in the dark. By the morning, the churches will be full.

{img:village-church-open-night-candles-people-kneeling-praying-1944}
In the last few days, all the Allied planes have been painted with broad black and white stripes. That way, the soldiers and sailors below will know them. The painters on the airfields worked day and night to finish in time. There was not enough time to do it neatly. So they used brooms and mops and buckets of paint.

--

The children are woken up too. They are brought out into the gardens, wrapped in blankets. Their fathers and mothers hold them up high to see the planes. Remember this, they tell them. And the children did remember. Many of them, when they were very old, still remembered the night of the aeroplanes. They remembered the sound and the moonlight. They remembered their parents' faces looking up. And they remembered the feeling that something enormous was happening.

{img:child-in-blanket-held-by-father-garden-gate-looking-up-night-sky}
The roar goes on all night, over England and the sea and the coast of France. At last, toward dawn, it begins to fade away. People go back inside their houses. They put the kettle on again. They switch on the radio and sit down at the kitchen table. And they wait for the news.
