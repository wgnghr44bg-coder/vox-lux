By the beginning of June, every harbour and river and creek along the south coast of England is full of ships.

In Portsmouth and Southampton and Plymouth, and in every little harbour in between, the ships lie at anchor. They are packed so close together that people say you could walk across the harbour from ship to ship. You would not even get your feet wet, they say.

--

There are great grey battleships and cruisers with their big guns. There are troop ships and supply ships. There are hospital ships, painted white with big red crosses on their sides. And there are landing craft, thousands of them, of every shape and size. Some are big flat ships with great doors at the front. The doors open onto a beach, so that tanks can drive straight out. Others are little boats for about thirty soldiers. They have a ramp at the front that drops down into the water.

Altogether there are nearly seven thousand ships and boats. It is the largest fleet ever gathered in the whole history of the world.

{img:landing-craft-loading-tanks-hard-ramp-concrete-slipway-soldiers}
Along the shore, special concrete ramps slope down into the water. The landing craft come in and open their great doors. Tanks and lorries drive on board, one after another, with a roar of engines. Soldiers march on board with their heavy packs, in long lines. Sailors shout directions and wave their arms. Seagulls circle overhead, crying, as if they too want to know what is happening.

--

In the harbours, there are stranger things too. There are huge blocks of concrete, as big as houses, floating in the water. They are waiting to be towed across the sea. They are parts of two whole harbours, made by people. These harbours will be put together off the coast of Normandy. Then ships can unload there, even where there is no real port.

Nobody has ever done anything like this before. Tens of thousands of workers have built these pieces in secret, for months. Most of them had no idea at all what they were building.

{img:mulberry-concrete-caissons-floating-estuary-tugs-evening-grey}
In the towns along the coast, people see the ships and the soldiers and the tanks rumbling toward the docks. And they understand what it means. They stand on the seafront and on the cliffs in the evenings. They look at the great fleet filling the harbours and the sea, as far as they can see. And they wonder quietly what the next days will bring.

In the little cafés on the seafront, the women behind the counters pour endless cups of tea. Sailors come in to sit for a moment and warm their hands. They say very little, and the women do not ask. But they give the young sailors an extra biscuit, and a kind word.

--

At night, the ships lie dark and silent in the harbours. No lights show anywhere, not even a lamp in a porthole. Guards walk slowly up and down the decks. Soldiers sleep in hammocks below, or wrapped in blankets on the decks. And the sea washes softly against the hulls of the ships, under the summer stars.
