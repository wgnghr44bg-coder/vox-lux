For the children of the villages, the spring of 1944 is the most exciting time of their lives.

Let us meet one of them, a boy of ten called Tom. He lives in a small village in Dorset, not far from the sea. His father is away in the navy, on a ship somewhere at sea. Tom lives with his mother, his little sister Peggy and his grandfather. And for months now, his quiet village has been full of soldiers.

--

Every morning on the way to school, Tom counts the tanks parked under the trees in the lane. He knows every kind of lorry and gun by heart. He knows the badges of every regiment. He has an old biscuit tin full of buttons and badges that the soldiers have given him. It is his greatest treasure, and he keeps it under his bed.

At school, the teacher has trouble keeping the children's attention. Every time a plane flies over, all the heads turn to the window. The teacher sighs and waits until the noise has passed. Then she taps the blackboard and goes on with the sums.

After school, Tom and his friends play soldiers in the fields. They use sticks for rifles and old saucepans for helmets. They crawl through the long grass and hide in the hedges. They pretend to land on beaches at the edge of the village pond. And sometimes the real soldiers stop to watch them. They laugh and give them advice on how to hide better.

{img:soldier-sharing-chocolate-boy-sitting-on-gate-english-lane-evening}
Tom's favourite soldier is a tall American called Joe. Joe comes from a farm in Iowa, far away in the middle of America. He misses his little brother at home, who is the same age as Tom. So in the evenings, Joe sometimes sits on the field gate with Tom. He shares his chocolate and tells him stories about home. He tells him about the cornfields that go on forever, as far as you can see. He tells him about the big red barn, and the dog called Rusty who sleeps by the stove. Tom tells Joe about the village and the sea, and about his father on the ship.

--

Sometimes Peggy comes along too, and sits on the grass by the gate. Joe makes her a little crown of daisies and puts it on her head. She wears it all evening and will not take it off, not even for bed.

At the end of May, Joe comes to say goodbye. We are moving out, he says quietly. I can't say where we are going. He takes the badge off his jacket and gives it to Tom. Keep this for me, he says. Tom holds it very tightly in his hand. Will you come back, he asks? Joe smiles and ruffles his hair. Sure I will, he says. And then he climbs into the lorry, and it drives away down the lane.

That night, Tom puts the badge in his tin box with all his other treasures. And when he says his prayers, he says an extra one. He says a prayer for his father on the ship. And he says a prayer for Joe.

--

Many years later, when Tom is an old man with white hair, he still has that badge. He keeps it on a shelf in his living room, next to a photograph of his father. And he tells his grandchildren about the tall American who sat on the gate. He tells them how Joe shared his chocolate and told stories about the cornfields. It was in the spring before D-Day, he says, when I was ten years old.
