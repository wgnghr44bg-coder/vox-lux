The fifth of June, 1944, is a long day of waiting.

Some soldiers have been on board their ships for days already. They are crowded below decks, in rows of narrow bunks and swinging hammocks. Others are on the open decks under canvas, in the wind and the rain. Many of them feel seasick, even in the harbour, as the ships roll gently at anchor.

--

First they were told that the landing had been put off by a day. Now they are told it will go ahead after all. Tonight, they will sail.

So they wait through the long, grey, windy day. They play cards on the decks, holding the cards tightly so they do not blow away. They read and doze and write letters home. They check their equipment once again. They talk and smoke and drink mugs of hot tea. And they look out at the sea and the other ships all around them, as far as they can see. Silver balloons sway in the wind above the ships.

{img:soldiers-playing-cards-ship-hold-hammocks-kit-bags-lamplight}
Some of them sing old songs together, softly. Some of them pray. Some of them sleep, which is perhaps the best thing to do. Some sit quietly and look at photographs of their wives and children, their sweethearts and their parents. Then they put the photographs back carefully in their pockets, close to their hearts.

Down in the ship's galley, the cooks are busy all day long. They make big pots of stew and thick slices of bread and butter. The soldiers queue with their mess tins and eat sitting on the stairs. Not everyone feels like eating. But the cooks make sure there is always something hot.

--

On some ships, the officers read out a message from General Eisenhower. It has been printed and handed to every soldier, sailor and airman. It begins like this. You are about to begin the great task toward which we have striven these many months. The eyes of the world are upon you. The hopes and prayers of freedom-loving people everywhere go with you.

{img:soldier-reading-printed-message-eisenhower-paper-deck-wind}
And it ends with these words. Good luck, and let us all ask the blessing of Almighty God upon this great and noble work.

--

Many soldiers keep the little printed message folded in their pockets. Later, they send it home to their families. Their families keep it safely for the rest of their lives.

On the British ships, a message from General Montgomery is read out too. The chaplains hold short services on the decks. The soldiers bow their heads in the wind and say the Lord's Prayer together.

{img:ship-chaplain-service-deck-soldiers-heads-bowed-wind-grey-sea}
As the afternoon turns into evening, the ships begin to move. The anchors come up with a long rattle of chains. The engines start, deep and steady. Slowly, one after another, in long lines, the ships sail out of the harbours and the bays. They gather together in the sea, south of the Isle of Wight. The sailors call this meeting place Piccadilly Circus, after a busy square in London. And then the ships turn south, toward France.

--

On the cliffs and the beaches of the south coast, people stand and watch them go in the evening light. There are more ships than anyone has ever seen, filling the sea right to the horizon. Nobody says very much as they watch. Mothers hold their children's hands. Old men stand with their caps in their hands. Some of them wave, and some of them pray quietly. And the ships sail slowly away into the evening.
