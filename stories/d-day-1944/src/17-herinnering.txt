Every year, on the sixth of June, D-Day is remembered.

For many years, the men who were there came back to Normandy every June. They came in their berets and blazers, with their medals pinned on their chests. They came to the beaches and the villages and the bridges where they had landed as young men. And they stood quietly and remembered their friends.

--

The people of Normandy welcomed them every year. They welcomed them with flowers and flags, with music and with gratitude. The old men and women of the villages had been children in 1944. They came out to shake the veterans' hands and to thank them once again. Teachers brought schoolchildren to meet the old soldiers and hear their stories. Families invited them into their homes for lunch, year after year. Some of these friendships lasted for fifty years or more.

On the big anniversaries, there were great ceremonies by the sea. Kings and queens and presidents came. Thousands of people gathered on the cliffs and the sand. And the old soldiers sat in the front rows, in the sunshine, by the sea.

{img:children-giving-flowers-veteran-wheelchair-normandy-village-square}
Eighty years after D-Day, a few of the last veterans came back once more. They were very old now, nearly a hundred, and some even older. They came in wheelchairs and with walking sticks, with their families beside them. And they were welcomed and honoured one last time by the whole world. Little children gave them flowers and drawings, and the old men smiled and thanked them.

--

Some of them did something rather special. In their nineties, and one at the age of a hundred, they jumped by parachute once again. They were tied to an instructor, and they floated down over the fields of Normandy. These were the same fields where they had landed so long ago. The crowds below cheered and clapped, and many of them cried.

Many veterans said the same thing when people called them heroes. I am not a hero, they said. The heroes are the ones who did not come home. I only did my job. And they looked out at the sea and the beach quietly for a long time. And they said nothing more.

{img:war-cemetery-normandy-white-headstones-rows-green-grass-roses-quiet}
Above the beaches there are cemeteries. They are quiet, green and peaceful places. There are rows and rows of white headstones in the grass, among roses and trees, looking out over the sea. Gardeners care for them beautifully, every single day. They cut the grass and water the roses and wash the stones. Visitors walk slowly among the rows in silence. They read the names and the ages. Eighteen, nineteen, twenty. They leave flowers and little flags. And sometimes they leave a letter.

--

And there are memorials all along the coast. There is one in every village, and at every bridge and crossroads where something happened. They have names and flags and flowers. So that nobody who passes by will ever forget.

In the evenings, after the visitors have gone home, the memorials stand quietly in the fading light. The wind blows softly in from the sea. The flags flutter a little and then hang still. And the swallows fly low over the grass, as they did in that summer long ago.
