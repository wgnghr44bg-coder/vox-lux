In the end, everything depends on the weather.

To cross the sea and land on the beaches, the Allies need the right conditions, all at the same time. They need a calm sea, so the small boats can cross safely. They need low water at dawn, so the obstacles on the beaches can be seen. They need a bright moon at night, so the pilots can see where they are going. And they need clear skies, without too much cloud and without too much wind.

--

There are only a few days each month when the tides and the moon are right. In early June, those days are the fifth, the sixth and the seventh. After that, the next chance is weeks away. The date chosen for D-Day is the fifth of June.

And the man who must tell the generals what the weather will be is a tall, serious Scotsman. He is a weather scientist called James Stagg.

{img:meteorologist-james-stagg-tall-scotsman-uniform-studying-weather-map}
Stagg leads a small team of weather forecasters, both British and American. They study reports from weather stations, ships and planes all over the Atlantic Ocean. They draw big weather maps covered with long curving lines. The lines show where the storms are, and where they are going next. The forecasters work at long tables, under bright lamps, with cups of tea going cold beside them.

--

Weather forecasting in 1944 is very hard indeed. There are no satellites and no computers to help. The forecasters work it all out with pencils and maps and years of experience. And the weather over the Atlantic and the Channel can change very quickly.

At the beginning of June, Stagg is worried. All through May, the weather was fine and sunny. Now it is changing. Storms are coming in from the Atlantic, one after another. On the fourth of June, he warns the generals. The weather on the fifth will be bad, he says. There will be strong winds, low clouds and rough seas.

{img:stormy-english-channel-june-1944-grey-waves-rain-ships-at-anchor}
And he is right. On the fourth of June, the wind rises. The rain comes down in grey sheets. The sea in the Channel turns rough, with white waves breaking everywhere. The ships at anchor roll and pull at their chains.

--

So the landing is put off by one day. Some ships have already set out to sea. They must turn round and come back to the harbours to wait. The soldiers crowded on the ships are already seasick. Now they must wait another whole day.

Then, on the evening of the fourth of June, Stagg and his team see something on their maps. Between two storms, there may be a gap. It could be a short break in the weather. There might be a few hours of calmer weather on the sixth of June.

{img:weather-station-report-arriving-teleprinter-paper-strip-night-office}
In a small office nearby, a machine clatters all through the night. It prints the new weather reports on long strips of paper. A young woman tears off each strip and carries it quickly to the forecasters. They read every line with great care.

Is the gap in the weather long enough? Nobody can be completely sure. If they wait for the next chance in two weeks, the secret might be discovered. The soldiers already know everything, and they are waiting on the ships. And the weather in two weeks might be even worse.

--

It is the most important forecast of James Stagg's whole life. He does not sleep at all that night. He sits and stares at his maps, under the lamp. He reads every new report as soon as it arrives. He checks his lines and his numbers again and again. Outside, the wind and rain beat against the windows. And so much now depends on him.
