In occupied France, on the evening of the fifth of June, people are listening to the radio in secret. They listen in hundreds of attics and cellars, in barns and farmhouse kitchens.

It is forbidden to listen to the BBC from London. People who are caught can get into serious trouble. So they listen with the sound turned down very low. The curtains are drawn tight across the windows. Someone keeps watch at the window, looking out into the dark street. The others lean close to the radio, with their ears almost touching it. They listen through all the crackling and whistling.

--

Every evening, after the French news from London, there is a strange part of the programme. It is called the personal messages. A calm voice reads out a long list of sentences that seem to make no sense at all. The carrots are cooked. John has a long moustache. The giraffe has a long neck. The dice are on the carpet.

These are secret messages for the French Resistance. These are the men and women all over France who have secretly worked against the occupation for years. Each message is meant for one group in one place. It tells them something important. Perhaps that supplies will be dropped by parachute tonight. Or that they must get ready. Or that the time has finally come.

{img:bbc-studio-1944-announcer-microphone-reading-messages-french-service}
In a small studio in London, the announcer sits at a microphone with a sheet of paper. He reads each strange sentence slowly and clearly, twice over. He does not know what most of them mean. He only knows that somewhere in France, someone is listening very carefully.

For weeks, the Resistance groups have been waiting for one message in particular.

--

On the first of June, the radio read out the first line of a famous French poem. It is a poem about autumn, by a poet called Verlaine. The long sobs of the violins of autumn, it begins.

That line meant, get ready. The landing will come within days.

And then, on the evening of the fifth of June, at a quarter past nine, the voice reads the second line.

{img:old-radio-glowing-dial-dark-kitchen-french-farmhouse-night-listeners}
They wound my heart with a slow and gentle sadness.

--

This line means that the landing will begin within two days. It is time to begin your work.

All over France that night, the men and women of the Resistance go out quietly into the darkness. They do what they have prepared for, for so long. They cut telephone wires and railway lines. They block the roads with fallen trees. In this way, the enemy will be slowed down when he tries to move his soldiers to the coast. They go on bicycles and on foot, through the fields and the woods in the dark. They move carefully and bravely, and they do not make a sound.

{img:resistance-members-bicycles-country-road-night-moonlight-france}
In the farmhouse kitchens and the attics, the families who heard the message look at each other in the lamplight. Some of them smile, and some of them cry. Some say a quiet prayer. They do not know exactly what the message means. But they know that something is coming. After four long years, something is finally coming.

--

The poem is a sad autumn poem, about violins and falling leaves and the passing of time. But for the people of France ever since, those two lines have meant something else. They have meant hope, and the end of a long, dark night.
