Today, the coast of Normandy is peaceful again.

The beaches are wide and sandy and very beautiful. In summer, families come to swim and build sandcastles and fly kites. People ride horses along the edge of the sea, where the sand is firm. They sit in the dunes and eat their picnics in the sunshine, as people did before the war. Children run in and out of the waves, shrieking at the cold water.

--

But the past is never very far away. In the dunes, there are still the remains of old concrete defences. They are grey and weathered, half buried in sand and grass. At Arromanches, out in the sea, the great concrete blocks of the floating harbour are still there. They are rusty and broken and covered with seaweed. The waves break gently over them, and seagulls sit on top of them. At low tide, you can walk out across the wet sand toward them.

In the villages there are museums full of old uniforms and jeeps, photographs and letters. On the walls of the houses there are little signs telling what happened there. And in the windows, the flags of America and Britain and Canada hang beside the flag of France.

{img:arromanches-today-mulberry-remains-sea-low-tide-town-church}
The people of Normandy have never forgotten what happened. Every June, the villages are decorated with flags and bunting. Old jeeps drive slowly through the streets in parades. People dress up in the clothes of 1944. They dance in the village squares to old music from the 1940s. In the evening, fireworks go up over the sea. And the church bells ring out across the fields.

--

In Portsmouth, across the sea in England, there is a museum about D-Day. It has a great embroidery of its own, eighty-three metres long. It was made about fifty years ago, by a team of patient needlewomen. It tells the story of D-Day in thirty-four panels of coloured cloth and thread. It is like a modern version of the old embroidery in Bayeux.

And in the little village in Dorset, the lanes are quiet again. The hedgerows are still white with flowers in June. The swallows still fly low over the pond. An old man walks slowly along the lane with his dog. Sometimes he stops at a certain field gate and leans on it for a while. He smiles, and remembers a tall American with a bar of chocolate.

{img:normandy-orchard-today-apple-blossom-old-stone-farmhouse-cows-peaceful}
In the countryside behind the coast of Normandy, the green fields are still there. The high hedges and the sunken lanes are still there. There are apple orchards and grey stone farmhouses, and the brown and white cows. In May, the apple trees blossom pink and white. The cows graze peacefully underneath them. And the farmers make their cider and their cheese, just as their grandparents did.

--

And on the sixth of June every year, at dawn, people come to the beaches. They come from France and Britain and America and Canada, and from many other countries. They stand quietly on the sand, as the sun rises over the sea and the tide comes in. Some of them hold flowers, and some hold the hands of their children. Nobody speaks very much. And together, they remember.
