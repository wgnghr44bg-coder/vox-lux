On the evening of the fifth of June, General Eisenhower drives to an airfield in the green countryside west of London. He wants to see the American paratroopers before they take off.

They will be among the very first to go. They will fly across the sea in the dark. Then they will jump by parachute behind the coast of Normandy, in the middle of the night. They will land hours before the soldiers arrive on the beaches.

--

The paratroopers have darkened their faces with soot and paint, so they will be hard to see at night. Some of them have shaved their heads, leaving a single strip of hair down the middle. They carry so much equipment that some of them can hardly walk. They have parachutes and packs and supplies of every kind. Their friends have to help them climb up into the planes.

Eisenhower walks among them without any fuss, in the soft evening light. He has his hands in his pockets, and he talks to them simply, as one man to another. Where are you from, son, he asks? What did you do before the war? Are you ready?

{img:general-walking-among-young-paratroopers-c47-aircraft-rows-dusk}
The young men answer him cheerfully. They come from Texas and Pennsylvania, from Michigan and from Kansas, just like him. Before the war they were farmers and clerks, factory workers and students. One of them tells him not to worry. We will take care of everything, sir, he says. They all laugh, and Eisenhower laughs with them. For a few minutes, under the evening sky, they are just men talking about home.

--

He stays until the planes start their engines. One after another, they roll out onto the runway and take off into the evening sky. They are heavy and slow as they climb. Black and white stripes have been painted on their wings, so the Allied gunners will know them. They climb and circle and gather into great groups. And then they turn south, toward the sea, as the light fades.

Eisenhower stands on the airfield in the dusk and watches them go. He raises his hand in a salute. And his driver, standing quietly beside him, sees that there are tears in his eyes.

{img:aircraft-taking-off-twilight-airfield-silhouette-figure-watching}
In his pocket, Eisenhower carries a little note. He wrote it in pencil earlier that day, just in case. It says that the landings have not succeeded, and that the decision was his. And it says that if anyone is to blame, it is him alone.

--

He never had to use that note. He put it in his wallet and forgot about it. Weeks later he found it again and showed it to a helper, who kept it safe. Today it is in a museum in Kansas, where Eisenhower grew up. It is a little scrap of paper with a few lines in pencil. It shows a commander who was ready to take all the blame if things went wrong. And it shows a man who would give all the credit to his soldiers if things went right.

That night, Eisenhower goes back to his caravan in the woods near Southwick House. It is a simple caravan, with a narrow bed, a little desk and a few books. He tries to sleep, but he cannot. So he lies on his bed and reads cowboy stories to pass the time. He has always liked them, ever since he was a boy in Kansas. Outside, the trees drip with rain, and the planes hum overhead. And he waits for the news.
