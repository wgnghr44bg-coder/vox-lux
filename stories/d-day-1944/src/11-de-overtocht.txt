On the night of the fifth to the sixth of June, the great fleet crosses the sea.

From the meeting place south of the Isle of Wight, the ships sail south toward France. They go in long lines, along ten paths through the sea. The paths are marked with little lit buoys, like lamps along a road in the dark.

--

In front go the minesweepers. These are small ships that go first, slowly and carefully, through the darkness. They clear safe paths through the hidden mines in the water. Then the great ships behind them can pass safely. It is careful and patient work. The crews do it perfectly, all night long. And the other ships follow behind them.

Then come the warships and the troop ships and the landing ships. Tugboats pull the first parts of the floating harbours. There are nearly seven thousand ships, stretching across the sea for many kilometres. They sail without lights, and in silence.

{img:soldiers-on-deck-night-crossing-wind-spray-life-belts-quiet-faces}
On the ships, the soldiers try to get some sleep. Some of them manage it, wrapped up in their blankets. Many of them are seasick, because the sea is still rough after the storm. The small flat landing boats roll and toss in the waves. Some soldiers sit on the decks in the dark, with spray blowing in their faces. They look out at the shapes of the other ships. They look at the sea and the clouds. And they watch the moon, coming and going between the clouds.

--

Some of them talk together quietly. Some pray, and some think about home. On one ship, someone plays a mouth organ softly in the dark. It is an old tune that everyone knows, and a few men hum along. On another ship, the men sing old songs and hymns together, very quietly. A sailor brings round mugs of hot cocoa, thick and sweet, and the soldiers wrap their cold hands around them.

On the bridges of the ships, the captains and the navigators watch the compass and the charts and the sea. They steer carefully along the safe paths, toward the coast of France. One wrong turn could lead them out of the cleared path. So they never take their eyes off the water.

{img:ship-bridge-night-navigator-compass-red-light-chart-table-1944}
On the bridge, there is only a dim red light, so the men's eyes stay used to the dark. The navigator leans over his chart table with a pencil and a ruler. He marks their position every few minutes. Beside him, the helmsman holds the big wheel steady. Nobody speaks unless they must.

The night is not completely dark. There is a moon in the sky, almost full. Now and then it shines through a break in the clouds. It makes a silver path across the water. For a moment, it lights up the shapes of the ships all around. Then it disappears again behind the clouds.

--

The sea is full of sounds in the night. There is the steady throb of the engines. There is the wind, and the waves slapping against the sides of the ships. There is the creaking of the hulls and the ropes. And overhead, more and more, there is the hum of planes flying south in the dark, toward France.

And the fleet sails on through the night, slowly, steadily and silently. It sails on toward the dawn.
