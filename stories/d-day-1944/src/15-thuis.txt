In the days and weeks after D-Day, the families at home wait.

They wait for news on the radio and in the newspapers every day. They follow the armies across Normandy on maps printed in the papers, with little arrows and lines. They wait for the postman every morning, hoping for a letter. And they try very hard not to worry too much.

--

Slowly, the letters begin to come. They are short letters, written in pencil on scraps of paper. They were written in fields and ditches and barns in France. I'm all right, they say. Don't worry. It's very green here, just like at home. The French people are very kind to us. They gave us cider and flowers. All my love.

The families read these letters again and again. They pass them round to the neighbours, who read them too. They keep them by their beds at night. And they go to sleep a little more easily.

{img:postman-delivering-letter-cottage-door-woman-relief-smiling-1944}
The postman knows how important his job is now. He walks a little faster up the garden paths. When he has a letter from France, he smiles as he hands it over. The women take the letters with shaking hands and read them right there on the doorstep.

For some families, the news is sad, and we will not stay there long. But we will think of them gently. That summer, in many homes, there were empty chairs at the table. It was so in Britain and America, in Canada and France, and in Germany too. And there were mothers and wives and children who missed someone for the rest of their lives.

--

And life at home goes on, as it must. Women go to work in the factories and on the farms and in the hospitals. Children go to school and play in the streets and the fields. The gardens are dug, and the vegetables grow in neat rows. The hay is cut in the meadows and stacked to dry. The harvest is gathered in, as it always is.

Slowly, the south of England empties. The camps in the woods are quiet now. The lanes that were full of tanks and lorries are empty again. Only the marks of the tracks in the grass show they were ever there. The villages and the pubs and the dance halls are quiet too.

{img:empty-army-camp-woods-after-june-1944-abandoned-tent-pegs-quiet-trees}
In the woods where the camps were, a few tent pegs are still stuck in the ground. Here and there lies an empty tin, or a forgotten button. The birds are singing again, and the rabbits come out in the evening. Soon the grass grows back, and the woods are just woods again.

The villagers miss the soldiers. They miss the cheerful Americans and Canadians and British who lived among them for months. They came to the dances and the churches, and they played with the children. Some children cry because their soldier friends have gone. Tom keeps Joe's badge in his tin, and he looks at it every night.

--

The young women who walked along the lanes with the soldiers wait for letters too. Some will marry after the war, and some will never see them again. And some will remember them all their lives. They will remember a summer romance, in a summer of war, among the hedgerows and the blossom.
