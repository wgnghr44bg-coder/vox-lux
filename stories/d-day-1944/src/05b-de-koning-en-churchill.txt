In the days before D-Day, the Prime Minister, Winston Churchill, has an idea. He wants to go with the fleet, on one of the warships. He wants to watch the landings from the sea.

He is sixty-nine years old, and he has led the country through four long years of war. He cannot bear the thought of staying at home in London. He does not want to sit safely at home while the young men he has sent go to France.

--

His advisers are very worried about this idea. If the Prime Minister were lost at sea, it would be a terrible blow for the whole country. They try hard to persuade him not to go. But he will not listen to them. I am the Prime Minister, he says firmly. I will go if I wish.

Then the King hears about it. And King George says, if the Prime Minister is going, then I will go too. I am the head of the navy, he says. My place is with my sailors.

{img:king-writing-letter-desk-buckingham-palace-pen-lamp-1944}
Of course, both of them cannot go, and the King knows it very well. So he sits down at his desk in the palace and writes a letter to Churchill. It is a kind and wise and gentle letter. He says he would very much like to go with the fleet himself. But he knows that he should stay at home. And he says the Prime Minister should stay at home too. Because the country needs them both, safe and well.

--

Churchill has great respect and warm affection for the King. He reads the letter slowly, more than once. And at last, he agrees to stay. Though he grumbles about it to everyone for days.

The King and Churchill meet every week for lunch, all through the war. They sit together in a small room in the palace and serve themselves from the sideboard. There are no servants in the room, so they can talk freely. They talk about the war and the country and the hard choices ahead. Over the years, they have become real friends.

So on the night before D-Day, the King is at Buckingham Palace. Churchill is in London too, in his rooms near Downing Street. Neither of them can sleep at all that night. Churchill tells his wife Clementine how worried he is. He thinks about all the young men crossing the sea in the dark. And she holds his hand and sits with him.

{img:churchill-sleepless-night-map-room-cigar-lamp-looking-at-map}
In the end, the losses on D-Day were far fewer than he had feared. Every one was a deep sorrow for a family somewhere. But the landings went better than many had dared to hope. The next morning, Churchill went to Parliament to tell the country. Things are going according to plan, he said.

--

A few days later, Churchill was allowed at last to cross the sea and visit Normandy. He walked on the sand among the soldiers and the lorries and the ships. He wore his long coat and his hat, and he carried his cigar and his walking stick. He beamed at everyone and made his famous V sign with his fingers. And the soldiers cheered him as he passed. A few days after that, the King went too, and visited his soldiers in Normandy. He shook their hands and thanked them, one by one.
