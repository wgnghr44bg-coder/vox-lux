D-Day could never have happened without the women.

In the map rooms at Southwick House, and at the headquarters of the navy and the air force, young women stand around great map tables. They are there day and night, wearing headphones. As the reports come in, they move little markers of ships and planes with long poles. That way, the commanders can see at a glance where everything is.

--

They work in shifts through the night, quietly and calmly. They know the greatest secret of the war, which is the time and the place of the landing. And they keep that secret perfectly. Not a single word slips out.

Other women work as radio operators and code breakers. They work as clerks and drivers and telephone operators. Some ride motorbikes along the country lanes, day and night, carrying messages. Others pack parachutes for the paratroopers, fold by careful fold. They know that a young man's life will depend on each one.

{img:women-packing-parachutes-long-tables-hangar-silk-careful-1944}
The parachute packers work at long tables in big hangars. They lay out the silk smooth and straight, and they check every line and every strap. They work slowly and carefully, without hurrying. Some of them write a little note and tuck it inside the pack. Good luck, the note says, from the girls in the hangar.

At the airfields, women check the weather and look after the radios and the radar. And some women fly planes themselves. They deliver new planes from the factories to the airfields, flying alone all over Britain.

--

And there are the nurses. On the white hospital ships with their big red crosses, nurses are waiting. In the hospitals along the coast, they are waiting too. They are ready to care for the wounded who will come back across the sea. A few days after D-Day, the first nurses cross to Normandy themselves. They set up hospitals in tents in the fields, among the apple orchards.

In factories all over Britain, women have built planes and ships and landing boats. They have built parts of the floating harbours too. They work long shifts in overalls and headscarves, with their sleeves rolled up.

{img:nurses-hospital-ship-white-red-cross-deck-ready-1944-calm-sea}
On the farms, women have grown the food that fed the soldiers and the whole country. They ploughed the fields and milked the cows and brought in the harvest. And at home, mothers and wives and grandmothers have kept everything going. They managed through the rationing and the blackouts, the worry and the long waiting. They queued at the shops and mended old clothes and grew vegetables in their gardens. They comforted the children and kept the house warm.

--

On the night before D-Day, many of these women work right through the night. They are in the map rooms and at the radios. They are in the hospitals and on the ships. They drink cups of strong tea to stay awake, and they hardly speak. And when the news comes in the morning, they look at each other and smile. They are very tired, and quietly proud. And then they go on working.
