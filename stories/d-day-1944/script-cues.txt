Tonight we go more than eighty years back. We go to the south of England, in the first days of June, 1944.

[long-pause]

It is early summer. The hedgerows are white with wild flowers. The wild roses are beginning to open. The fields are green and the hay is growing tall in the meadows. In the villages, the cottage gardens are full of colour. And the swallows fly low over the ponds.

[pause]

But all along the quiet country lanes, under the trees, there is something strange. Lorries and tanks and jeeps and guns. Parked one behind another, for miles. Covered with nets so they cannot be seen from the air. In the fields there are rows and rows of tents. And everywhere, in the villages and the towns and the lanes, there are soldiers.

[pause]

If you like calm stories from the past, like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

For four years, the coast of France across the sea has been occupied by the German army. And for months, everyone has known that one day the Allies will cross the sea. They will land in France. And they will begin to free Western Europe. Everyone knows it is coming. Nobody knows when. Or where.

[pause]

{img:english-channel-coast-white-cliffs-calm-sea-june-evening-light}
And now, in the first days of June, the whole south of England is waiting. Holding its breath.

[long-pause]

Tonight we will spend the last days and nights before the great day. It will be called D-Day. We will not follow the battles. We will stay, gently, with the waiting. With the soldiers in their camps and on their ships, writing letters and playing cards. With the weathermen, studying their charts through the night. With the general who must make the hardest decision of the war. With a young woman on a lonely coast of Ireland, reading a weather instrument. With people in France listening in secret to a poem on the radio. And with a farming family in Normandy, going to bed on a stormy night, not knowing what the morning will bring.

[pause]

And with the people of England, standing in their gardens at midnight. Looking up at a sky full of aeroplanes. And saying a quiet prayer.

[pause]

{img:village-church-tower-dusk-english-countryside-june-swifts-quiet}
So let us go back to the beginning of June, 1944. To the long light evenings. To the lanes full of soldiers. And to the sea, grey and restless, beyond the cliffs.

[long-pause]

{img:army-camp-tents-rows-english-field-soldiers-washing-morning-1944}
In the spring of 1944, the south of England has become one huge army camp.

[pause]

Nearly two million soldiers and sailors and airmen are gathered in Britain, getting ready. British and Americans and Canadians. Free French and Poles and Norwegians. Dutch and Belgians and Czechs. Men from Australia and New Zealand and many other countries.

[long-pause]

They live in camps in fields and woods and parks all over the south of England. Their tanks and lorries and guns are parked along the roads. Their supplies are stacked in great piles beside the roads, covered with canvas. The airfields are full of planes and gliders, lined up in rows on the grass.

[pause]

People joked that the island was so full of soldiers and equipment, it might sink. Only the big silver balloons floating above it were keeping it up.

[pause]

{img:barrage-balloons-silver-sky-over-harbour-town-1944-afternoon}
For months, the soldiers have been training. They practised landing from boats on beaches in the west and in Scotland. They climbed cliffs and crossed rivers. They marched for miles along the country lanes. They jumped from planes by parachute over the green fields.

[long-pause]

The people of the villages have grown used to them. The soldiers buy cups of tea and buns in the village cafés. They drink in the pubs and play darts with the local men. They go to the dances in the village halls on Saturday nights. And they walk along the lanes with the local girls in the long summer evenings.

[pause]

The children follow them everywhere. They collect badges and buttons. They ask for chewing gum and chocolate. They sit on the tanks when they are allowed. They watch the planes flying over and know the names of all of them.

[pause]

{img:children-watching-tanks-convoy-village-street-waving-1944}
And then, at the end of May, everything changes. The soldiers are told to pack their kit. The convoys begin to move toward the coast, day and night. Long slow lines of lorries and tanks and jeeps. The soldiers sit in the back and wave to the people by the road.

[long-pause]

The people of the villages stand at their gates and watch them go. Some of the women give them cups of tea and flowers. The children run beside the lorries. And everyone knows, without being told. It will be soon now. Very soon.

[long-pause]

{img:american-gis-english-village-pub-locals-darts-friendly-1944}
Of all the soldiers in England in 1944, the ones who have made the biggest impression are the Americans.

[pause]

They began to arrive two years ago. By the spring of 1944, there are more than one and a half million of them all over Britain. For many English villagers, they are the first Americans they have ever met. And for many of the Americans, it is the first time they have ever left home.

[long-pause]

The Americans seem very different. They are tall and well fed and cheerful. They talk loudly in their strange accents. They chew gum. Their uniforms are smarter than the British ones. And they are paid much more.

[pause]

They bring things the British have not seen for years. Chewing gum and chocolate. Oranges and tinned peaches. Nylon stockings. And they give them away generously. To the children, to the girls, to their new friends.

[pause]

{img:gis-children-candy-gum-english-lane-laughing-jeep-1944}
Got any gum, chum? the English children shout, running after the jeeps. And the Americans laugh and throw them chewing gum and sweets. The children love them.

[long-pause]

The Americans bring their music too. Jazz and swing and the big bands. And fast dances that they teach the English girls in the village halls. They whirl them round and swing them through the air. The old people watching shake their heads and laugh.

[pause]

There are misunderstandings. The Americans find English beer warm and English weather wet. They find English food dull and English trains small. The English find the Americans loud and boastful at first. But slowly, they get to know each other. And they become friends.

[pause]

{img:american-soldiers-thanksgiving-english-family-kitchen-table-shared-meal}
Many English families invite American soldiers into their homes for Sunday tea. They share their small rations. The Americans bring tins of meat and fruit and sugar from their stores. For the first time in years, the families have more than enough.

[long-pause]

Thousands of English girls fall in love with American soldiers and marry them. After the war, they will cross the ocean to America to begin new lives.

[pause]

And in the first days of June, the Americans too are in their camps by the sea, waiting. They write letters home to their mothers and girlfriends far away. They write about the pretty villages and the green fields and the rain. And they do not say a word about what is about to happen.

[long-pause]

{img:english-village-children-1944-playing-soldiers-lane-wooden-rifles-summer}
For the children of the villages, the spring of 1944 is the most exciting time of their lives.

[pause]

Let us meet one of them. A boy of ten called Tom. He lives in a small village in Dorset, near the sea. His father is away in the navy. He lives with his mother and his little sister and his grandfather. And for months now, his village has been full of soldiers.

[long-pause]

Every morning on the way to school, Tom counts the tanks parked under the trees in the lane. He knows every kind of lorry and gun. He knows the badges of every regiment. He has a tin box full of buttons and badges the soldiers have given him. It is his greatest treasure.

[pause]

After school, he and his friends play soldiers in the fields. They use sticks for rifles. They crawl through the long grass and hide in the hedges. They pretend to land on beaches in the village pond. And sometimes the real soldiers stop and watch them, and laugh, and give them advice.

[pause]

{img:soldier-sharing-chocolate-boy-sitting-on-gate-english-lane-evening}
Tom's favourite soldier is a tall American called Joe. Joe comes from a farm in Iowa. He misses his little brother, who is the same age as Tom. So in the evenings, Joe sometimes sits on the gate with Tom. He shares his chocolate. He tells stories about the cornfields at home that go on forever. And about the big red barn and the dog called Rusty. Tom tells him about the village and the sea and his father on the ship.

[long-pause]

At the end of May, Joe comes to say goodbye. We are moving out, he says. I can't say where. He gives Tom his badge. Keep it for me, he says. Tom holds it very tightly. Will you come back, he asks. Joe smiles. Sure I will, he says. And then the lorry drives away down the lane.

[pause]

That night, Tom puts the badge in his tin box with all his other treasures. And he says an extra prayer. For his father on the ship. And for Joe.

[pause]

Many years later, when Tom is an old man, he still has that badge. He keeps it on a shelf in his living room. And he tells his grandchildren about the tall American who sat on the gate and shared his chocolate, in the spring before D-Day.

[long-pause]

{img:sealed-marshalling-camp-barbed-wire-tents-woods-guards-1944}
At the end of May, the soldiers who will cross the sea first are moved into special camps near the coast. And the gates are closed behind them.

[pause]

The camps are sealed. Nobody may go in or out without special permission. There are wire fences all around and guards at the gates. The soldiers may not send letters or make phone calls or talk to anyone outside. Because now, they are going to be told the secret.

[long-pause]

The soldiers call these camps the sausage machines. The men go in at one end and come out at the other, onto the ships.

[pause]

Inside, the soldiers live in tents in the woods and the fields. And for the first time, they are told where they are going.

[pause]

{img:briefing-tent-soldiers-sand-table-model-coast-officer-pointing}
In big tents, the officers gather their men around tables. There are maps and photos taken from planes. There are models made of plaster and sand. They show the coast where they will land. The beaches and the dunes and the cliffs. The villages and the roads and the churches. Every house, every hedge, every path.

[long-pause]

But the names on the maps are false. Code names. So even now, if anyone talked, the enemy would not know the real place. The beaches have names like Utah and Omaha and Gold and Juno and Sword.

[pause]

The men study the maps and the models again and again until they know them by heart. Some of them recognise the coast from holidays before the war. And they realise. It is Normandy.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-waiting-camp-playing-cards-reading-writing-letters-sunshine}
They clean their rifles again and again. They check their equipment. They are given French money and little books of French words. They get pills for seasickness, and paper bags in case the pills do not work. They play cards for the French money. They read and write letters that will not be posted until later. They sleep and talk and play football. They eat better food than they have had in years. Steak and white bread and ice cream.

[long-pause]

On Sunday, the chaplains hold services in the open air. Many men go who have not been to church for years. They kneel on the grass in the sunshine and sing the old hymns and pray quietly.

[pause]

{img:chaplain-field-service-soldiers-kneeling-woods-morning-quiet}
And in the evenings, as the sun sets behind the trees, they sit outside their tents. They look at the sky. And they talk quietly about home and their families. And about what they will do after the war.

[long-pause]

{img:carrier-pigeons-basket-soldier-releasing-bird-1944-camp}
Not only people take part in D-Day. There are animals too.

[pause]

Among the most important are the pigeons. In 1944, there are no mobile phones. Radios are heavy and can break. And the enemy can listen to radio messages. So the armies and the newspapers use carrier pigeons. They carry messages home, across the sea, from France to England.

[long-pause]

Pigeons have a wonderful gift. Wherever they are taken, they can always find their way home to their own loft. Even across hundreds of kilometres of land and sea. Nobody knows exactly how they do it. Perhaps by the sun. Perhaps by feeling the earth's magnetism. Perhaps by smells and landmarks.

[pause]

So the pigeons are carried across the sea in baskets, with the soldiers and the reporters. When there is a message, it is written on a tiny piece of paper. It is rolled into a little tube on the pigeon's leg. The pigeon is let go. It flies up into the sky, circles once, and heads straight for home.

[pause]

{img:pigeon-flying-over-sea-channel-grey-clouds-wind-alone}
One of the most famous of these pigeons was called Gustav. On the morning of D-Day, a reporter on a ship off the French coast wrote a short message about the landings. He tied it to Gustav's leg and let him go. Gustav flew back across the sea, against a strong wind. Would he make it? He flew for more than five hours. Nearly three hundred kilometres. And he arrived at his loft near Portsmouth. With some of the very first news of the landings.

[long-pause]

For this, Gustav was given a medal for bravery. It is a special medal for animals. And he lived happily in his loft for years afterwards.

[pause]

There were dogs too. Some soldiers took dogs that were trained to jump by parachute with them. The dogs could hear and smell danger before people could. On the night before D-Day, a few of these brave dogs jumped into Normandy with their soldiers, in the dark.

[pause]

{img:paratrooper-with-alsatian-dog-airfield-1944-gentle-companionship}
And there were the cows of Normandy. Standing in their fields, amazed, as gliders landed around them in the night. Parachutes drifted down into their meadows. The next morning, the farmers came out to find their fields full of parachutes and gliders and strangers.

[long-pause]

Later, the farmers' wives collected the parachutes of white and green silk left in the fields. There was no other cloth to be had. So they made them into dresses and blouses and shirts. And even wedding dresses. Some of these parachute wedding dresses are still kept today, in the museums of Normandy. White and soft and beautiful.

[long-pause]

{img:inflatable-rubber-tank-dummy-field-soldiers-carrying-it-kent-1944}
The greatest secret of the war is this. Where, and when, will the Allies land?

[pause]

The enemy knows the landing is coming. They have built great defences all along the coast of France. Concrete and steel and guns. Obstacles on the beaches. But the coast is very long. They cannot be strong everywhere. If they knew where the landing would be, they could gather their armies there and wait.

[long-pause]

So the Allies have made a huge plan of tricks. They want the enemy to believe the landing will come somewhere else. At Calais, further north, where the sea is narrowest. Or even in Norway.

[pause]

In the east of England, across the sea from Calais, they create a whole army that does not exist. There are camps with empty tents. There are tanks and lorries made of rubber, blown up like balloons. Four men can carry one and set it up in a field. From the air, it looks like a real tank.

[pause]

{img:dummy-landing-craft-canvas-wood-estuary-moored-rows-dawn}
There are fake landing boats made of wood and canvas, floating in rows on the rivers. There are radio operators sending thousands of messages every day, from one empty camp to another. As if a great army were there, talking to itself. And a famous American general is put in charge of this ghost army, so the enemy will think it must be real.

[long-pause]

And it works. The enemy believes it. They keep a large army waiting near Calais for weeks. For a landing that never comes.

[pause]

But there are also funny moments, when the secret nearly seems to slip out.

[pause]

{img:schoolmaster-crossword-puzzle-newspaper-armchair-pipe-1944}
In the weeks before D-Day, the crossword puzzle in a big English newspaper had some very strange answers. One day, the answer was Utah. Another day, Omaha. Then Overlord, the secret code name for the whole landing. Then Mulberry. Then Neptune. All secret code names.

[long-pause]

The security officers were alarmed. They went to see the man who made the crosswords. He was a quiet schoolmaster. They questioned him for hours. But he had no idea what they were talking about.

[pause]

Many years later, one of his old pupils explained. The boys at the school spent a lot of time with the soldiers camped nearby. They heard these strange words. And the schoolmaster sometimes asked the boys to fill in empty crossword grids with words. Then he wrote clues for them. So the great secrets went innocently from the soldiers, to the schoolboys, into the newspaper. And luckily, nobody on the other side noticed.

[pause]

{img:quiet-country-road-signposts-removed-1944-hedges-afternoon}
All over Britain, the signposts have been taken down from the roads. If an enemy ever landed, he would not know where he was. And posters everywhere remind people not to talk about what they see. Careless talk costs lives, they say. And people do not talk.

[long-pause]

{img:portsmouth-harbour-1944-crowded-warships-landing-craft-barrage-balloons}
By the beginning of June, every harbour and river and creek along the south coast of England is full of ships.

[pause]

In Portsmouth and Southampton and Plymouth, and in every little harbour between them, the ships lie at anchor. They are packed so close together that people say you could walk across the harbour from ship to ship without getting your feet wet.

[long-pause]

There are great grey battleships and cruisers with their guns. There are troop ships and supply ships. There are hospital ships, painted white with red crosses. And there are landing craft, thousands of them, of every size. Some are big flat ships with great doors in the front. The doors open onto a beach so tanks can drive out. Others are little boats for about thirty soldiers, with a ramp at the front that drops down into the water.

[pause]

Altogether there are nearly seven thousand ships and boats. It is the largest fleet ever gathered in the history of the world.

[pause]

{img:landing-craft-loading-tanks-hard-ramp-concrete-slipway-soldiers}
Along the shore, special concrete ramps slope down into the water. The landing craft come in and open their doors. Tanks and lorries drive on board. Soldiers march on with their heavy packs.

[long-pause]

And there are stranger things in the harbours too. Huge blocks of concrete, as big as houses, floating in the water. They are waiting to be towed across the sea. They are parts of two whole artificial harbours. They will be put together off the coast of Normandy. So ships can unload there, even where there is no port.

[pause]

Nobody has ever done anything like it before. Tens of thousands of workers have built them in secret for months. Most of them had no idea what they were for.

[pause]

{img:mulberry-concrete-caissons-floating-estuary-tugs-evening-grey}
In the towns, people see the ships and the soldiers and the tanks rumbling toward the docks. And they know. They stand on the seafront and the cliffs. They look at the great fleet filling the harbours and the sea, as far as they can see. And they wonder.

[long-pause]

At night, the ships lie dark and silent in the harbours. No lights show. Guards walk the decks. Soldiers sleep in hammocks and on the decks. And the sea washes softly against the ships, under the summer stars.

[long-pause]

{img:king-george-vi-churchill-meeting-palace-study-1944-discussion}
In the days before D-Day, the Prime Minister, Winston Churchill, has an idea. He wants to go with the fleet. On one of the warships. To watch the landings from the sea.

[pause]

He is sixty-nine years old. He has led the country through four long years of war. And he cannot bear the thought of staying at home in London while the young men he has sent go into danger.

[long-pause]

His advisers are horrified. If the ship were attacked and the Prime Minister were lost, it would be a disaster for the whole country. They try to persuade him not to go. But he will not listen. I am the Prime Minister, he says. I will go if I wish.

[pause]

Then the King hears about it. And King George says, if the Prime Minister is going, then I will go too. I am the head of the navy. My place is with my sailors.

[pause]

{img:king-writing-letter-desk-buckingham-palace-pen-lamp-1944}
Of course, both of them cannot go. The King knows it. So he writes a letter to Churchill. A kind and wise and gentle letter. He says he would very much like to go. But he knows he should stay at home. And that the Prime Minister should stay at home too. Because the country needs them both.

[long-pause]

Churchill has great respect and affection for the King. He reads the letter. And at last, he agrees. Though he grumbles about it for days.

[pause]

So on the night before D-Day, the King is at Buckingham Palace. Churchill is in London, in his rooms near Downing Street. Neither of them can sleep. That night, Churchill tells his wife Clementine how afraid he is. So many young men could be lost by the time she wakes up. And she holds his hand.

[pause]

{img:churchill-sleepless-night-map-room-cigar-lamp-looking-at-map}
In the end, the losses on D-Day, though sad for every family who suffered them, were far fewer than he had feared. The next morning, he went to Parliament to tell the country that things were going according to plan.

[long-pause]

A few days later, Churchill was allowed at last to cross the sea and visit Normandy. He walked on the sand among the soldiers and the lorries and the ships. In his long coat and hat, with his cigar and his stick. Beaming and making his V sign. And the soldiers cheered him. A few days after that, the King went too, and visited his soldiers in Normandy.

[long-pause]

{img:weather-forecasters-charts-isobars-table-night-lamps-1944-room}
Everything depends on the weather.

[pause]

To cross the sea and land on the beaches, the Allies need the right conditions, all at the same time. They need a calm sea, so the small boats can cross without sinking. They need low water at dawn, so the obstacles on the beaches can be seen. They need a moon at night, so the pilots can see where they are going. And they need clear skies, with not too much cloud and not too much wind.

[long-pause]

There are only a few days each month when the tides and the moon are right. In early June, they are the fifth, sixth and seventh. After that, the next chance is weeks away. The date chosen for D-Day is the fifth of June.

[pause]

And the man who must tell the generals what the weather will be is a tall serious Scotsman. A weather scientist called James Stagg.

[pause]

{img:meteorologist-james-stagg-tall-scotsman-uniform-studying-weather-map}
He leads a small team of weather forecasters, British and American. They study reports from weather stations and ships and planes all over the Atlantic Ocean. They draw weather maps with curving lines. The lines show where the storms are and where they are going.

[long-pause]

Weather forecasting in 1944 is very hard. There are no satellites and no computers. The forecasters work it out with pencils and maps and experience. And the weather over the Atlantic and the Channel changes very quickly.

[pause]

At the beginning of June, Stagg is worried. The weather was fine and sunny all through May. Now it is changing. Storms are coming in from the Atlantic, one after another. On the fourth of June, he warns the generals. The weather on the fifth will be bad. Strong winds, low clouds, rough seas.

[pause]

{img:stormy-english-channel-june-1944-grey-waves-rain-ships-at-anchor}
He is right. On the fourth of June, the wind rises. The rain comes. The sea in the Channel turns grey and rough, with white waves.

[long-pause]

The landing is put off by one day. Some ships have already set out. They must turn back to the harbours and wait. The soldiers crowded on the ships are already seasick. Now they must wait another day.

[pause]

Then, on the evening of the fourth of June, Stagg and his team see something on their maps. Between two storms, there may be a gap. A short break in the weather. A few hours of calmer weather on the sixth of June.

[pause]

{img:weather-station-report-arriving-teleprinter-paper-strip-night-office}
Is it enough? Nobody can be sure. If they wait for the next chance in two weeks, the secret might be discovered. The soldiers already know everything. They are waiting on the ships. And the weather then might be even worse.

[long-pause]

It is the hardest forecast of James Stagg's life. He does not sleep that night. He stares at his maps. He reads every new report. He checks and checks again. Everything now depends on him.

[long-pause]

{img:blacksod-point-lighthouse-mayo-ireland-atlantic-coast-wild-grass-1944}
Far away, on the wild west coast of Ireland, there is a little lighthouse and a post office. They stand at the very end of a long narrow piece of land. The place is called Blacksod Point.

[pause]

It looks out over the Atlantic Ocean toward America. There is nothing between Blacksod and America but the sea.

[long-pause]

Ireland is not fighting in the war. But for years, the little weather station at Blacksod has been sending weather reports every hour to Dublin. From there, they are quietly passed on to the forecasters in Britain. Because Blacksod is one of the first places in Europe that the Atlantic weather reaches. Whatever happens at Blacksod will happen in England and France a day or so later.

[pause]

The lighthouse keeper, Ted Sweeney, and his young assistant, Maureen Flavin, take the readings. Every hour, day and night. The air pressure on the barometer. The temperature. The wind and the clouds and the rain.

[pause]

{img:young-irish-woman-reading-barometer-post-office-lighthouse-dawn}
On the third of June, 1944, it is Maureen's twenty-first birthday. Early that morning, at one o'clock, she goes to read the barometer. She sees the pressure is falling fast. A storm is coming.

[long-pause]

She sends the report by telephone, as usual. Soon the phone rings. A voice with an English accent asks her. Please check, and repeat. So she checks. The reading is the same. Later they ask again. And she checks again. Why do they keep asking? She does not know.

[pause]

She does not know that her readings are being carried across the sea to a house in the south of England. Where the generals and the forecasters are deciding the fate of the landing in Europe.

[pause]

{img:atlantic-waves-rocky-shore-mayo-storm-clouds-lighthouse-beam}
Her reports helped to warn of the storm on the fifth of June. That is why the landing was put off. And her later reports helped them see the break in the weather coming behind it.

[long-pause]

Maureen married Ted Sweeney, the lighthouse keeper. She lived at Blacksod for the rest of her long life and raised her family there. For many years, she did not know how important her birthday reading had been. Only much later did she learn the truth. When she was very old, she was honoured by the American government for her part in D-Day. She passed away at the age of a hundred, in her home on the edge of the Atlantic. Where she had watched the weather and the waves all her life.

[long-pause]

{img:southwick-house-hampshire-georgian-mansion-1944-night-rain-army-cars}
In the hills just north of Portsmouth, there is a large white country house. It has tall windows and columns at the door. Woods and parkland surround it. It is called Southwick House.

[pause]

In the spring of 1944, it has become the headquarters of the landing. Here, in the library and the drawing room, among the tall windows and the bookshelves, the generals and admirals meet. To plan and to decide.

[long-pause]

The top commander is the American general Dwight Eisenhower. People call him Ike. He is a tall friendly smiling man from Kansas. He is very good at getting people from many countries to work together. With him are the British general Montgomery and the admiral Ramsay and the air force chiefs.

[pause]

In one room there is a great map of the Channel and the coast of Normandy. It covers a whole wall. A toy company made it out of wood. Officers move little markers on it to show where every ship is. As the reports come in, the markers move. That map is still there today. On the wall. Exactly as it was on the morning of D-Day.

[pause]

{img:southwick-house-wall-map-channel-normandy-ships-markers-officers}
On the evening of the fourth of June, at half past nine, the commanders meet in the library. Rain beats against the tall windows. The wind howls in the trees outside. And James Stagg, the weatherman, comes in. He tells them about the break in the weather he expects on the sixth.

[long-pause]

The generals ask him many questions. He answers carefully. He cannot be certain. Then everyone looks at Eisenhower. It is his decision, and his alone.

[pause]

He sits silently for a long time, thinking. The rain beats on the glass. Nobody speaks. Then he says quietly. I am quite sure we must give the order. I don't like it. But there it is.

[pause]

{img:library-southwick-house-generals-armchairs-night-lamplight-rain-windows}
The orders go out. But there is still time to change them. So at four o'clock in the morning on the fifth of June, the commanders meet again in the library to hear the latest forecast. The rain is still beating on the windows. But Stagg says the break is still coming. Eisenhower thinks for a moment more. And then he says, OK. Let's go.

[long-pause]

The room empties quickly. The commanders hurry out into the dark and the rain, to their cars, to give their orders. And Eisenhower is left alone. The hardest decision of the war has been made. Now there is nothing more he can do but wait.

[pause]

Outside, as dawn comes on the fifth of June, the rain begins to ease a little. And the wind begins to drop.

[long-pause]

{img:soldiers-crowded-landing-ship-deck-waiting-harbour-grey-sky-june-5}
The fifth of June, 1944, is a long day of waiting.

[pause]

Some soldiers have been on board their ships for days. They are crowded below decks in rows of bunks and hammocks. Others are on the open decks under canvas, in the wind and the rain. Many are seasick, even in the harbour, as the ships roll gently at anchor.

[long-pause]

First they were told the landing was put off by a day. Now they are told it will go ahead. Tonight they will sail.

[pause]

So they wait through the long grey windy day. They play cards on the decks. They read and doze and write letters. They check their equipment again. They talk and smoke. And they look out at the sea and the other ships all around them, as far as they can see. Silver balloons sway in the wind above them.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-playing-cards-ship-hold-hammocks-kit-bags-lamplight}
Some of them sing. Some of them pray. Some of them sleep, which is the best thing to do. Some sit quietly and look at photographs of their wives and children and girlfriends and parents. Then they put them back carefully in their pockets, next to their hearts.

[long-pause]

On some ships, the officers read out a message from General Eisenhower. It has been printed and given to every soldier and sailor and airman. It begins like this. You are about to begin the great task we have prepared for these many months. The eyes of the world are upon you. The hopes and prayers of freedom-loving people everywhere go with you.

[pause]

{img:soldier-reading-printed-message-eisenhower-paper-deck-wind}
And it ends. Good luck! Let us all ask the blessing of Almighty God on this great and noble work.

[long-pause]

Many soldiers keep the little printed message folded in their pockets. Later they send it home. Their families keep it all their lives.

[pause]

On the British ships, a message from General Montgomery is read out too. The chaplains hold short services on the decks. The soldiers bow their heads in the wind.

[pause]

{img:ship-chaplain-service-deck-soldiers-heads-bowed-wind-grey-sea}
As afternoon turns into evening, the ships begin to move. The anchors come up. The engines start. Slowly, one after another, in long lines, the ships sail out of the harbours and the bays. They gather in the sea south of the Isle of Wight. The sailors call this meeting place Piccadilly Circus, after a busy square in London. And then they turn south. Toward France.

[long-pause]

On the cliffs and the beaches of the south coast, people stand and watch them go in the evening light. More ships than anyone has ever seen, filling the sea to the horizon. Nobody says very much. They just watch. Some of them wave. Some of them pray.

[long-pause]

{img:young-soldier-writing-letter-knee-pencil-tent-evening-light-1944}
In the last days and hours before D-Day, many soldiers write letters.

[pause]

They write to their mothers and fathers. To their wives and children and girlfriends. To their brothers and sisters. They cannot say where they are or what is about to happen. The letters will be checked and posted later. Perhaps after the landing.

[long-pause]

So they write about other things. The weather and the food. Their friends. The English countryside and the pretty villages. The cows and the hedges and the birds singing in the woods. They ask about home. About the garden and the dog and the neighbours.

[pause]

And they write, as simply as they can, how much they love their families. How much they miss them. How they will come home soon, when it is all over. And how nobody should worry.

[pause]

{img:mother-reading-letter-from-son-kitchen-window-light-1944}
Some write a second letter too. They give it to a friend to post, only if they do not come back. In these letters, they say the things that are hard to say. Thank you. I love you. Do not be too sad. Look after each other. Live a good life.

[long-pause]

Many of these letters have survived. Families have kept them for eighty years. In boxes and drawers and family Bibles. Now many are in museums and books. So we can read them. And hear the voices of the young men writing by lamplight on the night before D-Day.

[pause]

They are often very ordinary letters. A young American tells his mother the English food is terrible. Please send cookies, he writes. A young British soldier tells his wife to plant the beans and to kiss the baby for him. A young Canadian tells his girl he has seen the prettiest village in the world. One day, he writes, I will bring you here to see it.

[pause]

{img:bundle-old-letters-ribbon-photographs-medals-family-box-today}
And they end the way letters do. All my love. God bless. See you soon. Do not worry about me.

[long-pause]

At home, in America and Canada and Britain and many other countries, the families read the letters. And read them again. They keep them by their beds. They wait for the next one. They listen to the news on the radio every evening. And they wonder where their sons and husbands and brothers are tonight.

[long-pause]

{img:wrens-plotting-table-operations-room-southwick-markers-1944}
D-Day could not have happened without the women.

[pause]

In the map rooms at Southwick House and at the headquarters of the navy and the air force, young women stand around great map tables, day and night. They wear headphones. As reports come in, they move little markers of the ships and the planes with long poles. So the commanders can see at a glance where everything is.

[long-pause]

They work in shifts through the night, quietly and calmly. They know the greatest secret of the war. The time and the place of the landing. And they keep it. Not a word slips out.

[pause]

Other women work as radio operators and code breakers. As clerks and drivers. Some ride motorbikes along the country lanes day and night, carrying messages. Others pack parachutes for the paratroopers. Fold by careful fold. Knowing that a man's life will depend on each one.

[pause]

{img:women-packing-parachutes-long-tables-hangar-silk-careful-1944}
At the airfields, women check the weather and look after the radios and the radar. And some women fly. They deliver new planes from the factories to the airfields. Flying alone, all over Britain.

[long-pause]

And there are the nurses. On the white hospital ships with their big red crosses, and in hospitals along the coast, nurses wait. Ready to care for the wounded who will come back across the sea. A few days after D-Day, the first nurses cross to Normandy themselves. They set up hospitals in tents in the fields, among the apple orchards.

[pause]

In factories all over Britain, women have built planes and ships and landing boats. And parts of the floating harbours. Working long shifts in overalls and headscarves.

[pause]

{img:nurses-hospital-ship-white-red-cross-deck-ready-1944-calm-sea}
On the farms, women have grown the food that fed the soldiers and the country. And at home, mothers and wives and grandmothers have kept everything going. Through the rationing and the darkness and the worry and the long waiting.

[long-pause]

On the night before D-Day, many of these women work through the night. In the map rooms. At the radios. In the hospitals. On the ships. And when the news comes in the morning, they smile at each other. Tired, and quietly proud. And they go on working.

[long-pause]

{img:eisenhower-talking-paratroopers-blackened-faces-airfield-evening-1944}
On the evening of the fifth of June, General Eisenhower drives to an airfield in the green countryside west of London. He wants to see the American paratroopers before they take off.

[pause]

They will be among the first to go. They will fly across the sea in the dark. They will jump by parachute behind the coast of Normandy, in the night. Hours before the landings on the beaches.

[long-pause]

The paratroopers have blackened their faces with soot and paint, so they will not be seen in the dark. Some have shaved their heads, leaving a single strip of hair down the middle. They carry so much equipment that some can hardly walk. Parachutes and packs and supplies. They must be helped up into the planes.

[pause]

Eisenhower walks among them without any fuss, in the evening light, with his hands in his pockets. He talks to them simply, as one man to another. Where are you from, son? What did you do before the war? Are you ready?

[pause]

{img:general-walking-among-young-paratroopers-c47-aircraft-rows-dusk}
They answer cheerfully. They come from Texas and Pennsylvania and Michigan and Kansas, like him. They were farmers and clerks and factory workers and students. One tells him not to worry. We will take care of everything, sir. They laugh. And he laughs with them.

[long-pause]

He stays until the planes start their engines. One after another they roll out onto the runway and take off into the evening sky. Heavy and slow. Black and white stripes are painted on their wings so the Allied gunners will know them. They climb and circle and form up in great groups. And they turn south, toward the sea, as the light fades.

[pause]

Eisenhower stands on the airfield in the dusk and watches them go. He salutes. And his driver, standing beside him, sees that there are tears in his eyes.

[pause]

{img:aircraft-taking-off-twilight-airfield-silhouette-figure-watching}
In his pocket, he carries a little note. He wrote it in pencil earlier that day, just in case. It says the landings have failed. And that the decision was his. And that if anyone is to blame, it is him alone.

[long-pause]

He never had to use it. He put it in his wallet and forgot about it. Weeks later he found it and showed it to a helper, who kept it. It is now in a museum in Kansas, where Eisenhower grew up. A little scrap of paper with a few pencilled lines. It shows a commander who was ready to take all the blame if things went wrong. And to give all the credit to his men if things went right.

[pause]

That night, Eisenhower goes back to his caravan in the woods near Southwick House. He tries to sleep and cannot. So he reads cowboy stories to pass the time. And he waits for the news.

[long-pause]

{img:french-resistance-family-hidden-radio-attic-listening-candle-1944}
In occupied France, on the evening of the fifth of June, people are listening to the radio in secret. In hundreds of attics and cellars and barns and farmhouse kitchens.

[pause]

It is forbidden to listen to the BBC from London. People who are caught can be arrested. So they listen with the sound turned very low. The curtains are drawn. Someone watches at the window. They press their ears close to the radio. Through all the crackling and whistling.

[long-pause]

Every evening, after the French news from London, there is a strange part of the programme. It is called the personal messages. A calm voice reads out a long list of sentences that seem to make no sense at all. The carrots are cooked. John has a long moustache. The giraffe has a long neck. The dice are on the carpet.

[pause]

They are secret messages for the French Resistance. These are the men and women all over France who have secretly fought against the occupation for years. Each message is meant for one group in one place. It tells them something. That weapons will be dropped tonight by parachute. Or that they must get ready. Or that the time has come.

[pause]

{img:bbc-studio-1944-announcer-microphone-reading-messages-french-service}
For weeks, the Resistance groups have been waiting for one message in particular.

[long-pause]

On the first of June, the radio read out the first line of a famous French poem. It is about autumn. The long sobs of the violins of autumn.

[pause]

It meant, get ready. The landing will come within days.

[pause]

And then on the evening of the fifth of June, at a quarter past nine, the voice reads the second line.

[pause]

{img:old-radio-glowing-dial-dark-kitchen-french-farmhouse-night-listeners}
They wound my heart with a slow and gentle sadness.

[long-pause]

It means, the landing will begin within two days. Begin your work.

[pause]

All over France that night, the men and women of the Resistance go out quietly into the darkness. They do what they have prepared for, for so long. They cut telephone wires and railway lines. They block the roads. So the enemy will be slowed down when he tries to move his soldiers to the coast. They go on bicycles and on foot, through the fields and the woods in the dark. Carefully and bravely.

[pause]

{img:resistance-members-bicycles-country-road-night-moonlight-france}
In the farmhouse kitchens and the attics, the families who heard the message look at each other in the lamplight. Some smile. Some cry. Some say a prayer. They do not know exactly what it means. But they know something is coming. After four long years, something is coming.

[long-pause]

The poem is a sad autumn poem. About violins and falling leaves and time passing. But for the people of France ever since, those two lines have meant something else. Hope. And the end of a long night.

[long-pause]

{img:normandy-farmhouse-orchard-apple-trees-hedgerows-cows-evening-1944}
Let us go across the sea to Normandy, on the evening of the fifth of June, 1944.

[pause]

It is a green and gentle and beautiful land. It has small fields surrounded by thick high hedges on banks of earth. Narrow sunken lanes run between them. There are apple orchards and grey stone farmhouses. Little villages with stone churches. Market towns and the old city of Bayeux with its spires. And along the coast, long sandy beaches and cliffs.

[long-pause]

Let us visit a farming family in a stone farmhouse, a few kilometres from the sea.

[pause]

They have farmed this land for generations. There is a father and a mother and three children. And the grandmother, who remembers the last war. They keep brown and white Normandy cows, with patches around their eyes like spectacles. They make butter and cream and soft round cheeses. They grow apples for cider. And they keep chickens and pigs and a horse.

[pause]

{img:normandy-farm-kitchen-1944-family-supper-lamp-soup-bread-cider}
For four years, they have lived under the occupation. There have been foreign soldiers in the village. Rules and curfews and shortages and fear. Their eldest son is far away, a prisoner of war. They have not seen him for four years. Every night, the mother says his name in her prayers.

[long-pause]

Lately, there have been more and more planes flying overhead. The family has been told to stay away from the beaches. They are full of mines and obstacles. Everyone knows a landing will come one day. But everyone thinks it will come far to the north, at Calais.

[pause]

Tonight the weather is bad. The wind blows from the sea. Rain falls in showers. The sea is rough. Nobody, the father says, will cross the sea on a night like this.

[pause]

{img:normandy-family-going-to-bed-candle-stairs-old-farmhouse-wind}
They eat supper in the kitchen by the light of an oil lamp. Soup and bread and butter and cheese and a little cider. The grandmother says her prayers by the fire. The children go up to bed. The father goes out to check the cows in the barn. He looks at the sky. He listens to the wind in the orchard. Then he locks the door. They all go to bed and blow out the candles. And they lie listening to the wind and the rain on the roof, until they fall asleep.

[long-pause]

They do not know that out at sea, beyond the horizon, the greatest fleet in history is coming toward them through the rain and the wind. They do not know that in a few hours, their lives and their village and their country will change forever.

[pause]

And in the middle of the night, they will be woken by a sound they have never heard before. The roar of hundreds and hundreds of planes, flying low over the farmhouse and the orchard, in the dark.

[long-pause]

{img:night-channel-crossing-fleet-silhouettes-moonlight-clouds-1944}
On the night of the fifth to the sixth of June, the great fleet crosses the sea.

[pause]

From the meeting place south of the Isle of Wight, the ships sail south toward France. They go in long lines along ten paths through the sea. The paths are marked with little lit buoys, like lamps along a road in the dark.

[long-pause]

In front go the minesweepers. They are small ships that go first, slowly and carefully, in the dark. They clear the paths through the hidden mines in the water. So the great ships behind them can pass safely. It is dangerous and patient work. The crews do it perfectly all night long. And the ships follow behind them.

[pause]

Then come the warships and the troop ships and the landing ships. The tugs pull the first parts of the floating harbours. Nearly seven thousand of them, stretching across the sea for many kilometres. Without lights. In silence.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-on-deck-night-crossing-wind-spray-life-belts-quiet-faces}
On the ships, the soldiers try to sleep. Some do, wrapped in their blankets. Many are seasick. The sea is still rough after the storm. The small flat landing boats roll and toss in the waves. Some soldiers sit on the decks in the dark. They look out at the shapes of the other ships. At the sea and the clouds. And at the moon, coming and going between them.

[long-pause]

Some talk quietly. Some pray. Some think about home. On one ship, someone plays a mouth organ softly in the dark. On another, the men sing old songs and hymns together, very quietly.

[pause]

On the bridges of the ships, the captains and navigators watch the compass and the charts and the sea. They steer carefully along the safe paths, toward the coast of France. One wrong turn could lead into the mines. So they do not take their eyes off the water.

[pause]

{img:ship-bridge-night-navigator-compass-red-light-chart-table-1944}
The night is not completely dark. There is a moon, almost full. Now and then it shines through the breaks in the clouds. It makes a silver path on the water. For a moment it lights up the shapes of the ships all around. Then it disappears again.

[long-pause]

The sea is full of sounds. The throb of the engines. The wind and the waves slapping against the ships. The creaking of the hulls. And overhead, more and more, the hum of planes flying south in the dark, toward France.

[pause]

And the fleet sails on through the night. Slowly, steadily and silently. Toward the dawn.

[long-pause]

{img:english-family-garden-night-looking-up-sky-full-aircraft-moonlight-1944}
In the villages and towns of the south of England, on the night of the fifth of June, people are woken by a sound they will never forget.

[pause]

It begins at around eleven o'clock in the evening. A low hum, far away. It grows and grows into a great deep steady roar. It fills the whole sky. And it goes on for hours.

[long-pause]

It is the sound of planes. Hundreds, and then thousands of them, flying south over the houses and the fields and the coast. Toward France. Transport planes carrying paratroopers. Planes towing gliders behind them on long ropes. Bombers and fighters flying high above. More planes than anyone has ever seen or heard in one night.

[pause]

People get out of bed. They go to their windows and open them and look up. Then they go out into their gardens in their dressing gowns and slippers. They stand on the lawns and in the lanes and on the village greens. And they look up at the sky.

[pause]

{img:night-sky-formations-aircraft-navigation-lights-over-english-village}
In the moonlight, between the clouds, they can see them. Great groups of planes, wave after wave. Their little lights glow red and green and white, in rows across the sky. Like moving stars. Their dark shapes cross the moon. And all of them fly the same way. South.

[long-pause]

The neighbours come out too. They stand together at their garden gates and talk in low voices. This is it, they say. It has started. And nobody goes back to bed for a long time.

[pause]

Some people go to the churches, which are opened in the night. They kneel and pray for the young men flying over in the dark. In the morning, the churches will be full.

[pause]

{img:village-church-open-night-candles-people-kneeling-praying-1944}
All the Allied planes have been painted with broad black and white stripes in the last few days. That way soldiers and sailors below will know them and not mistake them for the enemy. The painters on the airfields worked day and night to finish in time. There was not enough time to do it neatly. So they used brooms and mops and buckets of paint.

[long-pause]

The children are woken too. They are brought out into the gardens, wrapped in blankets. Their fathers and mothers hold them up to see the planes. Remember this, they say. And the children did. Many of them, when they were very old, still remembered the night of the aeroplanes. The sound. The moonlight. Their parents' faces looking up. And the feeling that something enormous was happening.

[pause]

{img:child-in-blanket-held-by-father-garden-gate-looking-up-night-sky}
The roar goes on all night, over England and the sea and the coast of France. At last, toward dawn, it begins to fade. People go back inside. They put the kettle on. They switch on the radio. And they wait for the news.

[long-pause]

{img:gliders-landing-night-field-beside-canal-bridge-normandy-moonlight}
Shortly after midnight on the sixth of June, the first soldiers arrive in France. Silently. Out of the night sky.

[pause]

They come in gliders. Big wooden planes without engines. They are towed across the sea behind bombers. Then they are let go high above the coast. And they glide down in silence through the dark, to land in the fields.

[long-pause]

Six of them are heading for two bridges near a little village a few kilometres from the sea. One bridge crosses a canal. The other crosses a river beside it. The soldiers must take the bridges quickly and quietly, before they can be destroyed. So that the soldiers landing on the beaches in the morning can cross them.

[pause]

The gliders come down in the dark. The pilots cannot see much. Will they find the right field? They land at sixteen minutes past midnight. In a little field right beside the canal bridge. So close and so exact that the pilots were later praised for some of the finest flying of the whole war. Within minutes, the bridge is in the hands of the British soldiers. We will leave the short struggle there, in the dark and the distance.

[pause]

{img:canal-bridge-normandy-cafe-house-beside-dawn-quiet-1944}
Beside the bridge there is a little café. It belongs to a French family, the Gondrées. A husband and wife and their two little daughters. They have been hiding in their cellar, frightened, listening to the noise outside.

[long-pause]

In the early hours of the morning, there is a knock at the door. They hear voices speaking English. The father opens the door. The soldiers come in. And he realises. They are British. They have come at last.

[pause]

The Gondrées' home is one of the very first houses in France to be freed. The father goes out into his garden. He digs up bottles of champagne that he hid there four years ago, waiting for this day. He gives them to the soldiers. His wife and daughters come up from the cellar. They hug and kiss the soldiers. Their faces end up covered with the black paint from the soldiers' faces.

[pause]

{img:cafe-gondree-today-pegasus-bridge-flags-visitors-flowers}
The bridge was later named Pegasus Bridge, after the flying horse that was the badge of the British airborne soldiers.

[long-pause]

The café is still there today, beside the canal, with its flags and photographs and memories. For many years it was run by the youngest daughter. She was a little girl of four, hiding in the cellar that night. Every year she welcomed the old soldiers and their families. And the original bridge stands nearby, in a museum garden. Visitors can walk across it and remember.

[long-pause]

{img:dawn-june-6-1944-grey-sea-fleet-horizon-normandy-coast-clouds}
On the morning of the sixth of June, the dawn comes grey and pale over the sea. And the people of the Normandy coast look out of their windows and see something they cannot believe.

[pause]

The sea is full of ships.

[long-pause]

From one end of the horizon to the other, as far as they can see, there are ships. Great grey warships and transports and landing boats of every kind. Thousands of them, covering the sea. Like a great city that has appeared in the night, out of nowhere.

[pause]

And then the landings begin on the five beaches along the coast, as the tide comes in.

[pause]

{img:landing-craft-heading-to-shore-dawn-distant-beach-smoke-grey}
We will not follow the soldiers onto the beaches. It was a long and very hard day for many of them. And many did not come home. We will leave them there, with great respect and gratitude, in the grey morning by the sea. And think of them quietly for a moment.

[long-pause]

By the evening of the sixth of June, more than a hundred and fifty thousand soldiers have landed in France, by sea and by air. The beaches have been taken. The first villages and farms near the coast are free. And the long road to freeing Western Europe has begun.

[pause]

The farming family in Normandy has spent the whole day in the cellar. The grandmother, the children, the parents. The noise has been tremendous all day. In the evening, it grows quieter. Then there is a knock at the door. The father goes up slowly. He opens it. And there in the farmyard stand two young soldiers. Tired and dirty and wet. With a strange accent. Asking politely for some water.

[pause]

{img:normandy-farmer-giving-water-soldiers-farmyard-evening-1944}
The father gives them water, and then cider. The mother brings bread and butter. The children come up from the cellar and stare at the soldiers. The soldiers give them chocolate. And the grandmother cries and kisses them on both cheeks.

[long-pause]

It is the first day of freedom for the family, after four years. There will be many hard weeks still, all around them, in the fields and hedges of Normandy, before it is truly over.

[pause]

That night, the farmer goes out again to see his cows in the barn. They are frightened and need milking. He stands in the orchard in the dusk under the apple trees. He looks at the sky. He listens to the guns far away. He thinks of his son, the prisoner. And he wonders, now, if perhaps his boy will come home.

[long-pause]

{img:bbc-announcer-1944-studio-microphone-morning-news-d-day}
In Britain, on the morning of the sixth of June, everyone is listening to the radio.

[pause]

All night they heard the planes. Since dawn, there have been rumours. But people are waiting for their own news. From the BBC.

[long-pause]

At nine thirty-two in the morning, the voice of the BBC announcer comes over the radio. Calm and clear. Into every home and factory and office and school in Britain. This is the BBC Home Service. Here is a special bulletin. D-Day has come. Early this morning, the Allies began landing on the north-western coast of Europe.

[pause]

{img:factory-workers-stopped-listening-loudspeaker-d-day-news-1944}
All over Britain, people stop what they are doing and listen. In the factories, the machines are switched off. The workers gather around the loudspeakers. In the offices, the clerks put down their pens. In the schools, the teachers stop the lessons. In the shops, the shopkeepers and the customers stand still and listen together.

[long-pause]

There is no great celebration. People are quiet and serious. They know this is not the end, but the beginning. And they know that many young men they love are there now, on the beaches and in the fields of France. So they go on with their work, quietly. They think of them. And they wait for more news.

[pause]

{img:london-street-people-reading-newspapers-headlines-invasion-crowd-1944}
In London, people buy every new newspaper as soon as it comes out. They stand in the streets reading. They talk quietly to strangers. In America it is still the middle of the night. People are woken by the news on the radio. Church bells ring in the towns. And people go to the churches in the dark, to pray.

[long-pause]

At midday, Mr Churchill tells Parliament that the landings have begun. So far, he says, everything is going according to plan. And in the evening at nine o'clock, the King speaks to the nation on the radio.

[pause]

He does not speak of victory. He asks the people to pray. He asks for one great prayer, rising up from the whole nation, for the young men who are fighting. And all over Britain that evening, in the homes and the churches, people bow their heads. And they pray.

[pause]

{img:family-evening-listening-king-radio-heads-bowed-living-room-1944}
In America too, the President speaks on the radio that evening. Instead of a speech, he reads a prayer he wrote himself, for the soldiers and the nation. Millions of Americans pray with him. Almighty God, he begins. Our sons, the pride of our nation, have this day set out on a mighty task.

[long-pause]

{img:mother-standing-garden-gate-1944-looking-down-lane-summer-evening}
In the days and weeks after D-Day, the families at home wait.

[pause]

They wait for news on the radio and in the newspapers every day. They follow the armies across Normandy on maps printed in the papers, with little arrows and lines. They wait for the postman every morning, hoping for a letter. And they try not to think about the other kind of news.

[long-pause]

Slowly, the letters come. Short letters written in pencil on scraps of paper. Written in fields and ditches and barns in France. I'm all right. Don't worry. It's very green here, like at home. The French people are very kind. They gave us cider and flowers. All my love.

[pause]

The families read them again and again. They pass them round to the neighbours. They keep them by their beds. And they go to sleep a little easier.

[pause]

{img:postman-delivering-letter-cottage-door-woman-relief-smiling-1944}
For some families, the news is sad. We will not stay there. But we will think of them gently. That summer, in many homes, in Britain and America and Canada and France and Germany too, there were empty chairs at the table. And mothers and wives and children who missed someone for the rest of their lives.

[long-pause]

And life at home goes on. Women go to work in the factories and on the farms and in the hospitals. Children go to school and play in the streets and the fields. The gardens are dug. The vegetables grow. The hay is cut in the meadows. The harvest is gathered, as always.

[pause]

Slowly, the south of England empties. The camps in the woods are quiet. The lanes that were full of tanks and lorries are empty again. Only the marks of the tracks in the grass show they were ever there. The villages and pubs and dance halls are quiet.

[pause]

{img:empty-army-camp-woods-after-june-1944-abandoned-tent-pegs-quiet-trees}
The villagers miss the soldiers. The cheerful Americans and Canadians and British who lived among them for months. Who came to their dances and their churches and played with their children. Some children cry because their soldier friends have gone.

[long-pause]

The young women who walked along the lanes with the soldiers in the long May evenings wait for letters too. Some will marry after the war. Some will never see them again. And some will remember them all their lives. A summer romance, in a summer of war, among the hedgerows and the blossom.

[long-pause]

{img:mulberry-harbour-arromanches-1944-floating-roadways-ships-unloading}
In the weeks after D-Day, the great floating harbours are towed across the sea and put together off the coast of Normandy.

[pause]

The huge concrete blocks, as big as houses, are sunk in a long line out at sea. Old ships are sunk beside them. Behind them, the sea becomes calm. Inside, there are floating piers and long floating roads. They rise and fall with the tide. They stretch from the beach out to the ships. So lorries and supplies can drive straight off the ships onto the land.

[long-pause]

At Arromanches, a little seaside town, the great harbour is built in only a few weeks. For months, millions of tons of supplies come through it. And hundreds of thousands of soldiers.

[pause]

In the middle of June, a great storm strikes the coast. It is the worst summer storm in forty years. It lasts three days. It wrecks one of the two harbours and damages the other. For three days, nobody knows if the harbour will survive. But the harbour at Arromanches holds. It is repaired. And it goes on working for months.

[pause]

{img:normandy-village-liberated-1944-families-flags-soldiers-flowers-street}
Slowly, through the summer, the armies move forward through the hedges and fields and orchards of Normandy. It is a long hard summer. Many towns and villages are damaged. The people of Normandy suffer greatly too. We remember them gently, and with sorrow.

[long-pause]

At the end of August, the armies break out from Normandy and move quickly across France. On the twenty-fifth of August, Paris is free. The bells of Notre-Dame ring out. The people of Paris pour into the streets. Then Belgium is freed, and part of the Netherlands. And after a long hard winter, the war in Europe ends, in May 1945. Eleven months after D-Day.

[pause]

{img:normandy-farm-family-rebuilding-1945-orchard-blossom-son-returned}
And the farming family in Normandy, with their orchard and their cows? Their son, the prisoner, comes home at last in the spring of 1945. One evening in May, he walks up the lane to the farmhouse. He is thin and tired after five years away. The apple trees are in blossom. His mother sees him from the kitchen window. She drops what she is holding and runs out to meet him. And the whole family cries and laughs and holds him in the farmyard, under the white blossom.

[long-pause]

{img:bayeux-1944-cathedral-spires-old-town-streets-flags-liberated-morning}
A few kilometres inland from the beaches lies the old town of Bayeux. On the seventh of June, the day after D-Day, it becomes the first town in France to be freed.

[pause]

And by great luck, Bayeux is hardly damaged at all. Its old stone houses still stand. Its narrow streets and its little river with old water mills. And its great cathedral, with its tall grey spires, rising above the roofs.

[long-pause]

The people of Bayeux come out into the streets. They hang out the French flag, hidden away for four years. They give flowers to the soldiers. They ring the cathedral bells. And they can hardly believe it is true.

[pause]

A week later, on the fourteenth of June, a tall French general arrives. His name is Charles de Gaulle. For four years, he has led the Free French from London. He has spoken to the French people on the radio. Now he sets foot in France again for the first time. And in the square of Bayeux, he speaks to the people. They crowd around him and cheer. Many of them cry. France is coming back.

[pause]

{img:general-de-gaulle-speaking-crowd-bayeux-square-june-1944-flags}
Bayeux is also famous for something much older. A great embroidery, about seventy metres long. It is nearly a thousand years old. It tells the story of another crossing of the sea. In the year 1066, Duke William of Normandy sailed the other way. From Normandy to England. With his ships and his horses and his knights. The embroidery shows it all, in wool on linen. Ships and horses and castles and feasts. And a comet in the sky.

[long-pause]

During the war, the embroidery was hidden away to keep it safe. Now it is back in Bayeux, in its own museum. Visitors walk slowly past it, from one end to the other. It is like a very long picture book.

[pause]

{img:bayeux-tapestry-panel-ships-horses-sail-embroidered-wool-museum-light}
So in Bayeux, two great sea crossings meet. One in 1066, from Normandy to England. And one in 1944, from England to Normandy. Nine hundred years apart. And the old town has seen them both.

[long-pause]

{img:veterans-old-men-medals-normandy-beach-ceremony-flags-sunshine}
Every year, on the sixth of June, D-Day is remembered.

[pause]

For many years, the men who were there came back to Normandy every June. In their berets and blazers, with their medals on their chests. They came to the beaches and the villages and the bridges where they had landed as young men. And they stood quietly and remembered their friends.

[long-pause]

The people of Normandy welcomed them every year. With flowers and flags and music and gratitude. The old men and women of the villages, who had been children in 1944, came out to shake their hands and thank them again. And teachers brought schoolchildren to meet them and hear their stories.

[pause]

On the big anniversaries, there were great ceremonies. Kings and queens and presidents came. Thousands of people gathered. And the old soldiers sat in the front rows, in the sunshine, by the sea.

[pause]

{img:children-giving-flowers-veteran-wheelchair-normandy-village-square}
Eighty years after D-Day, a few of the last veterans came back again. Very old now, nearly a hundred, some even older. In wheelchairs and with sticks, with their families. And they were welcomed and honoured one last time by the whole world.

[long-pause]

Some of them did something special. In their nineties, and one at the age of a hundred, they jumped by parachute again. Tied to an instructor, over the fields of Normandy where they had landed so long ago. The crowds below cheered and clapped. And cried.

[pause]

Many veterans said the same thing when people called them heroes. I am not a hero, they said. The heroes are the ones who did not come home. I only did my job. And they looked out at the sea and the beach quietly for a long time. And said nothing more.

[pause]

{img:war-cemetery-normandy-white-headstones-rows-green-grass-roses-quiet}
Above the beaches there are cemeteries. Quiet green peaceful places. Rows and rows of white headstones in the grass, among roses and trees, looking out over the sea. Gardeners care for them beautifully every day. Visitors walk slowly among them in silence. They read the names and the ages. Eighteen. Nineteen. Twenty. They leave flowers and little flags. And sometimes a letter.

[long-pause]

And there are memorials all along the coast. In every village and at every bridge and crossroads where something happened. With names and flags and flowers. So that nobody who passes by will forget.

[long-pause]

{img:normandy-beach-today-wide-sand-families-walking-dunes-sea-calm}
Today, the coast of Normandy is peaceful again.

[pause]

The beaches are wide and sandy and beautiful. In summer, families come to swim and build sandcastles and fly kites. They ride horses along the edge of the sea. They sit in the dunes and eat their picnics in the sunshine, as people did before the war.

[long-pause]

But the past is never far away. In the dunes there are still the remains of old concrete defences. Grey and weathered, half buried in sand and grass. And at Arromanches, out in the sea, the great concrete blocks of the floating harbour are still there. Rusty and broken and covered with seaweed. The waves break on them. Seagulls sit on them. At low tide, you can walk out across the sand toward them.

[pause]

In the villages there are museums full of old uniforms and jeeps and photographs and letters. On the walls of the houses there are little signs. And in the windows, flags of America and Britain and Canada hang beside the flag of France.

[pause]

{img:arromanches-today-mulberry-remains-sea-low-tide-town-church}
The people of Normandy have never forgotten. Every June, the villages are decorated with flags. Old jeeps drive in parades. People dress in the clothes of 1944. They dance in the village squares to old music from the 1940s. Fireworks go up over the sea. And the church bells ring.

[long-pause]

In Portsmouth, across the sea in England, there is a museum about D-Day. It has a great embroidery, eighty-three metres long. It was made about fifty years ago. It tells the story of D-Day in thirty-four panels of coloured cloth and thread. Like a modern version of the old embroidery in Bayeux.

[pause]

{img:normandy-orchard-today-apple-blossom-old-stone-farmhouse-cows-peaceful}
And in the countryside behind the coast, the green fields are still there. The high hedges and the sunken lanes. The apple orchards and grey stone farmhouses. And the brown and white cows. In May, the apple trees blossom pink and white. The cows graze under them. And the farmers make their cider and their cheese, as their grandparents did.

[long-pause]

And on the sixth of June every year, at dawn, people come to the beaches. They stand quietly on the sand as the sun rises over the sea and the tide comes in. And they remember.

[long-pause]

{img:english-coast-night-june-1944-calm-sea-moon-clouds-cliffs-quiet}
So we leave the south of England tonight, on the night of the fifth of June, 1944.

[long-pause]

The harbours are empty now. The great fleet has gone, out into the dark, across the sea. The camps in the woods are quiet. On the airfields, the last planes have taken off. The ground crews stand on the grass in the moonlight, looking up. In the house in the hills above Portsmouth, a tall Scotsman sits by a window, looking at the clouds, hoping he was right. In a caravan in the woods nearby, a general lies awake, reading a cowboy story, waiting.

[pause]

In the villages, people stand in their gardens in their dressing gowns, looking up at a sky full of engines, holding hands. On a wild coast in the west of Ireland, a young woman climbs the stairs by lamplight to read the barometer once more. In a farmhouse in Normandy, a family sleeps under an old roof, while the wind blows through the apple trees. And out at sea, on thousands of ships, young men lie in the dark, listening to the waves, and thinking of home.

[pause]

{img:quiet-cottage-window-candle-night-garden-june-roses-moonlight}
And over all of them, the clouds are slowly breaking. The wind is slowly dropping. The moon shines through the gaps, on the sea and the fields and the roofs. The short summer night passes. And the dawn comes closer.
