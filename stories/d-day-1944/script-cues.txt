Tonight we go more than eighty years back in time. We go to the south of England, in the first days of June, in the year 1944.

[long-pause]

It is early summer, and the countryside is green and full of life. The hedgerows along the lanes are white with wild flowers. The wild roses are just beginning to open, pink and pale. The fields are green, and the hay is growing tall in the meadows. In the villages, the cottage gardens are bright with colour. There are lupins and foxgloves, sweet peas and early roses. And the swallows fly low over the village ponds in the evening light.

[pause]

But all along the quiet country lanes, under the trees, there is something unusual. There are lorries and tanks, jeeps and big guns, parked one behind another for miles. They are covered with green nets, so they cannot be seen from the air. In the fields there are long rows of tents. And everywhere, in the villages and the towns and the lanes, there are soldiers.

[pause]

If you like calm stories from the past, like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

For four years, the coast of France across the sea has been occupied by the German army. And for many months, everyone has known that one day the Allies will cross the sea. They will land in France, and they will begin to free Western Europe. Everyone knows that it is coming. But nobody knows when, and nobody knows where.

[pause]

{img:english-channel-coast-white-cliffs-calm-sea-june-evening-light}
And now, in these first days of June, the whole south of England is waiting. It is as if the whole country is holding its breath. On the white cliffs, people stop on their evening walks and look out across the sea. The water is calm and grey and silver. Somewhere over there, beyond the horizon, lies France.

[long-pause]

Tonight we will spend the last days and nights before the great day. Later it will be called D-Day. We will not follow the battles themselves. Instead, we will stay gently with the waiting. We will stay with the soldiers in their camps and on their ships, writing letters and playing cards. We will stay with the weathermen, studying their charts through the long night. We will meet the general who must make the hardest decision of the war. We will meet a young woman on a lonely coast of Ireland, reading a weather instrument. We will listen with people in France, who hear a poem on the radio in secret. And we will visit a farming family in Normandy, going to bed on a windy night.

[pause]

And we will stand with the people of England in their gardens at midnight. They look up at a sky full of aeroplanes, and they say a quiet prayer.

[pause]

{img:village-church-tower-dusk-english-countryside-june-swifts-quiet}
So let us go back to the beginning of June, 1944. Let us go back to the long, light summer evenings. Let us go back to the lanes full of soldiers and the gardens full of roses. And let us go back to the sea, grey and restless, beyond the cliffs. In a village, the church bell rings for evening, and the swifts are screaming around the tower. The sun goes down slowly over the fields. And everyone is waiting.

[long-pause]

{img:army-camp-tents-rows-english-field-soldiers-washing-morning-1944}
In the spring of 1944, the south of England has become one huge army camp.

[pause]

Nearly two million soldiers, sailors and airmen are gathered in Britain, getting ready. There are British and Americans and Canadians. There are Free French and Poles and Norwegians. There are Dutch and Belgians and Czechs. And there are men from Australia and New Zealand and many other countries too.

[long-pause]

They live in camps in fields and woods and parks, all over the south of England. Their tanks and lorries and guns are parked along the roads. Their supplies are stacked in great piles beside the roads, covered with canvas. The airfields are full of planes and gliders, lined up in long rows on the grass.

[pause]

In the mornings, the camps wake up early. Soldiers wash in tin basins outside their tents, splashing cold water on their faces. They shave with little mirrors hung on the tent poles. They queue for breakfast with their tin plates and mugs. There is porridge and bread and sweet tea, and sometimes a fried egg. Then the sergeants call out the orders for the day.

[pause]

People joked that the island was so full of soldiers and equipment that it might sink. Only the big silver balloons floating above it were holding it up, they said.

[pause]

{img:barrage-balloons-silver-sky-over-harbour-town-1944-afternoon}
For months, the soldiers have been training for what lies ahead. They practised landing from boats on beaches in the west and in Scotland. They climbed cliffs with ropes and crossed rivers on little bridges. They marched for miles along the country lanes, singing to keep in step. They jumped from planes by parachute over the green fields. In the evenings they came back to their camps tired and hungry, with mud on their boots.

[long-pause]

The people of the villages have grown used to having them around. The soldiers buy cups of tea and buns in the village cafés. They go to the pubs and play darts with the local men. They go to the dances in the village halls on Saturday nights. And they walk along the lanes with the local girls in the long summer evenings.

[pause]

The children follow them everywhere they go. They collect badges and buttons from the soldiers' uniforms. They ask for chewing gum and chocolate. They climb on the tanks when they are allowed to. They watch the planes flying over, and they know the names of all of them.

[pause]

{img:children-watching-tanks-convoy-village-street-waving-1944}
And then, at the end of May, everything changes. The soldiers are told to pack their kit. The long convoys begin to move toward the coast, day and night. There are long slow lines of lorries and tanks and jeeps, rumbling through the villages. The soldiers sit in the back of the lorries and wave to the people by the road.

[long-pause]

The people of the villages stand at their garden gates and watch them go. Some of the women hand up cups of tea and little bunches of flowers. An old man takes off his cap as the lorries pass. The children run beside the lorries for as long as they can, shouting goodbye. And everyone knows, without being told, what it means. It will be soon now, very soon. When the last lorry has gone, the lanes are strangely quiet. Only the swallows are left, flying low over the hedges.

[long-pause]

{img:american-gis-english-village-pub-locals-darts-friendly-1944}
Of all the soldiers in England in 1944, the ones who make the biggest impression are the Americans.

[pause]

They began to arrive about two years earlier. By the spring of 1944, there are more than one and a half million of them all over Britain. For many English villagers, these are the first Americans they have ever met. And for many of the Americans, it is the very first time they have ever left home.

[long-pause]

The Americans seem very different to the English villagers. They are tall and well fed and cheerful. They talk loudly in their unfamiliar accents. They chew gum all the time. Their uniforms are smarter than the British ones. And they are paid much more money.

[pause]

They bring things that the British have not seen for years, because of the war. They bring chewing gum and chocolate, oranges and tinned peaches. They bring nylon stockings, which the young women love. And they give these things away generously. They give them to the children, to the girls, and to their new friends.

[pause]

{img:gis-children-candy-gum-english-lane-laughing-jeep-1944}
Got any gum, chum, the English children shout, running after the jeeps. And the Americans laugh and throw them chewing gum and sweets. The children love them for it. Some of the children have never tasted an orange before. They hold it in their hands and smell it for a long time before they eat it.

[long-pause]

The Americans bring their music with them as well. They bring jazz and swing and the big band songs. They teach the English girls fast new dances in the village halls. They whirl them round and swing them through the air. The old people sitting at the side of the hall shake their heads and laugh.

[pause]

There are a few small misunderstandings, of course. The Americans find English beer too warm and English weather too wet. They find English food rather dull and English trains rather small. The English find the Americans loud and a bit boastful at first. But slowly, they get to know each other better. And many of them become good friends.

[pause]

{img:american-soldiers-thanksgiving-english-family-kitchen-table-shared-meal}
Many English families invite American soldiers into their homes for Sunday tea. They share their small wartime rations with them. In return, the Americans bring tins of meat and fruit and sugar from their stores. For the first time in years, the families have more than enough on the table. In the autumn, some of the Americans even cook a Thanksgiving dinner for their English friends. They explain what Thanksgiving is, and everyone sits together around the kitchen table. The children ask a hundred questions about America, and the soldiers answer every one.

[long-pause]

Thousands of English girls fall in love with American soldiers, and many of them marry. After the war, these young brides will cross the ocean to America. There they will begin new lives, far from their families and their villages.

[pause]

And in the first days of June, the Americans too are in their camps by the sea, waiting. They write letters home to their mothers and their sweethearts, far away across the ocean. They write about the pretty villages and the green fields and the rain. They write about the English family who gave them Sunday tea. And they do not say a word about what is about to happen.

[long-pause]

{img:english-village-children-1944-playing-soldiers-lane-wooden-rifles-summer}
For the children of the villages, the spring of 1944 is the most exciting time of their lives.

[pause]

Let us meet one of them, a boy of ten called Tom. He lives in a small village in Dorset, not far from the sea. His father is away in the navy, on a ship somewhere at sea. Tom lives with his mother, his little sister Peggy and his grandfather. And for months now, his quiet village has been full of soldiers.

[long-pause]

Every morning on the way to school, Tom counts the tanks parked under the trees in the lane. He knows every kind of lorry and gun by heart. He knows the badges of every regiment. He has an old biscuit tin full of buttons and badges that the soldiers have given him. It is his greatest treasure, and he keeps it under his bed.

[pause]

At school, the teacher has trouble keeping the children's attention. Every time a plane flies over, all the heads turn to the window. The teacher sighs and waits until the noise has passed. Then she taps the blackboard and goes on with the sums.

[pause]

After school, Tom and his friends play soldiers in the fields. They use sticks for rifles and old saucepans for helmets. They crawl through the long grass and hide in the hedges. They pretend to land on beaches at the edge of the village pond. And sometimes the real soldiers stop to watch them. They laugh and give them advice on how to hide better.

[pause]

{img:soldier-sharing-chocolate-boy-sitting-on-gate-english-lane-evening}
Tom's favourite soldier is a tall American called Joe. Joe comes from a farm in Iowa, far away in the middle of America. He misses his little brother at home, who is the same age as Tom. So in the evenings, Joe sometimes sits on the field gate with Tom. He shares his chocolate and tells him stories about home. He tells him about the cornfields that go on forever, as far as you can see. He tells him about the big red barn, and the dog called Rusty who sleeps by the stove. Tom tells Joe about the village and the sea, and about his father on the ship.

[long-pause]

Sometimes Peggy comes along too, and sits on the grass by the gate. Joe makes her a little crown of daisies and puts it on her head. She wears it all evening and will not take it off, not even for bed.

[pause]

At the end of May, Joe comes to say goodbye. We are moving out, he says quietly. I can't say where we are going. He takes the badge off his jacket and gives it to Tom. Keep this for me, he says. Tom holds it very tightly in his hand. Will you come back, he asks? Joe smiles and ruffles his hair. Sure I will, he says. And then he climbs into the lorry, and it drives away down the lane.

[pause]

That night, Tom puts the badge in his tin box with all his other treasures. And when he says his prayers, he says an extra one. He says a prayer for his father on the ship. And he says a prayer for Joe.

[long-pause]

Many years later, when Tom is an old man with white hair, he still has that badge. He keeps it on a shelf in his living room, next to a photograph of his father. And he tells his grandchildren about the tall American who sat on the gate. He tells them how Joe shared his chocolate and told stories about the cornfields. It was in the spring before D-Day, he says, when I was ten years old.

[long-pause]

{img:sealed-marshalling-camp-barbed-wire-tents-woods-guards-1944}
At the end of May, the soldiers who will cross the sea first are moved into special camps near the coast. And the gates are closed behind them.

[pause]

The camps are sealed, which means nobody may go in or out without special permission. There are wire fences all around and guards standing at the gates. The soldiers may not send letters or make telephone calls. They may not talk to anyone outside the fence. That is because now, at last, they are going to be told the great secret.

[long-pause]

The soldiers give these camps a funny name. They call them the sausage machines. The men go in at one end, they say, and come out at the other end, onto the ships.

[pause]

Inside, the soldiers live in tents among the trees and in the fields. And for the first time, they are told where they are going.

[pause]

{img:briefing-tent-soldiers-sand-table-model-coast-officer-pointing}
In big tents, the officers gather their men around long tables. There are maps, and photographs taken from planes flying low over the coast. There are models made of plaster and sand, painted carefully in green and brown. They show the coast where the men will land. They show the beaches and the dunes and the cliffs. They show the villages and the roads and the little churches. Every house, every hedge and every path is there.

[long-pause]

But the names written on the maps are not the real names. They are code names, so the real place stays secret. Even now, if anyone talked, the enemy would not learn the real place. The beaches have names like Utah and Omaha, Gold and Juno and Sword.

[pause]

The men study the maps and models again and again, until they know them by heart. Some of them recognise the coast from seaside holidays before the war. And then they realise where they are going. It is Normandy.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-waiting-camp-playing-cards-reading-writing-letters-sunshine}
Then there is the waiting, which takes up most of the days. The men clean their rifles again and again. They check their equipment and pack it and unpack it. They are given French money and little books of useful French words. They practise saying good morning and thank you, and they laugh at each other's accents. They are given pills for seasickness, and paper bags in case the pills do not work. They play cards for the French money, and lose it, and win it back again. They read, and they write letters that will not be posted until later. They sleep and talk and play football in the clearings. And they eat better food than they have had in years. There is steak and white bread, and even ice cream.

[long-pause]

On Sunday morning, the chaplains hold services in the open air. Many men go who have not been to church for years. They kneel on the grass in the sunshine among the trees. They sing the old hymns they learned as boys, and they pray quietly.

[pause]

{img:chaplain-field-service-soldiers-kneeling-woods-morning-quiet}
And in the evenings, as the sun sets behind the trees, they sit outside their tents. They look up at the sky, which is turning pink and gold. Birds are singing in the woods, as if nothing at all were different. The men talk quietly about home and their families. They talk about the girls they hope to marry and the jobs they hope to have. And they talk about what they will do when the war is over.

[long-pause]

{img:carrier-pigeons-basket-soldier-releasing-bird-1944-camp}
It is not only people who take part in D-Day. There are animals too.

[pause]

Among the most important of them are the pigeons. In 1944, there are no mobile phones, of course. Radios are heavy, and they can break easily. And the enemy can listen in to radio messages. So the armies and the newspapers use carrier pigeons. The pigeons carry messages home across the sea, from France to England.

[long-pause]

Pigeons have a wonderful gift. Wherever they are taken, they can always find their way home to their own loft. They can do it even across hundreds of kilometres of land and sea. Nobody knows exactly how they do it, even today. Perhaps they follow the sun. Perhaps they can feel the pull of the earth itself. Perhaps they use smells and landmarks along the way.

[pause]

So the pigeons are carried across the sea in wicker baskets, with the soldiers and the reporters. When there is a message to send, it is written on a tiny piece of thin paper. The paper is rolled up and put into a little tube on the pigeon's leg. Then the pigeon is let go. It flies up into the sky, circles once, and heads straight for home.

[pause]

The pigeons are looked after very carefully by their keepers. In their lofts in England, they have clean straw and fresh water and good seed. Their keepers know each bird by name. They talk to them softly and stroke their grey feathers.

[pause]

{img:pigeon-flying-over-sea-channel-grey-clouds-wind-alone}
One of the most famous of these pigeons was called Gustav. On the morning of D-Day, a reporter on a ship off the French coast wrote a short message. He tied it to Gustav's leg and let him go into the grey sky. Gustav flew back across the sea, against a strong wind. Would he make it all the way home? He flew for more than five hours, nearly three hundred kilometres. And at last he arrived at his loft near Portsmouth. He brought some of the very first news of the landings.

[long-pause]

For this, Gustav was given a medal for bravery. It is a special medal, given only to animals. And he lived happily in his loft for many years afterwards.

[pause]

There were dogs as well. Some soldiers had dogs that were trained to jump by parachute with them. The dogs could hear and smell things long before people could. On the night before D-Day, a few of these brave dogs jumped into Normandy with their soldiers, in the dark.

[pause]

{img:paratrooper-with-alsatian-dog-airfield-1944-gentle-companionship}
One of these dogs was a big Alsatian called Bing. He was gentle and calm, and his soldier loved him very much. Before the flight, they sat together on the grass by the planes. The soldier scratched Bing behind the ears and shared his biscuits with him.

[pause]

And then there were the cows of Normandy. They stood in their fields in amazement, as gliders landed around them in the night. Parachutes drifted down softly into their meadows. The next morning, the farmers came out to find their fields full of parachutes and gliders and strangers.

[long-pause]

Later, the farmers' wives gathered up the parachutes left lying in the fields. They were made of white and green silk. There was no other cloth to be had in those days. So the women made them into dresses and blouses and shirts. They even made wedding dresses from them. Some of these parachute wedding dresses are still kept today, in the museums of Normandy. They are white and soft and beautiful.

[long-pause]

{img:inflatable-rubber-tank-dummy-field-soldiers-carrying-it-kent-1944}
The greatest secret of the war is a simple question. Where, and when, will the Allies land?

[pause]

The enemy knows that the landing is coming. They have built great defences all along the coast of France. There is concrete and steel, and there are guns and obstacles on the beaches. But the coast is very long. They cannot be strong everywhere at once. If they knew where the landing would be, they could gather their armies there and wait.

[long-pause]

So the Allies have made a huge plan full of tricks. They want the enemy to believe that the landing will come somewhere else. Perhaps at Calais, further north, where the sea is narrowest. Or perhaps even far away in Norway.

[pause]

In the east of England, across the sea from Calais, they create a whole army that does not exist. There are camps full of empty tents, with smoke rising from a few cooking fires. There are tanks and lorries made of rubber, blown up like big balloons. Four men can carry one of these rubber tanks and set it up in a field. From the air, it looks just like a real tank.

[pause]

The men who look after this pretend army have a strange job. They move the rubber tanks around every few days, so they look busy. They make tracks in the mud with a special roller, as if real tanks had driven there. They hang washing on lines between the empty tents. Sometimes they laugh about it in the evenings. But they know it is very important work.

[pause]

{img:dummy-landing-craft-canvas-wood-estuary-moored-rows-dawn}
There are pretend landing boats made of wood and canvas, floating in rows on the rivers. There are radio operators sending thousands of messages every day, from one empty camp to another. It sounds as if a great army were there, talking to itself. And a famous American general is put in charge of this ghost army. That way, the enemy will think it must be real.

[long-pause]

And the plan works very well. The enemy believes it. They keep a large army waiting near Calais for many weeks. They wait there for a landing that never comes.

[pause]

But there are also some funny moments, when the secret nearly seems to slip out.

[pause]

{img:schoolmaster-crossword-puzzle-newspaper-armchair-pipe-1944}
In the weeks before D-Day, the crossword puzzle in a big English newspaper had some very strange answers. One day, one of the answers was Utah. Another day, it was Omaha. Then came Overlord, which was the secret code name for the whole landing. Then Mulberry, and then Neptune. All of them were secret code names.

[long-pause]

The security officers were very alarmed. They went to see the man who made the crosswords. He was a quiet schoolmaster, who liked his pipe and his garden. They asked him questions for hours. But he had no idea at all what they were talking about.

[pause]

Many years later, one of his old pupils explained what had happened. The boys at the school spent a lot of time with the soldiers camped nearby. They heard these strange words and remembered them. And the schoolmaster sometimes asked the boys to fill in empty crossword grids with any words they knew. Then he wrote clues for those words. So the great secrets went innocently from the soldiers to the schoolboys, and into the newspaper. And luckily, nobody on the other side noticed.

[pause]

{img:quiet-country-road-signposts-removed-1944-hedges-afternoon}
All over Britain, the signposts have been taken down from the roads. If an enemy ever landed, he would not know where he was. Even the names of the railway stations have been painted over. Travellers have to ask the porters where they are. And posters everywhere remind people not to talk about what they see. Careless talk costs lives, the posters say. And people do not talk.

[long-pause]

{img:portsmouth-harbour-1944-crowded-warships-landing-craft-barrage-balloons}
By the beginning of June, every harbour and river and creek along the south coast of England is full of ships.

[pause]

In Portsmouth and Southampton and Plymouth, and in every little harbour in between, the ships lie at anchor. They are packed so close together that people say you could walk across the harbour from ship to ship. You would not even get your feet wet, they say.

[long-pause]

There are great grey battleships and cruisers with their big guns. There are troop ships and supply ships. There are hospital ships, painted white with big red crosses on their sides. And there are landing craft, thousands of them, of every shape and size. Some are big flat ships with great doors at the front. The doors open onto a beach, so that tanks can drive straight out. Others are little boats for about thirty soldiers. They have a ramp at the front that drops down into the water.

[pause]

Altogether there are nearly seven thousand ships and boats. It is the largest fleet ever gathered in the whole history of the world.

[pause]

{img:landing-craft-loading-tanks-hard-ramp-concrete-slipway-soldiers}
Along the shore, special concrete ramps slope down into the water. The landing craft come in and open their great doors. Tanks and lorries drive on board, one after another, with a roar of engines. Soldiers march on board with their heavy packs, in long lines. Sailors shout directions and wave their arms. Seagulls circle overhead, crying, as if they too want to know what is happening.

[long-pause]

In the harbours, there are stranger things too. There are huge blocks of concrete, as big as houses, floating in the water. They are waiting to be towed across the sea. They are parts of two whole harbours, made by people. These harbours will be put together off the coast of Normandy. Then ships can unload there, even where there is no real port.

[pause]

Nobody has ever done anything like this before. Tens of thousands of workers have built these pieces in secret, for months. Most of them had no idea at all what they were building.

[pause]

{img:mulberry-concrete-caissons-floating-estuary-tugs-evening-grey}
In the towns along the coast, people see the ships and the soldiers and the tanks rumbling toward the docks. And they understand what it means. They stand on the seafront and on the cliffs in the evenings. They look at the great fleet filling the harbours and the sea, as far as they can see. And they wonder quietly what the next days will bring.

[pause]

In the little cafés on the seafront, the women behind the counters pour endless cups of tea. Sailors come in to sit for a moment and warm their hands. They say very little, and the women do not ask. But they give the young sailors an extra biscuit, and a kind word.

[long-pause]

At night, the ships lie dark and silent in the harbours. No lights show anywhere, not even a lamp in a porthole. Guards walk slowly up and down the decks. Soldiers sleep in hammocks below, or wrapped in blankets on the decks. And the sea washes softly against the hulls of the ships, under the summer stars.

[long-pause]

{img:king-george-vi-churchill-meeting-palace-study-1944-discussion}
In the days before D-Day, the Prime Minister, Winston Churchill, has an idea. He wants to go with the fleet, on one of the warships. He wants to watch the landings from the sea.

[pause]

He is sixty-nine years old, and he has led the country through four long years of war. He cannot bear the thought of staying at home in London. He does not want to sit safely at home while the young men he has sent go to France.

[long-pause]

His advisers are very worried about this idea. If the Prime Minister were lost at sea, it would be a terrible blow for the whole country. They try hard to persuade him not to go. But he will not listen to them. I am the Prime Minister, he says firmly. I will go if I wish.

[pause]

Then the King hears about it. And King George says, if the Prime Minister is going, then I will go too. I am the head of the navy, he says. My place is with my sailors.

[pause]

{img:king-writing-letter-desk-buckingham-palace-pen-lamp-1944}
Of course, both of them cannot go, and the King knows it very well. So he sits down at his desk in the palace and writes a letter to Churchill. It is a kind and wise and gentle letter. He says he would very much like to go with the fleet himself. But he knows that he should stay at home. And he says the Prime Minister should stay at home too. Because the country needs them both, safe and well.

[long-pause]

Churchill has great respect and warm affection for the King. He reads the letter slowly, more than once. And at last, he agrees to stay. Though he grumbles about it to everyone for days.

[pause]

The King and Churchill meet every week for lunch, all through the war. They sit together in a small room in the palace and serve themselves from the sideboard. There are no servants in the room, so they can talk freely. They talk about the war and the country and the hard choices ahead. Over the years, they have become real friends.

[pause]

So on the night before D-Day, the King is at Buckingham Palace. Churchill is in London too, in his rooms near Downing Street. Neither of them can sleep at all that night. Churchill tells his wife Clementine how worried he is. He thinks about all the young men crossing the sea in the dark. And she holds his hand and sits with him.

[pause]

{img:churchill-sleepless-night-map-room-cigar-lamp-looking-at-map}
In the end, the losses on D-Day were far fewer than he had feared. Every one was a deep sorrow for a family somewhere. But the landings went better than many had dared to hope. The next morning, Churchill went to Parliament to tell the country. Things are going according to plan, he said.

[long-pause]

A few days later, Churchill was allowed at last to cross the sea and visit Normandy. He walked on the sand among the soldiers and the lorries and the ships. He wore his long coat and his hat, and he carried his cigar and his walking stick. He beamed at everyone and made his famous V sign with his fingers. And the soldiers cheered him as he passed. A few days after that, the King went too, and visited his soldiers in Normandy. He shook their hands and thanked them, one by one.

[long-pause]

{img:weather-forecasters-charts-isobars-table-night-lamps-1944-room}
In the end, everything depends on the weather.

[pause]

To cross the sea and land on the beaches, the Allies need the right conditions, all at the same time. They need a calm sea, so the small boats can cross safely. They need low water at dawn, so the obstacles on the beaches can be seen. They need a bright moon at night, so the pilots can see where they are going. And they need clear skies, without too much cloud and without too much wind.

[long-pause]

There are only a few days each month when the tides and the moon are right. In early June, those days are the fifth, the sixth and the seventh. After that, the next chance is weeks away. The date chosen for D-Day is the fifth of June.

[pause]

And the man who must tell the generals what the weather will be is a tall, serious Scotsman. He is a weather scientist called James Stagg.

[pause]

{img:meteorologist-james-stagg-tall-scotsman-uniform-studying-weather-map}
Stagg leads a small team of weather forecasters, both British and American. They study reports from weather stations, ships and planes all over the Atlantic Ocean. They draw big weather maps covered with long curving lines. The lines show where the storms are, and where they are going next. The forecasters work at long tables, under bright lamps, with cups of tea going cold beside them.

[long-pause]

Weather forecasting in 1944 is very hard indeed. There are no satellites and no computers to help. The forecasters work it all out with pencils and maps and years of experience. And the weather over the Atlantic and the Channel can change very quickly.

[pause]

At the beginning of June, Stagg is worried. All through May, the weather was fine and sunny. Now it is changing. Storms are coming in from the Atlantic, one after another. On the fourth of June, he warns the generals. The weather on the fifth will be bad, he says. There will be strong winds, low clouds and rough seas.

[pause]

{img:stormy-english-channel-june-1944-grey-waves-rain-ships-at-anchor}
And he is right. On the fourth of June, the wind rises. The rain comes down in grey sheets. The sea in the Channel turns rough, with white waves breaking everywhere. The ships at anchor roll and pull at their chains.

[long-pause]

So the landing is put off by one day. Some ships have already set out to sea. They must turn round and come back to the harbours to wait. The soldiers crowded on the ships are already seasick. Now they must wait another whole day.

[pause]

Then, on the evening of the fourth of June, Stagg and his team see something on their maps. Between two storms, there may be a gap. It could be a short break in the weather. There might be a few hours of calmer weather on the sixth of June.

[pause]

{img:weather-station-report-arriving-teleprinter-paper-strip-night-office}
In a small office nearby, a machine clatters all through the night. It prints the new weather reports on long strips of paper. A young woman tears off each strip and carries it quickly to the forecasters. They read every line with great care.

[pause]

Is the gap in the weather long enough? Nobody can be completely sure. If they wait for the next chance in two weeks, the secret might be discovered. The soldiers already know everything, and they are waiting on the ships. And the weather in two weeks might be even worse.

[long-pause]

It is the most important forecast of James Stagg's whole life. He does not sleep at all that night. He sits and stares at his maps, under the lamp. He reads every new report as soon as it arrives. He checks his lines and his numbers again and again. Outside, the wind and rain beat against the windows. And so much now depends on him.

[long-pause]

{img:blacksod-point-lighthouse-mayo-ireland-atlantic-coast-wild-grass-1944}
Far away, on the wild west coast of Ireland, there is a little lighthouse and a post office. They stand at the very end of a long, narrow piece of land. The place is called Blacksod Point.

[pause]

It looks out over the Atlantic Ocean toward America. There is nothing between Blacksod and America but the wide grey sea. The grass is short and tough there, bent by the wind. Sheep graze among the stones, and the waves boom against the rocks all day and all night.

[long-pause]

Ireland is not fighting in the war. But for years, the little weather station at Blacksod has sent weather reports every hour to Dublin. From there, the reports are quietly passed on to the forecasters in Britain. That is because Blacksod is one of the first places in Europe that the Atlantic weather reaches. Whatever weather comes to Blacksod will reach England and France a day or so later.

[pause]

The lighthouse keeper, Ted Sweeney, and his young assistant, Maureen Flavin, take the readings. They do it every hour, day and night. They read the air pressure on the barometer. They measure the temperature. And they note the wind and the clouds and the rain.

[pause]

Between the readings, there is plenty of ordinary work to do. Maureen sorts the letters in the little post office and sells stamps to the neighbours. She makes tea on the stove and listens to the radio. In the evenings she walks along the shore, with her coat wrapped tightly around her. She knows every mood of the sea.

[pause]

{img:young-irish-woman-reading-barometer-post-office-lighthouse-dawn}
On the third of June, 1944, it is Maureen's twenty-first birthday. Very early that morning, at one o'clock, she goes to read the barometer. She holds up her lamp and looks closely. The pressure is falling fast. She knows what that means. A storm is coming.

[long-pause]

She sends the report by telephone, as she always does. Soon afterwards, the telephone rings. A voice with an English accent asks her a question. Please check that reading, and repeat it. So she checks it again carefully. The reading is the same. Later they ask her again, and again she checks. Why do they keep asking, she wonders? She has no idea.

[pause]

She does not know that her readings are being carried across the sea to a house in the south of England. There, the generals and the forecasters are making the most important decision of the war.

[pause]

{img:atlantic-waves-rocky-shore-mayo-storm-clouds-lighthouse-beam}
Her reports helped to warn of the storm on the fifth of June. That is why the landing was put off by a day. And her later reports helped them to see the break in the weather coming just behind it.

[long-pause]

Maureen later married Ted Sweeney, the lighthouse keeper. She lived at Blacksod for the rest of her long life, and she raised her family there. For many years, she did not know how important her birthday reading had been. Only much later did she learn the whole story. When she was very old, she was honoured by the American government for her part in D-Day. She passed away peacefully at the age of a hundred, in her home on the edge of the Atlantic. She had watched the weather and the waves there all her life.

[long-pause]

{img:southwick-house-hampshire-georgian-mansion-1944-night-rain-army-cars}
In the hills just north of Portsmouth, there is a large white country house. It has tall windows and white columns beside the front door. Woods and parkland surround it on every side. It is called Southwick House.

[pause]

In the spring of 1944, it has become the headquarters of the landing. Here, in the library and the drawing room, the generals and admirals meet. They sit among the tall windows and the old bookshelves, to plan and to decide.

[long-pause]

The top commander is the American general Dwight Eisenhower, whom people call Ike. He is a tall, friendly, smiling man from Kansas. He is very good at getting people from many countries to work together. With him are the British general Montgomery, the admiral Ramsay, and the chiefs of the air forces.

[pause]

In one room there is a huge map of the Channel and the coast of Normandy. It covers a whole wall, from the floor to the ceiling. A toy company made it out of wood. Officers move little markers on it, to show where every ship is. As the reports come in, the markers move slowly across the sea. That map is still there today, on the same wall. It looks exactly as it did on the morning of D-Day.

[pause]

{img:southwick-house-wall-map-channel-normandy-ships-markers-officers}
The house is busy at every hour of the day and night. Officers hurry along the corridors with papers in their hands. Telephones ring in every room. In the kitchen, cooks make endless sandwiches and huge pots of tea and coffee. Outside, army cars come and go on the gravel drive, their tyres crunching.

[pause]

On the evening of the fourth of June, at half past nine, the commanders meet in the library. Rain beats against the tall windows. The wind howls in the trees outside. And James Stagg, the weatherman, comes in. He tells them about the break in the weather he expects on the sixth.

[long-pause]

The generals ask him many questions, and he answers each one carefully. He tells them honestly that he cannot be certain. Then everyone in the room turns and looks at Eisenhower. It is his decision, and his alone.

[pause]

He sits silently for a long time, thinking. The rain beats on the glass, and nobody says a word. Then at last he speaks, quietly. I am quite sure we must give the order, he says. I don't like it, but there it is.

[pause]

{img:library-southwick-house-generals-armchairs-night-lamplight-rain-windows}
The orders go out. But there is still a little time to change them if needed. So at four o'clock in the morning on the fifth of June, the commanders meet again in the library. They want to hear the very latest forecast. The rain is still beating on the windows. But Stagg says the break in the weather is still coming. Eisenhower thinks for a few moments more. And then he says, OK, let's go.

[long-pause]

The room empties very quickly. The commanders hurry out into the dark and the rain, to their cars, to give their orders. And Eisenhower is left alone in the quiet library. The hardest decision of the war has been made. Now there is nothing more he can do but wait.

[pause]

Outside, as dawn comes on the fifth of June, the rain begins to ease a little. And slowly, the wind begins to drop.

[long-pause]

{img:soldiers-crowded-landing-ship-deck-waiting-harbour-grey-sky-june-5}
The fifth of June, 1944, is a long day of waiting.

[pause]

Some soldiers have been on board their ships for days already. They are crowded below decks, in rows of narrow bunks and swinging hammocks. Others are on the open decks under canvas, in the wind and the rain. Many of them feel seasick, even in the harbour, as the ships roll gently at anchor.

[long-pause]

First they were told that the landing had been put off by a day. Now they are told it will go ahead after all. Tonight, they will sail.

[pause]

So they wait through the long, grey, windy day. They play cards on the decks, holding the cards tightly so they do not blow away. They read and doze and write letters home. They check their equipment once again. They talk and smoke and drink mugs of hot tea. And they look out at the sea and the other ships all around them, as far as they can see. Silver balloons sway in the wind above the ships.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-playing-cards-ship-hold-hammocks-kit-bags-lamplight}
Some of them sing old songs together, softly. Some of them pray. Some of them sleep, which is perhaps the best thing to do. Some sit quietly and look at photographs of their wives and children, their sweethearts and their parents. Then they put the photographs back carefully in their pockets, close to their hearts.

[pause]

Down in the ship's galley, the cooks are busy all day long. They make big pots of stew and thick slices of bread and butter. The soldiers queue with their mess tins and eat sitting on the stairs. Not everyone feels like eating. But the cooks make sure there is always something hot.

[long-pause]

On some ships, the officers read out a message from General Eisenhower. It has been printed and handed to every soldier, sailor and airman. It begins like this. You are about to begin the great task toward which we have striven these many months. The eyes of the world are upon you. The hopes and prayers of freedom-loving people everywhere go with you.

[pause]

{img:soldier-reading-printed-message-eisenhower-paper-deck-wind}
And it ends with these words. Good luck, and let us all ask the blessing of Almighty God upon this great and noble work.

[long-pause]

Many soldiers keep the little printed message folded in their pockets. Later, they send it home to their families. Their families keep it safely for the rest of their lives.

[pause]

On the British ships, a message from General Montgomery is read out too. The chaplains hold short services on the decks. The soldiers bow their heads in the wind and say the Lord's Prayer together.

[pause]

{img:ship-chaplain-service-deck-soldiers-heads-bowed-wind-grey-sea}
As the afternoon turns into evening, the ships begin to move. The anchors come up with a long rattle of chains. The engines start, deep and steady. Slowly, one after another, in long lines, the ships sail out of the harbours and the bays. They gather together in the sea, south of the Isle of Wight. The sailors call this meeting place Piccadilly Circus, after a busy square in London. And then the ships turn south, toward France.

[long-pause]

On the cliffs and the beaches of the south coast, people stand and watch them go in the evening light. There are more ships than anyone has ever seen, filling the sea right to the horizon. Nobody says very much as they watch. Mothers hold their children's hands. Old men stand with their caps in their hands. Some of them wave, and some of them pray quietly. And the ships sail slowly away into the evening.

[long-pause]

{img:young-soldier-writing-letter-knee-pencil-tent-evening-light-1944}
In the last days and hours before D-Day, many soldiers sit down to write letters.

[pause]

They write to their mothers and fathers. They write to their wives and children and sweethearts, and to their brothers and sisters. They cannot say where they are, or what is about to happen. The letters will be checked by an officer and posted later, perhaps after the landing.

[long-pause]

So they write about other things, ordinary everyday things. They write about the weather and the food. They write about their friends in the camp, and the jokes they tell. They write about the English countryside and the pretty villages. They write about the cows and the hedges and the birds singing in the woods. And they ask about home. How is the garden, they ask, and the dog, and the neighbours?

[pause]

And they write, as simply as they can, how much they love their families. They write how much they miss them. They write that they will come home soon, when it is all over. And they write that nobody should worry about them.

[pause]

{img:mother-reading-letter-from-son-kitchen-window-light-1944}
Some of them write a second letter as well. They give it to a friend to keep, just in case. In these letters, they say the things that are hard to say out loud. Thank you for everything, they write. I love you. Look after each other, and live a good life.

[long-pause]

Many of these letters have survived until today. Families have kept them for eighty years. They kept them in boxes and drawers and in old family Bibles. Now many of them are in museums and in books. So we can still read them. And we can hear the voices of the young men, writing by lamplight on the night before D-Day.

[pause]

They are often very ordinary letters, and that is what makes them so touching. A young American tells his mother that the English food is terrible. Please send cookies, he writes, lots of them. A young British soldier tells his wife to plant the beans and to kiss the baby for him. A young Canadian tells his girl he has seen the prettiest village in the world. One day, he writes, I will bring you here to see it.

[pause]

{img:bundle-old-letters-ribbon-photographs-medals-family-box-today}
And they end the way letters always do. All my love, they write. God bless you. See you soon. Don't worry about me.

[long-pause]

At home, in America and Canada, in Britain and many other countries, the families read the letters. They read them again and again, until the paper grows soft. They keep them in their pockets, or under their pillows at night. They wait for the next one to come. They listen to the news on the radio every evening. And they wonder where their sons and husbands and brothers are tonight.

[pause]

In a small kitchen somewhere, a mother sits by the window with a letter in her lap. The kettle is singing on the stove, but she does not get up. She reads the last lines once more, slowly. Don't worry about me, Mum, it says. She smiles, and folds the letter carefully, and puts it back in its envelope. Then she gets up and makes the tea.

[long-pause]

{img:wrens-plotting-table-operations-room-southwick-markers-1944}
D-Day could never have happened without the women.

[pause]

In the map rooms at Southwick House, and at the headquarters of the navy and the air force, young women stand around great map tables. They are there day and night, wearing headphones. As the reports come in, they move little markers of ships and planes with long poles. That way, the commanders can see at a glance where everything is.

[long-pause]

They work in shifts through the night, quietly and calmly. They know the greatest secret of the war, which is the time and the place of the landing. And they keep that secret perfectly. Not a single word slips out.

[pause]

Other women work as radio operators and code breakers. They work as clerks and drivers and telephone operators. Some ride motorbikes along the country lanes, day and night, carrying messages. Others pack parachutes for the paratroopers, fold by careful fold. They know that a young man's life will depend on each one.

[pause]

{img:women-packing-parachutes-long-tables-hangar-silk-careful-1944}
The parachute packers work at long tables in big hangars. They lay out the silk smooth and straight, and they check every line and every strap. They work slowly and carefully, without hurrying. Some of them write a little note and tuck it inside the pack. Good luck, the note says, from the girls in the hangar.

[pause]

At the airfields, women check the weather and look after the radios and the radar. And some women fly planes themselves. They deliver new planes from the factories to the airfields, flying alone all over Britain.

[long-pause]

And there are the nurses. On the white hospital ships with their big red crosses, nurses are waiting. In the hospitals along the coast, they are waiting too. They are ready to care for the wounded who will come back across the sea. A few days after D-Day, the first nurses cross to Normandy themselves. They set up hospitals in tents in the fields, among the apple orchards.

[pause]

In factories all over Britain, women have built planes and ships and landing boats. They have built parts of the floating harbours too. They work long shifts in overalls and headscarves, with their sleeves rolled up.

[pause]

{img:nurses-hospital-ship-white-red-cross-deck-ready-1944-calm-sea}
On the farms, women have grown the food that fed the soldiers and the whole country. They ploughed the fields and milked the cows and brought in the harvest. And at home, mothers and wives and grandmothers have kept everything going. They managed through the rationing and the blackouts, the worry and the long waiting. They queued at the shops and mended old clothes and grew vegetables in their gardens. They comforted the children and kept the house warm.

[long-pause]

On the night before D-Day, many of these women work right through the night. They are in the map rooms and at the radios. They are in the hospitals and on the ships. They drink cups of strong tea to stay awake, and they hardly speak. And when the news comes in the morning, they look at each other and smile. They are very tired, and quietly proud. And then they go on working.

[long-pause]

{img:eisenhower-talking-paratroopers-blackened-faces-airfield-evening-1944}
On the evening of the fifth of June, General Eisenhower drives to an airfield in the green countryside west of London. He wants to see the American paratroopers before they take off.

[pause]

They will be among the very first to go. They will fly across the sea in the dark. Then they will jump by parachute behind the coast of Normandy, in the middle of the night. They will land hours before the soldiers arrive on the beaches.

[long-pause]

The paratroopers have darkened their faces with soot and paint, so they will be hard to see at night. Some of them have shaved their heads, leaving a single strip of hair down the middle. They carry so much equipment that some of them can hardly walk. They have parachutes and packs and supplies of every kind. Their friends have to help them climb up into the planes.

[pause]

Eisenhower walks among them without any fuss, in the soft evening light. He has his hands in his pockets, and he talks to them simply, as one man to another. Where are you from, son, he asks? What did you do before the war? Are you ready?

[pause]

{img:general-walking-among-young-paratroopers-c47-aircraft-rows-dusk}
The young men answer him cheerfully. They come from Texas and Pennsylvania, from Michigan and from Kansas, just like him. Before the war they were farmers and clerks, factory workers and students. One of them tells him not to worry. We will take care of everything, sir, he says. They all laugh, and Eisenhower laughs with them. For a few minutes, under the evening sky, they are just men talking about home.

[long-pause]

He stays until the planes start their engines. One after another, they roll out onto the runway and take off into the evening sky. They are heavy and slow as they climb. Black and white stripes have been painted on their wings, so the Allied gunners will know them. They climb and circle and gather into great groups. And then they turn south, toward the sea, as the light fades.

[pause]

Eisenhower stands on the airfield in the dusk and watches them go. He raises his hand in a salute. And his driver, standing quietly beside him, sees that there are tears in his eyes.

[pause]

{img:aircraft-taking-off-twilight-airfield-silhouette-figure-watching}
In his pocket, Eisenhower carries a little note. He wrote it in pencil earlier that day, just in case. It says that the landings have not succeeded, and that the decision was his. And it says that if anyone is to blame, it is him alone.

[long-pause]

He never had to use that note. He put it in his wallet and forgot about it. Weeks later he found it again and showed it to a helper, who kept it safe. Today it is in a museum in Kansas, where Eisenhower grew up. It is a little scrap of paper with a few lines in pencil. It shows a commander who was ready to take all the blame if things went wrong. And it shows a man who would give all the credit to his soldiers if things went right.

[pause]

That night, Eisenhower goes back to his caravan in the woods near Southwick House. It is a simple caravan, with a narrow bed, a little desk and a few books. He tries to sleep, but he cannot. So he lies on his bed and reads cowboy stories to pass the time. He has always liked them, ever since he was a boy in Kansas. Outside, the trees drip with rain, and the planes hum overhead. And he waits for the news.

[long-pause]

{img:french-resistance-family-hidden-radio-attic-listening-candle-1944}
In occupied France, on the evening of the fifth of June, people are listening to the radio in secret. They listen in hundreds of attics and cellars, in barns and farmhouse kitchens.

[pause]

It is forbidden to listen to the BBC from London. People who are caught can get into serious trouble. So they listen with the sound turned down very low. The curtains are drawn tight across the windows. Someone keeps watch at the window, looking out into the dark street. The others lean close to the radio, with their ears almost touching it. They listen through all the crackling and whistling.

[long-pause]

Every evening, after the French news from London, there is a strange part of the programme. It is called the personal messages. A calm voice reads out a long list of sentences that seem to make no sense at all. The carrots are cooked. John has a long moustache. The giraffe has a long neck. The dice are on the carpet.

[pause]

These are secret messages for the French Resistance. These are the men and women all over France who have secretly worked against the occupation for years. Each message is meant for one group in one place. It tells them something important. Perhaps that supplies will be dropped by parachute tonight. Or that they must get ready. Or that the time has finally come.

[pause]

{img:bbc-studio-1944-announcer-microphone-reading-messages-french-service}
In a small studio in London, the announcer sits at a microphone with a sheet of paper. He reads each strange sentence slowly and clearly, twice over. He does not know what most of them mean. He only knows that somewhere in France, someone is listening very carefully.

[pause]

For weeks, the Resistance groups have been waiting for one message in particular.

[long-pause]

On the first of June, the radio read out the first line of a famous French poem. It is a poem about autumn, by a poet called Verlaine. The long sobs of the violins of autumn, it begins.

[pause]

That line meant, get ready. The landing will come within days.

[pause]

And then, on the evening of the fifth of June, at a quarter past nine, the voice reads the second line.

[pause]

{img:old-radio-glowing-dial-dark-kitchen-french-farmhouse-night-listeners}
They wound my heart with a slow and gentle sadness.

[long-pause]

This line means that the landing will begin within two days. It is time to begin your work.

[pause]

All over France that night, the men and women of the Resistance go out quietly into the darkness. They do what they have prepared for, for so long. They cut telephone wires and railway lines. They block the roads with fallen trees. In this way, the enemy will be slowed down when he tries to move his soldiers to the coast. They go on bicycles and on foot, through the fields and the woods in the dark. They move carefully and bravely, and they do not make a sound.

[pause]

{img:resistance-members-bicycles-country-road-night-moonlight-france}
In the farmhouse kitchens and the attics, the families who heard the message look at each other in the lamplight. Some of them smile, and some of them cry. Some say a quiet prayer. They do not know exactly what the message means. But they know that something is coming. After four long years, something is finally coming.

[long-pause]

The poem is a sad autumn poem, about violins and falling leaves and the passing of time. But for the people of France ever since, those two lines have meant something else. They have meant hope, and the end of a long, dark night.

[long-pause]

{img:normandy-farmhouse-orchard-apple-trees-hedgerows-cows-evening-1944}
Now let us go across the sea to Normandy, on the evening of the fifth of June, 1944.

[pause]

Normandy is a green and gentle and beautiful land. It has small fields surrounded by thick, high hedges, growing on banks of earth. Narrow sunken lanes wind between them, shady and cool. There are apple orchards and grey stone farmhouses with steep roofs. There are little villages, each with its own stone church. There are market towns, and the old city of Bayeux with its tall spires. And along the coast there are long sandy beaches and cliffs.

[long-pause]

Let us visit a farming family in a stone farmhouse, a few kilometres from the sea.

[pause]

They have farmed this land for many generations. There is a father and a mother and three children, and a grandmother who remembers the last war. They keep brown and white Normandy cows, with dark patches around their eyes like spectacles. They make butter and cream and soft round cheeses. They grow apples for cider in their orchard. And they keep chickens and pigs, and an old brown horse called Pompon.

[pause]

{img:normandy-farm-kitchen-1944-family-supper-lamp-soup-bread-cider}
The kitchen is the heart of the farmhouse. There is a big wooden table, worn smooth by many years of meals. There is a great open fireplace, with copper pans hanging beside it. Strings of onions hang from the beams, and a cat sleeps on a chair by the fire.

[pause]

For four years, the family has lived under the occupation. There have been foreign soldiers in the village, and rules and curfews and shortages. Life has been hard and full of worry. Their eldest son is far away, a prisoner of war. They have not seen him for four years. Every night, his mother says his name in her prayers.

[long-pause]

Lately, there have been more and more planes flying overhead. The family has been told to stay away from the beaches. They are full of mines and obstacles now. Everyone knows that a landing will come one day. But everyone thinks it will come far to the north, near Calais.

[pause]

Tonight the weather is bad. The wind blows hard from the sea. Rain falls in heavy showers, and the sea is rough. Nobody, the father says, will cross the sea on a night like this.

[pause]

{img:normandy-family-going-to-bed-candle-stairs-old-farmhouse-wind}
They eat their supper in the kitchen, by the light of an oil lamp. There is soup and bread, butter and cheese, and a little cider. The grandmother says her prayers in her chair by the fire. The children go up the creaking wooden stairs to bed. The father goes out with a lantern to check the cows in the barn. He stands for a moment and looks at the sky. He listens to the wind in the orchard. Then he comes in and locks the door. They all go to bed and blow out their candles. And they lie listening to the wind and the rain on the roof, until they fall asleep.

[long-pause]

They do not know that out at sea, beyond the horizon, the greatest fleet in history is coming toward them. It is coming through the rain and the wind. They do not know that in a few hours, their lives and their village and their country will change forever.

[pause]

And in the middle of the night, they will be woken by a sound they have never heard before. It is the roar of hundreds and hundreds of planes, flying low over the farmhouse and the orchard, in the dark.

[long-pause]

{img:night-channel-crossing-fleet-silhouettes-moonlight-clouds-1944}
On the night of the fifth to the sixth of June, the great fleet crosses the sea.

[pause]

From the meeting place south of the Isle of Wight, the ships sail south toward France. They go in long lines, along ten paths through the sea. The paths are marked with little lit buoys, like lamps along a road in the dark.

[long-pause]

In front go the minesweepers. These are small ships that go first, slowly and carefully, through the darkness. They clear safe paths through the hidden mines in the water. Then the great ships behind them can pass safely. It is careful and patient work. The crews do it perfectly, all night long. And the other ships follow behind them.

[pause]

Then come the warships and the troop ships and the landing ships. Tugboats pull the first parts of the floating harbours. There are nearly seven thousand ships, stretching across the sea for many kilometres. They sail without lights, and in silence.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-on-deck-night-crossing-wind-spray-life-belts-quiet-faces}
On the ships, the soldiers try to get some sleep. Some of them manage it, wrapped up in their blankets. Many of them are seasick, because the sea is still rough after the storm. The small flat landing boats roll and toss in the waves. Some soldiers sit on the decks in the dark, with spray blowing in their faces. They look out at the shapes of the other ships. They look at the sea and the clouds. And they watch the moon, coming and going between the clouds.

[long-pause]

Some of them talk together quietly. Some pray, and some think about home. On one ship, someone plays a mouth organ softly in the dark. It is an old tune that everyone knows, and a few men hum along. On another ship, the men sing old songs and hymns together, very quietly. A sailor brings round mugs of hot cocoa, thick and sweet, and the soldiers wrap their cold hands around them.

[pause]

On the bridges of the ships, the captains and the navigators watch the compass and the charts and the sea. They steer carefully along the safe paths, toward the coast of France. One wrong turn could lead them out of the cleared path. So they never take their eyes off the water.

[pause]

{img:ship-bridge-night-navigator-compass-red-light-chart-table-1944}
On the bridge, there is only a dim red light, so the men's eyes stay used to the dark. The navigator leans over his chart table with a pencil and a ruler. He marks their position every few minutes. Beside him, the helmsman holds the big wheel steady. Nobody speaks unless they must.

[pause]

The night is not completely dark. There is a moon in the sky, almost full. Now and then it shines through a break in the clouds. It makes a silver path across the water. For a moment, it lights up the shapes of the ships all around. Then it disappears again behind the clouds.

[long-pause]

The sea is full of sounds in the night. There is the steady throb of the engines. There is the wind, and the waves slapping against the sides of the ships. There is the creaking of the hulls and the ropes. And overhead, more and more, there is the hum of planes flying south in the dark, toward France.

[pause]

And the fleet sails on through the night, slowly, steadily and silently. It sails on toward the dawn.

[long-pause]

{img:english-family-garden-night-looking-up-sky-full-aircraft-moonlight-1944}
In the villages and towns of the south of England, on the night of the fifth of June, people are woken by a sound. It is a sound they will never forget.

[pause]

It begins at around eleven o'clock in the evening. At first it is a low hum, far away. It grows and grows into a great, deep, steady roar. It fills the whole sky. And it goes on for hours and hours.

[long-pause]

It is the sound of planes, hundreds and then thousands of them. They are flying south over the houses and the fields and the coast, toward France. There are transport planes carrying paratroopers. There are planes towing gliders behind them on long ropes. There are bombers and fighters, flying high above the rest. There are more planes than anyone has ever seen or heard in a single night.

[pause]

People get out of bed and go to their windows. They open them and look up into the night. Then they go out into their gardens in their dressing gowns and slippers. They stand on the lawns and in the lanes and on the village greens. And they look up at the sky.

[pause]

{img:night-sky-formations-aircraft-navigation-lights-over-english-village}
In the moonlight, between the clouds, they can see them. There are great groups of planes, coming wave after wave. Their little lights glow red and green and white, in long rows across the sky. They look like moving stars. Their dark shapes pass across the face of the moon. And all of them are flying the same way, south.

[long-pause]

The neighbours come out of their houses too. They stand together at their garden gates and talk in low voices. This is it, they say to each other. It has started. And nobody goes back to bed for a long time.

[pause]

Somebody brings out a pot of tea and some cups on a tray. They stand by the gate and drink it together, still looking up. Nobody quite knows what to say. So they just stand there, side by side, in the summer night.

[pause]

Some people go to the churches, which have been opened in the night. They kneel and pray for the young men flying over in the dark. By the morning, the churches will be full.

[pause]

{img:village-church-open-night-candles-people-kneeling-praying-1944}
In the last few days, all the Allied planes have been painted with broad black and white stripes. That way, the soldiers and sailors below will know them. The painters on the airfields worked day and night to finish in time. There was not enough time to do it neatly. So they used brooms and mops and buckets of paint.

[long-pause]

The children are woken up too. They are brought out into the gardens, wrapped in blankets. Their fathers and mothers hold them up high to see the planes. Remember this, they tell them. And the children did remember. Many of them, when they were very old, still remembered the night of the aeroplanes. They remembered the sound and the moonlight. They remembered their parents' faces looking up. And they remembered the feeling that something enormous was happening.

[pause]

{img:child-in-blanket-held-by-father-garden-gate-looking-up-night-sky}
The roar goes on all night, over England and the sea and the coast of France. At last, toward dawn, it begins to fade away. People go back inside their houses. They put the kettle on again. They switch on the radio and sit down at the kitchen table. And they wait for the news.

[long-pause]

{img:gliders-landing-night-field-beside-canal-bridge-normandy-moonlight}
Shortly after midnight on the sixth of June, the first soldiers arrive in France. They come silently, out of the night sky.

[pause]

They come in gliders, which are big wooden planes without any engines. The gliders are towed across the sea behind bombers, on long ropes. Then, high above the coast, the ropes are let go. And the gliders float down in silence through the dark, to land in the fields.

[long-pause]

Six of these gliders are heading for two bridges near a little village, a few kilometres from the sea. One bridge crosses a canal, and the other crosses a river right beside it. The soldiers must take the bridges quickly and quietly, before they can be damaged. Then the soldiers landing on the beaches in the morning will be able to cross them.

[pause]

The gliders come down in the dark, and the pilots can hardly see the ground. Will they find the right field? They land at sixteen minutes past midnight, in a little field right beside the canal bridge. They land so close and so exactly that the pilots are praised for it ever after. People call it some of the finest flying of the whole war. Within minutes, the bridge is in the hands of the British soldiers. We will leave that brief, sharp moment there, in the dark and the distance.

[pause]

{img:canal-bridge-normandy-cafe-house-beside-dawn-quiet-1944}
Beside the bridge there is a little café. It belongs to a French family called Gondrée. There is a husband and wife, and their two little daughters. They have been hiding in their cellar all night, frightened, listening to the noise outside.

[long-pause]

In the early hours of the morning, there is a knock at the door. The family hears voices outside, speaking English. The father goes up the stairs and opens the door carefully. The soldiers come in, tired and dirty. And then he realises who they are. They are British. They have come at last.

[pause]

The Gondrées' home is one of the very first houses in France to be freed. The father goes out into his garden with a spade. He digs up bottles of champagne that he buried there four years earlier. He had been saving them for exactly this day. He opens them and pours them out for the soldiers. His wife and daughters come up from the cellar. They hug the soldiers and kiss them on both cheeks. Afterwards, their faces are covered with the black paint from the soldiers' faces. Everyone laughs and laughs.

[pause]

{img:cafe-gondree-today-pegasus-bridge-flags-visitors-flowers}
The bridge was later named Pegasus Bridge. It was named after the flying horse that was the badge of the British airborne soldiers.

[long-pause]

The café is still there today, beside the canal, with its flags and photographs and memories. For many years it was run by the youngest daughter. She was the little girl of four who had hidden in the cellar that night. Every year she welcomed the old soldiers and their families back to her café. She poured them drinks and listened to their stories. And the original bridge stands nearby, in the garden of a museum. Visitors can walk across it slowly and remember.

[long-pause]

{img:dawn-june-6-1944-grey-sea-fleet-horizon-normandy-coast-clouds}
On the morning of the sixth of June, the dawn comes grey and pale over the sea. And the people of the Normandy coast look out of their windows. They see something they can hardly believe.

[pause]

The sea is full of ships.

[long-pause]

From one end of the horizon to the other, as far as they can see, there are ships. There are great grey warships and transports, and landing boats of every kind. There are thousands of them, covering the whole sea. It is like a great city that has appeared in the night, out of nowhere.

[pause]

And then the landings begin on the five beaches along the coast, as the tide comes in.

[pause]

{img:landing-craft-heading-to-shore-dawn-distant-beach-smoke-grey}
We will not follow the soldiers onto the beaches. It was a long and very hard day for many of them. And many of them did not come home. We will leave them there, with great respect and gratitude, in the grey morning by the sea. And we will think of them quietly, just for a moment.

[long-pause]

By the evening of the sixth of June, more than a hundred and fifty thousand soldiers have landed in France. They came by sea and by air. The beaches have been taken. The first villages and farms near the coast are free. And the long road to freeing Western Europe has begun.

[pause]

The farming family in Normandy has spent the whole day in the cellar. The grandmother, the children and the parents sat together on old sacks and boxes. They had a candle, a jug of water and some bread and cheese. The grandmother said her prayers, and the mother held the youngest child on her lap. The noise outside went on all day long. In the evening, at last, it grows quieter.

[pause]

Then there is a knock at the door. The father goes up the cellar steps slowly. He opens the door and looks out. And there in the farmyard stand two young soldiers. They are tired and dirty and wet. They speak with a strange accent. And they are asking politely for some water.

[pause]

{img:normandy-farmer-giving-water-soldiers-farmyard-evening-1944}
The father gives them water, and then a jug of cider. The mother brings bread and butter and a whole round cheese. The children come up from the cellar and stare at the soldiers with big eyes. The soldiers smile and give them chocolate from their pockets. One of them shows the children a photograph of his own little sister at home. And the grandmother cries and kisses them both on both cheeks.

[long-pause]

It is the first day of freedom for the family, after four long years. There will still be many hard weeks all around them, in the fields and hedges of Normandy. But tonight, something has changed.

[pause]

That night, the farmer goes out again to see his cows in the barn. They are frightened and restless, and they need milking. He talks to them softly and strokes their necks until they are calm. Then he milks them one by one, by the light of his lantern. Afterwards he stands in the orchard in the dusk, under the apple trees. He looks up at the sky. He listens to the guns, far away now. He thinks of his son, the prisoner, so far from home. And he wonders, now, if perhaps his boy will come home at last.

[long-pause]

{img:bbc-announcer-1944-studio-microphone-morning-news-d-day}
In Britain, on the morning of the sixth of June, everyone is listening to the radio.

[pause]

All night long, they heard the planes going over. Since dawn, there have been rumours everywhere. But people are waiting for their own news, from the BBC.

[long-pause]

At nine thirty-two in the morning, the voice of the BBC announcer comes over the radio. It is calm and clear. It reaches into every home and factory, every office and school in Britain. This is the BBC Home Service, he says. Here is a special bulletin. D-Day has come. Early this morning, the Allies began landing on the north-western coast of Europe.

[pause]

{img:factory-workers-stopped-listening-loudspeaker-d-day-news-1944}
All over Britain, people stop what they are doing and listen. In the factories, the machines are switched off. The workers gather around the loudspeakers in their overalls. In the offices, the clerks put down their pens. In the schools, the teachers stop the lessons, and the children sit very still. In the shops, the shopkeepers and the customers stand together and listen.

[long-pause]

There is no great celebration. People are quiet and serious. They know this is not the end, but only the beginning. And they know that many young men they love are there now, on the beaches and in the fields of France. So they go quietly back to their work. They think of those young men all day long. And they wait for more news.

[pause]

{img:london-street-people-reading-newspapers-headlines-invasion-crowd-1944}
In London, people buy every new newspaper as soon as it comes out. The newspaper boys can hardly keep up. People stand in the streets reading the headlines. They talk quietly to strangers, which Londoners do not usually do. On the buses, people share their newspapers with each other.

[pause]

In America, it is still the middle of the night when the news comes. People are woken up by the news on their radios. Church bells ring out in the dark towns. And people go to the churches in the night, to pray.

[long-pause]

At midday, Mr Churchill tells Parliament that the landings have begun. So far, he says, everything is going according to plan. And in the evening, at nine o'clock, the King speaks to the nation on the radio.

[pause]

The King does not speak of victory. Instead, he asks the people to pray. He asks for one great prayer, rising up from the whole nation, for the young men who are far away. And all over Britain that evening, people bow their heads. In their homes and in the churches, they pray together.

[pause]

{img:family-evening-listening-king-radio-heads-bowed-living-room-1944}
In a small living room, a family sits around the wireless set. The grandmother sits in the armchair, with her knitting still in her lap. The father stands by the fireplace, and the mother sits on the sofa with the children. When the King asks them to pray, they all bow their heads together. The clock on the mantelpiece ticks softly. Outside, the long summer evening is still light.

[pause]

In America too, the President speaks on the radio that evening. Instead of a speech, he reads a prayer that he wrote himself. It is a prayer for the soldiers and for the whole nation. Millions of Americans pray along with him, in their homes. Almighty God, he begins. Our sons, the pride of our nation, have this day set out upon a mighty task.

[long-pause]

{img:mother-standing-garden-gate-1944-looking-down-lane-summer-evening}
In the days and weeks after D-Day, the families at home wait.

[pause]

They wait for news on the radio and in the newspapers every day. They follow the armies across Normandy on maps printed in the papers, with little arrows and lines. They wait for the postman every morning, hoping for a letter. And they try very hard not to worry too much.

[long-pause]

Slowly, the letters begin to come. They are short letters, written in pencil on scraps of paper. They were written in fields and ditches and barns in France. I'm all right, they say. Don't worry. It's very green here, just like at home. The French people are very kind to us. They gave us cider and flowers. All my love.

[pause]

The families read these letters again and again. They pass them round to the neighbours, who read them too. They keep them by their beds at night. And they go to sleep a little more easily.

[pause]

{img:postman-delivering-letter-cottage-door-woman-relief-smiling-1944}
The postman knows how important his job is now. He walks a little faster up the garden paths. When he has a letter from France, he smiles as he hands it over. The women take the letters with shaking hands and read them right there on the doorstep.

[pause]

For some families, the news is sad, and we will not stay there long. But we will think of them gently. That summer, in many homes, there were empty chairs at the table. It was so in Britain and America, in Canada and France, and in Germany too. And there were mothers and wives and children who missed someone for the rest of their lives.

[long-pause]

And life at home goes on, as it must. Women go to work in the factories and on the farms and in the hospitals. Children go to school and play in the streets and the fields. The gardens are dug, and the vegetables grow in neat rows. The hay is cut in the meadows and stacked to dry. The harvest is gathered in, as it always is.

[pause]

Slowly, the south of England empties. The camps in the woods are quiet now. The lanes that were full of tanks and lorries are empty again. Only the marks of the tracks in the grass show they were ever there. The villages and the pubs and the dance halls are quiet too.

[pause]

{img:empty-army-camp-woods-after-june-1944-abandoned-tent-pegs-quiet-trees}
In the woods where the camps were, a few tent pegs are still stuck in the ground. Here and there lies an empty tin, or a forgotten button. The birds are singing again, and the rabbits come out in the evening. Soon the grass grows back, and the woods are just woods again.

[pause]

The villagers miss the soldiers. They miss the cheerful Americans and Canadians and British who lived among them for months. They came to the dances and the churches, and they played with the children. Some children cry because their soldier friends have gone. Tom keeps Joe's badge in his tin, and he looks at it every night.

[long-pause]

The young women who walked along the lanes with the soldiers wait for letters too. Some will marry after the war, and some will never see them again. And some will remember them all their lives. They will remember a summer romance, in a summer of war, among the hedgerows and the blossom.

[long-pause]

{img:mulberry-harbour-arromanches-1944-floating-roadways-ships-unloading}
In the weeks after D-Day, the great floating harbours are towed across the sea. They are put together off the coast of Normandy.

[pause]

The huge concrete blocks, as big as houses, are sunk in a long line out at sea. Old ships are sunk beside them to make a wall. Behind this wall, the sea becomes calm. Inside, there are floating piers and long floating roads. They rise and fall with the tide. They stretch from the beach right out to the ships. So lorries and supplies can drive straight off the ships and onto the land.

[long-pause]

At Arromanches, a little seaside town, the great harbour is built in only a few weeks. For months afterwards, millions of tons of supplies come through it. Hundreds of thousands of soldiers come ashore there too.

[pause]

In the middle of June, a great storm strikes the coast. It is the worst summer storm in forty years, and it lasts for three days. It wrecks one of the two harbours and damages the other badly. For three days, nobody knows if the harbour at Arromanches will survive. But it holds. It is repaired quickly. And it goes on working for many months.

[pause]

{img:normandy-village-liberated-1944-families-flags-soldiers-flowers-street}
Slowly, through the summer, the armies move forward through the hedges and the fields and the orchards of Normandy. It is a long, hard summer. Many towns and villages are damaged, and the people of Normandy go through a great deal. We remember them gently, and with sorrow. But in village after village, the flags come out of hiding. People stand in the streets with flowers and wine and bread. The children run alongside the jeeps, just as Tom did in England.

[long-pause]

At the end of August, the armies break out of Normandy and move quickly across France. On the twenty-fifth of August, Paris is free again. The great bells of Notre-Dame ring out over the city. The people of Paris pour into the streets, singing and dancing. Then Belgium is freed, and part of the Netherlands. And after a long, hard winter, the war in Europe finally ends, in May 1945. It ends eleven months after D-Day.

[pause]

{img:normandy-farm-family-rebuilding-1945-orchard-blossom-son-returned}
And what about the farming family in Normandy, with their orchard and their cows? All through the summer and autumn, they work to repair their farm. The father mends the barn roof and the broken fences. The mother and the children plant new vegetables and look after the animals. The grandmother sits by the fire and knits warm socks for the winter. They wait for news of their son, and the months pass slowly.

[pause]

Their son, the prisoner, comes home at last in the spring of 1945. One evening in May, he walks slowly up the lane to the farmhouse. He is thin and tired, after five long years away. The apple trees are in full blossom, pink and white. His mother sees him from the kitchen window. She drops what she is holding and runs out to meet him. And the whole family cries and laughs and holds him in the farmyard, under the white blossom.

[pause]

That evening, they eat together at the big kitchen table, all of them at last. The grandmother lights a candle and says a prayer of thanks. The old horse Pompon looks in through the stable door. And outside, the blossom falls softly in the orchard, like snow.

[long-pause]

{img:bayeux-1944-cathedral-spires-old-town-streets-flags-liberated-morning}
A few kilometres inland from the beaches lies the old town of Bayeux. On the seventh of June, the day after D-Day, it becomes one of the first towns in France to be freed.

[pause]

And by great good luck, Bayeux is hardly damaged at all. Its old stone houses are still standing. Its narrow streets and its little river with old water mills are still there. And its great cathedral, with its tall grey spires, still rises above the roofs.

[long-pause]

The people of Bayeux come out into the streets in the morning sunshine. They hang out the French flag, which has been hidden away for four years. They give flowers to the soldiers and offer them glasses of cider. The cathedral bells ring out over the town. Old women weep with joy, and children climb onto the jeeps. And everyone can hardly believe that it is true.

[pause]

In the bakery, the baker makes extra bread that day, and he gives some away for free. In the café on the square, the owner brings out his best bottles from the cellar. People who have not smiled for years are laughing in the streets.

[pause]

A week later, on the fourteenth of June, a tall French general arrives in Bayeux. His name is Charles de Gaulle. For four years, he has led the Free French from London. He has spoken to the French people on the radio, giving them hope. Now he sets foot in France again for the first time in four years. In the square of Bayeux, he speaks to the people. They crowd around him and cheer, and many of them cry. France is coming back, they feel.

[pause]

{img:general-de-gaulle-speaking-crowd-bayeux-square-june-1944-flags}
Bayeux is also famous for something much older. It has a great embroidery, about seventy metres long and nearly a thousand years old. It tells the story of another crossing of the sea. In the year 1066, Duke William of Normandy sailed the other way, from Normandy to England. He took his ships and his horses and his knights with him. The embroidery shows it all, stitched in coloured wool on linen. There are ships and horses, castles and feasts. And there is a comet, shining in the sky.

[long-pause]

During the war, the embroidery was hidden away to keep it safe. Now it is back in Bayeux, in its own quiet museum. Visitors walk slowly past it, from one end to the other. It is like a very long picture book. You can see the men building the ships, cutting down trees with their axes. You can see the horses being led on board, and the sails filling with wind. You can even see the cooks preparing a feast, with meat on spits.

[pause]

{img:bayeux-tapestry-panel-ships-horses-sail-embroidered-wool-museum-light}
So in Bayeux, two great sea crossings meet. One was in 1066, from Normandy to England. And one was in 1944, from England to Normandy. They are nearly nine hundred years apart. And the old town has seen them both.

[pause]

Today, the streets of Bayeux are quiet and peaceful. Flowers hang in baskets from the windows. People sit at café tables in the sun, drinking coffee. And the cathedral bells still ring out every hour, over the old grey roofs.

[long-pause]

{img:veterans-old-men-medals-normandy-beach-ceremony-flags-sunshine}
Every year, on the sixth of June, D-Day is remembered.

[pause]

For many years, the men who were there came back to Normandy every June. They came in their berets and blazers, with their medals pinned on their chests. They came to the beaches and the villages and the bridges where they had landed as young men. And they stood quietly and remembered their friends.

[long-pause]

The people of Normandy welcomed them every year. They welcomed them with flowers and flags, with music and with gratitude. The old men and women of the villages had been children in 1944. They came out to shake the veterans' hands and to thank them once again. Teachers brought schoolchildren to meet the old soldiers and hear their stories. Families invited them into their homes for lunch, year after year. Some of these friendships lasted for fifty years or more.

[pause]

On the big anniversaries, there were great ceremonies by the sea. Kings and queens and presidents came. Thousands of people gathered on the cliffs and the sand. And the old soldiers sat in the front rows, in the sunshine, by the sea.

[pause]

{img:children-giving-flowers-veteran-wheelchair-normandy-village-square}
Eighty years after D-Day, a few of the last veterans came back once more. They were very old now, nearly a hundred, and some even older. They came in wheelchairs and with walking sticks, with their families beside them. And they were welcomed and honoured one last time by the whole world. Little children gave them flowers and drawings, and the old men smiled and thanked them.

[long-pause]

Some of them did something rather special. In their nineties, and one at the age of a hundred, they jumped by parachute once again. They were tied to an instructor, and they floated down over the fields of Normandy. These were the same fields where they had landed so long ago. The crowds below cheered and clapped, and many of them cried.

[pause]

Many veterans said the same thing when people called them heroes. I am not a hero, they said. The heroes are the ones who did not come home. I only did my job. And they looked out at the sea and the beach quietly for a long time. And they said nothing more.

[pause]

{img:war-cemetery-normandy-white-headstones-rows-green-grass-roses-quiet}
Above the beaches there are cemeteries. They are quiet, green and peaceful places. There are rows and rows of white headstones in the grass, among roses and trees, looking out over the sea. Gardeners care for them beautifully, every single day. They cut the grass and water the roses and wash the stones. Visitors walk slowly among the rows in silence. They read the names and the ages. Eighteen, nineteen, twenty. They leave flowers and little flags. And sometimes they leave a letter.

[long-pause]

And there are memorials all along the coast. There is one in every village, and at every bridge and crossroads where something happened. They have names and flags and flowers. So that nobody who passes by will ever forget.

[pause]

In the evenings, after the visitors have gone home, the memorials stand quietly in the fading light. The wind blows softly in from the sea. The flags flutter a little and then hang still. And the swallows fly low over the grass, as they did in that summer long ago.

[long-pause]

{img:normandy-beach-today-wide-sand-families-walking-dunes-sea-calm}
Today, the coast of Normandy is peaceful again.

[pause]

The beaches are wide and sandy and very beautiful. In summer, families come to swim and build sandcastles and fly kites. People ride horses along the edge of the sea, where the sand is firm. They sit in the dunes and eat their picnics in the sunshine, as people did before the war. Children run in and out of the waves, shrieking at the cold water.

[long-pause]

But the past is never very far away. In the dunes, there are still the remains of old concrete defences. They are grey and weathered, half buried in sand and grass. At Arromanches, out in the sea, the great concrete blocks of the floating harbour are still there. They are rusty and broken and covered with seaweed. The waves break gently over them, and seagulls sit on top of them. At low tide, you can walk out across the wet sand toward them.

[pause]

In the villages there are museums full of old uniforms and jeeps, photographs and letters. On the walls of the houses there are little signs telling what happened there. And in the windows, the flags of America and Britain and Canada hang beside the flag of France.

[pause]

{img:arromanches-today-mulberry-remains-sea-low-tide-town-church}
The people of Normandy have never forgotten what happened. Every June, the villages are decorated with flags and bunting. Old jeeps drive slowly through the streets in parades. People dress up in the clothes of 1944. They dance in the village squares to old music from the 1940s. In the evening, fireworks go up over the sea. And the church bells ring out across the fields.

[long-pause]

In Portsmouth, across the sea in England, there is a museum about D-Day. It has a great embroidery of its own, eighty-three metres long. It was made about fifty years ago, by a team of patient needlewomen. It tells the story of D-Day in thirty-four panels of coloured cloth and thread. It is like a modern version of the old embroidery in Bayeux.

[pause]

And in the little village in Dorset, the lanes are quiet again. The hedgerows are still white with flowers in June. The swallows still fly low over the pond. An old man walks slowly along the lane with his dog. Sometimes he stops at a certain field gate and leans on it for a while. He smiles, and remembers a tall American with a bar of chocolate.

[pause]

{img:normandy-orchard-today-apple-blossom-old-stone-farmhouse-cows-peaceful}
In the countryside behind the coast of Normandy, the green fields are still there. The high hedges and the sunken lanes are still there. There are apple orchards and grey stone farmhouses, and the brown and white cows. In May, the apple trees blossom pink and white. The cows graze peacefully underneath them. And the farmers make their cider and their cheese, just as their grandparents did.

[long-pause]

And on the sixth of June every year, at dawn, people come to the beaches. They come from France and Britain and America and Canada, and from many other countries. They stand quietly on the sand, as the sun rises over the sea and the tide comes in. Some of them hold flowers, and some hold the hands of their children. Nobody speaks very much. And together, they remember.

[long-pause]

{img:english-coast-night-june-1944-calm-sea-moon-clouds-cliffs-quiet}
So we leave the south of England tonight, on the night of the fifth of June, 1944.

[long-pause]

The harbours are empty now, and the water lies still and dark. The great fleet has gone, out into the night, across the sea. The camps in the woods are quiet, and the owls are calling among the trees. On the airfields, the last planes have taken off. The ground crews stand on the wet grass in the moonlight, looking up.

[pause]

In the villages, people stand in their gardens in their dressing gowns, looking up at a sky full of engines. Husbands and wives hold hands at the garden gate. In a little cottage in Dorset, a boy of ten lies in bed with a tin box beside him. He holds a soldier's badge in his hand, and he listens to the planes.

On a wild coast in the west of Ireland, a young woman climbs the stairs by lamplight. She goes to read the barometer once more, as she does every hour. In a farmhouse in Normandy, a family sleeps under an old roof. The wind blows softly through the apple trees, and the cows shift in the barn. And out at sea, on thousands of ships, young men lie in the dark. They listen to the waves against the hull, and they think of home.
