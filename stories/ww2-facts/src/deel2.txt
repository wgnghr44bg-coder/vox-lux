IMG: soldier-writing-letter-candle | a young 1940s soldier sitting on a wooden crate in a canvas tent at night writing a letter by candlelight, a tin mug of tea beside him, calm face
HEAD: For soldiers far from home, letters were the most precious thing of all.
BODY: Every day of the war, millions of letters crossed the seas. Soldiers wrote home from training camps, from ships, from tents in the desert and from farmhouses in France. Families wrote back with news of the garden, the dog, the neighbours and the baby who had just learned to walk. Letters were read again and again, folded and unfolded until the paper became soft. Many soldiers kept them in a breast pocket, close to the heart. Mail call, when letters were handed out, was one of the happiest moments of the day. Men listened carefully for their names, and those who received nothing would sometimes be given a letter to share by a friend. Soldiers wrote in pencil when there was no ink, on any paper they could find, and sometimes by the light of a single candle. They were not allowed to say exactly where they were, so they wrote about the weather, the food and the people they had met. Many letters ended with the same few words, asking the family to give love to everyone and not to worry. Countless wartime letters still survive today, kept in shoeboxes and attics. They are full of ordinary, loving details, and they show that even in the hardest times, people wanted most of all to stay close to home.

IMG: v-mail-sorting-room | a 1944 American mail sorting room with neat wooden shelves of small V-Mail envelopes, women in uniform calmly sorting letters under warm lamps
HEAD: American soldiers' letters were often shrunk onto film to save space on ships.
BODY: Letters are surprisingly heavy, and in a world war there were enormous numbers of them. So the United States used a clever system called V-Mail, short for Victory Mail. It was based on a British idea called the Airgraph. A soldier or family member wrote a letter on a special single sheet of paper. The letters were then photographed and copied onto rolls of microfilm. A single small roll could hold thousands of letters, and a few bags of film could carry what would otherwise have filled dozens of sacks. The film was flown or shipped across the ocean, and at the other end each letter was printed out again as a small photograph, about a quarter of the size of the original page. Then it was folded into a little envelope and delivered. This saved a great deal of space for food, fuel and equipment on ships and planes. The writing on V-Mail could be quite small to read, so people learned to write clearly and in large letters. Some drew little pictures in the corners, of houses or Christmas trees or cartoon dogs. Ordinary letters were still sent too, but V-Mail became a familiar part of the war. Today, old V-Mail letters can still be found in family papers, small and neat, like tiny windows into another time.

IMG: field-kitchen-morning | a 1944 army field kitchen under canvas at dawn, cooks in shirtsleeves stirring large steaming pots, soldiers queuing calmly with metal mess tins, mist over a field
HEAD: Feeding an army was a gigantic daily task.
BODY: An army, people often said, marches on its stomach. Every day, millions of soldiers needed breakfast, lunch and supper, often far away from any shop or farm. So armies built large supply systems. Behind the lines were field kitchens, set up in tents, barns or the backs of lorries, with huge pots and ovens. Cooks rose long before dawn to make porridge, powdered eggs, bread, stews and gallons of tea or coffee. Soldiers queued with their metal mess tins and ate wherever they could find a spot, sitting on crates, walls or the grass. When fresh food could not reach them, soldiers carried tinned rations. In the British army there were tins of corned beef, known as bully beef, and hard biscuits that were so dry they were sometimes soaked in tea before eating. American soldiers had C-rations, small tins of meat and beans, stew or hash, along with crackers, sugar and instant coffee. Each army had its favourite and least favourite dishes, and soldiers loved to grumble about the food, which was often a sign that morale was fine. When a hot meal arrived after a long cold day, it was a moment of real comfort. Many veterans remembered a particular cup of tea or a bowl of stew for the rest of their lives.

IMG: d-ration-chocolate-bar | close-up of a 1940s soldier's hands holding a plain brown wartime chocolate bar wrapped in waxed paper, a canteen and a folded map on a wooden table in lamplight
HEAD: An American emergency chocolate bar was designed to taste only a little better than a boiled potato.
BODY: Before the war, the United States Army asked the Hershey chocolate company to make a special bar for emergencies. It had to be small enough to fit in a pocket, it had to give a lot of energy, and it had to survive heat without melting. There was one more unusual request. It should not taste too good. The army worried that if the chocolate was delicious, soldiers would eat it as a snack instead of saving it for when they really needed it. So the officer in charge asked for a taste only a little better than a boiled potato. The result was the D ration bar, made from chocolate, sugar, cocoa butter, skimmed milk powder and oat flour. It was thick, very hard and rather bitter. Many soldiers had to shave pieces off with a knife, and some called it Hitler's secret weapon, as a joke, because of what it did to their stomachs. Later, a tastier tropical bar was made that could survive the heat of the Pacific. Millions of these bars were produced during the war. They were handed out to soldiers, airmen and sailors, and many were given away to children in liberated towns, who had not tasted chocolate for years and thought it was the best thing in the world.

IMG: tea-brew-up-desert | British soldiers in the North African desert at sunset sitting around a small tin stove brewing tea in a blackened can, a lorry parked nearby, long shadows on the sand
HEAD: For British soldiers, a brew of tea was the answer to almost everything.
BODY: Wherever British and Commonwealth soldiers went, they made tea. In the desert of North Africa, they made it in a simple stove called a Benghazi burner. This was an old petrol tin, cut in half and filled with sand. A little petrol was poured onto the sand and lit, and a can of water was set on top to boil. Within minutes, there was tea, strong and sweet, often with tinned milk. Soldiers stopped for a brew-up at almost every pause, and it was a moment of calm in the middle of long days. Tea was so important that, during the war, the British government bought up huge amounts of the world's supply to make sure it never ran out. Tea was rationed at home, but soldiers usually had plenty. Even British tanks were later fitted with a small electric water boiler inside, so that the crew could make tea without climbing out. The habit spread to the ships and airfields too. In naval canteens and in the huts where pilots waited for their next flight, there was nearly always a big brown pot on the go. For many soldiers far from home, the taste of a hot cup of tea was a small piece of their own kitchen, carried with them all the way across the world.

IMG: dog-tags-on-table | two oval metal identity tags on a beaded chain resting on a wooden table beside a folded letter and an old photograph, warm evening window light
HEAD: Every soldier wore small metal identity tags, known as dog tags.
BODY: Most soldiers in the Second World War wore identity discs around their neck. In the American army, these were small oblong tags made of metal, hung on a thin beaded chain. They were nicknamed dog tags, a name that has stayed ever since. Each tag was stamped with the soldier's name, a service number, a blood type and sometimes a religion and the name of a person to contact. Soldiers usually wore two identical tags, one on a long chain and one on a short one attached to it. British soldiers had a pair of discs made of compressed fibre, one green and one red, with similar details. These small objects had a serious purpose, but over time they also became something personal. Soldiers knew their service numbers by heart for the rest of their lives, and many veterans could recite them seventy years later without a moment's thought. Some men kept their tags after the war, in a drawer or a small box, and later passed them on to their children or grandchildren. Even today, now and then, an old tag is found by a farmer in a field in France or Belgium, or by a diver near an island in the Pacific. When that happens, people often work hard to find the family and send the tag back home.

IMG: sailors-hammocks-mess-deck | the lower deck of a 1940s warship at night with sailors asleep in canvas hammocks slung between pipes, a single dim red lamp, the gentle sway of the ship
HEAD: Many sailors slept in canvas hammocks that swayed gently with the ship.
BODY: On many warships of the Second World War, especially in the British Royal Navy, sailors still slept in hammocks, much as their great-grandfathers had done in the age of sail. Each man had his own canvas hammock, which he slung each evening from hooks or bars in the mess deck, the same space where he ate his meals during the day. In the morning, the hammocks were rolled up tightly and stowed away, so that the space could be used again. Hammocks had one great advantage at sea. As the ship rolled from side to side, the hammock stayed level, gently swinging, so the sailor inside hardly felt the movement. Many men said they slept better in a hammock than in any bed they had ever known. The mess decks were crowded, warm and full of sounds: the hum of engines, the creak of the hull, the breathing and murmuring of dozens of men. A dim red or blue light usually burned at night, so that men coming on and off watch could find their way without waking everyone. Sailors on watch would gently shake the next man awake when it was his turn. On some newer ships, bunks were fitted instead, but the hammock remained a familiar part of navy life all through the war.

IMG: navy-rum-ration-tot | a smiling 1940s Royal Navy sailor on deck at sunset receiving a small measure of rum from a wooden tub with brass letters, the calm sea behind
HEAD: British sailors still received a daily tot of rum, a tradition that lasted until 1970.
BODY: In the Royal Navy, an old tradition carried on all through the war. Every day, at around midday, sailors over the age of twenty who wished to have it were given a ration of rum. It was called the tot. The rum was kept in a large oak tub, often bound with brass, and on many ships the words The King, God bless him were written on the side in brass letters. At a pipe called Up Spirits, a petty officer measured out the rum carefully. For ordinary sailors it was usually mixed with water, which was known as grog. The custom dated back hundreds of years, to the days when fresh water on long voyages could go stale. Sailors had a whole language around the tot. A small sip offered to a friend as thanks was called sippers, and a larger gulp was called gulpers. Some men gave their tot as payment for small favours, like mending a shirt or taking a watch. Sailors who chose not to drink it received a small amount of money instead. The tradition lasted until the thirty-first of July 1970, a day still remembered in the navy as Black Tot Day. On that last evening, sailors around the world drank their final tot, and some held mock funerals for the old custom.

IMG: submarine-crew-quarters | the narrow interior of a 1940s submarine with bunks stacked close to pipes and valves, a sailor reading quietly in a bunk under a small lamp, warm dim light
HEAD: In submarines, men often shared a bunk with a shipmate on a different watch.
BODY: Life on a submarine was very cramped. The vessels were long and narrow, and almost every space was filled with machinery, pipes, torpedoes and supplies. Crews of around forty to sixty men lived inside for weeks at a time. There were not enough bunks for everyone, so many submariners practised something called hot bunking. When one man finished his watch and climbed out of his bunk, another man who was going off duty climbed in. The blanket was often still warm. Some men slept on top of the torpedoes, or in small spaces between pipes. Fresh food was stored wherever it would fit, and at the start of a patrol there might be loaves of bread hanging in nets, and boxes of tins under the floor plates. Water was precious, so washing was kept to a minimum, and after a few weeks everyone smelled of diesel oil and cooking. Yet many submariners loved the life. Crews were small and close, and officers and men knew each other well. When the submarine dived deep, the noise of the sea above disappeared, and the inside became very quiet, with only the hum of the electric motors. Men read paperbacks, played cards, wrote letters and listened to records on a small gramophone, deep beneath the waves.

IMG: pilots-waiting-dispersal-hut | RAF pilots in 1940 flying jackets resting in deckchairs outside a wooden hut on a grassy airfield at evening, one reading a book, one asleep, a dog lying on the grass
HEAD: Much of a fighter pilot's day was spent waiting.
BODY: In films, pilots seem to be always flying. But in reality, many hours were spent simply waiting. In the summer of 1940, during the Battle of Britain, RAF fighter pilots sat near their aircraft at small wooden huts called dispersal huts, on the edge of grassy airfields. They wore their flying clothes and their life jackets, known as Mae Wests, and they waited for the telephone to ring. While they waited, they did all kinds of ordinary things. They read books and newspapers, played chess, draughts and cards, wrote letters and dozed in old armchairs or deckchairs on the grass. Some had dogs, which lay in the sun beside them. A gramophone might be playing a dance tune. Tea came round in big pots. The telephone could ring at any moment, and every man in the hut knew the sound of it. When it rang, the orderly would answer and, if the call was to scramble, everyone ran for the aircraft. But very often it rang for something quite ordinary, like a message about lunch. Many pilots later said that the waiting was the hardest part. They remembered the warm summer evenings most of all, when the flying was over for the day and the sky over the fields turned pink and gold.

IMG: navigator-astrodome-stars | a 1940s aircraft navigator looking up through a clear glass dome at a sky full of stars, holding a sextant, faint blue instrument light inside the cabin
HEAD: Some aircraft navigators found their way by the stars, through a small glass dome.
BODY: Long flights at night, over sea or over darkened countryside, needed careful navigation. Radio aids were used when they could be, but they did not always work, and so many navigators still used the oldest method of all. They looked at the stars. Large aircraft often had a small transparent dome on the top of the fuselage, called an astrodome. The navigator would stand beneath it with an instrument called a sextant, rather like those used by sailors for centuries. He measured the angle of a known star above the horizon, noted the exact time and then used books of tables to work out where the aircraft was. It took practice, patience and a steady hand, especially in a plane that was bumping through the air. Navigators learned the names of dozens of stars: Vega, Capella, Arcturus, Polaris and many others. Some said that they came to think of the stars as old friends, because they were always there, in the same places, night after night. Navigation by the stars was also taught at training schools far from the war, in Canada, South Africa and Australia, where young men spent quiet evenings learning the shape of the sky. Many of them carried that knowledge for the rest of their lives, and could still name the stars above their gardens as old men.

IMG: wojtek-bear-soldiers | a friendly brown bear sitting among smiling 1940s Polish soldiers at a calm army camp in the evening, one soldier offering a tin mug, tents and hills behind
HEAD: A Polish army unit had a brown bear called Wojtek, who carried crates of supplies.
BODY: In 1942, in Iran, a group of Polish soldiers met a young boy with a small bear cub. The soldiers bought the cub, and they named him Wojtek, a Polish name that is often said to mean something like happy warrior. At first, the little bear was fed condensed milk from an old vodka bottle. He grew up among the soldiers of a supply company, and they treated him almost as one of their own. Wojtek travelled with them through Iraq, Syria, Palestine and Egypt. He loved to wrestle with the men, to splash in water and to ride in the cab of their lorries. He also became famous for drinking beer and, apparently, for eating cigarettes. When the unit sailed to Italy in 1944, animals were not allowed on the ship, so Wojtek was officially enlisted as a private soldier, with his own name and number. In Italy, soldiers later said that he helped carry heavy boxes of supplies, copying what he saw the men doing. The company even changed its badge to show a bear carrying a shell. After the war, Wojtek went to live at Edinburgh Zoo in Scotland, where he spent the rest of his life. Old Polish soldiers sometimes came to visit him, and they said that he seemed to recognise the sound of their language.

IMG: uso-show-stage | a 1940s USO show on a simple wooden stage outdoors at dusk, a singer in an evening dress at a microphone, a small band, rows of soldiers sitting on the grass smiling
HEAD: Famous singers and comedians travelled the world to entertain the troops.
BODY: To keep up spirits, entertainers went wherever soldiers were stationed. In America, an organisation called the USO, the United Service Organizations, arranged shows in camps and bases at home and abroad. Stars like Bob Hope, Marlene Dietrich and the Andrews Sisters performed on simple wooden stages, on the backs of lorries and on the decks of ships. Bob Hope became so closely linked with these shows that he kept doing them for decades after the war. In Britain, a similar organisation called ENSA sent comedians, dancers, musicians and actors to entertain the forces. Its standards were sometimes a little uneven, and soldiers joked that the letters stood for Every Night Something Awful. But many of its performers were very good, and the shows were greatly loved. Singers like Vera Lynn travelled to the jungles of Burma to sing for soldiers far from home. Her songs, like We'll Meet Again and The White Cliffs of Dover, became the sound of the war for millions. The USO also ran clubs where servicemen could go for a cup of coffee, a dance or a chat with friendly volunteers. For a few hours, men far from home could laugh, sing along and forget, at least for one evening, where they were.

IMG: christmas-at-the-front | soldiers in 1944 winter coats sitting quietly around a small candle-lit Christmas tree inside a stone farmhouse, snow outside the window, mugs of tea and paper decorations
HEAD: Even far from home, soldiers found ways to celebrate Christmas.
BODY: Every December of the war, soldiers in camps, ships and outposts around the world tried to make Christmas feel a little like home. They cut small trees or branches and decorated them with whatever they could find: tin foil from cigarette packets, paper chains, ration labels, even empty cartridge cases polished until they shone. Cooks did their best to make a special meal, and in many armies the officers served the men their Christmas dinner, an old tradition. When it was possible, there was turkey or chicken, tinned Christmas pudding and an extra ration of beer or rum. Parcels from home arrived in the weeks before, full of knitted socks, cakes, sweets, soap and letters. In 1944, the song I'll Be Home for Christmas, recorded by Bing Crosby the year before, was one of the most requested songs on forces radio. In some places, carols were sung by candlelight in old barns or churches. Men who did not usually sing joined in quietly. On Christmas Day, many letters were written, full of memories of past Christmases and hopes for the next. For a few hours, in many corners of the world, the war seemed to pause, and men sat together in the warmth, talking about home and about the people they loved.
