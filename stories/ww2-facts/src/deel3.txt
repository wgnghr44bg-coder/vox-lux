IMG: women-factory-rivets | American women in 1943 work overalls and headscarves working calmly on the riveted metal body of an aircraft in a large factory hall, warm evening light through high windows
HEAD: Millions of women went to work in factories, shipyards and aircraft plants.
BODY: As men left for the forces, someone had to build the ships, aircraft, lorries and tools that the war needed. In America, around six million women joined the workforce during the war. Many of them had never worked outside the home before. They learned to rivet, weld, drill and operate huge machines. They built bombers in great factory halls in California and Michigan, and cargo ships in the shipyards of the coast. A song from 1942 called Rosie the Riveter gave a name to all of them, and a poster of a woman in a red spotted headscarf, rolling up her sleeve, later became one of the best known images of the war, though it was not very famous at the time. In Britain, women were even called up for war work from 1941, one of the first countries to do so. They worked in munitions factories, on the railways, on buses as conductors, and in engineering works. Many wore trousers for the first time, which caused some surprise in the streets. The work was hard and the shifts were long, but many women later said that they had never felt so useful, or so independent. After the war, many returned home. But something had changed, and their daughters grew up knowing what their mothers had been able to do.

IMG: land-girls-harvest | young women of the 1940s Women's Land Army in green jumpers and breeches stacking sheaves of wheat in a golden English field at sunset, a horse and cart nearby
HEAD: Young women of the Land Army kept Britain's farms working.
BODY: With so many farm workers away in the forces, Britain needed help to grow food. So the Women's Land Army was formed again, as it had been in the First World War. By 1944, around eighty thousand women had joined. They were known as Land Girls. Many came from towns and cities and had never milked a cow or driven a tractor. They were given a uniform of green jumpers, brown breeches, thick socks and sturdy boots, and they were sent to farms all over the country. The work was very hard. They rose before dawn to milk the cows, mucked out stables, ploughed fields, harvested crops, picked potatoes and looked after sheep and pigs. Some joined the Women's Timber Corps instead, and they were called Lumberjills. They cut down trees, measured timber and worked in sawmills, providing wood for pit props, telegraph poles and packing crates. Many Land Girls lived in hostels together, while others lodged on the farms with the farming family. There were dances in village halls, bicycle rides along country lanes and long summer evenings in the fields. Many of these women later said that the Land Army years were the best of their lives. They had grown stronger, made lifelong friends and come to love the open countryside.

IMG: princess-elizabeth-mechanic | a young woman in a 1945 British army ATS uniform and overalls calmly working on the engine of an army truck in a garage, tools laid out neatly, soft daylight
HEAD: The young Princess Elizabeth trained as a lorry driver and mechanic in 1945.
BODY: In early 1945, the future Queen Elizabeth the Second, then a princess of eighteen, joined the Auxiliary Territorial Service, the women's branch of the British army. She was the first female member of the royal family to serve full time in the armed forces. She was given the number two three zero eight seven three, and she trained as a driver and mechanic at a camp in Surrey. She learned how to strip down and rebuild an engine, change a wheel, read a map and drive a heavy army lorry. Photographs from the time show her in overalls, with her sleeves rolled up and her hands on an engine. Newspapers loved the story, and her parents, the King and Queen, came to visit and watched her at work with oily hands. She was later given the rank of junior commander. The princess clearly enjoyed the training and the chance to be treated, at least partly, like the other young women. For the rest of her life she kept an interest in engines and cars, and she often drove herself around her estates. She was also the last surviving head of state to have served in uniform during the Second World War, a quiet link to those years that lasted until 2022.

IMG: bletchley-park-hut-night | the interior of a wooden hut at Bletchley Park at night in 1943, young women and men working quietly at desks covered with papers and index cards, green lampshades glowing
HEAD: At a country house called Bletchley Park, thousands of people worked in secret to break codes.
BODY: About fifty miles north west of London lies a Victorian mansion with a lake, a lawn and a curious mixture of building styles. During the war, this place, Bletchley Park, became the centre of British code breaking. Around ten thousand people worked there by 1945, and roughly three quarters of them were women. They worked in shifts, around the clock, in plain wooden huts and brick blocks scattered around the grounds. Each hut had a number, and people often referred to their work simply as Hut Six or Hut Eight. The staff included mathematicians, linguists, chess champions, crossword enthusiasts, clerks and young women who had just left school. Some were recruited after solving a particularly difficult crossword puzzle in a newspaper. Almost all of them signed the Official Secrets Act, and they were told never to speak about their work. Remarkably, most kept that promise for decades. Husbands and wives sometimes discovered only many years later that they had both worked there. In their free time, the staff played rounders on the lawn, skated on the frozen lake in winter, put on plays and formed a music society. Today Bletchley Park is a museum, and the huts have been restored. You can walk through them and see the desks, the lamps and the telephones, just as they were.

IMG: enigma-machine-desk | a 1940s Enigma cipher machine with its keyboard and lamp board in an open wooden box on a desk, a pencil and notebook beside it, warm lamplight
HEAD: The German Enigma machine could set its code in more ways than there are stars in many galaxies.
BODY: The Enigma machine looked rather like a typewriter in a wooden box. When the operator pressed a key, a small lamp lit up on a board above the keys, showing a different letter. Inside the machine, a set of wheels called rotors turned with every key press, so the same letter was turned into something different each time. There was also a plugboard at the front, with cables that swapped pairs of letters. The number of possible settings was enormous, around one hundred and fifty million million million for the commonly used army version. The settings were changed every day, so even if someone solved one day's code, they had to start again the next morning. Polish mathematicians were the first to make real progress against Enigma, in the 1930s. In 1939, just before the war, they shared their work with British and French code breakers. At Bletchley Park, the work continued. People like Alan Turing and Gordon Welchman designed electro-mechanical machines called Bombes, which tested possible settings very quickly. Code breakers also looked for small human habits, like operators who always began messages with the same words, or who sent a weather report at the same time every morning. Those little patterns, repeated day after day, were often the key that opened the lock.

IMG: colossus-computer-room | a 1944 room filled with tall racks of glowing valves and paper tape wheels of the Colossus machine, two women operators in cardigans calmly adjusting switches, warm orange glow
HEAD: One of the world's first electronic computers was built in secret to read wartime messages.
BODY: Not all German codes used the Enigma machine. The very highest commands used another, even more complex system, which the British code breakers nicknamed Tunny. To help break it, a post office engineer named Tommy Flowers designed a remarkable machine. It was called Colossus, and the first one began work at Bletchley Park in early 1944. Colossus was huge. It filled a room and used around two and a half thousand glowing glass valves, the kind of tubes once found in old radios. Paper tape with punched holes ran through it at great speed, about five thousand characters a second, on a set of wheels. Many people at the time doubted that a machine with so many valves could work reliably, but Flowers knew from his telephone work that valves rarely failed if they were left switched on. He was right. By the end of the war, ten Colossus machines were working. Most of the operators were young women from the Women's Royal Naval Service, known as Wrens. After the war, the machines were mostly taken apart and the plans were destroyed, and for decades almost no one knew they had existed. Today, a working rebuild of Colossus stands in a museum at Bletchley, softly humming, with its valves glowing orange.

IMG: radar-station-cliffs | tall lattice radar masts of a 1940 Chain Home station standing on green English cliffs above a calm sea at sunset, a small brick hut at their base
HEAD: A chain of tall radio masts along the coast could see aircraft long before anyone could hear them.
BODY: In the 1930s, a Scottish scientist named Robert Watson-Watt and his colleagues worked on a new idea. Radio waves, they found, would bounce off a distant aircraft, and the faint echo could be picked up and measured. This became known as radar, though in Britain at first it was called RDF. By the start of the war, a line of radar stations called Chain Home stood along the east and south coasts of England. Each station had tall steel masts, some of them over three hundred feet high, like the masts of enormous ships. Inside small huts at the base, operators watched a glowing line on a screen. When aircraft were approaching, small bumps appeared on the line. Skilled operators, many of them young women of the Women's Auxiliary Air Force, could estimate how far away the aircraft were, how many there might be and roughly how high they were flying. They passed this information by telephone to a central operations room, where it was shown on a large table map with coloured markers. This system gave precious time to prepare. Radar was later made small enough to fit inside aircraft and ships. After the war, the same technology helped build air traffic control, weather forecasting and even the microwave oven.

IMG: penicillin-laboratory-flasks | a 1940s laboratory bench with rows of glass flasks and bottles filled with pale golden liquid, a scientist in a white coat writing notes under a soft lamp
HEAD: Penicillin was first made in large amounts during the war, partly in milk bottles and bedpans.
BODY: Penicillin had been discovered by Alexander Fleming in London in 1928, when he noticed that a mould had stopped bacteria growing on a dish. But for years it was very hard to make enough of it to be useful. At the start of the war, a team at Oxford University, led by Howard Florey and Ernst Chain, worked to purify it. They needed huge amounts of mould juice, so they grew it in every container they could find: milk churns, baths, biscuit tins and even hospital bedpans. They then designed special ceramic vessels, and young women known as the penicillin girls tended them day after day. The results were remarkable, but Britain could not produce enough during the war. So in 1941, Florey travelled to America. There, scientists in a laboratory in Peoria, Illinois, worked on growing penicillin in large tanks. Famously, one of the best strains of mould was found on a cantaloupe melon from a local market. By 1944, American factories were producing enough penicillin to treat many thousands of people. It saved countless lives during the war and has saved many millions since. In 1945, Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the Nobel Prize for medicine, for what has often been called one of the greatest discoveries in the history of science.

IMG: leg-makeup-seam-line | a young woman in a 1943 bedroom carefully drawing a thin dark line down the back of her leg with an eyebrow pencil, a friend helping, a dressing table and lamp nearby
HEAD: When stockings ran out, women painted their legs and drew the seam on with a pencil.
BODY: In the years before the war, nylon stockings had just arrived in the shops, and they were extremely popular. When they first went on sale in America in 1940, millions of pairs were sold in a few days. But soon nylon was needed for parachutes, ropes and other wartime uses, and stockings almost disappeared. In Britain, silk and nylon stockings became very rare too, and clothing coupons made them expensive. So women found a clever answer. They painted their legs. Some used special leg make-up sold in bottles. Others used gravy browning, cold tea or a little cocoa mixed with water, though gravy browning had one drawback, because it was said to attract dogs. The finishing touch was the seam. Stockings of the time had a straight seam running up the back of the leg, so women drew a line with an eyebrow pencil, often with a friend's help, as it was difficult to keep straight. Beauty salons even offered a leg painting service. When nylon stockings finally came back after the war, there were long queues outside shops in America, sometimes thousands of people, in what newspapers called the nylon riots. For many women, a new pair of stockings was a small sign that ordinary life was returning at last.

IMG: scrap-metal-drive | children in a 1940s English street proudly pushing a small cart piled with old saucepans, kettles and iron railings, neighbours watching from doorsteps, evening light
HEAD: People gave away their pots, pans and iron railings to help build aircraft and ships.
BODY: Metal was needed in huge amounts for the war. In 1940, Lord Beaverbrook, the minister in charge of aircraft production, made an appeal. He asked housewives to give their aluminium saucepans, kettles and other kitchen items, which could be melted down to build Spitfires and Hurricanes. People responded with great enthusiasm. Piles of pots and pans appeared in town squares, and children went from door to door with handcarts. Later, iron railings were taken down from parks, squares and the fronts of houses all over Britain. Some historians now believe that much of the iron collected was never actually used, because there was more than the factories needed. But the collections made people feel that they were helping, and they gave everyone something they could do. Even today, if you walk through an old British town, you can sometimes see small stumps of metal along the top of a low garden wall, where railings were cut off in the war and never replaced. There were also salvage drives for paper, bones, rags and rubber. Children were sometimes given badges for collecting, and they took the job very seriously. Some schools had competitions to see who could gather the most. Very little was thrown away in those years, because almost everything could be used again.

IMG: victory-garden-american-backyard | an American backyard victory garden in 1943 at sunset, a mother and two children picking tomatoes and beans, a white picket fence, a wooden house behind
HEAD: In America, around twenty million victory gardens were planted.
BODY: Just as in Britain, people in the United States were encouraged to grow their own food. These gardens were called victory gardens, and they appeared everywhere: in back yards, on empty city lots, on rooftops, in school grounds and in public parks. By 1944, there were around twenty million of them, and they produced a large share of all the fresh vegetables eaten in the country. Families grew tomatoes, beans, carrots, lettuce, squash and corn. Seed companies, magazines and government leaflets gave advice on what to plant and when. Even Eleanor Roosevelt, the wife of the President, had a victory garden planted on the lawn of the White House. In the evenings and at weekends, whole neighbourhoods were out in their gardens. People shared tools, seeds and advice over the fence. Children helped with watering and weeding, and many learned to grow things for the first time. In late summer, kitchens were busy with home canning. Families filled glass jars with tomatoes, peaches and pickles, and stored them on cellar shelves for the winter. Many older Americans later remembered the smell of those kitchens, warm and full of steam, with rows of glowing jars cooling on the table and the sound of the lids popping softly as they sealed.

IMG: american-ration-stamps-kitchen | a 1943 American kitchen table with a small ration stamp book, red and blue tokens, a coffee pot and a sugar bowl, a mother writing a shopping list in lamplight
HEAD: American families used ration stamps and little red and blue tokens to buy food.
BODY: Rationing came to the United States in 1942. Sugar was among the first foods to be rationed, followed by coffee, meat, butter, cheese, canned foods and more. Every American was given a ration book, full of small stamps. Some foods had a points value, and the stamps were coloured. Red stamps were used for meat, fats and cheese, and blue stamps for canned and processed foods. Since stamps were often worth more than the price of a small item, the government also made tiny tokens out of red and blue fibre, about the size of a small coin. These were used as change, so that shoppers did not lose any points. Coffee was rationed for several months, and some people reused their coffee grounds, making a weaker second pot. Recipes appeared in magazines for cakes made with less sugar and less butter, sweetened with honey, syrup or molasses. Petrol was rationed too, mainly to save rubber from car tyres, and cars displayed small stickers in the windscreen showing how much fuel they were allowed. Most families had an A sticker, which allowed only a few gallons a week. Some old ration books and tokens can still be found today, tucked inside old cookbooks, a small reminder of a time when a cup of coffee was something to save and savour.

IMG: victory-speed-sign-road | a quiet 1942 American country road at dusk with a single old car driving slowly past a painted roadside sign reading only a speed number, fields and telegraph poles beside the road
HEAD: To save rubber, the whole of America was asked to drive at only thirty-five miles an hour.
BODY: In the early months of 1942, America lost access to much of the world's natural rubber, which came from plantations in Southeast Asia. Rubber was needed for tyres, hoses, boots and countless other things, so it suddenly became very precious. The government introduced a national speed limit of thirty-five miles an hour, because driving slowly made tyres last much longer. It was called the Victory Speed. Roads became quieter and slower. Families went on fewer long drives and took the bus, the train or their bicycles instead. Carpooling was encouraged, with posters suggesting that when you drove alone, you were wasting precious rubber. Rubber drives were held all over the country. People gave old tyres, garden hoses, rubber shoes, hot water bottles and even rubber bath mats. Gas stations collected the donations, and children brought in whatever they could find. Meanwhile, scientists and factories worked hard to make synthetic rubber from oil and other materials. By the end of the war, America was producing hundreds of thousands of tons of it every year. The speed limit was lifted in 1945. But for those few years, the roads were quieter than they had been for a long time, and many people rediscovered the pleasure of walking to their neighbours' houses.

IMG: jeep-countryside-evening | a small 1940s military jeep parked on a quiet country lane at sunset beside a stone wall, a soldier leaning on the bonnet reading a map, hedges and fields behind
HEAD: The jeep was designed in just a few weeks and became one of the most famous vehicles in history.
BODY: In the summer of 1940, the United States Army asked car companies to design a small, light four-wheel-drive vehicle. The requirements were strict and the deadline was very short. A small company called American Bantam, working with a designer named Karl Probst, drew up plans in only a couple of days and built the first working vehicle in about seven weeks. The army liked it, but asked two larger companies, Willys-Overland and Ford, to build versions too. In the end, the Willys design became the standard, and around six hundred and forty thousand were made during the war. No one is completely sure where the name jeep came from. Some say it came from the letters G P, for general purpose. Others think it came from a character called Eugene the Jeep in the Popeye cartoons, a small magical creature who could go anywhere. The jeep certainly could go almost anywhere. It carried officers, messages, wounded men, supplies and even small guns. It drove through mud, sand, snow and rivers. Soldiers loved it, and the journalist Ernie Pyle wrote that it was as faithful as a dog and as strong as a mule. After the war, many jeeps were sold to farmers around the world, and some are still driving on country lanes today.

IMG: candy-shop-1940s-jar | a 1940s American corner shop counter with glass jars of colourful small sweets and chocolates, a young boy in a cap counting coins, warm evening shop light
HEAD: The little chocolate sweets called M and M's were first made for soldiers.
BODY: The story is often told that Forrest Mars, the son of the founder of the Mars company, saw soldiers in the Spanish Civil War eating small pellets of chocolate covered in a hard sugar shell. The shell stopped the chocolate from melting in the heat. Back in America, he started a company with Bruce Murrie, the son of the president of Hershey, and in 1941 they began making their own version. They called them M and M's, after their two surnames, Mars and Murrie. The little sweets were sold in cardboard tubes, and soon the American military became one of the biggest buyers. The coloured shell meant that soldiers could carry the chocolate in hot places, like the Pacific islands, without it turning into a sticky mess. During the war, most of the production went to the armed forces. When soldiers came home after the war, they kept buying them, and the sweets became popular all over the country. Other well known products also have wartime roots. Spam, the tinned pork, was sent in enormous quantities to soldiers and to Allied countries. Instant coffee became much more common. And the soldiers' habit of chewing gum spread to many of the countries where they were stationed, carried home in the memories of children who had once asked them, got any gum, chum?
