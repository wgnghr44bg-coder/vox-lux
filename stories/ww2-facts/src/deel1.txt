IMG: sunday-radio-1939 | a British family in a small 1939 front room gathered quietly around a wooden wireless set, soft morning light through lace curtains, teacups on the table
HEAD: The war began for Britain on a quiet Sunday morning, over the radio.
BODY: On the third of September, 1939, families all over Britain were told to listen to the wireless at a quarter past eleven. Many had just come home from church, or had not gone at all, because they knew something important was coming. In small front rooms, people pulled their chairs close to the wooden radio set. The Prime Minister, Neville Chamberlain, spoke in a tired and careful voice. He said that no reply had come from Germany, and that the country was therefore at war. Then the broadcast ended, and for a moment many rooms were completely silent. Some people remembered that the kettle was still on. Some remembered the sound of a clock on the mantelpiece. A few minutes later, the air raid sirens sounded in London for the first time, though it turned out to be a false alarm, and people slowly came back out into the sunshine. In the afternoon, children were sent out to play as usual, and the Sunday dinner was eaten as usual, because there was nothing else to do. For a long time afterwards, people would remember exactly where they had been sitting when they heard those words. A whole country had changed in a single morning, and yet the tea still needed pouring.

IMG: blackout-curtains-evening | a woman in a 1940 English kitchen pinning dark blackout fabric over a window at dusk, a single oil lamp glowing on the table
HEAD: Every window in Britain had to be completely dark at night.
BODY: The blackout began even before war was declared. From the first evening of September 1939, every house, shop and factory had to make sure that not a single gleam of light could be seen from outside. The idea was simple: enemy aircraft should find nothing on the ground to guide them. So people bought yards of thick black cloth, or heavy card, or dark blinds, and fitted them carefully over every window. Some families painted the glass of their skylights. Wardens walked the streets in the evening, and if they saw a thin line of light at the edge of a curtain, they would knock and call out a firm reminder. Street lamps were switched off. Cars drove with hooded headlights that let only a narrow slit of light fall onto the road. Kerbs, tree trunks and lamp posts were painted with white stripes so that people would not walk into them. In the first months, many people found the dark towns strange and a little lonely. But they also noticed something lovely. In cities where the sky had always glowed orange, the stars came back. People who had never really seen the Milky Way could now look up from their own back gardens and find it there, quietly shining.

IMG: gas-mask-boxes-school | British schoolchildren in 1939 standing in a calm line in a schoolyard, each carrying a small cardboard box on a string over the shoulder, autumn trees behind
HEAD: Every person in Britain was given a gas mask, carried in a little cardboard box.
BODY: Before the war, many people feared that gas would be dropped from the air, as it had been used on the battlefields of the First World War. So the government made millions of gas masks, about thirty-eight million of them, and handed them out to almost everyone in the country. They came in a small square cardboard box with a piece of string, so that it could be carried over the shoulder. Adults had a plain black mask. Small children had a brightly coloured one, sometimes called a Mickey Mouse mask, with red rubber and blue eyepieces, to make it less frightening. For babies there was a kind of carrying case that the baby could be placed inside, with a little pump for air. At school, children practised putting their masks on during drills, and many of them discovered that if you breathed out hard, the rubber made a rude noise, which was a great favourite. People were told to carry their masks wherever they went, and in the first months most did. Shops sold smarter cases, in leather or tin, for those who wanted something more stylish. In the end, gas was never dropped on Britain. Over time, the little boxes were left on hooks by the door.

IMG: evacuees-railway-station | children in 1939 coats and caps with brown luggage labels on their lapels, waiting calmly on a railway platform with small suitcases, steam softly rising from a train
HEAD: In the first days of the war, around one and a half million people were moved out of the cities.
BODY: The plan had a cheerful name: Operation Pied Piper. On the first of September 1939, two days before war was declared, the great evacuation began. Schoolchildren from London, Liverpool, Manchester and other cities were walked to the railway stations by their teachers. Mothers with small children went too, and some teachers, and people who needed care. Each child carried a small bag or suitcase, a gas mask and usually a packet of sandwiches. Many had a brown luggage label tied to their coat, with their name and school written on it. Most of them did not know where they were going. The trains rolled out into the countryside, past fields and hedges, and stopped at small stations in places with names they had never heard. In village halls, local families came to choose a child to take home. Some children found it exciting. Many had never seen a cow, or a stream, or a field of wheat close up. Others felt very homesick, and wrote short letters in pencil to their parents. When the expected bombing did not come in those first months, many children quietly went home again. But for those who stayed, the countryside became a second home, and some of them never forgot the kindness of the people who took them in.

IMG: evacuee-farmhouse-kitchen | a city boy in a 1939 jumper sitting at a large wooden farmhouse table with a kindly farm woman, a fresh loaf, a jug of milk and warm lamplight
HEAD: For many city children, evacuation was the first time they ever saw the countryside.
BODY: Children from crowded streets arrived in places where the nearest neighbour might be a field away. Everything was new. There were cows in the lanes, and geese that hissed, and silence at night that was so deep it kept some of them awake. Some children had never had a garden, or a bath with hot water from a tap, or a bedroom of their own. Others came from comfortable homes and found themselves in small cottages with an outdoor toilet at the end of the garden. Country schools suddenly had twice as many pupils, so lessons were often held in shifts, with local children in the morning and evacuees in the afternoon. Village halls, chapels and even pubs were used as extra classrooms. The children learned to collect eggs, pick blackberries from the hedges and help with the harvest. In the evenings they wrote letters home, telling their parents about the strange food, the kind old man next door, or the pony that lived in the next field. Some of the host families became friends for life. Years later, grown men and women would travel back to the same villages, knock on the same doors and say thank you to people who had once given a frightened child a warm bed and a slice of bread.

IMG: identity-card-desk | a 1939 clerk's wooden desk with a small buff-coloured identity card, an ink pen, a lamp and neat stacks of forms, warm evening light
HEAD: On one evening in September 1939, almost every person in Britain was counted.
BODY: On the twenty-ninth of September, 1939, the government carried out something called the National Register. Enumerators went from house to house with forms, and every household had to write down the name, date of birth, occupation and address of everyone who was sleeping there that night. It was a little like a census, but it was done very quickly, in a single evening. From this register, every person was given a small identity card, made of buff-coloured paper, with their name and a number on it. People were expected to carry these cards with them, and to show them if a policeman asked. The register was also used later to give out ration books, so it quietly shaped daily life for years. Many people kept their old identity cards long after the war, tucked into a drawer with family papers. The National Register itself was used for a surprisingly long time. Its numbers were later used in the National Health Service, and some of them stayed in use for decades. Today, the 1939 Register is a treasure for family historians. Because the regular census of 1941 was never taken, these simple forms are often the best picture we have of who lived in each house, on one quiet autumn night.

IMG: ration-book-grocer | a 1940 British grocer's counter with a small ration book, a paper bag of sugar, a slab of butter on greaseproof paper and brass scales, in soft lamplight
HEAD: Food rationing in Britain began in January 1940, with bacon, butter and sugar.
BODY: Britain imported much of its food from overseas, and ships carrying it were in danger. So in January 1940, the government began rationing. Every person had a ration book, and every family had to register with a particular butcher and grocer. When you went shopping, the shopkeeper would cut out or mark the coupons in your book. The first foods on the list were bacon, ham, butter and sugar. Soon meat was added, then tea, margarine, cheese, jam and eggs. The amounts were small, but carefully worked out. An adult might have about two ounces of tea a week, and one fresh egg, when eggs were available. People learned to save, share and swap. A neighbour who did not take sugar in her tea might trade her sugar for someone else's cheese. Some foods, like bread and potatoes, were never rationed during the war, so they became the base of many meals. Interestingly, doctors later noticed that many people, especially poorer families, ate a more balanced diet under rationing than they had before. There was less sugar and fat, and more vegetables. Housewives became experts at planning the week, and at making a small piece of meat stretch across three different meals with a pot of good gravy.

IMG: dig-for-victory-allotment | an English allotment in 1941 at golden evening, neat rows of cabbages and beans, a man in a flat cap leaning on a spade beside a small wooden shed
HEAD: Parks, lawns and even the moat of the Tower of London were turned into vegetable gardens.
BODY: The campaign was called Dig for Victory. Its message was simple: if people grew their own food, fewer ships would be needed to bring it across the sea. So all over Britain, people began to dig. Flower beds became rows of potatoes. Lawns became cabbage patches. Railway embankments, school playing fields and the edges of golf courses were planted with beans and onions. In London, parts of Hyde Park and Kensington Gardens were turned into allotments, and the dry moat around the Tower of London became a vegetable garden too. Leaflets explained how to plan a garden so that something could be picked in every month of the year. A cheerful cartoon figure called Potato Pete appeared in advertisements, along with Doctor Carrot, to encourage people to eat what they grew. By the middle of the war there were well over a million allotments in the country. People also kept chickens in back gardens, and rabbits, and pigs, sometimes in shared pig clubs, where neighbours fed the pig with their kitchen scraps. In the long summer evenings, men and women came home from work and went straight out to their plots. They watered, weeded and talked over the fence, and at the end of the day the air smelled of earth and green leaves.

IMG: anderson-shelter-garden | a curved corrugated metal Anderson shelter half-buried in a small English back garden, covered with earth and flowers growing on top, warm dusk light, a cat sitting nearby
HEAD: Millions of families had a small curved shelter of corrugated steel at the bottom of the garden.
BODY: It was called the Anderson shelter, after Sir John Anderson, who was in charge of air raid protection. It was made of curved sheets of corrugated steel, bolted together into a little arch, about six feet high and four and a half feet wide. Families dug a hole in the back garden, set the shelter into it and then piled the earth back over the roof. Over two million of these shelters were put up across Britain, many of them handed out free to families with lower incomes. The insides were usually cold and damp, and in wet weather water could gather on the floor. So people made them as cosy as they could. They put in wooden bunks, old blankets, a candle or a paraffin lamp, a flask of tea and sometimes a little shelf for books. Children were often allowed to decorate the walls. On top, where the earth was piled, many families planted flowers or vegetables. Some grew lettuces on the roof, or marrows, or bright nasturtiums. In summer, a garden with an Anderson shelter could look like a small green hill. Many of these shelters were never taken down after the war. They were turned into garden sheds, tool stores and chicken houses, and a few of them still stand at the end of old gardens today.

IMG: morrison-shelter-table | a 1941 living room with a large steel table-like Morrison shelter covered with a lace tablecloth, children's books and a teapot on top, soft lamplight
HEAD: Some families slept inside a steel cage that also served as the kitchen table.
BODY: Not every house had a garden, so in 1941 a new kind of shelter was introduced for use indoors. It was named the Morrison shelter, after Herbert Morrison, the Home Secretary. It looked like a very large and very solid table, made of thick steel, with a flat top and wire mesh sides. During the day, families used it as a table. They ate their meals on it, did homework on it and spread a cloth over it so that it looked almost ordinary. At night, or when the siren sounded, they lifted the mesh side, crawled underneath and lay down on a mattress inside. It was big enough for two adults and a couple of small children, lying close together. It was not always comfortable, but it was warm, because it was inside the house, and many people found it far better than going out into a cold garden in the middle of the night. Children often remembered the Morrison shelter fondly, as a kind of den. They played games inside it during the day and read comics there by torchlight. Around half a million of these shelters were given out. After the war, some were kept for years and used as workbenches in garages and sheds. A few families even kept theirs as a kitchen table, long after anyone needed to crawl under it.

IMG: underground-station-sleepers | a 1940 London Underground platform at night with families resting calmly on blankets along the curved tiled wall, a woman knitting, a tea urn, soft yellow light
HEAD: At night, thousands of Londoners slept on the platforms of the Underground.
BODY: When the heavy air raids on London began in September 1940, many people looked for somewhere deep and safe to spend the night. At first, the authorities did not want the Underground used as a shelter. But people simply bought a platform ticket, went down with their blankets and stayed. Soon the stations were officially opened. On the busiest nights, around one hundred and seventy thousand people slept below the city. Families arrived in the late afternoon to claim a good spot along the curved tiled walls. They brought pillows, flasks, sandwiches and knitting. Children fell asleep on the platforms, while the last trains still rumbled in and out beside them. Over time, the shelters became more organised. Bunks were fitted along the walls, and tickets were given out so that families had their own regular place. Refreshment trains travelled down the lines, bringing tea, buns and hot cocoa from station to station. Some stations had small libraries, first aid posts and even concerts and film shows. Strangers became neighbours, and neighbours became friends. Many people later spoke of the sense of company down there, under the busy streets, with the smell of tea and the low murmur of hundreds of people settling down for the night.

IMG: knitting-by-fireside | an elderly woman in a 1940 armchair by a glowing fireplace knitting a thick khaki scarf, a ball of wool in a basket, a cat asleep on the rug
HEAD: People at home knitted millions of socks, scarves and gloves for the forces.
BODY: All over Britain, and in America, Canada and Australia too, knitting needles were busy every evening. Women's groups, church circles, schoolchildren and even some men knitted warm clothing for soldiers, sailors and airmen. There were official patterns for balaclavas, mittens, scarves and thick socks. Navy blue wool went to sailors, air force blue to airmen and khaki to the army. Seamen on the cold northern convoys were especially grateful for anything warm. Some knitters slipped a small note inside a finished pair of socks, with their name and address and a short message. Now and then, a soldier or sailor wrote back, and a friendship began across hundreds of miles. Knitting was also a calm thing to do while the evenings were long and the news was uncertain. You could knit by the fire while listening to the wireless. You could knit in the queue at the shops. You could knit in the shelter during an air raid, by the light of a candle, without looking at your hands. Old jumpers were unravelled and the wool was washed and wound into balls again, so that nothing went to waste. Many people who learned to knit during the war kept doing it for the rest of their lives, the soft click of needles still filling quiet evenings.

IMG: family-listening-wireless-night | a 1940s family gathered in a cosy sitting room at night around a glowing wireless set, the father with a pipe, the mother sewing, a child lying on the rug, warm lamplight
HEAD: In the evenings, the wireless was the heart of the home.
BODY: During the war, almost every household gathered around the radio set. In Britain, the BBC broadcast the news at regular times, and people planned their evenings around it. The nine o'clock news became a habit for millions. Newsreaders began to say their own names at the start of each bulletin, so that listeners would know it was a real BBC broadcast and not a trick. Between the news programmes came music, plays, talks and comedy. One of the most popular shows was a comedy called It's That Man Again, known as ITMA, full of silly characters and catchphrases that people repeated at work the next day. There were programmes for factory workers, called Music While You Work, played in the morning and afternoon to keep spirits up on the long shifts. There were cookery talks with ideas for using rations, and gardening advice from a calm gentleman known as the Radio Doctor or the Radio Gardener. On Sunday evenings, there might be a concert or a quiet service. In America, families listened the same way, to news, big band music and radio dramas. The wireless brought the wider world into small rooms. It was the voice in the corner that kept people company through long, dark evenings.

IMG: make-do-and-mend-sewing | a young woman in 1943 at a treadle sewing machine by a window at dusk, a child's dress being made from an old coat, buttons in a tin, a pin cushion on the table
HEAD: In wartime Britain, clothes were rationed too, so people learned to make do and mend.
BODY: Clothing rationing began in June 1941. Every person received a certain number of coupons each year, and every piece of clothing had a price in coupons as well as in money. A dress might cost eleven coupons, a pair of shoes five, a man's suit twenty-six. With only about sixty-six coupons a year at first, and fewer later, people had to choose very carefully. So the government started a campaign called Make Do and Mend. Booklets showed how to patch elbows, darn socks, turn a worn collar inside out and remake old garments into new ones. A man's old suit could become a skirt and jacket for his wife. A tired coat could become a little coat for a child. Curtains, blankets and even parachute silk, when it could be found, were made into clothes. Wedding dresses were often borrowed, and passed from bride to bride. There were also new Utility clothes, made in simple styles with fewer pleats and buttons to save material. Famous designers helped create them, so they were plain but well made. People became very clever with a needle and thread. Many families had a button tin, full of buttons saved from old clothes, and children loved to sort through them on rainy afternoons.

IMG: pigeon-loft-dusk | a wooden pigeon loft on an English rooftop at dusk with a man in a 1940s jumper gently holding a grey homing pigeon, other pigeons resting on the ledge, soft sunset sky
HEAD: Homing pigeons carried messages during the war, and some received medals.
BODY: Even in a war of radios and radar, the humble homing pigeon was still very useful. A pigeon could be carried in a small basket on an aircraft or a ship, or dropped in a container with a little parachute to people in occupied countries. If an aircraft had to come down in the sea and the radio was broken, the crew could release a pigeon with a message tied to its leg. The bird would fly home to its loft, sometimes over a great distance, and help would be sent. Hundreds of thousands of pigeons served in this way, many of them lent by ordinary pigeon fanciers in Britain, who kept birds in lofts on their rooftops and in their gardens. In 1943, a special award called the Dickin Medal was created for animals that had shown great courage or devotion in wartime. Of the first awards, many went to pigeons. One of them, named Winkie, helped rescue the crew of a bomber that had come down in the North Sea in 1942. She flew back to her loft in Scotland, tired and oily, and the rescuers were able to work out where to search. Today, a small memorial to the animals of the war stands in Park Lane in London, with two heavily laden mules walking towards a gap in a curved wall.
