HEAD: Tens of thousands of young women crossed the ocean after the war to join husbands they had met in wartime.
BODY: During the war, millions of American and Canadian soldiers were stationed in Britain, Australia and other countries for months or even years. Many of them met local young women at dances, in cafés and at church, and many of them fell in love and married. After the war, these wives, and often their babies, needed to travel to their husbands' homes. They became known as the war brides. In 1946, special laws were passed to help them, and great ocean liners like the Queen Mary and the Queen Elizabeth were used to carry them across the Atlantic. Around seventy thousand British war brides went to the United States, and around forty five thousand to Canada. Others went from Australia, New Zealand, France, Italy and elsewhere. For many of them, it was the first time they had ever left their home country. They arrived in New York or Halifax and then travelled by train for days to small towns, prairie farms and big cities they had only seen on a map. Some found the new life very hard at first, and missed their families terribly. Many others settled in happily and stayed for the rest of their lives. Their children and grandchildren today often tell the story of how a grandmother crossed an ocean to begin a new life.

IMG: last-ration-books-1954 | a smiling British housewife in 1954 at a butcher's counter, a large joint of meat wrapped in paper, an old ration book lying open on the counter, warm shop light
HEAD: In Britain, food rationing did not completely end until the summer of 1954.
BODY: Many people expected that rationing would end as soon as the war was over. In fact, in Britain it went on for years, and in some ways it became even stricter. The country was very short of money, and food was scarce all over Europe. Bread, which had never been rationed during the war, was rationed from 1946 to 1948. Potatoes were rationed for a while in 1947, after a terrible winter. Slowly, though, foods began to come off the ration one by one. Tea was freed in 1952, which was a great relief to the nation. Sweets were freed in early 1953, and children queued outside sweet shops with their pocket money. Sugar followed later that year, and eggs, cream and butter after that. Finally, on the fourth of July 1954, meat came off the ration, and rationing in Britain was over at last, nearly fifteen years after it had begun. Some housewives held small ceremonies and burned their ration books. Others kept them, tucked in a drawer, as a reminder. People who had grown up with rationing often kept the habits all their lives. They saved string and brown paper, scraped the butter thinly on their toast and never, ever threw away food that could be eaten the next day.

IMG: marshall-plan-ship-unloading | a cargo ship unloading sacks of grain and crates onto a European dock in 1948, workers in caps carrying sacks, a small flag painted on the crates, soft morning light
HEAD: After the war, America sent billions of dollars to help rebuild Europe.
BODY: When the war ended, much of Europe was exhausted. Factories, railways, bridges and homes had been destroyed, and many people did not have enough food or fuel. In June 1947, the American Secretary of State, George Marshall, gave a speech at Harvard University. He proposed that the United States should help the countries of Europe recover, if they would work together on a plan. The result was the European Recovery Program, which became known as the Marshall Plan. Between 1948 and 1952, the United States sent around thirteen billion dollars in aid to sixteen European countries, which would be well over a hundred billion today. The aid came as food, fuel, machinery, raw materials and loans. Ships arrived in European ports carrying grain, cotton and tractors, and the crates were often marked with a small shield of stars and stripes and the words, for European recovery, supplied by the United States of America. The Netherlands, Britain, France, Italy and West Germany were among the countries that received the most. The plan helped factories start again, farms grow more food and cities rebuild their homes. In 1953, George Marshall was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize, one of very few soldiers ever to receive it.

IMG: un-charter-signing-1945 | delegates in 1945 suits gathered calmly in a grand theatre hall with a semicircle of national flags behind a stage, one man signing a large document under bright warm lights
HEAD: The United Nations was founded in San Francisco in the summer of 1945.
BODY: Even before the war was over, leaders of the Allied countries began to plan for a new organisation, one that might help prevent another world war. In April 1945, delegates from fifty countries met in San Francisco, in the War Memorial Opera House and the Veterans Building. For two months they discussed, argued and wrote. On the twenty-sixth of June 1945, they signed the Charter of the United Nations. The Charter begins with the words, We the peoples of the United Nations, determined to save succeeding generations from the scourge of war. It came into force on the twenty-fourth of October that year, a date still celebrated each year as United Nations Day. The first meetings of the General Assembly were held in London in January 1946, in Methodist Central Hall in Westminster. Later, the United Nations moved to a new headquarters by the East River in New York. In its garden stands a Japanese peace bell, which was cast from coins and medals donated by people from many countries and presented in 1954. Every year, it is rung on the first day of spring and on the International Day of Peace. The sound is soft and clear, and it lingers in the air for a long time after it is struck.

IMG: coventry-cathedral-ruins-evening | the roofless ruined walls and tall Gothic windows of the old Coventry Cathedral at golden evening, a simple charred wooden cross standing at the far end, grass and quiet
HEAD: The ruins of Coventry Cathedral became a place of peace and reconciliation.
BODY: On the night of the fourteenth of November, 1940, the city of Coventry in the English Midlands was heavily bombed, and its medieval cathedral of Saint Michael was destroyed. Only the outer walls and the tall stone spire were left standing. The next morning, the cathedral stonemason, Jock Forbes, noticed two charred oak beams from the roof lying in the shape of a cross. He tied them together and set them up in the ruins. A local priest made another cross from three large medieval nails found among the rubble. This became known as the Cross of Nails. The cathedral's provost, Richard Howard, made a remarkable decision. Rather than speak of revenge, he spoke of forgiveness and of building a kinder world after the war. Behind the altar of the ruins, the words Father Forgive were carved into the wall. After the war, a new cathedral was built beside the old one, and it was consecrated in 1962. The ruins were kept as they were, open to the sky. Over the years, Crosses of Nails have been given to churches and communities around the world, including in Germany, as a sign of friendship. Today, people walk quietly through the old cathedral's roofless nave, where grass grows between the stones and pigeons nest in the empty windows.

IMG: prefab-houses-garden | a row of small white prefabricated bungalows in 1947 England with neat little gardens full of flowers, a woman hanging washing on a line, children playing, a soft summer evening
HEAD: Over a hundred and fifty thousand small factory-built houses were put up quickly after the war.
BODY: When the war ended, Britain had a great shortage of homes. Many houses had been destroyed or damaged, and few new ones had been built during the war years. Returning servicemen and young families needed somewhere to live, and fast. One answer was the prefab, a small prefabricated bungalow made in factories, often in old aircraft factories, and then delivered to the site in sections on the back of a lorry. A team of workers could put one together in a matter of days. Around one hundred and fifty six thousand of them were built under a temporary housing programme between 1945 and 1949. They were only meant to last about ten years. But many people loved them. For families who had lived in damp, crowded houses, a prefab felt like a small palace. It had a modern kitchen with a built-in fridge, which was a great novelty, an indoor bathroom with hot running water, two bedrooms and a little garden at the front and back. Families planted roses and vegetables and kept them spotless. Many prefabs lasted much longer than planned. Some families lived in them for forty years or more, and a small number are still lived in today. One estate in south London has even been carefully preserved, so that people can remember these little homes and the hope they gave.

IMG: rubble-women-berlin | women in 1946 headscarves and aprons standing in a long line passing bricks from hand to hand among the ruins of a city street, a small cart, the evening sun low and golden
HEAD: In Germany, women cleared the rubble from bombed cities by hand, one brick at a time.
BODY: At the end of the war, many German cities lay in ruins. In Berlin alone, there were tens of millions of cubic metres of rubble. With so many men dead, wounded or held as prisoners of war, much of the work of clearing it fell to women. They became known as Trümmerfrauen, the rubble women. Working in teams, they climbed over the ruins of houses and passed bricks from hand to hand in long lines. They knocked the old mortar off each brick with small hammers, so that it could be used again, and stacked them neatly in piles. They pushed the broken pieces away in carts and small railway wagons. The work was hard, dusty and poorly paid, but it often came with a better food ration card, which was very important in those hungry years. Much of the rubble was carried to the edges of the cities and piled up into great hills. In Berlin, one of these hills, the Teufelsberg, rises high above the forest on the western edge of the city. Over the years, trees and grass have grown over it, and today people walk their dogs there, fly kites and look out over the city. Many German towns have small memorials to the rubble women, often a simple statue of a woman with a hammer, sitting quietly on a stack of bricks.

IMG: candy-bomber-parachutes | a 1948 transport plane flying low over a city at sunset with tiny white handkerchief parachutes drifting down, children in a field below looking up and waving
HEAD: During the Berlin Airlift, an American pilot dropped sweets to children on tiny parachutes.
BODY: In 1948, three years after the war, the Soviet Union closed the roads, railways and canals into West Berlin. Over two million people in the western half of the city were cut off from food and fuel. So the United States, Britain and their allies began the Berlin Airlift. For almost a year, aircraft landed at Berlin's airports day and night, carrying flour, coal, milk and medicine. At its peak, a plane landed every few minutes. One American pilot, Lieutenant Gail Halvorsen, noticed a group of children watching the planes behind the fence at Tempelhof airport. He gave them two sticks of chewing gum, all he had, and he saw how carefully they shared them. He promised that the next day he would drop sweets from his aircraft. He told them he would wiggle the wings so they would know which plane was his. That night, he tied chocolate bars and gum to small parachutes made from handkerchiefs. The next day, he dropped them over the children. Soon other pilots joined in, and people in America sent sweets and handkerchiefs by the thousand. The children called him Uncle Wiggly Wings, and he became known as the Candy Bomber. Gail Halvorsen lived to be a hundred and one, and he returned to Berlin many times, always welcomed warmly.

IMG: anne-frank-house-canal | the narrow canal houses of the Prinsengracht in Amsterdam at quiet dusk, a large old chestnut tree behind the rooftops, warm lights glowing in a few windows, reflections on the water
HEAD: A young girl's diary, written in a hidden room in Amsterdam, became one of the most read books in the world.
BODY: In June 1942, a girl named Anne Frank received a red and white checked notebook for her thirteenth birthday. A few weeks later, she and her family went into hiding in a set of secret rooms at the back of her father's business building, on the Prinsengracht canal in Amsterdam. The entrance was hidden behind a moving bookcase. Eight people lived there for over two years, helped by a small group of brave office workers who brought them food and news. Anne wrote in her diary almost every day. She wrote to an imaginary friend she called Kitty, about her family, her hopes, her quarrels, her growing up and her wish to become a writer. From her window, she could see the top of a great chestnut tree, and she wrote about how it comforted her. In August 1944, the hiding place was discovered. Anne did not survive the war. Her father, Otto, was the only one of the eight who came home. One of the helpers, Miep Gies, had kept Anne's diary safe. In 1947, Otto published it in Dutch, under the title Het Achterhuis, the secret annex. Since then, it has been translated into more than seventy languages. Today, the house is a museum, and visitors walk quietly through the rooms, past the bookcase, where Anne once dreamed of the world outside.

HEAD: The beaches of Normandy are quiet places today, where families play in the sand.
BODY: If you visit Normandy today, the famous landing beaches look very peaceful. Their old code names are still used, Utah, Omaha, Gold, Juno and Sword, but most of the time they are simply long, wide stretches of sand, with dunes, small seaside villages and holiday homes. In summer, children build sandcastles, people swim in the cool sea and horses are ridden along the water's edge at low tide. Here and there, you can still see traces of the war: an old concrete bunker half buried in the dunes, a memorial stone, a tank standing quietly on a village green. Above Omaha Beach, at Colleville-sur-Mer, lies the American cemetery, where nearly ten thousand white marble crosses and stars of David stand in long, perfect rows on a green lawn, looking out over the sea. Along the coast there are British, Canadian, French, Polish and German cemeteries too, all very carefully tended. Every year in early June, veterans, families and visitors from many countries come to remember. There are ceremonies, wreaths and the sound of bagpipes on the cliffs. Local people often put out flags and flowers. Many families in Normandy still invite visitors into their homes and tell them, quietly and kindly, what their grandparents told them about the summer of 1944.

IMG: churchill-war-rooms-map-room | an underground 1945 map room with large wall maps dotted with coloured pins, rows of telephones in different colours on a long desk, a clock on the wall, soft yellow lamps, everything still and preserved
HEAD: The secret underground rooms where Churchill worked were left almost exactly as they were in 1945.
BODY: Beneath the government buildings of Whitehall in London lies a set of basement rooms that were used as a secure headquarters during the war. They were known as the Cabinet War Rooms. Here, Winston Churchill, his ministers and his staff met, planned and worked, often through the night. There was a Cabinet Room with a large table and chairs, a small bedroom for Churchill, though he rarely slept there, and a tiny room disguised as a private lavatory, which actually held a secret telephone line to the President of the United States. The most important space was the Map Room. Officers kept it staffed day and night, marking the movements of ships, armies and convoys with coloured pins on huge wall maps. There was a row of telephones in different colours, known as the beauty chorus. On the sixteenth of August 1945, the day after the war ended, the lights in the Map Room were switched off, the door was closed and the rooms were left as they were. The maps, the pins, the papers and even the sugar cubes hidden in a desk drawer by one of the officers stayed in place. Today, the rooms are a museum. Visitors walk through the quiet corridors and look into the Map Room, where the maps are still pinned to the walls, just as they were left on that summer day.

HEAD: Many code breakers kept their wartime work a secret for more than thirty years.
BODY: The people who worked at Bletchley Park had promised never to talk about what they did, and most of them kept that promise. After the war, they went back to ordinary lives as teachers, librarians, civil servants and mothers. Their families often had no idea what they had done. When asked about the war, many of them simply said that they had done some office work in the country. Then, in 1974, a book was published that revealed the story of Bletchley and the breaking of Enigma. Suddenly, these quiet men and women could begin to tell their stories, though many still found it difficult to speak openly after so many years of silence. Some husbands and wives discovered that they had both worked at Bletchley without ever knowing it. Some grown children were astonished to learn that their gentle, elderly mother had once worked through the night on secret German messages. In 2009, the British government began to award a special commemorative badge to those who had served at Bletchley and its outstations. Many veterans returned to the park for reunions, and they sat together on the lawn by the lake, drinking tea, remembering old friends and sharing the stories they had waited so long to tell. Some of them said it felt wonderful to finally be able to talk.

HEAD: Today, the voices of veterans are kept safe in great collections of recorded memories.
BODY: In the years after the war, many people who had lived through it did not talk about it very much. They wanted to get on with their lives, and some memories were hard to share. But as the years passed and they grew older, many veterans and civilians began to feel that their stories should be remembered. Museums, libraries and universities began to record them. The Imperial War Museum in London has gathered tens of thousands of hours of recorded interviews, with soldiers, sailors, airmen, nurses, factory workers, evacuees and people who lived through the Blitz. In the United States, the Library of Congress started the Veterans History Project in 2000, and it has collected over a hundred thousand personal stories, letters and photographs. Many of these recordings are very ordinary and very human. People talk about the food, the friends they made, the songs they sang, the dances they went to and the first time they saw the sea. They talk about being cold, being bored and being homesick. They laugh at old jokes. Some of the last veterans of the war are still alive today, now well over a hundred years old. When they are gone, their voices will remain, quietly speaking from the archives, telling the next generations what it was like to be young in those years.

HEAD: For many families, the first summers after the war brought the first seaside holiday in years.
BODY: During the war, many of Britain's beaches had been closed. They were covered with barbed wire, concrete blocks and warning signs, and some seaside piers had been partly taken apart so that they could not be used by an enemy landing. Families had not been able to go to the sea for years. After the war, slowly, the beaches were cleared. The wire was rolled up and the blocks were taken away. And in the summers of 1946 and 1947, families went back to the seaside in their millions. They travelled by crowded trains and charabancs to Blackpool, Brighton, Scarborough, Margate and countless other towns. There were striped deckchairs on the sand, donkeys giving rides, Punch and Judy shows and ice cream, although sometimes there was not very much of it. Holiday camps, like those run by Billy Butlin, became hugely popular, offering cheap holidays with meals, dances, games and entertainment for the whole family. Children who had been born during the war saw the sea for the first time, and paddled in the cold water with their trousers rolled up. Mothers and fathers sat in deckchairs with a newspaper or a flask of tea, and simply watched the waves. After everything the country had been through, a quiet afternoon by the sea seemed like the greatest luxury in the world.

IMG: quiet-village-evening-peace | an English village green at dusk with a small stone war memorial covered in poppy wreaths, warm lights glowing in cottage windows, a church tower, a few birds settling in the trees
HEAD: In almost every town and village, a small stone memorial quietly keeps the names.
BODY: Across Britain, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Canada, Australia, America and many other countries, there are memorials to the people who lived and died in the war. Many of them were first put up after the First World War, and then new names were added after the Second. Some are grand monuments in city squares. But most are small and simple: a stone cross on a village green, a plaque on a church wall, a list of names in a school hall or a railway station. Often, the same surnames appear more than once, brothers or cousins from the same family. Every November, and in many countries in May as well, people gather around these memorials. They lay wreaths of poppies or flowers, read the names aloud and stand in silence for two minutes. In the quiet, you can hear the wind in the trees and the birds in the hedges. The rest of the year, the memorials simply stand there, as people walk their dogs, children cycle past and neighbours stop to chat. The war has now passed out of living memory for most people. But in these small stone monuments, in the letters kept in old boxes, in the vegetable gardens, the old shelters and the stories told by grandparents, the memory of those years is still quietly kept, like a lamp left burning in a window.
