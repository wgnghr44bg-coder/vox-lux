HEAD: Winston Churchill often worked in bed in the morning and took a nap every afternoon.
BODY: Winston Churchill became Prime Minister in May 1940, at the age of sixty five, and he kept unusual hours. He usually woke around eight, and then worked in bed for much of the morning, with papers spread across the blankets, a breakfast tray beside him and secretaries taking notes. After lunch, he almost always took a nap, often for an hour or more, properly undressed and in bed. He believed that this rest allowed him to work very late into the night, often until two or three in the morning, and many of his staff found it exhausting to keep up. He liked to wear a garment he called his siren suit, a one-piece zip-up outfit, rather like a boiler suit, that could be put on quickly if the air raid siren sounded. He had them made in several colours, including a velvet one in deep green. He dictated his letters and speeches to typists while walking up and down the room, and they learned to type almost silently, because he disliked the noise of the keys. He also loved to paint, and to build brick walls in his garden at home in Kent, which he said rested his mind. His habits were often amusing to those around him, but they carried him through five very long years.

HEAD: President Roosevelt spoke to Americans through calm radio talks called fireside chats.
BODY: Franklin Delano Roosevelt was President of the United States from 1933 until 1945. Long before the war, he had begun giving radio addresses to the nation, speaking in a warm, simple and friendly way, as if he were sitting in each family's living room. A radio journalist named these broadcasts fireside chats, and the name stuck. Roosevelt gave around thirty of them over the years. During the war, millions of Americans gathered around their radios to hear him. He explained in plain words what was happening and what the country was being asked to do. Before one broadcast in February 1942, he asked people to have a map of the world ready at home, so that they could follow along as he talked about faraway places. Shops quickly sold out of maps and atlases. Roosevelt took great care over these talks. He practised them many times, and he spoke slowly, using short words that everyone could understand. Many people later remembered the sound of his voice coming out of the radio in the evening, and the feeling that someone was explaining things to them, calmly and honestly. Some even wrote him letters afterwards, telling him about their own families and their own worries, as if they were writing to a friend.

HEAD: On Christmas Day 1939, the King quoted a little known poem that became famous overnight.
BODY: King George the Sixth did not find public speaking easy. He had a stammer, and he had worked for many years with a speech therapist named Lionel Logue to help him. The King had been given the poem by his daughter, the young Princess Elizabeth. No one knew at first who had written it. Newspapers and the BBC soon discovered that the author was a quiet retired teacher named Minnie Louise Haskins, who had written it many years earlier and published it in a small book. She was astonished to hear her words read by the King. The poem was printed on cards and in newspapers across the country. Many years later, the same lines were read at the funeral of the Queen Mother.

HEAD: For more than two years, the church bells of Britain were silent.
BODY: In June 1940, an order was given that the church bells of Britain should no longer be rung for services, weddings or celebrations. Instead, they would only be rung as a warning, if enemy forces landed in the country. For the next two years, villages and towns that had heard their bells every Sunday for centuries fell silent. Many people said that this was one of the strangest things about the war. Sunday mornings felt different without the sound of bells drifting across the fields. Then, in November 1942, after the Allied victory at El Alamein in North Africa, Winston Churchill ordered that the bells should be rung in celebration. On the morning of Sunday the fifteenth of November, bells rang out across the whole country, from great cathedrals to tiny village churches. People came out of their houses to listen. Some wept. Bell ringers who had not rung together for over two years climbed the towers again, and some of them were a little out of practice. The BBC broadcast the sound of the bells around the world. In 1943, the ban was lifted for good, and the bells returned to their Sunday rhythm. Many people remembered that November morning as the first time they really felt that the long dark days might end.

HEAD: The biggest sea landing in history was postponed by a day because of a weather forecast.
BODY: The Allied invasion of Normandy, known as D-Day, needed very particular conditions. The commanders wanted a late rising moon, a low tide at dawn and calm weather for the ships, the aircraft and the landing craft. Only a few days each month were suitable. The planned date was the fifth of June, 1944. But in the days before, the weather turned stormy. The chief weather adviser to General Eisenhower was a quiet Scottish meteorologist named James Stagg. He and his team studied weather reports from ships, aircraft and stations across the Atlantic, including one small weather station in the west of Ireland, at Blacksod Point in County Mayo. Late on the fourth of June, Stagg told Eisenhower that a short break in the storm was coming, which would give just enough calm for the landings on the sixth. Eisenhower thought for a moment, and then gave the order to go. Meanwhile, the German forecasters, with less information from the Atlantic, believed the storm would continue, and some commanders went away for the weekend. Stagg later said that those were the most anxious hours of his life. After the war, he returned to his work as a meteorologist and became the president of the Royal Meteorological Society, never forgetting how much depended on a single forecast.

HEAD: A secret army of artists used inflatable tanks and sound effects to fool the enemy.
BODY: One of the most unusual units of the war was the American 23rd Headquarters Special Troops, later known as the Ghost Army. It was made up of around eleven hundred men, and many of them were artists, designers and actors, recruited from art schools and advertising agencies. Their job was not to fight, but to deceive. They used inflatable rubber tanks, lorries, guns and aircraft, which could be blown up with a pump and placed in fields, so that from the air they looked like a real army. Some of these rubber tanks were so light that two men could lift one. The unit also had huge loudspeakers mounted on vehicles, which played recorded sounds of tanks, bridges being built and soldiers shouting, so that the enemy would hear an army that was not there. They created fake radio traffic too, and some men pretended to be officers of other units in local cafés, wearing the badges of divisions far away. The Ghost Army carried out over twenty deceptions across France, Belgium, Luxembourg and Germany. Their work stayed secret for decades. Several of the men became famous artists and designers after the war, including the fashion designer Bill Blass and the painter Ellsworth Kelly. In 2022, the Ghost Army was awarded the Congressional Gold Medal.

HEAD: Two entire harbours were built in Britain and towed across the sea to France.
BODY: To supply an army in Normandy, the Allies needed a harbour. But the large French ports were strongly defended, and they might be badly damaged. So British engineers came up with an astonishing idea. They would build their own harbours, in pieces, and tow them across the Channel. These were called the Mulberry harbours. Huge concrete boxes called Phoenix caissons, some as tall as a five storey building, were built in shipyards and docks all over Britain, often by workers who had no idea what they were for. Long floating roadways, nicknamed whales, were made to link the harbour to the beach, resting on floating pontoons called beetles. After D-Day, old ships were deliberately sunk in a line to make a breakwater, and the concrete caissons were towed over and sunk in place to form a sheltered harbour. Two Mulberries were built, one for the American beaches and one for the British, at a small town called Arromanches. A great storm in late June damaged the American harbour badly, but the British one kept working for many months, landing millions of tons of supplies. Today, if you stand on the beach at Arromanches at low tide, you can still see the great grey concrete caissons, sitting quietly out in the sea, with gulls resting on top of them.

HEAD: American paratroopers carried small toy clickers to find each other in the dark.
BODY: On the night before D-Day, thousands of American paratroopers were dropped behind the beaches of Normandy. So many men of the 101st Airborne Division were given a simple tool. It was a small toy clicker, made of thin brass, sometimes called a cricket, because it made a sound like the insect. When a soldier heard a movement in the dark, he could press the clicker once. If the other person was a friend, he would answer by clicking twice. Some units also used spoken passwords. Afterwards, many soldiers kept their crickets as souvenirs. Today, they appear in museums in Normandy and in America, small and shiny, sometimes displayed beside a faded photograph of the young man who carried one. In the villages where the paratroopers landed, local people still remember that night, and every June, visitors walk the same lanes, between the same tall hedges.

The two days together, one quiet and one joyful, are a way of remembering both what was lost and what was won.

HEAD: British soldiers leaving the army were given a complete set of civilian clothes, called a demob suit.
BODY: After the war ended, millions of servicemen and women had to return to ordinary life. This was called demobilisation, or demob for short. Men were released in an order based on their age and how long they had served, so some went home quickly and others waited many months. When a British soldier was finally demobilised, he went to a large clothing depot, where he was given a complete set of civilian clothes. This included a suit, a shirt with two collars, a tie, a pair of shoes, a raincoat and a hat, often a trilby. It was known as the demob suit. The suits came in a few styles, such as a single breasted or a double breasted jacket, sometimes with a pinstripe. Because so many men left at the same time, the sizes were not always perfect, and many men came home with sleeves that were a little too long or trousers that were a little too short. But they were glad to have them, because clothing was still rationed. Some say that the phrase the full monty comes from this time, from the complete set of clothes made by the tailor Montague Burton, though no one knows for sure. Many men kept their demob suits for years, wearing them to weddings, to church and to work, as they slowly settled into peacetime life.

BODY: The war in Europe ended in May 1945, and the war with Japan ended in August, after Japan announced its surrender on the fifteenth. For people who had lived through six long years of war, these parties were a joyful, noisy and very welcome end.
