IMG: dutch-bicycles-canal | a quiet 1942 Dutch canal street at dusk with brick houses, a woman pushing an old bicycle with wooden tyres along the cobbles, a lamp in a window
HEAD: In the occupied Netherlands, many people rode bicycles on tyres made of wood or garden hose.
BODY: The Netherlands has always been a country of bicycles, and during the war they became more important than ever. Cars were taken away or left without fuel, and trams ran less and less. So almost everyone cycled, to work, to school, to the shops and to family in other towns. But rubber was very scarce, and new tyres could hardly be found. People became inventive. Some rode on solid tyres made from pieces of old car tyre. Others used lengths of garden hose, wrapped around the wheel and tied in place. Some had no tyres at all and rode on wooden blocks or on the bare metal rims, which made a terrible clattering noise on the cobbled streets. In 1942 and again later in the war, the occupying forces began to take bicycles away from Dutch people for their own use. Families hid their bicycles in attics, cellars, sheds and even under floorboards. A cherished bicycle could be kept hidden for years. The loss of bicycles was felt so deeply that, for decades afterwards, a half joking phrase was sometimes heard when Dutch and German football fans met: give me back my bicycle. When the war ended, one of the first things many families did was bring their old bicycle out into the sunshine again.

IMG: hidden-radio-attic | a Dutch family in 1943 sitting close together in a dim attic around a small radio hidden inside a wooden box, a single candle, the father holding a finger to his lips
HEAD: Listening to the BBC was forbidden in occupied countries, so people hid their radios.
BODY: In much of occupied Europe, people were told to hand in their radio sets. In the Netherlands, this happened in 1943. Listening to foreign broadcasts, especially the BBC from London, was strictly forbidden. But many people kept a radio hidden anyway. They hid them in cupboards behind a false wall, under floorboards, inside an old piano or in a box of potatoes. Some built tiny radios themselves, using a crystal and a pair of headphones, which needed no electricity at all. In the evenings, families gathered close together in the attic or a back room, with the volume turned down very low and someone listening at the door. From London came news in their own language. The Dutch government in exile ran a programme called Radio Oranje, and Queen Wilhelmina herself sometimes spoke to her people, her voice calm and firm across the sea. In France, the BBC programme Ici Londres began with a familiar four note rhythm, borrowed from Beethoven's Fifth Symphony, because it matched the letter V in Morse code, for victory. People later said that hearing those voices in the dark gave them hope. The news they heard was then quietly passed from neighbour to neighbour, written in small underground newspapers and whispered across garden walls.

IMG: bbc-personal-messages-cafe | a French farmhouse kitchen at night in 1944 with a small group of people listening intently to a radio on a dresser, a candle, bread and wine on the table, shutters closed
HEAD: Strange little sentences on the BBC sent secret signals to people in occupied France.
BODY: Every evening, after the French news from London, the BBC read out a long list of short and curious sentences. The announcer would say things like, the carrots are cooked, or, John has a long moustache, or, the giraffe has a long neck. To most listeners they made no sense at all. They were called the messages personnels, the personal messages. In fact, each one was a coded signal for a particular group of the French resistance, telling them that something was about to happen, or that a parcel would be dropped by parachute that night, or that a guest was on his way. Only the people who knew the meaning understood. The most famous example came in early June 1944, just before the Normandy landings. The BBC broadcast lines from a poem by Paul Verlaine, Chanson d'automne, the song of autumn, about the long sobs of the violins of autumn. The first part warned resistance groups that the landings were close. The second part told them they would begin within hours. People sitting around their radios in quiet farmhouses had no idea what most of the messages meant. But many of them liked to listen anyway, enjoying the gentle poetry of these strange sentences, read out slowly into the night.

IMG: paris-velo-taxi | a quiet Paris street in 1943 with a bicycle rickshaw taxi carrying a well-dressed couple past a café with wicker chairs, chestnut trees and soft evening light
HEAD: In wartime Paris, bicycle taxis replaced cars on the empty boulevards.
BODY: When the war came to Paris, petrol almost vanished. Private cars disappeared from the streets, and the great boulevards became strangely quiet. Parisians walked, rode bicycles, used the Métro or travelled in horse drawn carriages, which came back out of old stables after many years. A new kind of transport appeared too. It was called the vélo-taxi, a bicycle that pulled a small covered carriage behind it, with room for one or two passengers. Young men pedalled these through the city, and some of the carriages were quite elegant, with cushions and little curtains. Others were simple wooden boxes on wheels. Cafés stayed open, though coffee was often made from roasted barley or chicory, because real coffee was very rare. People called it ersatz coffee, and most agreed that it was not very good. Food was rationed, and Parisians kept rabbits on balconies and grew vegetables in the Tuileries gardens. There was a curfew at night, and the city was blacked out like London. Yet theatres, cinemas and concert halls remained open, and they were often full, because people wanted somewhere warm to go and something to take their minds off the war. When the lights finally came on again in 1944, the boulevards filled once more with people walking slowly in the evening air.

IMG: danish-fishing-boat-night | a small wooden Danish fishing boat leaving a quiet harbour at night under a starry sky, a fisherman at the wheel, a lantern glowing softly, the calm sea ahead
HEAD: In October 1943, Danish fishermen carried thousands of people to safety in Sweden.
BODY: In the autumn of 1943, word reached the Jewish community of Denmark that they were to be arrested. The warning came, remarkably, from a German diplomat named Georg Ferdinand Duckwitz, who passed the information to Danish politicians. Within days, the news spread. Ordinary Danes acted quickly. Neighbours hid Jewish families in their homes, in churches, in hospitals and in summer houses along the coast. Doctors checked people into hospitals under false names. Then, over the following weeks, fishermen began to take them across the narrow stretch of sea called the Øresund to neutral Sweden, only a few miles away. They sailed at night, in small wooden fishing boats, rowing boats and even kayaks. Passengers lay hidden under nets or in the holds. Sweden had announced that it would welcome them. In the end, around seven thousand people were carried across, almost the whole Jewish community of Denmark. The great majority survived the war. Many of those who helped never asked for anything in return, and many never spoke about it afterwards. Today, one of the boats used in the crossing can be seen at the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum in Washington. It is a small, plain fishing boat, and it is a quiet reminder of what a few ordinary people can do on a dark night.

HEAD: In 1942, a famous symphony was performed by a weakened orchestra inside the besieged city of Leningrad.
BODY: The city of Leningrad, now called Saint Petersburg, was surrounded for nearly nine hundred days during the war. Life there was extremely hard. Food was very scarce, the winters were bitter and there was little fuel for heating. The composer Dmitri Shostakovich began writing his Seventh Symphony in the city in 1941. He finished it after he had been evacuated, and it was performed in other places first. But people wanted it played in Leningrad itself. The score was flown into the city by a military aircraft. The only orchestra left was the Leningrad Radio Orchestra, and by then only a small number of its musicians were still able to play. The conductor, Karl Eliasberg, searched the city for anyone who could hold an instrument, and musicians were found in hospitals and military units. Rehearsals were short, because the players were so weak. Yet on the ninth of August, 1942, the symphony was performed in the Philharmonia hall. The concert was broadcast through loudspeakers across the city and towards the lines outside. Survivors later said that people in the streets stood still to listen. For many, it was a sign that the city was still alive. The hall is still used for concerts today, and the same symphony is sometimes played there on anniversaries.

IMG: moscow-metro-station-shelter | the grand marble and steel arches of the Mayakovskaya metro station in Moscow in 1941, families resting calmly on benches and blankets under the decorated ceiling, soft lamplight
HEAD: In Moscow, the beautiful metro stations became shelters, and one even hosted a great meeting.
BODY: The Moscow metro had opened in 1935, and its stations were famous for their beauty, with marble walls, chandeliers, mosaics and statues. During the war, as in London, the deep stations became shelters. On the worst nights, hundreds of thousands of people sheltered underground. Families brought blankets and pillows, and children slept on benches or on wooden boards laid across the tracks when trains stopped running at night. There was a library in some stations, and a small medical post. A few babies were even born down there. One of the grandest stations, Mayakovskaya, has a curved ceiling covered with mosaics of sky, aircraft and parachutes, all set into small domes like windows looking upward. On the sixth of November 1941, with enemy armies close to the city, the leaders of the country held their traditional anniversary meeting of the revolution in this station, deep beneath the ground. A train stood at the platform with tables of food for the guests, and the speech was broadcast across the country. The next morning, a military parade was held in Red Square as usual, with snow falling. Today, the metro still carries millions of people every day, and Mayakovskaya still has its quiet mosaics of sky, high above the passengers' heads.

HEAD: In wartime Japan, neighbours were organised into small groups of about ten households.
BODY: In Japan, daily life during the war was shaped by small local groups called tonarigumi, which means something like neighbourhood associations. Each group usually included around ten to fifteen households, living close to one another. Through these groups, the government handed out rations, shared news and organised air raid drills, fire watching and savings campaigns. Members met regularly, often in one of the houses, and passed around a circular board with notices written on it. If one household needed help, the others were expected to step in. Women did much of the organising, because so many men were away. For everyday clothes, many women wore a style of loose trousers called monpe, which had once been worn mainly by farm workers, because they were more practical than a kimono for work and drills. Rice was rationed, and people mixed it with barley, sweet potatoes or other grains to make it go further. In the cities, families grew vegetables in small plots and window boxes. There were slogans painted on walls, encouraging people to give up luxuries and save everything they could. Yet even in these hard years, people kept small seasonal customs. They still looked at the moon in autumn, and in spring, when the cherry blossom came out, families still stopped for a moment to look at it.

HEAD: Over a hundred thousand airmen learned to fly under the wide skies of the Canadian prairies.
BODY: Learning to fly in Britain during the war was difficult. The skies were crowded, the weather was unpredictable and there was a constant risk of enemy aircraft. So a great plan was made. It was called the British Commonwealth Air Training Plan, and it was based mainly in Canada. Canada had huge open spaces, flat prairies and reliable weather, far away from the fighting. Over a hundred airfields and schools were built, many of them in small prairie towns in Saskatchewan, Manitoba and Alberta. Young men came from Britain, Australia, New Zealand, Canada itself and many other countries. The trainees lived in wooden huts, studied in classrooms and flew for hours over wheat fields and grain elevators. Local families welcomed them to Sunday dinners, dances and hockey games. Some of the young airmen married local women and came back to live in Canada after the war. President Roosevelt once called Canada the aerodrome of democracy. Today, some of the old hangars still stand on the edges of prairie towns, quiet now, with the wind moving through the grass.

HEAD: The Mona Lisa travelled around the French countryside in a special wooden case.
BODY: In the summer of 1939, before the war began, the staff of the Louvre in Paris quietly began to empty the museum. Thousands of paintings, statues and objects were packed into crates and loaded onto lorries. The crates were marked with coloured circles to show how precious they were. One circle meant valuable, two meant very important, and three meant a true treasure. The Mona Lisa had a crate of her own, marked with three red circles, and she travelled lying flat in a special padded case. She was taken to the Château de Chambord in the Loire Valley first, and then moved several times during the war, to other châteaux and abbeys in quieter parts of France. Curators travelled with her, checking the temperature and the dampness of the rooms. Photographs from the time show the great galleries empty, with only the outlines of frames left on the walls and chalk marks where the paintings had hung. When the war ended, the treasures returned one by one. The Mona Lisa came home to the Louvre in 1945, and she has been smiling there ever since.

HEAD: A small group of art experts in uniform searched Europe for hidden treasures.
BODY: As the Allied armies moved across Europe, they were joined by an unusual group of soldiers. They were museum directors, curators, art historians, architects and archivists, and their official title was the Monuments, Fine Arts and Archives section. Today they are usually called the Monuments Men, though there were women among them too. Their task was to protect churches, museums and historic buildings, and to find works of art that had been taken from their owners during the war. Many artworks had been moved or hidden in castles, salt mines and caves. In the salt mine of Altaussee in Austria, the Monuments Men found thousands of paintings and sculptures stored deep underground, including the famous Ghent Altarpiece, a great painted panel from the fifteenth century, and a white marble Madonna by Michelangelo from the church in Bruges. They worked slowly and carefully, often by torchlight, recording every object, packing it and arranging for it to be sent home. Collecting points were set up in Munich and other cities, where the art was sorted and checked. Over the years after the war, millions of objects were returned to their countries. The work of finding missing artworks still continues today, and every few years, a long lost painting is found and quietly returned to its family.

HEAD: Neutral Lisbon was one of the few places where the lights stayed on.
BODY: Portugal stayed neutral during the war, and its capital, Lisbon, became a remarkable place. While much of Europe was blacked out, the streets of Lisbon were still lit at night, and the cafés stayed open. For people trying to leave Europe, Lisbon was often the last stop. Ships sailed from its harbour across the Atlantic to New York and South America, and from 1939, flying boats of Pan American Airways flew across the ocean from the river Tagus. The writer Erich Maria Remarque later called it a city of last hope. For many travellers, the sight of Lisbon's lights glowing on the hills at night was something they never forgot.

HEAD: From occupied Denmark, people could see the lights of neutral Sweden shining across the water.
BODY: Sweden also stayed neutral during the war, and it did not need a full blackout like its neighbours. On clear nights, people standing on the coast of occupied Denmark, near Elsinore or Copenhagen, could look across the narrow strait and see the lights of Swedish towns, glowing on the other side. For many Danes, those lights became a quiet symbol of a peaceful world just out of reach. Norwegians, too, crossed the mountains and forests into Sweden, sometimes on skis, in deep winter. Over the course of the war, tens of thousands of refugees from Norway, Denmark, Finland and the Baltic countries found shelter there. Sweden itself lived through hard years. There was rationing, many men were called up to guard the borders, and the country had to tread very carefully between the warring powers. Swedish families took in thousands of Finnish children, known as war children, who were sent across the border for safety, often wearing labels with their names, much like the British evacuees. Many of them stayed for years, and some stayed for good, growing up with two families and two languages. After the war, some of these children were reunited with their parents in Finland. Others kept close ties with their Swedish families for the rest of their lives.
