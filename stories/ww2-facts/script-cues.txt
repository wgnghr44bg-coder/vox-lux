{img:01-sunday-radio-1939}

The war began for Britain on a quiet Sunday morning. And it came through a small wooden box in the corner of the room.

[pause]

It is the third of September, 1939. A Sunday morning. All over Britain, families have been told to listen to the radio at a quarter past eleven. Everyone knows that something important is coming. In small front rooms, people pull their chairs close to the wooden set. Nobody talks. Then a tired voice begins. It is the Prime Minister, Neville Chamberlain. He says that Germany has not replied. And so, the country is at war. When the broadcast ends, many rooms are completely silent. Somewhere, a kettle is still boiling. A clock ticks on the mantelpiece. A few minutes later, the air raid sirens wail over London for the first time. People hurry to find shelter. They wait, and they listen. But no aircraft come. It was a false alarm. Slowly, everyone walks back out into the sunshine. That afternoon, children play outside as usual. The Sunday dinner is eaten as usual. But for the rest of their lives, people would remember exactly where they were sitting when they heard those words.

[long-pause]

{img:02-blackout-curtains-evening}

Every window in Britain had to go completely dark at night. And then, something beautiful appeared in the sky.

[pause]

The blackout began even before war was declared. From the first evening of September 1939, every house, shop and factory had to make sure that not a single gleam of light could be seen from outside. The idea was simple: enemy aircraft should find nothing on the ground to guide them. So people bought yards of thick black cloth, or heavy card, or dark blinds, and fitted them carefully over every window. Some families painted the glass of their skylights. Wardens walked the streets in the evening, and if they saw a thin line of light at the edge of a curtain, they would knock and call out a firm reminder. Street lamps were switched off. Cars drove with hooded headlights that let only a narrow slit of light fall onto the road. Kerbs, tree trunks and lamp posts were painted with white stripes so that people would not walk into them. In the first months, many people found the dark towns strange and a little lonely. But they also noticed something lovely. In cities where the sky had always glowed orange, the stars came back. People who had never really seen the Milky Way could now look up from their own back gardens and find it there, quietly shining.

[long-pause]

{img:03-gas-mask-boxes-school}

Every person in Britain was given a gas mask in a little cardboard box. But what happened to those boxes is a quiet surprise.

[pause]

Before the war, many people feared that gas would be dropped from the air, as it had been used on the battlefields of the First World War. So the government made millions of gas masks, about thirty-eight million of them, and handed them out to almost everyone in the country. They came in a small square cardboard box with a piece of string, so that it could be carried over the shoulder. Adults had a plain black mask. Small children had a brightly coloured one, sometimes called a Mickey Mouse mask, with red rubber and blue eyepieces, to make it less frightening. For babies there was a kind of carrying case that the baby could be placed inside, with a little pump for air. At school, children practised putting their masks on during drills, and many of them discovered that if you breathed out hard, the rubber made a rude noise, which was a great favourite. People were told to carry their masks wherever they went, and in the first months most did. Shops sold smarter cases, in leather or tin, for those who wanted something more stylish. In the end, gas was never dropped on Britain. Over time, the little boxes were left on hooks by the door.

[long-pause]

{img:04-evacuees-railway-station}

In the first days of the war, around one and a half million people were moved out of the cities.

[pause]

The plan had a cheerful name: Operation Pied Piper. On the first of September 1939, two days before war was declared, the great evacuation began. Schoolchildren from London, Liverpool, Manchester and other cities were walked to the railway stations by their teachers. Mothers with small children went too, and some teachers, and people who needed care. Each child carried a small bag or suitcase, a gas mask and usually a packet of sandwiches. Many had a brown luggage label tied to their coat, with their name and school written on it. Most of them did not know where they were going. The trains rolled out into the countryside, past fields and hedges, and stopped at small stations in places with names they had never heard. In village halls, local families came to choose a child to take home. Some children found it exciting. Many had never seen a cow, or a stream, or a field of wheat close up. Others felt very homesick, and wrote short letters in pencil to their parents. When the expected bombing did not come in those first months, many children quietly went home again. But for those who stayed, the countryside became a second home, and some of them never forgot the kindness of the people who took them in.

[long-pause]

{img:05-evacuee-farmhouse-kitchen}

For many city children, the war brought something they had never seen before in their lives.

[pause]

Children from crowded streets arrived in places where the nearest neighbour might be a field away. Everything was new. There were cows in the lanes, and geese that hissed, and silence at night that was so deep it kept some of them awake. Some children had never had a garden, or a bath with hot water from a tap, or a bedroom of their own. Others came from comfortable homes and found themselves in small cottages with an outdoor toilet at the end of the garden. Country schools suddenly had twice as many pupils, so lessons were often held in shifts, with local children in the morning and evacuees in the afternoon. Village halls, chapels and even pubs were used as extra classrooms. The children learned to collect eggs, pick blackberries from the hedges and help with the harvest. In the evenings they wrote letters home, telling their parents about the strange food, the kind old man next door, or the pony that lived in the next field. Some of the host families became friends for life. Years later, grown men and women would travel back to the same villages, knock on the same doors and say thank you to people who had once given a frightened child a warm bed and a slice of bread.

[long-pause]

{img:06-identity-card-desk}

On one evening in September 1939, almost every person in Britain was counted. Those simple forms still hold a secret picture of the country.

[pause]

On the twenty-ninth of September, 1939, the government carried out something called the National Register. Enumerators went from house to house with forms, and every household had to write down the name, date of birth, occupation and address of everyone who was sleeping there that night. It was a little like a census, but it was done very quickly, in a single evening. From this register, every person was given a small identity card, made of buff-coloured paper, with their name and a number on it. People were expected to carry these cards with them, and to show them if a policeman asked. The register was also used later to give out ration books, so it quietly shaped daily life for years. Many people kept their old identity cards long after the war, tucked into a drawer with family papers. The National Register itself was used for a surprisingly long time. Its numbers were later used in the National Health Service, and some of them stayed in use for decades. Today, the 1939 Register is a treasure for family historians. Because the regular census of 1941 was never taken, these simple forms are often the best picture we have of who lived in each house, on one quiet autumn night.

[long-pause]

{img:07-ration-book-grocer}

Food rationing in Britain began in January 1940, with bacon, butter and sugar.

[pause]

Britain imported much of its food from overseas, and ships carrying it were in danger. So in January 1940, the government began rationing. Every person had a ration book, and every family had to register with a particular butcher and grocer. When you went shopping, the shopkeeper would cut out or mark the coupons in your book. The first foods on the list were bacon, ham, butter and sugar. Soon meat was added, then tea, margarine, cheese, jam and eggs. The amounts were small, but carefully worked out. An adult might have about two ounces of tea a week, and one fresh egg, when eggs were available. People learned to save, share and swap. A neighbour who did not take sugar in her tea might trade her sugar for someone else's cheese. Some foods, like bread and potatoes, were never rationed during the war, so they became the base of many meals. Interestingly, doctors later noticed that many people, especially poorer families, ate a more balanced diet under rationing than they had before. There was less sugar and fat, and more vegetables. Housewives became experts at planning the week, and at making a small piece of meat stretch across three different meals with a pot of good gravy.

[long-pause]

{img:08-dig-for-victory-allotment}

Even the moat of the Tower of London was dug up. And what grew there was not what anyone expected.

[pause]

The campaign was called Dig for Victory. Its message was simple: if people grew their own food, fewer ships would be needed to bring it across the sea. So all over Britain, people began to dig. Flower beds became rows of potatoes. Lawns became cabbage patches. Railway embankments, school playing fields and the edges of golf courses were planted with beans and onions. In London, parts of Hyde Park and Kensington Gardens were turned into allotments, and the dry moat around the Tower of London became a vegetable garden too. Leaflets explained how to plan a garden so that something could be picked in every month of the year. A cheerful cartoon figure called Potato Pete appeared in advertisements, along with Doctor Carrot, to encourage people to eat what they grew. By the middle of the war there were well over a million allotments in the country. People also kept chickens in back gardens, and rabbits, and pigs, sometimes in shared pig clubs, where neighbours fed the pig with their kitchen scraps. In the long summer evenings, men and women came home from work and went straight out to their plots. They watered, weeded and talked over the fence, and at the end of the day the air smelled of earth and green leaves.

[long-pause]

{img:09-anderson-shelter-garden}

Millions of families had a small curved shelter of corrugated steel at the bottom of the garden.

[pause]

It was called the Anderson shelter, after Sir John Anderson, who was in charge of air raid protection. It was made of curved sheets of corrugated steel, bolted together into a little arch, about six feet high and four and a half feet wide. Families dug a hole in the back garden, set the shelter into it and then piled the earth back over the roof. Over two million of these shelters were put up across Britain, many of them handed out free to families with lower incomes. The insides were usually cold and damp, and in wet weather water could gather on the floor. So people made them as cosy as they could. They put in wooden bunks, old blankets, a candle or a paraffin lamp, a flask of tea and sometimes a little shelf for books. Children were often allowed to decorate the walls. On top, where the earth was piled, many families planted flowers or vegetables. Some grew lettuces on the roof, or marrows, or bright nasturtiums. In summer, a garden with an Anderson shelter could look like a small green hill. Many of these shelters were never taken down after the war. They were turned into garden sheds, tool stores and chicken houses, and a few of them still stand at the end of old gardens today.

[long-pause]

{img:10-morrison-shelter-table}

Some families slept every night inside a steel cage. In the daytime, they ate their dinner on top of it.

[pause]

Not every house had a garden, so in 1941 a new kind of shelter was introduced for use indoors. It was named the Morrison shelter, after Herbert Morrison, the Home Secretary. It looked like a very large and very solid table, made of thick steel, with a flat top and wire mesh sides. During the day, families used it as a table. They ate their meals on it, did homework on it and spread a cloth over it so that it looked almost ordinary. At night, or when the siren sounded, they lifted the mesh side, crawled underneath and lay down on a mattress inside. It was big enough for two adults and a couple of small children, lying close together. It was not always comfortable, but it was warm, because it was inside the house, and many people found it far better than going out into a cold garden in the middle of the night. Children often remembered the Morrison shelter fondly, as a kind of den. They played games inside it during the day and read comics there by torchlight. Around half a million of these shelters were given out. After the war, some were kept for years and used as workbenches in garages and sheds. A few families even kept theirs as a kitchen table, long after anyone needed to crawl under it.

[long-pause]

{img:11-underground-station-sleepers}

At night, thousands of Londoners went underground to sleep. And down there, something unexpected happened.

[pause]

It is September 1940. Every night, the sirens sound over London, and the bombers come. People need somewhere deep and safe to sleep. And right beneath their feet, there is the Underground. At first, the authorities do not want people sleeping in the stations. But the people simply buy a cheap platform ticket. They go down with their blankets, and they stay. Soon the stations are opened properly. On the busiest nights, around a hundred and seventy thousand people sleep below the city. Families arrive in the late afternoon to find a good spot along the curved walls. They bring pillows, flasks of tea and sandwiches. Above them, the city shakes. But down here, it is quiet. Children fall asleep on the platforms while the last trains rumble past. Later, bunks are put up along the walls. Special trains bring tea and hot cocoa from station to station. Some stations even have little libraries and concerts. Strangers become neighbours. And neighbours become friends. Many people later remembered the warm murmur of hundreds of voices, settling down for the night, deep under the busy streets.

[long-pause]

{img:12-knitting-by-fireside}

People at home knitted millions of socks, scarves and gloves for the forces.

[pause]

All over Britain, and in America, Canada and Australia too, knitting needles were busy every evening. Women's groups, church circles, schoolchildren and even some men knitted warm clothing for soldiers, sailors and airmen. There were official patterns for balaclavas, mittens, scarves and thick socks. Navy blue wool went to sailors, air force blue to airmen and khaki to the army. Seamen on the cold northern convoys were especially grateful for anything warm. Some knitters slipped a small note inside a finished pair of socks, with their name and address and a short message. Now and then, a soldier or sailor wrote back, and a friendship began across hundreds of miles. Knitting was also a calm thing to do while the evenings were long and the news was uncertain. You could knit by the fire while listening to the wireless. You could knit in the queue at the shops. You could knit in the shelter during an air raid, by the light of a candle, without looking at your hands. Old jumpers were unravelled and the wool was washed and wound into balls again, so that nothing went to waste. Many people who learned to knit during the war kept doing it for the rest of their lives, the soft click of needles still filling quiet evenings.

[long-pause]

{img:13-family-listening-wireless-night}

In the evenings, the wireless was the heart of the home.

[pause]

During the war, almost every household gathered around the radio set. In Britain, the BBC broadcast the news at regular times, and people planned their evenings around it. The nine o'clock news became a habit for millions. Newsreaders began to say their own names at the start of each bulletin, so that listeners would know it was a real BBC broadcast and not a trick. Between the news programmes came music, plays, talks and comedy. One of the most popular shows was a comedy called It's That Man Again, known as ITMA, full of silly characters and catchphrases that people repeated at work the next day. There were programmes for factory workers, called Music While You Work, played in the morning and afternoon to keep spirits up on the long shifts. There were cookery talks with ideas for using rations, and gardening advice from a calm gentleman known as the Radio Doctor or the Radio Gardener. On Sunday evenings, there might be a concert or a quiet service. In America, families listened the same way, to news, big band music and radio dramas. The wireless brought the wider world into small rooms. It was the voice in the corner that kept people company through long, dark evenings.

[long-pause]

{img:14-make-do-and-mend-sewing}

In wartime Britain, clothes were rationed too, so people learned to make do and mend.

[pause]

Clothing rationing began in June 1941. Every person received a certain number of coupons each year, and every piece of clothing had a price in coupons as well as in money. A dress might cost eleven coupons, a pair of shoes five, a man's suit twenty-six. With only about sixty-six coupons a year at first, and fewer later, people had to choose very carefully. So the government started a campaign called Make Do and Mend. Booklets showed how to patch elbows, darn socks, turn a worn collar inside out and remake old garments into new ones. A man's old suit could become a skirt and jacket for his wife. A tired coat could become a little coat for a child. Curtains, blankets and even parachute silk, when it could be found, were made into clothes. Wedding dresses were often borrowed, and passed from bride to bride. There were also new Utility clothes, made in simple styles with fewer pleats and buttons to save material. Famous designers helped create them, so they were plain but well made. People became very clever with a needle and thread. Many families had a button tin, full of buttons saved from old clothes, and children loved to sort through them on rainy afternoons.

[long-pause]

{img:15-pigeon-loft-dusk}

Some of the bravest messengers of the war were small grey birds. A few of them were even given medals.

[pause]

It is February 1942. A British bomber is flying home over the North Sea. Then something goes wrong. The aircraft comes down into the cold, grey water. The crew climb into a small rubber dinghy. Their radio is broken. Nobody knows where they are. But they have one last hope. In a small basket, they carry a homing pigeon. Her name is Winkie. The men let her go, and she flies off into the winter sky. She has no message tied to her leg. There was no time. She is tired, and her feathers are covered in oil from the crash. But she keeps flying. More than a hundred miles, all the way home to her loft in Scotland. When she arrives, her owner sees her and raises the alarm. From the time she landed and the state of her feathers, the rescuers work out where to search. And they find the crew, alive. A year later, Winkie was given a special medal for animals, called the Dickin Medal. Hundreds of thousands of pigeons served in the war like this. Many of them belonged to ordinary people, who kept them in lofts on their rooftops.

[long-pause]

{img:16-soldier-writing-letter-candle}

For soldiers far from home, letters were the most precious thing of all.

[pause]

Every day of the war, millions of letters crossed the seas. Soldiers wrote home from training camps, from ships, from tents in the desert and from farmhouses in France. Families wrote back with news of the garden, the dog, the neighbours and the baby who had just learned to walk. Letters were read again and again, folded and unfolded until the paper became soft. Many soldiers kept them in a breast pocket, close to the heart. Mail call, when letters were handed out, was one of the happiest moments of the day. Men listened carefully for their names, and those who received nothing would sometimes be given a letter to share by a friend. Soldiers wrote in pencil when there was no ink, on any paper they could find, and sometimes by the light of a single candle. They were not allowed to say exactly where they were, so they wrote about the weather, the food and the people they had met. Many letters ended with the same few words, asking the family to give love to everyone and not to worry. Countless wartime letters still survive today, kept in shoeboxes and attics. They are full of ordinary, loving details, and they show that even in the hardest times, people wanted most of all to stay close to home.

[long-pause]

{img:17-v-mail-sorting-room}

American soldiers' letters were often shrunk down to a tiny size. There was a clever reason for it.

[pause]

Letters are surprisingly heavy, and in a world war there were enormous numbers of them. So the United States used a clever system called V-Mail, short for Victory Mail. It was based on a British idea called the Airgraph. A soldier or family member wrote a letter on a special single sheet of paper. The letters were then photographed and copied onto rolls of microfilm. A single small roll could hold thousands of letters, and a few bags of film could carry what would otherwise have filled dozens of sacks. The film was flown or shipped across the ocean, and at the other end each letter was printed out again as a small photograph, about a quarter of the size of the original page. Then it was folded into a little envelope and delivered. This saved a great deal of space for food, fuel and equipment on ships and planes. The writing on V-Mail could be quite small to read, so people learned to write clearly and in large letters. Some drew little pictures in the corners, of houses or Christmas trees or cartoon dogs. Ordinary letters were still sent too, but V-Mail became a familiar part of the war. Today, old V-Mail letters can still be found in family papers, small and neat, like tiny windows into another time.

[long-pause]

{img:18-field-kitchen-morning}

Feeding an army was a gigantic daily task.

[pause]

An army, people often said, marches on its stomach. Every day, millions of soldiers needed breakfast, lunch and supper, often far away from any shop or farm. So armies built large supply systems. Behind the lines were field kitchens, set up in tents, barns or the backs of lorries, with huge pots and ovens. Cooks rose long before dawn to make porridge, powdered eggs, bread, stews and gallons of tea or coffee. Soldiers queued with their metal mess tins and ate wherever they could find a spot, sitting on crates, walls or the grass. When fresh food could not reach them, soldiers carried tinned rations. In the British army there were tins of corned beef, known as bully beef, and hard biscuits that were so dry they were sometimes soaked in tea before eating. American soldiers had C-rations, small tins of meat and beans, stew or hash, along with crackers, sugar and instant coffee. Each army had its favourite and least favourite dishes, and soldiers loved to grumble about the food, which was often a sign that morale was fine. When a hot meal arrived after a long cold day, it was a moment of real comfort. Many veterans remembered a particular cup of tea or a bowl of stew for the rest of their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:19-d-ration-chocolate-bar}

An American chocolate bar was made to taste bad. On purpose.

[pause]

Before the war, the United States Army asked the Hershey chocolate company to make a special bar for emergencies. It had to be small enough to fit in a pocket, it had to give a lot of energy, and it had to survive heat without melting. There was one more unusual request. It should not taste too good. The army worried that if the chocolate was delicious, soldiers would eat it as a snack instead of saving it for when they really needed it. So the officer in charge asked for a taste only a little better than a boiled potato. The result was the D ration bar, made from chocolate, sugar, cocoa butter, skimmed milk powder and oat flour. It was thick, very hard and rather bitter. Many soldiers had to shave pieces off with a knife, and some called it Hitler's secret weapon, as a joke, because of what it did to their stomachs. Later, a tastier tropical bar was made that could survive the heat of the Pacific. Millions of these bars were produced during the war. They were handed out to soldiers, airmen and sailors, and many were given away to children in liberated towns, who had not tasted chocolate for years and thought it was the best thing in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:20-tea-brew-up-desert}

For British soldiers, a brew of tea was the answer to almost everything.

[pause]

Wherever British and Commonwealth soldiers went, they made tea. In the desert of North Africa, they made it in a simple stove called a Benghazi burner. This was an old petrol tin, cut in half and filled with sand. A little petrol was poured onto the sand and lit, and a can of water was set on top to boil. Within minutes, there was tea, strong and sweet, often with tinned milk. Soldiers stopped for a brew-up at almost every pause, and it was a moment of calm in the middle of long days. Tea was so important that, during the war, the British government bought up huge amounts of the world's supply to make sure it never ran out. Tea was rationed at home, but soldiers usually had plenty. Even British tanks were later fitted with a small electric water boiler inside, so that the crew could make tea without climbing out. The habit spread to the ships and airfields too. In naval canteens and in the huts where pilots waited for their next flight, there was nearly always a big brown pot on the go. For many soldiers far from home, the taste of a hot cup of tea was a small piece of their own kitchen, carried with them all the way across the world.

[long-pause]

{img:21-dog-tags-on-table}

Every soldier wore two small metal tags around his neck. Even today, some of them are still being found.

[pause]

Most soldiers in the Second World War wore identity discs around their neck. In the American army, these were small oblong tags made of metal, hung on a thin beaded chain. They were nicknamed dog tags, a name that has stayed ever since. Each tag was stamped with the soldier's name, a service number, a blood type and sometimes a religion and the name of a person to contact. Soldiers usually wore two identical tags, one on a long chain and one on a short one attached to it. British soldiers had a pair of discs made of compressed fibre, one green and one red, with similar details. These small objects had a serious purpose, but over time they also became something personal. Soldiers knew their service numbers by heart for the rest of their lives, and many veterans could recite them seventy years later without a moment's thought. Some men kept their tags after the war, in a drawer or a small box, and later passed them on to their children or grandchildren. Even today, now and then, an old tag is found by a farmer in a field in France or Belgium, or by a diver near an island in the Pacific. When that happens, people often work hard to find the family and send the tag back home.

[long-pause]

{img:22-sailors-hammocks-mess-deck}

Many sailors slept in canvas hammocks that swayed gently with the ship.

[pause]

On many warships of the Second World War, especially in the British Royal Navy, sailors still slept in hammocks, much as their great-grandfathers had done in the age of sail. Each man had his own canvas hammock, which he slung each evening from hooks or bars in the mess deck, the same space where he ate his meals during the day. In the morning, the hammocks were rolled up tightly and stowed away, so that the space could be used again. Hammocks had one great advantage at sea. As the ship rolled from side to side, the hammock stayed level, gently swinging, so the sailor inside hardly felt the movement. Many men said they slept better in a hammock than in any bed they had ever known. The mess decks were crowded, warm and full of sounds: the hum of engines, the creak of the hull, the breathing and murmuring of dozens of men. A dim red or blue light usually burned at night, so that men coming on and off watch could find their way without waking everyone. Sailors on watch would gently shake the next man awake when it was his turn. On some newer ships, bunks were fitted instead, but the hammock remained a familiar part of navy life all through the war.

[long-pause]

{img:23-navy-rum-ration-tot}

British sailors were given a daily cup of rum. The custom lasted much longer than you might think.

[pause]

In the Royal Navy, an old tradition carried on all through the war. Every day, at around midday, sailors over the age of twenty who wished to have it were given a ration of rum. It was called the tot. The rum was kept in a large oak tub, often bound with brass, and on many ships the words The King, God bless him were written on the side in brass letters. At a pipe called Up Spirits, a petty officer measured out the rum carefully. For ordinary sailors it was usually mixed with water, which was known as grog. The custom dated back hundreds of years, to the days when fresh water on long voyages could go stale. Sailors had a whole language around the tot. A small sip offered to a friend as thanks was called sippers, and a larger gulp was called gulpers. Some men gave their tot as payment for small favours, like mending a shirt or taking a watch. Sailors who chose not to drink it received a small amount of money instead. The tradition lasted until the thirty-first of July 1970, a day still remembered in the navy as Black Tot Day. On that last evening, sailors around the world drank their final tot, and some held mock funerals for the old custom.

[long-pause]

{img:24-submarine-crew-quarters}

In a submarine, two men often shared one bed. But they never slept in it at the same time.

[pause]

Life on a submarine was very cramped. The vessels were long and narrow, and almost every space was filled with machinery, pipes, torpedoes and supplies. Crews of around forty to sixty men lived inside for weeks at a time. There were not enough bunks for everyone, so many submariners practised something called hot bunking. When one man finished his watch and climbed out of his bunk, another man who was going off duty climbed in. The blanket was often still warm. Some men slept on top of the torpedoes, or in small spaces between pipes. Fresh food was stored wherever it would fit, and at the start of a patrol there might be loaves of bread hanging in nets, and boxes of tins under the floor plates. Water was precious, so washing was kept to a minimum, and after a few weeks everyone smelled of diesel oil and cooking. Yet many submariners loved the life. Crews were small and close, and officers and men knew each other well. When the submarine dived deep, the noise of the sea above disappeared, and the inside became very quiet, with only the hum of the electric motors. Men read paperbacks, played cards, wrote letters and listened to records on a small gramophone, deep beneath the waves.

[long-pause]

{img:25-pilots-waiting-dispersal-hut}

A fighter pilot's day was mostly spent doing something you might not expect. Waiting.

[pause]

In films, pilots seem to be always flying. But in reality, many hours were spent simply waiting. In the summer of 1940, during the Battle of Britain, RAF fighter pilots sat near their aircraft at small wooden huts called dispersal huts, on the edge of grassy airfields. They wore their flying clothes and their life jackets, known as Mae Wests, and they waited for the telephone to ring. While they waited, they did all kinds of ordinary things. They read books and newspapers, played chess, draughts and cards, wrote letters and dozed in old armchairs or deckchairs on the grass. Some had dogs, which lay in the sun beside them. A gramophone might be playing a dance tune. Tea came round in big pots. The telephone could ring at any moment, and every man in the hut knew the sound of it. When it rang, the orderly would answer and, if the call was to scramble, everyone ran for the aircraft. But very often it rang for something quite ordinary, like a message about lunch. Many pilots later said that the waiting was the hardest part. They remembered the warm summer evenings most of all, when the flying was over for the day and the sky over the fields turned pink and gold.

[long-pause]

{img:26-navigator-astrodome-stars}

Some airmen found their way home in the dark using only the stars.

[pause]

Long flights at night, over sea or over darkened countryside, needed careful navigation. Radio aids were used when they could be, but they did not always work, and so many navigators still used the oldest method of all. They looked at the stars. Large aircraft often had a small transparent dome on the top of the fuselage, called an astrodome. The navigator would stand beneath it with an instrument called a sextant, rather like those used by sailors for centuries. He measured the angle of a known star above the horizon, noted the exact time and then used books of tables to work out where the aircraft was. It took practice, patience and a steady hand, especially in a plane that was bumping through the air. Navigators learned the names of dozens of stars: Vega, Capella, Arcturus, Polaris and many others. Some said that they came to think of the stars as old friends, because they were always there, in the same places, night after night. Navigation by the stars was also taught at training schools far from the war, in Canada, South Africa and Australia, where young men spent quiet evenings learning the shape of the sky. Many of them carried that knowledge for the rest of their lives, and could still name the stars above their gardens as old men.

{img:27-wojtek-bear-soldiers}

One soldier in the Polish army was not a man at all. He was a large brown bear.

[pause]

In 1942, in Iran, a group of Polish soldiers met a young boy with a small bear cub. The soldiers bought the cub, and they named him Wojtek, a Polish name that is often said to mean something like happy warrior. At first, the little bear was fed condensed milk from an old vodka bottle. He grew up among the soldiers of a supply company, and they treated him almost as one of their own. Wojtek travelled with them through Iraq, Syria, Palestine and Egypt. He loved to wrestle with the men, to splash in water and to ride in the cab of their lorries. He also became famous for drinking beer and, apparently, for eating cigarettes. When the unit sailed to Italy in 1944, animals were not allowed on the ship, so Wojtek was officially enlisted as a private soldier, with his own name and number. In Italy, soldiers later said that he helped carry heavy boxes of supplies, copying what he saw the men doing. The company even changed its badge to show a bear carrying a shell. After the war, Wojtek went to live at Edinburgh Zoo in Scotland, where he spent the rest of his life. Old Polish soldiers sometimes came to visit him, and they said that he seemed to recognise the sound of their language.

{img:28-uso-show-stage}

Famous singers and comedians travelled the world to entertain the troops.

[pause]

To keep up spirits, entertainers went wherever soldiers were stationed. In America, an organisation called the USO, the United Service Organizations, arranged shows in camps and bases at home and abroad. Stars like Bob Hope, Marlene Dietrich and the Andrews Sisters performed on simple wooden stages, on the backs of lorries and on the decks of ships. Bob Hope became so closely linked with these shows that he kept doing them for decades after the war. In Britain, a similar organisation called ENSA sent comedians, dancers, musicians and actors to entertain the forces. Its standards were sometimes a little uneven, and soldiers joked that the letters stood for Every Night Something Awful. But many of its performers were very good, and the shows were greatly loved. Singers like Vera Lynn travelled to the jungles of Burma to sing for soldiers far from home. Her songs, like We'll Meet Again and The White Cliffs of Dover, became the sound of the war for millions. The USO also ran clubs where servicemen could go for a cup of coffee, a dance or a chat with friendly volunteers. For a few hours, men far from home could laugh, sing along and forget, at least for one evening, where they were.

{img:29-christmas-at-the-front}

Even far from home, soldiers found ways to celebrate Christmas.

[pause]

Every December of the war, soldiers in camps, ships and outposts around the world tried to make Christmas feel a little like home. They cut small trees or branches and decorated them with whatever they could find: tin foil from cigarette packets, paper chains, ration labels, even empty cartridge cases polished until they shone. Cooks did their best to make a special meal, and in many armies the officers served the men their Christmas dinner, an old tradition. When it was possible, there was turkey or chicken, tinned Christmas pudding and an extra ration of beer or rum. Parcels from home arrived in the weeks before, full of knitted socks, cakes, sweets, soap and letters. In 1944, the song I'll Be Home for Christmas, recorded by Bing Crosby the year before, was one of the most requested songs on forces radio. In some places, carols were sung by candlelight in old barns or churches. Men who did not usually sing joined in quietly. On Christmas Day, many letters were written, full of memories of past Christmases and hopes for the next. For a few hours, in many corners of the world, the war seemed to pause, and men sat together in the warmth, talking about home and about the people they loved.

{img:30-women-factory-rivets}

Millions of women went to work in factories, shipyards and aircraft plants.

[pause]

As men left for the forces, someone had to build the ships, aircraft, lorries and tools that the war needed. In America, around six million women joined the workforce during the war. Many of them had never worked outside the home before. They learned to rivet, weld, drill and operate huge machines. They built bombers in great factory halls in California and Michigan, and cargo ships in the shipyards of the coast. A song from 1942 called Rosie the Riveter gave a name to all of them, and a poster of a woman in a red spotted headscarf, rolling up her sleeve, later became one of the best known images of the war, though it was not very famous at the time. In Britain, women were even called up for war work from 1941, one of the first countries to do so. They worked in munitions factories, on the railways, on buses as conductors, and in engineering works. Many wore trousers for the first time, which caused some surprise in the streets. The work was hard and the shifts were long, but many women later said that they had never felt so useful, or so independent. After the war, many returned home. But something had changed, and their daughters grew up knowing what their mothers had been able to do.

{img:31-land-girls-harvest}

Young women of the Land Army kept Britain's farms working.

[pause]

With so many farm workers away in the forces, Britain needed help to grow food. So the Women's Land Army was formed again, as it had been in the First World War. By 1944, around eighty thousand women had joined. They were known as Land Girls. Many came from towns and cities and had never milked a cow or driven a tractor. They were given a uniform of green jumpers, brown breeches, thick socks and sturdy boots, and they were sent to farms all over the country. The work was very hard. They rose before dawn to milk the cows, mucked out stables, ploughed fields, harvested crops, picked potatoes and looked after sheep and pigs. Some joined the Women's Timber Corps instead, and they were called Lumberjills. They cut down trees, measured timber and worked in sawmills, providing wood for pit props, telegraph poles and packing crates. Many Land Girls lived in hostels together, while others lodged on the farms with the farming family. There were dances in village halls, bicycle rides along country lanes and long summer evenings in the fields. Many of these women later said that the Land Army years were the best of their lives. They had grown stronger, made lifelong friends and come to love the open countryside.

{img:32-princess-elizabeth-mechanic}

A young princess learned to drive a lorry and fix engines in 1945. She would one day become Queen.

[pause]

In early 1945, the future Queen Elizabeth the Second, then a princess of eighteen, joined the Auxiliary Territorial Service, the women's branch of the British army. She was the first female member of the royal family to serve full time in the armed forces. She was given the number two three zero eight seven three, and she trained as a driver and mechanic at a camp in Surrey. She learned how to strip down and rebuild an engine, change a wheel, read a map and drive a heavy army lorry. Photographs from the time show her in overalls, with her sleeves rolled up and her hands on an engine. Newspapers loved the story, and her parents, the King and Queen, came to visit and watched her at work with oily hands. She was later given the rank of junior commander. The princess clearly enjoyed the training and the chance to be treated, at least partly, like the other young women. For the rest of her life she kept an interest in engines and cars, and she often drove herself around her estates. She was also the last surviving head of state to have served in uniform during the Second World War, a quiet link to those years that lasted until 2022.

{img:33-bletchley-park-hut-night}

At a quiet country house in England, thousands of people kept a secret for many, many years.

[pause]

About fifty miles from London, there is a large old house with a lake and a lawn. It is called Bletchley Park. From the outside, it looks very ordinary. But during the war, it holds one of Britain's greatest secrets. Inside wooden huts in the garden, thousands of people are trying to read the enemy's secret messages. By 1945, around ten thousand people work there. Most of them are women. They work day and night, in shifts, in plain huts with simple numbers, like Hut Six or Hut Eight. Among them are mathematicians, chess players and young women just out of school. Some were chosen because they could solve a very hard crossword puzzle in the newspaper. Every one of them has signed a promise. They must never tell anyone what they do. Not their friends. Not their parents. And almost all of them keep that promise, for many, many years. In their free time, they play games on the lawn. In winter, they skate on the frozen lake. Today, Bletchley Park is a museum. You can walk into the huts, and see the desks and the lamps, just as they were.

[long-pause]

{img:34-enigma-machine-desk}

The German Enigma machine had more possible codes than there are stars in many galaxies. And yet, it could be broken.

[pause]

The German Enigma machine looks a little like a typewriter in a wooden box. When you press a key, a small lamp lights up with a different letter. And every time you press, little wheels inside turn, so the code keeps changing. There are so many possible settings that the number is almost too big to say. And every single day, at midnight, the settings change. So even if someone solves the code one day, the next morning, they must start all over again. It seems impossible. But in the 1930s, a few young Polish mathematicians make the first breakthrough. Just before the war, they share their secret with Britain and France. At Bletchley Park, people like Alan Turing take the work further. They build big humming machines, called Bombes, that test the settings very fast. But the code breakers also watch for something else. Tiny human habits. One operator always starts his messages with the same words. Another sends a weather report at the same time every morning. Those little habits, day after day, become the key that opens the lock.

[long-pause]

{img:35-colossus-computer-room}

One of the first electronic computers in the world was built in secret. For years, almost no one knew it existed.

[pause]

Not all German messages are sent by Enigma. The most secret ones, from the very top, use an even harder code. At Bletchley Park, they call it Tunny. To break it, they need something new. A quiet post office engineer named Tommy Flowers has an idea. He wants to build a machine with thousands of glowing glass valves, like the tubes inside old radios. Many experts tell him it will never work. They say the valves will keep breaking. But Flowers knows something they do not. From his work on telephones, he has learned that valves rarely fail if you just leave them switched on. So he builds it anyway, mostly with his own team, and partly with his own money. It is called Colossus. It fills a whole room. In early 1944, it starts work. And it works. Paper tape races through it, at five thousand letters a second. By the end of the war, there are ten of these machines. Then, when the war is over, most of them are taken apart. The plans are burned. For thirty years, almost nobody knows that one of the first computers in the world was ever built.

{img:36-radar-station-cliffs}

Along the English coast, tall masts could see aircraft long before anyone could hear them.

[pause]

It is the summer of 1940. Along the coast of England stand tall steel masts, higher than church towers. Nobody is quite sure what they are for. Only a few people know the secret. At the bottom of each mast is a small hut. Inside, a young woman sits in front of a glowing screen. On the screen there is a thin green line. She watches it, very carefully. Then, a tiny bump appears on the line. And another. She knows what it means. Aircraft are coming across the sea. Many of them. They are still far away, too far to see or hear. But she can tell how far, roughly how high, and roughly how many. She picks up the telephone. The message goes to a big room where people move small markers across a map on a table. And now, the pilots have a little precious time to get ready. This invention is called radar. It works by sending out radio waves and catching the faint echo that bounces back. After the war, the same idea helped build air traffic control, weather forecasts, and even the microwave oven.

{img:37-penicillin-laboratory-flasks}

One of the greatest medicines in history was first grown in milk bottles and bedpans.

[pause]

In 1928, a scientist in London named Alexander Fleming notices something strange. A little mould has grown on one of his dishes. And around it, the germs have died. He calls it penicillin. But there is a problem. It is very, very hard to make enough of it. Then the war comes, and suddenly, it matters more than ever. Wounded soldiers are dying from infections. At Oxford University, a small team, led by Howard Florey and Ernst Chain, decides to try. They need huge amounts of mould. So they grow it in anything they can find. Milk bottles. Baths. Biscuit tins. Even hospital bedpans. Young women, called the penicillin girls, look after them day after day. In 1941, they try it on a sick policeman. He starts to get better. But then, the medicine runs out. They even collect it from his body to use again. It is still not enough. So Florey flies to America. There, in a laboratory in Illinois, they find a better kind of mould, growing on a melon from a local market. By 1944, factories are making enough to save many thousands of lives. And it has saved many millions since.

{img:38-leg-makeup-seam-line}

When stockings ran out, women found a very clever way to fool the eye.

[pause]

In the years before the war, nylon stockings had just arrived in the shops, and they were extremely popular. When they first went on sale in America in 1940, millions of pairs were sold in a few days. But soon nylon was needed for parachutes, ropes and other wartime uses, and stockings almost disappeared. In Britain, silk and nylon stockings became very rare too, and clothing coupons made them expensive. So women found a clever answer. They painted their legs. Some used special leg make-up sold in bottles. Others used gravy browning, cold tea or a little cocoa mixed with water, though gravy browning had one drawback, because it was said to attract dogs. The finishing touch was the seam. Stockings of the time had a straight seam running up the back of the leg, so women drew a line with an eyebrow pencil, often with a friend's help, as it was difficult to keep straight. Beauty salons even offered a leg painting service. When nylon stockings finally came back after the war, there were long queues outside shops in America, sometimes thousands of people, in what newspapers called the nylon riots. For many women, a new pair of stockings was a small sign that ordinary life was returning at last.

{img:39-scrap-metal-drive}

People gave away their pots, their pans and even their garden railings. What happened to them is still a small mystery.

[pause]

Metal was needed in huge amounts for the war. In 1940, Lord Beaverbrook, the minister in charge of aircraft production, made an appeal. He asked housewives to give their aluminium saucepans, kettles and other kitchen items, which could be melted down to build Spitfires and Hurricanes. People responded with great enthusiasm. Piles of pots and pans appeared in town squares, and children went from door to door with handcarts. Later, iron railings were taken down from parks, squares and the fronts of houses all over Britain. Some historians now believe that much of the iron collected was never actually used, because there was more than the factories needed. But the collections made people feel that they were helping, and they gave everyone something they could do. Even today, if you walk through an old British town, you can sometimes see small stumps of metal along the top of a low garden wall, where railings were cut off in the war and never replaced. There were also salvage drives for paper, bones, rags and rubber. Children were sometimes given badges for collecting, and they took the job very seriously. Some schools had competitions to see who could gather the most. Very little was thrown away in those years, because almost everything could be used again.

{img:40-victory-garden-american-backyard}

In America, around twenty million victory gardens were planted.

[pause]

Just as in Britain, people in the United States were encouraged to grow their own food. These gardens were called victory gardens, and they appeared everywhere: in back yards, on empty city lots, on rooftops, in school grounds and in public parks. By 1944, there were around twenty million of them, and they produced a large share of all the fresh vegetables eaten in the country. Families grew tomatoes, beans, carrots, lettuce, squash and corn. Seed companies, magazines and government leaflets gave advice on what to plant and when. Even Eleanor Roosevelt, the wife of the President, had a victory garden planted on the lawn of the White House. In the evenings and at weekends, whole neighbourhoods were out in their gardens. People shared tools, seeds and advice over the fence. Children helped with watering and weeding, and many learned to grow things for the first time. In late summer, kitchens were busy with home canning. Families filled glass jars with tomatoes, peaches and pickles, and stored them on cellar shelves for the winter. Many older Americans later remembered the smell of those kitchens, warm and full of steam, with rows of glowing jars cooling on the table and the sound of the lids popping softly as they sealed.

{img:41-american-ration-stamps-kitchen}

American families used ration stamps and little red and blue tokens to buy food.

[pause]

Rationing came to the United States in 1942. Sugar was among the first foods to be rationed, followed by coffee, meat, butter, cheese, canned foods and more. Every American was given a ration book, full of small stamps. Some foods had a points value, and the stamps were coloured. Red stamps were used for meat, fats and cheese, and blue stamps for canned and processed foods. Since stamps were often worth more than the price of a small item, the government also made tiny tokens out of red and blue fibre, about the size of a small coin. These were used as change, so that shoppers did not lose any points. Coffee was rationed for several months, and some people reused their coffee grounds, making a weaker second pot. Recipes appeared in magazines for cakes made with less sugar and less butter, sweetened with honey, syrup or molasses. Petrol was rationed too, mainly to save rubber from car tyres, and cars displayed small stickers in the windscreen showing how much fuel they were allowed. Most families had an A sticker, which allowed only a few gallons a week. Some old ration books and tokens can still be found today, tucked inside old cookbooks, a small reminder of a time when a cup of coffee was something to save and savour.

{img:42-victory-speed-sign-road}

In America, the whole country was asked to drive slowly. Very slowly.

[pause]

In the early months of 1942, America lost access to much of the world's natural rubber, which came from plantations in Southeast Asia. Rubber was needed for tyres, hoses, boots and countless other things, so it suddenly became very precious. The government introduced a national speed limit of thirty-five miles an hour, because driving slowly made tyres last much longer. It was called the Victory Speed. Roads became quieter and slower. Families went on fewer long drives and took the bus, the train or their bicycles instead. Carpooling was encouraged, with posters suggesting that when you drove alone, you were wasting precious rubber. Rubber drives were held all over the country. People gave old tyres, garden hoses, rubber shoes, hot water bottles and even rubber bath mats. Gas stations collected the donations, and children brought in whatever they could find. Meanwhile, scientists and factories worked hard to make synthetic rubber from oil and other materials. By the end of the war, America was producing hundreds of thousands of tons of it every year. The speed limit was lifted in 1945. But for those few years, the roads were quieter than they had been for a long time, and many people rediscovered the pleasure of walking to their neighbours' houses.

{img:43-jeep-countryside-evening}

The jeep was designed in just a few weeks and became one of the most famous vehicles in history.

[pause]

In the summer of 1940, the United States Army asked car companies to design a small, light four-wheel-drive vehicle. The requirements were strict and the deadline was very short. A small company called American Bantam, working with a designer named Karl Probst, drew up plans in only a couple of days and built the first working vehicle in about seven weeks. The army liked it, but asked two larger companies, Willys-Overland and Ford, to build versions too. In the end, the Willys design became the standard, and around six hundred and forty thousand were made during the war. No one is completely sure where the name jeep came from. Some say it came from the letters G P, for general purpose. Others think it came from a character called Eugene the Jeep in the Popeye cartoons, a small magical creature who could go anywhere. The jeep certainly could go almost anywhere. It carried officers, messages, wounded men, supplies and even small guns. It drove through mud, sand, snow and rivers. Soldiers loved it, and the journalist Ernie Pyle wrote that it was as faithful as a dog and as strong as a mule. After the war, many jeeps were sold to farmers around the world, and some are still driving on country lanes today.

{img:44-candy-shop-1940s-jar}

A famous little chocolate sweet was first made for soldiers. Its secret was a thin, hard shell.

[pause]

The story is often told that Forrest Mars, the son of the founder of the Mars company, saw soldiers in the Spanish Civil War eating small pellets of chocolate covered in a hard sugar shell. The shell stopped the chocolate from melting in the heat. Back in America, he started a company with Bruce Murrie, the son of the president of Hershey, and in 1941 they began making their own version. They called them M and M's, after their two surnames, Mars and Murrie. The little sweets were sold in cardboard tubes, and soon the American military became one of the biggest buyers. The coloured shell meant that soldiers could carry the chocolate in hot places, like the Pacific islands, without it turning into a sticky mess. During the war, most of the production went to the armed forces. When soldiers came home after the war, they kept buying them, and the sweets became popular all over the country. Other well known products also have wartime roots. Spam, the tinned pork, was sent in enormous quantities to soldiers and to Allied countries. Instant coffee became much more common. And the soldiers' habit of chewing gum spread to many of the countries where they were stationed, carried home in the memories of children who had once asked them, got any gum, chum?

{img:45-dutch-bicycles-canal}

In the occupied Netherlands, people rode bicycles on tyres made of wood and garden hose.

[pause]

The Netherlands has always been a country of bicycles, and during the war they became more important than ever. Cars were taken away or left without fuel, and trams ran less and less. So almost everyone cycled, to work, to school, to the shops and to family in other towns. But rubber was very scarce, and new tyres could hardly be found. People became inventive. Some rode on solid tyres made from pieces of old car tyre. Others used lengths of garden hose, wrapped around the wheel and tied in place. Some had no tyres at all and rode on wooden blocks or on the bare metal rims, which made a terrible clattering noise on the cobbled streets. In 1942 and again later in the war, the occupying forces began to take bicycles away from Dutch people for their own use. Families hid their bicycles in attics, cellars, sheds and even under floorboards. A cherished bicycle could be kept hidden for years. The loss of bicycles was felt so deeply that, for decades afterwards, a half joking phrase was sometimes heard when Dutch and German football fans met: give me back my bicycle. When the war ended, one of the first things many families did was bring their old bicycle out into the sunshine again.

{img:46-hidden-radio-attic}

In occupied countries, listening to one radio station was forbidden. So people found ways to hide their radios.

[pause]

It is 1943, in the occupied Netherlands. Everyone has been told to hand in their radio. Listening to the BBC from London is strictly forbidden. If you are caught, you can be arrested. But many families keep a radio anyway. They hide it behind a false wall in a cupboard. Under the floorboards. Inside an old piano. Even in a box of potatoes. Some people build tiny radios themselves, with just a crystal and a pair of headphones. They need no electricity at all. In the evening, the family gathers in the attic or a back room. The curtains are drawn. Someone stands quietly by the door, listening for footsteps. The volume is turned down very low. And then, from far away, a voice speaks in their own language. It is the news from London. Sometimes, it is Queen Wilhelmina herself, calm and steady across the sea. People later said those voices in the dark gave them hope. And the next day, the news was passed on in whispers. From neighbour to neighbour. Across the garden wall.

{img:47-bbc-personal-messages-cafe}

Every evening, the BBC read out strange little sentences that made no sense at all. Except to a few people in France.

[pause]

Every evening, after the French news from London, the BBC reads out a long list of very strange sentences. The carrots are cooked. John has a long moustache. The giraffe has a long neck. To most people listening, they make no sense at all. But somewhere in France, in a quiet farmhouse, a few people are listening very carefully. Because each sentence is a secret signal. One might mean that a parcel will drop by parachute tonight. Another might mean that a visitor is on his way. Only the right people know which sentence is theirs. Then, at the start of June 1944, the BBC reads out a few lines from an old French poem. The poem is about the long, sad sound of violins in autumn. To most listeners, it is just a pretty verse. But to the resistance, it is the most important message of the war. The first lines say that the great landing is coming soon. And when the next lines follow, it means it will begin within hours. In farmhouses all over France, people quietly put on their coats. And go out into the night.

{img:48-paris-velo-taxi}

In wartime Paris, bicycle taxis replaced cars on the empty boulevards.

[pause]

When the war came to Paris, petrol almost vanished. Private cars disappeared from the streets, and the great boulevards became strangely quiet. Parisians walked, rode bicycles, used the Métro or travelled in horse drawn carriages, which came back out of old stables after many years. A new kind of transport appeared too. It was called the vélo-taxi, a bicycle that pulled a small covered carriage behind it, with room for one or two passengers. Young men pedalled these through the city, and some of the carriages were quite elegant, with cushions and little curtains. Others were simple wooden boxes on wheels. Cafés stayed open, though coffee was often made from roasted barley or chicory, because real coffee was very rare. People called it ersatz coffee, and most agreed that it was not very good. Food was rationed, and Parisians kept rabbits on balconies and grew vegetables in the Tuileries gardens. There was a curfew at night, and the city was blacked out like London. Yet theatres, cinemas and concert halls remained open, and they were often full, because people wanted somewhere warm to go and something to take their minds off the war. When the lights finally came on again in 1944, the boulevards filled once more with people walking slowly in the evening air.

{img:49-danish-fishing-boat-night}

In October 1943, on a series of dark nights, Danish fishermen quietly carried thousands of people across the sea.

[pause]

It is the autumn of 1943. In Copenhagen, the evenings are growing cold. Then a quiet message begins to pass from house to house. In a few days, the Jewish families of Denmark will be arrested. All of them. The warning has come from a very unlikely place. A German diplomat named Georg Duckwitz has decided he cannot stay silent. He tells Danish friends. And they tell everyone they can. Now there is very little time. Families leave their homes with one small bag each. Neighbours hide them in attics and cellars. Doctors check them into hospitals under false names. But hiding is not enough. They must reach Sweden, a free country, across a narrow strip of sea. On a clear night, you can see its lights from the Danish shore. It looks so close. But German boats patrol the water. So the fishermen of Denmark make a choice. Night after night, they wait in the dark harbours. The passengers lie very still under fishing nets. A child is given something to help him sleep, so that he will not cry. The engine starts, softly. And the little boat slips out onto the black water. Nobody speaks. Everyone is listening. Then, at last, the lights of Sweden grow bigger. And the boat bumps gently against a wooden pier. In just a few weeks, around seven thousand people crossed that water. Almost the whole Jewish community of Denmark. Most of the fishermen never asked for anything in return. And many of them never spoke about it again.

{img:50-leningrad-symphony-concert}

In a city surrounded for many months, a weak and hungry orchestra played a great symphony. And the whole world listened.

[pause]

The city of Leningrad is surrounded. For nearly nine hundred days, nothing can get in or out by road. There is very little food. The winters are bitterly cold. And yet, a composer named Dmitri Shostakovich has written a great new symphony for his city. People want to hear it played here, in Leningrad itself. But how? The music is flown in by a military aircraft, over the enemy lines. The only orchestra left is the radio orchestra. And most of its musicians are gone, or too weak to play. The conductor, Karl Eliasberg, goes looking through the city for anyone who can still hold an instrument. Some come from hospitals. Some come from the army. At first, the rehearsals last only a few minutes, because the players are so tired. But they keep going. On the ninth of August, 1942, the hall is full. The concert is sent out through loudspeakers, across the whole city, and even towards the enemy outside. In the streets, people stop and stand still to listen. For many of them, it is a sign. The city is still alive.

{img:51-moscow-metro-station-shelter}

In Moscow, the beautiful metro stations became shelters, and one even hosted a great meeting.

[pause]

The Moscow metro had opened in 1935, and its stations were famous for their beauty, with marble walls, chandeliers, mosaics and statues. During the war, as in London, the deep stations became shelters. On the worst nights, hundreds of thousands of people sheltered underground. Families brought blankets and pillows, and children slept on benches or on wooden boards laid across the tracks when trains stopped running at night. There was a library in some stations, and a small medical post. A few babies were even born down there. One of the grandest stations, Mayakovskaya, has a curved ceiling covered with mosaics of sky, aircraft and parachutes, all set into small domes like windows looking upward. On the sixth of November 1941, with enemy armies close to the city, the leaders of the country held their traditional anniversary meeting of the revolution in this station, deep beneath the ground. A train stood at the platform with tables of food for the guests, and the speech was broadcast across the country. The next morning, a military parade was held in Red Square as usual, with snow falling. Today, the metro still carries millions of people every day, and Mayakovskaya still has its quiet mosaics of sky, high above the passengers' heads.

{img:52-japanese-neighbourhood-evening}

In wartime Japan, neighbours were organised into small groups of about ten households.

[pause]

In Japan, daily life during the war was shaped by small local groups called tonarigumi, which means something like neighbourhood associations. Each group usually included around ten to fifteen households, living close to one another. Through these groups, the government handed out rations, shared news and organised air raid drills, fire watching and savings campaigns. Members met regularly, often in one of the houses, and passed around a circular board with notices written on it. If one household needed help, the others were expected to step in. Women did much of the organising, because so many men were away. For everyday clothes, many women wore a style of loose trousers called monpe, which had once been worn mainly by farm workers, because they were more practical than a kimono for work and drills. Rice was rationed, and people mixed it with barley, sweet potatoes or other grains to make it go further. In the cities, families grew vegetables in small plots and window boxes. There were slogans painted on walls, encouraging people to give up luxuries and save everything they could. Yet even in these hard years, people kept small seasonal customs. They still looked at the moon in autumn, and in spring, when the cherry blossom came out, families still stopped for a moment to look at it.

{img:53-canadian-prairie-airfield}

Over a hundred thousand airmen learned to fly under the wide skies of the Canadian prairies.

[pause]

Learning to fly in Britain during the war was difficult. The skies were crowded, the weather was unpredictable and there was a constant risk of enemy aircraft. So a great plan was made. It was called the British Commonwealth Air Training Plan, and it was based mainly in Canada. Canada had huge open spaces, flat prairies and reliable weather, far away from the fighting. Over a hundred airfields and schools were built, many of them in small prairie towns in Saskatchewan, Manitoba and Alberta. Young men came from Britain, Australia, New Zealand, Canada itself and many other countries. Between 1940 and 1945, the plan trained around one hundred and thirty thousand aircrew: pilots, navigators, wireless operators and air gunners. They began on small yellow training aircraft, and the skies over the prairies filled with them. The trainees lived in wooden huts, studied in classrooms and flew for hours over wheat fields and grain elevators. Local families welcomed them to Sunday dinners, dances and hockey games. Some of the young airmen married local women and came back to live in Canada after the war. President Roosevelt once called Canada the aerodrome of democracy. Today, some of the old hangars still stand on the edges of prairie towns, quiet now, with the wind moving through the grass.

{img:54-dutch-princess-ottawa-hospital}

A Dutch princess was born in Canada, in a hospital room that was Dutch for one day only.

During the war, Princess Juliana of the Netherlands, the daughter of Queen Wilhelmina, lived in Ottawa, the capital of Canada, with her two young daughters. Her husband, Prince Bernhard, was in London. In January 1943, she was expecting her third child. There was a small problem. If the baby was born in Canada, she might be counted as a Canadian citizen, and Dutch law said that an heir to the throne should be born a Dutch citizen. After the war, the Dutch royal family sent thousands of tulip bulbs to Ottawa as a thank you, both for the birth and for the part Canadian soldiers played in freeing the Netherlands. Princess Juliana continued to send bulbs every year. Today, Ottawa holds a great tulip festival every May, and the city's parks are filled with millions of tulips, red, yellow, white and purple, gently moving in the spring breeze.

{img:55-paintings-in-slate-mine}

The greatest paintings in London spent the war hidden deep inside a mountain.

[pause]

In 1939, the great paintings of London's National Gallery are carefully taken down from the walls. They must be kept safe. At first, they are hidden in houses and castles in Wales. But as the war goes on, even that does not feel safe enough. Where can you hide hundreds of priceless pictures? Then someone has an idea. An old slate quarry, deep inside a mountain in North Wales. Inside, there are huge dark caves where slate was cut out long ago. Small brick buildings are built inside the caves, with heating, so the paintings stay dry and warm. The entrance has to be made wider. Some of the biggest paintings only just fit through, on lorries that creep slowly up the narrow mountain road. Then the paintings rest there, quietly in the dark, for years. But the people of London miss them. So, from 1942, the gallery brings back just one painting each month, and shows it on its own. People queue in the street to see it. And in the empty rooms of the gallery, the pianist Myra Hess plays lunchtime concerts, almost every weekday of the war.

{img:56-mona-lisa-crate-chateau}

The Mona Lisa spent the war travelling in secret, from one quiet château to another.

[pause]

It is the summer of 1939. War has not started yet. But at the Louvre in Paris, the staff are already quietly emptying the museum. Thousands of paintings and statues are packed into wooden crates. Each crate is marked with coloured circles. One circle means valuable. Two means very important. Three means a true treasure. The Mona Lisa gets a crate of her own, with three red circles. She travels lying flat, in a soft padded case. First she goes to a great château in the Loire Valley. Then, as the danger grows, she is moved again. And again. From one quiet château to another, deeper into the countryside. Her guardians travel with her. They check the temperature of every room, and the damp on every wall. Nobody must know exactly where she is. Back in Paris, the great halls stand empty. Only chalk marks on the walls show where the paintings used to hang. When the war ends at last, the treasures come home, one by one. And in 1945, the Mona Lisa is back on her wall in the Louvre, smiling as if nothing had happened.

{img:57-monuments-men-church-art}

A small group of art experts in uniform went looking for lost treasures. Some of them are still being found today.

[pause]

As the armies move across Europe, an unusual group of soldiers travels with them. They are not fighters. They are museum directors, art experts and architects, in uniform. Today they are called the Monuments Men, though there were women among them too. They have one job. To find the great works of art that have been stolen or hidden during the war. And there are many. Paintings in castles. Statues in caves. Treasures hidden in old mines. In the spring of 1945, they hear about a salt mine high in the mountains of Austria, called Altaussee. Deep inside, they find a hidden treasure house. Thousands of paintings and sculptures, stored in the dark tunnels. Among them is a famous painted altarpiece from Ghent, more than five hundred years old. And a white marble Madonna by Michelangelo, taken from a church in Bruges. By torchlight, they slowly and carefully write down every object, pack it and send it home. Over the years, millions of objects were returned. And the search still goes on today. Every few years, a long lost painting is found, and quietly given back to its family.

{img:58-lisbon-harbour-evening}

Neutral Lisbon was one of the few places where the lights stayed on.

[pause]

Portugal stayed neutral during the war, and its capital, Lisbon, became a remarkable place. While much of Europe was blacked out, the streets of Lisbon were still lit at night, and the cafés stayed open. For people trying to leave Europe, Lisbon was often the last stop. Ships sailed from its harbour across the Atlantic to New York and South America, and from 1939, flying boats of Pan American Airways flew across the ocean from the river Tagus. Tens of thousands of refugees passed through the city, waiting for visas and tickets. They filled the hotels, the boarding houses and the cafés along the river. Some waited weeks, some waited months. Many were later grateful to a Portuguese diplomat in Bordeaux named Aristides de Sousa Mendes, who in June 1940 issued thousands of visas to people fleeing France, against the orders of his own government. Lisbon was also a city of diplomats and spies, where people from both sides of the war sat in the same cafés and sometimes stayed in the same hotels. The writer Erich Maria Remarque later called it a city of last hope. For many travellers, the sight of Lisbon's lights glowing on the hills at night was something they never forgot.

{img:59-swedish-lights-across-water}

From occupied Denmark, people could look across the water at night and see the lights of a free country.

[pause]

Sweden also stayed neutral during the war, and it did not need a full blackout like its neighbours. On clear nights, people standing on the coast of occupied Denmark, near Elsinore or Copenhagen, could look across the narrow strait and see the lights of Swedish towns, glowing on the other side. For many Danes, those lights became a quiet symbol of a peaceful world just out of reach. Norwegians, too, crossed the mountains and forests into Sweden, sometimes on skis, in deep winter. Over the course of the war, tens of thousands of refugees from Norway, Denmark, Finland and the Baltic countries found shelter there. Sweden itself lived through hard years. There was rationing, many men were called up to guard the borders, and the country had to tread very carefully between the warring powers. Swedish families took in thousands of Finnish children, known as war children, who were sent across the border for safety, often wearing labels with their names, much like the British evacuees. Many of them stayed for years, and some stayed for good, growing up with two families and two languages. After the war, some of these children were reunited with their parents in Finland. Others kept close ties with their Swedish families for the rest of their lives.

{img:60-churchill-study-lamplight}

Winston Churchill often worked in bed in the morning and took a nap every afternoon.

[pause]

Winston Churchill became Prime Minister in May 1940, at the age of sixty five, and he kept unusual hours. He usually woke around eight, and then worked in bed for much of the morning, with papers spread across the blankets, a breakfast tray beside him and secretaries taking notes. After lunch, he almost always took a nap, often for an hour or more, properly undressed and in bed. He believed that this rest allowed him to work very late into the night, often until two or three in the morning, and many of his staff found it exhausting to keep up. He liked to wear a garment he called his siren suit, a one-piece zip-up outfit, rather like a boiler suit, that could be put on quickly if the air raid siren sounded. He had them made in several colours, including a velvet one in deep green. He dictated his letters and speeches to typists while walking up and down the room, and they learned to type almost silently, because he disliked the noise of the keys. He also loved to paint, and to build brick walls in his garden at home in Kent, which he said rested his mind. His habits were often amusing to those around him, but they carried him through five very long years.

{img:61-fireside-chat-radio-evening}

President Roosevelt spoke to Americans through calm radio talks called fireside chats.

[pause]

Franklin Delano Roosevelt was President of the United States from 1933 until 1945. Long before the war, he had begun giving radio addresses to the nation, speaking in a warm, simple and friendly way, as if he were sitting in each family's living room. A radio journalist named these broadcasts fireside chats, and the name stuck. Roosevelt gave around thirty of them over the years. During the war, millions of Americans gathered around their radios to hear him. He explained in plain words what was happening and what the country was being asked to do. Before one broadcast in February 1942, he asked people to have a map of the world ready at home, so that they could follow along as he talked about faraway places. Shops quickly sold out of maps and atlases. Roosevelt took great care over these talks. He practised them many times, and he spoke slowly, using short words that everyone could understand. Many people later remembered the sound of his voice coming out of the radio in the evening, and the feeling that someone was explaining things to them, calmly and honestly. Some even wrote him letters afterwards, telling him about their own families and their own worries, as if they were writing to a friend.

{img:62-king-christmas-broadcast-1939}

On Christmas Day 1939, the King read a short poem. By the next morning, the whole country knew it.

[pause]

King George the Sixth did not find public speaking easy. He had a stammer, and he had worked for many years with a speech therapist named Lionel Logue to help him. On Christmas Day 1939, in the first winter of the war, he gave his Christmas radio message to the nation and the Empire. Near the end, he read a few lines from a poem. It began with the words, I said to the man who stood at the gate of the year, give me a light that I may tread safely into the unknown. The poem went on to answer that one should go out into the darkness and put one's hand into the hand of God, which would be better than light, and safer than a known way. The King had been given the poem by his daughter, the young Princess Elizabeth. No one knew at first who had written it. Newspapers and the BBC soon discovered that the author was a quiet retired teacher named Minnie Louise Haskins, who had written it many years earlier and published it in a small book. She was astonished to hear her words read by the King. The poem was printed on cards and in newspapers across the country. Many years later, the same lines were read at the funeral of the Queen Mother.

{img:63-little-ships-dunkirk-dawn}

In 1940, hundreds of small private boats set out across the Channel on a dangerous journey.

[pause]

It is May 1940. A large British and French army is trapped on the beaches of Dunkirk, in France. The enemy is closing in. The men must be brought home across the sea. But there is a problem. The big ships cannot come close to the shallow beaches. So a message goes out across England. Small boats are needed. Any small boats. Within days, hundreds of little craft gather on the River Thames and along the coast. Fishing boats. Pleasure boats. Lifeboats. Old paddle steamers. Even fire boats from London. Some are sailed by their owners. Some by volunteers who have never been further than a quiet river. They set off across the Channel. And by great luck, the sea stays calm. On the beaches, the soldiers wade out into the water, waiting in long patient lines. The little boats carry them out to the bigger ships, again and again. Day and night. In the end, around three hundred and thirty eight thousand men are brought home. Many of the little ships still exist today. And every few years, some of them sail back across the Channel together, slowly, on a calm sea.

{img:64-church-bells-ringing-1942}

For more than two years, every church bell in Britain stayed silent. When they rang again, it meant something wonderful.

[pause]

It is June 1940. An unusual order goes out across Britain. From now on, church bells must not ring. Not for Sunday services. Not for weddings. Not for anything. The bells will only ring for one reason. As a warning, if the enemy lands. And so, for more than two years, the bells of Britain fall silent. Villages that have heard their bells every Sunday for hundreds of years hear nothing at all. Sunday mornings feel strange and empty. Everyone knows that if the bells ever ring, something terrible has happened. Then, in November 1942, news comes from the desert of North Africa. A great victory, at a place called El Alamein. And Winston Churchill gives a new order. The bells may ring. On the morning of the fifteenth of November, bells ring out all over the country. From great cathedrals to tiny village churches. People come out of their houses, and stand in the street to listen. Some of them cry. The bell ringers are a little out of practice. But nobody minds. Many people later said it was the first time they felt that the long dark days might end.

{img:65-weather-forecaster-maps}

The biggest sea landing in history depended on one weather forecast. And one quiet man had to make it.

[pause]

It is June 1944. On the south coast of England, thousands of ships are waiting. Soldiers are already on board. Everything is ready for the invasion of France. Everything except the weather. The landing needs a calm sea, a low tide at dawn and a quiet moon. There are only a few days each month when all of this comes together. The chosen day is the fifth of June. But the wind is rising. Rain is beating on the windows. In a country house near Portsmouth, a tall Scottish weatherman named James Stagg studies his charts. He has reports from ships at sea, from aircraft and from a tiny weather station on the west coast of Ireland. If he is wrong, thousands of men could be lost in the storm. If he waits too long, the chance may not come again for weeks. Late in the evening, he walks in to see General Eisenhower. He tells him that a short break in the storm is coming. And on the morning of the sixth of June, the sea was calm enough.

{img:66-inflatable-tank-field}

A secret army of artists fought with balloons, paint and sound effects. And it worked.

[pause]

In a field in France, a row of big tanks stands under the trees. From the air, they look very real. But if you walked up and gave one a push, it would wobble. Because it is made of rubber, and filled with air. Two men could lift it. This is the work of a very strange unit. The Americans call it the Ghost Army. It has about eleven hundred men. Many of them are artists, designers and actors. Their job is not to fight. Their job is to fool the enemy. At night, they blow up rubber tanks, rubber lorries and rubber guns, and place them in the fields. Huge loudspeakers on trucks play the sounds of an army. Tanks rumbling. Bridges being built. Men shouting orders. On the radio, they send out fake messages. Some of them even sit in village cafés, wearing the badges of other units, and chat loudly so that spies will overhear. Somewhere across the lines, the enemy listens, and believes there is an army where there is almost nothing. They did this more than twenty times. And for fifty years, it stayed a secret.

{img:67-mulberry-harbour-calm-sea}

Two whole harbours were built in Britain in secret, and towed slowly across the sea to France.

[pause]

To land an army in France, you need a harbour. Ships must bring food, fuel and supplies every single day. But the big French harbours are strongly guarded. Some are as tall as a five storey building. Most of the workers have no idea what they are for. It is all a secret. Long floating roads are made too, resting on floating pontoons, nicknamed beetles. After D-Day, the giant boxes are slowly towed across the Channel by tugboats. Old ships are sunk in a line, to make a wall against the waves. Then the concrete boxes are sunk beside them. {img:68-paratrooper-cricket-clicker}

American paratroopers jumped into the dark carrying a tiny toy. It had a very important job.

It is the night before D-Day. Thousands of American paratroopers jump out of their aircraft into the darkness over Normandy. Many of them land far from where they should be. In fields. In orchards. In wet marshes. All around them are tall, thick hedges. It is very dark. And it is very quiet. Somewhere nearby, there may be friends. Or there may be enemies. How can you tell, without making a sound? Many of the men carry a tiny tool in their pocket. It is a small brass clicker, a child's toy. It is called a cricket. One man whispers flash. The other must answer thunder. All through that long night, in the fields and lanes of Normandy, small clicks sound in the dark, as the men slowly find each other. {img:69-notre-dame-bells-liberation}

When Paris was liberated, the great bell of Notre-Dame rang out across the city.

On the evening of the twenty-fourth of August, 1944, the first Allied soldiers reached the centre of Paris. After the great fire of 2019 and the long restoration, the bells rang again, and the sound once more drifted across the river and the rooftops.

{img:70-food-drop-tulip-field}

In the spring of 1945, aircraft flew low over the Netherlands. But they did not drop bombs.

It is the spring of 1945, in the western Netherlands. The winter has been terrible. There is almost no food left. People are eating sugar beets, and even tulip bulbs. In the Netherlands, Liberation Day is celebrated every year on the fifth of May.

Much of the Netherlands was freed in the spring of 1945, many towns by Canadian soldiers, along with British, Polish and American troops. On the fifth of May, 1945, at a hotel in the town of Wageningen, the German forces in the Netherlands formally agreed to surrender. The news spread quickly. People brought out orange flags and ribbons, which had been hidden for years, and decorated their houses and streets. {img:73-big-ben-lit-again}

For more than five years, the great clock of London stayed dark at night. Then, one evening, the lights came back.

For most of the war, Britain had been in blackout. The great clock faces of Big Ben, on the tower of the Houses of Parliament, were lit again for the first time since 1939. The Speaker of the House of Commons switched on the lights, and the four glowing faces shone once more over Westminster. Crowds stood on the bridge and along the river to watch. St Paul's Cathedral, which had stood through the Blitz, was lit up, and its great dome seemed to float above the city. Children who had been born during the war saw street lamps lit for the first time, and some of them were a little frightened by the brightness, until their parents explained that the lights meant peace.

{img:74-demob-suit-tailor}

British soldiers leaving the army were given a complete set of civilian clothes, called a demob suit.

[pause]

After the war ended, millions of servicemen and women had to return to ordinary life. This was called demobilisation, or demob for short. Men were released in an order based on their age and how long they had served, so some went home quickly and others waited many months. When a British soldier was finally demobilised, he went to a large clothing depot, where he was given a complete set of civilian clothes. This included a suit, a shirt with two collars, a tie, a pair of shoes, a raincoat and a hat, often a trilby. It was known as the demob suit. The suits came in a few styles, such as a single breasted or a double breasted jacket, sometimes with a pinstripe. Because so many men left at the same time, the sizes were not always perfect, and many men came home with sleeves that were a little too long or trousers that were a little too short. But they were glad to have them, because clothing was still rationed. Some say that the phrase the full monty comes from this time, from the complete set of clothes made by the tailor Montague Burton, though no one knows for sure. Many men kept their demob suits for years, wearing them to weddings, to church and to work, as they slowly settled into peacetime life.

{img:75-vj-day-street-party}

The war in Europe ended in May 1945, and the war with Japan ended in August, after Japan announced its surrender on the fifteenth. In towns and cities all over the country, neighbours carried out kitchen tables and chairs and set them end to end down the middle of the street. Families pooled their sugar and butter coupons to make cakes and jellies. There were sandwiches with fish paste or jam, sausage rolls, homemade biscuits and big jugs of lemonade and tea. Children wore paper hats and played games, and in the evening there was often music from a piano that had been wheeled out of someone's front room. Bonfires were lit on bomb sites and street corners. In America, huge crowds gathered in Times Square in New York, where a famous photograph captured a sailor kissing a nurse in white. For people who had lived through six long years of war, these parties were a joyful, noisy and very welcome end.

{img:76-war-brides-ship-deck}

Tens of thousands of young women crossed the ocean after the war to join husbands they had met in wartime.

During the war, millions of American and Canadian soldiers were stationed in Britain, Australia and other countries for months or even years. In 1946, special laws were passed to help them, and great ocean liners like the Queen Mary and the Queen Elizabeth were used to carry them across the Atlantic. They arrived in New York or Halifax and then travelled by train for days to small towns, prairie farms and big cities they had only seen on a map. {img:77-last-ration-books-1954}

In Britain, food rationing did not completely end until the summer of 1954.

[pause]

Many people expected that rationing would end as soon as the war was over. In fact, in Britain it went on for years, and in some ways it became even stricter. The country was very short of money, and food was scarce all over Europe. Bread, which had never been rationed during the war, was rationed from 1946 to 1948. Potatoes were rationed for a while in 1947, after a terrible winter. Slowly, though, foods began to come off the ration one by one. Tea was freed in 1952, which was a great relief to the nation. Sweets were freed in early 1953, and children queued outside sweet shops with their pocket money. Sugar followed later that year, and eggs, cream and butter after that. Finally, on the fourth of July 1954, meat came off the ration, and rationing in Britain was over at last, nearly fifteen years after it had begun. Some housewives held small ceremonies and burned their ration books. Others kept them, tucked in a drawer, as a reminder. People who had grown up with rationing often kept the habits all their lives. They saved string and brown paper, scraped the butter thinly on their toast and never, ever threw away food that could be eaten the next day.

[long-pause]

{img:78-marshall-plan-ship-unloading}

After the war, America sent billions of dollars to help rebuild Europe.

[pause]

When the war ended, much of Europe was exhausted. Factories, railways, bridges and homes had been destroyed, and many people did not have enough food or fuel. In June 1947, the American Secretary of State, George Marshall, gave a speech at Harvard University. He proposed that the United States should help the countries of Europe recover, if they would work together on a plan. The result was the European Recovery Program, which became known as the Marshall Plan. Between 1948 and 1952, the United States sent around thirteen billion dollars in aid to sixteen European countries, which would be well over a hundred billion today. The aid came as food, fuel, machinery, raw materials and loans. Ships arrived in European ports carrying grain, cotton and tractors, and the crates were often marked with a small shield of stars and stripes and the words, for European recovery, supplied by the United States of America. The Netherlands, Britain, France, Italy and West Germany were among the countries that received the most. The plan helped factories start again, farms grow more food and cities rebuild their homes. In 1953, George Marshall was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize, one of very few soldiers ever to receive it.

[long-pause]

{img:79-un-charter-signing-1945}

The United Nations was founded in San Francisco in the summer of 1945.

[pause]

Even before the war was over, leaders of the Allied countries began to plan for a new organisation, one that might help prevent another world war. In April 1945, delegates from fifty countries met in San Francisco, in the War Memorial Opera House and the Veterans Building. For two months they discussed, argued and wrote. On the twenty-sixth of June 1945, they signed the Charter of the United Nations. The Charter begins with the words, We the peoples of the United Nations, determined to save succeeding generations from the scourge of war. It came into force on the twenty-fourth of October that year, a date still celebrated each year as United Nations Day. The first meetings of the General Assembly were held in London in January 1946, in Methodist Central Hall in Westminster. Later, the United Nations moved to a new headquarters by the East River in New York. In its garden stands a Japanese peace bell, which was cast from coins and medals donated by people from many countries and presented in 1954. Every year, it is rung on the first day of spring and on the International Day of Peace. The sound is soft and clear, and it lingers in the air for a long time after it is struck.

[long-pause]

{img:80-coventry-cathedral-ruins-evening}

The ruins of Coventry Cathedral became a place of peace and reconciliation.

[pause]

On the night of the fourteenth of November, 1940, the city of Coventry in the English Midlands was heavily bombed, and its medieval cathedral of Saint Michael was destroyed. Only the outer walls and the tall stone spire were left standing. The next morning, the cathedral stonemason, Jock Forbes, noticed two charred oak beams from the roof lying in the shape of a cross. He tied them together and set them up in the ruins. A local priest made another cross from three large medieval nails found among the rubble. This became known as the Cross of Nails. The cathedral's provost, Richard Howard, made a remarkable decision. Rather than speak of revenge, he spoke of forgiveness and of building a kinder world after the war. Behind the altar of the ruins, the words Father Forgive were carved into the wall. After the war, a new cathedral was built beside the old one, and it was consecrated in 1962. The ruins were kept as they were, open to the sky. Over the years, Crosses of Nails have been given to churches and communities around the world, including in Germany, as a sign of friendship. Today, people walk quietly through the old cathedral's roofless nave, where grass grows between the stones and pigeons nest in the empty windows.

[long-pause]

{img:81-prefab-houses-garden}

Over a hundred and fifty thousand small factory-built houses were put up quickly after the war.

[pause]

When the war ended, Britain had a great shortage of homes. Many houses had been destroyed or damaged, and few new ones had been built during the war years. Returning servicemen and young families needed somewhere to live, and fast. One answer was the prefab, a small prefabricated bungalow made in factories, often in old aircraft factories, and then delivered to the site in sections on the back of a lorry. A team of workers could put one together in a matter of days. Around one hundred and fifty six thousand of them were built under a temporary housing programme between 1945 and 1949. They were only meant to last about ten years. But many people loved them. For families who had lived in damp, crowded houses, a prefab felt like a small palace. It had a modern kitchen with a built-in fridge, which was a great novelty, an indoor bathroom with hot running water, two bedrooms and a little garden at the front and back. Families planted roses and vegetables and kept them spotless. Many prefabs lasted much longer than planned. Some families lived in them for forty years or more, and a small number are still lived in today. One estate in south London has even been carefully preserved, so that people can remember these little homes and the hope they gave.

[long-pause]

{img:82-rubble-women-berlin}

In Germany, women cleared the rubble from bombed cities by hand, one brick at a time.

[pause]

At the end of the war, many German cities lay in ruins. In Berlin alone, there were tens of millions of cubic metres of rubble. With so many men dead, wounded or held as prisoners of war, much of the work of clearing it fell to women. They became known as Trümmerfrauen, the rubble women. Working in teams, they climbed over the ruins of houses and passed bricks from hand to hand in long lines. They knocked the old mortar off each brick with small hammers, so that it could be used again, and stacked them neatly in piles. They pushed the broken pieces away in carts and small railway wagons. The work was hard, dusty and poorly paid, but it often came with a better food ration card, which was very important in those hungry years. Much of the rubble was carried to the edges of the cities and piled up into great hills. In Berlin, one of these hills, the Teufelsberg, rises high above the forest on the western edge of the city. Over the years, trees and grass have grown over it, and today people walk their dogs there, fly kites and look out over the city. Many German towns have small memorials to the rubble women, often a simple statue of a woman with a hammer, sitting quietly on a stack of bricks.

[long-pause]

{img:83-candy-bomber-parachutes}

During the Berlin Airlift, one pilot dropped something unexpected from the sky. Sweets, on tiny parachutes.

[pause]

It is 1948. The war has been over for three years. But now, the city of West Berlin is cut off. All the roads, railways and canals into the city have been closed. More than two million people have no way to get food or coal. There is only one way in. By air. So the Americans, the British and their friends start flying in everything the city needs. Flour. Coal. Milk. Medicine. Day and night, a plane lands every few minutes. One day, an American pilot named Gail Halvorsen sees a group of children standing behind the fence at the airport. He only has two sticks of chewing gum. He gives them to the children. And he watches as they carefully share them, tearing the wrappers into tiny pieces so that everyone gets something. He makes them a promise. Tomorrow, I will drop sweets from my plane. How will we know which plane is yours, they ask? I will wiggle my wings, he says. That night, he ties chocolate bars to tiny parachutes made from handkerchiefs. And the next day, as his plane comes in to land, it wiggles its wings. The children called him Uncle Wiggly Wings.

[long-pause]

{img:84-anne-frank-house-canal}

A young girl kept a diary in a hidden room in Amsterdam. Behind a bookcase.

[pause]

In June 1942, a girl in Amsterdam named Anne Frank gets a red and white checked notebook for her thirteenth birthday. She decides to use it as a diary. A few weeks later, her family must hide. They move into a set of secret rooms at the back of her father's office building, beside a canal. The door to the hiding place is hidden behind a bookcase that swings open. Eight people live there. For more than two years, they must be very quiet during the day, so that the workers downstairs will not hear them. A few brave office workers bring them food and news. And every day, Anne writes. She writes to an imaginary friend she calls Kitty. About her family. About growing up. About her dream of becoming a writer. From the attic window, she can see the top of a big chestnut tree, and it comforts her. In August 1944, the hiding place is found. Anne did not survive the war. But one of the helpers, Miep Gies, kept her diary safe. Her father published it, and today it has been read by millions of people, in more than seventy languages.

[long-pause]

{img:85-normandy-beach-evening}

The beaches of Normandy are quiet places today, where families play in the sand.

[pause]

If you visit Normandy today, the famous landing beaches look very peaceful. Their old code names are still used, Utah, Omaha, Gold, Juno and Sword, but most of the time they are simply long, wide stretches of sand, with dunes, small seaside villages and holiday homes. In summer, children build sandcastles, people swim in the cool sea and horses are ridden along the water's edge at low tide. Here and there, you can still see traces of the war: an old concrete bunker half buried in the dunes, a memorial stone, a tank standing quietly on a village green. Above Omaha Beach, at Colleville-sur-Mer, lies the American cemetery, where nearly ten thousand white marble crosses and stars of David stand in long, perfect rows on a green lawn, looking out over the sea. Along the coast there are British, Canadian, French, Polish and German cemeteries too, all very carefully tended. Every year in early June, veterans, families and visitors from many countries come to remember. There are ceremonies, wreaths and the sound of bagpipes on the cliffs. Local people often put out flags and flowers. Many families in Normandy still invite visitors into their homes and tell them, quietly and kindly, what their grandparents told them about the summer of 1944.

[long-pause]

{img:86-churchill-war-rooms-map-room}

The secret underground rooms where Churchill worked were left almost exactly as they were in 1945.

[pause]

Beneath the government buildings of Whitehall in London lies a set of basement rooms that were used as a secure headquarters during the war. They were known as the Cabinet War Rooms. Here, Winston Churchill, his ministers and his staff met, planned and worked, often through the night. There was a Cabinet Room with a large table and chairs, a small bedroom for Churchill, though he rarely slept there, and a tiny room disguised as a private lavatory, which actually held a secret telephone line to the President of the United States. The most important space was the Map Room. Officers kept it staffed day and night, marking the movements of ships, armies and convoys with coloured pins on huge wall maps. There was a row of telephones in different colours, known as the beauty chorus. On the sixteenth of August 1945, the day after the war ended, the lights in the Map Room were switched off, the door was closed and the rooms were left as they were. The maps, the pins, the papers and even the sugar cubes hidden in a desk drawer by one of the officers stayed in place. Today, the rooms are a museum. Visitors walk through the quiet corridors and look into the Map Room, where the maps are still pinned to the walls, just as they were left on that summer day.

[long-pause]

{img:87-bletchley-veterans-reunion}

Many code breakers kept their wartime work secret for more than thirty years. Not even their families knew.

[pause]

When the war ends, the people of Bletchley Park go home. They become teachers, librarians, office workers and mothers. And they say nothing. When their families ask what they did in the war, they give a small smile. I did some office work in the country, they say. That is all. Years go by. Then decades. Children grow up. Grandchildren are born. And still, the secret is kept. Some husbands and wives both worked there, and never told each other. Then, in 1974, a book is published. For the first time, it tells the story of Bletchley Park and the breaking of the codes. Suddenly, these quiet old men and women can begin to speak. Some grown children are amazed to learn that their gentle mother once worked through the night on secret enemy messages. Even then, many find it hard to talk, after so many years of silence. Later, many of them came back to Bletchley Park for reunions. They sat together on the lawn by the lake, drinking tea, and sharing the stories they had waited so long to tell.

[long-pause]

{img:88-veteran-telling-grandchild}

Today, the voices of veterans are kept safe in great collections of recorded memories.

[pause]

In the years after the war, many people who had lived through it did not talk about it very much. They wanted to get on with their lives, and some memories were hard to share. But as the years passed and they grew older, many veterans and civilians began to feel that their stories should be remembered. Museums, libraries and universities began to record them. The Imperial War Museum in London has gathered tens of thousands of hours of recorded interviews, with soldiers, sailors, airmen, nurses, factory workers, evacuees and people who lived through the Blitz. In the United States, the Library of Congress started the Veterans History Project in 2000, and it has collected over a hundred thousand personal stories, letters and photographs. Many of these recordings are very ordinary and very human. People talk about the food, the friends they made, the songs they sang, the dances they went to and the first time they saw the sea. They talk about being cold, being bored and being homesick. They laugh at old jokes. Some of the last veterans of the war are still alive today, now well over a hundred years old. When they are gone, their voices will remain, quietly speaking from the archives, telling the next generations what it was like to be young in those years.

[long-pause]

{img:89-seaside-summer-1946}

For many families, the first summers after the war brought the first seaside holiday in years.

[pause]

During the war, many of Britain's beaches had been closed. They were covered with barbed wire, concrete blocks and warning signs, and some seaside piers had been partly taken apart so that they could not be used by an enemy landing. Families had not been able to go to the sea for years. After the war, slowly, the beaches were cleared. The wire was rolled up and the blocks were taken away. And in the summers of 1946 and 1947, families went back to the seaside in their millions. They travelled by crowded trains and charabancs to Blackpool, Brighton, Scarborough, Margate and countless other towns. There were striped deckchairs on the sand, donkeys giving rides, Punch and Judy shows and ice cream, although sometimes there was not very much of it. Holiday camps, like those run by Billy Butlin, became hugely popular, offering cheap holidays with meals, dances, games and entertainment for the whole family. Children who had been born during the war saw the sea for the first time, and paddled in the cold water with their trousers rolled up. Mothers and fathers sat in deckchairs with a newspaper or a flask of tea, and simply watched the waves. After everything the country had been through, a quiet afternoon by the sea seemed like the greatest luxury in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:90-quiet-village-evening-peace}

In almost every town and village, a small stone memorial quietly keeps the names.

[pause]

Across Britain, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Canada, Australia, America and many other countries, there are memorials to the people who lived and died in the war. Many of them were first put up after the First World War, and then new names were added after the Second. Some are grand monuments in city squares. But most are small and simple: a stone cross on a village green, a plaque on a church wall, a list of names in a school hall or a railway station. Often, the same surnames appear more than once, brothers or cousins from the same family. Every November, and in many countries in May as well, people gather around these memorials. They lay wreaths of poppies or flowers, read the names aloud and stand in silence for two minutes. In the quiet, you can hear the wind in the trees and the birds in the hedges. The rest of the year, the memorials simply stand there, as people walk their dogs, children cycle past and neighbours stop to chat. The war has now passed out of living memory for most people. But in these small stone monuments, in the letters kept in old boxes, in the vegetable gardens, the old shelters and the stories told by grandparents, the memory of those years is still quietly kept, like a lamp left burning in a window.
