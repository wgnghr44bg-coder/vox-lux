IMG: counting-winters | A weathered farmer and his wife stand at a wooden farm gate at dusk in early autumn, golden birch leaves falling, a grey fjord behind them, smoke rising from a distant turf-roofed longhouse, warm low light.
HEAD: The Vikings counted their years in winters.
BODY: When a Norse person spoke of age, they often counted winters rather than summers. A man might be called twelve winters old, and a long life was a life of many winters. This tells us something about how the northern year felt. Most people in the Viking world seem to have thought of the year as having two great halves, a summer and a winter. Spring and autumn were short bridges. The old Norse word for summer is sumar, and the word for winter is vetr, and both are close cousins of the words we use today. The winter half was the one that mattered most, because it was the one that tested a household. Summer brought light and travel. Winter asked whether the barn was full, whether the roof was sound and whether the wood pile was high enough. If a family came through it, that was worth counting. So the winters became the marks on the long stick of a life. A wish for a good winter was a kind of blessing, a hope for warmth and enough food. In the days to come, we will follow one household through all those dark months, beginning with the work of autumn, when every hand on the farm turned toward the cold that was coming. A person who has counted many winters has also counted many returns of the light.

IMG: last-hay | Farm workers in wool tunics rake the last golden hay in a green meadow beside a fjord, a wooden hay rack and wooden rakes, low autumn sun, soft mist on the water, calm cinematic mood.
HEAD: The last hay of autumn decided how many animals would live through winter.
BODY: On a Viking farm, the grass of summer was the most valuable crop of all. People cut it with short iron scythes, and archaeologists have found many of these blades. They spread the cut grass on the ground to dry in the sun and the wind, and they turned it with wooden rakes so that no damp pocket was left inside. Damp hay could rot or heat up in the barn, so the work was careful and slow. When the hay was dry and sweet, it was carried in bundles to the farm and stacked in barns or in tall stacks near the house, sometimes covered to keep the rain off. Every cow, sheep, goat and horse would eat from that store through the cold months, when the fields lay white and bare. A farmer had to guess how long the winter would be, and the guess was never certain. Some hay was saved for the late weeks, when the animals were thin and the snow still lay deep. Leaves from trees were sometimes gathered as extra feed too, and the sagas hint that a hard spring could be harder than the winter itself. The smell of dry grass hung over the farm in these days, green and warm like a memory of summer. Every stack was a small promise made to the animals before the first snow.

IMG: autumn-larder | Inside a dim storehouse, a farmwife in a linen headdress hangs smoked mutton and sides of meat from wooden beams beside salt barrels, amber lantern light, steam from a pot outside the door, calm homely scene.
HEAD: Autumn was the month when meat was saved for the cold.
BODY: In the old Icelandic calendar, the month around late October was called Gormánuðr, and many scholars connect the name with slaughter. It was the time when a farmer decided which animals could be fed through the winter, and which could not. Only the strongest were kept, and the others were used for food, quietly and with respect, as the cold drew in. Nothing was wasted. The meat was cut up and preserved in several ways. Some was salted, if salt could be had, and salt was precious. Some was hung in the smoky rafters of the house, where the fire cured it slowly over weeks. Some was dried in the cold wind. Other cuts were put in barrels or troughs with sour whey, which kept them fresh. Fat was melted and saved for cooking and for lamps. Skins were set aside for leather, and the wool and horn and bone all found a use. A household that did this well could look at its full storehouse with calm satisfaction. The air in the farmyard would smell of wood smoke and of cooking pots, and children carried wood to the kitchen fires. We cannot know every detail, but the habit seems clear. A winter pantry was built from many small, careful decisions in the cooling days of autumn.

IMG: stockfish-racks | Wooden racks on a rocky northern shore hold rows of split cod drying in cold sea wind, a small fishing boat pulled up on pebbles, pale golden dusk light over calm water, a lone fisherman tending the racks.
HEAD: Fish were dried on wooden racks until they were hard as boards.
BODY: Along the cold coasts of Norway, people dried fish in the open air. The method is old, and it seems to have been used in Viking times. Cod was cleaned and tied in pairs by the tails, then hung on tall wooden frames above the shore. There was no salt and no fire needed. Only cold wind, clear air and time did the work. Over many weeks the fish lost its water and became stiff and pale, with a faint sweet smell like the sea. This dried fish is known today as stockfish, a word that probably comes from the wooden stocks or poles it hung on. Done properly, it could keep for months, even years. That made it a wonderful food for a long winter, and also for a long voyage, since it was light to carry. Archaeologists think that dried cod was traded far from where it was made, and that it fed people well away from the sea. To eat it, a cook would beat it softly with a mallet to loosen the fibres, then soak it and boil it. The cold months that spoiled so many other foods were exactly what helped this fish. On a still evening, the rows of pale fish on their racks must have looked like a quiet fence at the edge of the water.

IMG: whey-barrels | A cool stone-floored dairy room with wooden barrels and tubs, a woman in a plain wool dress ladling pale whey from a vat, round cheeses on shelves, soft window light, calm cozy atmosphere.
HEAD: Food was kept fresh in barrels of sour whey.
BODY: Without refrigerators, a Viking household had to think about how food could be kept. One of the most useful tools was a very ordinary one, whey. When milk was turned into cheese or skyr, a thin liquid was left over. If it was left to sour, it became sharp and tangy, and the sourness protected whatever was placed inside it. Later Icelandic writers describe meat, butter and even other foods being stored in sour whey, and many scholars think the habit was old. Salt was used too, where people could get it, and it was boiled from sea water or traded from afar. Barrels, tubs and troughs of wood held the food, and cool corners of the house or a half buried storeroom kept it from warming. Skyr, a thick, creamy dairy food, could be saved as well. Butter was packed away in wooden containers, and some of it was kept for a very long time. None of this was fancy, but it was clever, and each method used what the farm already had. The room would smell of milk and wood and a slight sourness, a cool, clean smell. On the shelves of such a room, the whole summer was kept in small, quiet portions, ready to be opened one at a time through the dark.

IMG: woodpile-peat | A tidy stack of split birch logs and drying blocks of dark peat beside a turf-walled longhouse, golden autumn light, a woman carrying kindling in a basket, mountains in the distance, calm rural scene.
HEAD: A winter fire needed a mountain of wood and turf.
BODY: The fire was the heart of a Viking house, and a fire is hungry. From autumn, people cut firewood and stacked it where the air could pass through it. Birch, alder and other local woods were common in many places, and charcoal was needed for the smith, too. In places with few trees, such as Iceland, Greenland and parts of the northern isles, people relied more on other fuels. They cut peat or turf from the bogs in blocks, then stacked them in small stands so the wind could dry them. Dung and driftwood were used where they could be found, and driftwood was a gift from the sea. Gathering all this was hard, steady work, and the children could help by carrying small loads. The wood pile near the house was often covered or set under eaves to keep the rain away. A household that ran short in the middle of winter was in real trouble, so people were careful to cut more than they thought they would need. The smell of cut birch is sweet and clean, and peat smoke is earthy and deep. When the stack was finished, a farmer could stand back and see the shape of the winter ahead. Each log or block of turf was a little stored warmth waiting for the cold to arrive.

IMG: turf-roof-repair | Men and women kneel on a sloping turf roof of a longhouse, laying fresh green sod squares over birch bark, ladder against the wall, soft grey-gold autumn sky, distant hills, peaceful working scene.
HEAD: Before the snow came, the turf roof had to be made weatherproof.
BODY: Many Viking houses, especially in the north and west, were covered with turf. A turf roof is thick and heavy, and it has a secret. It keeps warmth inside and cold outside in a way that thin materials cannot. Under the turf, builders laid layers of birch bark or similar material, and under that lay the rafters and boards. The bark helped shed water. Every autumn, a careful household climbed up to look for gaps, thin patches or places where the wind had lifted the edges. New squares of turf were cut from the meadow, with the roots still holding the soil together, and they were laid in place like a green patchwork. The walls needed care as well. Turf and stone walls could crack or slump after a wet summer, so they were patched and packed. Any hole in the walls would let in the winter wind, and the wind in the north can be sharp and long. Archaeologists think that such roofs lasted many years with this kind of upkeep. Grass sometimes grew on top, and it may be that a goat or two wandered up to graze, as farm tales from later times describe. When the work was done, the house sat low and calm in the landscape, like a green hill with smoke rising from it.

IMG: ship-ashore | A Viking longship hauled up on a pebbly beach on rollers, covered with a large wool sailcloth tent, men tying ropes under a pale evening sky, calm sea, golden light on the mast.
HEAD: In autumn the ships were pulled ashore and covered for the winter.
BODY: A Viking ship was a farm's greatest treasure, and it was never left to the mercy of the winter sea. When the autumn storms began, owners hauled their ships up onto the beach. They did this with many hands, rope and rollers made of logs, and a good deal of patient shouting. Once the ship was out of the water, it was often tilted or propped so that rain would run off. Some were pulled into a boathouse, a long building with a roof and a slipway, and archaeologists have found the remains of several in Scandinavia. Others were covered with sailcloth or turf, or simply tied down and left under the sky. The sails and oars and ropes were taken inside and stored in dry places. Wood that stays wet and then freezes can crack, and the planks of a clinker built ship were fastened with iron rivets and tied with fine rope, so careful care mattered. The sea that had carried people so far was now left to the wind and the gulls. A ship on the shore in winter has a patient look, sitting on its keel with its high prow turned toward the water. It would wait there, quiet in the snow, until the ice was gone and the days were long again.

IMG: market-closes | A small seaside trading place at the end of the season, merchants loading a last cargo ship under a pearly autumn sky, empty wooden stalls, bundles of fur and barrels stacked, gulls, soft cinematic light.
HEAD: When the last trading ships left, the markets fell silent.
BODY: Every summer, the Viking world was full of motion. Traders sailed between the market towns of the north, such as Birka, Hedeby, Kaupang and Dublin, and they carried furs, amber, iron, wool, silver and many other goods. Some traded as far as the lands of the east and the south. Then the days grew short, and the sea grew rough, and this movement slowed. Long voyages in late autumn and winter were dangerous, and most people seem to have preferred to travel in the sailing season, from spring to early autumn. So the last ships left the harbours with their final loads, and the market places grew quiet. Stalls and workshops stood empty, or only a few craftspeople stayed on through the cold. Archaeologists have found that in some trading towns, workshops for combs and jewellery were used all year, so not everything stopped. But the busy crowds and the shouting of sellers were gone. Merchants who lived far from home might spend the winter where they were, in a hall or a rented house, waiting for spring. For ordinary farmers, the ships leaving meant that no new goods would arrive until the thaw. Whatever the household needed now, it already had. The sound of the harbour thinned out until only the water and the wind remained.

IMG: gathering-wool-berries | A woman and two children gather last berries and hazelnuts in baskets at the edge of an autumn birch wood, a bundle of raw wool and flax stalks beside a wooden fence, golden low sun, calm country mood.
HEAD: The last wool, flax and wild fruits were gathered before the cold.
BODY: Autumn was also a time for small, quiet gathering. Along the edges of the fields and woods, people picked what the land still offered. Berries such as lingonberries, cloudberries and crowberries grew in northern places, and hazelnuts fell from the trees. Apples were grown in some regions, and archaeologists have found seeds that show people ate wild and cultivated fruit. Herbs were collected and dried for food and for simple medicine. Some were hung in bundles from the rafters, where their scent mixed with the smoke. At the same time, the household gathered materials for the work of winter. Wool from the sheep was sorted and kept in sacks, ready for spinning. Flax, the plant that gives linen, was pulled, dried and soaked, then beaten until the fibres could be used. There was no wasting these things, because the long evenings would be spent turning them into thread and cloth. Children went along with baskets, and their small hands were good at finding the last berries under low leaves. Some of this was light work, done on mild afternoons with the sun low in the sky, and the days still smelled of leaves and damp earth. By the end of the season, the baskets and sacks stood filled in the storeroom, holding the beginning of next year's clothing and meals.

IMG: building-longhouse | A timber and turf longhouse under construction in a green valley, builders raising wooden posts and weaving hazel wattle, bundles of reeds, a gentle stream, warm late afternoon light, peaceful scene.
HEAD: A longhouse was built from timber, turf and woven branches.
BODY: The typical Viking farmhouse was long and narrow, and that is why we call it a longhouse. Its shape depended on where people lived. In Denmark and parts of Scandinavia, builders raised strong wooden posts in the ground and made the walls from planks, or from wattle, which is a weaving of thin branches that was often covered with clay. In Iceland and Greenland, where good timber was scarce, walls were made from thick layers of turf and stone, with a wooden frame inside. Some houses had a gently curved outline, with the walls bowing slightly outward, and archaeologists have found several of these at places such as Trelleborg and Fyrkat. Roofs were held up by rows of posts inside, and then covered with thatch, wooden shingles or turf, depending on the region. The house might be twenty or thirty metres long, and some were much larger, though many were far smaller. A big hall could hold family, workers and animals under one roof. The builders did not have power tools. They used axes, adzes, saws, augers and good judgement. Each part of the house came from the land around it. When the last turf was set, a family could look at their home and see that it was made from forest, field and hill, shaped into a shelter strong enough to face the dark.

IMG: open-hearth | The warm interior of a Viking longhouse, a long stone-lined fire burning in the middle of the floor, soft smoke curling up into the rafters, wooden benches along the walls, amber light on faces, calm cozy atmosphere.
HEAD: A Viking longhouse had no chimney at all.
BODY: Inside the great room of a longhouse, a long fire burned in the middle of the floor. It lay in a stone lined hearth that ran along the centre of the room, and it gave both light and heat to the whole house. There was no chimney. Instead, smoke rose slowly into the roof space and found its way out through small openings, a gap in the ridge or through the thatch itself. Archaeologists think that the air inside must often have been hazy, especially higher up, so people probably sat and worked on benches lower down, where the air was clearer. The smoke was not only a nuisance. It cured meat and fish hanging in the rafters, it helped keep insects away from the timber, and it gave the whole house a deep, sweet smell of wood. The fire was also where cooking happened, where cauldrons hung from iron chains and where bread was baked on flat stones. People gathered around it, and the warmth reached out across the room in a slow circle. The glow on the walls moved gently as the flames shifted. A house without a chimney sounds odd to us now, but it worked, and it held families through countless winters. In its quiet light, the whole household shared a single circle of warmth.
