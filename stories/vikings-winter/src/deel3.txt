IMG: wool-tunic-cloak | A Viking woman and man in layered wool tunics and a blue cloak pinned with a silver brooch, standing by a turf longhouse door on a frosty morning, soft golden light, warm colours.
HEAD: Winter clothing was built in layers of wool.
BODY: Most Viking clothing began as wool, and in winter people wore several layers of it. Next to the skin, many wore a soft linen undershirt where they could get one. Over that went a long woollen tunic, which for men usually reached about the knee and for women often fell to the ankle. Men wore trousers, sometimes loose and sometimes narrow, and they wrapped the lower legs in strips of cloth wound neatly from ankle to knee. Women often wore a long dress that hung from the shoulders,. The outer layer was the cloak, a large rectangle of thick wool that could be thrown around the shoulders. It was fastened with a brooch or a pin, often made of bronze or silver, usually on the right shoulder so the right arm stayed free. Women often pinned their dresses with a pair of oval brooches. Wool is a clever cloth. It holds warmth even when damp, and it sheds a light dusting of snow. Dyes came from plants such as madder, woad and lichens, so a winter gathering could glow in dull reds, blues and yellows. A cloak could also serve as a blanket by the fire. Each garment was made by hand, thread by thread, and worn until it softened to the shape of its owner.

IMG: spinning-by-fire | A woman sits beside a glowing hearth in a longhouse spinning wool with a drop spindle, a basket of fleece at her feet, amber firelight on her hands, curling smoke.
HEAD: Wool was spun into thread by the fire all winter long.
BODY: The long evenings had a job in them, and the job was spinning. Before wool could become cloth, it had to become thread. A woman, or sometimes a child or an older man, would sit near the hearth with a bundle of cleaned and combed fleece tucked under one arm. In the other hand was a spindle, a slim wooden stick with a small round weight called a whorl, often made of stone, clay or bone. The spinner pulled out a few fibres, twisted them, and let the spindle turn and hang, so the twist ran up into the thread. Then the thread was wound onto the stick, and the whole motion began again. It made a soft whirring sound, like a very small wheel. Archaeologists find spindle whorls on almost every Viking farm site, which suggests how steady and common the work was. A single cloak or sail needed an enormous length of thread, so the spinning never really ended. It could be done while talking, while listening to a story, or while watching the flames. The hands knew the rhythm without help from the mind. The fleece smelled faintly of lanolin and smoke, and the thread felt slightly greasy and warm. Piece by piece, a basket of loose wool turned into something strong enough to cross an ocean, and the fire watched over every turn of it.

IMG: warp-weighted-loom | A tall wooden loom leaning against a longhouse wall, rows of clay weights hanging from vertical threads, a weaver standing and passing a shuttle, warm lamplight, dust in the air.
HEAD: Cloth was woven on a tall upright loom hung with stones.
BODY: The Viking loom was not a table-sized machine. It was a tall upright frame, leaned against a wall or a roof post, and a weaver stood in front of it much as a painter stands at an easel. This kind is called a warp-weighted loom. The long vertical threads, known as the warp, hung down from a beam at the top. At the bottom, each small bundle of threads was tied to a heavy weight of clay or stone, which kept the threads pulled tight. Archaeologists often find these loom weights lying in neat rows where a house once stood, and that is how we know where the loom stood. The weaver passed a shuttle with the weft thread through the warp, then pushed it upward with a flat wooden beater, often made of bone or whalebone. The cloth grew from the top downward, slowly, a hand's width at a time. Patterns such as diamond twill were possible, and they gave the wool a gentle ridged look. The clack of the weights, the creak of the frame and the pat of the beater made a steady sound in a winter room. Weaving a single length could take many days of work. When it was done, a whole season of thread had become one piece of cloth, and the loom stood empty, waiting for the next.

IMG: furs-and-felt-hats | A Viking man in a fur-lined hood, leather jerkin and round felt cap, holding a folded fox pelt in a bright storeroom, snow glittering outside the open door, soft warm light.
HEAD: Furs, leather and felt added warmth where wool alone was not enough.
BODY: On the coldest days, wool was not always enough, so people turned to what the land and animals could give. Furs were prized in Scandinavia, and the Vikings were known across Europe as traders of them. Pelts from fox, marten, squirrel, beaver and bear were sewn into hoods, trims and linings, and a fur cloak was a sign of comfort or wealth. The sagas mention fur-lined cloaks as valued gifts. Leather was another everyday material. Cattle, goat and sheep hides were cured and cut into belts, straps, pouches and jerkins. Felt was made by pressing and rubbing wool with warm water until the fibres locked together into a dense mat. It needed no spinning or weaving, and it was good against wind. Archaeologists think caps and hats were often made of wool, felt or fur, and finds from towns such as Hedeby and York show many small scraps of such headwear. A hat matters more than we expect, since so much heat leaves the head. A hood pulled forward made a little tunnel of warmth around the face. When someone came in from the snow, the fur would glisten with melting drops and smell faintly of damp animal and smoke. Hung from a peg by the door, it waited, soft and heavy, for the next walk out into the cold.

IMG: nalbinding-mittens | Close view of hands working a bone needle and thick wool yarn making a mitten, a pair of wool socks and leather shoes beside them on a bench, firelight, cosy winter room.
HEAD: Vikings kept their feet and hands warm with leather shoes and knotted wool mittens.
BODY: Feet and hands feel the cold first, so winter clothing paid close attention to them. Shoes were usually made of leather, often in a simple style called a turnshoe. The leather was stitched inside out and then turned the right way round, so the seams stayed on the inside, away from the weather. They were low or ankle-high and tied with a thong. Inside, people wore woollen socks, and a stuffing of dry grass or moss may sometimes have helped. The socks and mittens were often made by a technique called nålebinding, a Scandinavian word meaning binding with a needle. It is not knitting. The maker used a blunt needle, perhaps of bone or wood, and a length of yarn, pulling the thread through a series of loops to build up a dense, springy fabric. Fragments of this fabric survive from Viking sites, including a famous sock found in York. Knitting, with two needles, seems to have arrived in Europe much later. The nålebound cloth is warm and thick, and it does not unravel easily when snagged. A new mitten took patience and many evenings. The wool smelled of sheep and smoke, and the yarn slid slowly through the fingers. When you pulled on a pair of them, they held the shape of the hand that made them.

IMG: saturday-wash | A woman combing her long hair with an antler comb beside a wooden tub of steaming water in a warm timber room, soft steam, towels of linen, golden evening light.
HEAD: Even in deep winter, Vikings had a regular day for washing.
BODY: The old Icelandic word for Saturday was laugardagr, which means washing day. That tells us that bathing had a place in the weekly rhythm, even in the cold months. Water was carried in from a stream or a well and heated near the fire, then poured into a wooden tub or a trough. Some places had small bathhouses, and in Iceland, hot springs were later used for bathing as well. Soap was not common, but people used water, ash lye, and cloth to clean the skin. English writers of the time even complained that Danish men combed their hair and bathed every week, and thought them too neat. Combs were a valued everyday tool. Many were made from antler, carefully cut and riveted, and they were often kept in a small case. Archaeologists have found them in almost every Viking community, which shows that hair care mattered to both men and women. Combing also helped remove lice and tangles. Along with a comb, a person might own small tweezers and an ear scoop. After a wash, clean linen felt cool and soft against the skin, and the wool smelled fresh. A warm room, a clean shirt and neat hair were small comforts, and in the long dark of winter they were worth the trouble of carrying water.

IMG: antler-comb-carving | A craftsman carving a comb from reindeer antler at a workbench by a smoky window, shavings on the floor, small carved wooden animals, warm lamp glow, quiet winter workshop.
HEAD: Long evenings were spent carving wood, bone and antler.
BODY: When the work outdoors slowed, the carving began. Wood, bone and antler were everywhere, and a sharp knife was something almost every person owned. Wood was shaped into spoons, bowls, ladles, handles, pegs, toys and gaming pieces. Bone from cattle and other animals could be sawn, scraped and polished into pins, needles, beads and small tools. Antler was especially useful. It is hard but not brittle, and it could be split into plates to make combs, with the teeth cut one by one using a fine saw. Archaeologists find workshop waste, such as offcuts and half-finished pieces, in towns like Hedeby, Birka and York, and that shows that craftspeople made these goods for sale as well as for the household. Decoration was often added with simple tools. Curling lines, knots, animal heads and small circles were cut into the surface, and the style changed from place to place and from decade to decade. Carving needs a still hand and a steady light, so people sat near the hearth or a lamp. The soft sound of the knife and the pale curls of wood falling into a lap made a small, pleasant rhythm. A plain piece of antler could become something lovely, and some of those things still exist, held in glass cases today.

IMG: winter-smithy | A village smithy with a glowing forge, a blacksmith hammering a bar of iron on an anvil, orange sparks, snow falling outside the doorway, warm cinematic glow, no violence.
HEAD: In winter the smithy was one of the warmest places in the community.
BODY: The smithy was a place of fire, and in winter that must have felt welcome. Iron was essential to Viking life. It made knives, axes, nails, ship rivets, hinges, tools for farm and carpentry, and also the shoes and studs that kept travellers safe on ice. Many farms had a small forge of their own, and larger communities had specialist smiths. Iron in Scandinavia was often made from bog iron, which is a rusty deposit found in wet ground. It was gathered, dried, and heated in a clay furnace with charcoal until a spongy lump called a bloom formed. The smith then reheated the bloom and hammered it, driving out impurities and shaping it. Bellows blew air into the charcoal to keep the forge hot, and the metal glowed from dull red to bright orange. The sound of hammer on anvil carried across the snow, and the smell of charcoal and hot iron floated out of the open door. The sagas often treat smiths with respect, and some of the old stories say that smiths had a special kind of skill. Because the farm's work outdoors was limited, winter was a good season for making and mending. Beside the forge, neighbours may have gathered, simply to stand in the heat and talk for a while.

IMG: rope-and-sailcloth | Inside a large boathouse, women and men twisting rope and sewing a pale woollen sail laid across the floor, a ship hull on shore behind them, soft winter daylight through the doorway.
HEAD: Ships were mended, and rope and sails were made, indoors during the dark months.
BODY: A ship did not stop needing care just because it was out of the water. The Vikings hauled their vessels ashore and often kept them under covers or in boathouses, and winter was the time to look them over. Planks were checked, caulked with tarred wool or animal hair, and replaced where needed. Iron rivets were tapped back into place. Archaeologists think many of the long, careful jobs were done in this season, so that a ship would be ready for the first sailing days. Rope was twisted by hand from several materials. Walrus hide, sealskin, lime bast, which is the inner bark of the lime tree, and plant fibres were all used. Sails were made of woollen cloth, woven in long strips, then sewn together. The cloth was sometimes treated with fat or ochre to help it shed water and hold the wind. A sail for a large ship could take a great amount of wool and many months of labour, much of it by women on the farms. Think of the long, pale fabric spread across the floor of a boathouse, with several people stitching along its edges. The smell was of tar and wool, and the sound was the tap of a mallet. All of that patient indoor work was a promise made to the spring, written in thread and wood.

IMG: skis-and-snowshoes | A traveller on wooden skis with a long pole gliding across a sunlit snowfield beside a frozen lake, spruce woods in the distance, soft pink winter light, calm and clear.
HEAD: In the far north, people crossed the snow on skis, snowshoes and ice skates made of bone.
BODY: Deep snow can stop a walker, but the people of the north had old answers to that problem. Skis are very ancient in Scandinavia, and rock carvings and finds from bogs show that people were using them long before the Viking Age. In Norse tradition, the god Ullr and the goddess Skaði were both linked to skis and winter hunting. Skaði is sometimes called the ski goddess. Skis of the time were wooden, and a traveller used a single long pole or a staff for balance. They were used for hunting and for getting about, rather than for sport. Snowshoes, made with a light frame and woven strips, could also spread a person's weight over soft snow. For travel on ice, there were skates made from the leg bones of horses or cattle. Archaeologists have found many of them in places such as York and Hedeby. The bone was polished flat on one side, then strapped under the shoe, and the skater pushed along with a pointed stick. It is hard to know how much was done for fun, but a frozen lake must have tempted people. The snow made a dry squeak under the wood, and the air tasted of cold stone. Across white fields, such simple tools turned a barrier into a road.

IMG: winter-hunting-and-ice-fishing | A hunter in furs and a child at a hole in the ice of a frozen lake, a line in the water, hares and a bundle of fish on a sledge nearby, low golden sun, blue shadows.
HEAD: Winter hunting and fishing helped fill the larder.
BODY: Even in the cold months, the land and sea still gave some food, and people went out to find it. In the woods, hunters sought elk, red deer, hare and birds such as grouse. Snow made tracks easy to read, and skis let hunters move quietly and quickly. Hares could be caught by snares, and archaeologists think traps of many kinds were in use. On the coasts, seals were hunted for meat, oil and skins, and the sagas mention seal-hunting among the resources of farms near the shore. Some people also took fish through holes in frozen lakes and rivers. A hole was cut, a line or a net let down, and the catch hauled up into the pale light. Fish bones and fishing gear found at Viking sites show how much people depended on this food. Fresh food was precious in winter, since the stored supplies had to last until spring. A fish pulled from a lake in January would taste clean and sweet, and it would be cooked in the cauldron or on a spit that same evening. The quiet of a frozen lake, with only the faint creak of ice, is something modern fishermen in the north still know. The same patience, the same cold hands and the same small reward belong to a very old tradition, and it still links a modern shore to a Viking one.

IMG: sledge-and-ice-horse | A sturdy small horse pulling a wooden sledge loaded with firewood along a snowy track by a frozen fjord, breath rising in the cold air, pastel dawn sky, calm cinematic scene.
HEAD: Sledges and horses with studded shoes carried loads across frozen ground.
BODY: Winter closed many roads, but it also opened some. Snow and ice can make a smooth surface, and a loaded sledge slides much more easily than a wheeled cart through mud. Sledges were common in Scandinavia, and a fine decorated one was found in the Oseberg ship burial, which is dated to the early ninth century. Farmers used them to haul firewood, hay, hunted game and goods. A sturdy horse was the usual puller, and the Icelandic horse, small and strong, may be a descendant of those the newcomers brought. On ice, a horse needs grip, so ice-studs were used. Archaeologists have found iron spikes and special horse shoes of this sort, and some sagas mention them being used for races or fights on the ice. They were fixed to the hooves, or a pointed tool was used to give the animal a better hold. Harness bells and bits of metal gear have also turned up. The sound of runners on packed snow is a soft hiss, and the horse's breath rises in little clouds. A frozen river could serve as a highway for miles. In the longest, darkest part of the year, a plain wooden sledge and a patient animal kept the scattered farms and communities connected through the cold, bright weeks of snow.
