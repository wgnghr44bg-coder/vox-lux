{img:01-counting-winters}

Tonight we are going about eleven hundred years back, to the cold north of Scandinavia, where the Viking farms lie quiet under the first snow.

[long-pause]

On a small farm beside a grey fjord, a family is carrying the last armfuls of firewood in from the yard.

[long-pause]

The Vikings counted their years in winters.

[pause]

When a Norse person spoke of age, they often counted winters rather than summers. A man might be called twelve winters old, and a long life was a life of many winters. This tells us something about how the northern year felt. Most people in the Viking world seem to have thought of the year as having two great halves, a summer and a winter. Spring and autumn were short bridges. The old Norse word for summer is sumar, and the word for winter is vetr, and both are close cousins of the words we use today. The winter half was the one that mattered most, because it was the one that tested a household. Summer brought light and travel. Winter asked whether the barn was full, whether the roof was sound and whether the wood pile was high enough. If a family came through it, that was worth counting. So the winters became the marks on the long stick of a life. A wish for a good winter was a kind of blessing, a hope for warmth and enough food. In the days to come, we will follow one household through all those dark months, beginning with the work of autumn, when every hand on the farm turned toward the cold that was coming. A person who has counted many winters has also counted many returns of the light.

[long-pause]

{img:02-last-hay}

The last hay of autumn decided how many animals would live through winter.

[pause]

On a Viking farm, the grass of summer was the most valuable crop of all. People cut it with short iron scythes, and archaeologists have found many of these blades. They spread the cut grass on the ground to dry in the sun and the wind, and they turned it with wooden rakes so that no damp pocket was left inside. Damp hay could rot or heat up in the barn, so the work was careful and slow. When the hay was dry and sweet, it was carried in bundles to the farm and stacked in barns or in tall stacks near the house, sometimes covered to keep the rain off. Every cow, sheep, goat and horse would eat from that store through the cold months, when the fields lay white and bare. A farmer had to guess how long the winter would be, and the guess was never certain. Some hay was saved for the late weeks, when the animals were thin and the snow still lay deep. Leaves from trees were sometimes gathered as extra feed too, and the sagas hint that a hard spring could be harder than the winter itself. The smell of dry grass hung over the farm in these days, green and warm like a memory of summer. Every stack was a small promise made to the animals before the first snow.

[long-pause]

{img:03-autumn-larder}

Autumn was the month when meat was saved for the cold.

[pause]

In the old Icelandic calendar, the month around late October was called Gormánuðr, and many scholars connect the name with slaughter. It was the time when a farmer decided which animals could be fed through the winter, and which could not. Only the strongest were kept, and the others were used for food, quietly and with respect, as the cold drew in. Nothing was wasted. The meat was cut up and preserved in several ways. Some was salted, if salt could be had, and salt was precious. Some was hung in the smoky rafters of the house, where the fire cured it slowly over weeks. Some was dried in the cold wind. Other cuts were put in barrels or troughs with sour whey, which kept them fresh. Fat was melted and saved for cooking and for lamps. Skins were set aside for leather, and the wool and horn and bone all found a use. A household that did this well could look at its full storehouse with calm satisfaction. The air in the farmyard would smell of wood smoke and of cooking pots, and children carried wood to the kitchen fires. We cannot know every detail, but the habit seems clear. A winter pantry was built from many small, careful decisions in the cooling days of autumn.

[long-pause]

{img:04-stockfish-racks}

Fish were dried on wooden racks until they were hard as boards.

[pause]

Along the cold coasts of Norway, people dried fish in the open air. The method is old, and it seems to have been used in Viking times. Cod was cleaned and tied in pairs by the tails, then hung on tall wooden frames above the shore. There was no salt and no fire needed. Only cold wind, clear air and time did the work. Over many weeks the fish lost its water and became stiff and pale, with a faint sweet smell like the sea. This dried fish is known today as stockfish, a word that probably comes from the wooden stocks or poles it hung on. Done properly, it could keep for months, even years. That made it a wonderful food for a long winter, and also for a long voyage, since it was light to carry. Archaeologists think that dried cod was traded far from where it was made, and that it fed people well away from the sea. To eat it, a cook would beat it softly with a mallet to loosen the fibres, then soak it and boil it. The cold months that spoiled so many other foods were exactly what helped this fish. On a still evening, the rows of pale fish on their racks must have looked like a quiet fence at the edge of the water.

[long-pause]

{img:05-whey-barrels}

Food was kept fresh in barrels of sour whey.

[pause]

Without refrigerators, a Viking household had to think about how food could be kept. One of the most useful tools was a very ordinary one, whey. When milk was turned into cheese or skyr, a thin liquid was left over. If it was left to sour, it became sharp and tangy, and the sourness protected whatever was placed inside it. Later Icelandic writers describe meat, butter and even other foods being stored in sour whey, and many scholars think the habit was old. Salt was used too, where people could get it, and it was boiled from sea water or traded from afar. Barrels, tubs and troughs of wood held the food, and cool corners of the house or a half buried storeroom kept it from warming. Skyr, a thick, creamy dairy food, could be saved as well. Butter was packed away in wooden containers, and some of it was kept for a very long time. None of this was fancy, but it was clever, and each method used what the farm already had. The room would smell of milk and wood and a slight sourness, a cool, clean smell. On the shelves of such a room, the whole summer was kept in small, quiet portions, ready to be opened one at a time through the dark.

[long-pause]

{img:06-woodpile-peat}

A winter fire needed a mountain of wood and turf.

[pause]

The fire was the heart of a Viking house, and a fire is hungry. From autumn, people cut firewood and stacked it where the air could pass through it. Birch, alder and other local woods were common in many places, and charcoal was needed for the smith, too. In places with few trees, such as Iceland, Greenland and parts of the northern isles, people relied more on other fuels. They cut peat or turf from the bogs in blocks, then stacked them in small stands so the wind could dry them. Dung and driftwood were used where they could be found, and driftwood was a gift from the sea. Gathering all this was hard, steady work, and the children could help by carrying small loads. The wood pile near the house was often covered or set under eaves to keep the rain away. A household that ran short in the middle of winter was in real trouble, so people were careful to cut more than they thought they would need. The smell of cut birch is sweet and clean, and peat smoke is earthy and deep. When the stack was finished, a farmer could stand back and see the shape of the winter ahead. Each log or block of turf was a little stored warmth waiting for the cold to arrive.

[long-pause]

{img:07-turf-roof-repair}

Before the snow came, the turf roof had to be made weatherproof.

[pause]

Many Viking houses, especially in the north and west, were covered with turf. A turf roof is thick and heavy, and it has a secret. It keeps warmth inside and cold outside in a way that thin materials cannot. Under the turf, builders laid layers of birch bark or similar material, and under that lay the rafters and boards. The bark helped shed water. Every autumn, a careful household climbed up to look for gaps, thin patches or places where the wind had lifted the edges. New squares of turf were cut from the meadow, with the roots still holding the soil together, and they were laid in place like a green patchwork. The walls needed care as well. Turf and stone walls could crack or slump after a wet summer, so they were patched and packed. Any hole in the walls would let in the winter wind, and the wind in the north can be sharp and long. Archaeologists think that such roofs lasted many years with this kind of upkeep. Grass sometimes grew on top, and it may be that a goat or two wandered up to graze, as farm tales from later times describe. When the work was done, the house sat low and calm in the landscape, like a green hill with smoke rising from it.

[long-pause]

{img:08-ship-ashore}

In autumn the ships were pulled ashore and covered for the winter.

[pause]

A Viking ship was a farm's greatest treasure, and it was never left to the mercy of the winter sea. When the autumn storms began, owners hauled their ships up onto the beach. They did this with many hands, rope and rollers made of logs, and a good deal of patient shouting. Once the ship was out of the water, it was often tilted or propped so that rain would run off. Some were pulled into a boathouse, a long building with a roof and a slipway, and archaeologists have found the remains of several in Scandinavia. Others were covered with sailcloth or turf, or simply tied down and left under the sky. The sails and oars and ropes were taken inside and stored in dry places. Wood that stays wet and then freezes can crack, and the planks of a clinker built ship were fastened with iron rivets and tied with fine rope, so careful care mattered. The sea that had carried people so far was now left to the wind and the gulls. A ship on the shore in winter has a patient look, sitting on its keel with its high prow turned toward the water. It would wait there, quiet in the snow, until the ice was gone and the days were long again.

[long-pause]

{img:09-market-closes}

When the last trading ships left, the markets fell silent.

[pause]

Every summer, the Viking world was full of motion. Traders sailed between the market towns of the north, such as Birka, Hedeby, Kaupang and Dublin, and they carried furs, amber, iron, wool, silver and many other goods. Some traded as far as the lands of the east and the south. Then the days grew short, and the sea grew rough, and this movement slowed. Long voyages in late autumn and winter were dangerous, and most people seem to have preferred to travel in the sailing season, from spring to early autumn. So the last ships left the harbours with their final loads, and the market places grew quiet. Stalls and workshops stood empty, or only a few craftspeople stayed on through the cold. Archaeologists have found that in some trading towns, workshops for combs and jewellery were used all year, so not everything stopped. But the busy crowds and the shouting of sellers were gone. Merchants who lived far from home might spend the winter where they were, in a hall or a rented house, waiting for spring. For ordinary farmers, the ships leaving meant that no new goods would arrive until the thaw. Whatever the household needed now, it already had. The sound of the harbour thinned out until only the water and the wind remained.

[long-pause]

{img:10-gathering-wool-berries}

The last wool, flax and wild fruits were gathered before the cold.

[pause]

Autumn was also a time for small, quiet gathering. Along the edges of the fields and woods, people picked what the land still offered. Berries such as lingonberries, cloudberries and crowberries grew in northern places, and hazelnuts fell from the trees. Apples were grown in some regions, and archaeologists have found seeds that show people ate wild and cultivated fruit. Herbs were collected and dried for food and for simple medicine. Some were hung in bundles from the rafters, where their scent mixed with the smoke. At the same time, the household gathered materials for the work of winter. Wool from the sheep was sorted and kept in sacks, ready for spinning. Flax, the plant that gives linen, was pulled, dried and soaked, then beaten until the fibres could be used. There was no wasting these things, because the long evenings would be spent turning them into thread and cloth. Children went along with baskets, and their small hands were good at finding the last berries under low leaves. Some of this was light work, done on mild afternoons with the sun low in the sky, and the days still smelled of leaves and damp earth. By the end of the season, the baskets and sacks stood filled in the storeroom, holding the beginning of next year's clothing and meals.

[long-pause]

{img:11-building-longhouse}

A longhouse was built from timber, turf and woven branches.

[pause]

The typical Viking farmhouse was long and narrow, and that is why we call it a longhouse. Its shape depended on where people lived. In Denmark and parts of Scandinavia, builders raised strong wooden posts in the ground and made the walls from planks, or from wattle, which is a weaving of thin branches that was often covered with clay. In Iceland and Greenland, where good timber was scarce, walls were made from thick layers of turf and stone, with a wooden frame inside. Some houses had a gently curved outline, with the walls bowing slightly outward, and archaeologists have found several of these at places such as Trelleborg and Fyrkat. Roofs were held up by rows of posts inside, and then covered with thatch, wooden shingles or turf, depending on the region. The house might be twenty or thirty metres long, and some were much larger, though many were far smaller. A big hall could hold family, workers and animals under one roof. The builders did not have power tools. They used axes, adzes, saws, augers and good judgement. Each part of the house came from the land around it. When the last turf was set, a family could look at their home and see that it was made from forest, field and hill, shaped into a shelter strong enough to face the dark.

[long-pause]

{img:12-open-hearth}

A Viking longhouse had no chimney at all.

[pause]

Inside the great room of a longhouse, a long fire burned in the middle of the floor. It lay in a stone lined hearth that ran along the centre of the room, and it gave both light and heat to the whole house. There was no chimney. Instead, smoke rose slowly into the roof space and found its way out through small openings, a gap in the ridge or through the thatch itself. Archaeologists think that the air inside must often have been hazy, especially higher up, so people probably sat and worked on benches lower down, where the air was clearer. The smoke was not only a nuisance. It cured meat and fish hanging in the rafters, it helped keep insects away from the timber, and it gave the whole house a deep, sweet smell of wood. The fire was also where cooking happened, where cauldrons hung from iron chains and where bread was baked on flat stones. People gathered around it, and the warmth reached out across the room in a slow circle. The glow on the walls moved gently as the flames shifted. A house without a chimney sounds odd to us now, but it worked, and it held families through countless winters. In its quiet light, the whole household shared a single circle of warmth.

[long-pause]

{img:13-wall-benches}

Along the walls of a longhouse ran wide raised benches that served as seats and beds.

[pause]

Step inside a longhouse and look past the fire, toward the walls. There, running the length of the hall, are long raised platforms. Archaeologists often find traces of them in the ground, a low strip of packed earth or a line of stones where the bench once stood. These platforms were built of wood or earth, edged with planks, and they were wide enough for a person to lie down. By day, people sat on them to eat, to work, and to talk. By night, the same places became beds. The platforms stood a little above the floor, which helped, because the floor was often damp, and cold air tends to gather low. Closer to the hearth meant more warmth, and a family would likely have had its own stretch of bench. Straw or dry grass may have been laid down for softness, with hides and woollen covers on top. The wooden edges were worn smooth by years of hands and elbows. Everything in the hall had more than one use, because the house had only one big room, and space was precious when winter kept everyone indoors. A guest might be offered a place on the bench, and that offer meant something. A bench was not only furniture. It was a small claim on warmth, and a quiet way of saying that a person belonged to the household.

[long-pause]

{img:14-wool-bedding}

Viking beds were piled with wool, fur and sheepskin, and some stood in enclosed wooden frames.

[pause]

A Viking bed was less a single object than a heap of soft things. At the bottom lay straw, hay, or perhaps a mattress stuffed with feathers or down, if the family could afford it. Over that went sheepskins, with the wool turned upward, and heavy blankets woven from wool in plain or patterned weaves. On the coldest nights a fur might be added, from fox, or sheep, or other animals of the hills. Archaeologists have found well preserved beds in rich burials, including some in the Oseberg ship burial, where carved wooden bed frames survived in the damp ground. Some beds in later northern houses were built like little boxes, with a board along the side and sometimes a roof. Whether the Vikings used such enclosed beds widely is not certain, but the idea of keeping warmth close to the body makes sense in a cold land. Pillows may have been small bags of feathers or wool. Wool was the great gift of the north. It holds warmth even when a little damp, and it has a clean, faintly sweet smell, like a field after rain. The weight of such covers pressed gently down, holding in the heat of a body like a hand. In a house where winter pressed against the walls, a bed was the one place where warmth could be made, kept, and carried through the dark hours.

[long-pause]

{img:15-night-fire}

The fire in a Viking house was never allowed to go out completely.

[pause]

In winter, a fire was not something to light fresh every morning. Making fire took time and patience, using flint and steel and dry tinder, so a household preferred to keep its flames alive. At night, someone would bank the hearth. This means the glowing coals were gathered together and covered with a layer of ash. Under that grey blanket, the heat could last for many hours, giving off only a dim red glow and a faint smell of warm ash. In the morning, a person would rake the ash aside, add a little dry kindling, and blow gently until the flames woke again. The sagas and later northern customs suggest that tending the fire was a household task, often given to the woman of the farm or to a trusted servant. Fire was precious for another reason too. It cooked the food, dried the wet clothes, and kept the damp from creeping into the timber. Wood was not endless, and so careful tending meant less waste. A house with a living fire felt different from a house without one, even in the dark. You could hear the small ticking of cooling stones and the occasional soft shifting of a log. Through the long nights, that low red light was a quiet promise that morning would come, and that the hearth would still be there to greet it.

[long-pause]

{img:16-oil-lamp}

Light in the long winter nights came from the hearth, small oil lamps and burning rushes.

[pause]

When the sun barely lifted above the horizon, light became a treasure. The main source was the hearth itself, but its glow did not reach every corner. For smaller tasks, people used lamps. Archaeologists have found simple lamps made of soapstone, a soft grey stone that carvers could shape into a shallow bowl. Others were made of iron, or of clay. They were filled with oil, perhaps from fish or seals, or with melted animal fat called tallow, and a wick of moss or plant fibre was set in it. Such lamps gave a small, steady, yellowish flame, and they probably had a strong, warm smell. Another source was the rushlight. A rush is a thin plant that grows in wet ground. Its soft inner pith could be dipped in fat and set in an iron holder to burn slowly. True candles of beeswax were rare and costly in the Viking world, and they may have been used mostly in churches later on. A woman spinning wool or a man carving a comb could work by such a flame, with the light falling on their hands. Everything beyond that pool of light faded into shadow. Yet a single small flame is enough to see the next stitch, and the next, and so the work went on, patient and quiet, one pool of light at a time.

[long-pause]

{img:17-high-seat}

In the middle of a great hall stood the high seat, flanked by two carved pillars.

[pause]

In a chieftain's hall, one place stood above all the others. It was the high seat, the seat of honour, and the Old Norse word for it is ondvegi. It was usually set on the long side of the hall, near the middle, and beside it stood two tall wooden posts. The sagas speak of these as the high-seat pillars, and they were often carved. Some stories say that settlers heading for Iceland threw their pillars into the sea and made their home where the posts drifted to shore. This may be partly legend, but it shows how much the pillars meant. The master of the house sat in the high seat, and an honoured guest might be placed opposite him. Where a person sat said something about who they were. Seats nearer the high seat were places of more respect, and seats farther toward the door were less so. Every meal, then, was also a quiet map of the household. The wood of the seat was often dark with age and with smoke, and the carved patterns of animals and knots caught the firelight and seemed to move. When the winter wind howled outside, the high seat remained fixed, solid and still. It reminded the household that a hall was more than walls and a roof. It was a place with order, where everyone knew their own place.

[long-pause]

{img:18-smoke-hole}

Smoke left a Viking house through a small hole in the roof, and light came through a tiny wind-eye.

[pause]

A fire without a chimney needs somewhere for the smoke to go. In many Viking houses, it drifted upward into the roof space and found its way out through gaps in the thatch or turf, or through an opening near the ridge. Some halls may have had a dedicated smoke hole, perhaps with a wooden shutter that could be moved to suit the wind. Archaeologists cannot always tell, because roofs rarely survive. The word we use for window comes from the old Norse vindauga, meaning wind-eye. It gives us a hint of how such openings were seen. They were small holes in the wall or roof that let in light and air, and that could be closed against the weather. Glass was rare in ordinary homes, although it was known and used for beads and for a few fine objects. A window might be covered with a thin stretched animal membrane, or simply a wooden shutter. The result was a dim house, with pale daylight in thin shafts and a gentle haze of smoke hanging high above. The air near the floor was clearer, which is one reason people sat low. A wind-eye is a lovely name. It makes the house sound like a living creature, with one small eye that opened toward the sky and watched the weather go by.

[long-pause]

{img:19-byre-animals}

In many farms, cattle and sheep lived under the same roof as the family.

[pause]

On some Viking farms, the animals did not stay outside in the snow. They lived in a byre, a cowshed, which was sometimes part of the same building as the family's hall, divided by a wall or a passage. Archaeologists have found such houses in Norway, Iceland, and Greenland, and in some cases the line of stalls can still be traced in the earth. The animals brought a gift to the household. Cattle and sheep give off a surprising amount of warmth, and their closeness helped to take the edge off the cold. There was a gentle smell of hay and milk and straw, and the low sounds of animals shifting and chewing in the dark. Hay gathered in summer was now carried in armfuls, day after day, to feed them. Cows were valuable, and their milk was the base of much of the winter diet. A farmer who looked after his animals well was looking after the household too. In the coldest months, the animals were fed with care, and the stored hay was watched like a savings box. Few things say more about winter farming than this closeness. The people and the animals depended on each other. The warmth of the byre was part of the warmth of the home, and both lasted through the dark because they were shared.

[long-pause]

{img:20-hall-dog}

Dogs, cats and horses were part of the winter household.

[pause]

A Viking farm was never only people. Dogs lay near the fire, guarded the animals, and may have helped to herd sheep. Archaeologists have found dog bones of various sizes, and some wealthy graves contain dogs buried with their owners, suggesting real affection. Cats were present in the Viking world too. Their bones have been found at trading towns such as Hedeby and Birka. Cats are practical animals on a farm, because they keep mice away from stored grain and dried fish. They also, no doubt, found the warmest spot in the hall and kept it. Horses were prized. In Iceland, the small, sturdy breed that descends from Viking horses is still known for its thick winter coat. Horses could stand outside in cold weather if they had shelter and feed, though some were brought into stables. The sagas treat horses with great respect, and mention fights between stallions as a favourite entertainment. In winter, the horse was a way to travel over snow and frozen ground, when it was needed. The sound of a dog's claws on the plank floor, the soft huff of a horse in the stable, the quiet shape of a cat on a bench, these were ordinary sounds of the house. Animals do not mark seasons by the calendar, and perhaps their calm made the long dark easier for everyone.

[long-pause]

{img:21-evening-meal}

Most Vikings probably ate two meals a day, one in the morning and one in the evening.

[pause]

Historians think that Vikings ate two main meals each day. The first was called dagverðr, which means the day meal, and it was eaten fairly early, after the first work of the morning. The second was náttverðr, the night meal, eaten in the evening when the day's work was finished. This pattern is described in some later Norse sources, and it fits what we know of farm life. In winter, with short days, the evening meal was the heart of the day. The household gathered in the hall, and food was brought from the cauldron. Bowls and spoons were made of wood, and drinking horns or cups were passed around. A plain meal might be porridge, bread, dried fish, or a stew with whatever meat and vegetables were at hand. Wealthier households had more meat and more variety. People ate with knives and spoons and their fingers, and there were probably no forks. There was talk, and perhaps a story or a song. A meal in the dark months was more than food. It was one of the few moments in the day when every person in the household sat together in the same circle of light. The steam from the pot, the clink of wooden bowls, and the murmur of voices made the evening feel full, even when outside the world was empty and still.

[long-pause]

{img:22-griddle-bread}

Porridge, bread and flatbread cooked on a griddle were everyday winter food.

[pause]

Much of what Vikings ate each day was made from grain. Barley, oats, and rye grew in the cooler lands of the north, and they were ground into flour using a hand quern, a pair of round stones. Grinding was slow, rhythmic work, and the sound was a low steady rumble. Porridge was probably the simplest meal. Grain was boiled in water or milk until thick, and it may have been flavoured with a little butter or honey, or with berries gathered in autumn. Archaeologists have found remains of bread in Viking sites, and also of flat cakes cooked on a griddle, a flat iron or stone plate placed over the fire. The dough might be mixed with barley flour, sometimes with a little ground bark or seeds, and sometimes with a pinch of salt. Loaves were baked in ashes or on hot stones. The bread was likely denser and darker than the bread we know, with a nutty, earthy flavour, and it kept well through the winter. The smell of toasting grain would have drifted through the hall, warm and homely. A person arriving cold from outside might have thought first of that smell. Bread is a humble food, but it carries a kind of promise. As long as the grain bins held enough, the household would be fed, and the cold could do little to change that.

[long-pause]

{img:23-ale-barrel}

Ale and mead were the drinks of the winter hall, and brewing went on through the cold months.

[pause]

Water was available on a Viking farm, and so was milk, but ale was the great everyday drink of the north. It was made from malted barley, which means barley that has been soaked until it begins to sprout, then dried and crushed. The grain was steeped in hot water in a wooden tub, and the sweet liquid was drawn off and left to ferment. Brewing was often a woman's task, and the sagas speak of skilled brewers with respect. Winter was a good time for it, because the fire was always lit, and the cool air was kind to the fermenting liquid. Mead was another drink. It was made from honey and water, and it was costlier, since honey was not easy to gather in the far north. Mead appears often in poetry as a drink of kings and gods. Both ale and mead were served in drinking horns and wooden cups, and in a rich hall perhaps in vessels of silver or glass. Archaeologists have found the remains of cups and horn mounts in many graves. A cup of ale was a sign of welcome. To be offered a drink when you came in from the cold meant that the household accepted you. In a hard season, a warm cup in the hand was a small, honest kind of comfort.

[long-pause]

{img:24-cauldron-skyr}

Skyr, butter, cheese and a bubbling cauldron of soup filled many winter meals.

[pause]

The cauldron was the centre of Viking cooking. It hung from a chain or a tripod over the hearth, and in it went whatever the household had. A soup or stew might hold barley, onions, cabbage, peas or beans, with salted or dried meat, or fish, cooked slowly through the afternoon. It would have been thick, savoury, and steaming. Milk gave other foods. Butter was churned from cream and stored, sometimes salted. In Iceland, butter was so valued that it was sometimes used as payment. Cheese was made from the curds of milk, pressed and aged. Skyr is a thick, tangy, soft curd that people in Iceland still eat today, and the word is old. It seems to have come from the Viking age. The whey left over could be used to preserve other foods and also as a sour drink. A household that had milk, butter, and cheese had real wealth in the winter months, because these foods were rich in fat and protein when little else was growing. Eating from a shared pot of soup meant that everyone received something warm. The smell of herbs drifting upward, the slow bubbling, the patient stirring, all this marked the hours of the winter day. A pot kept simmering on the fire was one of the plainest signs that a home was well and the family was together.

[long-pause]

{img:25-wool-tunic-cloak}

Winter clothing was built in layers of wool.

[pause]

Most Viking clothing began as wool, and in winter people wore several layers of it. Next to the skin, many wore a soft linen undershirt where they could get one. Over that went a long woollen tunic, which for men usually reached about the knee and for women often fell to the ankle. Men wore trousers, sometimes loose and sometimes narrow, and they wrapped the lower legs in strips of cloth wound neatly from ankle to knee. Women often wore a long dress that hung from the shoulders,. The outer layer was the cloak, a large rectangle of thick wool that could be thrown around the shoulders. It was fastened with a brooch or a pin, often made of bronze or silver, usually on the right shoulder so the right arm stayed free. Women often pinned their dresses with a pair of oval brooches. Wool is a clever cloth. It holds warmth even when damp, and it sheds a light dusting of snow. Dyes came from plants such as madder, woad and lichens, so a winter gathering could glow in dull reds, blues and yellows. A cloak could also serve as a blanket by the fire. Each garment was made by hand, thread by thread, and worn until it softened to the shape of its owner.

[long-pause]

{img:26-spinning-by-fire}

Wool was spun into thread by the fire all winter long.

[pause]

The long evenings had a job in them, and the job was spinning. Before wool could become cloth, it had to become thread. A woman, or sometimes a child or an older man, would sit near the hearth with a bundle of cleaned and combed fleece tucked under one arm. In the other hand was a spindle, a slim wooden stick with a small round weight called a whorl, often made of stone, clay or bone. The spinner pulled out a few fibres, twisted them, and let the spindle turn and hang, so the twist ran up into the thread. Then the thread was wound onto the stick, and the whole motion began again. It made a soft whirring sound, like a very small wheel. Archaeologists find spindle whorls on almost every Viking farm site, which suggests how steady and common the work was. A single cloak or sail needed an enormous length of thread, so the spinning never really ended. It could be done while talking, while listening to a story, or while watching the flames. The hands knew the rhythm without help from the mind. The fleece smelled faintly of lanolin and smoke, and the thread felt slightly greasy and warm. Piece by piece, a basket of loose wool turned into something strong enough to cross an ocean, and the fire watched over every turn of it.

[long-pause]

{img:27-warp-weighted-loom}

Cloth was woven on a tall upright loom hung with stones.

[pause]

The Viking loom was not a table-sized machine. It was a tall upright frame, leaned against a wall or a roof post, and a weaver stood in front of it much as a painter stands at an easel. This kind is called a warp-weighted loom. The long vertical threads, known as the warp, hung down from a beam at the top. At the bottom, each small bundle of threads was tied to a heavy weight of clay or stone, which kept the threads pulled tight. Archaeologists often find these loom weights lying in neat rows where a house once stood, and that is how we know where the loom stood. The weaver passed a shuttle with the weft thread through the warp, then pushed it upward with a flat wooden beater, often made of bone or whalebone. The cloth grew from the top downward, slowly, a hand's width at a time. Patterns such as diamond twill were possible, and they gave the wool a gentle ridged look. The clack of the weights, the creak of the frame and the pat of the beater made a steady sound in a winter room. Weaving a single length could take many days of work. When it was done, a whole season of thread had become one piece of cloth, and the loom stood empty, waiting for the next.

[long-pause]

{img:28-furs-and-felt-hats}

Furs, leather and felt added warmth where wool alone was not enough.

[pause]

On the coldest days, wool was not always enough, so people turned to what the land and animals could give. Furs were prized in Scandinavia, and the Vikings were known across Europe as traders of them. Pelts from fox, marten, squirrel, beaver and bear were sewn into hoods, trims and linings, and a fur cloak was a sign of comfort or wealth. The sagas mention fur-lined cloaks as valued gifts. Leather was another everyday material. Cattle, goat and sheep hides were cured and cut into belts, straps, pouches and jerkins. Felt was made by pressing and rubbing wool with warm water until the fibres locked together into a dense mat. It needed no spinning or weaving, and it was good against wind. Archaeologists think caps and hats were often made of wool, felt or fur, and finds from towns such as Hedeby and York show many small scraps of such headwear. A hat matters more than we expect, since so much heat leaves the head. A hood pulled forward made a little tunnel of warmth around the face. When someone came in from the snow, the fur would glisten with melting drops and smell faintly of damp animal and smoke. Hung from a peg by the door, it waited, soft and heavy, for the next walk out into the cold.

[long-pause]

{img:29-nalbinding-mittens}

Vikings kept their feet and hands warm with leather shoes and knotted wool mittens.

[pause]

Feet and hands feel the cold first, so winter clothing paid close attention to them. Shoes were usually made of leather, often in a simple style called a turnshoe. The leather was stitched inside out and then turned the right way round, so the seams stayed on the inside, away from the weather. They were low or ankle-high and tied with a thong. Inside, people wore woollen socks, and a stuffing of dry grass or moss may sometimes have helped. The socks and mittens were often made by a technique called nålebinding, a Scandinavian word meaning binding with a needle. It is not knitting. The maker used a blunt needle, perhaps of bone or wood, and a length of yarn, pulling the thread through a series of loops to build up a dense, springy fabric. Fragments of this fabric survive from Viking sites, including a famous sock found in York. Knitting, with two needles, seems to have arrived in Europe much later. The nålebound cloth is warm and thick, and it does not unravel easily when snagged. A new mitten took patience and many evenings. The wool smelled of sheep and smoke, and the yarn slid slowly through the fingers. When you pulled on a pair of them, they held the shape of the hand that made them.

[long-pause]

{img:30-saturday-wash}

Even in deep winter, Vikings had a regular day for washing.

[pause]

The old Icelandic word for Saturday was laugardagr, which means washing day. That tells us that bathing had a place in the weekly rhythm, even in the cold months. Water was carried in from a stream or a well and heated near the fire, then poured into a wooden tub or a trough. Some places had small bathhouses, and in Iceland, hot springs were later used for bathing as well. Soap was not common, but people used water, ash lye, and cloth to clean the skin. English writers of the time even complained that Danish men combed their hair and bathed every week, and thought them too neat. Combs were a valued everyday tool. Many were made from antler, carefully cut and riveted, and they were often kept in a small case. Archaeologists have found them in almost every Viking community, which shows that hair care mattered to both men and women. Combing also helped remove lice and tangles. Along with a comb, a person might own small tweezers and an ear scoop. After a wash, clean linen felt cool and soft against the skin, and the wool smelled fresh. A warm room, a clean shirt and neat hair were small comforts, and in the long dark of winter they were worth the trouble of carrying water.

[long-pause]

{img:31-antler-comb-carving}

Long evenings were spent carving wood, bone and antler.

[pause]

When the work outdoors slowed, the carving began. Wood, bone and antler were everywhere, and a sharp knife was something almost every person owned. Wood was shaped into spoons, bowls, ladles, handles, pegs, toys and gaming pieces. Bone from cattle and other animals could be sawn, scraped and polished into pins, needles, beads and small tools. Antler was especially useful. It is hard but not brittle, and it could be split into plates to make combs, with the teeth cut one by one using a fine saw. Archaeologists find workshop waste, such as offcuts and half-finished pieces, in towns like Hedeby, Birka and York, and that shows that craftspeople made these goods for sale as well as for the household. Decoration was often added with simple tools. Curling lines, knots, animal heads and small circles were cut into the surface, and the style changed from place to place and from decade to decade. Carving needs a still hand and a steady light, so people sat near the hearth or a lamp. The soft sound of the knife and the pale curls of wood falling into a lap made a small, pleasant rhythm. A plain piece of antler could become something lovely, and some of those things still exist, held in glass cases today.

[long-pause]

{img:32-winter-smithy}

In winter the smithy was one of the warmest places in the community.

[pause]

The smithy was a place of fire, and in winter that must have felt welcome. Iron was essential to Viking life. It made knives, axes, nails, ship rivets, hinges, tools for farm and carpentry, and also the shoes and studs that kept travellers safe on ice. Many farms had a small forge of their own, and larger communities had specialist smiths. Iron in Scandinavia was often made from bog iron, which is a rusty deposit found in wet ground. It was gathered, dried, and heated in a clay furnace with charcoal until a spongy lump called a bloom formed. The smith then reheated the bloom and hammered it, driving out impurities and shaping it. Bellows blew air into the charcoal to keep the forge hot, and the metal glowed from dull red to bright orange. The sound of hammer on anvil carried across the snow, and the smell of charcoal and hot iron floated out of the open door. The sagas often treat smiths with respect, and some of the old stories say that smiths had a special kind of skill. Because the farm's work outdoors was limited, winter was a good season for making and mending. Beside the forge, neighbours may have gathered, simply to stand in the heat and talk for a while.

[long-pause]

{img:33-rope-and-sailcloth}

Ships were mended, and rope and sails were made, indoors during the dark months.

[pause]

A ship did not stop needing care just because it was out of the water. The Vikings hauled their vessels ashore and often kept them under covers or in boathouses, and winter was the time to look them over. Planks were checked, caulked with tarred wool or animal hair, and replaced where needed. Iron rivets were tapped back into place. Archaeologists think many of the long, careful jobs were done in this season, so that a ship would be ready for the first sailing days. Rope was twisted by hand from several materials. Walrus hide, sealskin, lime bast, which is the inner bark of the lime tree, and plant fibres were all used. Sails were made of woollen cloth, woven in long strips, then sewn together. The cloth was sometimes treated with fat or ochre to help it shed water and hold the wind. A sail for a large ship could take a great amount of wool and many months of labour, much of it by women on the farms. Think of the long, pale fabric spread across the floor of a boathouse, with several people stitching along its edges. The smell was of tar and wool, and the sound was the tap of a mallet. All of that patient indoor work was a promise made to the spring, written in thread and wood.

[long-pause]

{img:34-skis-and-snowshoes}

In the far north, people crossed the snow on skis, snowshoes and ice skates made of bone.

[pause]

Deep snow can stop a walker, but the people of the north had old answers to that problem. Skis are very ancient in Scandinavia, and rock carvings and finds from bogs show that people were using them long before the Viking Age. In Norse tradition, the god Ullr and the goddess Skaði were both linked to skis and winter hunting. Skaði is sometimes called the ski goddess. Skis of the time were wooden, and a traveller used a single long pole or a staff for balance. They were used for hunting and for getting about, rather than for sport. Snowshoes, made with a light frame and woven strips, could also spread a person's weight over soft snow. For travel on ice, there were skates made from the leg bones of horses or cattle. Archaeologists have found many of them in places such as York and Hedeby. The bone was polished flat on one side, then strapped under the shoe, and the skater pushed along with a pointed stick. It is hard to know how much was done for fun, but a frozen lake must have tempted people. The snow made a dry squeak under the wood, and the air tasted of cold stone. Across white fields, such simple tools turned a barrier into a road.

[long-pause]

{img:35-winter-hunting-and-ice-fishing}

Winter hunting and fishing helped fill the larder.

[pause]

Even in the cold months, the land and sea still gave some food, and people went out to find it. In the woods, hunters sought elk, red deer, hare and birds such as grouse. Snow made tracks easy to read, and skis let hunters move quietly and quickly. Hares could be caught by snares, and archaeologists think traps of many kinds were in use. On the coasts, seals were hunted for meat, oil and skins, and the sagas mention seal-hunting among the resources of farms near the shore. Some people also took fish through holes in frozen lakes and rivers. A hole was cut, a line or a net let down, and the catch hauled up into the pale light. Fish bones and fishing gear found at Viking sites show how much people depended on this food. Fresh food was precious in winter, since the stored supplies had to last until spring. A fish pulled from a lake in January would taste clean and sweet, and it would be cooked in the cauldron or on a spit that same evening. The quiet of a frozen lake, with only the faint creak of ice, is something modern fishermen in the north still know. The same patience, the same cold hands and the same small reward belong to a very old tradition, and it still links a modern shore to a Viking one.

[long-pause]

{img:36-sledge-and-ice-horse}

Sledges and horses with studded shoes carried loads across frozen ground.

[pause]

Winter closed many roads, but it also opened some. Snow and ice can make a smooth surface, and a loaded sledge slides much more easily than a wheeled cart through mud. Sledges were common in Scandinavia, and a fine decorated one was found in the Oseberg ship burial, which is dated to the early ninth century. Farmers used them to haul firewood, hay, hunted game and goods. A sturdy horse was the usual puller, and the Icelandic horse, small and strong, may be a descendant of those the newcomers brought. On ice, a horse needs grip, so ice-studs were used. Archaeologists have found iron spikes and special horse shoes of this sort, and some sagas mention them being used for races or fights on the ice. They were fixed to the hooves, or a pointed tool was used to give the animal a better hold. Harness bells and bits of metal gear have also turned up. The sound of runners on packed snow is a soft hiss, and the horse's breath rises in little clouds. A frozen river could serve as a highway for miles. In the longest, darkest part of the year, a plain wooden sledge and a patient animal kept the scattered farms and communities connected through the cold, bright weeks of snow.

[long-pause]

{img:37-longhouse-household}

A Viking longhouse was home to far more than one family.

[pause]

A farm in the Viking Age was less like a family home and more like a small village under one roof. At the centre stood the household: a farmer, his wife, and their children. Grandparents often lived there too, and sometimes a brother or cousin who had no land of his own. Archaeologists think many farms also housed servants and farmhands, who worked in return for food, shelter and a little more. On a great farm, thirty or forty people might share the hall. The sagas also mention the quieter people, the shepherd, the dairymaid, the foster child raised by another family. Fostering was common, and it tied two families together with a bond of trust. The house had to hold all of them. Each person had a place along the wall, a bench to sit on, and a spot within reach of the warmth. By day the hall filled with the sounds of work, the soft thud of a loom weight, the scrape of a knife on wood, low voices over the cooking pot. By evening everyone drew closer to the hearth. Winter pressed many lives together, young and old, free and unfree, kin and stranger, and the long dark braided them into one household. Perhaps that closeness was the truest shelter the house gave.

[long-pause]

{img:38-farm-mistress-keys}

The mistress of the farm carried the keys to every store.

[pause]

On a Viking farm, the woman of the house held a quiet kind of power, and it hung from her belt. Archaeologists often find keys in the graves of women, and they think these show her duty over the locked chests and storerooms. In winter that duty mattered more than ever. Every sack of grain, every cheese, every barrel of skyr and every bundle of dried fish had to last until spring. She decided what was brought out, and how much, and for whom. The sagas describe women who ran farms with a firm and careful hand, especially when husbands were away at sea or at the assembly. She also oversaw the dairy, the weaving, the brewing and the care of guests. A key was a small thing, cold and heavy in the palm, but it meant trust. When she walked through the hall, the keys made a faint chime against her brooches and beads, and people knew she was near. Law sources suggest that a wife held real standing in the household, and that the keys were a sign of it. Her work was mostly unseen. Nobody would praise the stew that did not run out, or the bread that lasted until the thaw. Yet the whole winter depended on her counting. A bunch of iron keys, jingling softly in the firelight, may have been the most important tool in the house.

[long-pause]

{img:39-thralls-winter-work}

Thralls and farmhands kept the winter work moving.

[pause]

Not everyone in a Viking household was free. Many farms had thralls, people who belonged to their owners and who did a great deal of the daily work. Alongside them were free farmhands, hired for the season. Historians know of thralls mainly from laws and sagas, and the picture is not always clear, so we should speak carefully. In winter the work changed but did not stop. Someone had to carry water from the stream and break the thin ice on its surface. Someone carried hay to the animals, milked the cows, cleaned the byre and brought in peat and firewood. Others carded wool, mended harnesses, or ground grain on the quern until the sound rolled through the house like slow thunder. These were long, plain, repeated tasks, and a farm could not survive without them. Thralls ate the same porridge as the others, as far as we can tell, and shared the same hall, though they sat in the lower places, farthest from the high seat. Their lives were hard, and their own stories were rarely written down. What remains are tools, worn thresholds and a few lines in the law. When we picture a warm Viking hall, it is worth remembering that its warmth was carried in on many tired shoulders, one armful of wood at a time.

[long-pause]

{img:40-children-winter-play}

Viking children had chores in winter, and also toys.

[pause]

Winter on a Viking farm was a busy time for children, but it was not all work. Even small ones had tasks. They fed hens, carried small bundles of kindling, helped to wind yarn, and watched the younger ones near the fire. Older children learned the skills they would need as grown people. A girl might practise spinning and weaving beside her mother, and a boy might learn to whittle, mend a net, or care for the animals. Much of this learning was done by watching and copying, with no school at all. Yet archaeologists have also found small toys. There are tiny wooden boats, little swords made of wood, and miniature horses. Dolls were probably made from cloth and wood, and cloth rots, so very few survive. Children also played with knucklebones and spinning tops, and probably invented games of their own with sticks and pebbles. In the long evenings they listened. They heard old stories, learned names of ancestors and gods, and picked up verses, simply by being near the grown-ups. Some children were sent to foster families, where they learned new skills from another household. A wooden boat the size of a hand, carved by a patient adult and sailed on the edge of a bench, reminds us that even in a hard season there were small hours of play.

[long-pause]

{img:41-hnefatafl-game}

By the fire, Vikings played a board game called hnefatafl.

[pause]

When work was done and the lamp burned low, many Viking households turned to a board game. It is called hnefatafl, which roughly means the king's table. The board was a grid, and the pieces were small figures made of bone, antler, wood, stone or glass. One player had a king in the centre, with a few defenders around him. The other player had more pieces, and they set out to surround the king. The king, meanwhile, tried to slip away to the edge or a corner. It was a game of patience and quiet thought. Archaeologists have found boards and pieces in graves and settlements across the Viking world, from Scandinavia to Ireland and Iceland. Some boards were simple, even scratched on a plank, while a few were beautifully made. Nobody wrote the rules down at the time, so scholars have pieced them together from later sources, and not every detail is certain. Later, in Sweden, a similar game was still played long after hnefatafl was forgotten. A glass piece, smooth and cool in the hand, was set down with a soft click. Opponents sat close, shoulders touching, as the fire cracked. Perhaps this is one reason the game lasted: it asked nothing of the players except attention, and in return it made a long winter evening feel shorter.

[long-pause]

{img:42-fireside-storyteller}

Stories were told aloud by the fire, since most people did not write.

[pause]

In the Viking Age, nearly all stories lived in memory and voice. Writing existed, in the form of runes, but it was used for short messages, not for long tales. So when winter closed the roads, the evening entertainment was a person who knew a story. It might be a tale of gods and giants, of a family feud, or of a voyage to a far shore. Listeners knew many of the characters already, and part of the pleasure was hearing a familiar story told well. Later, in thirteenth-century Iceland, writers set down many of these tales as sagas. The sagas themselves sometimes describe people entertaining one another at feasts and gatherings, though we cannot be sure how closely the written tales match the spoken ones. Telling a story took skill. The voice rose and fell, a pause let the listeners lean forward, and a gesture might suggest a sword or a ship. A good storyteller was valued in a household and welcomed as a guest. Over many winters the same stories were told again, changed a little each time. They carried names, places and old advice from one generation to the next. Long before books filled shelves, a memory and a warm hall were enough. Each retelling was a small act of care, passing a piece of the past along.

[long-pause]

{img:43-skald-poetry-hall}

Skalds turned winter evenings into poetry full of riddles.

[pause]

Besides stories in plain speech, the Norse loved a very different art: skaldic poetry. A skald was a poet, often attached to a chief or a king, who composed verses to praise, remember or entertain. These poems were not simple. They followed strict patterns of rhythm, with lines that echoed one another in sound. The most famous feature was the kenning. A kenning is a short poetic phrase that replaces an ordinary word. The sea might be called the whale road, a ship might be the horse of the waves, and a poet's skill might be named the mead of poetry. To enjoy such a verse, a listener had to unpack the images one by one, a little like solving a puzzle. Many were drawn from myths, so knowing the old stories made the poems richer. Much skaldic verse survives because later Icelandic writers copied it into their books. Scholars think it was handed down by memory for generations first. On a dark evening, the voice of a skald, steady and rhythmic, would have filled the hall. Listeners nodded when a clever phrase landed. The poetry was meant to be heard, not read, and its sound mattered as much as its meaning. Perhaps the long nights gave such careful words a stage on which every small image could glow.

[long-pause]

{img:44-riddles-by-fire}

Riddles and wordplay made the dark hours pass.

[pause]

Another pastime of the long evenings was the riddle. People of the Viking world enjoyed asking a question in disguise and waiting to see who could solve it. Some riddles survive in Old Norse literature. In one famous saga, a riddle contest takes place between a king and a disguised stranger, and the questions are about things like a mysterious creature or an object that is described in strange terms. We cannot know how many of these were ordinary household games, but the taste for wordplay clearly ran deep. Kennings, the poetic phrases for familiar things, were a bit like riddles already. A listener who heard the swan's road had to think of the sea. Children probably learned to love such puzzles early, as they do in many cultures. A good riddle needed a clear picture, a small surprise, and an answer that felt obvious afterwards. Players might lean on the bench, frown in thought, and then laugh when someone guessed it. These were games of the mind that cost nothing and needed no tools, perfect for a house where light was precious. They also trained memory, since a riddle was often repeated and passed along. A dark hall in winter may have rung with these small mysteries, and perhaps the delight of solving one lasted longer than the lamp.

[long-pause]

{img:45-runes-on-stick}

Vikings carved runes on sticks and bone, not on paper.

[pause]

The Vikings had their own alphabet, the runes. It was made of straight lines, which suited carving into wood, bone, antler and stone. Most runes were not written on grand monuments. Archaeologists have found many small objects with short inscriptions, scratched onto sticks, bone pieces and tools. Because wood rots, we have only a fraction of what was once cut, mostly from places where it stayed damp or frozen. What do these messages say? Many are ordinary. There are names, owners' marks, short messages, and simple greetings. Some are prayers or charms. A few seem to be little jokes. In the town of Bergen in Norway, later in the Middle Ages, dozens of everyday rune sticks were found, which gives a hint of how common such writing was. During winter, when the fields were silent and work moved indoors, a knife and a smooth stick could carry a message or mark a possession. Carving runes took patience. Each stroke had to be clean, and the surface had to be right. Some stones carved for the dead are famous, but the small sticks tell us something warmer: that ordinary people could also leave words behind. A thin piece of wood, marked with a name, still speaks across a thousand years, saying simply that someone was here, and wished to be remembered.

[long-pause]

{img:46-primstav-calendar}

A notched stick helped people count the days of winter.

[pause]

How did people know what day it was, in a world with no printed calendar? Many used a calendar stick. In Scandinavia it was later known as a primstav. It was a flat piece of wood with notches along its edges, one for each day, and small symbols marking important dates. A farmer could move a finger along it and see how far winter had gone. The earliest surviving sticks come from the Middle Ages, so we cannot be sure how exactly the Vikings used them, but the idea of tracking time with notches and marks is very old. Archaeologists think people watched the sun and moon as well, noting where the light fell at midday and when the first quarter of the moon arrived. Counting mattered, since stores of food had to match the number of weeks until spring. A notch on a stick, cut each evening, meant one more day gone and one fewer to come. It also helped the household to know when a feast or an assembly was due. In the dark months, there is something steady about such a habit. A small knife, a quiet cut, a thumb run along the grooves. Time was not a clock on the wall but a line in the wood, slowly filling, and every household could see it.

[long-pause]

{img:47-winter-month-names}

Each winter month had its own old name.

[pause]

The old Norse calendar was built around the moon and the farm year, and the winter months had names that still sound strange and lovely. Our best source is Icelandic writing from later centuries, so historians speak carefully about how far back they reach. In this reckoning, the first winter month was Gormánuðr, often explained as the slaughter month, though some think it simply meant the month of entrails or of autumn work. Next came Ýlir, the month around Yule. Then Mörsugur, a name whose meaning is debated, perhaps relating to fat or to a time of feasting. After that came Þorri, the heart of winter, with its hard frost and its tired stores. Last came Góa, whose meaning is also uncertain, a month that led towards spring. Each month was marked by customs and small celebrations. In Iceland, people later held festivals at the start of Þorri and Góa. The names tied the year to the work of the farm, the weather and the animals. They told you where you stood in the long dark without any need of a clock. To say that it was Þorri was to say that the cold was at its sharpest and that the hay was dwindling. Old names, carried through many winters, gave each stretch of darkness a face.

[long-pause]

{img:48-stranger-at-door}

Even in the hardest winter, a stranger at the door was welcomed.

[pause]

Among the Norse, hospitality was a serious duty, and winter made it a matter of life. A traveller caught in snow, far from any farm, might knock at the door of a stranger. Old poems, such as Hávamál, speak about this directly. The speaker says that a guest who comes in cold needs fire, and that he who has travelled far needs food and dry clothes. The poem also advises good manners from both host and visitor. A guest should not stay too long, and a host should not make a guest feel unwelcome. We cannot say how closely everyone followed such advice, but the ideal was strong enough to be written down and repeated. Many farms lay a long way from any neighbour, and the road might be dangerous in the dark. A shared fire could be the difference between a bad night and a good one. The host offered a place on the bench, a bowl of porridge, perhaps a cup of ale, and then listened to the news from afar. Travellers brought stories, and stories were a kind of payment. In this way, news crossed the snowy country from farm to farm. A door opened to a stranger in the cold was a small promise that, someday, another door would open for you too.

[long-pause]

{img:49-winter-nights}

The Norse welcomed winter with a festival called Winter Nights.

[pause]

When the last days of October came, farms across the Norse lands marked the turn of the year with a gathering known as Vetrnætr, the Winter Nights. The name tells us how people thought. Winter did not simply arrive. It was welcomed in, like a guest who would stay for months. The sagas mention feasts at this time, and some describe a blót, a ritual meal in which food and drink were offered to the gods and to the powers of the land. Archaeologists think such gatherings were usually held at home or in a neighbour's hall, with family, workers and friends coming together. The harvest was finished by now. The hay was stacked, the barrels were filled, and the ships were drawn up on the shore. There was a sense of having done what could be done. Meat was cooked in the great cauldron, and the smell of it mixed with wood smoke under the dark roof. Horns of ale went from hand to hand. People spoke of the summer that had passed and of what the cold months might bring. We do not know every detail of the old rites, because most were written down long afterwards by Christian authors. Still, the feeling seems clear enough. Before the long dark began, people sat close together, shared what they had, and said a quiet thank you to the year behind them.

[long-pause]

{img:50-alfablot}

Some farms held a private feast for the elves, called the Álfablót.

[pause]

Among the stranger customs mentioned in the old poems and sagas is the Álfablót, the sacrifice to the elves. It seems to have been held in autumn, around the start of winter, and it was very much a household matter. In one saga, a farmer's home is closed to visitors while it takes place, and a traveller who knocks is politely turned away at the door. The woman of the house tells him that the family is holding an Álfablót and that he cannot come in. That small scene is one of the few windows we have into the custom. Scholars are not sure who the elves were. They may have been the spirits of ancestors, or powers tied to the farm and the land, or something in between. The old stories do not explain it neatly, and we should be careful not to pretend they do. What seems likely is that the family offered food and drink and kept the occasion quiet and within the walls. Perhaps it was a way of keeping good company with those who were thought to watch over the place. The fire burned low and steady, the family spoke softly, and outside the autumn darkness gathered over the fields. It is a gentle thought that even the unseen neighbours were remembered before the cold set in.

[long-pause]

{img:51-yule-midwinter}

Yule, or Jól, was the great midwinter festival of the Norse.

[pause]

Of all the seasons of the Norse year, Yule was the one that people looked toward most. The word was Jól, and it named a festival that fell somewhere around the middle of winter. Nobody today can say the exact dates for certain. The old sources suggest it lasted several days, and that it was tied to the turning of the sun, when the darkest part of the year had been passed. In the far north the days at this time are very short. The sun barely climbs above the horizon, and the light that comes has a soft, low, golden colour. A farm would have spent weeks preparing. Meat had been set aside, ale had been brewed, and wood had been stacked high so the fire could burn freely. Neighbours and kin travelled across the snow to be together. The sagas speak of Yule as a time for guests, for gifts, and for oaths spoken in front of others. Some old customs from this season survived long into Christian times, which is partly how we know of them. The word itself is still with us today, in the English Yule. When the hall was full of voices and warm light, the cold outside seemed a little farther away. The year had reached its lowest point, and the slow turn back toward light had quietly begun.

[long-pause]

{img:52-yule-feast}

The Yule feast centred on a roast boar and many toasts.

[pause]

At the heart of Yule stood the feast. The old sources describe long tables, plenty of food, and a great deal of drinking, and they give us a few particular details. One is the boar. The pig was linked in the stories with Freyr, a god of fertility and good harvests, and a boar was said to be a fitting dish for the season. The Norse tales mention a sacred boar, and later folk customs in Scandinavia kept the habit of serving pork at midwinter. How much of this goes back to the Viking Age is hard to say, so it is wise to speak with care. We do know that toasts mattered. Horns were raised to the gods, to the kings and chieftains, and to departed kin. The sagas also tell of solemn vows made over a cup, which were taken seriously by those who heard them. Picture the smell of roast meat in the warm hall, the sound of the fire popping, and the low hum of many voices. Bread, butter, cheese and ale were set out for everyone. The hosts wanted guests to leave well fed, and generosity was a matter of honour. For a few evenings the darkness outside was held back by laughter, song and a table that did not seem to run empty.

[long-pause]

{img:53-wild-hunt-sky}

Some Norse people saw Odin riding across the winter night sky.

[pause]

On wild winter nights, when the wind howled over the roof and clouds raced across the stars, people in the north told stories about riders in the sky. Later Scandinavian folklore speaks of a Wild Hunt, a ghostly company that rushed through the air at midwinter. Many scholars connect this idea to Odin, the old god of wisdom, poetry and the dead, who was known to travel on an eight-legged horse called Sleipnir. In the Viking Age itself, our evidence for such a hunt is thin, so it is best to say that the tradition grew from older beliefs and that its exact shape is uncertain. Still, the picture fits the season well. The wind in the thatch and turf can sound like galloping, and the long dark gives the imagination plenty of room. In some regions, people were said to leave a little food or a sheaf of grain outside for the passing rider's horse. That detail comes from much later times, but it shows how long the idea lasted. Inside the longhouse, the family heard the wind and perhaps shared a story or two. The door was shut, the fire was bright, and the sky outside belonged to older and larger things. A stormy night, when seen from a warm seat, can feel like a tale being told by the world itself.

[long-pause]

{img:54-house-spirits-mound}

Families believed their ancestors still watched over the farm.

[pause]

For many Norse families, the people who had lived on the land before them were never completely gone. Grave mounds stood close to farms, often within sight of the house, and they were treated with a certain respect. The sagas tell of a man who stayed near the mound of his father, and of others who spoke of the dead as neighbours. Some stories describe ancestors who remained within their mounds and were consulted on important matters. We should remember that these are tales, not records, and that beliefs varied from region to region and from person to person. There were also ideas about guardian spirits of the land and the household, known by various names. Later Scandinavian folklore speaks of small helpers living in barns and farmhouses, who were given a bowl of porridge in winter to keep them content. Whether the Vikings had exactly that custom is unknown. What seems certain is that the line between the living and the dead was softer than ours. The farm was a long chain of people, each looking after the same fields and walls. In the dark months, when the mound lay under snow and the wind moved over it, a family might feel that the past was nearby, quietly keeping watch, and that they themselves were only the latest to live there.

[long-pause]

{img:55-fimbulwinter-sky}

Norse myth spoke of a great winter called Fimbulwinter.

[pause]

In the old myths, there was a winter greater than any other, called Fimbulwinter. The poem Völuspá and the prose writer Snorri Sturluson both describe it as a time before the end of the world. According to Snorri, three winters would follow one another with no summer between them. Snow would drive in from every direction, the frost would be hard, and the wind would be sharp. The sun would give no warmth. These stories were told long ago, and they were later written down in Iceland, so the details come to us through a Christian age. Even so, the image would have meant something to people who lived through real northern winters. A year with a cold, late spring could be hard on farms, and the old fear of a summer that never came was perhaps rooted in daily life. Archaeologists and climate scientists note that the Viking Age saw some changing weather, though they are careful about drawing links to the myth. To the listener, Fimbulwinter was a story about endings and about what might come afterward. Even in the poem, the world is said to rise again, green and fresh. Told by the fire, in a house with a full storeroom, the tale of the great winter could be heard with a certain calm. The real winter outside would always, in the end, give way.

[long-pause]

{img:56-sun-moon-wolves}

In myth, Sól and Máni were chased across the sky by wolves.

[pause]

The Norse imagined the sun and the moon as two travellers, a sister and a brother named Sól and Máni. According to Snorri Sturluson, Sól drove a chariot pulled by horses across the heavens, and Máni guided the moon on its path. Close behind each of them ran a wolf, always hungry and always following. The poems say that one day the wolves would catch them, but that day would only come at the end of the world. This is a myth, of course, and it comes to us through medieval Icelandic writers. Still, it gives a lovely picture of the seasons. In deep winter, the sun seems to hurry. It rises late, skims low over the hills, and is gone again before long. In the far north the moon can seem to stay up for a long while, pale on the snow. It is easy to imagine how a people who watched these lights daily could see them as living, moving things with a task to do. Time itself was tied to this idea. Days, months and years were counted by the sun and moon, and the sagas often mention the passing of nights rather than days. When people looked up on a winter evening and saw the moon shining over the white fields, they were seeing an old companion still running steadily on.

[long-pause]

{img:57-northern-lights}

The Northern Lights shimmered over the Norse winter, with no name we can be sure of.

[pause]

Over the far north, on clear and frosty nights, the sky sometimes fills with soft green and pale violet light. Today we call it the aurora borealis. The Norse people must have seen it often, especially in Iceland, northern Norway and Greenland. Strangely, the surviving Viking Age texts say very little about it. A medieval Norwegian book called the King's Mirror, written in the thirteenth century, mentions the northern lights and offers a few guesses about their cause. It suggests, for instance, that they might be fire, or sunlight reflected from the ocean. That is later than the Viking Age, but it shows that people wondered about it for a long time. Some modern writers connect the aurora to the Valkyries or to the Bifröst bridge, but there is little firm evidence, so these ideas should be treated as guesses. What we can say is that the lights were there, rippling quietly above the roofs, and that no one could have ignored them. A farmer stepping out to check on the animals might stop for a moment and look up. The sky would be silent, cold and brilliant. Nothing in it needed to be explained. It was simply there, a slow glow moving over the snow, and the mind could follow it for a long time.

[long-pause]

{img:58-stars-navigation}

The long winter nights gave sailors time to know the stars.

[pause]

A great deal of Viking skill at sea depended on knowing the sky. Over the long winter nights, with the ships pulled ashore, people had plenty of time to look up. The stars were a kind of calendar and a kind of map. Sailors in the north knew that one star, which we now call the Pole Star, stayed nearly still while the others turned around it. The sagas and later texts mention sailors using the sun, the stars and the colour of the sea, as well as the flight of birds and the shape of clouds. Archaeologists have also found a small wooden disc at Uunartoq in Greenland, thought by some to have been a kind of sun compass, though other scholars doubt this and it may date from after the Viking Age. We should be careful about claiming too much. Nobody knows exactly how every voyage was steered. Yet it seems clear that watching the sky was part of the knowledge handed down in families. On a still winter night, the stars are sharp and many, and a person who knew them could name shapes and seasons. Without city lights, the sky is very dark and very full. Under it, a farmer or a sailor could feel the voyages of the next summer waiting, small and patient, among the lights above.

[long-pause]

{img:59-thor-thunder-winter}

Thor, god of thunder, was quiet in the cold months.

[pause]

Thor was one of the best known of the Norse gods. He carried a hammer called Mjölnir and was linked with thunder, storms and the strength that protected people and their homes. Many Vikings wore small hammer pendants as a sign of this protection, and archaeologists have found them in many places. Thunderstorms in the north tend to come in the warmer months, so in the dead of winter the sky is usually silent in that way. The snow lies heavy, the air is still and the thunder is gone. It would be natural for people to feel that the great hammer was held back until spring. We should say plainly that this is a modern thought. The old sources do not state it. They do tell us that Thor was thought of as a friend of farmers and travellers, a god who drove away dangers that came from the wild. In some tales, people prayed to him for good weather at sea. A winter family had no need of storms. What they wanted was a steady fire, shelter, and the strength to endure the cold. A small hammer worn at the neck might still be a comfort in this season. It was a little reminder that someone strong stood between the household and the wide, dark world. When the first spring thunder rolled over the hills, it was perhaps heard as a welcome sound.

[long-pause]

{img:60-freyr-freyja-spring}

Winter households hoped Freyr and Freyja would bring a fertile spring.

[pause]

Among the Norse gods, Freyr and Freyja were linked with growth, fertility and plenty. Freyr was said to bring good harvests and peace, and Freyja was associated with love, beauty and also with powerful magic. They were a brother and sister, according to the poems and to Snorri Sturluson. For a farm living through winter, these were gods worth remembering. Spring meant new lambs, the sowing of seed, and the return of green grass for the animals. Sources from the period are limited, and we know little of the exact rites performed. Place names across Scandinavia, however, include the names of these gods, which suggests that people honoured them in many places. In Sweden, a great temple at Uppsala was described by a medieval writer as having a statue of Freyr, though we should treat that account with care. Winter was a time of hope as much as of waiting. A family looking at their last measure of grain might quietly wish for a gentle spring. Wishing was part of the season. In the evening they might raise a horn in the name of the gods of the fields, asking for peace and good yield. The snow outside was still deep, but everyone in the hall knew in their hearts that the ground below was alive, holding the seeds of the year to come.

[long-pause]

{img:61-iceland-turf-farm}

An Icelandic farm in winter was built almost entirely from earth.

[pause]

Iceland had very little woodland, and that shaped every winter day on its farms. Settlers began arriving around AD 870, and they found a green island with birch scrub, wide grassland and black volcanic ground. Archaeologists think the early farmers built their houses mostly from turf and stone, because good timber was scarce and had to be saved. The walls were thick, made of layers of grass sod, and they held the warmth like a heavy blanket. Sheep, cattle and horses were kept close by, and the hay cut in summer was counted with care, because it had to last until the grass came back. The sagas describe households gathering in the main room, where work went on by the light of the fire and small lamps. Wool was the great winter task. People carded it, spun it and wove it, and the cloth was one of the things Iceland could offer to traders. Outside, the wind came in from the sea and the snow piled against the walls until the house looked like part of the hillside. Inside, smoke rose slowly through the roof, and the air smelled of peat, dried fish and damp wool. Neighbours lived far apart, so each farm was almost a small world of its own, with its own sounds and its own routines. A house made from the ground itself keeps the cold at a gentle distance.

[long-pause]

{img:62-greenland-fjord-farm}

In Greenland, Norse farmers lived at the very edge of the farming world.

[pause]

Around AD 985, settlers sailing from Iceland reached the southwest coast of Greenland and found sheltered fjords with grass along the shores. They named the place Greenland, and the sagas say this was meant to attract new settlers. Archaeologists have found the ruins of many farms there, with stone walls, byres and small churches in the later years. Winters were long and hard, and the farms depended on cattle, sheep and goats, which needed hay and shelter. People also hunted seals, which gave meat, oil and skins, and in summer some travelled north to hunt walrus and gather ivory. Stone was used far more than wood, since timber had to come as washed-up logs or from distant voyages. A winter house probably smelled of smoke, animals and seal oil, and was lit by low lamps. Sea ice could fill the fjords, and a ship from Iceland or Norway might not arrive for a long time. Researchers still discuss, with great care, why the settlements eventually faded, and most think there were several causes working together. What is clear is that for several centuries, people kept homes, church bells and herds in a place most Europeans had never seen. There is a quiet courage in tending a small fire at the edge of the known map.

[long-pause]

{img:63-late-winter-larder}

Late winter was the hungry season, and households measured their food with care.

[pause]

By the time the old months of Þorri and Góa arrived, the larder had been in use for a long time. The meat of autumn was mostly gone, the hay was shrinking, and the weather could still be bitter. Historians think this was one of the hardest parts of the year, and the old calendar seems to reflect it, with later winter marked by hope as well as patience. Households had to ration. Dried fish was shared in small portions, and porridge was made thinner. Skyr, butter and cheese were kept for the days when they mattered most. The mistress of the farm held the keys to the storerooms, and her careful counting could decide how far the food would stretch. Animals were fed lightly with the last of the hay, and in some places they were let out to find a little grass on bare patches when the snow thinned. Fishing and hunting could add something when the weather allowed, and a fresh catch was always welcome at the hearth, shared out in careful portions. The sagas mention hard years when farms ran short, and neighbours sometimes helped one another. Meals became simple, but they were still shared at the same hearth. Even a thin bowl tastes of belonging when everyone at the table is counting the days toward spring.

[long-pause]

{img:64-viking-sky-watcher}

Farmers read the sky for weather, because nothing else could warn them.

[pause]

There were no forecasts in the Viking Age, so people learned to watch the world closely. A farmer or sailor noticed the colour of the dawn, the shape of the clouds and the way the wind changed in the evening. Smoke rising straight up from a chimney hole, or lying low across the roof, could say something about the air. Animals were watched too. Sheep gathering near the wall, or birds flying low over the water, might be taken as signs that a change was coming. Much of this knowledge was probably passed from parent to child in spoken sayings, and only a small part of it was ever written down later. Some of these sayings may have been accurate, since they came from generations of experience, and some were probably hopeful guesses. The moon and stars were also watched, partly for time and partly for the weather. Sailors in particular needed to know when the sea would be gentle, and farmers needed to know when snow would arrive on the high pastures. Archaeologists and historians can only guess at the full range of this lore, since so little of it survives. Still, we can picture a person standing in the cold at first light, reading a sky as carefully as a page. Patience, and long looking, was a kind of weather instrument in itself.

[long-pause]

{img:65-herb-bundles-healer}

Winter ailments were treated with herbs, warmth and patient care.

[pause]

Colds and coughs travelled through crowded winter houses, and families had to deal with them using what they had. Much of the care probably fell to the women of the household, who knew which herbs had been gathered and dried in summer. Archaeologists have found seeds and plant remains at Viking Age sites, and they suggest that people used a range of wild and garden plants for food, flavour and perhaps for simple remedies. The sagas mention women who bound wounds and prepared drinks, though we should be careful, since the stories were written down later. Warmth was probably the first help. A person was given a place near the fire, a pile of extra wool, and hot broth or milk. Honey, where it could be had, was a precious sweetener, and some herbs were chosen for their smell as much as for their taste. Beliefs about healing were mixed with ideas about luck and charms, and we cannot always separate practical care from tradition. What the sources suggest is that people looked after one another within the household, and that knowledge was shared quietly between neighbours. A bundle of dried herbs hanging in the smoke is a small picture of that care, gathered in a green summer and kept for a cold night.

[long-pause]

{img:66-midwife-lamplit-room}

Children were born in winter too, with other women close at hand.

[pause]

A birth did not wait for spring. In a Viking Age farm, a woman who was about to have a child was probably attended by other women of the household or neighbourhood, and perhaps by someone known for her experience. We have few direct records, so historians read between the lines of sagas, laws and later folk custom. The main task was to make a warm, quiet space. The fire was fed, water was heated, and clean cloth and wool were gathered. Some sources hint, gently, that charms or runes were used to ask for protection, and these may have been a comfort as much as a practice. The Icelandic laws, written down later, show that communities cared about the welfare of children and mothers, and about who would be responsible for a newborn. When a baby arrived, the household probably welcomed it with relief. Later sources describe a naming custom, in which a child might be given a name from an older relative, and so carry a family's memory forward. Even in the longest dark of the year, the arrival of a new person was a small bright event for the whole household, remembered in the family afterward. A baby wrapped in wool, held near the hearth, was a plain and ancient promise that the family would continue into the light.

[long-pause]

{img:67-grandmother-storyteller}

The oldest people in the house were its living libraries.

[pause]

In a household without books, memory mattered a great deal. Older family members carried the stories, the family histories, the names of places and the old sayings. A grandparent might remember where a boundary stone stood, how a feud had been settled, or who had sailed on a particular voyage. These things were not written down in the Viking Age in the way we know today, so they travelled by voice. The sagas, which were set down later in Iceland, show how much people valued genealogy, and how a person's place in the world was tied to their ancestors. In winter, when work moved indoors, there was time for these tales. Children probably listened as wool was spun and tools were mended, and the same story might be told again and again, slightly changed each time. Archaeologists cannot recover the voice itself, but they can show us the houses where this happened, with benches along the walls and a long fire in the middle. Older people also knew many practical things, such as how to read the weather, mend a net or care for an animal. Their knowledge was part of the household's strength. A story told by an old voice, near a warm fire, connects the room to people long gone, and to children not yet grown.

[long-pause]

{img:68-althing-valley-gathering}

Even in winter, people travelled to meet and settle matters by law.

[pause]

The Icelanders created a general assembly called the Althing, which is usually dated to about AD 930, and it met in summer at Thingvellir. Alongside it were smaller local assemblies, and the sources suggest that these gatherings could happen at different times of year. Law was spoken aloud, and the Law Speaker was said to recite parts of it from memory. Meetings dealt with disputes, agreements, marriages and property, and they gave scattered farms a way to belong to something larger. Travel in winter was not easy. Roads were few, rivers were hard to cross, and snow could hide the way. Frozen ground, though, could sometimes make crossing wet land simpler, and horses were valuable for the journey. People packed dried food, spare wool and fodder for the horses, and often travelled in company for safety and conversation. Historians think that trading, news and gossip moved along with the riders. A gathering was also a social event, with greetings, meals and a chance to see distant relatives. The idea that a community should settle its arguments by agreed rules was a serious one, and it grew from shared habit as much as from written law. A line of small figures riding over a white landscape toward a meeting place shows how much people valued each other's company.

[long-pause]

{img:69-frozen-river-road}

Frozen waters became roads for those who knew them well.

[pause]

In summer, rivers and fjords were routes for boats. In winter, in the colder lands of the north, ice could turn the same waters into a firm, flat surface. Vikings, who came from Scandinavia and settled in many places, knew snow and ice well, and the sources suggest that travelling over frozen ground and water was an accepted part of life. Sledges pulled by horses or oxen could carry firewood, hay and goods more easily over snow than a cart could over mud. Archaeologists have found sledges, skis and ice skates made of bone, and these show how practical people were with the materials around them. Travellers still had to be careful. Ice could be thin near a current or a shore, and local knowledge mattered. A person who knew the river would know where it froze evenly and where it did not. Journeys could be short visits between neighbours or longer trips to a market or meeting, and the pace was always slow and steady. The sound of runners on snow, the creak of leather harness, and the breath of horses in cold air must have been familiar. A frozen river is a road that lasts only a season, and then returns to being water. It reminds us that the landscape itself changed with the months, and people changed with it.

[long-pause]

{img:70-first-light-snowmelt}

Spring arrived first as a little more light each morning.

[pause]

Far in the north, the turning of the year was seen in the sky. After midwinter, the days began to lengthen, slowly at first, and a farmer, walking out to the animals, would notice that the sun rose a little earlier and stayed a little longer. Icicles dripped at the roof edge, and the snow began to look grey and soft on the south side of the hill. The old calendar had a place for this change. The Norse months continued through Þorri and Góa, and then into Einmánuður, the last month of winter, and later Harpa, the first of summer. Archaeologists and historians cannot always date the old months exactly, but the idea of counting toward spring seems clear. A calendar stick may have helped a household keep track. People watched for returning birds, for shoots of green on bare ground, and for the first mild wind that smelled of wet earth. The animals, too, seemed to change, and the hens might begin to lay again. The sagas do not describe these signs at length, but later folk custom shows how much they were noticed. After so many weeks of fire and shadow, a bright morning must have felt like a door quietly opening. Light, once it begins to return, tends to do so without any hurry.

[long-pause]

{img:71-spring-lambs-ploughing}

New lambs and turned soil marked the end of the hard season.

[pause]

When the ground softened, the farm turned its attention outward. Ewes gave birth to lambs, and the farmers watched over them, bringing weak ones into the warmth of the house or byre. The new milk was welcome after the thin weeks of late winter, and cheese and butter making could begin again. Soon the fields had to be prepared. Archaeologists have found parts of ards, which were simple ploughs that scratched the soil rather than turning it deeply, and they were pulled by oxen or sometimes by horses. Barley was an important crop, and its green shoots were a welcome sight, and in some places oats and rye were grown as well, though this depended on the climate. In the far north, farming was more limited, and animals mattered more than grain. Seeds were saved from the previous harvest and sown by hand. Fences were mended, dung was spread, and cattle were moved to pasture when the grass was ready. The work was busy but hopeful. People who had spent months by the fire now spent long hours in daylight, and the smell of fresh earth replaced the smell of smoke. There was a rhythm to it all, and each task prepared the next. A handful of seed, thrown into dark soil, is a small act of faith in a summer not yet seen.

[long-pause]

{img:72-ship-launch-spring}

When the ice broke, the ships went back into the water, and archaeology has kept their story.

[pause]

As spring grew warmer, the ships that had been hauled ashore in autumn were checked and made ready. Seams were examined by hand and by eye, ropes were replaced, and sails that had been mended indoors were carried out again. With many hands, and perhaps with rollers of wood, a ship could be eased down to the water. Then the season of voyages began once more, with trade, travel and visits between settlements. What we know about this world comes largely from archaeology. At Oseberg in Norway, a ship buried around AD 834 was found with wooden objects, textiles and carvings that show fine craftsmanship. At Jorvik, now York in England, excavations revealed streets, workshops and everyday items preserved in damp soil. At L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland, the remains of turf buildings show that Norse people reached North America around the year 1000. These places do not give us every answer, and they were found by patient work over many years, and scholars still debate many details. But they show people who built, mended, cooked, travelled and endured long winters. Their houses and tools tell us that daily life, more than battle, was what filled most of their years. A ship sliding into bright water after months on shore is a fitting picture of winter ending, and of a past that still reaches us.

[long-pause]

And so our long winter comes to its end. The snow has gone from the fields, the fjord is open, and a family steps out into the thin spring sun.
