The Iliad ends with the funeral of Hector. But the story of Troy goes on, in the other poems, and in the stories of later writers.

Soon after Hector, Achilles himself falls, as was foretold, struck in the heel by an arrow, shot by Paris, and guided by the god Apollo. His heel, the stories say, was the only part of him that could be harmed, because when he was a baby, his mother dipped him in the magic river Styx, holding him by the heel. Today, we still call a person's one weakness their Achilles heel.

--

And then Paris, too, is lost, wounded by an arrow.

But still the walls of Troy stand. The Greeks cannot take the city by force. And so, in the tenth year of the war, they decide to take it by a trick.

The trick is the idea of Odysseus, the clever one. Odysseus of many wiles, Homer calls him.

--

The Greeks build a great horse, of wood.

It is built by a carpenter named Epeius, with the help of the goddess Athena, from the timber of fir trees, cut on Mount Ida. It is huge, taller than the walls of Troy, and hollow inside. And in its belly, there is a secret door, and room for a group of the bravest Greek warriors to hide, with Odysseus himself as their leader.

{img:giant-wooden-horse-standing-beach-dawn-empty-camp}
Then, one night, the Greeks hide the warriors inside the horse, and leave the horse standing on the plain, in front of their camp. They burn their huts, and launch their ships, and sail away, out of sight. But not home. Only to the island of Tenedos, just off the coast, where they hide their ships behind the island, and wait.

--

In the morning, the Trojans look out from their walls, and cannot believe their eyes.

The Greek camp is empty. The ships are gone. The beach is deserted, except for the smoking ruins of the huts. And there, on the plain, stands a great wooden horse.

The people of Troy open the gates, for the first time in ten years, and pour out onto the plain, laughing and shouting, to see the place where the Greek camp had been, and the place where Achilles had his hut, and where the ships had been drawn up on the beach. They are free. The war is over.

And they gather around the wooden horse, and wonder what it is.

--

Some of them want to bring it into the city. Some want to throw it into the sea, or burn it, or cut it open, to see what is inside.

The story, as Virgil tells it, says that a priest of Neptune, named Laocoön, comes running down from the citadel, and warns them. He says that it is a trick of the Greeks. I fear the Greeks, he says, even when they bring gifts. And he throws a spear at the side of the horse, and it sticks there, quivering, and the hollow belly of the horse echoes.

{img:priest-warning-crowd-horse-plain-dramatic-sky-calm}
But then, the story goes on, two great serpents come out of the sea, from the direction of Tenedos, and come to Laocoön, and his two sons, and take them away. And the Trojans believe that the gods have punished him for harming the horse, which must be a sacred gift for the goddess Athena.

--

And then some shepherds bring in a young Greek, whom they have found hiding in the reeds, with his hands tied.

His name is Sinon. And he tells the Trojans a clever story. He says that he has escaped from the Greeks, who wanted to sacrifice him to the gods, to get a good wind for their voyage home. He says that the Greeks have given up, and gone home. And he says that the horse is an offering to the goddess Athena, to ask her forgiveness. They made it so big, he says, so that the Trojans could not bring it into their city. Because, if the horse is brought into Troy, then Troy will be protected by Athena forever.

{img:shepherds-bringing-young-man-to-king-priam-plain}
And kind old Priam believes him, and orders his hands to be untied, and tells him that he is welcome among the Trojans.

--

So the Trojans decide to bring the horse into the city.

But it is too big to go through the gate. So they pull down part of the wall, beside the gate, to make room. And they put wheels under the horse's feet, and tie ropes around its neck, and pull it, singing, into the city, with the boys and the girls dancing around it, and touching the ropes for luck.

Four times, the story says, the horse stops, at the very threshold of the gate, and four times, there is a sound of bronze, of armour, from inside it. But nobody notices.

--

Only Cassandra knows the truth.

She runs through the streets, with her hair loose, crying out to everyone that the horse is full of Greeks, and that it will be the end of Troy. But nobody believes her, because of Apollo's curse. They think she is out of her mind, and they laugh at her, or feel sorry for her.

{img:cassandra-in-crowded-street-calling-out-ignored}
And the Trojans bring the horse up to the citadel, and leave it there, in front of the temple of Athena. And they hang garlands of flowers on the temples, all over the city. And they celebrate, with feasting, and wine, and music, and dancing, late into the night. Because, after ten years, the war is over.
