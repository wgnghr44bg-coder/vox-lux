And so, in the stories, the Greeks come to Troy.

They come across the sea, in their black ships, long and narrow, with a single square sail, and rows of oars, and high, curved prows, painted with eyes. Each ship carries fifty or a hundred men. They come from every part of Greece: from Mycenae, and Sparta, and Pylos, and Athens, and Thessaly, and Crete, and the islands.

--

Homer, in the Iliad, gives a long list of all the ships, and their leaders, and where they came from: twenty-nine contingents, more than eleven hundred ships. It is called the Catalogue of Ships.

The interesting thing is that many of the places in this list are real places, in Bronze Age Greece, that were important in the time of the Mycenaean palaces, but had become small or forgotten by the time of Homer. So the list may be very old, a real memory of the Greek world around the year 1200 before Christ, passed down by the singers for hundreds of years.

{img:mycenae-lion-gate-citadel-stone-walls-greece}
The leader of the Greeks is Agamemnon, king of Mycenae, the richest and most powerful of the Greek kings. He is proud, and stubborn, and quick-tempered.

--

The other great heroes of the Greeks are Menelaus, Helen's husband, brave and honest, and Odysseus, king of the little rocky island of Ithaca, the cleverest of all, and a great speaker, who always knows what to do.

There is the old king Nestor, of sandy Pylos, the oldest and wisest of the Greeks, who loves to tell long stories about the great deeds of his youth. There is great Ajax, the biggest and strongest of the Greeks, with his huge shield, like a tower. And there is Diomedes, young and brave.

{img:greek-heroes-council-tent-shore-agamemnon-nestor}
And there is Achilles.

--

Achilles is the greatest warrior of the Greeks, and of the whole world.

He is the son of a king, Peleus, and a sea goddess, Thetis. He is young, and handsome, and strong, and very fast: swift-footed Achilles, Homer calls him. He is proud, and passionate, and quick to anger, but also capable of great kindness.

His mother, Thetis, knows that he has a choice. He can stay at home, and live a long, quiet life, and be forgotten. Or he can go to Troy, and win great glory, and be remembered forever, but die young. Achilles chooses glory.

He comes to Troy with his men, the Myrmidons, and with his dearest friend, Patroclus, who has grown up with him since they were boys.

--

The Greeks land on the shore near Troy, and drag their ships up onto the beach, and build a great camp, along the shore, with huts and shelters, and a wall and a ditch to protect it.

And the Trojans close the gates of their city, and the siege begins.

{img:greek-camp-beach-ships-pulled-up-huts-smoke}
The Trojans are not alone. Their allies come from all over Anatolia, and from Thrace, across the strait, to help them: the Dardanians, and the Lycians, from the far south, and the Mysians, and the Phrygians, and the Thracians, and many more. Each speaks a different language. Homer says that the Trojan army is like a flock of sheep, all bleating in different voices.

--

According to the stories, the war lasts for ten years.

That is very hard to believe. No Bronze Age army could stay so far from home for so long. Perhaps the ten years are the poets' way of saying a long time. Or perhaps the memories of many different wars, and raids, over many years, have been joined together into one great story.

{img:plain-of-troy-view-from-walls-distant-camp-ships}
But in the stories, the Greeks cannot take the city, because the walls are too strong. And the Trojans cannot drive the Greeks away, because they are too many. So, for years, the Greeks raid the towns and the islands around Troy, to get food and treasure and captives, and the two armies sometimes fight on the plain, between the city and the ships. And the people of Troy go on living, as best they can, inside their walls.
