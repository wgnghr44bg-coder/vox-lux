The story of Troy comes to us, first of all, from a poet called Homer.

We know almost nothing about Homer. The Greeks believed that he was a blind old man, who wandered from town to town, singing his songs. Seven different cities claimed to be his birthplace. Some scholars today think that he lived in the eighth century before Christ, on the coast of what is now Turkey, or on one of the islands near it, perhaps four or five hundred years after the Trojan War. Some think that there never was a single Homer at all.

--

Two great poems are said to be by Homer: the Iliad, and the Odyssey.

The Iliad is the story of a few weeks in the tenth year of the war at Troy. Its name comes from Ilios, or Ilion, the Greek name for Troy. It tells of the anger of the great Greek hero Achilles, and of the battles on the plain, and of Hector, the prince of Troy, and of his family, and of the sorrow of war.

The Odyssey is the story of what happened after the war: of the long journey home of the Greek hero Odysseus, who wandered for ten years over the sea, before he came back to his island, Ithaca, and to his wife, Penelope, and his son, Telemachus.

--

These poems were not first written down. They were sung.

For hundreds of years, before writing came to Greece, singers called bards travelled from palace to palace, and town to town, and sang the old stories of the heroes, in the halls of the kings, in the evenings, after the feasts, to the music of the lyre.

{img:bard-singing-palace-hall-hearth-warriors-listening}
They did not learn the songs by heart, word for word. They made them up anew each time, as they sang, using the old stories, and lines and phrases that they had learned from older singers. Swift-footed Achilles. Rosy-fingered dawn. The wine-dark sea. Horse-taming Trojans. Hector of the shining helmet. These phrases fit the rhythm of the verse, and help the singer as he sings.

--

So the story of Troy was passed down, from singer to singer, for hundreds of years. And as it was passed down, it changed.

The old memories of the Bronze Age, of the palaces and the chariots and the bronze armour, were mixed with things from later times. Gods and miracles were added. The heroes grew bigger, and stronger, and the war grew longer, and grander.

And at last, perhaps in the eighth or seventh century before Christ, the poems were written down, in the new Greek alphabet. And they became the most famous poems in the Greek world, which every Greek child learned at school, and every Greek knew by heart.

--

There were other poems too, about the war of Troy, which told the parts of the story that Homer does not tell: how the war began, and how it ended. These poems are now lost. We know them only from short summaries, and from what later writers tell us.

The most famous of these later writers is the Roman poet Virgil, who lived in the time of Augustus, about seven hundred years after Homer. In his great poem, the Aeneid, he tells the story of the last night of Troy, and of the wooden horse, and of the Trojan prince Aeneas, who escaped from the city, and sailed away to Italy.

{img:roman-poet-virgil-writing-garden-villa-quiet}
So our story of the last days of Troy comes from many voices, over many hundreds of years: Homer, and the lost poems, and the Greek playwrights of Athens, and Virgil, and others.

--

Is any of it true?

For a long time, many scholars believed that it was all a legend, and that Troy had never existed at all, or that nobody could know where it had been.

{img:hisarlik-mound-grassy-hill-plain-nineteenth-century}
But in the nineteenth century, some travellers and scholars began to look for Troy. And they thought that it might have been on a low hill near the Dardanelles, called Hisarlik, which means the place of the fortress, in Turkish. A man named Frank Calvert, who owned part of the hill, dug some trenches there, and found old walls.

And then, in 1870, a rich German businessman named Heinrich Schliemann came, and began to dig. We shall hear more about him later.

--

Today, almost all archaeologists agree that the hill of Hisarlik is Troy.

They have found the remains of at least nine cities there, one on top of the other, from about 3000 before Christ until Roman times, over more than three thousand years. And one of them, or perhaps two of them, around the year 1200 before Christ, the time when the Greeks believed the Trojan War took place, was a strong and rich city, with great walls, and a large lower town.

And there is more. In the archives of the Hittites, the great empire of Anatolia, there are clay tablets that mention a city called Wilusa, in the far west of Anatolia, near the sea. Many scholars think that Wilusa is the same name as Ilios, Troy. One of the tablets is a treaty, made with a king of Wilusa called Alaksandu, a name very like Alexandros, which is the other name of Paris, the prince of Troy in Homer's story.

--

And the tablets also mention a people called the Ahhiyawa, living over the sea, to the west, who sometimes cause trouble on the coast of Anatolia. Many scholars think that the Ahhiyawa are the Achaeans, which is what Homer calls the Greeks.

One Hittite letter even mentions that there was a quarrel, or a war, between the Hittites and the Ahhiyawa, about Wilusa.

{img:hittite-clay-tablet-cuneiform-scribe-desk-lamp}
So perhaps there really was a war at Troy. Perhaps there were several. We do not know. But there is enough to make us think that behind the old songs of the poets, there are memories of a real city, and real people, and real events, a very long time ago.
