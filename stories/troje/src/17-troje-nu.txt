Today, you can visit Troy.

The hill of Hisarlik is in Turkey, about thirty kilometres south of the town of Çanakkale, on the Dardanelles. It is now an archaeological park, and a World Heritage Site, and every year, hundreds of thousands of people come to see it.

--

It is smaller than most people expect.

You walk up a gentle path, among pine trees and olive trees, and suddenly you are among the ruins: walls of stone, and the foundations of houses, and ramps, and gates, from many different times, all mixed up together, one on top of the other, with little signs to tell you which city each one belongs to.

And then you come to the great walls of Troy Six, the city of fine walls, with their sloping faces of limestone, and their little offsets, still standing several metres high, after more than three thousand years. If you stand beside them, in the late afternoon, when the sun is low, you can see the light catching the edges of the stones, just as it did when Hector walked here.

--

You can see the south gate, where the main road came into the citadel, with the bases of the standing stones beside it.

You can walk up the old stone ramp of Troy Two, from the time of the Treasure of Priam, a thousand years before the war. You can stand at the edge of Schliemann's great trench, and look down into it, and see all the layers of the cities, one above the other, like the pages of a book. And you can see the ruins of the Greek and Roman temple of Athena, and the Roman theatre, and the council house.

{img:stone-ramp-ancient-citadel-wildflowers-spring}
In the spring, the ruins are covered with wild flowers: red poppies, and yellow daisies, and purple anemones. Lizards sun themselves on the warm stones. Storks nest on the poles. And the wind blows from the north, as it always did, the strong wind of the Dardanelles. Homer called Troy windy Ilios.

--

From the top of the hill, you can look out over the plain, toward the sea.

The sea is further away now than it was three thousand years ago. In the time of the war, there was a great bay, coming close to the city, where the ships could anchor. But over the centuries, the rivers have filled the bay with mud and sand, and now the plain stretches for several kilometres, green with fields of wheat and cotton and tomatoes, to the shore.

But you can still see the line of the sea, shining in the distance. And beyond it, the island of Tenedos, which is now called Bozcaada, where the Greek ships hid, with its little harbour, and its castle, and its vineyards. And the great ships still go past, through the Dardanelles, on their way to and from the Black Sea, just as the ships did in the time of the Trojans.

--

At the entrance to the site, there is a large wooden horse.

It was built in the 1970s, and children love to climb up the stairs inside it, and look out of the little windows in its side, pretending to be Greek warriors. It is not very beautiful, or very old. But nobody minds.

{img:wooden-horse-replica-site-entrance-children-stairs}
And in the town of Çanakkale, on the seafront, there is another wooden horse. It is the horse that was used in a Hollywood film about Troy, made in 2004, which the film company gave to the town. People take photographs in front of it, in the evening, with the sea behind.

--

Near the site, there is a fine new museum, the Troy Museum, opened in 2018.

It is a large, square building, the colour of rusty iron, as if it had just been dug out of the earth. Inside, you walk slowly down a long ramp, past all the layers of Troy's history, from the oldest at the bottom to the newest at the top, as if you were digging down through the hill.

There are pots, and tools, and weapons, and jewellery, and statues, and coins, and the little bronze seal with Luwian writing on it, the only writing ever found at Troy from the time of the war. And there is some of the gold from Troy, which was found at Troy, or nearby, and has come home.

--

And on the plain, and the hills around Troy, the great mounds that the ancient Greeks called the tombs of the heroes are still there: the tomb of Achilles, and of Patroclus, and of Ajax, standing on the low ridges near the sea, covered with grass and wild thyme.

Nobody knows who is really buried in them. Some of them seem to be from later times. But it does not matter very much. They are part of the story, which has been told on this plain for three thousand years.

{img:grassy-burial-mound-ridge-sea-evening-light}
Further along the coast, on the peninsula of Gallipoli, across the water, there are the cemeteries and the memorials of another war, a war of 1915, fought by soldiers from Britain, and Australia, and New Zealand, and France, and Turkey, many of whom knew the Iliad, and wrote about Troy, which they could see across the strait.

--

And the story goes on.

It is still told in schools, and books, and plays, and paintings, and poems, and films. Children still learn about the wooden horse, and Achilles's heel. People still say beware of Greeks bearing gifts, and call a long journey an odyssey, and a hidden danger a Trojan horse.

And all over the world, people still read the Iliad and the Odyssey, the old songs of the bards, about the windy city on the hill, and the old king, and Hector and Andromache on the tower, and the man who wanted only to go home.

--

So we leave the city of Priam on its hill above the plain, as the evening wind comes in from the sea.

The walls, the horses, the weavers at their looms, and the long story that has outlived them all.
