Let us walk up to the citadel of Troy.

The walls of the citadel are magnificent. They are built of great blocks of limestone, carefully cut and fitted together, sloping gently outward at the bottom, and rising straight up at the top, perhaps ten metres high, with a wall of mud brick on top. At intervals along the walls, there are great square towers, from which the guards can look out over the plain.

--

The walls are built in a special way. Every few metres, there is a little step, or offset, in the face of the wall, so that the wall is not one long, flat surface, but a series of slightly angled sections.

Nobody knows exactly why. Perhaps it makes the wall stronger, against earthquakes, which are common in this part of the world. Perhaps it is just for beauty. In the evening, when the sun is low, the light catches the edges, and the walls seem to shimmer.

In the Iliad, Homer calls Troy the city of fine walls, and the city of lofty gates, and well-built Troy. He is right.

--

There are several gates into the citadel. The main gate faces south, toward the lower town, and has a great tower beside it.

Beside the gate, there are tall stones, standing upright in a row, like pillars, which seem to be sacred, perhaps standing for the gods who protect the city. In front of them, people leave offerings.

{img:south-gate-tower-standing-stones-offerings-morning}
In the stories, the most famous gate is the Scaean gate, the western gate, facing the plain and the sea, where the old men of Troy sit on the wall, and watch the battles, and where Hector says goodbye to his wife.

--

Inside the citadel, the ground rises in terraces, toward the top of the hill.

On the terraces, there are large houses, built of stone, with walls a metre thick, and flat roofs of timber and earth. Some of them have two storeys. These are the houses of the noble families of Troy, the king's relatives, and his officers, and his chief men.

At the very top of the hill, there would have been the palace of the king, with its great hall, and its storerooms, and its courtyards. But the top of the hill was flattened, many centuries later, by the Greeks and the Romans, to build their temples, and the palace is gone. We can only imagine it.

--

In Homer's poem, the palace of King Priam is a wonderful place, built of polished stone, with fifty bedrooms for his fifty sons and their wives, and twelve more, across the courtyard, for his daughters and their husbands.

There is a great hall, with a hearth in the middle, where the king feasts with his family and his guests, and storerooms full of bronze and gold and fine cloth and fragrant cedar wood.

{img:palace-great-hall-hearth-columns-painted-walls}
And on the citadel, too, there is the temple of Athena, the goddess of wisdom, where the women of Troy bring offerings of fine robes, and pray to her to protect the city. And there is a temple of Apollo, the god of the sun and of music, who loves Troy.

--

Below the citadel, around the foot of the hill, is the lower town.

For a long time, archaeologists did not know that it was there. They thought that Troy was only the little citadel on the top of the hill. But in the 1990s, a German archaeologist named Manfred Korfmann and his team used special instruments to look under the ground of the fields around the hill, and found the traces of a great ditch, cut into the rock, running all around the lower town, far out from the citadel.

{img:lower-town-ditch-wooden-palisade-houses-beyond}
So we know that the lower town was large, perhaps twenty or thirty hectares, and that it was protected by the ditch, and perhaps by a wall or a palisade of wood behind it, with gates where the roads came in.

--

In the lower town, the houses are small, and crowded together, along narrow, winding lanes.

They are built of stone foundations, with walls of mud brick above, plastered and whitewashed, and flat roofs, where the people sleep in the summer, and dry their figs and their grapes. Most houses have one or two rooms, and a little courtyard, with an oven, and a place for the animals.

Inside, it is dark and cool, with little windows, and floors of beaten earth. There is a hearth, for cooking and for warmth in winter, and big jars of pottery, set into the floor, for storing grain and oil and wine. In the later years of the city, as we shall see, there are more and more of these big storage jars, as if the people were preparing for something.

--

There is water inside the walls too.

On the side of the hill, there is a cave, cut deep into the rock, with tunnels leading back into the hill, more than a hundred metres, to springs of fresh, cold water. The people of Troy have been using it for many hundreds of years. The water never runs dry, even in summer, and even if the city is besieged.

{img:spring-cave-rock-tunnel-water-jars-women-cool}
The Hittite treaty with Alaksandu of Wilusa, long ago, mentions a god of Wilusa, called KASKAL.KUR, which means a road under the earth, or an underground watercourse. Perhaps that is this very spring. Perhaps the people of Troy worshipped the spring as a god, because it gave them life.
