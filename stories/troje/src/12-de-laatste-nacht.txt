Late that night, the city of Troy is quiet at last.

The feasting is over. The music has stopped. The lamps have gone out, one by one. The people have gone home, tired and happy, and have lain down to sleep, for the first time in ten years without fear. Even the guards on the walls have left their posts, or fallen asleep, wrapped in their cloaks.

--

Up on the citadel, in front of the temple of Athena, the great wooden horse stands in the moonlight, silent, with the garlands of flowers hanging on it.

And out at sea, behind the island of Tenedos, the Greek ships are quietly setting out, in the darkness, with their oars dipping softly in the calm water, rowing back toward the shore of Troy, under the light of the moon.

In the city, Sinon, the young Greek, gets up quietly, and goes to the horse, and opens the secret door. And the Greek warriors climb down, by a rope, one by one, onto the stones of the citadel. And one of them lights a fire on the walls, to signal to the ships. And others go to the broken place in the wall, by the gate, to let in the Greek army.

--

The rest of the story is very sad, and we will tell it gently, and from a distance, as if we were looking at the city from far away, on the plain, in the night.

The Greeks come into the sleeping city. And by the morning, after ten years, Troy has fallen.

{img:distant-city-on-hill-night-orange-glow-plain-stars}
From far away, on the plain, you would have seen the glow, rising into the sky above the hill, and heard the sounds, faintly, on the wind. And in the morning, there would have been a great cloud of smoke, drifting slowly over the plain toward the sea.

--

Virgil, in the Aeneid, tells the story of that night through the eyes of one Trojan, Aeneas, the cousin of the royal family, who, many years later, tells it to the queen of Carthage, Dido.

He tells how he was sleeping in his house, when, in a dream, Hector appeared to him, sad and pale, and told him to wake up, and flee, because the enemy was within the walls, and Troy was lost. Take the holy things of Troy, Hector told him, and the household gods, and find a new city for them, across the sea.

{img:aeneas-waking-night-room-dream-figure-pale-light}
Aeneas woke, and climbed onto the roof of his house, and saw the city around him, and understood.

--

At first, Aeneas wants to fight, to save the city, or to fall with it. But his mother, the goddess Venus, appears to him, and shows him that the gods themselves have turned against Troy, and that it cannot be saved. And she tells him to go home, to his family, and save them.

So Aeneas goes home, to his father, the old Anchises, who is too old and too weak to walk, and to his wife, Creusa, and his little son, Iulus, also called Ascanius.

At first, old Anchises refuses to go. He says that he is too old, and wants to stay, and end his life in Troy. But then a strange sign appears: a little flame, harmless, flickering over the head of the little boy, Iulus, without burning him. And a star shoots across the sky, toward Mount Ida, leaving a trail of light. And Anchises believes that the gods want them to go.

--

So Aeneas lifts his old father up onto his shoulders, and carries him. And old Anchises carries in his arms the household gods of Troy, the little sacred statues, because Aeneas's hands are not clean enough, after the night, to touch holy things.

And Aeneas takes his little son by the hand, and the boy trots along beside him, with little steps, trying to keep up. And his wife Creusa follows behind.

{img:refugees-leaving-city-gate-night-mount-ida-distance}
And they go out of the city, through the dark streets, and out of the gate, toward an old temple outside the city, by an ancient cypress tree, where the servants are to meet them.

--

But when they reach the temple, Creusa is not with them. She has been lost, somewhere in the dark streets.

Aeneas goes back, alone, into the city, searching for her, calling her name. And at last, she appears to him: not Creusa herself, but her spirit, her shade, taller than she was in life. And she speaks to him gently.

{img:gentle-pale-figure-woman-farewell-moonlight-ruins}
She tells him not to grieve too much. She says that it was the will of the gods. She tells him that he will go on a long journey, over the sea, to a land in the west, called Hesperia, Italy, by the river Tiber, where he will find a new home, and a kingdom, and a new wife. And she asks him to look after their son, Iulus, and to love him.

Three times, Aeneas tries to put his arms around her. Three times, she slips through his arms, like the wind, like a dream. And she is gone.

--

Aeneas goes back to the temple, outside the city, and finds that many other Trojans have gathered there, men and women and children, who have escaped from the city, and are waiting for him, to lead them.

And as the morning star rises over the top of Mount Ida, he lifts up his father again, and leads them all away, toward the mountains.

They spend the winter in the forests of Mount Ida, building ships. And in the spring, they set sail, a little band of Trojans, with their household gods, to look for a new home across the sea.

--

According to the Roman story, after many years of wandering, Aeneas and his Trojans come at last to Italy, and settle there, by the river Tiber.

And from Aeneas's son, Iulus, are descended the kings of Alba Longa, and Romulus and Remus, the founders of Rome, and, many hundreds of years later, the family of the Julii, the family of Julius Caesar, who was so proud of his descent from Venus, through Aeneas, and Iulus, and of his Trojan ancestors.

{img:ships-arriving-italian-coast-river-mouth-forest}
So the Romans believed that they were the children of Troy. And that, in a way, Troy never truly fell, but crossed the sea, and was born again, as Rome.
