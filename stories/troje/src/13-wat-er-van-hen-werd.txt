What happened to the other people of Troy, in the stories?

The Greek poets, and later the great playwrights of Athens, such as Euripides, wrote about the women of Troy, after the city fell. Their stories are very sad. Euripides wrote them to make the people of Athens think about the sorrows of war, even for the enemy.

--

Old Hecuba, the queen, is taken away by the Greeks, across the sea. In the stories, she ends her days in Thrace, on the far shore of the Hellespont, and a place on that coast is named after her, Cynossema.

Cassandra is taken by Agamemnon, the king of Mycenae, to his home in Greece. She knows what will happen there, as she always does. And when they arrive, at the great palace of Mycenae, Agamemnon and Cassandra are both lost, in a plot by Agamemnon's own wife, Clytemnestra, who has never forgiven him for leaving her, and for the sorrows of the war.

So the great king who led the Greeks to Troy comes home, after ten years, only to lose his life on the day of his return.

--

Andromache is taken by Neoptolemus, the son of Achilles, to his home in the north of Greece.

But her story, in the end, is a little less sad. After many years, she is married to Helenus, Hector's brother, the prophet, who was also taken to Greece, and they rule together over a little kingdom in Epirus, in the northwest of Greece. And there, Virgil says, they build a little copy of Troy, with a little Scaean gate, and a little river that they call the Scamander, so that they can remember their home.

{img:little-copy-of-troy-small-gate-river-greece-afternoon}
In the Aeneid, Aeneas, on his journey, visits them there, and finds Andromache sitting by an empty tomb of Hector, beside the little river, making offerings to his memory. Still, after all those years, she is grieving for Hector.

--

And Helen?

Menelaus, her husband, finds her, on the last night of Troy. The stories say that he had sworn to take his revenge on her. But when he sees her again, he lets his sword fall from his hand, and forgives her.

{img:menelaus-helen-ship-sailing-home-calm-sea}
And they sail home together, to Sparta. But they are blown off course, by storms, and wander for eight years, to Cyprus, and Phoenicia, and Egypt, where they are given wonderful gifts by the king and queen of Egypt, before they come home at last.

--

In the Odyssey, Homer gives us a glimpse of them, many years later, at home in Sparta.

Odysseus's young son, Telemachus, comes to Sparta, looking for news of his father, who has not come home from the war. And he finds Menelaus and Helen living quietly and happily together, in their beautiful palace, gleaming with bronze and gold and silver and ivory, as if nothing had ever happened.

{img:spartan-palace-hall-helen-spinning-golden-distaff}
Helen comes into the hall, like a goddess, with her maids, carrying a golden spindle, and a silver work basket on wheels, full of fine purple wool, a gift from the queen of Egypt. And she sits down, and spins, and talks to the young man, kindly, and recognizes him at once as the son of Odysseus, because he looks so like his father.

--

And that evening, when they all grow sad, remembering the war, and the friends they have lost, Helen puts a drug into their wine, a medicine from Egypt, given to her by an Egyptian lady, which takes away all sorrow, and all grief, and makes people forget their troubles, for a whole day.

And then she tells them stories, about Odysseus, at Troy.

She tells how, during the war, Odysseus came into Troy in disguise, dressed as a beggar, in rags, with his face bruised, to spy on the Trojans. Nobody recognized him, except Helen. And she washed him, and gave him clothes, and swore not to tell the Trojans, and he told her all the plans of the Greeks. And she says that, by then, she was glad, because she longed to go home, and was sorry for the folly that Aphrodite had brought upon her.

--

Of the other heroes, many never came home.

Ajax the Lesser was lost at sea, in a storm. Diomedes found that his wife had married someone else, and sailed away to Italy, and founded cities there. Old Nestor got home safely, and lived to a great age, in his palace at Pylos, telling stories.

{img:old-king-nestor-palace-porch-sunlight-storytelling}
And Odysseus, the cleverest of them all, who had thought of the wooden horse, set out for his home, the little rocky island of Ithaca, with his twelve ships. It should have taken him a few weeks. It took him ten years.
