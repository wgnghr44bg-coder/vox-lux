{img:01-wooden-horse-before-troy-walls-golden-evening}

More than three thousand years ago, a walled city stood on a low hill near the sea, at the edge of Asia. Its name was Troy. And according to the old poems, one evening a great wooden horse stood silent on the plain before its gates.

[long-pause]

According to the ancient poets, Troy fell because of a giant wooden horse. The story of Troy's last days is one of the most famous tales in the world. According to the old poems, the Greeks had besieged the city for ten long years. They could not break its walls. So they tried a trick. They built a huge wooden horse. Inside it, they hid some of their best warriors. Then the rest of the army burned their camp and sailed away, as if they had given up. They only went as far as a nearby island, where they hid behind the shore. The Trojans came out and found the horse standing on the empty beach. They did not know what to make of it. Some wanted to burn it. Others wanted to bring it into the city as a gift to the gods. The full story is told by the Roman poet Virgil, more than a thousand years later. Yet the horse had already been mentioned by Homer. It became the most famous trick in history.

[long-pause]

{img:02-empty-beach-greek-camp-ashes-dawn-troy}

The Trojans woke one morning to find the Greek camp burned and the ships gone. For ten years, the Greek army had camped on the shore near Troy. Their ships were pulled up on the sand. Their huts and tents stood in long rows behind them. Then, one morning, everything changed. The Trojans looked out from their walls and saw smoke rising from the beach. The camp was burning. The ships were gone. The sea was empty. The poets describe the joy of the Trojans. The gates were opened for the first time in years. People streamed out onto the plain. They walked through the empty camp. They pointed out where the great warriors had stayed. Here was the hut of Achilles. There was the place where the ships had stood. Even so, not everything was gone. In the middle of the beach stood the strange wooden horse. Yet for that one morning, the people of Troy believed their long war was over.

[long-pause]

When myths and ancient walls help the evening slow down, a subscription quietly brings the next old legend your way.

[long-pause]

{img:03-laocoon-priest-warning-crowd-wooden-horse}

A priest named Laocoon warned the Trojans not to trust the horse. In Virgil's poem, not all Trojans were fooled. A priest named Laocoon came running down from the city. He shouted at the crowd. He told them that the Greeks could not be trusted. He said that the horse might be full of hidden soldiers, or a machine to spy on the city. He spoke the famous line that is still quoted today. In English, it warns people to beware of Greeks, even when they bring gifts. He threw a spear at the side of the horse. It stuck there, and the hollow wood echoed. Even so, the Trojans did not listen. In the story, a terrible sign seemed to prove him wrong. Two great serpents came out of the sea and attacked the priest and his sons. The Trojans took this as a punishment from the gods. Yet the reader of the poem knows that Laocoon was right. His warning is one of the great moments of ancient literature.

[long-pause]

{img:04-sinon-greek-captive-hands-tied-trojans}

A Greek named Sinon persuaded the Trojans that the horse was a holy offering. According to Virgil, the Greeks had left one man behind on purpose. His name was Sinon. Trojan shepherds found him and brought him before King Priam with his hands tied. Sinon told a clever and moving story. He said that the Greeks had wanted to sacrifice him to the gods, but he had escaped. He said the horse was an offering to the goddess Athena. The Greeks had built it so large on purpose. If the Trojans brought it into their city, Athena would protect Troy forever. If they harmed it, Troy would be destroyed. Priam felt pity for him and ordered his ropes to be removed. Sinon's story was a lie from beginning to end. Even so, it was very convincing. It played on the Trojans' hopes and fears. In the poem, Sinon is the second half of the trick. The horse alone was not enough. It needed a good story to bring it inside the walls.

[long-pause]

{img:05-trojans-pulling-wooden-horse-through-gate-garlands}

The Trojans broke open part of their own wall to bring the horse inside. The Trojans decided to take the horse into the city. It was too big to pass through the gate. So, in Virgil's story, they pulled down part of their own wall. They put wheels or rollers under the horse and tied ropes around its neck. Then they dragged it slowly across the plain. Children and young women sang hymns and touched the ropes for luck. Four times, the poem says, the horse stopped at the gate. Four times, the sound of weapons rang from inside it. Yet no one noticed. The Trojans hung garlands of flowers on it and placed it near the temple of Athena. Then the whole city celebrated. People feasted and drank late into the night. Even so, there was one more warning. The princess Cassandra cried out that the city was doomed. But no one ever believed her. That evening, Troy fell asleep happy for the last time.

[long-pause]

{img:06-cassandra-princess-temple-steps-night-torch}

The princess Cassandra could see the future, but she was cursed so that no one believed her. Cassandra was one of the daughters of King Priam. In the old myths, she was very beautiful. The god Apollo fell in love with her. He gave her the gift of seeing the future. But when she refused him, he could not take the gift back. Instead, he added a curse. She would always see the truth, but no one would ever believe her. Throughout the war, Cassandra warned the Trojans of what was coming. She warned against Paris bringing Helen to Troy. She warned against the wooden horse. Each time, people thought she was mad. Her story is one of the saddest in Greek myth. Even so, her name lives on. Today, a person who gives warnings that are ignored is sometimes called a Cassandra. Yet in Homer's Iliad, she appears only briefly. She is the first to see her father bringing home Hector's body. Much of her famous story was developed by later poets and playwrights.

[long-pause]

{img:07-quiet-troy-night-stars-walls-sleeping-city}

In the story, the Greek warriors climbed out of the horse in the middle of the night. According to the poems, Troy slept after its great celebration. Late in the night, Sinon crept to the horse. He opened a hidden door in its side. The Greek warriors climbed down a rope. Among them, the story says, was Odysseus, who had planned the whole trick. Meanwhile, the Greek fleet sailed back from the island in the darkness. The warriors from the horse opened the gates. The army poured in. Troy fell that night. The ancient poets describe its end in great detail. Even so, historians ask whether any of this really happened. There is no direct evidence of a wooden horse. Some scholars think it may be a memory of a siege machine. Others think it may stand for an earthquake, because the Greek god of earthquakes, Poseidon, was also the god of horses. Yet whatever the truth, the story never lost its power. People have told it for nearly three thousand years.

[long-pause]

{img:08-hisarlik-mound-troy-ruins-plain-dardanelles}

The ruins of Troy lie on a low hill in Turkey, near the entrance to the Dardanelles. For centuries, people wondered whether Troy had ever existed. Then, in the 1800s, archaeologists turned their attention to a low hill in northwest Turkey. It is called Hisarlik. It stands near the Aegean coast, close to the entrance of the Dardanelles. The Dardanelles is a narrow strait that connects the Aegean Sea with the Sea of Marmara and, beyond it, the Black Sea. The ancient Greeks called it the Hellespont. Today, most scholars agree that Hisarlik is the site of Troy. Greeks and Romans who lived there in later times believed so too. They built a new city on the hill and called it Ilion. Even so, the site does not look like a great city today. It is a small hill of stones, walls and trenches. Yet beneath it lie layers of history, piled up over thousands of years. Each layer is the ruin of a different city.

[long-pause]

{img:09-troy-layers-cross-section-trench-ancient-walls}

Archaeologists found not one Troy, but at least nine cities built on top of each other. When archaeologists dug into the hill of Hisarlik, they made a surprising discovery. There was not just one city there. There were many. One after another, cities had been built, destroyed and built again on the same spot. Each new city stood on the ruins of the one before. Over time, the hill grew higher. Archaeologists have numbered the main layers from Troy One, at the bottom, to Troy Nine, at the top. The oldest goes back to around three thousand BC. The youngest is the Roman city of Ilium. Within these main layers, there are dozens of smaller building phases. Even so, it was hard to know which layer belonged to Homer's Troy. Early diggers made mistakes. Yet today, most scholars look at two layers. They are known as Troy Six and Troy Seven A. These cities stood in the late Bronze Age, around the time when the Trojan War may have taken place.

[long-pause]

{img:10-troy-vi-sloping-stone-walls-tower-bronze-age}

The walls of Troy Six were built of carefully cut stone and leaned gently inwards. Troy Six was a strong and wealthy city. It stood from around seventeen hundred to thirteen hundred BC. Its citadel was surrounded by impressive walls. They were built of large blocks of limestone. The lower part of the walls sloped gently inwards. Above, there was once a wall of mud brick. Several great towers guarded the gates. Parts of these walls still stand today, several metres high. Visitors can walk beside them and touch the old stones. Homer describes Troy as a city with fine walls and high gates. The walls of Troy Six fit that description well. Some scholars believe that the poems preserve a distant memory of this real city. Even so, Troy Six did not end in a war. Around thirteen hundred BC, it was damaged, perhaps by an earthquake. The next city, Troy Seven A, was built on its ruins. That city ended in fire, around eleven hundred and eighty BC.

[long-pause]

{img:11-lower-town-troy-houses-outside-citadel}

In the 1990s, archaeologists discovered that Troy had a large lower town outside the citadel. For a long time, people thought Troy was quite small. The citadel on the hill covered only a few acres. It seemed too small to be the great city of the poems. Then, from 1988, a German archaeologist named Manfred Korfmann led new excavations. His team used modern methods to study the ground around the hill. They found that the city had been much larger. Below the citadel lay a lower town, where ordinary people lived. It was protected by a ditch cut into the rock. Korfmann believed that several thousand people lived in Troy at its height. Some estimates go as high as ten thousand. Even so, these numbers are still debated. Other archaeologists think he was too generous. Yet the discovery changed the picture. Troy was not just a fortress. It was a real city, with houses, streets and workshops, spread out around the foot of the hill.

[long-pause]

{img:12-hittite-clay-tablet-cuneiform-wilusa}

Clay tablets from the Hittite Empire mention a city called Wilusa, which may be Troy. Troy did not only appear in Greek poems. It may also appear in real records from the Bronze Age. The Hittites were a powerful people who ruled much of Anatolia at that time. Their capital was at Hattusa, in the centre of modern Turkey. Thousands of their clay tablets have been found there. Some of them mention a land in the west called Wilusa. Many scholars believe Wilusa is the same name as Ilios, the Greek name for Troy. One tablet records a treaty with a king of Wilusa named Alaksandu. His name sounds very like Alexandros. That was another name for Paris, the prince of Troy in the poems. Other tablets mention a people called the Ahhiyawa. They may be the Achaeans, Homer's name for the Greeks. Even so, these links are not certain. Scholars still argue about them. Yet the tablets suggest that the world of the poems was based on a real world of kings and kingdoms.

[long-pause]

{img:13-luwian-bronze-seal-small-object-museum}

A small bronze seal found at Troy is the only piece of writing ever discovered there from the Bronze Age. In 1995, archaeologists working at Troy found a tiny object. It was a small round seal made of bronze. It had writing on both sides. The writing was not Greek. It was in Luwian hieroglyphs, a script used in Anatolia in the Bronze Age. The seal names a scribe and his wife. It probably dates from the late 1100s BC. It is the only piece of writing from Bronze Age Troy that has ever been found. Its discovery was important. It suggests that the people of Troy belonged to the world of Anatolia, not Greece. Their language may have been Luwian, which was related to Hittite. Even so, one small seal cannot tell us everything. It may have been brought from somewhere else. Yet it is a precious clue. In the poems, Trojans and Greeks speak to each other easily. In reality, they may have spoken quite different languages.

[long-pause]

{img:14-troy-gate-ramp-paved-road-ancient-stones}

An ancient paved ramp still leads up to one of the gates of Troy. Among the ruins of Troy, one of the most striking sights is a stone ramp. It leads up from the lower ground to a gateway in the walls. It is paved with large flat stones. Heinrich Schliemann found it in the 1870s. He was very excited. He believed it led to the palace of King Priam. He called it the Scaean Gate, after a gate mentioned by Homer. In fact, the ramp is much older than the time of the Trojan War. It belongs to Troy Two, around twenty-five hundred BC. That is more than a thousand years before the events of the poems. Even so, the ramp is impressive. Its stones were laid by people who lived at Troy in the early Bronze Age. Visitors still walk up it today. Yet it is also a reminder of how easy it is to see what we want to see. The search for Troy was full of such hopeful mistakes.

[long-pause]

{img:15-bronze-age-house-interior-hearth-loom-clay-jars}

Ordinary Trojans lived in houses of stone and mud brick, with a hearth in the main room. Most people in Bronze Age Troy lived simple lives. Their houses had stone foundations. The walls above were made of mud bricks, dried in the sun. Roofs were flat, made of wooden beams covered with reeds and clay. In summer, people could sleep on the roof in the cool night air. Inside, the main room had a hearth for cooking and warmth. Smoke escaped through a hole in the roof. Large clay jars stood along the walls. They held grain, oil, wine and water. Some houses had a second room for storage or animals. Floors were made of beaten earth. Archaeologists have found the remains of these houses in the excavations. Even so, the houses of the rich were larger. They had more rooms and finer walls. The palace on top of the hill was the grandest of all. Yet for most families, life centred on one warm room, a fire and the work of the day.

[long-pause]

{img:16-women-weaving-loom-courtyard-bronze-age}

Archaeologists have found thousands of spindle whorls at Troy, showing how much spinning and weaving went on. Making cloth was one of the most important tasks in any Bronze Age home. Wool from sheep and fibres from flax were spun into thread. This was done with a spindle, a stick with a small weight at the end called a whorl. At Troy, archaeologists have found thousands of these whorls. They are small round objects of clay or stone, with a hole in the middle. The thread was then woven on a tall upright loom. Heavy clay weights hung from the bottom to keep the threads tight. In the poems, even queens and princesses weave. In the Iliad, Helen weaves a great cloth showing the battles of the Trojans and Greeks. Andromache weaves in her room when she hears the sad news of Hector's death. Even so, weaving was hard and slow. A single large cloth could take months. Yet it was work that women did together, talking and singing as their hands moved.

[long-pause]

{img:17-horses-grazing-trojan-plain-river-evening}

Homer often called the Trojans "tamers of horses". In the Iliad, the Trojans are often described with a special phrase. They are called the horse-taming Trojans. The great hero Hector is called Hector, tamer of horses. The very last line of the Iliad uses this phrase. It describes the funeral of Hector, the tamer of horses. Horses were precious in the Bronze Age. They pulled the light war chariots of kings and nobles. They were a sign of wealth and power. Archaeology seems to support Homer's picture. In the layer of Troy Six, archaeologists found horse bones. They suggest that horses were important at Troy from around that time. The wide plain below the city had good grass and water from the rivers. Even so, ordinary people rarely owned horses. Farm work was done by oxen and donkeys. Yet the image of the Trojans as horse people stayed with them. It may be one more echo of the real city behind the poems.

[long-pause]

{img:18-bronze-age-meal-bread-figs-olives-wine-table}

The people of Troy ate bread made from barley and wheat, with lentils, fish, figs and cheese. Archaeologists can learn a great deal about ancient diets. They study seeds, bones and remains of food found in the ruins. At Troy, they found evidence of barley, wheat, lentils, peas and beans. People made flat bread and porridge from the grain. They ate figs, grapes and other fruit. They kept sheep, goats, pigs and cattle. Milk was made into cheese. Fish and shellfish came from the nearby sea and rivers. Wine was made from grapes. In the poems, heroes often eat roast meat at great feasts. Yet that was not daily food for most people. Meat was usually eaten only at festivals and sacrifices. Even so, the land around Troy was fertile. The plain was watered by rivers and good for farming. The sea was close. A family in Troy, in good years, would not have gone hungry. Their meals were simple, but healthy and fresh.

[long-pause]

{img:19-potter-wheel-grey-pottery-workshop-bronze-age}

The potters of Troy made a distinctive grey pottery that archaeologists can easily recognise. Pottery is one of the most useful things for archaeologists. Clay pots break easily, but the broken pieces survive for thousands of years. At Troy, one type of pottery is very common in the late Bronze Age. It is a smooth grey ware, often with a slightly shiny surface. Archaeologists call it Anatolian grey ware. It was made on a potter's wheel and fired in a kiln. Potters made jugs, bowls, cups and storage jars. Alongside it, archaeologists have found pottery from the Mycenaean world of Greece. These pieces were brought to Troy by trade. Their style is quite different, with painted patterns. Even so, the presence of Greek pottery does not mean that Greeks lived at Troy. It shows that the two worlds traded with each other. Yet it also tells us something important. Troy was connected to Greece long before any war, through the quiet exchange of goods.

[long-pause]

{img:20-ships-trading-bay-near-troy-bronze-age}

Troy's position at the mouth of the Hellespont made it a centre of trade. Troy stood in a very good spot. The Hellespont was the sea route between the Aegean and the Black Sea. Ships passing through the strait often had to wait. Strong currents and winds blew from the north for much of the summer. In the Bronze Age, ships had simple square sails and oars. They could not easily sail against the wind. So they waited in sheltered bays near Troy until the wind changed. This gave the Trojans a chance to trade with them. They may also have charged tolls. In the Bronze Age, there was a bay closer to Troy than the coast today. Over thousands of years, the rivers filled it with mud. Even so, historians still debate how important trade was to Troy. Yet its location must have made it rich. Some scholars think that rivalry over this trade could have been one real cause of the war.

[long-pause]

{img:21-bronze-age-children-playing-clay-toys-courtyard}

Children in Bronze Age Anatolia played with clay animals, rattles and balls. Children have always played, even in the distant past. Archaeologists have found clay toys at many Bronze Age sites in the region. There were small animals, such as horses, oxen and birds. There were rattles made of clay with small stones inside. Some figurines may have been toys, though others had religious uses. Children also played with balls, sticks and knucklebones, the small bones of sheep. Knucklebones were thrown like dice in a game. Even so, childhood was short. Children helped their families from an early age. They watched the animals, fetched water and helped with the harvest. Girls learned to spin and weave. Boys learned to farm, fish or work in a craft. In the Iliad, Hector's baby son is held in his nurse's arms. He is frightened by his father's helmet. It is one of the few moments in the poem when a small child appears, and it is one of the most gentle.

[long-pause]

{img:22-storage-jars-sunk-into-house-floor-troy}

In the last city of the Bronze Age at Troy, families sank large storage jars into the floors of their houses. When the archaeologist Carl Blegen excavated the layer called Troy Seven A, he noticed something unusual. The houses were small and crowded close together, even inside the citadel. Many of them had large clay storage jars sunk into the floor. Only the tops showed above the ground. Each jar was covered with a flat stone. These jars could hold grain, oil, wine or water. Blegen thought they showed a city preparing for a siege. People had moved inside the walls and stored as much food as they could. The city was then destroyed by fire, around eleven hundred and eighty BC. Arrowheads and signs of fighting were found in the ruins. Blegen believed this might be the Troy of the Trojan War. Even so, other explanations are possible. The jars might simply show a crowded and anxious time. Yet the picture is striking. It seems to show ordinary families, quietly preparing for hard days ahead.

[long-pause]

{img:23-spring-cave-water-channel-rock-troy}

Beneath Troy, archaeologists explored a long underground water system cut into the rock. Every city needs water. At Troy, the people found it underground. On the edge of the lower town, there is a cave cut into the rock. Long tunnels run from it deep into the hill. Water from underground springs flows out through them. Manfred Korfmann's team explored and dated this system. They found that it was first cut in the early Bronze Age. It was used and extended for many centuries. In the Roman period, it was still a source of water. The discovery interested scholars of the Hittite tablets. A treaty with the king of Wilusa lists the gods who protected the agreement. Among them is a god of the underground watercourse of Wilusa. Some think this may refer to the spring cave at Troy. Even so, the link is not certain. Yet it is a beautiful idea. A small underground stream may connect the stones of Troy with words written in a distant Hittite city.

[long-pause]

{img:24-uluburun-shipwreck-copper-ingots-seabed}

A shipwreck from the late Bronze Age, found off the coast of Turkey, shows what traders carried in Troy's time. In 1982, a sponge diver off the south coast of Turkey saw strange metal objects on the seabed. They were flat copper ingots, shaped a little like animal hides. Archaeologists soon realised they had found a very old ship. It is called the Uluburun wreck, after the place where it sank. It went down around thirteen hundred BC. Its cargo was amazing. There were about ten tonnes of copper and one tonne of tin. Melted together, these would make bronze. There were glass ingots, ebony wood, ivory, amber and jars of resin. There was gold jewellery from many lands. The goods came from Egypt, Cyprus, Syria, Greece and further away. Even so, no one knows exactly where the ship was going. Yet it gives a wonderful picture of the late Bronze Age. This was a world connected by trade. Ships like this one may well have passed close to the walls of Troy.

[long-pause]

{img:25-cremation-urns-cemetery-outside-walls-bronze-age}

The people of Troy Six burned their dead and buried the ashes in clay urns outside the walls. In 1934, Carl Blegen's team found a cemetery just outside the walls of Troy Six. It contained clay urns holding the ashes and burned bones of the dead. Some urns also held small gifts, such as beads or pins. The people of Troy at that time cremated their dead. This was unusual. In Mycenaean Greece, the dead were mostly buried, not burned. Yet in the poems of Homer, the heroes are burned on great funeral pyres. Patroclus and Hector both receive cremation. Their bones are gathered and placed in golden urns or boxes. The cemetery at Troy shows that cremation was a real practice there in the late Bronze Age. Even so, the graves at Troy are simple. They do not contain great treasures. Yet they bring the ancient people of the city very close. Each small urn held someone who had once walked the streets of Troy, beneath its tall walls.

[long-pause]

{img:26-lyre-tortoise-shell-strings-resting-table}

Music in the age of Troy was played on lyres, flutes and drums. Music was part of life in the Bronze Age. Paintings and carvings from the period show people playing instruments. The most important was the lyre. It had a sound box, two curved arms and strings made from animal gut. The player plucked the strings with fingers or a small pick. A wall painting from the palace at Pylos shows a musician playing a lyre. A bird flies up from it, as if carried by the music. People also played pipes, made of reed or bone, and simple drums. Music was heard at feasts, at religious festivals and at funerals. In the poems, singers perform at the feasts of kings. Achilles plays the lyre in his tent. Even so, the actual tunes have been lost. No one knows exactly how the music sounded. Yet scholars have tried to rebuild ancient instruments and play them. Their gentle, bright tones give a faint echo of the songs heard in Troy.

[long-pause]

{img:27-blind-bard-lyre-singing-hall-firelight}

The story of Troy was first told in songs, long before it was written down. The Trojan War, if it happened, took place around twelve hundred BC. Yet the great poems about it were composed much later. For centuries, the story was kept alive by singers. They travelled from place to place and sang at feasts and festivals. They did not read from books. They knew the stories by heart. They used fixed phrases and patterns that helped them remember. Each time they sang, the poem changed a little. Scholars call this oral poetry. In the 1930s, an American scholar named Milman Parry studied singers in Yugoslavia. They could still perform very long poems from memory in the same way. His work helped explain how Homer's poems were made. Even so, at some point, the songs were written down. This probably happened in the eighth or seventh century BC, after the Greeks adopted a new alphabet. Yet the poems still carry the rhythm of a voice, singing beside a fire.

[long-pause]

{img:28-homer-bust-marble-bearded-old-man}

No one knows for certain who Homer was, or even whether he existed. The two great poems about Troy are the Iliad and the Odyssey. The ancient Greeks believed both were the work of one poet named Homer. They said he was blind and that he came from the eastern Aegean, perhaps from the island of Chios or the city of Smyrna. Yet very little is known about him. The stories about his life were written centuries later and are mostly legend. Many statues of Homer survive, showing an old man with a beard and closed eyes. But these were made long after his time. They show what people imagined, not what he looked like. Scholars have argued about Homer for over two hundred years. Some believe a single great poet shaped each poem. Others think the poems grew over generations. Even so, most agree on one thing. Whoever composed them was a master. The Iliad and the Odyssey are among the greatest works in all of literature.

[long-pause]

{img:29-ancient-scroll-greek-writing-wooden-table}

The Iliad has about fifteen thousand lines, yet it tells only a few weeks of the war. Many people think the Iliad tells the whole story of the Trojan War. In fact, it does not. It covers only a few weeks in the tenth and final year of the war. It begins with a quarrel between two Greek leaders, Agamemnon and Achilles. It ends with the funeral of the Trojan hero Hector. It does not include the wooden horse. It does not describe the fall of Troy. The poem has about fifteen thousand seven hundred lines. They are written in a special rhythm called dactylic hexameter. Each line has six beats, like the steady roll of waves. Even so, the Iliad manages to contain the whole war. Through memories, speeches and stories, the listener learns about the past and the future. Yet its main subject is not the war itself. It is the anger of Achilles, and what that anger costs. The poem begins with that very word, anger, as its first word.

[long-pause]

{img:30-greek-festival-crowd-listening-reciter-stage}

In ancient Athens, the poems of Homer were recited at a great festival every four years. By the sixth century BC, Homer's poems were famous all over the Greek world. In Athens, they were recited at a great festival called the Panathenaia. It was held every four years in honour of the goddess Athena. Professional performers, called rhapsodes, took turns reciting the Iliad and the Odyssey. They stood before large crowds, holding a staff. They performed the poems with great feeling and drama. Some could move their listeners to tears. Children learned Homer at school. They memorised long passages. Educated Greeks quoted him in speeches and letters. The poems became like a shared book of wisdom and history. Even so, the Greeks did not treat Homer as perfect. Some philosophers, like Plato, criticised the way he described the gods. Yet nearly everyone knew his poems. For a thousand years, Homer was at the very heart of Greek education.

[long-pause]

{img:31-boar-tusk-helmet-museum-display}

Homer describes a helmet made of boar's tusks, and archaeologists have found real ones. In the tenth book of the Iliad, Odysseus is given a special helmet. Homer describes it in detail. It is made of leather, with rows of white boar's tusks sewn on the outside. For a long time, scholars wondered if such a helmet had ever existed. Then archaeologists began finding them in Mycenaean graves in Greece. Pieces of boars' tusks, carefully cut and drilled, were found arranged in rows. Small ivory carvings show warriors wearing these helmets. Making one took the tusks of many wild boars. It was a sign of the owner's skill as a hunter. Yet these helmets were common earlier in the Bronze Age, centuries before the probable date of the war. Even so, the description is remarkable. It means that a detail of Bronze Age life survived in songs for perhaps five hundred years. The poems were not history. But they held real memories, passed down from singer to singer.

[long-pause]

{img:32-aegean-sea-islands-sunset-sailing-ship}

Homer often called the sea "wine-dark". Homer's poems are full of repeated phrases. Dawn is rosy-fingered. Achilles is swift-footed. Athena is grey-eyed. The sea is often wine-dark. These phrases helped singers fill out the rhythm of their lines. They also gave the poems a calm and familiar sound. Listeners knew them and waited for them. The wine-dark sea has puzzled readers for a long time. The sea is not usually the colour of wine. Some scholars once suggested that the ancient Greeks saw colours differently. Others think the phrase describes the deep, shining darkness of the sea at evening. Greek wine was often mixed with water, and its colour may have been different from wine today. Even so, the phrase is beautiful. It captures something real about the sea. Yet no one can be sure exactly what Homer meant. Perhaps it is enough to imagine the Aegean at sunset, glowing deep and dark between the islands.

[long-pause]

{img:33-king-priam-old-throne-palace-hall}

In the poems, King Priam of Troy had fifty sons. At the time of the war, Troy was ruled by an old king named Priam. In the poems, he is wise, gentle and much loved. He had many children. Homer says he had fifty sons and many daughters. Nineteen of the sons were children of his queen, Hecuba. The others were born to other women of the palace. His sons lived with their wives in rooms around his palace. His daughters lived nearby with their husbands. It was a huge royal household. Priam had been a great warrior in his youth. By the time of the war, he was too old to fight. He watched the battles from the city walls. Even so, the war took a terrible toll on his family. One by one, many of his sons were killed. Yet in the Iliad, Priam never loses his dignity. He remains a figure of kindness. Many readers find him one of the most moving characters in the poem.

[long-pause]

{img:34-queen-hecuba-palace-courtyard-women}

Queen Hecuba became a symbol of grief in later Greek literature. Hecuba was the wife of Priam and the queen of Troy. She was the mother of many of his children, including Hector, Paris and Cassandra. In the Iliad, she appears several times. She begs Hector not to go out and fight Achilles. She leads the women of Troy in prayer to the goddess Athena. When Hector dies, she cries out in grief from the walls. After the war, her story becomes even sadder. In the plays of the Athenian writer Euripides, she has lost her husband, her sons and her city. She is taken away as a captive. Euripides wrote two plays about her. They are among the most powerful plays about the suffering of war. Even so, Hecuba is not only a figure of sorrow. She is also strong and fierce. Yet for later Greeks, she stood for every mother who lost her children in war. Shakespeare's Hamlet even asks what Hecuba means to an actor who weeps for her.

[long-pause]

{img:35-prince-hector-armour-palace-steps-dawn}

Prince Hector was the greatest warrior of Troy and its main defender. Hector was the eldest son of Priam and Hecuba. He was the leader of the Trojan army and its greatest fighter. In the Iliad, he is brave, loyal and thoughtful. He fights not for glory alone, but to protect his family and his city. He knows that Troy may fall. Yet he keeps fighting. He is also a loving husband and father. Many readers find Hector the most sympathetic hero in the whole poem. He is a good man in a terrible situation. He did not start the war. His brother Paris did. Even so, he takes responsibility for defending everyone. In the ancient world, Hector was admired by both Greeks and Romans. Later, in the Middle Ages, he was counted among the Nine Worthies, the nine greatest heroes of history. Yet in the Iliad, he is very human. He feels fear. At one moment, he even runs from Achilles before turning to face him.

[long-pause]

{img:36-paris-prince-archer-bow-palace-garden}

Paris, the prince who started the war, was famous for his beauty, not his courage. Paris was another son of Priam. He was also called Alexandros. In the poems, he is handsome, charming and fond of fine clothes. He is a skilled archer. Yet he is not much of a warrior. In the Iliad, he agrees to fight Menelaus in single combat to end the war. He is losing badly. Then the goddess Aphrodite snatches him away and carries him back to his bedroom. His brother Hector is furious with him. He tells Paris that it would have been better if he had never been born. Paris accepts the criticism with a smile. Even so, he is not a complete coward. Later in the war, according to later tales, it is his arrow that kills Achilles. Yet he remains the cause of Troy's troubles. His love for Helen began the war that destroyed his family. In Greek art and poetry, he is often shown as a beautiful young man who loved pleasure more than duty.

[long-pause]

{img:37-aeneas-young-warrior-old-father-shoulders}

The Trojan prince Aeneas escaped the fall of the city, and the Romans claimed him as their ancestor. Aeneas was a member of the Trojan royal family. He was a cousin of Hector. In the Iliad, he is a strong warrior, protected by the gods. His mother was said to be the goddess Aphrodite. When Troy fell, according to later stories, Aeneas escaped. He carried his old father, Anchises, on his shoulders. He led his young son by the hand. He also carried the sacred images of the gods of Troy. After long wanderings, he reached Italy. The Romans believed that he was the ancestor of their people. The Roman poet Virgil told his story in the Aeneid, written around twenty BC. In it, Aeneas visits Carthage and the underworld before arriving in Italy. The family of Julius Caesar even claimed to be descended from his son. Even so, these stories were legend, not history. Yet for the Romans, they mattered deeply. They made Rome the heir of Troy, rising from its ashes.

[long-pause]

{img:38-helen-of-sparta-palace-window-spring}

Helen of Sparta was said to be the most beautiful woman in the world. In Greek myth, Helen was the daughter of the god Zeus and a mortal queen named Leda. She grew up in Sparta, in the south of Greece. She was famous for her beauty. Many princes wanted to marry her. Her father made all of them swear an oath. They promised to defend whoever she married. She chose, or was given to, Menelaus, the king of Sparta. They had a daughter named Hermione. Then a Trojan prince arrived as a guest. His name was Paris. When he left, Helen went with him to Troy. The Greeks saw this as a terrible insult. Because of the old oath, the princes of Greece gathered an army to bring her back. Even so, the poets did not agree on whether Helen went willingly. Some said she was carried off by force. Others said she fell in love. Yet the result was the same. Her departure began the Trojan War.

[long-pause]

{img:39-judgement-of-paris-three-goddesses-mountain}

According to myth, the war began with a golden apple and a beauty contest between three goddesses. The Greeks told a story about how the war began. At a wedding of the gods, the goddess of strife was not invited. Angry, she threw a golden apple among the guests. On it were the words, for the most beautiful. Three goddesses claimed it. They were Hera, the queen of the gods, Athena, goddess of wisdom, and Aphrodite, goddess of love. Zeus did not want to choose. So he sent them to a young shepherd on Mount Ida, near Troy. The shepherd was Paris, who had been raised away from the palace. Each goddess offered him a gift. Hera offered power. Athena offered wisdom and victory. Aphrodite offered him the most beautiful woman in the world. Paris chose Aphrodite. Even so, this story is not in the Iliad in detail. Homer mentions it only briefly. Yet it became one of the most popular scenes in art. Painters have shown the judgement of Paris for more than two thousand years.

[long-pause]

{img:40-helen-on-walls-old-men-watching-plain}

When Helen walked on the walls of Troy, the old men said no one could blame them for fighting over her. In the third book of the Iliad, there is a quiet and famous scene. Helen leaves her room and walks to the walls of Troy. There, King Priam sits with the old men of the city. They are too old to fight, but they love to talk. As Helen comes near, they speak softly to each other. They say that no one could blame the Trojans and Greeks for suffering so long for such a woman. She looks like a goddess. Yet they also say that she should go home, so that their children do not suffer. Priam calls Helen to sit beside him. He speaks kindly to her. He tells her that he does not blame her. He blames the gods. Then he asks her to name the Greek leaders on the plain below. Helen points them out one by one. Even so, she feels deep regret. She calls herself shameful and wishes she had died before coming to Troy.

[long-pause]

{img:41-menelaus-king-sparta-throne-hall}

In the Odyssey, Helen and Menelaus are back together in Sparta, living calmly after the war. Menelaus was the king of Sparta and the husband of Helen. He was the younger brother of Agamemnon. In the Iliad, he is a brave but not the greatest warrior. His fight with Paris is the closest the war comes to a quick end. After Troy fell, the poems say that Menelaus found Helen. Some later stories say that he meant to kill her, but dropped his sword when he saw her beauty. In any case, he took her home. Their journey was long. Storms drove them to Egypt, and they wandered for eight years. In the Odyssey, the young Telemachus visits them in Sparta. He finds them living calmly in their palace. Helen serves wine with a special drug that takes away sorrow. She and Menelaus tell stories about the war. Even so, there are hints of old tension between them. Yet the scene is peaceful. After so much suffering, the couple at the heart of the war have found a quiet home.

[long-pause]

{img:42-egyptian-temple-river-nile-helen-legend}

Some ancient writers claimed that Helen never went to Troy at all. Not all ancient Greeks believed the usual story of Helen. The poet Stesichorus, in the sixth century BC, told a different version. In his story, Helen never went to Troy. The gods made a phantom, an image of Helen made from cloud. Paris took this phantom to Troy, while the real Helen was carried to Egypt. The Greeks and Trojans fought for ten years over an illusion. The historian Herodotus also reported that Egyptian priests told him Helen had stayed in Egypt. Later, the playwright Euripides wrote a whole play based on this idea. It was called Helen. In it, Menelaus finds his real wife in Egypt after the war. Even so, this version was never as popular as Homer's. Yet it carries a quiet and serious thought. If the war was fought over a phantom, then all those men died for nothing real. Some ancient writers seem to have wondered whether many wars are like that.

[long-pause]

{img:43-mount-ida-forest-peak-clouds-above-plain}

In the Iliad, Zeus watched the war from the top of Mount Ida, near Troy. In Homer's world, the gods were never far away. They watched the war closely, and they took sides. Zeus, the king of the gods, had a special place to watch from. It was the peak of Mount Ida, a high mountain to the south-east of Troy. From there, he could see the whole plain, the city and the Greek ships. In the poem, he sometimes holds up a pair of golden scales. He places the fates of the two sides on them, or of two heroes. Whichever side sinks lower is doomed. Mount Ida is a real mountain. Today it is called Kaz Dağı. Its forests of pine and fir still cover its slopes. Even so, Zeus is not completely in control in the poem. The other gods argue with him and even trick him. Yet the image of the god on the mountain, watching the mortals below, gives the Iliad a sense of something larger than the war.

[long-pause]

{img:44-temple-athena-statue-robe-offering-women}

The women of Troy offered their finest robe to the goddess Athena, but she refused their prayer. Athena was the goddess of wisdom and of crafts like weaving. She was also a goddess of war. In the Trojan War, she supported the Greeks. Yet the Trojans also worshipped her. Her temple stood high in the citadel of Troy. In the sixth book of the Iliad, Hector asks his mother, Queen Hecuba, to pray to Athena. Hecuba goes to her storeroom. She chooses the largest and most beautiful robe she owns, embroidered by women from the city of Sidon. It shines like a star. With the older women of Troy, she carries it to the temple. They place it on the knees of the goddess's statue. They pray for mercy for the city, its wives and its children. Even so, the poem says plainly that Athena refused. It is a short and sad moment. Yet it shows how the Trojans hoped. Even in war, they turned to the gods with their most precious things.

[long-pause]

{img:45-apollo-temple-columns-hillside-near-troy}

The god Apollo, who sided with the Trojans, was worshipped near Troy as a god of mice. Apollo was the god of music, light, healing and plague. In the Iliad, he supports the Trojans. The poem begins with his anger. Agamemnon has insulted a priest of Apollo, named Chryses. The god comes down from Mount Olympus in fury. For nine days, he shoots arrows of plague into the Greek camp. Many men and animals die. Near Troy, Apollo was worshipped under a special name. He was called Apollo Smintheus. The word probably comes from an old word for mouse. Mice could spread disease and damage crops. So a god who could send or stop plagues of mice was important to farmers. Archaeologists have found the remains of a temple of Apollo Smintheus not far from Troy. It was built much later, in Greek times. Even so, the cult was very old. Yet the link between mice and plague is interesting. Ancient people seem to have noticed a connection long before science explained it.

[long-pause]

{img:46-hephaestus-forge-shield-glowing-workshop}

The shield that the god Hephaestus made for Achilles showed a whole world at peace. After Patroclus died wearing Achilles' armour, Achilles had nothing to fight in. So his mother Thetis went to Hephaestus, the god of fire and metalwork. She asked him to make new armour for her son. In the eighteenth book of the Iliad, Homer describes the shield in great detail. It is one of the most famous passages in ancient poetry. The shield is round and made of bronze, tin, gold and silver. On it, Hephaestus shows the earth, the sky, the sea, the sun, the moon and the stars. He shows two cities. One is at peace, with weddings, dancing and a trial in the marketplace. The other is at war. He shows ploughing, harvest and a vineyard, where young people carry baskets of grapes while a boy plays music. He shows cattle, sheep and a great dance. Even so, the shield is made for war. Yet on its surface, Homer gathers almost all of ordinary human life.

[long-pause]

{img:47-harvest-field-reapers-king-oak-tree-shade}

One scene on the shield of Achilles shows a king standing happily in his field at harvest time. The shield of Achilles contains many small, peaceful scenes. One of them is a harvest. Hired workers cut the ripe grain with sharp sickles. Others follow behind and tie the stalks into bundles. Children gather the grain in their arms and carry it to them. In the middle of the field stands the king. He holds his staff and says nothing. Yet his heart is glad as he watches the work. Under an oak tree, servants prepare a meal. They have killed an ox. Women sprinkle white barley for the workers' supper. Another scene shows a vineyard heavy with dark grapes. Young men and women carry the fruit in baskets. A boy plays a lyre and sings a harvest song, and the others dance along behind him. Even so, these scenes appear in the middle of a war poem. Yet perhaps that is the point. They remind the listener of everything the war is destroying.

[long-pause]

{img:48-alexander-the-great-visiting-tomb-mound-troy}

Alexander the Great visited Troy and honoured the tomb of Achilles before his great campaign. For the ancient Greeks, Troy was a holy place. Many famous people visited it. In 480 BC, the Persian king Xerxes came on his way to invade Greece. The historian Herodotus says that he climbed up to the citadel and sacrificed a thousand oxen to Athena of Ilion. In 334 BC, a young Macedonian king crossed the Hellespont. He was Alexander, later called the Great. He had grown up reading Homer. According to ancient writers, he slept with a copy of the Iliad under his pillow. At Troy, he made offerings at the temple of Athena. He laid a wreath on a mound believed to be the tomb of Achilles. His friend Hephaestion did the same at the tomb of Patroclus. Alexander also took a shield from the temple, said to date from the Trojan War. Even so, the story may have been polished by later writers. Yet it shows how strongly Homer's heroes inspired people, even a thousand years later.

[long-pause]

{img:49-aulis-harbour-many-ships-beached-greek-army}

The Iliad lists more than a thousand Greek ships that sailed to Troy. In the second book of the Iliad, there is a long and unusual passage. It is called the Catalogue of Ships. It lists the Greek leaders who came to Troy, where they came from and how many ships each brought. Altogether, it counts one thousand one hundred and eighty-six ships. The list names many places in Greece, from big kingdoms to small villages. Some of these places were already forgotten by the time of Homer. Yet archaeologists have found that many of them were real Bronze Age settlements. This suggests that the catalogue may preserve a very old memory. Even so, the numbers are probably exaggerated. A fleet of more than a thousand ships would have been enormous for the time. Yet the catalogue gives a picture of a united Greek world, sailing together to war. According to the story, the fleet gathered at Aulis, a harbour on the east coast of Greece, before crossing the Aegean Sea.

[long-pause]

{img:50-lion-gate-mycenae-stone-lions-entrance}

The leader of the Greeks, Agamemnon, was said to be king of Mycenae. In the poems, the Greek army was led by Agamemnon. He was the brother of Menelaus and the most powerful king in Greece. His city was Mycenae, in the south of Greece. Mycenae was a real place. Its ruins still stand on a hill above a fertile plain. It was one of the greatest centres of the late Bronze Age. Its walls were built of stones so large that later Greeks believed giants had placed them. They called them Cyclopean walls, after the one-eyed giants of myth. The main entrance is the famous Lion Gate. Above the doorway, two stone lions face each other. It was built around twelve hundred and fifty BC. Even so, no one knows the names of the real kings of Mycenae. No inscriptions with their names have been found. Yet the city fits the picture in the poems. Homer called it Mycenae, rich in gold.

[long-pause]

{img:51-gold-mask-mycenae-museum-glass-case}

A golden mask found at Mycenae is called the Mask of Agamemnon, though it is much older than him. In 1876, Heinrich Schliemann excavated at Mycenae. Inside the walls, he found a circle of royal graves. They were full of treasures. There were gold cups, swords, jewellery and five gold masks. The masks had been placed over the faces of dead kings. One of them shows a man with a fine beard and moustache. Schliemann believed he had found the face of Agamemnon. According to a famous story, he sent a message saying that he had gazed upon the face of Agamemnon. Historians doubt that he really used those words. Even so, the name stuck. The golden mask is still called the Mask of Agamemnon. Yet modern dating shows that it is around three hundred years older than the probable time of the Trojan War. It belonged to an unknown king who lived long before. Today, it is displayed in the National Archaeological Museum in Athens, where it is one of the most famous objects in Greece.

[long-pause]

{img:52-achilles-young-warrior-tent-beach-lyre}

Achilles, the greatest Greek warrior, is shown in the Iliad playing the lyre in his tent. Achilles was the greatest warrior of the Greeks. He was the son of a mortal king, Peleus, and a sea goddess named Thetis. He was swift, strong and almost impossible to defeat in battle. Yet in the Iliad, he is not only a fighter. When the Greek envoys come to visit him, they find him in his tent, playing a lyre. He is singing songs about the glorious deeds of heroes. His close friend Patroclus sits beside him, listening. It is a peaceful moment in a violent poem. Achilles has withdrawn from the fighting. He is angry with Agamemnon, who has taken from him a woman he had won as a prize. Even so, his anger will have terrible results. Many Greeks die while he refuses to fight. Yet the image of the young hero with his lyre is one of the most gentle in the poem. It reminds readers that warriors also loved music and stories.

[long-pause]

{img:53-thetis-dipping-baby-river-styx-mist}

The famous story of Achilles' heel does not appear in Homer. Many people know the story of Achilles' heel. According to this legend, his mother Thetis wanted to make him immortal. When he was a baby, she dipped him into the River Styx, the river of the underworld. Its waters made him invulnerable. But she held him by the heel, so that one small spot stayed dry. Later, Achilles was killed by an arrow in that heel. Today, an Achilles heel means a small weakness in something strong. Yet this story is not in the Iliad or the Odyssey. In Homer, Achilles can be wounded like any other man. The heel story appears much later, in the writings of the Roman poet Statius in the first century AD. Even so, it became one of the best-known parts of the legend. The tendon at the back of the ankle is still called the Achilles tendon. Yet the original Achilles of Homer had no magic protection. His courage was entirely his own.

[long-pause]

{img:54-odysseus-clever-king-ithaca-ship-planning}

Odysseus, king of the small island of Ithaca, was famous for his cleverness rather than his strength. Among the Greek heroes, Odysseus stood out. He was not the strongest or the fastest. He was the cleverest. He ruled the small, rocky island of Ithaca, off the west coast of Greece. In the poems, he is a skilled speaker and a careful planner. He is often called the man of many ways, or of many turns. According to later stories, he did not want to go to war at all. When the Greek envoys came to take him, he pretended to be mad. He ploughed his field with an ox and a donkey and sowed salt instead of seed. But they placed his baby son in front of the plough. Odysseus turned aside, showing he was sane. So he had to go. Even so, he became one of the most valuable leaders of the army. It was Odysseus, in the story, who planned the wooden horse. Yet his greatest adventure came after the war, on the long journey home.

[long-pause]

{img:55-greek-camp-huts-beach-campfires-evening}

The Greek army lived for years in a camp of huts beside their ships. According to the poems, the Greeks did not live in tents for ten years. They built huts of wood and earth along the beach. The ships were pulled up on the sand behind them. In time, they also built a wall and a ditch to protect the camp. Each leader had his own hut and his own men. Achilles had a fine hut with a courtyard, built by his men from pine wood. Life in the camp was a mixture of fighting and waiting. Men went out to raid nearby towns for food and supplies. They held athletic games and feasts. They told stories and sang around the fires. Even so, the long war brought sickness and boredom. In the first book of the Iliad, a plague strikes the camp. Many men die. Yet the camp was also a kind of town. It had its own leaders, its own laws and its own daily routine, stretched out along the shore.

[long-pause]

{img:56-dendra-bronze-armour-panoply-museum}

Archaeologists have found a complete suit of Bronze Age armour made of bronze plates. In the poems, heroes wear shining bronze armour. Archaeologists have found real examples from the Bronze Age. The most famous was found at a place called Dendra, in southern Greece, in 1960. It is a full suit of armour made of bronze plates. It covered the body from the neck to the knees. It also had shoulder pieces and a high collar. It dates from around fourteen hundred BC. It weighs about fifteen kilograms. For a long time, scholars thought it was too heavy to fight in. They believed it was used only by men in chariots. Then, in a study published in 2023, researchers tested a copy. Greek marines wore it for eleven hours while doing battlefield tasks. They found that a trained soldier could fight in it. Even so, such armour was rare and expensive. Most soldiers had much simpler protection. Yet the Dendra armour shows that Homer's shining bronze warriors were not only imagination.

[long-pause]

{img:57-chariot-two-horses-plain-troy-dust}

In the Iliad, heroes often ride to battle in chariots, then step down to fight on foot. The chariot was the great war machine of the Bronze Age. It was light, with two wheels and a small platform. It was pulled by two horses. Kings and nobles of Egypt, the Hittite Empire and Mycenaean Greece all used chariots. In some battles, thousands of them were used. The great battle of Kadesh, in about twelve seventy-four BC, involved huge numbers of chariots on both sides. In the Iliad, however, chariots are used strangely. Heroes ride them to the battlefield like taxis. Then they get down and fight on foot. Some scholars think this shows that the poets no longer understood Bronze Age warfare. By their time, chariots were no longer used in battle. Even so, the poems keep the chariots as a sign of greatness. Each hero has his own driver and his own horses, often with names. Yet the gentle moments with the horses are often the most memorable parts of these scenes.

[long-pause]

{img:58-achilles-horses-weeping-patroclus-plain}

In the Iliad, the horses of Achilles weep for the death of Patroclus. Achilles had two immortal horses. Their names were Xanthus and Balius. They had been a gift from the gods to his father. When Achilles refused to fight, his friend Patroclus borrowed his armour and his chariot. He drove these horses into battle. Patroclus fought bravely, but he was killed by Hector. Then something strange happens in the poem. The horses stand still on the battlefield. They will not move. Tears run from their eyes onto the ground. Their manes hang down in the dust. Zeus sees them and feels pity. He says it was a mistake to give immortal horses to mortal men, who are the most wretched of all creatures. Even so, he gives them new strength so they can carry the driver to safety. Yet the image of the weeping horses stays with readers. It is one of the most touching moments in the Iliad, and it shows how deeply the poem feels the cost of war.

[long-pause]

{img:59-funeral-games-chariot-race-beach-crowd}

Achilles held athletic games in memory of Patroclus, with prizes for the winners. After the death of Patroclus, Achilles held a great funeral. Then, according to the twenty-third book of the Iliad, he held athletic games in his friend's honour. There was a chariot race, a boxing match and a wrestling contest. There was a foot race, archery and spear throwing. Achilles offered fine prizes. There were bronze cauldrons, tripods, horses, gold and even a lump of iron, which was very valuable at the time. The games were lively and sometimes funny. In the foot race, one runner slipped in the dung of sacrificed cattle. The other heroes laughed at him. Old Nestor gave his son long advice about how to win the chariot race. Even so, the games ended peacefully. Achilles was generous and fair, even to those who lost. Some historians see these games as an early form of the athletic contests that later became the Olympic Games. They were a way to turn grief into something shared.

[long-pause]

{img:60-hector-andromache-baby-farewell-gate}

Hector's baby son cried when he saw his father's helmet, so Hector took it off and laughed. In the sixth book of the Iliad, there is one of the most famous scenes in all literature. Hector returns briefly to Troy from the battlefield. He looks for his wife, Andromache. He finds her at the Scaean Gate, with their baby son Astyanax in the arms of a nurse. Andromache begs Hector not to go back to the fighting. She has already lost her father and brothers. Hector is everything to her now. Hector answers gently. He says he must fight. Then he reaches for his little son. But the baby is frightened by the bronze helmet and the nodding horsehair plume. He shrinks back crying into the nurse's arms. Hector and Andromache both laugh. Hector takes off his helmet and lays it on the ground. He kisses his son and prays that the boy will one day be greater than his father. Even so, both parents know what may come. Yet for a moment, they are simply a family.

[long-pause]

{img:61-troy-walls-three-times-running-plain-dusk}

In the Iliad, Hector ran three times around the walls of Troy before facing Achilles. After the death of Patroclus, Achilles returned to battle. He was filled with grief and rage. The Trojans fled back into their city. Only Hector stayed outside the gates. He knew he must face Achilles. But when Achilles came near, shining in new armour, Hector felt fear. He turned and ran. The two men ran three times around the walls of Troy. The poem describes the places they passed, including two springs where Trojan women used to wash their clothes in peaceful times. Homer notes this small detail as the two men run past. It is a reminder of a gentler world before the war. Even so, Hector finally stopped. He turned to face Achilles. He asked that whoever won would return the other's body to his family. Achilles refused. The fight was short. Hector was killed. Yet his last words warned Achilles that his own death was near.

[long-pause]

{img:62-priam-night-journey-wagon-gifts-moonlight}

Old King Priam crossed the battlefield at night to beg Achilles for his son's body. After Hector's death, Achilles kept his body in the Greek camp. He would not give it back. For days, Priam grieved. Then the gods decided that this must end. They told Priam to go to Achilles himself. It was a dangerous journey. Priam was old, and Achilles had killed many of his sons. Yet he agreed. He loaded a cart with rich gifts, cloaks, gold and fine cups. He set out at night with only one old herald. The god Hermes, disguised as a young man, guided them safely past the Greek guards. Priam arrived at the hut of Achilles. Achilles was sitting at his table after supper. No one saw the old king come in. Even so, Achilles did not strike him. Priam knelt before him. He took the hands that had killed his son, and he kissed them. It is one of the most powerful moments in all of Greek poetry.

[long-pause]

{img:63-priam-achilles-weeping-together-hut-lamplight}

Achilles and Priam wept together, each for the people he had lost. When Priam knelt and kissed his hands, Achilles was deeply moved. Priam asked him to remember his own father, Peleus. Peleus was also old, far away, waiting for a son who would not come home. Achilles thought of his father. He thought of Patroclus. He began to weep. Priam wept too, for Hector. The two enemies sat together and cried, each for his own loss. Then Achilles raised the old king gently. He agreed to return Hector's body. He even promised a truce of eleven days, so the Trojans could hold a proper funeral. Afterwards, they shared a meal. They looked at each other with wonder. Priam admired Achilles' beauty and strength. Achilles admired Priam's dignity. Even so, the war was not over. Yet in this moment, the poem shows something deeper than victory or defeat. It shows that enemies share the same grief, and the same humanity.

[long-pause]

{img:64-hector-funeral-pyre-trojans-dawn-plain}

The Iliad ends with the funeral of Hector, not with the fall of Troy. Priam brought Hector's body back to Troy. The people came out to meet him. Andromache, Hecuba and Helen each spoke words of mourning. Helen said that Hector had always been kind to her, when others blamed her. For nine days, the Trojans gathered wood. On the tenth day, they burned Hector's body on a great pyre. They put out the flames with wine. His brothers and friends gathered his white bones. They wrapped them in purple cloth and placed them in a golden box. They buried it under a mound of earth and stones. Then they held a funeral feast in Priam's palace. Even so, the poem says nothing more. It does not describe the death of Achilles or the fall of the city. It simply ends with the line about the funeral of Hector, tamer of horses. Yet many readers feel this is the perfect ending. The great war poem closes, not with triumph, but with quiet grief.

[long-pause]

{img:65-andromache-ship-leaving-coast-veil-sea}

After the fall of Troy, Hector's widow Andromache was taken across the sea to Greece. The fall of Troy did not end the stories. Ancient poets and playwrights told what happened to the survivors. Their fates were mostly sad. Andromache, the wife of Hector, was taken as a captive. She was given to Neoptolemus, the son of Achilles. He took her to his kingdom in northwest Greece. The playwright Euripides wrote a play about her life there. Later stories give her a gentler ending. After Neoptolemus died, she married Helenus, a Trojan prince and a brother of Hector. Together they ruled a small kingdom. In Virgil's Aeneid, Aeneas visits them on his journey. He finds Andromache still mourning Hector. She has built a small copy of Troy, with a little river named after a Trojan one. Even so, she has made a life for herself. Yet she has never forgotten. Her story shows how the memory of a lost home could last a whole lifetime.

[long-pause]

{img:66-palace-of-nestor-pylos-ruins-roof-hill}

The palace of old King Nestor, a wise Greek leader at Troy, has been found at Pylos. Nestor was the oldest of the Greek leaders at Troy. In the poems, he is wise and very talkative. He often tells long stories about his youth. He gives advice that is sometimes useful and sometimes not. Of all the Greek leaders, Nestor had one of the happiest returns. He sailed home quickly and safely to his kingdom of Pylos. In the Odyssey, Telemachus visits him there. He finds the old king making a sacrifice on the beach with his sons. In 1939, the American archaeologist Carl Blegen began digging at a hill near Pylos. He found the remains of a large Bronze Age palace. It had a great hall with a round hearth, storerooms full of jars and even a bathtub made of clay. Archaeologists call it the Palace of Nestor. Even so, there is no proof that a real Nestor lived there. Yet the palace fits the poem very well. Visitors can still see the bathtub today.

[long-pause]

{img:67-linear-b-clay-tablets-museum-case}

Clay tablets found at Pylos were written in the earliest known form of Greek. On his very first day of digging at Pylos, Carl Blegen made an amazing find. In a small room near the entrance of the palace, he found hundreds of clay tablets. They were covered with an unknown script. Scholars called it Linear B. Similar tablets had been found on Crete. For years, no one could read them. Then, in 1952, a young English architect named Michael Ventris cracked the code. He showed that Linear B was an early form of Greek. It was about five hundred years older than Homer. The tablets were not poems or stories. They were lists. They recorded sheep, grain, oil, bronze, chariot wheels and workers. One tablet lists women who worked as spinners and weavers. Even so, these dry lists were thrilling. They proved that people spoke Greek in the age of the Trojan War. Yet they also showed how different the real palaces were. They were busy offices, full of careful record-keepers.

[long-pause]

{img:68-aeschylus-theatre-stone-seats-hillside}

The story of Agamemnon's return home became one of the greatest plays of ancient Athens. Agamemnon, the leader of the Greeks, came home from Troy as a victor. Yet his return was not happy. While he was away, his wife Clytemnestra had turned against him. She had never forgiven him for sacrificing their daughter to the gods before the war. According to the myths, she and her lover killed Agamemnon soon after he came home. Their son Orestes later took revenge. These events became the subject of a famous set of three plays. They were written by the Athenian playwright Aeschylus and performed in 458 BC. Together they are called the Oresteia. They are the only complete set of three Greek tragedies that survives. The plays end not with more killing, but with a trial. The goddess Athena sets up a court of law in Athens to judge Orestes. Even so, the plays are dark and powerful. Yet they end with the hope that justice can replace revenge.

[long-pause]

{img:69-roman-city-ilium-temple-columns-hill}

The Romans believed they were descended from the Trojans, and they rebuilt Troy as a proud Roman city. Long after the Bronze Age, people lived on the hill of Troy again. Greeks built a city there and called it Ilion. Later, under the Romans, it was called Ilium. The Romans had a special love for the place. Because of the legend of Aeneas, they saw Troy as their mother city. Julius Caesar visited the site and gave it privileges. The emperor Augustus paid for new buildings. Ilium received a theatre, temples, baths and a council house. Roman visitors came to see the places named in Homer. Local guides showed them the tombs of heroes. Even so, the Roman city was quite small. It was never as important as the legend. Yet according to some later writers, the emperor Constantine at first thought of building his new capital near Troy. He chose Byzantium instead, which became Constantinople. Troy remained a quiet place of memory, its old walls hidden under the newer town.

[long-pause]

{img:70-odysseus-ship-leaving-troy-coast-sunrise}

After the war, Odysseus took ten years to get home to Ithaca. When Troy fell, the Greek heroes set off for home. Many had difficult journeys. Some never arrived. The journey of Odysseus was the longest of all. His story is told in the Odyssey, Homer's second great poem. It has about twelve thousand lines. Odysseus spent ten years trying to get home. Together with ten years at Troy, he was away from Ithaca for twenty years. Along the way, he lost all his ships and all his men. He met monsters, witches, goddesses and ghosts. He visited strange lands at the edges of the world. Even so, the Odyssey is not only an adventure story. It is about home, about patience and about recognising those we love. Yet its adventures have become some of the most famous tales ever told. Today, the word odyssey means any long and eventful journey. The poem has been retold many times, in books, plays and films, right up to the present day.

[long-pause]

{img:71-cyclops-cave-sheep-giant-shadow-entrance}

Odysseus escaped from the Cyclops by telling him that his name was Nobody. One of the most famous adventures happens on an island of giants. Odysseus and some of his men enter a large cave. It is full of cheeses and lambs. It belongs to a Cyclops, a one-eyed giant named Polyphemus. When the giant returns with his sheep, he blocks the entrance with a huge stone. The men are trapped. Odysseus thinks of a plan. He gives the giant strong wine. The Cyclops asks his name. Odysseus says that his name is Nobody. When the giant falls asleep, Odysseus and his men blind his single eye with a sharpened stake. The Cyclops cries out for help. Other giants come and ask who is hurting him. He answers that Nobody is hurting him. So they go away. In the morning, the men escape by clinging to the bellies of the sheep. Even so, Odysseus makes a mistake. As he sails away, he shouts his real name. The giant's father, the sea god Poseidon, never forgives him.

[long-pause]

{img:72-circe-island-palace-forest-lions-wolves}

On the island of Circe, Odysseus's men were turned into pigs. After many adventures, Odysseus reached an island ruled by a goddess named Circe. She lived in a stone house in the middle of a forest. Around it lay wolves and lions. They were strangely tame. They wagged their tails like dogs. Odysseus sent some of his men to explore. Circe welcomed them and gave them food and wine. But the food contained a magic drug. When she touched them with her wand, they turned into pigs. Only one man escaped to warn Odysseus. Odysseus went to rescue them. On the way, the god Hermes gave him a special herb called moly. It protected him from Circe's magic. When her drug did not work on him, Circe was amazed. She agreed to turn his men back into humans. Even so, the story does not end in hatred. Odysseus and his men stayed with Circe for a whole year. They feasted and rested before continuing their journey.

[long-pause]

{img:73-ship-passing-rocky-island-sirens-mast}

To hear the song of the Sirens safely, Odysseus had himself tied to the mast. The Sirens were mysterious creatures who lived on an island in the sea. They sang a song so beautiful that no sailor could resist it. Sailors who heard it steered towards them and never came home. Circe warned Odysseus about them. She told him what to do. As his ship came near the island, Odysseus gave his men soft wax. They put it in their ears so that they could not hear. But Odysseus wanted to hear the song. So he told his men to tie him tightly to the mast. He ordered them not to untie him, whatever he said. As they rowed past, the Sirens began to sing. They promised Odysseus wisdom and knowledge of all things. He begged his men to set him free. Instead, they tied him tighter. The ship passed safely. Even so, the Sirens remain a powerful image. Today, a siren song means a tempting call that leads to danger.

[long-pause]

{img:74-calypso-island-cave-vines-beach-quiet}

The nymph Calypso kept Odysseus on her island for seven years and offered to make him immortal. After his last ship was destroyed, Odysseus drifted alone across the sea. He reached an island called Ogygia. It was the home of a nymph named Calypso. Her island was beautiful. Homer describes her cave, covered with grape vines, with springs of clear water and soft meadows of violets and parsley around it. Calypso loved Odysseus. She kept him there for seven years. She offered to make him immortal and ageless if he would stay with her forever. Yet Odysseus refused. Every day, he sat on the shore and looked out to sea, weeping. He longed to see the smoke rising from his home on Ithaca. Finally, the gods ordered Calypso to let him go. She helped him build a raft. Even so, she asked him why he preferred his mortal wife to a goddess. Odysseus answered kindly. He said that Penelope could not compare with her beauty, yet he still longed for home.

[long-pause]

{img:75-penelope-weaving-loom-night-lamp-palace}

Penelope kept her suitors waiting by weaving a cloth by day and unpicking it by night. While Odysseus was away, his wife Penelope waited for him on Ithaca. Many years passed. Most people believed Odysseus was dead. More than a hundred young men from the islands came to her palace. Each wanted to marry her and become king. They stayed in the palace, eating and drinking at her expense. Penelope did not want to marry any of them. So she made a clever plan. She said she would choose a husband only after she had woven a burial cloth for Odysseus's old father. Every day she wove at her loom. Every night, by lamplight, she secretly unpicked what she had woven. For three years, the cloth was never finished. Then one of her maids revealed the trick. Even so, Penelope still found ways to delay. In the poem, she is as clever as her husband. Yet her patience and loyalty are what most readers remember about her.

[long-pause]

{img:76-old-dog-argos-lying-by-palace-door}

When Odysseus came home in disguise, his old dog Argos recognised him and wagged his tail. After twenty years, Odysseus finally reached Ithaca. The goddess Athena disguised him as an old beggar, so that no one would recognise him. He walked towards his palace. Outside the door, on a pile of dung, lay an old dog. His name was Argos. Odysseus had raised him as a puppy before leaving for Troy. Argos had once been a fine hunting dog. Now he was old and neglected, covered in fleas. Yet when Argos heard his master's voice, he lifted his head. He wagged his tail and dropped his ears. He had no strength to come closer. Odysseus saw him. He secretly wiped away a tear, so that no one would notice. Then the old dog died, having seen his master again after twenty years. Even so, the scene is very short. It is only a few lines long. Yet it is one of the most loved passages in the whole poem. Many readers remember Argos long after they forget the monsters.

[long-pause]

{img:77-penelope-odysseus-reunion-olive-tree-bed}

Penelope tested Odysseus with a secret about their bed, which was built around a living olive tree. When Odysseus finally revealed himself, Penelope did not believe him at once. Twenty years had passed. Perhaps a god was tricking her. So she set a test. She told her maid to move the great bed out of their bedroom for the stranger. Odysseus was suddenly angry. He said no one could move that bed. He had built it himself. One of its posts was the trunk of a living olive tree, still rooted in the ground. He had built the bedroom around it. Only he and Penelope knew this secret. When she heard him describe the bed, Penelope knew it was really him. She ran to him and threw her arms around him. They both wept. The goddess Athena held back the dawn so their night together would be longer. Even so, they had many stories to tell each other. Yet the poem lets them simply rest. The long journey of Odysseus ends where it began, at home.

[long-pause]

{img:78-heinrich-schliemann-trench-workers-hisarlik-1870s}

In 1870, a German businessman named Heinrich Schliemann began digging at Hisarlik. For a long time, many scholars thought Troy was only a legend. Heinrich Schliemann believed it was real. He was a German businessman who had made a fortune in trade. He loved Homer's poems and was determined to find Troy. In 1870, he began digging at the hill of Hisarlik. He had been encouraged by an Englishman named Frank Calvert. Calvert owned part of the hill and had already done some digging there himself. Schliemann worked on a huge scale. He hired many workers and cut a great trench through the middle of the hill. He was looking for the city of Priam. He believed it would be near the bottom. Even so, his methods were rough. He destroyed many upper layers to reach the lower ones. Some of the Troy he was looking for may have been lost in the process. Yet he proved that a great ancient city had stood on the hill. His work made Troy famous all over the world.

[long-pause]

{img:79-gold-jewellery-diadem-ancient-treasure-table}

Schliemann found a hoard of gold that he called the Treasure of Priam, though it was a thousand years too old. In 1873, near the end of his excavation, Schliemann made a spectacular find. Near the walls of the citadel, he found a hoard of treasure. There were gold and silver cups, bronze weapons and thousands of small gold beads. There were two gold headdresses made of many fine chains. Schliemann called it the Treasure of Priam. He believed it belonged to the king of Troy. A famous photograph shows his Greek wife, Sophia, wearing the headdress. Schliemann smuggled the treasure out of the Ottoman Empire. It was later given to Berlin. Even so, modern research shows that the treasure is much older than Priam. It belongs to Troy Two, around twenty-four hundred BC. That is more than a thousand years before the Trojan War. In 1945, at the end of the Second World War, the treasure vanished from Berlin. For decades, its fate was unknown. In 1993, it was revealed to be in a museum in Moscow, where it remains today.

[long-pause]

{img:80-frank-calvert-farmhouse-garden-dardanelles}

An Englishman named Frank Calvert pointed to Hisarlik as Troy before Schliemann arrived. Frank Calvert is often forgotten in the story of Troy. He was an Englishman who lived near the Dardanelles. His family worked as consuls and owned land in the area. Calvert was a keen amateur archaeologist. He studied the old sites around the Trojan plain. He became convinced that Hisarlik was the site of Troy. He even bought part of the hill and dug some trial trenches there. But he did not have enough money for a large excavation. When Schliemann visited the area in 1868, Calvert showed him Hisarlik. He shared his ideas and encouraged him to dig. Schliemann followed his advice. Even so, in his books, Schliemann gave Calvert little credit. He presented the discovery as his own. Calvert felt hurt by this. Yet historians today recognise his important role. Without Frank Calvert, the search for Troy might have taken much longer.

[long-pause]

{img:81-modern-archaeologists-careful-excavation-troy}

Later archaeologists spent more than a century studying the layers that Schliemann had cut through. After Schliemann, others continued to dig at Troy. His assistant, Wilhelm Dörpfeld, studied the layers more carefully. He realised that Troy Six was the city of the late Bronze Age. In the 1930s, an American team led by Carl Blegen made very careful excavations. Blegen suggested that Troy Seven A, which ended in fire, was the city of the Trojan War. From 1988, the German archaeologist Manfred Korfmann led a large international team. They used modern methods, such as measuring the magnetism of the ground to find buried walls. This was how they discovered the lower town. Since 2014, Turkish archaeologists have continued the work. Each new season adds small pieces to the picture. Even so, the big question remains open. No one can prove that a real Trojan War took place. Most scholars think there may have been real conflicts at Troy. Yet the poems are not history. They are memories, shaped and reshaped by singers over centuries.

[long-pause]

{img:82-bronze-age-collapse-abandoned-palace-ruins-dusk}

Around the time Troy fell, many great cities of the Bronze Age were destroyed or abandoned. The end of Troy Seven A came around eleven hundred and eighty BC. It was not an isolated event. In the same decades, much of the eastern Mediterranean was in crisis. The great palaces of Mycenaean Greece were destroyed or abandoned. The Hittite Empire collapsed, and its capital was burned. Cities in Syria and Cyprus were destroyed. Egypt survived, but only after fighting off invaders. Egyptian inscriptions describe groups of people moving by land and sea, whom historians call the Sea Peoples. Scholars still debate the causes of this collapse. Drought, earthquakes, famine, rebellion and broken trade routes may all have played a part. Even so, the result was clear. Writing disappeared from Greece for several centuries. Trade shrank. Life became simpler and poorer. Yet out of this dark age came the songs that became Homer's poems. They remembered a lost world of rich palaces and great kings.

[long-pause]

{img:83-troy-ruins-today-wooden-horse-replica-visitors}

Today, Troy is a World Heritage Site, and a large wooden horse greets visitors at the entrance. Today, the ruins of Troy are open to visitors. They lie in the Turkish province of Çanakkale, near the Dardanelles. In 1998, Troy was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Visitors walk along paths through the different layers of the city. They see the sloping walls of Troy Six, the old ramp of Troy Two and the remains of temples from Greek and Roman times. Near the entrance stands a large wooden horse, built for tourists. Children love to climb inside it. In the nearby city of Çanakkale, there is another wooden horse. It was used in the 2004 film Troy and was later given to the city. A new museum opened near the site in 2018. It displays many objects found during the excavations. Even so, the site itself is quiet and modest. Yet standing on the walls, looking out over the plain towards the sea, it is easy to imagine the ships on the shore and the songs that began there.

[long-pause]

So the old songs of Troy grow quiet once more.

[pause]

The plain lies still under the stars. The sea moves softly along the empty shore, where the ships once rested on the sand. On the hill, the sloping walls hold the warmth of the day, and the wind passes gently through the grass between the stones. Far to the west, on a rocky island, a lamp burns in a palace window, and an old dog sleeps by the door.

[pause]

The heroes and their names can all slip away now, like ships fading over the horizon.

Tomorrow, or whenever the dark comes round again, another gentle story of old heroes will sit ready on the screen. A subscription lets those old tales keep arriving.
