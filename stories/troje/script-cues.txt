Tonight we are going more than three thousand years back, to a walled city on a low hill, near the sea, at the edge of Asia, called Troy.

[long-pause]

It is a city of tall stone walls and towers, of horses and weavers and traders, looking out over a wide green plain, toward the sea, where the ships pass by on their way to the Black Sea.

[pause]

And it is a city whose last days will be sung about, and written about, and told, for three thousand years, in poems and plays and paintings and films, more than the story of almost any other city on earth.

[pause]

If you like slow evenings in faraway times, you can subscribe to the channel, and there will be more of them for you.

[long-pause]

Troy stands in the northwest corner of what is now Turkey, a few miles from the sea, near the entrance to a long, narrow strait of water, which the ancient Greeks called the Hellespont, and which we call the Dardanelles.

[pause]

The Hellespont is the gateway between two seas. To the south, there is the Aegean Sea, with its islands, and beyond it, Greece. To the north, the strait leads into the Sea of Marmara, and on to the Black Sea, with its rich lands of grain and gold and timber. Whoever holds the entrance to the Hellespont can watch every ship that passes.

[long-pause]

Let us imagine that we are standing on the walls of Troy, on a spring morning, more than three thousand years ago.

[pause]

The sun is rising behind us, over the low hills of the east. Before us, to the west and the north, the land slopes gently down to a wide, flat plain, green with young barley and wheat, crossed by two rivers, winding among willows and tamarisks and reeds, toward the sea.

[pause]

{img:green-plain-two-rivers-willows-reeds-morning-mist}
The rivers are called the Scamander and the Simois. In the spring, they are full and fast, with the melted snow from the mountains. In the summer, they shrink to little streams, among the stones.

[long-pause]

Beyond the plain, about three or four miles away, there is the sea, shining in the morning light. And out in the sea, there are islands: Tenedos, low and close, and beyond it, Imbros, and far away, on a clear day, the tall dark peak of Samothrace, rising out of the water.

[pause]

To the south, there is a range of high, blue mountains, with snow on their tops in winter and spring: Mount Ida, where, the old stories say, the gods sat and watched the battles on the plain below.

[pause]

The air is fresh and clear, and smells of the sea, and of the wild thyme growing on the hills. Larks sing high above the plain. Storks stand in the marshes by the rivers. And everywhere, there are horses, grazing in the meadows.

[long-pause]

The city itself is not very large, by our standards.

[pause]

At the top of the hill, there is the citadel, the high fortress, surrounded by great walls of stone, about five hundred metres around. Inside the citadel, there are the palaces of the king and the nobles, and the temples, and the storehouses.

[pause]

{img:citadel-great-sloping-stone-walls-towers-gate}
And around the foot of the hill, spreading out to the south, there is the lower town, where most of the people live, in small houses of stone and mud brick, along narrow lanes, protected by a wall and a ditch.

[pause]

Altogether, perhaps five thousand, or perhaps as many as ten thousand people live in Troy. It is one of the great cities of its time and its part of the world.

[long-pause]

When is this?

[pause]

We are in the Late Bronze Age, more than three thousand years ago, around the year 1200 before Christ. It is the age before iron, when tools and weapons are made of bronze, a mixture of copper and tin. It is the age of the great palaces of Mycenae in Greece, and of the Hittite empire in Anatolia, and of the pharaohs of Egypt, Ramesses the Great and those who came after him.

[pause]

{img:bronze-age-trade-ship-sail-oars-aegean-sea}
It is a world of kings and palaces, and of trade between them, by sea and by land: copper from Cyprus, and tin from far away in the east, and gold from Egypt, and ivory, and glass, and purple cloth, and olive oil, and wine. A world of scribes writing on clay tablets, and messengers carrying letters between the great kings, who call each other brother.

[long-pause]

And Troy, standing at the gateway of the Hellespont, is part of this world.

[pause]

It is rich, and strong, and proud, with its great walls, and its fine horses, and its weavers, and its traders. It has stood on this hill for a very long time already. People have been living here for more than two thousand years, building city after city on the ruins of the one before.

[pause]

But tonight, we are going to visit Troy in its last days. The days of the great war, which the poets will remember forever. The days of Priam, and Hector, and Andromache, and Helen, and of the wooden horse.

[long-pause]

Now, we must be careful.

[pause]

Because the story of Troy is two stories. One of them is the story told by the poets: the story of gods and heroes, of Helen and Paris and Achilles, of a war that lasted ten years, and a wooden horse. The other is the story told by the archaeologists, who have dug in the hill of Troy for a hundred and fifty years, and found the walls, and the houses, and the pots, and the bones of animals, and the traces of a fire.

[pause]

{img:poet-singing-lyre-firelit-hall-listeners}
Nobody knows for certain how much of the first story is true. Perhaps a little. Perhaps more than we think. Tonight, we will listen to both stories, side by side, as we walk slowly through the last days of the city.

[long-pause]

{img:blind-old-poet-homer-lyre-courtyard-olive-tree}
The story of Troy comes to us, first of all, from a poet called Homer.

[pause]

We know almost nothing about Homer. The Greeks believed that he was a blind old man, who wandered from town to town, singing his songs. Seven different cities claimed to be his birthplace. Some scholars today think that he lived in the eighth century before Christ, on the coast of what is now Turkey, or on one of the islands near it, perhaps four or five hundred years after the Trojan War. Some think that there never was a single Homer at all.

[long-pause]

Two great poems are said to be by Homer: the Iliad, and the Odyssey.

[pause]

The Iliad is the story of a few weeks in the tenth year of the war at Troy. Its name comes from Ilios, or Ilion, the Greek name for Troy. It tells of the anger of the great Greek hero Achilles, and of the battles on the plain, and of Hector, the prince of Troy, and of his family, and of the sorrow of war.

[pause]

The Odyssey is the story of what happened after the war: of the long journey home of the Greek hero Odysseus, who wandered for ten years over the sea, before he came back to his island, Ithaca, and to his wife, Penelope, and his son, Telemachus.

[long-pause]

These poems were not first written down. They were sung.

[pause]

For hundreds of years, before writing came to Greece, singers called bards travelled from palace to palace, and town to town, and sang the old stories of the heroes, in the halls of the kings, in the evenings, after the feasts, to the music of the lyre.

[pause]

{img:bard-singing-palace-hall-hearth-warriors-listening}
They did not learn the songs by heart, word for word. They made them up anew each time, as they sang, using the old stories, and lines and phrases that they had learned from older singers. Swift-footed Achilles. Rosy-fingered dawn. The wine-dark sea. Horse-taming Trojans. Hector of the shining helmet. These phrases fit the rhythm of the verse, and help the singer as he sings.

[long-pause]

So the story of Troy was passed down, from singer to singer, for hundreds of years. And as it was passed down, it changed.

[pause]

The old memories of the Bronze Age, of the palaces and the chariots and the bronze armour, were mixed with things from later times. Gods and miracles were added. The heroes grew bigger, and stronger, and the war grew longer, and grander.

[pause]

And at last, perhaps in the eighth or seventh century before Christ, the poems were written down, in the new Greek alphabet. And they became the most famous poems in the Greek world, which every Greek child learned at school, and every Greek knew by heart.

[long-pause]

There were other poems too, about the war of Troy, which told the parts of the story that Homer does not tell: how the war began, and how it ended. These poems are now lost. We know them only from short summaries, and from what later writers tell us.

[pause]

The most famous of these later writers is the Roman poet Virgil, who lived in the time of Augustus, about seven hundred years after Homer. In his great poem, the Aeneid, he tells the story of the last night of Troy, and of the wooden horse, and of the Trojan prince Aeneas, who escaped from the city, and sailed away to Italy.

[pause]

{img:roman-poet-virgil-writing-garden-villa-quiet}
So our story of the last days of Troy comes from many voices, over many hundreds of years: Homer, and the lost poems, and the Greek playwrights of Athens, and Virgil, and others.

[long-pause]

Is any of it true?

[pause]

For a long time, many scholars believed that it was all a legend, and that Troy had never existed at all, or that nobody could know where it had been.

[pause]

{img:hisarlik-mound-grassy-hill-plain-nineteenth-century}
But in the nineteenth century, some travellers and scholars began to look for Troy. And they thought that it might have been on a low hill near the Dardanelles, called Hisarlik, which means the place of the fortress, in Turkish. A man named Frank Calvert, who owned part of the hill, dug some trenches there, and found old walls.

[pause]

And then, in 1870, a rich German businessman named Heinrich Schliemann came, and began to dig. We shall hear more about him later.

[long-pause]

Today, almost all archaeologists agree that the hill of Hisarlik is Troy.

[pause]

They have found the remains of at least nine cities there, one on top of the other, from about 3000 before Christ until Roman times, over more than three thousand years. And one of them, or perhaps two of them, around the year 1200 before Christ, the time when the Greeks believed the Trojan War took place, was a strong and rich city, with great walls, and a large lower town.

[pause]

And there is more. In the archives of the Hittites, the great empire of Anatolia, there are clay tablets that mention a city called Wilusa, in the far west of Anatolia, near the sea. Many scholars think that Wilusa is the same name as Ilios, Troy. One of the tablets is a treaty, made with a king of Wilusa called Alaksandu, a name very like Alexandros, which is the other name of Paris, the prince of Troy in Homer's story.

[long-pause]

And the tablets also mention a people called the Ahhiyawa, living over the sea, to the west, who sometimes cause trouble on the coast of Anatolia. Many scholars think that the Ahhiyawa are the Achaeans, which is what Homer calls the Greeks.

[pause]

One Hittite letter even mentions that there was a quarrel, or a war, between the Hittites and the Ahhiyawa, about Wilusa.

[pause]

{img:hittite-clay-tablet-cuneiform-scribe-desk-lamp}
So perhaps there really was a war at Troy. Perhaps there were several. We do not know. But there is enough to make us think that behind the old songs of the poets, there are memories of a real city, and real people, and real events, a very long time ago.

[long-pause]

{img:troy-citadel-walls-sloping-limestone-towers-sunlight}
Let us walk up to the citadel of Troy.

[pause]

The walls of the citadel are magnificent. They are built of great blocks of limestone, carefully cut and fitted together, sloping gently outward at the bottom, and rising straight up at the top, perhaps ten metres high, with a wall of mud brick on top. At intervals along the walls, there are great square towers, from which the guards can look out over the plain.

[long-pause]

The walls are built in a special way. Every few metres, there is a little step, or offset, in the face of the wall, so that the wall is not one long, flat surface, but a series of slightly angled sections.

[pause]

Nobody knows exactly why. Perhaps it makes the wall stronger, against earthquakes, which are common in this part of the world. Perhaps it is just for beauty. In the evening, when the sun is low, the light catches the edges, and the walls seem to shimmer.

[pause]

In the Iliad, Homer calls Troy the city of fine walls, and the city of lofty gates, and well-built Troy. He is right.

[long-pause]

There are several gates into the citadel. The main gate faces south, toward the lower town, and has a great tower beside it.

[pause]

Beside the gate, there are tall stones, standing upright in a row, like pillars, which seem to be sacred, perhaps standing for the gods who protect the city. In front of them, people leave offerings.

[pause]

{img:south-gate-tower-standing-stones-offerings-morning}
In the stories, the most famous gate is the Scaean gate, the western gate, facing the plain and the sea, where the old men of Troy sit on the wall, and watch the battles, and where Hector says goodbye to his wife.

[long-pause]

Inside the citadel, the ground rises in terraces, toward the top of the hill.

[pause]

On the terraces, there are large houses, built of stone, with walls a metre thick, and flat roofs of timber and earth. Some of them have two storeys. These are the houses of the noble families of Troy, the king's relatives, and his officers, and his chief men.

[pause]

At the very top of the hill, there would have been the palace of the king, with its great hall, and its storerooms, and its courtyards. But the top of the hill was flattened, many centuries later, by the Greeks and the Romans, to build their temples, and the palace is gone. We can only imagine it.

[long-pause]

In Homer's poem, the palace of King Priam is a wonderful place, built of polished stone, with fifty bedrooms for his fifty sons and their wives, and twelve more, across the courtyard, for his daughters and their husbands.

[pause]

There is a great hall, with a hearth in the middle, where the king feasts with his family and his guests, and storerooms full of bronze and gold and fine cloth and fragrant cedar wood.

[pause]

{img:palace-great-hall-hearth-columns-painted-walls}
And on the citadel, too, there is the temple of Athena, the goddess of wisdom, where the women of Troy bring offerings of fine robes, and pray to her to protect the city. And there is a temple of Apollo, the god of the sun and of music, who loves Troy.

[long-pause]

Below the citadel, around the foot of the hill, is the lower town.

[pause]

For a long time, archaeologists did not know that it was there. They thought that Troy was only the little citadel on the top of the hill. But in the 1990s, a German archaeologist named Manfred Korfmann and his team used special instruments to look under the ground of the fields around the hill, and found the traces of a great ditch, cut into the rock, running all around the lower town, far out from the citadel.

[pause]

{img:lower-town-ditch-wooden-palisade-houses-beyond}
So we know that the lower town was large, perhaps twenty or thirty hectares, and that it was protected by the ditch, and perhaps by a wall or a palisade of wood behind it, with gates where the roads came in.

[long-pause]

In the lower town, the houses are small, and crowded together, along narrow, winding lanes.

[pause]

They are built of stone foundations, with walls of mud brick above, plastered and whitewashed, and flat roofs, where the people sleep in the summer, and dry their figs and their grapes. Most houses have one or two rooms, and a little courtyard, with an oven, and a place for the animals.

[pause]

Inside, it is dark and cool, with little windows, and floors of beaten earth. There is a hearth, for cooking and for warmth in winter, and big jars of pottery, set into the floor, for storing grain and oil and wine. In the later years of the city, as we shall see, there are more and more of these big storage jars, as if the people were preparing for something.

[long-pause]

There is water inside the walls too.

[pause]

On the side of the hill, there is a cave, cut deep into the rock, with tunnels leading back into the hill, more than a hundred metres, to springs of fresh, cold water. The people of Troy have been using it for many hundreds of years. The water never runs dry, even in summer, and even if the city is besieged.

[pause]

{img:spring-cave-rock-tunnel-water-jars-women-cool}
The Hittite treaty with Alaksandu of Wilusa, long ago, mentions a god of Wilusa, called KASKAL.KUR, which means a road under the earth, or an underground watercourse. Perhaps that is this very spring. Perhaps the people of Troy worshipped the spring as a god, because it gave them life.

[long-pause]

{img:troy-dawn-women-grinding-grain-saddle-quern-courtyard}
Let us spend an ordinary day in Troy, in the years before the war.

[pause]

The day begins at dawn, the rosy-fingered dawn, as Homer calls it. The cocks crow in the courtyards. The dogs bark. The women and the girls are the first to get up, and the first sound of the day, in every house, is the sound of grinding.

[long-pause]

Every day, the grain must be ground into flour, by hand, for the day's bread.

[pause]

The women kneel in front of a saddle quern, a large flat stone, slightly hollowed in the middle, and push a smaller stone backward and forward over it, crushing the grains of barley and wheat into a coarse flour. It is hard, slow work, and takes several hours every day. The women sing as they grind, to pass the time.

[pause]

Then the bread is baked, in a dome-shaped oven of clay in the courtyard, or on a hot stone on the hearth: flat round loaves, of barley or wheat. With the bread, the people eat porridge, and lentils and beans and chickpeas, and onions and garlic, and cheese from their sheep and goats, and olives, and figs, and grapes, and honey.

[long-pause]

Meat is eaten mostly at feasts and festivals, when animals are sacrificed to the gods: sheep and goats and pigs and cattle.

[pause]

But the people of Troy also eat a great deal of fish and shellfish, from the sea and the rivers and the lagoons near the coast. The archaeologists have found thousands of shells of mussels and oysters and cockles in the houses of Troy.

[pause]

{img:fishermen-small-boats-lagoon-nets-reeds-morning}
In the houses of the lower town, they have also found the bones of deer, and wild boar, and hares, hunted in the hills and the woods around the city.

[long-pause]

The men go out to work in the fields, on the plain, with their oxen.

[pause]

They plough the land with wooden ploughs, and sow barley and wheat, and peas and lentils, and harvest them with sickles of bronze, or of flint, set into wooden handles. They tend the vineyards and the olive trees on the hillsides, and the orchards of figs and pomegranates.

[pause]

The boys and the young men take the sheep and the goats out to graze, on the hills and the meadows, and bring them back in the evening. Troy is famous for its wool, and for its fine woven cloth.

[long-pause]

And Troy is famous, above all, for its horses.

[pause]

In the Iliad, the Trojans are always called the horse-taming Trojans. The plain around Troy is full of horses, grazing in the meadows by the rivers. The archaeologists have found the bones of horses at Troy, in the layers of the city from about the time of the war, for the first time in large numbers.

[pause]

{img:horses-grazing-meadow-river-young-men-training}
The young nobles of Troy train the horses, and drive them in chariots: light, fast, two-wheeled carts, made of wood and leather, pulled by two horses. In the old stories, the horses of Troy are descended from divine horses, given by Zeus himself to a king of Troy long ago, as a gift. And kings from all around come to Troy to buy them.

[long-pause]

Meanwhile, at home, the women weave.

[pause]

Every house has a loom, an upright wooden frame, leaning against the wall, with the threads of the warp hanging down from the top, kept straight by little weights of clay tied to the bottom. The archaeologists have found hundreds of these loom weights at Troy, and spindle whorls, the little round weights used in spinning wool into thread.

[pause]

{img:upright-loom-clay-weights-woman-weaving-colourful-cloth}
The women spin the wool, and dye it with plants and minerals and with purple from sea snails, and weave it into cloth, for clothes, and blankets, and hangings, and sails. In the Iliad, even Helen, and Andromache, and Queen Hecuba, the greatest ladies of Troy, spend their days at the loom, weaving beautiful cloth, with patterns and pictures woven into it.

[long-pause]

In the middle of the day, the market is busy, near the gate of the citadel.

[pause]

Traders come to Troy from all over: from the islands of the Aegean, and from Greece, and from Cyprus, and from the Hittite lands in the east, and from the shores of the Black Sea. They bring pottery, and copper and tin, and wine and oil, and fine weapons, and jewellery.

[pause]

The archaeologists have found pottery at Troy from Mycenae, in Greece, and from Cyprus, and from many other places. And they have found a little bronze seal, with writing on it, in the Luwian language, the language spoken in western Anatolia, which was perhaps also the language of the Trojans.

[long-pause]

In the late afternoon, the potters and the smiths and the other craftsmen finish their work.

[pause]

The Trojan potters make a fine grey pottery, smooth and shiny, which the archaeologists call grey Minyan ware, and a tan-coloured pottery too. The smiths melt copper and tin together, in little clay crucibles, over charcoal fires, blown hot with bellows, and pour the bronze into moulds of stone, to make knives, and pins, and arrowheads, and tools.

[pause]

{img:bronze-smith-crucible-charcoal-fire-bellows-workshop}
As the sun sets over the sea, behind the island of Tenedos, the men come home from the fields, and the shepherds bring the flocks in through the gates. The gates are closed. The lamps are lit, little dishes of clay, filled with olive oil, with a wick of flax. The families eat their supper together, around the hearth.

[long-pause]

And then, in the darkness, the city sleeps, behind its walls, under the stars.

[pause]

On the walls, the guards keep watch, wrapped in their cloaks, looking out over the dark plain toward the sea. And the only sounds are the wind, and the frogs in the marshes, and, far away, the waves breaking on the shore.

[long-pause]

{img:king-priam-elderly-white-beard-throne-palace-hall}
In the stories, the king of Troy, in its last days, is Priam.

[pause]

He is an old man, with a long white beard, gentle, and dignified, and wise. He has ruled Troy for a long time, and has made it rich and strong. In his youth, he was a great warrior. Now he is too old to fight, and sits with the other old men of Troy, on the wall, by the Scaean gate, and watches.

[long-pause]

The old stories say that, when Priam was a little boy, Troy was attacked and taken by the great hero Heracles, because Priam's father, the king Laomedon, had cheated him.

[pause]

Heracles spared only the little boy, whose name was then Podarces. His sister Hesione bought his freedom, with her golden veil. And so he was given a new name, Priam, which the Greeks said meant the one who was bought. And he grew up to rebuild Troy, stronger and richer than before.

[pause]

Priam's wife, the queen, is Hecuba. In the stories, they have nineteen children together. And Priam has many more children, by other wives. Fifty sons, Homer says, and many daughters.

[long-pause]

The eldest son of Priam and Hecuba, and the greatest, is Hector.

[pause]

Hector is the hero of Troy. He is the bravest and strongest of all the Trojans, and the leader of the Trojan army. He is called Hector of the shining helmet, because of his great bronze helmet, with a tall crest of horsehair, nodding on the top.

[pause]

{img:hector-tall-young-prince-bronze-helmet-crest-calm}
But Hector is not just a warrior. He is also kind, and wise, and loyal. He loves his city, and his family, and his people. He fights not for glory, but to protect them. He is gentle with his mother and his father, and with Helen, even though she has brought so much trouble on Troy. Of all the heroes in the Iliad, many readers love Hector best.

[long-pause]

Hector's wife is Andromache.

[pause]

She is the daughter of a king, from a city to the south, called Thebe, under the wooded mountain of Placos. She loves Hector with all her heart. In the Iliad, she tells him that he is everything to her: her father, and her mother, and her brother, as well as her husband.

[pause]

{img:andromache-young-woman-weaving-palace-room-window}
And they have a little son, a baby boy, whom Hector calls Scamandrius, after the river, but whom the people of Troy call Astyanax, which means lord of the city, because his father is the defender of the city.

[long-pause]

Then there is Paris, another son of Priam and Hecuba.

[pause]

Paris, who is also called Alexandros, is very handsome, and charming, and rather vain. He is a fine archer, but he does not much like fighting. He prefers music, and beautiful things, and his beautiful wife.

[pause]

{img:paris-handsome-young-prince-lyre-garden-afternoon}
There is a strange story about his birth. Before he was born, Hecuba dreamed that she gave birth to a burning torch, which set the whole city on fire. The soothsayers told Priam that the child would bring the destruction of Troy. So, when he was born, the baby was taken away, and left on Mount Ida. But a she-bear looked after him, and then a shepherd found him, and brought him up as his own son. And Paris grew up as a shepherd, on the mountain, not knowing that he was a prince.

[long-pause]

When he was a young man, Paris came down to Troy, to take part in the games, and won all the contests, and was recognized by his sister. And Priam, happy to have his son back, forgot the old prophecy, and welcomed him home.

[pause]

Another of Priam's daughters is Cassandra.

[pause]

Cassandra is beautiful, and a priestess of the god Apollo. The stories say that Apollo loved her, and gave her the gift of prophecy, the power to see the future. But when she would not return his love, he could not take back the gift. So instead he added a curse: that nobody would ever believe her.

[pause]

So all her life, Cassandra sees what is going to happen to Troy, and warns her family, again and again. And nobody listens.

[long-pause]

There are many more members of the great family.

[pause]

There is Deiphobus, another brave son. There is Helenus, who also has the gift of prophecy, like his sister. There is Troilus, the youngest, a boy. There is Polyxena, a gentle daughter. And there is Aeneas, a cousin of the royal family, the son of the goddess Aphrodite and a Trojan prince named Anchises, who will play an important part at the very end.

[pause]

{img:royal-family-feast-palace-hall-hearth-evening}
And there is one more member of the family of Priam, who was not born a Trojan, but who has come to Troy from far away, over the sea, and whose coming has changed everything.

[pause]

Her name is Helen.

[long-pause]

{img:helen-of-sparta-graceful-woman-palace-courtyard-greece}
Helen comes from Sparta, in the south of Greece, a kingdom in a green valley, between high mountains, beside a river called the Eurotas.

[pause]

In the stories, she is the most beautiful woman in the world. She is the daughter of Leda, the queen of Sparta, and, the stories say, of Zeus himself, the king of the gods. When she was a girl, all the princes of Greece wanted to marry her.

[long-pause]

There were so many suitors, and they were so powerful, that her stepfather, King Tyndareus, was afraid that whichever one he chose, the others would be angry, and there would be war.

[pause]

So the clever Odysseus, one of the suitors, suggested a plan. Before Helen chose her husband, all the suitors should swear an oath, that they would accept her choice, and that, if anyone ever took her away from her husband, they would all come together to bring her back.

[pause]

All the suitors swore the oath. And Helen chose Menelaus, a prince of Mycenae, the younger brother of the great king Agamemnon. Menelaus became king of Sparta, and they had a daughter, called Hermione. For some years, they lived happily together.

[long-pause]

Meanwhile, the stories say, something happened among the gods.

[pause]

At a wedding feast of the gods, Eris, the goddess of quarrels, who had not been invited, threw a golden apple among the guests. On it were written the words: for the fairest.

[pause]

{img:golden-apple-marble-table-wedding-feast-gods}
Three goddesses claimed the apple: Hera, the queen of the gods, and Athena, the goddess of wisdom, and Aphrodite, the goddess of love. They asked Zeus to decide between them. But Zeus wisely refused. He said that a mortal should decide. And he chose the young shepherd, on Mount Ida, near Troy, who was the most handsome of all men: Paris.

[long-pause]

So the three goddesses came to Paris, on the mountain, where he was looking after his sheep.

[pause]

Each of them offered him a gift, if he would choose her. Hera offered him power, to be king of all Asia. Athena offered him wisdom, and victory in war. And Aphrodite offered him the love of the most beautiful woman in the world.

[pause]

Paris gave the apple to Aphrodite.

[long-pause]

Some time later, Paris sails to Greece, to Sparta, and is welcomed as a guest by King Menelaus, in his palace.

[pause]

Then Menelaus has to leave, to go to the island of Crete, for the funeral of his grandfather. And while he is away, Paris and Helen sail away together, to Troy, taking with them many treasures from the palace.

[pause]

{img:ship-sailing-night-sea-stars-two-figures-stern}
Did Helen go willingly, because she had fallen in love with Paris, as some of the stories say? Or was she taken by force? Or was it the work of the goddess Aphrodite, who made her fall in love, against her will? The ancient writers argued about it for centuries. And one of them, a poet called Stesichorus, even told a story that Helen never went to Troy at all, but only a phantom, a cloud in her shape, while the real Helen waited in Egypt for the war to end.

[long-pause]

In Homer's Iliad, Helen is a sad and lonely figure, in Troy.

[pause]

She is sorry for what she has done. She calls herself shameless, and wishes she had died before she left her home. She misses her daughter, and her brothers, and her husband, and her home in Sparta. Many of the Trojans, especially the women, do not like her, because the war is her fault.

[pause]

{img:helen-weaving-tapestry-battle-scenes-palace-room}
But old Priam is always kind to her. He does not blame her. He says that it is the fault of the gods. And Hector, too, is always kind to her, and gentle. Helen spends her days weaving, in her room in the palace of Paris. And in one scene, she is weaving a great purple cloth, with pictures of the battles between the Trojans and the Greeks, which are being fought because of her.

[long-pause]

In one of the most famous scenes of the Iliad, Helen comes to the walls, by the Scaean gate, where Priam and the old men of Troy are sitting, watching the Greek army on the plain.

[pause]

When the old men see her coming, they whisper to each other that it is no wonder that the Trojans and the Greeks should suffer so much, for so long, for such a woman. She is like an immortal goddess. But still, they say, let her go home in the ships, and not stay here, to bring sorrow to us and to our children.

[pause]

Priam calls Helen to sit beside him, and asks her to tell him the names of the Greek heroes he can see on the plain below. And Helen points them out, one by one: there is Agamemnon, the great king. There is Odysseus, the clever one, shorter than Agamemnon, but broader in the shoulders. There is Ajax, huge and tall, like a tower. And she looks for her own two brothers, Castor and Polydeuces, among the Greeks. But she cannot see them. She does not know that they have already passed away, at home, in Sparta.

[long-pause]

In the story, then, when Menelaus came home to Sparta, and found that Helen was gone, he went to his brother Agamemnon.

[pause]

And Agamemnon called on all the princes and kings of Greece, who had sworn the oath, to keep their promise. And they gathered their ships, and their armies, from all over Greece, at a port called Aulis, to sail to Troy, and bring Helen back.

[pause]

{img:many-ships-gathered-harbour-aulis-greek-coast-dawn}
Homer says there were more than a thousand ships. The face that launched a thousand ships, as an English poet, Christopher Marlowe, would write, more than two thousand years later.

[long-pause]

{img:greek-fleet-black-ships-sea-approaching-coast-troy}
And so, in the stories, the Greeks come to Troy.

[pause]

They come across the sea, in their black ships, long and narrow, with a single square sail, and rows of oars, and high, curved prows, painted with eyes. Each ship carries fifty or a hundred men. They come from every part of Greece: from Mycenae, and Sparta, and Pylos, and Athens, and Thessaly, and Crete, and the islands.

[long-pause]

Homer, in the Iliad, gives a long list of all the ships, and their leaders, and where they came from: twenty-nine contingents, more than eleven hundred ships. It is called the Catalogue of Ships.

[pause]

The interesting thing is that many of the places in this list are real places, in Bronze Age Greece, that were important in the time of the Mycenaean palaces, but had become small or forgotten by the time of Homer. So the list may be very old, a real memory of the Greek world around the year 1200 before Christ, passed down by the singers for hundreds of years.

[pause]

{img:mycenae-lion-gate-citadel-stone-walls-greece}
The leader of the Greeks is Agamemnon, king of Mycenae, the richest and most powerful of the Greek kings. He is proud, and stubborn, and quick-tempered.

[long-pause]

The other great heroes of the Greeks are Menelaus, Helen's husband, brave and honest, and Odysseus, king of the little rocky island of Ithaca, the cleverest of all, and a great speaker, who always knows what to do.

[pause]

There is the old king Nestor, of sandy Pylos, the oldest and wisest of the Greeks, who loves to tell long stories about the great deeds of his youth. There is great Ajax, the biggest and strongest of the Greeks, with his huge shield, like a tower. And there is Diomedes, young and brave.

[pause]

{img:greek-heroes-council-tent-shore-agamemnon-nestor}
And there is Achilles.

[long-pause]

Achilles is the greatest warrior of the Greeks, and of the whole world.

[pause]

He is the son of a king, Peleus, and a sea goddess, Thetis. He is young, and handsome, and strong, and very fast: swift-footed Achilles, Homer calls him. He is proud, and passionate, and quick to anger, but also capable of great kindness.

[pause]

His mother, Thetis, knows that he has a choice. He can stay at home, and live a long, quiet life, and be forgotten. Or he can go to Troy, and win great glory, and be remembered forever, but die young. Achilles chooses glory.

[pause]

He comes to Troy with his men, the Myrmidons, and with his dearest friend, Patroclus, who has grown up with him since they were boys.

[long-pause]

The Greeks land on the shore near Troy, and drag their ships up onto the beach, and build a great camp, along the shore, with huts and shelters, and a wall and a ditch to protect it.

[pause]

And the Trojans close the gates of their city, and the siege begins.

[pause]

{img:greek-camp-beach-ships-pulled-up-huts-smoke}
The Trojans are not alone. Their allies come from all over Anatolia, and from Thrace, across the strait, to help them: the Dardanians, and the Lycians, from the far south, and the Mysians, and the Phrygians, and the Thracians, and many more. Each speaks a different language. Homer says that the Trojan army is like a flock of sheep, all bleating in different voices.

[long-pause]

According to the stories, the war lasts for ten years.

[pause]

That is very hard to believe. No Bronze Age army could stay so far from home for so long. Perhaps the ten years are the poets' way of saying a long time. Or perhaps the memories of many different wars, and raids, over many years, have been joined together into one great story.

[pause]

{img:plain-of-troy-view-from-walls-distant-camp-ships}
But in the stories, the Greeks cannot take the city, because the walls are too strong. And the Trojans cannot drive the Greeks away, because they are too many. So, for years, the Greeks raid the towns and the islands around Troy, to get food and treasure and captives, and the two armies sometimes fight on the plain, between the city and the ships. And the people of Troy go on living, as best they can, inside their walls.

[long-pause]

{img:troy-inside-walls-crowded-lanes-people-calm-siege}
What is it like to live in a city under siege?

[pause]

In the stories, the life of Troy goes on, in its last years, even while the Greek army is camped on the shore. And the archaeologists have found some interesting things, in the layer of the city that many of them think may be the Troy of the war, which they call Troy Seven A.

[long-pause]

In this layer, the houses of the lower town have been crowded together, inside the walls of the citadel, and against the walls, filling every open space. Little houses have been built in the streets and the courtyards, as if many more people had come to live inside the citadel, for safety.

[pause]

And in almost every house, there are great storage jars, called pithoi, sunk into the floor, up to their necks, with stone lids. They are full of grain and oil and wine. As if the people were storing up food, for a long time of danger.

[pause]

Of course, this does not prove that there was a Trojan War, like the one in the stories. But it does tell us that, in its last years, the city was preparing for trouble.

[long-pause]

In the Iliad, we see glimpses of the ordinary life of the city, during the war.

[pause]

We see the women of Troy, going down to the springs outside the walls, to wash their clothes, in the great stone washing troughs, as they did in the days of peace, before the Greeks came. We see the old men, sitting on the walls, in the sun, talking, like grasshoppers chirping in a tree, Homer says.

[pause]

{img:stone-washing-troughs-spring-women-linen-sunlight}
We see the women going up to the temple of Athena, on the citadel, in a long procession, led by Queen Hecuba, carrying the most beautiful robe in the palace, to lay it on the knees of the goddess's statue, and pray for the safety of the city.

[long-pause]

We see the funerals, too, gently, from a distance.

[pause]

Homer tells how, during a truce, both armies go out onto the plain, to gather up their dead, and wash them, and carry them home, weeping, and lay them on great fires, and build mounds of earth over their ashes. On the plain of Troy, there are still many great mounds, which the ancient Greeks believed to be the tombs of the heroes of the war.

[pause]

But in the Iliad, most of the war takes place in a single place: the plain between the city and the sea. The city itself is never attacked. The walls hold. And behind them, life goes on.

[long-pause]

Inside the walls, the children play in the lanes and the courtyards.

[pause]

The archaeologists have found little toys at Troy: clay animals, and rattles, and little round pieces of pottery, perhaps used as counters in a game. The children play with knucklebones, the little bones from the ankles of sheep, which are thrown like dice, and with balls, and spinning tops, and dolls.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-knucklebones-courtyard-shade}
The women go on grinding the grain, and baking the bread, and spinning, and weaving. The potters go on making their pots. The smiths go on making bronze, now mostly for weapons and armour. The priests go on making sacrifices to the gods.

[long-pause]

There are feasts too, in the stories, in the palace, in the evenings.

[pause]

The king and his family, and his nobles, and his allies, gather in the great hall, around the fire. Animals are sacrificed, and roasted on spits. The wine is mixed with water in great bowls, and poured into golden cups. And after the meal, a singer sings, to the lyre, the old stories of the heroes, as Homer himself would do, hundreds of years later.

[pause]

{img:palace-feast-roasting-spits-wine-bowls-singer}
And the Trojans talk, in the evenings, about the war, and about whether they should send Helen back to the Greeks, to end it. Some of the wiser Trojans, like Antenor, an old counsellor, say that they should. But Paris will not give her up. And Priam will not force him.

[long-pause]

Every morning, the people of Troy look out from the walls, at the Greek camp on the shore, with its ships, and its huts, and the smoke of its fires rising into the sky.

[pause]

And every evening, they look again, and see the watch fires of the Greek camp, burning along the shore.

[pause]

Homer has a beautiful passage about the fires on the plain, at night, when the Trojans, for once, are camped on the plain, close to the Greek ships. As when, he says, in the sky, the stars shine brightly around the moon, on a still night, when there is no wind, and all the hills and valleys and the high peaks can be seen, and the endless sky opens up above, and all the stars are seen, and the shepherd is glad in his heart. So many were the fires, between the ships and the river, burning in front of Troy.

[long-pause]

But many people of Troy, ordinary people, are suffering too.

[pause]

The fields of the plain are trampled, and the harvests are lost. The flocks have been taken. The trade with the outside world has stopped. And in the Iliad, many of the Trojans, and many of the Greeks, are tired of the war, and long for it to end, and to go home, to their families, and their fields, and their ordinary lives.

[pause]

{img:empty-trampled-fields-plain-grey-sky-quiet}
And there is one man, more than any other, on whom the hope of the city rests. Hector, the son of Priam.

[long-pause]

{img:scaean-gate-tall-tower-plain-beyond-afternoon}
In the sixth book of the Iliad, there is one of the most beautiful scenes in all of literature.

[pause]

Hector has come back from the battle on the plain, into the city, to ask his mother to pray to Athena. And then he goes to find his wife, Andromache, to see her, perhaps for the last time.

[long-pause]

She is not at home. A servant tells him that she has gone to the great tower by the Scaean gate, with the nurse and the baby, because she heard that the Trojans were being driven back, and she was afraid for Hector. She went running to the walls, like a woman out of her mind.

[pause]

Hector hurries through the streets, back toward the gate. And as he comes to it, Andromache comes running to meet him. And behind her comes the nurse, carrying the baby, Astyanax, in her arms, like a beautiful star.

[pause]

Hector looks at his little son, and smiles, in silence.

[long-pause]

Andromache takes his hand, and weeps, and speaks to him.

[pause]

She begs him not to go back to the battle. She says that his courage will be the end of him. She tells him that she has no one else in the world. Her father and her seven brothers were all lost in the war, when Achilles attacked her city. Her mother has passed away. Hector, she says, you are my father, and my mother, and my brother, and my husband. Have pity on me. Stay here on the tower. Do not make your son an orphan, and your wife a widow.

[pause]

{img:hector-andromache-on-tower-holding-hands-tender}
And Hector answers her gently.

[long-pause]

He says that he has thought of all these things too. But he would be ashamed before the men and the women of Troy, if he hid from the battle, like a coward. He has learned always to be brave, and to fight in the front line, for the glory of his father, and his own.

[pause]

And then he says something very sad. He says that he knows, in his heart, that the day will come when Troy will fall, and Priam, and the people of Priam. But he says that none of this grieves him so much as the thought of Andromache herself, being taken away, weeping, by some Greek, to a far country, to weave at another woman's loom, and carry water from another people's spring.

[pause]

{img:hector-reaching-for-baby-nurse-tower-soft-light}
And he hopes that the earth will cover him, before he hears her cry.

[long-pause]

Then Hector reaches out his arms, to take his little son.

[pause]

But the baby is frightened by the great bronze helmet, with its tall horsehair crest, nodding fiercely on top, and he shrinks back, crying, into the arms of the nurse.

[pause]

{img:hector-taking-off-helmet-laughing-baby-wife-smiling}
And Hector and Andromache both laugh.

[pause]

And Hector takes off his helmet, and lays it on the ground, shining. And he takes his son, and kisses him, and rocks him gently in his arms.

[long-pause]

And he prays to Zeus, and to all the gods, that this boy may grow up to be like him, the first among the Trojans, strong and brave, and a great king of Troy. And that one day, people will say of him, when he comes home from battle: he is far better than his father. And that his mother's heart may be glad.

[pause]

Then he gives the baby back to Andromache, and she takes him to her breast, smiling through her tears.

[pause]

And Hector, seeing her, feels pity for her, and strokes her with his hand, and says to her: my dear, do not grieve too much for me. No man will send me down to Hades before my time. But no man, brave or cowardly, has ever escaped his fate, once he is born. Go home now, and see to your work, the loom and the spindle, and tell the servants to get on with theirs. War is the work of men.

[long-pause]

Then Hector picks up his helmet, and puts it on, and goes away.

[pause]

And Andromache goes home. But she keeps turning back, again and again, to look at him, weeping. And when she gets home, she and all her servants weep together for Hector, in his own house, while he is still alive. Because they do not believe that he will ever come back from the battle.

[pause]

{img:andromache-looking-back-walking-home-lane-tears}
It is one of the oldest love stories in the world. And it is still read today, nearly three thousand years after it was first sung, all over the world, by people who know nothing else about Troy.

[long-pause]

{img:greek-camp-tent-achilles-alone-sea-evening}
In the story of the Iliad, the war goes on, through many battles.

[pause]

Achilles, angry with Agamemnon, who has taken from him a captive woman he cared for, refuses to fight, and sits by his ships, with his friends. Without him, the Greeks are driven back, again and again, by Hector and the Trojans, all the way to their ships, on the beach.

[long-pause]

At last, Achilles's dearest friend, Patroclus, puts on Achilles's armour, and goes out to fight, to drive the Trojans back. And on the plain, near the walls of Troy, Patroclus falls, in battle with Hector.

[pause]

When Achilles hears the news, his grief is terrible. He lies in the dust, and weeps, and will not eat. His mother, the sea goddess Thetis, comes up out of the sea, with all her sea nymphs, to comfort him. And Achilles says that he will go back to the battle, and avenge his friend, even though he knows that, if he does, his own death will follow soon after.

[pause]

His mother asks the god of fire and the forge, Hephaestus, to make him new armour. And Hephaestus makes for him the most wonderful shield in the world.

[long-pause]

Homer describes the shield in great detail. And it is strange, and beautiful, that, in the middle of the war, the shield shows a picture of the whole world at peace.

[pause]

On it, there is the earth, and the sky, and the sea, and the sun, and the moon, and the stars. There are two cities. In one of them, there is a wedding, with music and dancing, and torches, and a crowd of people in the market place, listening to old men judging a quarrel. There are fields being ploughed, and a harvest of wheat being cut, while a king watches, happily, and the women prepare a meal for the workers.

[pause]

{img:bronze-shield-detailed-scenes-harvest-dancing-vineyard}
There is a vineyard, with grapes being picked by young men and girls, carrying the fruit in baskets, while a boy plays the lyre, and sings. There is a herd of cattle, going out to the pasture, by a river, with reeds. There is a dancing floor, where young men and women dance, holding each other by the wrists, while a crowd watches, delighted. And all around the edge, there is the great river Ocean, which flows around the world.

[long-pause]

Then Achilles goes out to fight.

[pause]

And the Trojans flee before him, back into the city, through the gates. All except Hector, who stays outside, by the Scaean gate, alone, to face Achilles. His father and his mother call to him from the walls, and beg him to come inside. But he will not.

[pause]

{img:priam-hecuba-on-walls-calling-down-pleading}
We will not watch the battle between Hector and Achilles. It is enough to say that Hector, the defender of Troy, falls, outside the walls of his city, and that Achilles, in his grief and anger, takes his body away, to the Greek camp.

[pause]

On the walls, Priam and Hecuba see it, and the whole city weeps.

[long-pause]

Andromache does not see it at first. She is at home, weaving a purple cloth, with flowers on it. She has told her servants to heat water, for Hector's bath, when he comes home from the battle.

[pause]

Then she hears the sound of weeping from the tower, and she knows. She runs to the walls, and sees. And she faints, and her headdress falls from her hair: the veil that Aphrodite had given her, on the day that Hector came to her father's house, to take her home as his bride.

[pause]

For twelve days, Achilles keeps Hector's body in his camp. He will not give it back to the Trojans for a funeral. But the gods protect it, and keep it whole and fresh.

[long-pause]

Then, on the twelfth night, old Priam does something extraordinary.

[pause]

He goes, alone, at night, with a single old servant driving a wagon, across the plain, to the Greek camp, to the hut of Achilles, the man who has taken so many of his sons. He brings with him a great treasure, as a ransom, to buy back the body of his son.

[pause]

{img:old-king-wagon-night-plain-stars-single-servant}
The god Hermes, the messenger of the gods, disguised as a young man, guides him, unseen, through the Greek camp, past the guards, to the door of Achilles's hut.

[long-pause]

Priam goes in. Achilles is sitting at his table, having just finished his supper, with two of his friends.

[pause]

And Priam goes straight to Achilles, and kneels down before him, and takes hold of his knees, and kisses his hands. The terrible hands, Homer says, that had taken so many of his sons.

[pause]

{img:priam-kneeling-before-achilles-hut-lamplight-quiet}
And he says: remember your own father, Achilles, who is as old as I am, far away, at home, waiting for you. Have pity on me. Think of your father. I am more to be pitied than he is. Because I have done what no man on earth has ever done before. I have kissed the hands of the man who took my son.

[long-pause]

And Achilles thinks of his own father, Peleus, far away in Greece, old and alone, whom he will never see again.

[pause]

And he takes the old man's hand, and gently pushes him away. And the two of them weep together: Priam for Hector, and Achilles for his father, and for Patroclus. And the sound of their weeping fills the hut.

[pause]

Then Achilles raises Priam up, and looks at him, at his white hair and his white beard, with pity. And he tells him to sit down, and eat with him. And they eat together, and look at each other, with wonder: Priam at Achilles, so tall and handsome, like a god, and Achilles at Priam, so noble in his face and his speech.

[long-pause]

Achilles gives Hector's body back, washed, and wrapped in fine cloth. And he promises to hold back the Greeks from fighting for twelve days, so that the Trojans can give Hector a proper funeral.

[pause]

And Priam goes home, in the night, across the plain, with his son.

[pause]

{img:wagon-returning-dawn-plain-city-gates-opening}
For nine days, the Trojans gather wood. And on the tenth day, they give Hector his funeral, in the city, with all his family mourning around him: Andromache, and Hecuba, and Helen, who says that Hector was the only one in all of Troy who was always kind to her.

[pause]

And that is how the Iliad ends: with the funeral of Hector, tamer of horses.

[long-pause]

{img:greek-camp-carpenters-building-large-wooden-horse}
The Iliad ends with the funeral of Hector. But the story of Troy goes on, in the other poems, and in the stories of later writers.

[pause]

Soon after Hector, Achilles himself falls, as was foretold, struck in the heel by an arrow, shot by Paris, and guided by the god Apollo. His heel, the stories say, was the only part of him that could be harmed, because when he was a baby, his mother dipped him in the magic river Styx, holding him by the heel. Today, we still call a person's one weakness their Achilles heel.

[long-pause]

And then Paris, too, is lost, wounded by an arrow.

[pause]

But still the walls of Troy stand. The Greeks cannot take the city by force. And so, in the tenth year of the war, they decide to take it by a trick.

[pause]

The trick is the idea of Odysseus, the clever one. Odysseus of many wiles, Homer calls him.

[long-pause]

The Greeks build a great horse, of wood.

[pause]

It is built by a carpenter named Epeius, with the help of the goddess Athena, from the timber of fir trees, cut on Mount Ida. It is huge, taller than the walls of Troy, and hollow inside. And in its belly, there is a secret door, and room for a group of the bravest Greek warriors to hide, with Odysseus himself as their leader.

[pause]

{img:giant-wooden-horse-standing-beach-dawn-empty-camp}
Then, one night, the Greeks hide the warriors inside the horse, and leave the horse standing on the plain, in front of their camp. They burn their huts, and launch their ships, and sail away, out of sight. But not home. Only to the island of Tenedos, just off the coast, where they hide their ships behind the island, and wait.

[long-pause]

In the morning, the Trojans look out from their walls, and cannot believe their eyes.

[pause]

The Greek camp is empty. The ships are gone. The beach is deserted, except for the smoking ruins of the huts. And there, on the plain, stands a great wooden horse.

[pause]

The people of Troy open the gates, for the first time in ten years, and pour out onto the plain, laughing and shouting, to see the place where the Greek camp had been, and the place where Achilles had his hut, and where the ships had been drawn up on the beach. They are free. The war is over.

[pause]

And they gather around the wooden horse, and wonder what it is.

[long-pause]

Some of them want to bring it into the city. Some want to throw it into the sea, or burn it, or cut it open, to see what is inside.

[pause]

The story, as Virgil tells it, says that a priest of Neptune, named Laocoön, comes running down from the citadel, and warns them. He says that it is a trick of the Greeks. I fear the Greeks, he says, even when they bring gifts. And he throws a spear at the side of the horse, and it sticks there, quivering, and the hollow belly of the horse echoes.

[pause]

{img:priest-warning-crowd-horse-plain-dramatic-sky-calm}
But then, the story goes on, two great serpents come out of the sea, from the direction of Tenedos, and come to Laocoön, and his two sons, and take them away. And the Trojans believe that the gods have punished him for harming the horse, which must be a sacred gift for the goddess Athena.

[long-pause]

And then some shepherds bring in a young Greek, whom they have found hiding in the reeds, with his hands tied.

[pause]

His name is Sinon. And he tells the Trojans a clever story. He says that he has escaped from the Greeks, who wanted to sacrifice him to the gods, to get a good wind for their voyage home. He says that the Greeks have given up, and gone home. And he says that the horse is an offering to the goddess Athena, to ask her forgiveness. They made it so big, he says, so that the Trojans could not bring it into their city. Because, if the horse is brought into Troy, then Troy will be protected by Athena forever.

[pause]

{img:shepherds-bringing-young-man-to-king-priam-plain}
And kind old Priam believes him, and orders his hands to be untied, and tells him that he is welcome among the Trojans.

[long-pause]

So the Trojans decide to bring the horse into the city.

[pause]

But it is too big to go through the gate. So they pull down part of the wall, beside the gate, to make room. And they put wheels under the horse's feet, and tie ropes around its neck, and pull it, singing, into the city, with the boys and the girls dancing around it, and touching the ropes for luck.

[pause]

Four times, the story says, the horse stops, at the very threshold of the gate, and four times, there is a sound of bronze, of armour, from inside it. But nobody notices.

[long-pause]

Only Cassandra knows the truth.

[pause]

She runs through the streets, with her hair loose, crying out to everyone that the horse is full of Greeks, and that it will be the end of Troy. But nobody believes her, because of Apollo's curse. They think she is out of her mind, and they laugh at her, or feel sorry for her.

[pause]

{img:cassandra-in-crowded-street-calling-out-ignored}
And the Trojans bring the horse up to the citadel, and leave it there, in front of the temple of Athena. And they hang garlands of flowers on the temples, all over the city. And they celebrate, with feasting, and wine, and music, and dancing, late into the night. Because, after ten years, the war is over.

[long-pause]

{img:troy-night-quiet-streets-garlands-lamps-sleeping-city}
Late that night, the city of Troy is quiet at last.

[pause]

The feasting is over. The music has stopped. The lamps have gone out, one by one. The people have gone home, tired and happy, and have lain down to sleep, for the first time in ten years without fear. Even the guards on the walls have left their posts, or fallen asleep, wrapped in their cloaks.

Up on the citadel, in front of the temple of Athena, the great wooden horse stands in the moonlight, silent, with the garlands of flowers hanging on it.

[pause]

The rest of the story is very sad, and we will tell it gently, and from a distance, as if we were looking at the city from far away, on the plain, in the night.

[pause]

The Greeks come into the sleeping city. And by the morning, after ten years, Troy has fallen.

[long-pause]

[long-pause]

[pause]

{img:women-of-troy-seashore-ships-waiting-grey-dawn}
What happened to the other people of Troy, in the stories?

[pause]

The Greek poets, and later the great playwrights of Athens, such as Euripides, wrote about the women of Troy, after the city fell. Their stories are very sad. Euripides wrote them to make the people of Athens think about the sorrows of war, even for the enemy.

[long-pause]

Old Hecuba, the queen, is taken away by the Greeks, across the sea. In the stories, she ends her days in Thrace, on the far shore of the Hellespont, and a place on that coast is named after her, Cynossema.

[pause]

Cassandra is taken by Agamemnon, the king of Mycenae, to his home in Greece. She knows what will happen there, as she always does. And when they arrive, at the great palace of Mycenae, Agamemnon and Cassandra are both lost, in a plot by Agamemnon's own wife, Clytemnestra, who has never forgiven him for leaving her, and for the sorrows of the war.

[pause]

So the great king who led the Greeks to Troy comes home, after ten years, only to lose his life on the day of his return.

[long-pause]

Andromache is taken by Neoptolemus, the son of Achilles, to his home in the north of Greece.

[pause]

[pause]

{img:little-copy-of-troy-small-gate-river-greece-afternoon}
In the Aeneid, Aeneas, on his journey, visits them there, and finds Andromache sitting by an empty tomb of Hector, beside the little river, making offerings to his memory. Still, after all those years, she is grieving for Hector.

[long-pause]

And Helen?

[pause]

Menelaus, her husband, finds her, on the last night of Troy. The stories say that he had sworn to take his revenge on her. But when he sees her again, he lets his sword fall from his hand, and forgives her.

[pause]

{img:menelaus-helen-ship-sailing-home-calm-sea}
And they sail home together, to Sparta. But they are blown off course, by storms, and wander for eight years, to Cyprus, and Phoenicia, and Egypt, where they are given wonderful gifts by the king and queen of Egypt, before they come home at last.

[long-pause]

In the Odyssey, Homer gives us a glimpse of them, many years later, at home in Sparta.

[pause]

Odysseus's young son, Telemachus, comes to Sparta, looking for news of his father, who has not come home from the war. [pause]

{img:spartan-palace-hall-helen-spinning-golden-distaff}
Helen comes into the hall, like a goddess, with her maids, carrying a golden spindle, and a silver work basket on wheels, full of fine purple wool, a gift from the queen of Egypt. And she sits down, and spins, and talks to the young man, kindly, and recognizes him at once as the son of Odysseus, because he looks so like his father.

[long-pause]

And that evening, when they all grow sad, remembering the war, and the friends they have lost, Helen puts a drug into their wine, a medicine from Egypt, given to her by an Egyptian lady, which takes away all sorrow, and all grief, and makes people forget their troubles, for a whole day.

[pause]

And then she tells them stories, about Odysseus, at Troy.

[pause]

She tells how, during the war, Odysseus came into Troy in disguise, dressed as a beggar, in rags, with his face bruised, to spy on the Trojans. Nobody recognized him, except Helen. And she washed him, and gave him clothes, and swore not to tell the Trojans, and he told her all the plans of the Greeks. And she says that, by then, she was glad, because she longed to go home, and was sorry for the folly that Aphrodite had brought upon her.

[long-pause]

Of the other heroes, many never came home.

[pause]

Ajax the Lesser was lost at sea, in a storm. Diomedes found that his wife had married someone else, and sailed away to Italy, and founded cities there. Old Nestor got home safely, and lived to a great age, in his palace at Pylos, telling stories.

[pause]

{img:old-king-nestor-palace-porch-sunlight-storytelling}
And Odysseus, the cleverest of them all, who had thought of the wooden horse, set out for his home, the little rocky island of Ithaca, with his twelve ships. It should have taken him a few weeks. It took him ten years.

[long-pause]

{img:odysseus-ship-sail-wine-dark-sea-islands-sunset}
The story of Odysseus's journey home from Troy is told in Homer's other great poem, the Odyssey.

[pause]

It is one of the most famous stories ever told. And in 2026, almost three thousand years after it was first sung, it has been made into a great new film, by the director Christopher Nolan, called simply The Odyssey, and people all over the world have gone to the cinema to see it.

[long-pause]

The Odyssey begins not with Odysseus, but with his family, at home on Ithaca, waiting.

[pause]

His wife, Penelope, has been waiting for him for almost twenty years: ten years of the war, and nearly ten years since. Their son, Telemachus, was a baby when his father left. Now he is a young man, and he does not even remember his father's face.

[pause]

And the palace of Odysseus is full of suitors: more than a hundred young nobles from Ithaca and the islands around, who believe that Odysseus is dead, and want to marry Penelope, and become king. They eat and drink in the palace every day, and waste Odysseus's flocks and his wine, and behave very badly.

[long-pause]

Penelope is clever, like her husband.

[pause]

She tells the suitors that she will choose one of them as her husband, when she has finished weaving a shroud, a burial cloth, for Odysseus's old father, Laertes. And every day, she weaves at her great loom. And every night, by the light of torches, she secretly unpicks what she has woven during the day. And so, for three years, the shroud is never finished.

[pause]

{img:penelope-unweaving-night-torchlight-loom-quiet}
But at last, one of her maids tells the suitors, and they find out the trick.

[long-pause]

Meanwhile, Odysseus has been wandering, over the sea, from one adventure to another.

[pause]

He has been blown far off course by storms, to strange lands at the edge of the world. He has visited the land of the Lotus-eaters, where the people eat a sweet fruit that makes you forget your home, and want to stay forever, and he had to drag his men back to the ships.

[pause]

He has been trapped in a cave by the Cyclops, a giant with one eye in the middle of his forehead, who keeps sheep. And he escaped by a trick, telling the giant that his name was Nobody, and hiding his men under the bellies of the sheep, when the giant let them out to graze in the morning.

[long-pause]

He has visited the island of Aeolus, the king of the winds, who gave him all the winds of the world, tied up in a leather bag, so that only the gentle west wind would blow him home. And he came so close to Ithaca that he could see the smoke of the fires on the island. But while he slept, his men opened the bag, thinking it was full of gold, and all the winds rushed out, and blew them all the way back again.

[pause]

He has stayed for a year on the island of the enchantress Circe, who turned his men into pigs, and then turned them back again, and became his friend, and helped him.

[pause]

{img:enchantress-circe-palace-island-forest-tame-lions-wolves}
He has sailed past the Sirens, whose beautiful song draws sailors onto the rocks. Odysseus wanted to hear the song. So he told his men to tie him to the mast, and to fill their own ears with beeswax, so that they could not hear it. And he heard the song, the most beautiful song in the world, and begged them to untie him. But they rowed on, and did not hear him, and so they passed by safely.

[long-pause]

At last, after all his men and all his ships have been lost, Odysseus comes, alone, to the island of the nymph Calypso, far away in the middle of the sea.

[pause]

Calypso loves him, and keeps him there, in her beautiful cave, with its vines and its gardens and its four springs of clear water, for seven years. She offers to make him immortal, and young forever, if he will stay with her.

[pause]

{img:odysseus-sitting-shore-looking-out-to-sea-longing}
But Odysseus does not want to be immortal. He wants to go home. And every day, he sits on the shore, and looks out over the sea, toward home, and weeps.

[long-pause]

At last, the gods take pity on him. Zeus sends Hermes, the messenger, to tell Calypso to let him go.

[pause]

And Calypso, sadly, helps him to build a raft, and gives him food and wine and clothes, and sends him a gentle wind. And after many more adventures, he comes, shipwrecked, to the land of the Phaeacians, a kind and happy people, who live on a beautiful island, and love music and dancing and feasting.

[pause]

There, the princess Nausicaa finds him, on the beach, where she has gone with her maids to wash the family's clothes in the river, and play ball. She brings him to her father's palace. And at the feast, a blind singer sings about the war of Troy, and about the wooden horse. And Odysseus, hearing it, covers his face with his cloak, and weeps. And then he tells them who he is, and all his adventures.

[long-pause]

The Phaeacians take him home, in one of their magic ships, which need no steering.

[pause]

And while he is asleep, they lay him gently on the shore of Ithaca, with all the gifts they have given him, and sail away.

[pause]

{img:sleeping-man-shore-ithaca-olive-tree-cave-dawn}
After twenty years, Odysseus is home.

[long-pause]

{img:ithaca-rocky-island-goats-olive-groves-morning}
When Odysseus wakes up, on the shore of Ithaca, he does not recognize his own island.

[pause]

The goddess Athena, who loves him for his cleverness, has wrapped the island in a mist. She comes to him, disguised as a young shepherd, and tells him where he is. And then she shows herself to him, and together they make a plan.

[long-pause]

Athena disguises Odysseus as an old beggar, in rags, with wrinkled skin and thin hair, so that no one will recognize him, and the suitors will not know that he has come home.

[pause]

And he goes, first, to the hut of his old swineherd, Eumaeus, who looks after his pigs, up in the hills. Eumaeus does not recognize him. But he welcomes the old beggar kindly, and gives him food, and a place to sleep, and talks to him, sadly, about his master, Odysseus, who has never come home.

[pause]

There, too, Odysseus meets his son, Telemachus, who has come home from his journey to Sparta. And when they are alone, Odysseus tells him who he is. And father and son embrace, and weep, for the first time in twenty years.

[long-pause]

Then Odysseus goes to the palace, still disguised as a beggar.

[pause]

As he comes to the gate, there is an old dog, lying on a heap of dung, neglected, full of fleas, too old and weak to move. It is Argos, the dog that Odysseus raised as a puppy, before he went to Troy, and trained to hunt.

[pause]

{img:old-dog-lying-gate-palace-ithaca-recognizing-master}
And the old dog knows him. After twenty years, in spite of the rags and the disguise, he recognizes his master. He wags his tail, and lowers his ears. But he is too weak to come to him.

[pause]

And Odysseus, seeing him, secretly wipes away a tear, so that Eumaeus will not see it. And the old dog Argos, having seen his master again, after twenty years, closes his eyes, and passes away peacefully.

[long-pause]

In the palace, the suitors mock the old beggar, and throw things at him.

[pause]

But Penelope is kind to him, and asks him to come and talk to her, in the evening, by the fire, because she has heard that he has news of Odysseus. And Odysseus, still disguised, sits with his wife, and tells her that Odysseus is alive, and will come home soon. And Penelope weeps, and does not believe him.

[pause]

{img:penelope-talking-old-beggar-hearth-evening-firelight}
She tells her old nurse, Eurycleia, to wash the beggar's feet. And she knows him, and almost cries out. But Odysseus stops her, gently, with his hand on her mouth, and tells her to keep his secret.

[long-pause]

The next day, Penelope sets a contest for the suitors.

[pause]

She brings out Odysseus's great bow, which only he could string. And she says that she will marry whichever of the suitors can string the bow, and shoot an arrow through the holes in twelve axe heads, set up in a row.

[pause]

{img:great-bow-twelve-axe-heads-row-palace-hall}
One by one, the suitors try. But none of them can even bend the bow. Then the old beggar asks if he may try. The suitors laugh. But Telemachus says that he may. And Odysseus takes up the bow, and strings it, easily, as a musician strings a lyre, and plucks the string, which sings like a swallow. And he shoots the arrow straight through all twelve axe heads.

[long-pause]

What happens next, in the hall, we will pass over quickly. It is enough to say that the suitors are punished for all their wrongs, and the palace is cleared of them.

[pause]

And then Penelope comes down from her room. And she does not know what to think. Is this really Odysseus, after twenty years? Or is it a god, or a stranger, trying to trick her?

[pause]

So she tests him. She tells her maid to move their bed out of the bedroom, for the stranger to sleep on. And Odysseus is upset. Because, he says, nobody can move that bed. He built it himself, long ago, around the trunk of an old olive tree, growing in the middle of the room, and the tree is one of its legs.

[long-pause]

Only Odysseus and Penelope know the secret of the bed.

[pause]

And Penelope runs to him, and throws her arms around his neck, and kisses him, and weeps. And Odysseus weeps too, holding his dear and faithful wife. As welcome as the land is to shipwrecked sailors, swimming through the sea, Homer says, so welcome was her husband to her.

[pause]

{img:olive-tree-bed-bedroom-lamplight-reunion-quiet}
And the goddess Athena holds back the dawn, and makes the night longer, so that they can talk, and tell each other everything that has happened in twenty years.

[long-pause]

{img:heinrich-schliemann-nineteenth-century-gentleman-mound-workers}
For more than two thousand years after Homer, nobody knew for certain where Troy had been.

[pause]

The ancient Greeks and Romans had no doubt. They believed that Troy had stood on the hill near the Dardanelles, where there was a little Greek town called Ilion, and later a Roman city, Ilium. Alexander the Great came there, on his way to conquer Persia, and laid a wreath on the tomb of Achilles. Julius Caesar came there too. The Roman emperors rebuilt the town, in honour of their Trojan ancestors.

[long-pause]

But after the end of the Roman empire, the town was abandoned, and forgotten, and slowly covered with earth and grass.

[pause]

By the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, many scholars believed that Troy had never existed, and that the Iliad was only a story. Others thought that it might have been on another hill, a few miles to the south, called Pinarbasi.

[pause]

One man, Frank Calvert, an Englishman who lived on the Dardanelles, and worked as a consul, and loved archaeology, believed that Troy was the hill of Hisarlik. He owned part of the hill, and dug some trenches there, and found ancient walls. But he did not have enough money to dig properly.

[long-pause]

Then, in 1868, Calvert met a German businessman named Heinrich Schliemann.

[pause]

Schliemann was a remarkable man. He was born in 1822, the son of a poor pastor, in a village in northern Germany. He later said that, when he was a little boy of seven, his father gave him a book of history for Christmas, with a picture of Aeneas escaping from the burning city of Troy, carrying his father on his back. And he decided then that he would one day find Troy. Nobody knows if this story is true. Schliemann liked to tell stories about himself.

[pause]

{img:small-boy-reading-history-book-christmas-candle-germany}
He became a grocer's boy, and then a ship's cabin boy, and was shipwrecked, and then a clerk in Amsterdam. He had an extraordinary gift for languages, and taught himself more than a dozen. He became a merchant, in Russia, trading in indigo, and later in the gold rush in California, and during the Crimean War, and became very rich.

[long-pause]

At the age of forty-six, he gave up business, and decided to devote the rest of his life to finding Troy.

[pause]

He married a young Greek woman, Sophia Engastromenos, who was only seventeen, and who could recite Homer by heart. And in 1870, with Calvert's advice, he began to dig at Hisarlik.

[pause]

Schliemann was not a careful archaeologist. He was in a hurry to find Homer's Troy, which he believed must be at the very bottom of the hill, the oldest city. So he dug a great trench, right through the middle of the hill, from north to south, with a hundred and sixty workmen, cutting through all the layers of the later cities, and destroying much of them, as he went. The trench is still there today, and is called Schliemann's trench.

[long-pause]

He found the remains of many cities, one on top of the other. And near the bottom, in a city which archaeologists now call Troy Two, he found walls, and a gate, and signs of fire.

[pause]

And then, on a hot day in May 1873, near the end of the season, he found a treasure.

[pause]

{img:gold-jewellery-diadems-cups-laid-on-cloth-lamp}
According to his own story, he saw a gleam of gold in the earth, beside a wall. He sent the workmen away to their breakfast, and dug it out himself, with a knife, while Sophia carried the pieces away in her red shawl. It was a great hoard of gold and silver and bronze: cups, and vases, and thousands of little gold beads, and earrings, and two magnificent golden diadems, made of thousands of tiny gold pieces, hanging in chains.

[long-pause]

Schliemann called it the Treasure of Priam. He photographed Sophia wearing the great golden diadem, and the picture went around the world. He believed that she was wearing the jewels of Helen of Troy.

[pause]

In fact, as we now know, the treasure was more than a thousand years older than the time of the Trojan War, from about 2400 before Christ. Sophia, it seems, was not even at Troy on the day it was found. But Schliemann did not let the truth spoil a good story.

[pause]

{img:old-photograph-style-woman-wearing-golden-diadem-portrait}
He smuggled the treasure out of Turkey, to Greece, and later gave it to the city of Berlin. At the end of the Second World War, it disappeared from Berlin. For almost fifty years, nobody knew where it was. Then, in the 1990s, it was revealed that it had been taken to Moscow, where it is today, in the Pushkin Museum.

[long-pause]

Schliemann went on to dig at Mycenae, in Greece, the city of Agamemnon, and found great shaft graves, full of gold, and a golden mask, which he believed was the face of Agamemnon himself. It is said that he sent a telegram to the king of Greece, saying that he had gazed on the face of Agamemnon. Again, it was older than he thought, by about three hundred years.

[pause]

But Schliemann had shown the world that the cities of Homer were real places, and that there was a great civilization in Greece in the Bronze Age, which we now call the Mycenaean civilization.

[pause]

He passed away in 1890, in Naples, at the age of sixty-eight. He is buried in Athens, in a great tomb like a little Greek temple, decorated with scenes of the Trojan War, and of himself, digging at Troy.

[long-pause]

After Schliemann, other archaeologists came to Troy, and dug more carefully.

[pause]

His assistant, Wilhelm Dörpfeld, found the great walls of Troy Six, with their towers and their offsets, which seem to fit Homer's description of the city of fine walls. In the 1930s, an American team, led by Carl Blegen, dug for seven years, and studied the layers very carefully, and believed that the city of the war was Troy Seven A, with its crowded houses, and its storage jars, and its traces of fire.

[pause]

{img:archaeologists-careful-digging-brushes-layers-sunhat}
And from 1988, a German team, led by Manfred Korfmann, and later by others, dug for many years, and found the great lower town, and showed that Troy was much larger and richer than anyone had thought. The work goes on today.

[long-pause]

{img:bronze-age-world-map-mosaic-palaces-ships-trade}
What really happened to Troy, around the year 1200 before Christ?

[pause]

The truth is that we do not know for certain. But we do know that, around that time, something happened, not just at Troy, but all across the eastern Mediterranean, which historians call the Late Bronze Age collapse.

[long-pause]

Within about fifty years, between roughly 1200 and 1150 before Christ, almost all the great palaces and cities of the Bronze Age world were destroyed, or abandoned.

[pause]

In Greece, the great palaces of Mycenae, and Pylos, and Tiryns, and Thebes, were burned, and never rebuilt. The writing of the palace scribes, a script that we call Linear B, was forgotten. For four hundred years, the Greeks could not read or write.

[pause]

In Anatolia, the great Hittite empire came to an end, and its capital, Hattusa, was abandoned. On the coast of Syria, the rich trading city of Ugarit was destroyed, and never lived in again. In its ruins, the archaeologists found clay letters from those last days, telling of enemy ships off the coast, and asking for help.

[long-pause]

Egypt survived, but only just. The pharaoh Ramesses the Third, on the walls of his temple at Medinet Habu, tells of great battles against invaders from the sea, whom historians call the Sea Peoples, who came by land and by sea, with their families, in ox carts, looking for new homes.

[pause]

Why did it all happen?

[pause]

{img:egyptian-temple-wall-carvings-ships-sunlit-stone}
Historians still argue about it. There were probably many causes, all coming together. There was a long drought, which brought famine. There were earthquakes. There may have been diseases. Trade broke down, and the palaces, which depended on trade, could not survive. And there were wandering peoples, driven from their homes, moving across the land and the sea, raiding and settling.

[long-pause]

And Troy?

[pause]

Troy Six, the city of the great walls, was badly damaged, around 1300 before Christ, probably by an earthquake. The people rebuilt it, as Troy Seven A, crowded, with its storage jars. And around 1180 before Christ, Troy Seven A was destroyed by fire. In the ruins, the archaeologists found some bronze arrowheads, and a few signs of fighting.

[pause]

{img:archaeologist-holding-small-bronze-arrowhead-palm}
Was this the Trojan War? Was it the work of the Greeks, the Achaeans, the Ahhiyawa of the Hittite letters? Or the Sea Peoples? Or an earthquake, and a fire? We do not know. Perhaps the memory of many such disasters, over many years, came together in the songs of the poets, as the story of one great war.

[long-pause]

But Troy did not end.

[pause]

People went on living on the hill, in a smaller, poorer town, for many years. New people came, from the Balkans, across the Hellespont, with a different kind of pottery. Later, Greek settlers came, and built a little town, which they called Ilion, and a temple to Athena, on the top of the hill.

[pause]

{img:greek-temple-athena-hilltop-small-town-sea-view}
And they, and all the Greeks, believed that this was the Troy of Homer. They pointed out to visitors the tombs of the heroes on the plain, and the places where the famous things had happened. Every year, for hundreds of years, the people of the Greek town of Locris sent two young girls to serve in the temple of Athena at Troy, to make amends for the wrong done by their hero, Ajax the Lesser, on the last night of Troy.

[long-pause]

Kings and emperors came to visit.

[pause]

In 480 before Christ, the king of Persia, Xerxes, on his way to invade Greece, came to Troy, and climbed up to the citadel, and sacrificed a thousand oxen to Athena of Ilion. In 334 before Christ, Alexander the Great came, and ran a race around the tomb of Achilles, with his friends, as the old custom was, and laid a wreath on it. He said that Achilles was lucky, to have had Homer to tell his story.

[pause]

And the Romans, who believed that they were the descendants of the Trojans, rebuilt the town, with a theatre, and baths, and a council house, and gave it special privileges. The emperor Augustus came, and Hadrian, and Caracalla. And for a while, the emperor Constantine even thought of building his new capital at Troy, before he chose Byzantium, which became Constantinople.

[long-pause]

Slowly, after the end of the Roman empire, the town became smaller and smaller. And by about the fourteenth century, the hill was abandoned, and covered with grass, and the city of Troy, after more than four thousand years, was forgotten.

[pause]

Only the story was not forgotten. The story went on, sung and read and told, in Greek, and Latin, and in every language of Europe, for all those centuries.

[pause]

{img:medieval-manuscript-painting-troy-knights-castle-gold}
In the Middle Ages, the story of Troy was one of the most popular stories of all. Knights and ladies read about Hector and Achilles and Helen, imagining them as knights and ladies too, in castles. Many peoples of Europe, the French, and the British, and others, liked to believe, like the Romans, that their first ancestors had come from Troy.

[long-pause]

{img:troy-archaeological-site-walls-path-visitors-pines}
Today, you can visit Troy.

[pause]

The hill of Hisarlik is in Turkey, about thirty kilometres south of the town of Çanakkale, on the Dardanelles. It is now an archaeological park, and a World Heritage Site, and every year, hundreds of thousands of people come to see it.

[long-pause]

It is smaller than most people expect.

[pause]

You walk up a gentle path, among pine trees and olive trees, and suddenly you are among the ruins: walls of stone, and the foundations of houses, and ramps, and gates, from many different times, all mixed up together, one on top of the other, with little signs to tell you which city each one belongs to.

[pause]

And then you come to the great walls of Troy Six, the city of fine walls, with their sloping faces of limestone, and their little offsets, still standing several metres high, after more than three thousand years. If you stand beside them, in the late afternoon, when the sun is low, you can see the light catching the edges of the stones, just as it did when Hector walked here.

[long-pause]

You can see the south gate, where the main road came into the citadel, with the bases of the standing stones beside it.

[pause]

You can walk up the old stone ramp of Troy Two, from the time of the Treasure of Priam, a thousand years before the war. You can stand at the edge of Schliemann's great trench, and look down into it, and see all the layers of the cities, one above the other, like the pages of a book. And you can see the ruins of the Greek and Roman temple of Athena, and the Roman theatre, and the council house.

[pause]

{img:stone-ramp-ancient-citadel-wildflowers-spring}
In the spring, the ruins are covered with wild flowers: red poppies, and yellow daisies, and purple anemones. Lizards sun themselves on the warm stones. Storks nest on the poles. And the wind blows from the north, as it always did, the strong wind of the Dardanelles. Homer called Troy windy Ilios.

[long-pause]

From the top of the hill, you can look out over the plain, toward the sea.

[pause]

The sea is further away now than it was three thousand years ago. In the time of the war, there was a great bay, coming close to the city, where the ships could anchor. But over the centuries, the rivers have filled the bay with mud and sand, and now the plain stretches for several kilometres, green with fields of wheat and cotton and tomatoes, to the shore.

[pause]

But you can still see the line of the sea, shining in the distance. And beyond it, the island of Tenedos, which is now called Bozcaada, where the Greek ships hid, with its little harbour, and its castle, and its vineyards. And the great ships still go past, through the Dardanelles, on their way to and from the Black Sea, just as the ships did in the time of the Trojans.

[long-pause]

At the entrance to the site, there is a large wooden horse.

[pause]

It was built in the 1970s, and children love to climb up the stairs inside it, and look out of the little windows in its side, pretending to be Greek warriors. It is not very beautiful, or very old. But nobody minds.

[pause]

{img:wooden-horse-replica-site-entrance-children-stairs}
And in the town of Çanakkale, on the seafront, there is another wooden horse. It is the horse that was used in a Hollywood film about Troy, made in 2004, which the film company gave to the town. People take photographs in front of it, in the evening, with the sea behind.

[long-pause]

Near the site, there is a fine new museum, the Troy Museum, opened in 2018.

[pause]

It is a large, square building, the colour of rusty iron, as if it had just been dug out of the earth. Inside, you walk slowly down a long ramp, past all the layers of Troy's history, from the oldest at the bottom to the newest at the top, as if you were digging down through the hill.

[pause]

There are pots, and tools, and weapons, and jewellery, and statues, and coins, and the little bronze seal with Luwian writing on it, the only writing ever found at Troy from the time of the war. And there is some of the gold from Troy, which was found at Troy, or nearby, and has come home.

[long-pause]

And on the plain, and the hills around Troy, the great mounds that the ancient Greeks called the tombs of the heroes are still there: the tomb of Achilles, and of Patroclus, and of Ajax, standing on the low ridges near the sea, covered with grass and wild thyme.

[pause]

Nobody knows who is really buried in them. Some of them seem to be from later times. But it does not matter very much. They are part of the story, which has been told on this plain for three thousand years.

[pause]

{img:grassy-burial-mound-ridge-sea-evening-light}
Further along the coast, on the peninsula of Gallipoli, across the water, there are the cemeteries and the memorials of another war, a war of 1915, fought by soldiers from Britain, and Australia, and New Zealand, and France, and Turkey, many of whom knew the Iliad, and wrote about Troy, which they could see across the strait.

[long-pause]

And the story goes on.

[pause]

It is still told in schools, and books, and plays, and paintings, and poems, and films. Children still learn about the wooden horse, and Achilles's heel. People still say beware of Greeks bearing gifts, and call a long journey an odyssey, and a hidden danger a Trojan horse.

[pause]

And all over the world, people still read the Iliad and the Odyssey, the old songs of the bards, about the windy city on the hill, and the old king, and Hector and Andromache on the tower, and the man who wanted only to go home.

[long-pause]

So we leave the city of Priam on its hill above the plain, as the evening wind comes in from the sea.

[pause]

The walls, the horses, the weavers at their looms, and the long story that has outlived them all.
