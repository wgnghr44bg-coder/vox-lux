TOPIC: The last days of Troy
===
IMG: wooden-horse-before-troy-walls-golden-evening | a giant wooden horse standing on a grassy plain before the high sloping stone walls and gate of Bronze Age Troy, golden evening light, the sea far behind, calm and still
HEAD: According to the ancient poets, Troy fell because of a giant wooden horse.
BODY: The story of Troy's last days is one of the most famous tales in the world. According to the old poems, the Greeks had besieged the city for ten long years. They could not break its walls. So they tried a trick. They built a huge wooden horse. Inside it, they hid some of their best warriors. Then the rest of the army burned their camp and sailed away, as if they had given up. They only went as far as a nearby island, where they hid behind the shore. The Trojans came out and found the horse standing on the empty beach. They did not know what to make of it. Some wanted to burn it. Others wanted to bring it into the city as a gift to the gods. The full story is told by the Roman poet Virgil, more than a thousand years later. Yet the horse had already been mentioned by Homer. It became the most famous trick in history.
===
IMG: empty-beach-greek-camp-ashes-dawn-troy | an empty sandy beach at dawn near Troy with the blackened remains of a great camp, burned wooden huts and ashes, the calm sea with no ships, soft grey morning light
HEAD: The Trojans woke one morning to find the Greek camp burned and the ships gone.
BODY: For ten years, the Greek army had camped on the shore near Troy. Their ships were pulled up on the sand. Their huts and tents stood in long rows behind them. Then, one morning, everything changed. The Trojans looked out from their walls and saw smoke rising from the beach. The camp was burning. The ships were gone. The sea was empty. The poets describe the joy of the Trojans. The gates were opened for the first time in years. People streamed out onto the plain. They walked through the empty camp. They pointed out where the great warriors had stayed. Here was the hut of Achilles. There was the place where the ships had stood. Even so, not everything was gone. In the middle of the beach stood the strange wooden horse. Yet for that one morning, the people of Troy believed their long war was over.
===
IMG: laocoon-priest-warning-crowd-wooden-horse | an old bearded priest in a long robe raising his arm in warning before a crowd of Bronze Age Trojans gathered around a giant wooden horse on a plain, soft afternoon light
HEAD: A priest named Laocoon warned the Trojans not to trust the horse.
BODY: In Virgil's poem, not all Trojans were fooled. A priest named Laocoon came running down from the city. He shouted at the crowd. He told them that the Greeks could not be trusted. He said that the horse might be full of hidden soldiers, or a machine to spy on the city. He spoke the famous line that is still quoted today. In English, it warns people to beware of Greeks, even when they bring gifts. He threw a spear at the side of the horse. It stuck there, and the hollow wood echoed. Even so, the Trojans did not listen. In the story, a terrible sign seemed to prove him wrong. Two great serpents came out of the sea and attacked the priest and his sons. The Trojans took this as a punishment from the gods. Yet the reader of the poem knows that Laocoon was right. His warning is one of the great moments of ancient literature.
===
IMG: sinon-greek-captive-hands-tied-trojans | a young Greek man with his hands tied being led before an old king and Trojan elders on a sunlit plain beside a giant wooden horse, Bronze Age clothing, soft afternoon light
HEAD: A Greek named Sinon persuaded the Trojans that the horse was a holy offering.
BODY: According to Virgil, the Greeks had left one man behind on purpose. His name was Sinon. Trojan shepherds found him and brought him before King Priam with his hands tied. Sinon told a clever and moving story. He said that the Greeks had wanted to sacrifice him to the gods, but he had escaped. He said the horse was an offering to the goddess Athena. The Greeks had built it so large on purpose. If the Trojans brought it into their city, Athena would protect Troy forever. If they harmed it, Troy would be destroyed. Priam felt pity for him and ordered his ropes to be removed. Sinon's story was a lie from beginning to end. Even so, it was very convincing. It played on the Trojans' hopes and fears. In the poem, Sinon is the second half of the trick. The horse alone was not enough. It needed a good story to bring it inside the walls.
===
IMG: trojans-pulling-wooden-horse-through-gate-garlands | crowds of Bronze Age Trojans pulling a giant wooden horse on rollers with long ropes towards a great stone gate, flowers and garlands on the horse, warm golden evening light
HEAD: The Trojans broke open part of their own wall to bring the horse inside.
BODY: The Trojans decided to take the horse into the city. It was too big to pass through the gate. So, in Virgil's story, they pulled down part of their own wall. They put wheels or rollers under the horse and tied ropes around its neck. Then they dragged it slowly across the plain. Children and young women sang hymns and touched the ropes for luck. Four times, the poem says, the horse stopped at the gate. Four times, the sound of weapons rang from inside it. Yet no one noticed. The Trojans hung garlands of flowers on it and placed it near the temple of Athena. Then the whole city celebrated. People feasted and drank late into the night. Even so, there was one more warning. The princess Cassandra cried out that the city was doomed. But no one ever believed her. That evening, Troy fell asleep happy for the last time.
===
IMG: cassandra-princess-temple-steps-night-torch | a young princess in a long pale robe standing alone on the steps of a Bronze Age temple at night, a torch burning beside her, a quiet city below under the stars
HEAD: The princess Cassandra could see the future, but she was cursed so that no one believed her.
BODY: Cassandra was one of the daughters of King Priam. In the old myths, she was very beautiful. The god Apollo fell in love with her. He gave her the gift of seeing the future. But when she refused him, he could not take the gift back. Instead, he added a curse. She would always see the truth, but no one would ever believe her. Throughout the war, Cassandra warned the Trojans of what was coming. She warned against Paris bringing Helen to Troy. She warned against the wooden horse. Each time, people thought she was mad. Her story is one of the saddest in Greek myth. Even so, her name lives on. Today, a person who gives warnings that are ignored is sometimes called a Cassandra. Yet in Homer's Iliad, she appears only briefly. She is the first to see her father bringing home Hector's body. Much of her famous story was developed by later poets and playwrights.
===
IMG: quiet-troy-night-stars-walls-sleeping-city | the walls and flat-roofed houses of Bronze Age Troy at night under a sky full of stars, a few lamps glowing in windows, the dark plain beyond, quiet and peaceful
HEAD: In the story, the Greek warriors climbed out of the horse in the middle of the night.
BODY: According to the poems, Troy slept after its great celebration. Late in the night, Sinon crept to the horse. He opened a hidden door in its side. The Greek warriors climbed down a rope. Among them, the story says, was Odysseus, who had planned the whole trick. Meanwhile, the Greek fleet sailed back from the island in the darkness. The warriors from the horse opened the gates. The army poured in. Troy fell that night. The ancient poets describe its end in great detail. Even so, historians ask whether any of this really happened. There is no direct evidence of a wooden horse. Some scholars think it may be a memory of a siege machine. Others think it may stand for an earthquake, because the Greek god of earthquakes, Poseidon, was also the god of horses. Yet whatever the truth, the story never lost its power. People have told it for nearly three thousand years.

TOPIC: The city on the hill
===
IMG: hisarlik-mound-troy-ruins-plain-dardanelles | a low grassy mound with ancient stone ruins on a wide green plain in northwest Turkey, the narrow strait of the Dardanelles shining in the distance, soft morning light
HEAD: The ruins of Troy lie on a low hill in Turkey, near the entrance to the Dardanelles.
BODY: For centuries, people wondered whether Troy had ever existed. Then, in the 1800s, archaeologists turned their attention to a low hill in northwest Turkey. It is called Hisarlik. It stands near the Aegean coast, close to the entrance of the Dardanelles. The Dardanelles is a narrow strait that connects the Aegean Sea with the Sea of Marmara and, beyond it, the Black Sea. The ancient Greeks called it the Hellespont. Today, most scholars agree that Hisarlik is the site of Troy. Greeks and Romans who lived there in later times believed so too. They built a new city on the hill and called it Ilion. Even so, the site does not look like a great city today. It is a small hill of stones, walls and trenches. Yet beneath it lie layers of history, piled up over thousands of years. Each layer is the ruin of a different city.
===
IMG: troy-layers-cross-section-trench-ancient-walls | a deep archaeological trench cut through a hill revealing many layers of ancient stone walls one above another, earth banks and old stones, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Archaeologists found not one Troy, but at least nine cities built on top of each other.
BODY: When archaeologists dug into the hill of Hisarlik, they made a surprising discovery. There was not just one city there. There were many. One after another, cities had been built, destroyed and built again on the same spot. Each new city stood on the ruins of the one before. Over time, the hill grew higher. Archaeologists have numbered the main layers from Troy One, at the bottom, to Troy Nine, at the top. The oldest goes back to around three thousand BC. The youngest is the Roman city of Ilium. Within these main layers, there are dozens of smaller building phases. Even so, it was hard to know which layer belonged to Homer's Troy. Early diggers made mistakes. Yet today, most scholars look at two layers. They are known as Troy Six and Troy Seven A. These cities stood in the late Bronze Age, around the time when the Trojan War may have taken place.
===
IMG: troy-vi-sloping-stone-walls-tower-bronze-age | the massive sloping limestone walls and a square tower of Bronze Age Troy, carefully fitted stones, a gate opening between them, grass growing at the foot, warm golden light
HEAD: The walls of Troy Six were built of carefully cut stone and leaned gently inwards.
BODY: Troy Six was a strong and wealthy city. It stood from around seventeen hundred to thirteen hundred BC. Its citadel was surrounded by impressive walls. They were built of large blocks of limestone. The lower part of the walls sloped gently inwards. Above, there was once a wall of mud brick. Several great towers guarded the gates. Parts of these walls still stand today, several metres high. Visitors can walk beside them and touch the old stones. Homer describes Troy as a city with fine walls and high gates. The walls of Troy Six fit that description well. Some scholars believe that the poems preserve a distant memory of this real city. Even so, Troy Six did not end in a war. Around thirteen hundred BC, it was damaged, perhaps by an earthquake. The next city, Troy Seven A, was built on its ruins. That city ended in fire, around eleven hundred and eighty BC.
===
IMG: lower-town-troy-houses-outside-citadel | a Bronze Age town of simple flat-roofed houses of mud brick and stone spreading below a walled citadel on a hill, people walking between the houses, soft morning light
HEAD: In the 1990s, archaeologists discovered that Troy had a large lower town outside the citadel.
BODY: For a long time, people thought Troy was quite small. The citadel on the hill covered only a few acres. It seemed too small to be the great city of the poems. Then, from 1988, a German archaeologist named Manfred Korfmann led new excavations. His team used modern methods to study the ground around the hill. They found that the city had been much larger. Below the citadel lay a lower town, where ordinary people lived. It was protected by a ditch cut into the rock. Korfmann believed that several thousand people lived in Troy at its height. Some estimates go as high as ten thousand. Even so, these numbers are still debated. Other archaeologists think he was too generous. Yet the discovery changed the picture. Troy was not just a fortress. It was a real city, with houses, streets and workshops, spread out around the foot of the hill.
===
IMG: hittite-clay-tablet-cuneiform-wilusa | an ancient clay tablet covered in small wedge-shaped cuneiform writing lying on a stone table, lit by the soft light of an oil lamp
HEAD: Clay tablets from the Hittite Empire mention a city called Wilusa, which may be Troy.
BODY: Troy did not only appear in Greek poems. It may also appear in real records from the Bronze Age. The Hittites were a powerful people who ruled much of Anatolia at that time. Their capital was at Hattusa, in the centre of modern Turkey. Thousands of their clay tablets have been found there. Some of them mention a land in the west called Wilusa. Many scholars believe Wilusa is the same name as Ilios, the Greek name for Troy. One tablet records a treaty with a king of Wilusa named Alaksandu. His name sounds very like Alexandros. That was another name for Paris, the prince of Troy in the poems. Other tablets mention a people called the Ahhiyawa. They may be the Achaeans, Homer's name for the Greeks. Even so, these links are not certain. Scholars still argue about them. Yet the tablets suggest that the world of the poems was based on a real world of kings and kingdoms.
===
IMG: luwian-bronze-seal-small-object-museum | a small round bronze seal with tiny ancient carved symbols on both sides, lying on dark cloth in soft museum light
HEAD: A small bronze seal found at Troy is the only piece of writing ever discovered there from the Bronze Age.
BODY: In 1995, archaeologists working at Troy found a tiny object. It was a small round seal made of bronze. It had writing on both sides. The writing was not Greek. It was in Luwian hieroglyphs, a script used in Anatolia in the Bronze Age. The seal names a scribe and his wife. It probably dates from the late 1100s BC. It is the only piece of writing from Bronze Age Troy that has ever been found. Its discovery was important. It suggests that the people of Troy belonged to the world of Anatolia, not Greece. Their language may have been Luwian, which was related to Hittite. Even so, one small seal cannot tell us everything. It may have been brought from somewhere else. Yet it is a precious clue. In the poems, Trojans and Greeks speak to each other easily. In reality, they may have spoken quite different languages.
===
IMG: troy-gate-ramp-paved-road-ancient-stones | an ancient paved stone ramp leading up through a gate in the walls of Troy, worn stones, grass between them, quiet and empty, soft warm late afternoon light
HEAD: An ancient paved ramp still leads up to one of the gates of Troy.
BODY: Among the ruins of Troy, one of the most striking sights is a stone ramp. It leads up from the lower ground to a gateway in the walls. It is paved with large flat stones. Heinrich Schliemann found it in the 1870s. He was very excited. He believed it led to the palace of King Priam. He called it the Scaean Gate, after a gate mentioned by Homer. In fact, the ramp is much older than the time of the Trojan War. It belongs to Troy Two, around twenty-five hundred BC. That is more than a thousand years before the events of the poems. Even so, the ramp is impressive. Its stones were laid by people who lived at Troy in the early Bronze Age. Visitors still walk up it today. Yet it is also a reminder of how easy it is to see what we want to see. The search for Troy was full of such hopeful mistakes.

TOPIC: Daily life in a Bronze Age city
===
IMG: bronze-age-house-interior-hearth-loom-clay-jars | an indoor scene, enclosed by stone and mud brick walls, a plain wooden ceiling, the inside of a Bronze Age house at Troy with a central hearth, a standing loom, large clay storage jars and woven mats, warm firelight
HEAD: Ordinary Trojans lived in houses of stone and mud brick, with a hearth in the main room.
BODY: Most people in Bronze Age Troy lived simple lives. Their houses had stone foundations. The walls above were made of mud bricks, dried in the sun. Roofs were flat, made of wooden beams covered with reeds and clay. In summer, people could sleep on the roof in the cool night air. Inside, the main room had a hearth for cooking and warmth. Smoke escaped through a hole in the roof. Large clay jars stood along the walls. They held grain, oil, wine and water. Some houses had a second room for storage or animals. Floors were made of beaten earth. Archaeologists have found the remains of these houses in the excavations. Even so, the houses of the rich were larger. They had more rooms and finer walls. The palace on top of the hill was the grandest of all. Yet for most families, life centred on one warm room, a fire and the work of the day.
===
IMG: women-weaving-loom-courtyard-bronze-age | two women in long woollen dresses weaving at a tall upright loom in a sunny courtyard of a Bronze Age house, clay loom weights hanging below, warm morning light
HEAD: Archaeologists have found thousands of spindle whorls at Troy, showing how much spinning and weaving went on.
BODY: Making cloth was one of the most important tasks in any Bronze Age home. Wool from sheep and fibres from flax were spun into thread. This was done with a spindle, a stick with a small weight at the end called a whorl. At Troy, archaeologists have found thousands of these whorls. They are small round objects of clay or stone, with a hole in the middle. The thread was then woven on a tall upright loom. Heavy clay weights hung from the bottom to keep the threads tight. In the poems, even queens and princesses weave. In the Iliad, Helen weaves a great cloth showing the battles of the Trojans and Greeks. Andromache weaves in her room when she hears the sad news of Hector's death. Even so, weaving was hard and slow. A single large cloth could take months. Yet it was work that women did together, talking and singing as their hands moved.
===
IMG: horses-grazing-trojan-plain-river-evening | a small herd of horses grazing on a wide green plain near a river below the walls of Troy in the evening, reeds along the water, soft golden light
HEAD: Homer often called the Trojans "tamers of horses".
BODY: In the Iliad, the Trojans are often described with a special phrase. They are called the horse-taming Trojans. The great hero Hector is called Hector, tamer of horses. The very last line of the Iliad uses this phrase. It describes the funeral of Hector, the tamer of horses. Horses were precious in the Bronze Age. They pulled the light war chariots of kings and nobles. They were a sign of wealth and power. Archaeology seems to support Homer's picture. In the layer of Troy Six, archaeologists found horse bones. They suggest that horses were important at Troy from around that time. The wide plain below the city had good grass and water from the rivers. Even so, ordinary people rarely owned horses. Farm work was done by oxen and donkeys. Yet the image of the Trojans as horse people stayed with them. It may be one more echo of the real city behind the poems.
===
IMG: bronze-age-meal-bread-figs-olives-wine-table | a simple Bronze Age meal on a low wooden table with flat barley bread, figs, olives, cheese and a clay cup of wine, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: The people of Troy ate bread made from barley and wheat, with lentils, fish, figs and cheese.
BODY: Archaeologists can learn a great deal about ancient diets. They study seeds, bones and remains of food found in the ruins. At Troy, they found evidence of barley, wheat, lentils, peas and beans. People made flat bread and porridge from the grain. They ate figs, grapes and other fruit. They kept sheep, goats, pigs and cattle. Milk was made into cheese. Fish and shellfish came from the nearby sea and rivers. Wine was made from grapes. In the poems, heroes often eat roast meat at great feasts. Yet that was not daily food for most people. Meat was usually eaten only at festivals and sacrifices. Even so, the land around Troy was fertile. The plain was watered by rivers and good for farming. The sea was close. A family in Troy, in good years, would not have gone hungry. Their meals were simple, but healthy and fresh.
===
IMG: potter-wheel-grey-pottery-workshop-bronze-age | a potter shaping a grey clay jug on a simple wheel in a Bronze Age workshop at Troy, finished grey bowls drying on a shelf, warm afternoon light through a doorway
HEAD: The potters of Troy made a distinctive grey pottery that archaeologists can easily recognise.
BODY: Pottery is one of the most useful things for archaeologists. Clay pots break easily, but the broken pieces survive for thousands of years. At Troy, one type of pottery is very common in the late Bronze Age. It is a smooth grey ware, often with a slightly shiny surface. Archaeologists call it Anatolian grey ware. It was made on a potter's wheel and fired in a kiln. Potters made jugs, bowls, cups and storage jars. Alongside it, archaeologists have found pottery from the Mycenaean world of Greece. These pieces were brought to Troy by trade. Their style is quite different, with painted patterns. Even so, the presence of Greek pottery does not mean that Greeks lived at Troy. It shows that the two worlds traded with each other. Yet it also tells us something important. Troy was connected to Greece long before any war, through the quiet exchange of goods.
===
IMG: ships-trading-bay-near-troy-bronze-age | small Bronze Age wooden ships with square sails anchored in a calm bay near a hill with a walled city, traders unloading jars onto the beach, soft morning light
HEAD: Troy's position at the mouth of the Hellespont made it a centre of trade.
BODY: Troy stood in a very good spot. The Hellespont was the sea route between the Aegean and the Black Sea. Ships passing through the strait often had to wait. Strong currents and winds blew from the north for much of the summer. In the Bronze Age, ships had simple square sails and oars. They could not easily sail against the wind. So they waited in sheltered bays near Troy until the wind changed. This gave the Trojans a chance to trade with them. They may also have charged tolls. In the Bronze Age, there was a bay closer to Troy than the coast today. Over thousands of years, the rivers filled it with mud. Even so, historians still debate how important trade was to Troy. Yet its location must have made it rich. Some scholars think that rivalry over this trade could have been one real cause of the war.
===
IMG: bronze-age-children-playing-clay-toys-courtyard | Bronze Age children playing with small clay animal figures and a wooden ball in a sunny courtyard, a woman grinding grain nearby, soft warm light
HEAD: Children in Bronze Age Anatolia played with clay animals, rattles and balls.
BODY: Children have always played, even in the distant past. Archaeologists have found clay toys at many Bronze Age sites in the region. There were small animals, such as horses, oxen and birds. There were rattles made of clay with small stones inside. Some figurines may have been toys, though others had religious uses. Children also played with balls, sticks and knucklebones, the small bones of sheep. Knucklebones were thrown like dice in a game. Even so, childhood was short. Children helped their families from an early age. They watched the animals, fetched water and helped with the harvest. Girls learned to spin and weave. Boys learned to farm, fish or work in a craft. In the Iliad, Hector's baby son is held in his nurse's arms. He is frightened by his father's helmet. It is one of the few moments in the poem when a small child appears, and it is one of the most gentle.
===
IMG: storage-jars-sunk-into-house-floor-troy | an indoor scene, enclosed by stone walls, a plain ceiling, large clay storage jars sunk up to their necks into the earthen floor of a small Bronze Age house, flat stone lids on top, soft light through a doorway
HEAD: In the last city of the Bronze Age at Troy, families sank large storage jars into the floors of their houses.
BODY: When the archaeologist Carl Blegen excavated the layer called Troy Seven A, he noticed something unusual. The houses were small and crowded close together, even inside the citadel. Many of them had large clay storage jars sunk into the floor. Only the tops showed above the ground. Each jar was covered with a flat stone. These jars could hold grain, oil, wine or water. Blegen thought they showed a city preparing for a siege. People had moved inside the walls and stored as much food as they could. The city was then destroyed by fire, around eleven hundred and eighty BC. Arrowheads and signs of fighting were found in the ruins. Blegen believed this might be the Troy of the Trojan War. Even so, other explanations are possible. The jars might simply show a crowded and anxious time. Yet the picture is striking. It seems to show ordinary families, quietly preparing for hard days ahead.
===
IMG: spring-cave-water-channel-rock-troy | the dark entrance of a cave cut into rock at the foot of a hill, clear water flowing out along a stone channel, ferns and moss around it, soft cool morning light
HEAD: Beneath Troy, archaeologists explored a long underground water system cut into the rock.
BODY: Every city needs water. At Troy, the people found it underground. On the edge of the lower town, there is a cave cut into the rock. Long tunnels run from it deep into the hill. Water from underground springs flows out through them. Manfred Korfmann's team explored and dated this system. They found that it was first cut in the early Bronze Age. It was used and extended for many centuries. In the Roman period, it was still a source of water. The discovery interested scholars of the Hittite tablets. A treaty with the king of Wilusa lists the gods who protected the agreement. Among them is a god of the underground watercourse of Wilusa. Some think this may refer to the spring cave at Troy. Even so, the link is not certain. Yet it is a beautiful idea. A small underground stream may connect the stones of Troy with words written in a distant Hittite city.
===
IMG: uluburun-shipwreck-copper-ingots-seabed | ancient flat copper ingots shaped like ox hides lying on a sandy seabed among broken clay jars, clear blue water above, soft rays of sunlight
HEAD: A shipwreck from the late Bronze Age, found off the coast of Turkey, shows what traders carried in Troy's time.
BODY: In 1982, a sponge diver off the south coast of Turkey saw strange metal objects on the seabed. They were flat copper ingots, shaped a little like animal hides. Archaeologists soon realised they had found a very old ship. It is called the Uluburun wreck, after the place where it sank. It went down around thirteen hundred BC. Its cargo was amazing. There were about ten tonnes of copper and one tonne of tin. Melted together, these would make bronze. There were glass ingots, ebony wood, ivory, amber and jars of resin. There was gold jewellery from many lands. The goods came from Egypt, Cyprus, Syria, Greece and further away. Even so, no one knows exactly where the ship was going. Yet it gives a wonderful picture of the late Bronze Age. This was a world connected by trade. Ships like this one may well have passed close to the walls of Troy.
===
IMG: cremation-urns-cemetery-outside-walls-bronze-age | rows of simple clay urns set into the ground in a quiet cemetery outside ancient stone city walls, wild grass and poppies around them, soft evening light
HEAD: The people of Troy Six burned their dead and buried the ashes in clay urns outside the walls.
BODY: In 1934, Carl Blegen's team found a cemetery just outside the walls of Troy Six. It contained clay urns holding the ashes and burned bones of the dead. Some urns also held small gifts, such as beads or pins. The people of Troy at that time cremated their dead. This was unusual. In Mycenaean Greece, the dead were mostly buried, not burned. Yet in the poems of Homer, the heroes are burned on great funeral pyres. Patroclus and Hector both receive cremation. Their bones are gathered and placed in golden urns or boxes. The cemetery at Troy shows that cremation was a real practice there in the late Bronze Age. Even so, the graves at Troy are simple. They do not contain great treasures. Yet they bring the ancient people of the city very close. Each small urn held someone who had once walked the streets of Troy, beneath its tall walls.
===
IMG: lyre-tortoise-shell-strings-resting-table | an ancient lyre made from a tortoise shell with curved wooden arms and gut strings resting on a wooden table, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: Music in the age of Troy was played on lyres, flutes and drums.
BODY: Music was part of life in the Bronze Age. Paintings and carvings from the period show people playing instruments. The most important was the lyre. It had a sound box, two curved arms and strings made from animal gut. The player plucked the strings with fingers or a small pick. A wall painting from the palace at Pylos shows a musician playing a lyre. A bird flies up from it, as if carried by the music. People also played pipes, made of reed or bone, and simple drums. Music was heard at feasts, at religious festivals and at funerals. In the poems, singers perform at the feasts of kings. Achilles plays the lyre in his tent. Even so, the actual tunes have been lost. No one knows exactly how the music sounded. Yet scholars have tried to rebuild ancient instruments and play them. Their gentle, bright tones give a faint echo of the songs heard in Troy.
