TOPIC: Homer and the songs of Troy
===
IMG: blind-bard-lyre-singing-hall-firelight | an old blind poet with a white beard singing and playing a lyre beside a hearth in an ancient Greek hall, listeners sitting quietly on benches, an indoor scene, enclosed by stone walls, a plain wooden ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: The story of Troy was first told in songs, long before it was written down.
BODY: The Trojan War, if it happened, took place around twelve hundred BC. Yet the great poems about it were composed much later. For centuries, the story was kept alive by singers. They travelled from place to place and sang at feasts and festivals. They did not read from books. They knew the stories by heart. They used fixed phrases and patterns that helped them remember. Each time they sang, the poem changed a little. Scholars call this oral poetry. In the 1930s, an American scholar named Milman Parry studied singers in Yugoslavia. They could still perform very long poems from memory in the same way. His work helped explain how Homer's poems were made. Even so, at some point, the songs were written down. This probably happened in the eighth or seventh century BC, after the Greeks adopted a new alphabet. Yet the poems still carry the rhythm of a voice, singing beside a fire.
===
IMG: homer-bust-marble-bearded-old-man | a white marble bust of an old bearded man with closed eyes and a calm face, standing on a stone pedestal in a quiet room, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: No one knows for certain who Homer was, or even whether he existed.
BODY: The two great poems about Troy are the Iliad and the Odyssey. The ancient Greeks believed both were the work of one poet named Homer. They said he was blind and that he came from the eastern Aegean, perhaps from the island of Chios or the city of Smyrna. Yet very little is known about him. The stories about his life were written centuries later and are mostly legend. Many statues of Homer survive, showing an old man with a beard and closed eyes. But these were made long after his time. They show what people imagined, not what he looked like. Scholars have argued about Homer for over two hundred years. Some believe a single great poet shaped each poem. Others think the poems grew over generations. Even so, most agree on one thing. Whoever composed them was a master. The Iliad and the Odyssey are among the greatest works in all of literature.
===
IMG: ancient-scroll-greek-writing-wooden-table | an ancient papyrus scroll partly unrolled on a wooden table, covered with columns of Greek letters, a clay oil lamp burning beside it, warm lamplight
HEAD: The Iliad has about fifteen thousand lines, yet it tells only a few weeks of the war.
BODY: Many people think the Iliad tells the whole story of the Trojan War. In fact, it does not. It covers only a few weeks in the tenth and final year of the war. It begins with a quarrel between two Greek leaders, Agamemnon and Achilles. It ends with the funeral of the Trojan hero Hector. It does not include the wooden horse. It does not describe the fall of Troy. The poem has about fifteen thousand seven hundred lines. They are written in a special rhythm called dactylic hexameter. Each line has six beats, like the steady roll of waves. Even so, the Iliad manages to contain the whole war. Through memories, speeches and stories, the listener learns about the past and the future. Yet its main subject is not the war itself. It is the anger of Achilles, and what that anger costs. The poem begins with that very word, anger, as its first word.
===
IMG: greek-festival-crowd-listening-reciter-stage | a crowd of ancient Greeks in white robes sitting on stone steps listening to a performer reciting poetry on a small platform at a festival, olive trees around, bright afternoon light
HEAD: In ancient Athens, the poems of Homer were recited at a great festival every four years.
BODY: By the sixth century BC, Homer's poems were famous all over the Greek world. In Athens, they were recited at a great festival called the Panathenaia. It was held every four years in honour of the goddess Athena. Professional performers, called rhapsodes, took turns reciting the Iliad and the Odyssey. They stood before large crowds, holding a staff. They performed the poems with great feeling and drama. Some could move their listeners to tears. Children learned Homer at school. They memorised long passages. Educated Greeks quoted him in speeches and letters. The poems became like a shared book of wisdom and history. Even so, the Greeks did not treat Homer as perfect. Some philosophers, like Plato, criticised the way he described the gods. Yet nearly everyone knew his poems. For a thousand years, Homer was at the very heart of Greek education.
===
IMG: boar-tusk-helmet-museum-display | an ancient helmet made of rows of curved boar's tusks sewn onto a leather cap, displayed on a stand in a quiet museum, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft light
HEAD: Homer describes a helmet made of boar's tusks, and archaeologists have found real ones.
BODY: In the tenth book of the Iliad, Odysseus is given a special helmet. Homer describes it in detail. It is made of leather, with rows of white boar's tusks sewn on the outside. For a long time, scholars wondered if such a helmet had ever existed. Then archaeologists began finding them in Mycenaean graves in Greece. Pieces of boars' tusks, carefully cut and drilled, were found arranged in rows. Small ivory carvings show warriors wearing these helmets. Making one took the tusks of many wild boars. It was a sign of the owner's skill as a hunter. Yet these helmets were common earlier in the Bronze Age, centuries before the probable date of the war. Even so, the description is remarkable. It means that a detail of Bronze Age life survived in songs for perhaps five hundred years. The poems were not history. But they held real memories, passed down from singer to singer.
===
IMG: aegean-sea-islands-sunset-sailing-ship | a calm Aegean sea at sunset with distant rocky islands, a single small ancient ship with a square sail far away, soft orange and violet light on the water
HEAD: Homer often called the sea "wine-dark".
BODY: Homer's poems are full of repeated phrases. Dawn is rosy-fingered. Achilles is swift-footed. Athena is grey-eyed. The sea is often wine-dark. These phrases helped singers fill out the rhythm of their lines. They also gave the poems a calm and familiar sound. Listeners knew them and waited for them. The wine-dark sea has puzzled readers for a long time. The sea is not usually the colour of wine. Some scholars once suggested that the ancient Greeks saw colours differently. Others think the phrase describes the deep, shining darkness of the sea at evening. Greek wine was often mixed with water, and its colour may have been different from wine today. Even so, the phrase is beautiful. It captures something real about the sea. Yet no one can be sure exactly what Homer meant. Perhaps it is enough to imagine the Aegean at sunset, glowing deep and dark between the islands.

TOPIC: King Priam and his family
===
IMG: king-priam-old-throne-palace-hall | an old king with a long white beard sitting on a carved wooden throne in a Bronze Age palace hall with painted walls and a central hearth, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: In the poems, King Priam of Troy had fifty sons.
BODY: At the time of the war, Troy was ruled by an old king named Priam. In the poems, he is wise, gentle and much loved. He had many children. Homer says he had fifty sons and many daughters. Nineteen of the sons were children of his queen, Hecuba. The others were born to other women of the palace. His sons lived with their wives in rooms around his palace. His daughters lived nearby with their husbands. It was a huge royal household. Priam had been a great warrior in his youth. By the time of the war, he was too old to fight. He watched the battles from the city walls. Even so, the war took a terrible toll on his family. One by one, many of his sons were killed. Yet in the Iliad, Priam never loses his dignity. He remains a figure of kindness. Many readers find him one of the most moving characters in the poem.
===
IMG: queen-hecuba-palace-courtyard-women | an older queen in a long embroidered robe standing in a palace courtyard with other women of the court, Bronze Age clothing, a fountain and painted columns, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Queen Hecuba became a symbol of grief in later Greek literature.
BODY: Hecuba was the wife of Priam and the queen of Troy. She was the mother of many of his children, including Hector, Paris and Cassandra. In the Iliad, she appears several times. She begs Hector not to go out and fight Achilles. She leads the women of Troy in prayer to the goddess Athena. When Hector dies, she cries out in grief from the walls. After the war, her story becomes even sadder. In the plays of the Athenian writer Euripides, she has lost her husband, her sons and her city. She is taken away as a captive. Euripides wrote two plays about her. They are among the most powerful plays about the suffering of war. Even so, Hecuba is not only a figure of sorrow. She is also strong and fierce. Yet for later Greeks, she stood for every mother who lost her children in war. Shakespeare's Hamlet even asks what Hecuba means to an actor who weeps for her.
===
IMG: prince-hector-armour-palace-steps-dawn | a tall Bronze Age warrior prince in bronze armour with a crested helmet under his arm standing on the stone steps of a palace at dawn, calm and serious, soft golden light
HEAD: Prince Hector was the greatest warrior of Troy and its main defender.
BODY: Hector was the eldest son of Priam and Hecuba. He was the leader of the Trojan army and its greatest fighter. In the Iliad, he is brave, loyal and thoughtful. He fights not for glory alone, but to protect his family and his city. He knows that Troy may fall. Yet he keeps fighting. He is also a loving husband and father. Many readers find Hector the most sympathetic hero in the whole poem. He is a good man in a terrible situation. He did not start the war. His brother Paris did. Even so, he takes responsibility for defending everyone. In the ancient world, Hector was admired by both Greeks and Romans. Later, in the Middle Ages, he was counted among the Nine Worthies, the nine greatest heroes of history. Yet in the Iliad, he is very human. He feels fear. At one moment, he even runs from Achilles before turning to face him.
===
IMG: paris-prince-archer-bow-palace-garden | a handsome young Bronze Age prince with long dark hair holding a bow in a palace garden with flowering trees, wearing a fine embroidered tunic, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Paris, the prince who started the war, was famous for his beauty, not his courage.
BODY: Paris was another son of Priam. He was also called Alexandros. In the poems, he is handsome, charming and fond of fine clothes. He is a skilled archer. Yet he is not much of a warrior. In the Iliad, he agrees to fight Menelaus in single combat to end the war. He is losing badly. Then the goddess Aphrodite snatches him away and carries him back to his bedroom. His brother Hector is furious with him. He tells Paris that it would have been better if he had never been born. Paris accepts the criticism with a smile. Even so, he is not a complete coward. Later in the war, according to later tales, it is his arrow that kills Achilles. Yet he remains the cause of Troy's troubles. His love for Helen began the war that destroyed his family. In Greek art and poetry, he is often shown as a beautiful young man who loved pleasure more than duty.
===
IMG: aeneas-young-warrior-old-father-shoulders | a young Bronze Age warrior carrying his old father on his shoulders and leading a small boy by the hand along a quiet road at dusk, soft blue evening light
HEAD: The Trojan prince Aeneas escaped the fall of the city, and the Romans claimed him as their ancestor.
BODY: Aeneas was a member of the Trojan royal family. He was a cousin of Hector. In the Iliad, he is a strong warrior, protected by the gods. His mother was said to be the goddess Aphrodite. When Troy fell, according to later stories, Aeneas escaped. He carried his old father, Anchises, on his shoulders. He led his young son by the hand. He also carried the sacred images of the gods of Troy. After long wanderings, he reached Italy. The Romans believed that he was the ancestor of their people. The Roman poet Virgil told his story in the Aeneid, written around twenty BC. In it, Aeneas visits Carthage and the underworld before arriving in Italy. The family of Julius Caesar even claimed to be descended from his son. Even so, these stories were legend, not history. Yet for the Romans, they mattered deeply. They made Rome the heir of Troy, rising from its ashes.

TOPIC: Helen and the cause of the war
===
IMG: helen-of-sparta-palace-window-spring | a beautiful young queen with long golden hair standing at a window in a Bronze Age palace in Sparta, looking out at green hills, wearing a long embroidered dress, soft spring light
HEAD: Helen of Sparta was said to be the most beautiful woman in the world.
BODY: In Greek myth, Helen was the daughter of the god Zeus and a mortal queen named Leda. She grew up in Sparta, in the south of Greece. She was famous for her beauty. Many princes wanted to marry her. Her father made all of them swear an oath. They promised to defend whoever she married. She chose, or was given to, Menelaus, the king of Sparta. They had a daughter named Hermione. Then a Trojan prince arrived as a guest. His name was Paris. When he left, Helen went with him to Troy. The Greeks saw this as a terrible insult. Because of the old oath, the princes of Greece gathered an army to bring her back. Even so, the poets did not agree on whether Helen went willingly. Some said she was carried off by force. Others said she fell in love. Yet the result was the same. Her departure began the Trojan War.
===
IMG: judgement-of-paris-three-goddesses-mountain | three tall graceful women in flowing ancient robes standing before a young shepherd on a green mountainside, a golden apple in his hand, sheep grazing nearby, soft golden light
HEAD: According to myth, the war began with a golden apple and a beauty contest between three goddesses.
BODY: The Greeks told a story about how the war began. At a wedding of the gods, the goddess of strife was not invited. Angry, she threw a golden apple among the guests. On it were the words, for the most beautiful. Three goddesses claimed it. They were Hera, the queen of the gods, Athena, goddess of wisdom, and Aphrodite, goddess of love. Zeus did not want to choose. So he sent them to a young shepherd on Mount Ida, near Troy. The shepherd was Paris, who had been raised away from the palace. Each goddess offered him a gift. Hera offered power. Athena offered wisdom and victory. Aphrodite offered him the most beautiful woman in the world. Paris chose Aphrodite. Even so, this story is not in the Iliad in detail. Homer mentions it only briefly. Yet it became one of the most popular scenes in art. Painters have shown the judgement of Paris for more than two thousand years.
===
IMG: helen-on-walls-old-men-watching-plain | a beautiful woman in a long white veil standing on the high stone walls of Troy beside a group of old men, looking out over a plain with distant armies, soft afternoon light
HEAD: When Helen walked on the walls of Troy, the old men said no one could blame them for fighting over her.
BODY: In the third book of the Iliad, there is a quiet and famous scene. Helen leaves her room and walks to the walls of Troy. There, King Priam sits with the old men of the city. They are too old to fight, but they love to talk. As Helen comes near, they speak softly to each other. They say that no one could blame the Trojans and Greeks for suffering so long for such a woman. She looks like a goddess. Yet they also say that she should go home, so that their children do not suffer. Priam calls Helen to sit beside him. He speaks kindly to her. He tells her that he does not blame her. He blames the gods. Then he asks her to name the Greek leaders on the plain below. Helen points them out one by one. Even so, she feels deep regret. She calls herself shameful and wishes she had died before coming to Troy.
===
IMG: menelaus-king-sparta-throne-hall | a strong Bronze Age king with red-brown hair sitting in a palace hall with painted walls and a central hearth, his helmet resting beside him, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: In the Odyssey, Helen and Menelaus are back together in Sparta, living calmly after the war.
BODY: Menelaus was the king of Sparta and the husband of Helen. He was the younger brother of Agamemnon. In the Iliad, he is a brave but not the greatest warrior. His fight with Paris is the closest the war comes to a quick end. After Troy fell, the poems say that Menelaus found Helen. Some later stories say that he meant to kill her, but dropped his sword when he saw her beauty. In any case, he took her home. Their journey was long. Storms drove them to Egypt, and they wandered for eight years. In the Odyssey, the young Telemachus visits them in Sparta. He finds them living calmly in their palace. Helen serves wine with a special drug that takes away sorrow. She and Menelaus tell stories about the war. Even so, there are hints of old tension between them. Yet the scene is peaceful. After so much suffering, the couple at the heart of the war have found a quiet home.
===
IMG: egyptian-temple-river-nile-helen-legend | a quiet ancient Egyptian temple with tall columns beside the River Nile, palm trees and a small boat on the water, soft golden evening light
HEAD: Some ancient writers claimed that Helen never went to Troy at all.
BODY: Not all ancient Greeks believed the usual story of Helen. The poet Stesichorus, in the sixth century BC, told a different version. In his story, Helen never went to Troy. The gods made a phantom, an image of Helen made from cloud. Paris took this phantom to Troy, while the real Helen was carried to Egypt. The Greeks and Trojans fought for ten years over an illusion. The historian Herodotus also reported that Egyptian priests told him Helen had stayed in Egypt. Later, the playwright Euripides wrote a whole play based on this idea. It was called Helen. In it, Menelaus finds his real wife in Egypt after the war. Even so, this version was never as popular as Homer's. Yet it carries a quiet and serious thought. If the war was fought over a phantom, then all those men died for nothing real. Some ancient writers seem to have wondered whether many wars are like that.

TOPIC: The gods above the plain
===
IMG: mount-ida-forest-peak-clouds-above-plain | a high forested mountain peak with soft clouds drifting around it, looking down over a wide green plain and a distant walled city by the sea, early morning light
HEAD: In the Iliad, Zeus watched the war from the top of Mount Ida, near Troy.
BODY: In Homer's world, the gods were never far away. They watched the war closely, and they took sides. Zeus, the king of the gods, had a special place to watch from. It was the peak of Mount Ida, a high mountain to the south-east of Troy. From there, he could see the whole plain, the city and the Greek ships. In the poem, he sometimes holds up a pair of golden scales. He places the fates of the two sides on them, or of two heroes. Whichever side sinks lower is doomed. Mount Ida is a real mountain. Today it is called Kaz Dağı. Its forests of pine and fir still cover its slopes. Even so, Zeus is not completely in control in the poem. The other gods argue with him and even trick him. Yet the image of the god on the mountain, watching the mortals below, gives the Iliad a sense of something larger than the war.
===
IMG: temple-athena-statue-robe-offering-women | Bronze Age women in long robes placing a richly embroidered robe on the knees of a seated wooden goddess statue inside a quiet temple, an indoor scene, enclosed by stone walls, a plain ceiling, soft lamplight
HEAD: The women of Troy offered their finest robe to the goddess Athena, but she refused their prayer.
BODY: Athena was the goddess of wisdom and of crafts like weaving. She was also a goddess of war. In the Trojan War, she supported the Greeks. Yet the Trojans also worshipped her. Her temple stood high in the citadel of Troy. In the sixth book of the Iliad, Hector asks his mother, Queen Hecuba, to pray to Athena. Hecuba goes to her storeroom. She chooses the largest and most beautiful robe she owns, embroidered by women from the city of Sidon. It shines like a star. With the older women of Troy, she carries it to the temple. They place it on the knees of the goddess's statue. They pray for mercy for the city, its wives and its children. Even so, the poem says plainly that Athena refused. It is a short and sad moment. Yet it shows how the Trojans hoped. Even in war, they turned to the gods with their most precious things.
===
IMG: apollo-temple-columns-hillside-near-troy | the ruins of an ancient stone temple with a few standing columns on a quiet hillside near the sea, wild flowers growing among the stones, soft warm evening light
HEAD: The god Apollo, who sided with the Trojans, was worshipped near Troy as a god of mice.
BODY: Apollo was the god of music, light, healing and plague. In the Iliad, he supports the Trojans. The poem begins with his anger. Agamemnon has insulted a priest of Apollo, named Chryses. The god comes down from Mount Olympus in fury. For nine days, he shoots arrows of plague into the Greek camp. Many men and animals die. Near Troy, Apollo was worshipped under a special name. He was called Apollo Smintheus. The word probably comes from an old word for mouse. Mice could spread disease and damage crops. So a god who could send or stop plagues of mice was important to farmers. Archaeologists have found the remains of a temple of Apollo Smintheus not far from Troy. It was built much later, in Greek times. Even so, the cult was very old. Yet the link between mice and plague is interesting. Ancient people seem to have noticed a connection long before science explained it.
===
IMG: hephaestus-forge-shield-glowing-workshop | an indoor scene, enclosed by stone walls, a plain ceiling, a strong bearded smith working at a glowing forge in a great workshop, hammering a large round shield of bronze, gold and silver, sparks and warm firelight
HEAD: The shield that the god Hephaestus made for Achilles showed a whole world at peace.
BODY: After Patroclus died wearing Achilles' armour, Achilles had nothing to fight in. So his mother Thetis went to Hephaestus, the god of fire and metalwork. She asked him to make new armour for her son. In the eighteenth book of the Iliad, Homer describes the shield in great detail. It is one of the most famous passages in ancient poetry. The shield is round and made of bronze, tin, gold and silver. On it, Hephaestus shows the earth, the sky, the sea, the sun, the moon and the stars. He shows two cities. One is at peace, with weddings, dancing and a trial in the marketplace. The other is at war. He shows ploughing, harvest and a vineyard, where young people carry baskets of grapes while a boy plays music. He shows cattle, sheep and a great dance. Even so, the shield is made for war. Yet on its surface, Homer gathers almost all of ordinary human life.
===
IMG: harvest-field-reapers-king-oak-tree-shade | reapers cutting golden wheat in a wide field with sickles, others binding sheaves, a meal being prepared under a large oak tree, an old king watching quietly, Bronze Age clothing, warm afternoon light
HEAD: One scene on the shield of Achilles shows a king standing happily in his field at harvest time.
BODY: The shield of Achilles contains many small, peaceful scenes. One of them is a harvest. Hired workers cut the ripe grain with sharp sickles. Others follow behind and tie the stalks into bundles. Children gather the grain in their arms and carry it to them. In the middle of the field stands the king. He holds his staff and says nothing. Yet his heart is glad as he watches the work. Under an oak tree, servants prepare a meal. They have killed an ox. Women sprinkle white barley for the workers' supper. Another scene shows a vineyard heavy with dark grapes. Young men and women carry the fruit in baskets. A boy plays a lyre and sings a harvest song, and the others dance along behind him. Even so, these scenes appear in the middle of a war poem. Yet perhaps that is the point. They remind the listener of everything the war is destroying.

IMG: alexander-the-great-visiting-tomb-mound-troy | a young king in a simple cloak and a few companions standing quietly beside a grassy burial mound on a plain near Troy, laying a wreath of flowers, the sea behind, soft morning light
HEAD: Alexander the Great visited Troy and honoured the tomb of Achilles before his great campaign.
BODY: For the ancient Greeks, Troy was a holy place. Many famous people visited it. In 480 BC, the Persian king Xerxes came on his way to invade Greece. The historian Herodotus says that he climbed up to the citadel and sacrificed a thousand oxen to Athena of Ilion. In 334 BC, a young Macedonian king crossed the Hellespont. He was Alexander, later called the Great. He had grown up reading Homer. According to ancient writers, he slept with a copy of the Iliad under his pillow. At Troy, he made offerings at the temple of Athena. He laid a wreath on a mound believed to be the tomb of Achilles. His friend Hephaestion did the same at the tomb of Patroclus. Alexander also took a shield from the temple, said to date from the Trojan War. Even so, the story may have been polished by later writers. Yet it shows how strongly Homer's heroes inspired people, even a thousand years later.
