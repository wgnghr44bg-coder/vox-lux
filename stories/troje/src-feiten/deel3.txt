TOPIC: The Greeks gather their ships
===
IMG: aulis-harbour-many-ships-beached-greek-army | hundreds of Bronze Age wooden ships with square sails pulled up on a long beach in a calm Greek bay, small figures moving between them, green hills behind, soft morning light
HEAD: The Iliad lists more than a thousand Greek ships that sailed to Troy.
BODY: In the second book of the Iliad, there is a long and unusual passage. It is called the Catalogue of Ships. It lists the Greek leaders who came to Troy, where they came from and how many ships each brought. Altogether, it counts one thousand one hundred and eighty-six ships. The list names many places in Greece, from big kingdoms to small villages. Some of these places were already forgotten by the time of Homer. Yet archaeologists have found that many of them were real Bronze Age settlements. This suggests that the catalogue may preserve a very old memory. Even so, the numbers are probably exaggerated. A fleet of more than a thousand ships would have been enormous for the time. Yet the catalogue gives a picture of a united Greek world, sailing together to war. According to the story, the fleet gathered at Aulis, a harbour on the east coast of Greece, before crossing the Aegean Sea.

IMG: lion-gate-mycenae-stone-lions-entrance | the ancient Lion Gate of Mycenae with two carved stone lions above a massive doorway of huge stone blocks, dry hills behind, warm golden afternoon light
HEAD: The leader of the Greeks, Agamemnon, was said to be king of Mycenae.
BODY: In the poems, the Greek army was led by Agamemnon. He was the brother of Menelaus and the most powerful king in Greece. His city was Mycenae, in the south of Greece. Mycenae was a real place. Its ruins still stand on a hill above a fertile plain. It was one of the greatest centres of the late Bronze Age. Its walls were built of stones so large that later Greeks believed giants had placed them. They called them Cyclopean walls, after the one-eyed giants of myth. The main entrance is the famous Lion Gate. Above the doorway, two stone lions face each other. It was built around twelve hundred and fifty BC. Even so, no one knows the names of the real kings of Mycenae. No inscriptions with their names have been found. Yet the city fits the picture in the poems. Homer called it Mycenae, rich in gold.

IMG: gold-mask-mycenae-museum-glass-case | a golden funeral mask of a bearded face with closed eyes displayed in a glass case in a quiet museum, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft warm light
HEAD: A golden mask found at Mycenae is called the Mask of Agamemnon, though it is much older than him.
BODY: In 1876, Heinrich Schliemann excavated at Mycenae. Inside the walls, he found a circle of royal graves. They were full of treasures. There were gold cups, swords, jewellery and five gold masks. The masks had been placed over the faces of dead kings. One of them shows a man with a fine beard and moustache. Schliemann believed he had found the face of Agamemnon. According to a famous story, he sent a message saying that he had gazed upon the face of Agamemnon. Historians doubt that he really used those words. Even so, the name stuck. The golden mask is still called the Mask of Agamemnon. Yet modern dating shows that it is around three hundred years older than the probable time of the Trojan War. It belonged to an unknown king who lived long before. Today, it is displayed in the National Archaeological Museum in Athens, where it is one of the most famous objects in Greece.

IMG: achilles-young-warrior-tent-beach-lyre | a young Bronze Age warrior with long hair sitting outside a tent on a beach, playing a lyre, his bronze armour resting beside him, the sea behind, soft evening light
HEAD: Achilles, the greatest Greek warrior, is shown in the Iliad playing the lyre in his tent.
BODY: Achilles was the greatest warrior of the Greeks. He was the son of a mortal king, Peleus, and a sea goddess named Thetis. He was swift, strong and almost impossible to defeat in battle. Yet in the Iliad, he is not only a fighter. When the Greek envoys come to visit him, they find him in his tent, playing a lyre. He is singing songs about the glorious deeds of heroes. His close friend Patroclus sits beside him, listening. It is a peaceful moment in a violent poem. Achilles has withdrawn from the fighting. He is angry with Agamemnon, who has taken from him a woman he had won as a prize. Even so, his anger will have terrible results. Many Greeks die while he refuses to fight. Yet the image of the young hero with his lyre is one of the most gentle in the poem. It reminds readers that warriors also loved music and stories.

IMG: thetis-dipping-baby-river-styx-mist | a sea goddess in flowing pale robes kneeling at the edge of a misty dark river, holding a baby by one heel above the water, soft blue twilight
HEAD: The famous story of Achilles' heel does not appear in Homer.
BODY: Many people know the story of Achilles' heel. According to this legend, his mother Thetis wanted to make him immortal. When he was a baby, she dipped him into the River Styx, the river of the underworld. Its waters made him invulnerable. But she held him by the heel, so that one small spot stayed dry. Later, Achilles was killed by an arrow in that heel. Today, an Achilles heel means a small weakness in something strong. Yet this story is not in the Iliad or the Odyssey. In Homer, Achilles can be wounded like any other man. The heel story appears much later, in the writings of the Roman poet Statius in the first century AD. Even so, it became one of the best-known parts of the legend. The tendon at the back of the ankle is still called the Achilles tendon. Yet the original Achilles of Homer had no magic protection. His courage was entirely his own.

IMG: odysseus-clever-king-ithaca-ship-planning | a bearded Bronze Age king with thoughtful eyes standing beside a beached wooden ship, studying the horizon, a small cloak over his shoulders, warm morning light
HEAD: Odysseus, king of the small island of Ithaca, was famous for his cleverness rather than his strength.
BODY: Among the Greek heroes, Odysseus stood out. He was not the strongest or the fastest. He was the cleverest. He ruled the small, rocky island of Ithaca, off the west coast of Greece. In the poems, he is a skilled speaker and a careful planner. He is often called the man of many ways, or of many turns. According to later stories, he did not want to go to war at all. When the Greek envoys came to take him, he pretended to be mad. He ploughed his field with an ox and a donkey and sowed salt instead of seed. But they placed his baby son in front of the plough. Odysseus turned aside, showing he was sane. So he had to go. Even so, he became one of the most valuable leaders of the army. It was Odysseus, in the story, who planned the wooden horse. Yet his greatest adventure came after the war, on the long journey home.

TOPIC: Ten years outside the walls
===
IMG: greek-camp-huts-beach-campfires-evening | a long row of simple wooden huts and campfires on a beach near Troy in the evening, beached ships behind them, warriors resting around the fires, the walled city faint on a hill in the distance, warm firelight
HEAD: The Greek army lived for years in a camp of huts beside their ships.
BODY: According to the poems, the Greeks did not live in tents for ten years. They built huts of wood and earth along the beach. The ships were pulled up on the sand behind them. In time, they also built a wall and a ditch to protect the camp. Each leader had his own hut and his own men. Achilles had a fine hut with a courtyard, built by his men from pine wood. Life in the camp was a mixture of fighting and waiting. Men went out to raid nearby towns for food and supplies. They held athletic games and feasts. They told stories and sang around the fires. Even so, the long war brought sickness and boredom. In the first book of the Iliad, a plague strikes the camp. Many men die. Yet the camp was also a kind of town. It had its own leaders, its own laws and its own daily routine, stretched out along the shore.

IMG: dendra-bronze-armour-panoply-museum | an ancient suit of bronze armour made of curved plates displayed in a quiet museum, with a matching helmet beside it, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft warm light
HEAD: Archaeologists have found a complete suit of Bronze Age armour made of bronze plates.
BODY: In the poems, heroes wear shining bronze armour. Archaeologists have found real examples from the Bronze Age. The most famous was found at a place called Dendra, in southern Greece, in 1960. It is a full suit of armour made of bronze plates. It covered the body from the neck to the knees. It also had shoulder pieces and a high collar. It dates from around fourteen hundred BC. It weighs about fifteen kilograms. For a long time, scholars thought it was too heavy to fight in. They believed it was used only by men in chariots. Then, in a study published in 2023, researchers tested a copy. Greek marines wore it for eleven hours while doing battlefield tasks. They found that a trained soldier could fight in it. Even so, such armour was rare and expensive. Most soldiers had much simpler protection. Yet the Dendra armour shows that Homer's shining bronze warriors were not only imagination.
===
IMG: chariot-two-horses-plain-troy-dust | a light two-wheeled Bronze Age war chariot pulled by two horses moving across a wide plain near Troy, a driver standing in it, dust in the warm afternoon light
HEAD: In the Iliad, heroes often ride to battle in chariots, then step down to fight on foot.
BODY: The chariot was the great war machine of the Bronze Age. It was light, with two wheels and a small platform. It was pulled by two horses. Kings and nobles of Egypt, the Hittite Empire and Mycenaean Greece all used chariots. In some battles, thousands of them were used. The great battle of Kadesh, in about twelve seventy-four BC, involved huge numbers of chariots on both sides. In the Iliad, however, chariots are used strangely. Heroes ride them to the battlefield like taxis. Then they get down and fight on foot. Some scholars think this shows that the poets no longer understood Bronze Age warfare. By their time, chariots were no longer used in battle. Even so, the poems keep the chariots as a sign of greatness. Each hero has his own driver and his own horses, often with names. Yet the gentle moments with the horses are often the most memorable parts of these scenes.

IMG: achilles-horses-weeping-patroclus-plain | two beautiful horses standing still with lowered heads on a quiet plain beside an empty chariot, a soft grey evening light, calm and sad
HEAD: In the Iliad, the horses of Achilles weep for the death of Patroclus.
BODY: Achilles had two immortal horses. Their names were Xanthus and Balius. They had been a gift from the gods to his father. When Achilles refused to fight, his friend Patroclus borrowed his armour and his chariot. He drove these horses into battle. Patroclus fought bravely, but he was killed by Hector. Then something strange happens in the poem. The horses stand still on the battlefield. They will not move. Tears run from their eyes onto the ground. Their manes hang down in the dust. Zeus sees them and feels pity. He says it was a mistake to give immortal horses to mortal men, who are the most wretched of all creatures. Even so, he gives them new strength so they can carry the driver to safety. Yet the image of the weeping horses stays with readers. It is one of the most touching moments in the Iliad, and it shows how deeply the poem feels the cost of war.

IMG: funeral-games-chariot-race-beach-crowd | a chariot race on a sandy beach with spectators in Bronze Age clothing cheering, the sea and beached ships behind, prizes of bronze tripods and cauldrons on display, bright afternoon light
HEAD: Achilles held athletic games in memory of Patroclus, with prizes for the winners.
BODY: After the death of Patroclus, Achilles held a great funeral. Then, according to the twenty-third book of the Iliad, he held athletic games in his friend's honour. There was a chariot race, a boxing match and a wrestling contest. There was a foot race, archery and spear throwing. Achilles offered fine prizes. There were bronze cauldrons, tripods, horses, gold and even a lump of iron, which was very valuable at the time. The games were lively and sometimes funny. In the foot race, one runner slipped in the dung of sacrificed cattle. The other heroes laughed at him. Old Nestor gave his son long advice about how to win the chariot race. Even so, the games ended peacefully. Achilles was generous and fair, even to those who lost. Some historians see these games as an early form of the athletic contests that later became the Olympic Games. They were a way to turn grief into something shared.

TOPIC: Hector, Achilles and Priam
===
IMG: hector-andromache-baby-farewell-gate | a Bronze Age warrior in bronze armour taking off his crested helmet to hold a small baby, his wife beside him with a nurse, at a great stone gate of the city, soft warm light
HEAD: Hector's baby son cried when he saw his father's helmet, so Hector took it off and laughed.
BODY: In the sixth book of the Iliad, there is one of the most famous scenes in all literature. Hector returns briefly to Troy from the battlefield. He looks for his wife, Andromache. He finds her at the Scaean Gate, with their baby son Astyanax in the arms of a nurse. Andromache begs Hector not to go back to the fighting. She has already lost her father and brothers. Hector is everything to her now. Hector answers gently. He says he must fight. Then he reaches for his little son. But the baby is frightened by the bronze helmet and the nodding horsehair plume. He shrinks back crying into the nurse's arms. Hector and Andromache both laugh. Hector takes off his helmet and lays it on the ground. He kisses his son and prays that the boy will one day be greater than his father. Even so, both parents know what may come. Yet for a moment, they are simply a family.

IMG: troy-walls-three-times-running-plain-dusk | the high stone walls of Bronze Age Troy seen from the plain at dusk, a dusty path running along the foot of the walls, a spring and fig tree nearby, soft fading light
HEAD: In the Iliad, Hector ran three times around the walls of Troy before facing Achilles.
BODY: After the death of Patroclus, Achilles returned to battle. He was filled with grief and rage. The Trojans fled back into their city. Only Hector stayed outside the gates. He knew he must face Achilles. But when Achilles came near, shining in new armour, Hector felt fear. He turned and ran. The two men ran three times around the walls of Troy. The poem describes the places they passed, including two springs where Trojan women used to wash their clothes in peaceful times. Homer notes this small detail as the two men run past. It is a reminder of a gentler world before the war. Even so, Hector finally stopped. He turned to face Achilles. He asked that whoever won would return the other's body to his family. Achilles refused. The fight was short. Hector was killed. Yet his last words warned Achilles that his own death was near.

IMG: priam-night-journey-wagon-gifts-moonlight | an old king driving a mule cart loaded with treasures and fine cloth across a dark plain at night, an older companion beside him, a soft silver moonlight and stars
HEAD: Old King Priam crossed the battlefield at night to beg Achilles for his son's body.
BODY: After Hector's death, Achilles kept his body in the Greek camp. He would not give it back. For days, Priam grieved. Then the gods decided that this must end. They told Priam to go to Achilles himself. It was a dangerous journey. Priam was old, and Achilles had killed many of his sons. Yet he agreed. He loaded a cart with rich gifts, cloaks, gold and fine cups. He set out at night with only one old herald. The god Hermes, disguised as a young man, guided them safely past the Greek guards. Priam arrived at the hut of Achilles. Achilles was sitting at his table after supper. No one saw the old king come in. Even so, Achilles did not strike him. Priam knelt before him. He took the hands that had killed his son, and he kissed them. It is one of the most powerful moments in all of Greek poetry.

IMG: priam-achilles-weeping-together-hut-lamplight | an old king and a young warrior sitting together in a simple wooden hut, both weeping quietly, an oil lamp burning between them, an indoor scene, enclosed by wooden walls, a plain ceiling, soft warm lamplight
HEAD: Achilles and Priam wept together, each for the people he had lost.
BODY: When Priam knelt and kissed his hands, Achilles was deeply moved. Priam asked him to remember his own father, Peleus. Peleus was also old, far away, waiting for a son who would not come home. Achilles thought of his father. He thought of Patroclus. He began to weep. Priam wept too, for Hector. The two enemies sat together and cried, each for his own loss. Then Achilles raised the old king gently. He agreed to return Hector's body. He even promised a truce of eleven days, so the Trojans could hold a proper funeral. Afterwards, they shared a meal. They looked at each other with wonder. Priam admired Achilles' beauty and strength. Achilles admired Priam's dignity. Even so, the war was not over. Yet in this moment, the poem shows something deeper than victory or defeat. It shows that enemies share the same grief, and the same humanity.

IMG: hector-funeral-pyre-trojans-dawn-plain | Trojans gathered quietly around a great funeral pyre on a plain at dawn, smoke rising slowly into a pale sky, women in mourning veils, the walls of Troy behind, soft grey light
HEAD: The Iliad ends with the funeral of Hector, not with the fall of Troy.
BODY: Priam brought Hector's body back to Troy. The people came out to meet him. Andromache, Hecuba and Helen each spoke words of mourning. Helen said that Hector had always been kind to her, when others blamed her. For nine days, the Trojans gathered wood. On the tenth day, they burned Hector's body on a great pyre. They put out the flames with wine. His brothers and friends gathered his white bones. They wrapped them in purple cloth and placed them in a golden box. They buried it under a mound of earth and stones. Then they held a funeral feast in Priam's palace. Even so, the poem says nothing more. It does not describe the death of Achilles or the fall of the city. It simply ends with the line about the funeral of Hector, tamer of horses. Yet many readers feel this is the perfect ending. The great war poem closes, not with triumph, but with quiet grief.

TOPIC: After the fall
===
IMG: andromache-ship-leaving-coast-veil-sea | a woman in a dark veil standing at the stern of a small Bronze Age ship, looking back towards a distant coastline, the calm sea around her, soft grey morning light
HEAD: After the fall of Troy, Hector's widow Andromache was taken across the sea to Greece.
BODY: The fall of Troy did not end the stories. Ancient poets and playwrights told what happened to the survivors. Their fates were mostly sad. Andromache, the wife of Hector, was taken as a captive. She was given to Neoptolemus, the son of Achilles. He took her to his kingdom in northwest Greece. The playwright Euripides wrote a play about her life there. Later stories give her a gentler ending. After Neoptolemus died, she married Helenus, a Trojan prince and a brother of Hector. Together they ruled a small kingdom. In Virgil's Aeneid, Aeneas visits them on his journey. He finds Andromache still mourning Hector. She has built a small copy of Troy, with a little river named after a Trojan one. Even so, she has made a life for herself. Yet she has never forgotten. Her story shows how the memory of a lost home could last a whole lifetime.
===
IMG: palace-of-nestor-pylos-ruins-roof-hill | the low stone foundations of a Bronze Age palace on a green hilltop in southern Greece, with a modern protective roof over the ruins, olive groves around, soft afternoon light
HEAD: The palace of old King Nestor, a wise Greek leader at Troy, has been found at Pylos.
BODY: Nestor was the oldest of the Greek leaders at Troy. In the poems, he is wise and very talkative. He often tells long stories about his youth. He gives advice that is sometimes useful and sometimes not. Of all the Greek leaders, Nestor had one of the happiest returns. He sailed home quickly and safely to his kingdom of Pylos. In the Odyssey, Telemachus visits him there. He finds the old king making a sacrifice on the beach with his sons. In 1939, the American archaeologist Carl Blegen began digging at a hill near Pylos. He found the remains of a large Bronze Age palace. It had a great hall with a round hearth, storerooms full of jars and even a bathtub made of clay. Archaeologists call it the Palace of Nestor. Even so, there is no proof that a real Nestor lived there. Yet the palace fits the poem very well. Visitors can still see the bathtub today.

IMG: linear-b-clay-tablets-museum-case | several small ancient clay tablets covered with lines of simple signs, displayed in a glass case in a quiet museum, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft warm light
HEAD: Clay tablets found at Pylos were written in the earliest known form of Greek.
BODY: On his very first day of digging at Pylos, Carl Blegen made an amazing find. In a small room near the entrance of the palace, he found hundreds of clay tablets. They were covered with an unknown script. Scholars called it Linear B. Similar tablets had been found on Crete. For years, no one could read them. Then, in 1952, a young English architect named Michael Ventris cracked the code. He showed that Linear B was an early form of Greek. It was about five hundred years older than Homer. The tablets were not poems or stories. They were lists. They recorded sheep, grain, oil, bronze, chariot wheels and workers. One tablet lists women who worked as spinners and weavers. Even so, these dry lists were thrilling. They proved that people spoke Greek in the age of the Trojan War. Yet they also showed how different the real palaces were. They were busy offices, full of careful record-keepers.

IMG: aeschylus-theatre-stone-seats-hillside | an ancient Greek stone theatre with curved rows of seats on a hillside, a round stage area below, quiet and empty, soft golden evening light
HEAD: The story of Agamemnon's return home became one of the greatest plays of ancient Athens.
BODY: Agamemnon, the leader of the Greeks, came home from Troy as a victor. Yet his return was not happy. While he was away, his wife Clytemnestra had turned against him. She had never forgiven him for sacrificing their daughter to the gods before the war. According to the myths, she and her lover killed Agamemnon soon after he came home. Their son Orestes later took revenge. These events became the subject of a famous set of three plays. They were written by the Athenian playwright Aeschylus and performed in 458 BC. Together they are called the Oresteia. They are the only complete set of three Greek tragedies that survives. The plays end not with more killing, but with a trial. The goddess Athena sets up a court of law in Athens to judge Orestes. Even so, the plays are dark and powerful. Yet they end with the hope that justice can replace revenge.

IMG: roman-city-ilium-temple-columns-hill | a Roman city with marble temples, columns and a small theatre built on a low hill above a wide green plain near the sea, soft afternoon light
HEAD: The Romans believed they were descended from the Trojans, and they rebuilt Troy as a proud Roman city.
BODY: Long after the Bronze Age, people lived on the hill of Troy again. Greeks built a city there and called it Ilion. Later, under the Romans, it was called Ilium. The Romans had a special love for the place. Because of the legend of Aeneas, they saw Troy as their mother city. Julius Caesar visited the site and gave it privileges. The emperor Augustus paid for new buildings. Ilium received a theatre, temples, baths and a council house. Roman visitors came to see the places named in Homer. Local guides showed them the tombs of heroes. Even so, the Roman city was quite small. It was never as important as the legend. Yet according to some later writers, the emperor Constantine at first thought of building his new capital near Troy. He chose Byzantium instead, which became Constantinople. Troy remained a quiet place of memory, its old walls hidden under the newer town.

TOPIC: The long voyage of Odysseus
===
IMG: odysseus-ship-leaving-troy-coast-sunrise | a single small Bronze Age wooden ship with a square sail and oars leaving a coastline at sunrise, the ruins of a city smoking faintly on a distant hill, calm sea, soft pink light
HEAD: After the war, Odysseus took ten years to get home to Ithaca.
BODY: When Troy fell, the Greek heroes set off for home. Many had difficult journeys. Some never arrived. The journey of Odysseus was the longest of all. His story is told in the Odyssey, Homer's second great poem. It has about twelve thousand lines. Odysseus spent ten years trying to get home. Together with ten years at Troy, he was away from Ithaca for twenty years. Along the way, he lost all his ships and all his men. He met monsters, witches, goddesses and ghosts. He visited strange lands at the edges of the world. Even so, the Odyssey is not only an adventure story. It is about home, about patience and about recognising those we love. Yet its adventures have become some of the most famous tales ever told. Today, the word odyssey means any long and eventful journey. The poem has been retold many times, in books, plays and films, right up to the present day.

IMG: cyclops-cave-sheep-giant-shadow-entrance | the entrance of a large cave on a rocky island with flocks of sheep grazing outside, a huge shadowy figure far inside, wheels of cheese stacked near the entrance, soft evening light
HEAD: Odysseus escaped from the Cyclops by telling him that his name was Nobody.
BODY: One of the most famous adventures happens on an island of giants. Odysseus and some of his men enter a large cave. It is full of cheeses and lambs. It belongs to a Cyclops, a one-eyed giant named Polyphemus. When the giant returns with his sheep, he blocks the entrance with a huge stone. The men are trapped. Odysseus thinks of a plan. He gives the giant strong wine. The Cyclops asks his name. Odysseus says that his name is Nobody. When the giant falls asleep, Odysseus and his men blind his single eye with a sharpened stake. The Cyclops cries out for help. Other giants come and ask who is hurting him. He answers that Nobody is hurting him. So they go away. In the morning, the men escape by clinging to the bellies of the sheep. Even so, Odysseus makes a mistake. As he sails away, he shouts his real name. The giant's father, the sea god Poseidon, never forgives him.

IMG: circe-island-palace-forest-lions-wolves | a stone palace in a clearing of a dense forest on an island, gentle lions and wolves lying calmly outside the door, smoke rising from the roof, soft golden afternoon light
HEAD: On the island of Circe, Odysseus's men were turned into pigs.
BODY: After many adventures, Odysseus reached an island ruled by a goddess named Circe. She lived in a stone house in the middle of a forest. Around it lay wolves and lions. They were strangely tame. They wagged their tails like dogs. Odysseus sent some of his men to explore. Circe welcomed them and gave them food and wine. But the food contained a magic drug. When she touched them with her wand, they turned into pigs. Only one man escaped to warn Odysseus. Odysseus went to rescue them. On the way, the god Hermes gave him a special herb called moly. It protected him from Circe's magic. When her drug did not work on him, Circe was amazed. She agreed to turn his men back into humans. Even so, the story does not end in hatred. Odysseus and his men stayed with Circe for a whole year. They feasted and rested before continuing their journey.

HEAD: To hear the song of the Sirens safely, Odysseus had himself tied to the mast.
BODY: The Sirens were mysterious creatures who lived on an island in the sea. They sang a song so beautiful that no sailor could resist it. Sailors who heard it steered towards them and never came home. Circe warned Odysseus about them. She told him what to do. As his ship came near the island, Odysseus gave his men soft wax. They put it in their ears so that they could not hear. But Odysseus wanted to hear the song. So he told his men to tie him tightly to the mast. He ordered them not to untie him, whatever he said. As they rowed past, the Sirens began to sing. They promised Odysseus wisdom and knowledge of all things. He begged his men to set him free. Instead, they tied him tighter. The ship passed safely. Even so, the Sirens remain a powerful image. Today, a siren song means a tempting call that leads to danger.

HEAD: The nymph Calypso kept Odysseus on her island for seven years and offered to make him immortal.
BODY: After his last ship was destroyed, Odysseus drifted alone across the sea. He reached an island called Ogygia. It was the home of a nymph named Calypso. Her island was beautiful. Homer describes her cave, covered with grape vines, with springs of clear water and soft meadows of violets and parsley around it. Calypso loved Odysseus. She kept him there for seven years. She offered to make him immortal and ageless if he would stay with her forever. Yet Odysseus refused. Every day, he sat on the shore and looked out to sea, weeping. He longed to see the smoke rising from his home on Ithaca. Finally, the gods ordered Calypso to let him go. She helped him build a raft. Even so, she asked him why he preferred his mortal wife to a goddess. Odysseus answered kindly. He said that Penelope could not compare with her beauty, yet he still longed for home.

IMG: penelope-weaving-loom-night-lamp-palace | a woman at a large upright loom in a quiet palace room at night, carefully unpicking threads by the light of a single oil lamp, an indoor scene, enclosed by stone walls, a plain wooden ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: Penelope kept her suitors waiting by weaving a cloth by day and unpicking it by night.
BODY: While Odysseus was away, his wife Penelope waited for him on Ithaca. Many years passed. Most people believed Odysseus was dead. More than a hundred young men from the islands came to her palace. Each wanted to marry her and become king. They stayed in the palace, eating and drinking at her expense. Penelope did not want to marry any of them. So she made a clever plan. She said she would choose a husband only after she had woven a burial cloth for Odysseus's old father. Every day she wove at her loom. Every night, by lamplight, she secretly unpicked what she had woven. For three years, the cloth was never finished. Then one of her maids revealed the trick. Even so, Penelope still found ways to delay. In the poem, she is as clever as her husband. Yet her patience and loyalty are what most readers remember about her.

HEAD: When Odysseus came home in disguise, his old dog Argos recognised him and wagged his tail.
BODY: After twenty years, Odysseus finally reached Ithaca. The goddess Athena disguised him as an old beggar, so that no one would recognise him. He walked towards his palace. Outside the door, on a pile of dung, lay an old dog. His name was Argos. Odysseus had raised him as a puppy before leaving for Troy. Argos had once been a fine hunting dog. Now he was old and neglected, covered in fleas. Yet when Argos heard his master's voice, he lifted his head. He wagged his tail and dropped his ears. He had no strength to come closer. Odysseus saw him. He secretly wiped away a tear, so that no one would notice. Then the old dog died, having seen his master again after twenty years. Even so, the scene is very short. It is only a few lines long. Yet it is one of the most loved passages in the whole poem. Many readers remember Argos long after they forget the monsters.

HEAD: Penelope tested Odysseus with a secret about their bed, which was built around a living olive tree.
BODY: When Odysseus finally revealed himself, Penelope did not believe him at once. Twenty years had passed. Perhaps a god was tricking her. So she set a test. She told her maid to move the great bed out of their bedroom for the stranger. Odysseus was suddenly angry. He said no one could move that bed. He had built it himself. One of its posts was the trunk of a living olive tree, still rooted in the ground. He had built the bedroom around it. Only he and Penelope knew this secret. When she heard him describe the bed, Penelope knew it was really him. She ran to him and threw her arms around him. They both wept. The goddess Athena held back the dawn so their night together would be longer. Even so, they had many stories to tell each other. Yet the poem lets them simply rest. The long journey of Odysseus ends where it began, at home.

TOPIC: The search for Troy
===
IMG: heinrich-schliemann-trench-workers-hisarlik-1870s | a man in a 19th-century suit and hat standing beside a deep excavation trench on a hill with many workers digging and carrying baskets, a wide plain beyond, bright morning light
HEAD: In 1870, a German businessman named Heinrich Schliemann began digging at Hisarlik.
BODY: For a long time, many scholars thought Troy was only a legend. Heinrich Schliemann believed it was real. He was a German businessman who had made a fortune in trade. He loved Homer's poems and was determined to find Troy. In 1870, he began digging at the hill of Hisarlik. He had been encouraged by an Englishman named Frank Calvert. Calvert owned part of the hill and had already done some digging there himself. Schliemann worked on a huge scale. He hired many workers and cut a great trench through the middle of the hill. He was looking for the city of Priam. He believed it would be near the bottom. Even so, his methods were rough. He destroyed many upper layers to reach the lower ones. Some of the Troy he was looking for may have been lost in the process. Yet he proved that a great ancient city had stood on the hill. His work made Troy famous all over the world.

IMG: gold-jewellery-diadem-ancient-treasure-table | a collection of ancient gold jewellery laid out on a dark cloth, including a delicate gold headdress made of many hanging chains, earrings and small cups, soft warm light
HEAD: Schliemann found a hoard of gold that he called the Treasure of Priam, though it was a thousand years too old.
BODY: In 1873, near the end of his excavation, Schliemann made a spectacular find. Near the walls of the citadel, he found a hoard of treasure. There were gold and silver cups, bronze weapons and thousands of small gold beads. There were two gold headdresses made of many fine chains. Schliemann called it the Treasure of Priam. He believed it belonged to the king of Troy. A famous photograph shows his Greek wife, Sophia, wearing the headdress. Schliemann smuggled the treasure out of the Ottoman Empire. It was later given to Berlin. Even so, modern research shows that the treasure is much older than Priam. It belongs to Troy Two, around twenty-four hundred BC. That is more than a thousand years before the Trojan War. In 1945, at the end of the Second World War, the treasure vanished from Berlin. For decades, its fate was unknown. In 1993, it was revealed to be in a museum in Moscow, where it remains today.

IMG: frank-calvert-farmhouse-garden-dardanelles | a quiet 19th-century farmhouse with a garden and olive trees on a hillside overlooking the Dardanelles strait, a man in a straw hat reading under a tree, soft evening light
HEAD: An Englishman named Frank Calvert pointed to Hisarlik as Troy before Schliemann arrived.
BODY: Frank Calvert is often forgotten in the story of Troy. He was an Englishman who lived near the Dardanelles. His family worked as consuls and owned land in the area. Calvert was a keen amateur archaeologist. He studied the old sites around the Trojan plain. He became convinced that Hisarlik was the site of Troy. He even bought part of the hill and dug some trial trenches there. But he did not have enough money for a large excavation. When Schliemann visited the area in 1868, Calvert showed him Hisarlik. He shared his ideas and encouraged him to dig. Schliemann followed his advice. Even so, in his books, Schliemann gave Calvert little credit. He presented the discovery as his own. Calvert felt hurt by this. Yet historians today recognise his important role. Without Frank Calvert, the search for Troy might have taken much longer.

IMG: modern-archaeologists-careful-excavation-troy | archaeologists in sun hats carefully brushing soil from ancient stone walls in a neatly marked excavation area on a hill, measuring tapes and sieves nearby, bright morning light
HEAD: Later archaeologists spent more than a century studying the layers that Schliemann had cut through.
BODY: After Schliemann, others continued to dig at Troy. His assistant, Wilhelm Dörpfeld, studied the layers more carefully. He realised that Troy Six was the city of the late Bronze Age. In the 1930s, an American team led by Carl Blegen made very careful excavations. Blegen suggested that Troy Seven A, which ended in fire, was the city of the Trojan War. From 1988, the German archaeologist Manfred Korfmann led a large international team. They used modern methods, such as measuring the magnetism of the ground to find buried walls. This was how they discovered the lower town. Since 2014, Turkish archaeologists have continued the work. Each new season adds small pieces to the picture. Even so, the big question remains open. No one can prove that a real Trojan War took place. Most scholars think there may have been real conflicts at Troy. Yet the poems are not history. They are memories, shaped and reshaped by singers over centuries.
===
IMG: bronze-age-collapse-abandoned-palace-ruins-dusk | the quiet ruins of a Bronze Age palace with fallen stone walls and burned beams, grass growing between the stones, soft fading dusk light, calm and still
HEAD: Around the time Troy fell, many great cities of the Bronze Age were destroyed or abandoned.
BODY: The end of Troy Seven A came around eleven hundred and eighty BC. It was not an isolated event. In the same decades, much of the eastern Mediterranean was in crisis. The great palaces of Mycenaean Greece were destroyed or abandoned. The Hittite Empire collapsed, and its capital was burned. Cities in Syria and Cyprus were destroyed. Egypt survived, but only after fighting off invaders. Egyptian inscriptions describe groups of people moving by land and sea, whom historians call the Sea Peoples. Scholars still debate the causes of this collapse. Drought, earthquakes, famine, rebellion and broken trade routes may all have played a part. Even so, the result was clear. Writing disappeared from Greece for several centuries. Trade shrank. Life became simpler and poorer. Yet out of this dark age came the songs that became Homer's poems. They remembered a lost world of rich palaces and great kings.

IMG: troy-ruins-today-wooden-horse-replica-visitors | the ancient stone ruins of Troy on a grassy hill today with a large wooden horse replica standing at the edge of the site, a few visitors walking on paths, soft golden evening light
HEAD: Today, Troy is a World Heritage Site, and a large wooden horse greets visitors at the entrance.
BODY: Today, the ruins of Troy are open to visitors. They lie in the Turkish province of Çanakkale, near the Dardanelles. In 1998, Troy was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Visitors walk along paths through the different layers of the city. They see the sloping walls of Troy Six, the old ramp of Troy Two and the remains of temples from Greek and Roman times. Near the entrance stands a large wooden horse, built for tourists. Children love to climb inside it. In the nearby city of Çanakkale, there is another wooden horse. It was used in the 2004 film Troy and was later given to the city. A new museum opened near the site in 2018. It displays many objects found during the excavations. Even so, the site itself is quiet and modest. Yet standing on the walls, looking out over the plain towards the sea, it is easy to imagine the ships on the shore and the songs that began there.
