{img:01-samurai-house-morning-tatami-sliding-doors-1600}

In the spring of 1600, the cherry trees of Japan were in flower below the white walls of the castles. A samurai in lacquered armour stood quietly under the blossoms, his sword in its sheath, while the long age of war drew towards its end.

[long-pause]

In 1600, the day of a samurai began early, in a quiet wooden house near his lord's castle. The day of a samurai began before sunrise. Most samurai did not live in grand halls. They lived in modest wooden houses close to the castle of their lord. The floors were covered with thick mats of woven straw, called tatami. Light came in through sliding doors of thin paper on wooden frames. In the morning, the bedding was folded and put away in a cupboard. The same room then served for meals, for work and for receiving guests. A samurai washed his face and hands with cold water from a well. He dressed in a kimono and wide trousers called hakama. A servant or his wife helped him tie his hair in the old warrior style. Yet the house was simple, almost bare. Furniture was rare, and a few objects were kept with great care. This simplicity was not a sign of poverty. It was the way a well-ordered home was meant to look.

[long-pause]

{img:02-samurai-tying-topknot-wife-helping-morning}

A samurai wore his hair in a topknot, and the front of his head was often shaved. The hairstyle of a samurai was a sign of his rank. Many men shaved the front and top of the head. The long hair at the back was oiled, gathered and folded into a small knot. This style was called the chonmage. Historians believe it began in times of war. A shaved head stayed cooler under a heavy iron helmet. By 1600, the topknot had become part of daily life, even in times of peace. A barber or a family member kept it neat every few days. European visitors found the style very strange. The Jesuit Luis Frois wrote a long list of differences between Europe and Japan. He noted that Europeans grew their hair, while Japanese men carefully plucked and shaved it. Even so, the topknot was worn with pride. To cut it off was a deep disgrace. It meant that a man had left the world of the warrior.

[long-pause]

For those who enjoy a slow journey to old Japan, a subscription keeps the next quiet story close at hand.

[long-pause]

{img:03-samurai-two-swords-sash-kimono-hakama-garden}

Before leaving the house, a samurai placed two swords through his sash. Every samurai carried two swords on his left side. The long sword was the katana. The short sword was the wakizashi. Both were pushed through the sash, with the cutting edge facing upward. This allowed a sword to be drawn and used in one smooth movement. The pair of swords was called the daisho, meaning large and small. In 1600, the right to wear two swords was becoming a mark of the samurai class. A few years earlier, the great ruler Toyotomi Hideyoshi had ordered farmers to hand in their swords. Officials travelled from village to village to collect them. Yet in daily life, the swords were rarely drawn. Inside a house, the long sword was laid on a rack by the door. The short sword stayed in the sash. It was a quiet reminder of who its owner was.

[long-pause]

{img:04-samurai-walking-to-castle-gate-morning-street}

Most samurai spent their days serving their lord as officials, guards and clerks. In 1600, many samurai worked more with brush and paper than with swords. A great lord, called a daimyo, needed many officials. Some samurai collected taxes in rice. Others kept records of land and harvests. Some guarded the gates of the castle or trained young soldiers. Others carried messages across the country. Each morning, men walked from their houses to the castle to begin their duties. Their rank decided where they lived, what they wore and how much rice they received. Historians note that a samurai's income was measured in koku. One koku was the amount of rice thought to feed one person for a year. A great lord might hold many thousands of koku. An ordinary samurai might receive only a few dozen. Even so, every samurai was bound to his lord by loyalty. That bond shaped every part of his life.

[long-pause]

{img:05-samurai-practising-archery-courtyard-target}

Part of every day was spent in training, with the sword, the spear and the bow. A samurai was expected to stay ready for war. Many spent part of each day in practice. They trained with the sword, the spear and the long bow. The Japanese bow was taller than a man and made of bamboo and wood. Archers held it below the middle, which gave it a special shape. Riding was also important. Older warriors taught young men to shoot arrows from a moving horse. In 1600, the spear and the gun were the most important weapons on the battlefield. Yet the sword kept a special place. Schools of swordsmanship were growing in number. Teachers passed on their methods to a small circle of students. Training was not only about fighting. It was meant to build calm, patience and self-control. Those qualities were thought to matter in peace as much as in war.

[long-pause]

{img:06-samurai-writing-with-brush-low-desk-scroll}

Many samurai were also expected to read, write and enjoy poetry. In the afternoon, a samurai might sit at a low desk with an ink brush. Writing in Japan used thousands of Chinese characters, together with simpler Japanese signs. Learning them took many years. Samurai boys began their lessons young, often at a Buddhist temple. They copied old texts again and again until the brush moved easily. Educated warriors also read Chinese classics and books on strategy. Many wrote short poems. Some great generals left poems written just before their death. Historians note that the ideal warrior was skilled in both the brush and the sword. This idea was very old in Japan. Even so, not every samurai was well educated in 1600. Years of war had left little time for study. In the long peace that followed, learning would become far more common among the samurai class.

[long-pause]

{img:07-samurai-family-evening-meal-lantern-house}

In the evening, the family ate together, and the house grew quiet early. When the day's work was done, a samurai returned home before dark. Each person ate from a small tray placed on the floor. A simple evening meal included rice, soup, pickled vegetables and perhaps some fish. Light came from small oil lamps or candles inside paper lanterns. Lamp oil and candles were costly, so most families went to bed soon after dark. Thick bedding was laid out on the tatami mats. In winter, a small charcoal brazier gave a little warmth. The paper walls let in the sounds of the night. Crickets sang in the garden in summer. In autumn, the wind moved through the pine trees. A night watchman might pass along the street, striking two wooden blocks together. The sound told the town that all was well. Then the house slept until dawn.

[long-pause]

{img:08-swordsmith-forging-blade-workshop-sparks}

A Japanese sword was made by hand, over many weeks, by a master swordsmith. Making a sword was slow and careful work. The swordsmith heated steel in a charcoal fire until it glowed. Then he hammered it, folded it and hammered it again. This was repeated many times. Folding helped to remove impurities and spread the carbon evenly through the steel. The smith often made the blade from steels of different hardness. A softer core kept the sword from breaking. A harder edge allowed it to stay very sharp. Before the final cooling, the blade was coated with clay. A thin layer covered the edge and a thicker layer covered the back. When the hot blade was plunged into water, the edge cooled faster. This gave the sword its gentle curve. Yet the work was not finished. A polisher then spent days bringing out the line of the hardened edge.

[long-pause]

{img:09-polished-katana-blade-on-stand-lamp}

A good sword was treated as a family treasure and passed down through the generations. A finished blade could be worth more than a house. The best swords came from famous schools of smiths in a few regions. Some blades were already centuries old by 1600. They had been carried by grandfathers and great-grandfathers. A sword was cleaned with care, oiled and wrapped in silk. Some were given as rewards by great lords. Others were offered as gifts to shrines and temples. Hideyoshi collected famous swords, and so did Tokugawa Ieyasu. Many of the blades they owned still survive. The idea that the sword was the soul of the samurai became popular in later centuries. Yet even in 1600, a sword was more than a weapon. It stood for the honour of the family. Losing it, or using it badly, brought shame on the whole house.

[long-pause]

{img:10-samurai-armour-displayed-on-stand-lacquered-plates}

Samurai armour was made of many small plates, laced together with silk cords. Japanese armour looked very different from the armour of European knights. It was made from many small plates of iron or hardened leather. The plates were lacquered to protect them from rain. Then they were laced together with coloured cords of silk or leather. This made the armour flexible and lighter than it looked. By 1600, many suits used larger solid plates on the chest, which could stop a musket ball better. The helmet often carried a crest. Some crests were simple. Others were shaped like horns, moons or flames. They helped soldiers recognise their commanders in battle. A good suit of armour was costly, and it was kept with great care. Even so, ordinary foot soldiers wore much simpler armour. It was often a plain chest plate and a light helmet shaped like a cone.

[long-pause]

{img:11-foot-soldiers-ashigaru-spears-banners-field}

By 1600, most soldiers in Japanese armies were foot soldiers called ashigaru. The armies of 1600 were very large. Most of the men were not mounted warriors. They were foot soldiers, called ashigaru, which means light feet. Many came from farming families. They fought in tight rows with very long spears. Some spears were more than five metres long. Others carried bows or guns. On their backs, many soldiers wore tall narrow banners. The banners showed the crest of their lord. On a crowded battlefield, they helped commanders see where each unit stood. Historians note that the rise of the foot soldier changed warfare in Japan. Battles were no longer won by a few brave warriors. They were won by discipline, numbers and good supplies. Yet the mounted samurai still led the armies. They gave the orders, and they took the greatest honours.

[long-pause]

{img:12-portuguese-matchlock-gun-tanegashima-island-1543}

Guns had arrived in Japan about sixty years earlier, on a small southern island. In 1543, a Chinese ship with a few Portuguese traders on board landed on the island of Tanegashima. The Portuguese carried matchlock guns. The local lord was so impressed that he bought two of them. He ordered his smiths to study them and make copies. Within a few years, Japanese craftsmen were making guns of their own. For a long time, the Japanese simply called guns tanegashima, after the island. By 1600, there were probably tens of thousands of guns in Japan. Historians believe Japan had more guns than many countries in Europe at that time. A matchlock was slow to load. Rain could put out its burning cord. Yet in large numbers, fired in turns, guns could break a charge of horsemen. This new weapon helped bring the long age of war to an end.

[long-pause]

{img:13-samurai-on-horseback-crest-banner-field}

Horses were important to the samurai, though Japanese horses were small and sturdy. For centuries, the samurai had fought as mounted archers. By 1600, horses were still a sign of rank. Only higher samurai rode into battle. Japanese horses were smaller than the great war horses of Europe. Archaeologists who studied horse bones from this period found animals about the size of ponies. They were strong and sure-footed on mountain paths. Horses were not usually shod with iron. Instead, they sometimes wore sandals woven from straw. Riders sat in deep wooden saddles with wide stirrups. In peace, horses were used for travel and for ceremonies. Riding well was part of a samurai's education. Yet horses were costly to keep. A poor samurai often had to walk, even on long journeys. Only the wealthy could afford a stable, grooms and fodder for the winter.

[long-pause]

{img:14-white-castle-keep-stone-base-curved-roofs-1600}

In 1600, the castle was the heart of every great lord's domain. The great castles of Japan were built in the last decades of the age of war. They stood on hills or in the middle of plains, close to rivers and roads. The main tower, called the tenshu, rose many floors high. Its walls were covered in white plaster. Its roofs were made of grey clay tiles with curved edges. Below it lay huge walls of fitted stone. Some stones weighed many tons and were moved without machines. Thousands of workers dragged them on sledges and rollers. Around the castle ran moats, gates and narrow paths. They were designed to confuse and slow down any attacker. Yet the castle was also a home and an office. The lord lived there with his family and servants. From its windows, he could look out over the land he ruled.

[long-pause]

{img:15-castle-stone-walls-moat-gate-bridge}

The stone walls of a castle were built with great skill and curved gently upward. Japanese castle walls had a special shape. They sloped steeply and then curved upward near the top, like the line of a fan. This shape made them very strong. It also made them hard to climb. Builders fitted large stones together without mortar. Smaller stones filled the gaps behind them. Walls built in this way could survive earthquakes, which were common in Japan. The work was done by skilled teams of stonemasons. Some groups of masons were famous across the country. Lords sometimes marked their stones with small carved signs. They showed which family had supplied each block. Many of these marks can still be seen. Yet the walls were only the first line of defence. Behind them lay a maze of courtyards, gates and towers.

[long-pause]

{img:16-castle-interior-painted-screens-gold-leaf-hall}

Inside, the halls of the greatest castles shone with gold and painted screens. The rooms where a lord received his guests were richly decorated. Sliding doors and folding screens were covered in gold leaf. On them, painters showed pine trees, flowering plums, tigers, cranes and mountains. The most famous painters of the time worked for the great lords. The Kano school of painters was especially admired. In the hall, everyone sat on the floor in a fixed order. The lord sat at the far end, on a raised level. His most important followers sat closest to him. Others knelt further back, according to their rank. A visitor bowed deeply on entering. Every movement followed careful rules. Yet behind the golden halls, the living rooms were much simpler. There, the lord's family lived quietly, among plain wood, paper and straw mats.

[long-pause]

{img:17-castle-town-streets-shops-wooden-houses-busy}

Around each castle grew a town of samurai, merchants and craftsmen. A castle needed people to serve it. So around each castle, a town slowly grew. Lords often ordered merchants and craftsmen to move there. The town was carefully planned. Samurai lived closest to the castle, in streets of walled houses. Further out lived the townspeople. Carpenters, smiths, dyers and cooks often had their own streets. Temples were placed along the edge of the town. In an attack, their strong walls and wide grounds could help defend it. Historians call these places castle towns. Many of Japan's large cities began in this way. Osaka, Nagoya and Kanazawa all grew around castles. Even so, in 1600 many of these towns were still young. Some were only a few years old. The sound of hammers and saws filled their streets from morning to evening.

[long-pause]

{img:18-osaka-castle-1600-great-keep-black-gold}

The largest castle in Japan in 1600 was Osaka Castle, built by Toyotomi Hideyoshi. Toyotomi Hideyoshi began building Osaka Castle in 1583. He wanted a fortress that showed his power to the whole country. Tens of thousands of workers took part. Stones were brought by boat from far away. The main tower was decorated with gold. Its outer walls were dark, with golden ornaments shining in the sun. Hideyoshi died in 1598. His young son Hideyori lived in the castle with his mother, Lady Yodo. In 1600, Osaka was the richest city in Japan. Merchants from across the land traded rice, cloth, oil and salt there. Ships came and went along its rivers. Yet the castle's future was uncertain. Many lords wondered who would truly rule Japan now that Hideyoshi was gone. The answer would come later that same year, on a misty battlefield.

[long-pause]

{img:19-castle-garden-pond-stone-bridge-maple}

Many castles also had peaceful gardens, made for walking, resting and quiet thought. Behind the walls of many castles lay gardens. They were designed to feel calm and natural. A garden might hold a pond with carp, small islands and stone bridges. Paths of stepping stones wound between trees and mossy rocks. Stone lanterns stood among the plants. Some gardens copied famous landscapes in miniature. A small hill might stand for a mountain. A stretch of raked gravel might stand for the sea. Lords walked in these gardens to rest. They also received guests there for tea or poetry. Gardeners worked every day to keep each plant in shape. Historians note that many garden ideas came from Zen Buddhist temples. Yet castle gardens were also a sign of wealth. Only a powerful lord could set aside so much land for beauty alone.

[long-pause]

{img:20-castle-guards-night-lanterns-gate-watch}

At night, guards kept watch at the gates, and the castle fell silent. When darkness came, the castle gates were closed. Guards took their places by the entrances and on the walls. They carried spears and paper lanterns. Every few hours, a new group of men took over. Fire was the greatest fear in a castle built of wood. Watchmen walked along the paths to check that no lamp or brazier had been left burning. Water was stored in large barrels near the buildings. In the town below, the streets grew quiet. Only the night watchman passed by, calling out and clapping his wooden blocks. Historians note that many castles burned down in later years. Lightning, accidents and war all took their toll. Yet in the calm of a peaceful night, the castle stood dark and still above the sleeping town, its white walls pale in the moonlight.

[long-pause]

{img:21-farming-village-thatched-houses-rice-paddies}

Most people in Japan in 1600 were farmers, living in small villages among the rice fields. In 1600, Japan probably had between twelve and eighteen million people. Historians believe that about four out of five were farmers. They lived in villages scattered across the valleys and plains. Farmhouses were built of wood, with thick roofs of thatch. Inside, there was often an earthen floor for work and cooking. A raised wooden floor served for eating and sleeping. In the middle of the house was a sunken hearth. Smoke rose slowly through the thatch, which helped to keep insects away. Around the village lay the rice fields. Every family knew each field, each ditch and each path by heart. Villages were close communities. Neighbours worked together at planting and harvest. Yet life was hard. A bad year could bring hunger to the whole valley.

[long-pause]

{img:22-rice-planting-early-summer-women-line-flooded-field}

Rice was planted by hand in early summer, in fields flooded with water. The year of rice began in spring. First, seeds were sown in small, sheltered beds. When the young plants were strong, they were moved to the fields. By then, the fields had been flooded with water from rivers and ponds. Planting took place in early summer, often during the rainy season. Rows of farmers waded through the mud, pressing the seedlings into the ground by hand. Women did much of this work. In many villages, planting was a festival as well as a task. Drums and flutes played at the edge of the fields. Songs helped the workers keep time. Historians note that this custom is very old in Japan. Yet the work was exhausting. Bending all day in the water hurt the back. Leeches clung to the legs of the planters.

[long-pause]

{img:23-terraced-rice-fields-mountain-slope-water-channels}

In the hills, farmers built terraces, so that rice could grow even on steep slopes. Japan is a land of mountains. Flat land was precious. So in many places, farmers built terraces on the hillsides. Each terrace was a narrow flat field held up by a wall of earth or stone. Water flowed from the highest field down to the next, and then to the next. Small channels and gates controlled its flow. Keeping the water moving correctly was a shared duty. Villages chose leaders to settle disputes over water. A quarrel over water could divide a village for years. Some terraces had been built centuries earlier. Each generation repaired the walls after heavy rain. Historians note that rice fields covered a large part of the farmland of Japan. Even so, farmers also grew barley, wheat, millet, beans and vegetables, often on drier land beside the paddies.

[long-pause]

{img:24-rice-harvest-autumn-sickles-drying-racks}

In autumn, the ripe rice was cut with sickles and hung on racks to dry. By autumn, the rice fields had turned from green to gold. The water was drained away, and the harvest began. Farmers cut the stalks by hand with small curved sickles. They tied them in bundles. Then they hung the bundles upside down on long wooden racks. There the rice dried in the sun and the wind. After drying, the grains were separated from the stalks. The straw was not wasted. It was used for roofs, sandals, raincoats, mats and rope. Every family worked in the fields at harvest time, even the children. When the work was finished, villages gave thanks at the local shrine. Yet the harvest did not belong only to the farmer. A large share had to be handed over as tax. The lord's officials came to measure it carefully.

[long-pause]

{img:25-officials-measuring-rice-tax-bales-village}

Farmers paid their taxes in rice, often almost half of everything they grew. The whole system of power in Japan rested on rice. Lords received their income from the land they controlled. Farmers paid it in rice, packed in straw bales. The tax was often between forty and fifty percent of the harvest. Sometimes it was even higher. Toyotomi Hideyoshi had ordered a great survey of the land in the years before 1600. Officials measured fields across the country. They recorded how much rice each one should produce. This survey set the tax for many years to come. The village as a whole was responsible for paying. If one family could not pay, the others had to help. Historians note that this made villages very close, and very careful. Yet in bad years, the tax could leave families with too little to eat until spring.

[long-pause]

{img:26-farmhouse-winter-hearth-family-straw-work}

In winter, farm families stayed close to the hearth and made things from straw. When winter came, the fields lay empty. In the north and in the mountains, snow could cover the villages for months. Families gathered around the sunken hearth in the middle of the house. A kettle hung above the fire on a long hook. The warmth reached only a little way into the room. Even so, winter was not a time of rest. Farmers used the dark months to make things. They wove straw into sandals, baskets, mats and raincoats. They repaired their tools. Some made paper, cloth or charcoal to sell. Children listened to old stories by the fire. Grandparents told tales of foxes, mountain spirits and brave heroes. Historians note that many folk tales were passed on in this way. In the spring, when the snow melted, the year of rice would begin again.

[long-pause]

{img:27-village-shrine-harvest-festival-lanterns-drums}

Villages held festivals at their local shrine to pray for rain and to give thanks for the harvest. Every village had its own shrine. It honoured the kami, the spirits of the land, the mountains and the rice. Farmers believed these spirits could bring good weather or bad. So in spring, they prayed for rain and a good year. In autumn, they gave thanks for the harvest. Offerings of rice, sake and fruit were placed before the shrine. Drums were beaten, and people danced. Sometimes a portable shrine was carried through the village on the shoulders of young men. Festivals were among the happiest days of the year. They brought the whole village together. Old quarrels were set aside. People ate, drank and sang late into the night. Yet the next morning, the work began again. The rhythm of the year was always set by the rice and the seasons.

[long-pause]

{img:28-simple-meal-tray-rice-miso-soup-pickles}

A typical meal in 1600 was simple: rice, soup and pickled vegetables. Food in Japan was plain and carefully prepared. At the centre of almost every meal was a bowl of rice. With it came a bowl of soup, often made with miso, a paste of fermented soybeans. Small dishes of pickled vegetables added flavour. Radish, cucumber and plums were pickled in salt or rice bran. For those who could afford it, there was also a piece of fish. Many people ate two main meals a day. Historians note that a third meal became common only later. Each person ate from a small tray, using chopsticks. European visitors were surprised by this. The Jesuit Luis Frois noted that Japanese people ate with two sticks, while Europeans used their fingers. Yet white rice was not eaten by everyone. Many farmers mixed their rice with barley or millet to make it last.

[long-pause]

{img:29-fishermen-boats-nets-coast-morning-1600}

Fish was the most important source of meat, and fishing villages lined the coasts. Japan is an island country, surrounded by rich seas. Along its coasts lay many fishing villages. Fishermen went out in small wooden boats before dawn. They used nets, lines and traps. They caught sardines, mackerel, sea bream, squid and many other fish. Seaweed and shellfish were also gathered from the shore. Fresh fish did not keep for long. So much of the catch was dried, salted or pickled. Dried fish could travel far inland, to mountain villages that never saw the sea. Sea bream was a fish for celebrations. Its red colour was thought to bring good fortune. Even so, fish was a luxury for many farmers. In poorer homes, a small piece of dried fish might be shared by the whole family. It was saved for festivals and special days.

[long-pause]

{img:30-buddhist-temple-vegetarian-food-tofu-vegetables}

Buddhist teachings meant that most people ate very little meat from animals. For many centuries, Buddhism had taught respect for living creatures. As a result, eating the meat of four-legged animals was avoided by most people. Fish and birds were usually allowed. Monks in many temples ate no meat at all. They developed a careful cooking style based on vegetables, beans and grains. Tofu, made from soybeans, was an important part of this food. It was rich in protein and easy to prepare. Mountain vegetables, mushrooms and seaweed added flavour. Temple cooking spread slowly to the rest of the country. Yet the rules were not followed everywhere. Hunters in the mountains ate deer and wild boar. Soldiers on long campaigns sometimes did the same. They often called it by another name, such as mountain whale, so that it sounded less like meat.

[long-pause]

{img:31-sake-brewery-wooden-barrels-workers-steam}

Rice was also used to brew sake, which was drunk at festivals and celebrations. Sake is an alcoholic drink made from rice, water and a special mould called koji. Brewing it was skilled work. The rice was polished, washed and steamed. Then the koji turned its starch into sugar. Yeast changed the sugar into alcohol. The whole process took many weeks, usually in the cold months of winter. By 1600, some towns were famous for their sake. Temples had brewed it for centuries. Merchants now sold it in many cities. Sake was offered to the gods at shrines. It was shared at weddings, festivals and New Year. Samurai drank it after a victory. Historians note that drinking together was an important way to build trust. Yet sake was not cheap. Ordinary families drank it mainly on special days, poured warm from small flasks into tiny cups.

[long-pause]

{img:32-portuguese-and-japanese-cooks-frying-batter-kitchen}

New foods had also arrived with the Portuguese, including sweets and food fried in batter. The Portuguese traders brought more than guns to Japan. They also brought new foods and ways of cooking. Sugar became better known through their trade. Sponge cake, called castella, was first made in Nagasaki in this period. It was based on a Portuguese recipe. Small hard sweets also came with the foreign ships. Historians believe that frying food in batter was another Portuguese influence. It later developed into the famous Japanese dish of tempura. The Portuguese also brought sweet potatoes, chilli peppers and tobacco to the region. Some of these spread slowly across Japan. Tobacco became popular surprisingly fast. Within a few years, many people were smoking long thin pipes. Yet for most Japanese, daily food changed very little. Rice, soup, pickles and fish remained at the heart of every meal.

[long-pause]

{img:33-green-tea-picking-hillside-terraces-women}

Green tea had become a daily drink in many homes, though the finest tea was still costly. Tea had come to Japan from China many centuries earlier. Buddhist monks first used it to stay awake during long hours of meditation. Slowly, tea drinking spread to the warrior class and then to ordinary people. By 1600, tea was grown on hillsides in several regions. The Uji district near Kyoto was the most famous. In spring, the young leaves were picked by hand. They were steamed, dried and stored carefully. The finest tea was ground into a bright green powder. It was whisked with hot water in a bowl. This was the tea used in the formal tea ceremony. Ordinary people drank simpler tea, made from coarser leaves. They brewed it in pots or kettles. Yet both kinds were valued. A cup of tea was a sign of welcome in almost every Japanese home.

[long-pause]

{img:34-kitchen-hearth-rice-pot-steam-farmhouse-woman}

Cooking was done over open fires, in heavy pots of iron and clay. Japanese kitchens in 1600 were simple and practical. In farmhouses, cooking was done on a raised clay stove or over the open hearth. Wood and charcoal were the main fuels. Rice was cooked in a heavy iron pot with a thick wooden lid. Cooking rice well was a skill. The fire had to be strong at first and then gentle. The lid was not lifted until the rice was ready. Water came from a well or a nearby stream. It was carried in wooden buckets and stored in large jars. Knives were sharp and finely made, like small swords. Food was cut into neat pieces before cooking. Historians note that Japanese cooking used little oil. Most food was boiled, steamed or grilled. Yet a good meal was not only about taste. It was also arranged with care, so that it looked pleasing on the tray.

[long-pause]

{img:35-kyoto-street-1600-shops-curtains-crowds}

Kyoto, the old capital, was one of the largest cities in the world in 1600. Kyoto had been the home of the emperor for about eight hundred years. In the age of war, much of the city had burned. By 1600, it had been rebuilt and was busy again. Historians estimate that a few hundred thousand people lived there. Its streets were laid out in a grid, an ancient pattern copied from China. Shops lined the main roads. Cloth curtains hung in their doorways, printed with the name or sign of the business. Behind the shops lay narrow houses with small inner gardens. Kyoto was famous for fine crafts. Silk weavers, potters, lacquer makers and fan makers worked there. Temples and shrines stood in every district. Yet the emperor had almost no real power. He lived quietly in his palace, while warlords fought for control of the country.

[long-pause]

{img:36-sakai-port-merchants-warehouses-ships-1600}

The port of Sakai was a rich trading town, run for many years by its own merchants. Sakai lay on the coast near Osaka. During the age of war, it grew rich from trade with China and Southeast Asia. Its merchants imported silk, medicine, books and fine pottery. They sold copper, silver, swords and lacquer ware. For a time, the merchants governed the town themselves. They dug moats around it and hired guards. A Jesuit visitor compared it to Venice, a wealthy city of traders in Europe. Sakai was also a centre of gun making. Many of the guns used in the wars were made there. Some Sakai merchants became famous masters of the tea ceremony. Sen no Rikyu himself came from a merchant family in Sakai. Yet the town lost much of its freedom under the great warlords. By 1600, it served the rulers of Japan like every other town.

[long-pause]

{img:37-craftsman-lacquer-workshop-bowls-brushes}

Japanese craftsmen were famous for lacquer, a hard and shining coating made from tree sap. Lacquer is made from the sap of the lacquer tree. When it dries in a damp place, it becomes very hard and glossy. Craftsmen painted it onto wooden bowls, trays, boxes and armour. They applied many thin layers, one after another. Each layer had to dry before the next was added. A fine piece could take months to make. The raw sap could cause painful rashes, so the work needed great care. The best pieces were decorated with gold and silver powder. This technique was called maki-e, which means sprinkled picture. Portuguese traders loved Japanese lacquer. They ordered chests, desks and boxes to take back to Europe. Some of these objects can still be seen in European museums. Yet most lacquer was made for daily use in Japan, for eating, drinking and storing small treasures.

[long-pause]

{img:38-potter-wheel-kiln-workshop-tea-bowls}

Potters made the simple, uneven tea bowls that tea masters had come to love. Pottery had a long history in Japan. By 1600, new styles were becoming popular. Tea masters admired bowls that looked simple, rough and a little uneven. They believed such bowls had a quiet beauty. One famous style was raku. It was made by a family of potters in Kyoto. Their bowls were shaped by hand, without a wheel. After the wars in Korea in the 1590s, many Korean potters were brought to Japan. Some came by force. They set up new kilns in the south of the country. Their skills changed Japanese pottery for centuries. Historians note that this is a painful part of the story. Yet it also explains why some Japanese pottery towns still trace their origins to Korean craftsmen. A good tea bowl could become more valuable than land.

[long-pause]

{img:39-porters-and-travellers-on-road-inn-pine-trees}

Roads connected the towns of Japan, and travellers stopped at inns along the way. Most people travelled on foot. They walked along roads that linked the castle towns and the old capital. The most famous road ran along the eastern coast. Pine trees were planted along the roads to give shade. At regular distances, there were small towns with inns and teahouses. Travellers could rest, eat and sleep there. Porters carried goods on poles across their shoulders. Wealthy people were carried in small wooden chairs called kago. Only higher samurai rode horses. A traveller could walk perhaps thirty or forty kilometres a day. Rivers often had no bridges. Ferrymen carried people across, sometimes on their backs. Historians note that roads became much safer after 1600. Yet in the age of war, bandits and soldiers had made every journey a risk.

[long-pause]

{img:40-market-day-town-square-stalls-vegetables-fish}

Markets brought farmers, merchants and townspeople together on fixed days each month. In many towns, markets were held on set days. Farmers brought vegetables, firewood and straw goods. Fishermen brought dried fish and seaweed. Merchants sold cloth, pots, tools and medicine. Customers paid with coins, rice or silver. Coins in Japan were often old Chinese coins, with a square hole in the middle. They were strung together on cords. Gold and silver were used for larger sums. The great lords had begun to mine more silver. Historians estimate that Japan produced a large share of the world's silver around this time. Much of it went to China in exchange for silk. Markets were also places for news and gossip. Storytellers and entertainers performed there. Yet merchants had a low position in society. In theory, they ranked below farmers, because they produced nothing themselves.

[long-pause]

{img:41-town-bathhouse-steam-wooden-tubs-1600}

Bathing was an important part of daily life, and towns had public bathhouses. The Japanese were known for their love of cleanliness. European visitors often remarked on it. They noted that Japanese people washed far more often than people in Europe. In towns, public bathhouses were becoming common. Some used steam, rather like a sauna. Others had large tubs of hot water. People washed themselves first, outside the tub, and then soaked in the warm water. The bath was also a place to meet friends and share news. In the countryside, families heated water in a wooden tub at home. Hot springs were valued for health. Lords and samurai sometimes travelled to them to rest after a campaign. Historians note that the first public bathhouse in Edo is said to have opened in 1591. Yet hot water was costly to heat. In many homes, the whole family used the same bath in turn.

[long-pause]

{img:42-samurai-wife-managing-household-keys-storeroom}

In samurai families, the wife managed the household and its money. In a samurai household, the husband served his lord. His wife ran the home. She managed the rice, the stores and the servants. She often kept the keys to the storerooms. She raised the children and looked after the family's elderly members. In wealthy houses, this was a large task with many people to direct. Historians note that women of the warrior class were expected to be strong and loyal. Some were trained to use a long curved spear called the naginata. In times of war, they might defend the house while the men were away. Yet women had fewer rights than men. Marriages were arranged by the families. A daughter often married to build an alliance between two clans. She left her parents' home and joined the household of her husband.

[long-pause]

{img:43-noble-ladies-castle-room-painting-screens}

Some women of great families held real power, even in the age of war. The lives of a few women from this period are well known. Lady Yodo was the mother of Hideyoshi's son. After Hideyoshi died, she lived in Osaka Castle and guarded her son's interests. Another famous woman was Nene, Hideyoshi's widow. She was respected by many lords. Historians believe she had influence over important decisions. Other women ran estates when their husbands were away. Some sent letters that helped to arrange peace between families. Many of these letters still survive. They were written in flowing Japanese script, often on fine paper. Yet such women were exceptions. Most women's lives went unrecorded. They worked in the fields, in shops and in kitchens. Their names were rarely written down, though their work kept the country fed and clothed.

[long-pause]

{img:44-farm-woman-weaving-loom-house-1600}

Farm women worked in the fields and also spun, wove and sewed for the whole family. In farming families, women worked as hard as men. They planted and harvested rice. They cared for vegetables and animals. At home, they cooked, washed and looked after the children. They also made the family's clothes. In 1600, cotton was spreading across Japan. Before, most ordinary clothes were made from hemp. Cotton was softer and warmer. Women spun the thread and wove the cloth on simple wooden looms. They dyed it, often with indigo, which gave a deep blue colour. Blue cotton became the everyday colour of working people. Historians note that weaving brought extra income to many families. Cloth could be sold at the market. Yet the work never seemed to end. Many women sat at the loom long after the rest of the family had gone to sleep.

[long-pause]

{img:45-children-playing-village-lane-tops-kites}

Children played with tops, kites and balls, and helped their parents from a young age. Japanese children in 1600 had simple toys. They spun wooden tops and flew paper kites. They played with balls made of cloth. Girls played with dolls made of paper or clay. European visitors noticed how kindly Japanese parents treated their children. Luis Frois wrote that Japanese children were rarely beaten. He thought they were raised with words rather than punishment. Yet childhood was short. Farm children helped in the fields from an early age. They looked after younger brothers and sisters. They gathered firewood and carried water. Samurai boys began training with wooden swords. They also learned to read and write. Many children did not survive to adulthood. Illness was common, and medicine was limited. Families often visited shrines to pray for their children's health and long life.

[long-pause]

{img:46-wedding-procession-bride-white-kimono-lanterns}

Marriages were arranged by families, and the bride often wore white. In 1600, most marriages were arranged by parents. A go-between helped the two families meet and agree. For samurai, marriage was often a matter of politics. It could tie two houses together. For farmers and merchants, it joined two families in work and property. On the wedding day, the bride left her parents' house. She often wore a white kimono. White was the colour of purity, and also of leaving one family for another. A procession carried her belongings to her new home. The ceremony was usually held there. Bride and groom drank sake from shared cups, in a fixed order. Historians note that this exchange of cups was the heart of the wedding. Yet a bride's new life could be lonely. She had to win the trust of her husband's mother.

[long-pause]

{img:47-elderly-grandmother-telling-stories-children-hearth}

Grandparents were respected, and many lived with their children and grandchildren. In Japanese families, several generations often lived under one roof. Grandparents were treated with respect. They gave advice and helped to raise the children. In samurai families, the head of the house sometimes retired while still alive. He passed his duties to his son. He might then shave his head and live a quieter life, with time for poetry and prayer. Many retired men took Buddhist names. Grandmothers had a special place in the home. They looked after the youngest children. They passed on songs, stories and old customs. Historians note that care for parents was seen as a basic duty. This idea came partly from Confucian teaching, which was growing more important in Japan. Yet not every old person was well cared for. In hard years, the poorest families struggled to feed everyone.

[long-pause]

{img:48-kimono-dyer-workshop-indigo-cloth-hanging}

Clothing showed a person's place in society, from fine silk to plain cotton. In 1600, clothes told others who a person was. Lords and wealthy samurai wore silk. Their robes were dyed in rich colours and patterns. Some were embroidered with gold thread. Ordinary townspeople and farmers wore cotton or hemp. Their clothes were simple and often blue, from indigo dye. Everyone wore a garment shaped like the kimono. It was wrapped left over right and held with a sash. In summer, people wore thin, light robes. In winter, they wore several layers, some padded with cotton. Footwear was simple. People wore straw sandals or wooden clogs called geta. Indoors, they went barefoot or wore split-toed socks. Historians note that the rulers sometimes made laws about clothing. These laws forbade lower classes from wearing silk. Yet merchants who had grown rich often found quiet ways to wear fine cloth beneath plain outer robes.

[long-pause]

{img:49-small-tea-house-garden-path-stone-lantern}

The tea ceremony was one of the most admired arts in Japan in 1600. The tea ceremony is a careful way of preparing and serving green tea. In 1600, it was loved by lords, samurai and rich merchants. Guests walked along a garden path to a small tea house. The path was meant to leave the busy world behind. Stepping stones, moss and a stone lantern lined the way. Guests washed their hands at a stone basin. Then they entered the tea room through a very low door. Everyone had to bow to pass through, even a great lord. Inside, the room was small and simple. A scroll hung in an alcove, beside a single flower. The host prepared the tea in silence. Each movement followed fixed rules. Yet the ceremony was not only about rules. It was meant to bring calm, respect and a sense of the present moment.

[long-pause]

{img:50-tea-master-whisking-green-tea-bowl-iron-kettle}

The greatest tea master of the age was Sen no Rikyu, who died nine years before 1600. Sen no Rikyu was born into a merchant family in Sakai. He became the tea master of Oda Nobunaga and later of Toyotomi Hideyoshi. Rikyu shaped the tea ceremony that is still practised in Japan. He favoured a simple style called wabi. It valued plain materials, quiet rooms and rough, natural objects. He built tea rooms only a few mats wide. In 1591, Hideyoshi ordered Rikyu to end his own life. The exact reason is still unknown. Historians have suggested a quarrel over politics, money or pride. Rikyu was about seventy years old. Yet his ideas did not die with him. His students and grandsons carried on his teaching. In 1600, the great lords still competed to own tea bowls, kettles and jars that Rikyu had once admired.

[long-pause]

{img:51-zen-temple-rock-garden-raked-gravel-monk}

Zen temples were known for their gardens of stone and raked gravel. Some of the most famous gardens in Japan were made in Zen Buddhist temples. They were very different from flower gardens. A few large stones stood in a bed of white gravel. Monks raked the gravel into lines that looked like water. Moss grew around the stones. These gardens were made for looking, not for walking. Monks sat on the wooden veranda and studied them quietly. Each viewer could see something different. The stones might be islands, mountains or animals. Historians note that many of these gardens were made in the century before 1600. The garden of Ryoan-ji in Kyoto is the best known. Nobody knows exactly who designed it. Yet its simple beauty has been admired for centuries. Raking the gravel was itself part of the monks' daily practice.

[long-pause]

{img:52-noh-theatre-stage-masked-actor-pine-backdrop}

Noh theatre, with its slow movements and masks, was a favourite of the samurai. Noh is one of the oldest forms of theatre in the world. It began in the fourteenth century. By 1600, it was greatly loved by the warrior class. Toyotomi Hideyoshi enjoyed it so much that he performed on stage himself. A Noh play was slow and solemn. The main actor wore a carved wooden mask and rich silk robes. He moved with great control, sometimes taking only a step at a time. A small group of musicians played drums and a flute. A chorus sang parts of the story. Many plays told of ghosts, gods and warriors from the past. Behind the stage stood a painting of a single pine tree. Historians note that the masks were made to show different feelings as the actor tilted his head. Yet the plays were not meant to be exciting. They were meant to move the heart quietly.

[long-pause]

{img:53-calligraphy-master-brush-large-paper-ink}

Writing with a brush was an art, and fine calligraphy was greatly prized. In Japan, beautiful writing was considered an art form. It was called shodo, the way of writing. Calligraphers used brushes of animal hair and ink made from soot and glue. The ink was ground by hand on a stone with a little water. Grinding it slowly was thought to calm the mind before writing. A good calligrapher could show strength, grace or sadness in a single character. Scrolls of fine writing were hung in tea rooms and halls. Poems and Buddhist sayings were the most common subjects. Many samurai and monks practised calligraphy every day. Historians note that letters from this period are often admired for their writing as much as their words. Yet good calligraphy took years to learn. Each stroke had to be made in one movement, without any correction.

[long-pause]

{img:54-cherry-blossom-viewing-picnic-hillside-1600}

In spring, people gathered under the cherry trees to enjoy the blossoms. Cherry blossom viewing is an old custom in Japan. It is called hanami. In spring, people gathered under the flowering trees. They shared food and sake. They wrote poems and sang songs. The blossoms lasted only a short time, often about a week. Their quick beauty was a reminder that all things pass. In the spring of 1598, Toyotomi Hideyoshi held a famous blossom party at the Daigo temple near Kyoto. Hundreds of guests were invited. About seven hundred cherry trees were planted for the occasion. Women of his household wore new kimono, changing them during the day. It was one of the grandest parties of the age. Yet Hideyoshi died only a few months later. Many people saw a lesson in this. Even the most powerful life was as brief as the cherry blossom.

[long-pause]

{img:55-ikebana-flower-arrangement-alcove-scroll}

Arranging flowers was also an art, practised in temples and in the homes of the wealthy. Flower arranging in Japan grew out of the custom of offering flowers at Buddhist altars. Over time, it became an art of its own. It was called ikebana, which means living flowers. Arrangements were placed in the alcove of a room, beside a hanging scroll. Some were tall and grand, built of many branches. They stood for mountains, valleys and the whole natural world. Others were very simple. A single branch or flower in a plain vase could be enough. Tea masters preferred this quiet style. Flowers were chosen to match the season. Plum blossom suited late winter. Iris suited early summer. Maple leaves and chrysanthemums suited autumn. Historians note that teachers of flower arranging passed on their methods within families, as in other arts. Yet the aim was always the same. A small arrangement was meant to bring the feeling of nature into the room.

[long-pause]

{img:56-shinto-shrine-red-torii-gate-forest-path}

Shinto shrines honoured the kami, the spirits found in nature and in the land. Shinto is the oldest religion of Japan. It teaches that spirits, called kami, live in many places. A kami might live in a mountain, a waterfall, an old tree or a large rock. Some kami were the spirits of ancestors or great heroes. Shrines were built to honour them. A shrine was often entered through a gate called a torii. It marked the boundary between the everyday world and a sacred place. Visitors washed their hands and mouth before approaching. They bowed, clapped their hands and said a short prayer. Shrines stood in villages, towns and deep in the forests. Some were very old. The great shrine at Ise was rebuilt every twenty years, an old custom that had been interrupted by the wars. Yet Shinto had no single holy book. It lived mainly in customs, festivals and prayers.

[long-pause]

{img:57-buddhist-temple-hall-monks-chanting-incense}

Buddhism had been in Japan for about a thousand years, and temples were found everywhere. Buddhism came to Japan from Korea and China in the sixth century. By 1600, there were many Buddhist schools. Some taught meditation, like Zen. Others taught that faith in the Buddha Amida would bring rebirth in a pure land. Temples served as places of prayer and learning. They also kept the records of families and held funerals. Most Japanese people followed both Shinto and Buddhism. They saw no conflict between them. A family might visit a shrine for a birth and a temple for a funeral. Some Buddhist temples had been very powerful in the age of war. They owned land and even kept armies. Historians note that Oda Nobunaga destroyed the temple complex on Mount Hiei in 1571. Yet Buddhism remained deeply rooted in daily life. Temple bells rang across the towns every morning and evening.

[long-pause]

{img:58-jesuit-church-nagasaki-japanese-christians}

Christianity had also come to Japan, and by 1600 there were many thousands of Japanese Christians. In 1549, the Jesuit missionary Francis Xavier arrived in Japan. Other priests followed him. They preached in the south and west of the country. Some great lords became Christians. Their people often followed them. Historians estimate that by 1600 there were about three hundred thousand Japanese Christians. Nagasaki became the centre of the Christian community. Churches, schools and a printing press were built there. Yet the faith also caused fear among the rulers. In 1587, Hideyoshi ordered the missionaries to leave, though the order was not fully enforced. In 1597, twenty-six Christians were executed in Nagasaki. Among them were Japanese believers and foreign priests. In 1600, Christians could still practise their faith quietly. In later decades, the religion would be banned completely and driven underground.

[long-pause]

{img:59-obon-lanterns-floating-river-night-summer}

In summer, families welcomed the spirits of their ancestors during the festival of Obon. Obon was a Buddhist festival held in late summer. People believed that the spirits of their ancestors returned home for a few days. Families cleaned the graves and placed offerings of food and flowers. They lit small fires or lanterns to guide the spirits to the house. At night, villagers danced in a circle. These dances were called bon odori. They were joyful and simple, with drums and songs. On the last evening, the spirits were sent back. In some places, lanterns were floated down rivers or out to sea. The small lights drifted slowly into the dark. Historians note that Obon has been celebrated in Japan for more than a thousand years. Yet it was not a sad festival. It was a time for families to feel close to those they had lost.

[long-pause]

{img:60-new-year-preparations-pine-decorations-rice-cakes}

New Year was the most important festival, a time for cleaning, rice cakes and new beginnings. The New Year was the greatest celebration of the year in Japan. In 1600, it followed the old lunar calendar. It usually fell in late January or February. Before the New Year, families cleaned their houses from top to bottom. Debts were paid where possible. Pine branches and bamboo were placed by the gates to welcome the year's god. Families pounded steamed rice into sticky cakes called mochi. They used a large wooden mallet and a mortar. It was hard work, often done by the men while the women turned the rice. On New Year's morning, people dressed in their best clothes. They visited shrines and temples to pray for good fortune. Historians note that everyone became one year older at New Year, rather than on their own birthday. Yet the festival was also quiet. Many shops closed for several days.

[long-pause]

{img:61-mountain-pilgrims-white-clothes-temple-steps}

Pilgrims walked long distances to visit holy mountains, shrines and temples. Pilgrimage was an important part of religious life in Japan. People walked to sacred places to pray, to give thanks or to ask for help. Some climbed holy mountains, where they believed the gods lived. Others followed routes that linked many temples. On the island of Shikoku, pilgrims walked a long circle of eighty-eight temples. The route was linked to the great monk Kukai. Pilgrims often wore white clothes and carried a walking staff. They slept in temples, inns or the homes of kind villagers. Giving food or shelter to a pilgrim was considered a good deed. Historians note that pilgrimage became much more popular after 1600. Roads grew safer during the long peace. Yet even in the age of war, many people had made these journeys. Some never returned home.

[long-pause]

{img:62-autumn-moon-viewing-veranda-family-1600}

In autumn, families gathered on their verandas to admire the full moon. Moon viewing was a quiet autumn custom. It took place on the night of the full moon in the eighth month of the old calendar. Families placed small rice dumplings and stalks of pampas grass on a stand. These were offerings of thanks for the harvest. Then they sat on the veranda and looked at the moon rising over the hills. Some wrote poems. Others simply talked softly, or sat in silence. The custom had come from China many centuries earlier. At first, it was enjoyed mainly by the court. By 1600, it had spread to samurai, merchants and farmers. Historians note that the moon appears in countless Japanese poems. It was a symbol of beauty and of things that change and return. Some temples held moon viewing gatherings beside their ponds, where the moon could be seen twice, once in the sky and once on the still water. Boats were sometimes rowed out onto the lakes for a better view. Yet moon viewing asked for nothing grand. A clear sky, a quiet place and a little time were all that it needed.

[long-pause]

{img:63-burned-kyoto-temple-ruins-civil-war-smoke}

Before 1600, Japan had suffered more than a century of civil war. In 1467, a quarrel between powerful families broke out into war in Kyoto. It was called the Onin War. It lasted ten years and left much of the capital in ruins. After that, the central government lost its power. Across Japan, local lords fought each other for land. Historians call this time the Sengoku period, the age of warring states. It lasted for more than a hundred years. Castles were built and destroyed. Families rose and fell. Villages were burned, and fields were trampled by armies. Yet daily life did not stop. Farmers still planted their rice. Merchants still traded. Some regions knew long periods of peace. Even so, the danger of war was never far away. Many people longed for a ruler strong enough to bring the whole country together again.

[long-pause]

{img:64-oda-nobunaga-portrait-style-samurai-lord-armour}

The first of the three great unifiers was Oda Nobunaga, a bold and ruthless lord. Oda Nobunaga came from a small domain in central Japan. As a young man, he was thought to be wild and strange. Yet he proved to be a brilliant general. In 1560, he defeated a much larger army in a surprise attack during a storm. In 1568, he marched into Kyoto. He used guns on a large scale and built a magnificent castle at Azuchi. He welcomed the Portuguese and their Jesuit priests. He also fought the powerful Buddhist temples without mercy. By the early 1580s, he controlled much of central Japan. Then, in 1582, one of his own generals turned against him. Nobunaga was trapped in the Honno-ji temple in Kyoto. The temple was set on fire, and he died there. His body was never found. Historians still debate why his general betrayed him.

[long-pause]

{img:65-toyotomi-hideyoshi-seated-castle-hall-retainers}

The second unifier, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, rose from a humble background to rule all of Japan. Toyotomi Hideyoshi was born into a poor family in a farming village. He began as a simple foot soldier in Nobunaga's army. Through skill and hard work, he rose to become one of Nobunaga's best generals. After Nobunaga's death, Hideyoshi quickly defeated his rivals. By 1590, he had brought all of Japan under his control. He never became shogun, because of his low birth. Instead, he took other high titles from the emperor. Hideyoshi carried out great reforms. He ordered the survey of the land and the hunt for farmers' swords. He also fixed the social classes more firmly. Yet his last years were troubled. He launched two invasions of Korea, in 1592 and 1597. They cost many lives and failed. Hideyoshi died in 1598, leaving a young son only five years old.

[long-pause]

{img:66-council-of-five-elders-castle-room-tension-1599}

Before he died, Hideyoshi set up a council to rule until his son grew up. Hideyoshi worried about the future of his son, Hideyori. So he chose five great lords to govern Japan together. They were known as the Five Elders. He also chose five officials to manage daily affairs. On his deathbed, Hideyoshi asked them all to protect his son. They promised to do so. Yet the balance did not last long. The most powerful of the Five Elders was Tokugawa Ieyasu. He held vast lands in the east, around the town of Edo. Many lords began to look to him for leadership. Others feared that he wanted to rule Japan himself. Historians note that Ieyasu began to arrange marriages between his family and other lords. This had been forbidden by Hideyoshi's rules. Tension grew between Ieyasu and Hideyoshi's loyal official, Ishida Mitsunari.

[long-pause]

{img:67-tokugawa-ieyasu-patient-lord-garden-1600}

The third great unifier was Tokugawa Ieyasu, a patient and careful lord. Tokugawa Ieyasu was born in 1543. As a boy, he was sent as a hostage to other families. He spent many years living in the households of powerful lords. He learned patience and caution. As an adult, he became an ally of Nobunaga and later a follower of Hideyoshi. In 1590, Hideyoshi moved Ieyasu to the eastern plains, far from Kyoto. Some saw this as a punishment. Yet Ieyasu turned it into a strength. He built up the small castle town of Edo. He organised his lands carefully and gathered loyal followers. He loved falconry and was careful about his health. A Japanese saying later compared the three unifiers. Nobunaga mixed the rice cake. Hideyoshi baked it. Ieyasu sat down and ate it. In 1600, he was fifty-seven years old.

[long-pause]

{img:68-ishida-mitsunari-official-writing-letters-night}

Against Ieyasu stood Ishida Mitsunari, a loyal official of the Toyotomi family. Ishida Mitsunari had served Hideyoshi for many years. He was an able administrator. He had organised supplies for the armies in Korea and helped with the land survey. Yet many generals disliked him. They saw him as proud and cold. Some blamed him for problems during the war in Korea. Mitsunari believed that Ieyasu was a threat to Hideyori. He began to gather allies among the western lords. He sent letters across the country, asking lords to join him. In the summer of 1600, Ieyasu marched north to deal with a rebellious lord. While he was away, Mitsunari acted. He raised an army in the west and declared war on Ieyasu. Japan was now divided into two great camps. Historians call them the Eastern Army and the Western Army.

[long-pause]

{img:69-messengers-on-horseback-mountain-road-1600}

In the summer of 1600, messengers raced along the roads as lords chose their side. Every lord in Japan now had to make a difficult choice. Should he support Ieyasu, or the Western Army in the name of Hideyori? The wrong choice could cost him his lands, or his life. Messengers galloped along the roads day and night. They carried letters, promises and threats. Some lords made their decision quickly. Others waited to see which side looked stronger. A few secretly promised help to both. Families were sometimes split. A father might join one side and his son the other. That way, the family would survive whatever happened. Historians note that many letters from these months still survive. They show how tense and uncertain the time was. Yet most ordinary people knew little of these plans. They only saw the armies passing along the roads, and hoped the fighting would not reach their village.

[long-pause]

{img:70-portuguese-black-ship-nagasaki-harbour-1600}

Each year, a great Portuguese ship sailed into Nagasaki, bringing silk and news from the world. The Portuguese had traded with Japan since the 1540s. Their large ships came from Macao, a Portuguese trading post in southern China. The Japanese called them the black ships, because of their dark hulls. The ships brought Chinese silk, which Japanese lords and merchants valued highly. They returned with Japanese silver. This trade made both sides rich. The arrival of the black ship was a great event in Nagasaki. Crowds gathered on the shore to watch. Japanese painters made large folding screens showing the scene. These paintings are called nanban screens. Nanban means southern barbarians, the name the Japanese used for Europeans. Historians note that these screens show tall foreigners with baggy trousers and big hats. Yet the Portuguese were few in number. Japan stayed firmly in Japanese hands.

[long-pause]

{img:71-nanban-screen-painting-europeans-in-port}

Japanese artists painted the foreign visitors with great curiosity. The nanban screens are among the most charming works of art from this period. They were painted on gold leaf, like other fine screens. On one side, a black ship arrives in port. On the other, the Portuguese walk through a Japanese town. Their clothes look strange and colourful. Their servants carry boxes of goods. Jesuit priests in dark robes greet them. Japanese townspeople watch from windows and doorways. The painters had often never seen Europeans themselves. They worked from descriptions and from earlier pictures. So the faces and clothes are sometimes exaggerated. Yet the screens show a real sense of wonder. Rich Japanese families liked to own them. They were a sign of wealth and of interest in the wider world. Several dozen of these screens still survive today in museums and private collections.

[long-pause]

{img:72-jesuit-priest-and-japanese-lord-conversation-room}

Jesuit priests learned Japanese and wrote long letters describing life in Japan. The Jesuits were a Catholic religious order. Their missionaries in Japan worked hard to understand the country. Many learned to speak Japanese well. They wrote dictionaries and grammar books. They also sent long letters back to Europe. These letters described Japanese customs, food, houses and beliefs. Historians use them as an important source for this period. Luis Frois lived in Japan for more than thirty years. He wrote a history of the Japanese mission. He also wrote a short book comparing Europe and Japan point by point. Another Jesuit, Joao Rodrigues, became an interpreter for Hideyoshi and later for Ieyasu. He wrote about the tea ceremony in great detail. Yet the Jesuits did not see everything clearly. Their main goal was to win converts. Their letters often praised Christian lords and criticised Buddhist monks.

[long-pause]

{img:73-dutch-ship-de-liefde-battered-arriving-coast-1600}

In April 1600, a battered Dutch ship called De Liefde reached the coast of Japan. In 1598, five Dutch ships set sail from Rotterdam. They hoped to reach Asia by crossing the Pacific Ocean. The voyage was a disaster. Storms, disease and hunger took many lives. Only one ship, De Liefde, finally reached Japan. Its name means Charity. It arrived in April 1600 off the coast of Kyushu, in the south. Of the more than one hundred men who had started on board, only about two dozen were still alive. Many were too weak to stand. Japanese fishermen helped them ashore. Local officials sent word to Osaka, where Ieyasu was staying. Among the survivors was an English pilot named William Adams. He was about thirty-five years old. The Portuguese in Japan were alarmed by the arrival of these Protestant sailors. They told Ieyasu that the newcomers were pirates.

[long-pause]

{img:74-william-adams-meeting-ieyasu-osaka-castle-1600}

William Adams was taken to meet Tokugawa Ieyasu, who questioned him for many hours. Adams was brought to Osaka in the spring of 1600. Ieyasu wanted to know who these strangers were. He questioned Adams through an interpreter. He asked about the voyage, about the Dutch and English, and about their religion. Adams later described these meetings in a letter. He wrote that Ieyasu asked about the wars between European nations. He was especially curious about ships and navigation. Adams answered as well as he could. Ieyasu was impressed by his knowledge. He did not believe the Portuguese claims that the newcomers were pirates. Yet Adams was kept in prison for several weeks while Ieyasu decided what to do. He feared he would be executed. In the end, he was released. Ieyasu kept the guns and cannon from De Liefde, which may have been useful in the war that was coming.

[long-pause]

{img:75-william-adams-building-western-ship-japanese-carpenters}

Adams later became a trusted adviser to Ieyasu and was given the name Miura Anjin. After 1600, William Adams stayed in Japan for the rest of his life. Ieyasu asked him to build ships in the European style. Adams had learned shipbuilding as a young man in England. With Japanese carpenters, he built two ships. Ieyasu was pleased. He made Adams a samurai and gave him an estate at Hemi, near Edo. Adams received a new name, Miura Anjin, which means the pilot of Miura. He married a Japanese woman and had children. He also helped the Dutch and English to set up trading posts in Japan. Historians note that he was one of the very few Europeans to become a samurai. Yet he never forgot England. He asked several times to be allowed to go home. Ieyasu refused. Adams died in Japan in 1620, at the port of Hirado.

[long-pause]

{img:76-japanese-red-seal-ship-sailing-asia-trade}

Japanese merchants also sailed abroad, trading across Southeast Asia. The trade of Japan did not depend only on foreign ships. Japanese merchants also sailed far from home. They travelled to the Philippines, Vietnam, Cambodia and Siam. Some Japanese settled in these places. Small Japanese communities grew in several Southeast Asian ports. After 1600, Ieyasu gave official licences to trusted traders. Each licence carried a red seal. The ships that carried them were known as red seal ships. Historians estimate that more than three hundred such voyages were made in the following decades. The ships brought back silk, deer skins, sugar and sharkskin, which was used to cover sword handles. Yet this open trade did not last. In the 1630s, the Tokugawa rulers closed the country. Japanese were forbidden to travel abroad, and contact with Europe was limited to a small Dutch post in Nagasaki.

[long-pause]

{img:77-armies-marching-mountain-pass-autumn-1600-banners}

In the autumn of 1600, two huge armies marched towards a narrow valley called Sekigahara. By September 1600, the Eastern and Western Armies were moving towards each other. Both sides had gathered tens of thousands of men. Historians estimate that around one hundred and sixty thousand soldiers took part in the coming battle. Some estimates are even higher. The armies met in central Japan, near a village called Sekigahara. Its name means the plain of the barrier. It lay where important roads crossed between the east and the west of the country. Hills surrounded it on several sides. The Western Army arrived first and took positions on the high ground. Ieyasu's Eastern Army came from the east. It had rained heavily for days. The roads were deep in mud. Soldiers were wet, cold and tired. Yet both sides knew that this battle might decide the future of Japan.

[long-pause]

{img:78-misty-battlefield-sekigahara-dawn-fog-banners}

On the morning of the battle, thick fog covered the valley. The Battle of Sekigahara took place on the twenty-first of October 1600, by the modern calendar. Before dawn, the two armies faced each other in the darkness. Then a heavy fog rose from the wet ground. Soldiers could see only a few metres ahead. Commanders listened for the sound of drums and gunfire. Around eight in the morning, the fog began to lift. Units from the Eastern Army moved forward and opened fire. The fighting quickly spread across the valley. Guns cracked, and spears clashed. Historians describe the first hours as fierce and evenly matched. The Western Army held strong positions on the hills. Yet some of its units did not move at all. Their commanders waited on the slopes, watching. Ieyasu had secretly been in contact with several of them. He was waiting too.

[long-pause]

{img:79-kobayakawa-troops-on-hill-waiting-battle-below}

The battle turned when a young lord on a hillside changed sides. On Mount Matsuo, overlooking the battlefield, stood the army of Kobayakawa Hideaki. He was a young lord of about nineteen. He had joined the Western Army. Yet he had secretly promised to help Ieyasu. Through the morning, he did nothing. His troops watched the battle from the hill. According to later accounts, Ieyasu grew impatient. He ordered his guns to fire towards Kobayakawa's hill as a warning. Historians still debate whether this really happened. Around midday, Kobayakawa made his choice. His army came down the hill and attacked the Western Army from the side. Several other lords then joined him. The Western Army began to collapse. Its soldiers were caught between enemies on two sides. By the afternoon, the battle was over. The Eastern Army had won.

[long-pause]

{img:80-ieyasu-camp-after-battle-helmet-evening}

After the victory, Ieyasu is said to have tightened the cords of his helmet. There is a famous story about Ieyasu after the battle. His generals gathered around him to celebrate. Instead of removing his helmet, Ieyasu tied its cords more tightly. He is said to have spoken an old warrior saying. After victory, tighten the cords of your helmet. It meant that a wise man should never relax too soon. Historians cannot be sure the story is true. Yet it fits what is known about him. Ieyasu then held a ceremony to inspect the heads of defeated enemy commanders. This was an old custom of war in Japan. It may seem harsh. It was a way to confirm who had fallen and to reward those who had fought. After the battle, Ieyasu treated the lords who had changed sides generously. Those who had opposed him to the end were punished.

[long-pause]

{img:81-kyoto-autumn-execution-ground-quiet-crowd}

Ishida Mitsunari fled from the battlefield but was soon captured. After the defeat, Ishida Mitsunari escaped into the mountains. For several days, he hid in the countryside. He was tired and ill. Then he was found and captured. He was taken to Kyoto. There, in November 1600, he was executed along with two other leaders of the Western Army. Their heads were displayed at a bridge over the Kamo River. This was a warning to anyone who might still resist. A story is told about Mitsunari's last days. On the way to his execution, he was offered a persimmon. He refused it, saying it would be bad for his stomach. A guard laughed, because he would soon be dead anyway. Mitsunari answered that a man with great plans must care for his health until the very end. Historians cannot confirm the story. Yet it is still told in Japan.

[long-pause]

{img:82-lords-bowing-before-ieyasu-castle-hall-1600}

After Sekigahara, Ieyasu reshaped the map of Japan, rewarding friends and punishing enemies. The victory at Sekigahara made Ieyasu the most powerful man in Japan. In the months that followed, he redistributed land on a huge scale. Lords who had fought against him lost their domains, or saw them greatly reduced. Lords who had supported him received new land. Historians estimate that land producing several million koku of rice changed hands. Ieyasu placed his most loyal followers in key positions. They guarded important roads and castles. He moved less trusted lords to distant regions, far from Edo and Kyoto. This careful arrangement would help keep the peace for a very long time. Yet one great question remained. Toyotomi Hideyori still lived in Osaka Castle. He was only a boy of seven. Many people still saw him as Hideyoshi's rightful heir.

[long-pause]

{img:83-autumn-village-after-war-farmers-returning-fields}

For ordinary people, the end of the fighting brought a slow return to normal life. After the battle, the soldiers went home. Many were farmers who had been taken away from their fields. They returned to their villages in time for the end of the harvest. Some found their homes damaged or their crops trampled. Others came back wounded, or did not come back at all. Families mourned their dead and repaired what had been broken. Yet there was also hope. People sensed that the long age of war might truly be ending. A single strong ruler now held power. Roads would become safer. Taxes would be more regular. Historians note that peace did not come all at once. There would be one more great conflict, at Osaka, in 1614 and 1615. Even so, for most villages, the autumn of 1600 marked the beginning of a quieter world.

[long-pause]

{img:84-ieyasu-becomes-shogun-imperial-envoy-1603}

In 1603, the emperor named Tokugawa Ieyasu shogun, the military ruler of Japan. The title of shogun was very old. It meant the great general who subdues the barbarians. For centuries, shoguns had ruled Japan in the name of the emperor. The title could only be given by the emperor himself. Toyotomi Hideyoshi had never received it. In 1603, three years after Sekigahara, the emperor gave the title to Ieyasu. With it came the right to govern the whole country. Ieyasu chose to rule from Edo, his own castle town in the east. The emperor stayed in Kyoto, with great dignity but little power. Only two years later, Ieyasu passed the title to his son Hidetada. This showed that the office would stay in the Tokugawa family. Yet Ieyasu kept real control until his death. He continued to guide his son from his retirement castle at Sunpu.

[long-pause]

{img:85-edo-castle-construction-workers-stones-1600s}

Edo was a small town when Ieyasu arrived, but it grew quickly into a great castle city. When Ieyasu first came to Edo in 1590, it was a modest place. There was a small old castle, some fishing villages and marshy land along a bay. Ieyasu began to change it. He had hills levelled and marshes filled in. Canals were dug to bring goods into the town. After he became shogun, the work grew much larger. Lords from across Japan were ordered to help build Edo Castle. They sent workers, stones and money. Huge blocks of stone were shipped from distant regions. Edo Castle became the largest castle in Japan. Around it, the city spread in every direction. Historians estimate that by the 1700s Edo had about one million people. It may then have been the largest city in the world. Edo would later be renamed Tokyo, meaning eastern capital.

[long-pause]

{img:86-daimyo-procession-road-edo-porters-banners}

Under the new rulers, great lords had to spend part of their time in Edo. The Tokugawa rulers found clever ways to keep the lords under control. One of the most important was a system of alternate attendance. It developed over the following decades. Each lord had to live in Edo for part of the time, often every other year. When he returned to his own domain, his wife and heir stayed behind in Edo. The journeys between Edo and the domains were long and costly. Great lords travelled with hundreds or even thousands of followers. Their processions moved slowly along the roads. Villagers knelt as they passed. Historians note that this system had many effects. The lords spent much of their wealth on travel and on their houses in Edo. They had little left to raise armies. Yet the journeys also helped trade, inns and towns along the roads to grow.

[long-pause]

{img:87-peaceful-castle-town-street-evening-lanterns-1600s}

The peace that began around 1600 lasted for more than two and a half centuries. The Tokugawa family ruled Japan from 1603 until 1868. That is more than two hundred and sixty years. Historians often call it the Edo period. For most of that time, Japan saw no major wars. This was a remarkable change after the long age of fighting. In the peace, towns grew and trade flourished. Roads and bridges were improved. Many new crafts and arts developed. Kabuki theatre began in Kyoto only a few years after 1600. It soon became hugely popular. Woodblock prints, poetry and new kinds of novels followed later. Many samurai became officials and scholars rather than fighting men. Yet the peace came at a price. Society was strictly controlled. Each class had its own rules. People were not free to change their position, to travel abroad or to follow forbidden beliefs.

[long-pause]

{img:88-samurai-reading-books-study-lamp-peace}

In the long peace, the samurai slowly changed from warriors into officials and scholars. After 1600, there were few battles left to fight. Yet the samurai remained the ruling class. They still wore their two swords. They still trained in martial arts. But most of their work now took place in offices. They kept records, collected taxes, judged disputes and managed towns. Many became deeply interested in learning. Schools for samurai sons spread across the country. Confucian teachings became very important. They taught loyalty, duty, respect for parents and good government. Historians note that the idea of bushido, the way of the warrior, was written down mainly in these peaceful centuries. Some writers looked back with longing to the age of war. Others believed that a true samurai should now serve with the brush. Yet the old pride remained. A samurai was still expected to be ready to give his life for his lord.

[long-pause]

{img:89-ieyasu-last-years-sunpu-castle-garden-old-man}

Ieyasu spent his last years at Sunpu Castle, within sight of Mount Fuji. After handing the title of shogun to his son, Ieyasu retired to Sunpu Castle. It stood in a town that is now called Shizuoka. From there, he could see Mount Fuji. Ieyasu stayed active. He met foreign visitors, including William Adams. He collected books and supported scholars. He also kept a careful eye on politics. In 1614 and 1615, his armies besieged Osaka Castle. Toyotomi Hideyori and his mother Lady Yodo died when the castle fell. With that, the last challenge to Tokugawa rule was gone. Ieyasu died the following year, in 1616, at the age of seventy-three. He was buried and later honoured as a god. A great shrine was built for him at Nikko, in the mountains north of Edo. Its gates and halls are covered in carvings and gold.

[long-pause]

{img:90-cherry-blossoms-castle-moat-spring-calm-evening}

The year 1600 is remembered as the moment when Japan turned from war towards a long peace. For Japanese historians, the year 1600 is a turning point. Before it lay more than a century of war. After it came the long peace of the Edo period. The Battle of Sekigahara is often called the battle that decided the realm. Yet daily life in 1600 was shaped by much more than battles. It was shaped by the planting of rice and the turning of the seasons. It was shaped by the craft of potters and swordsmiths, by the quiet of tea rooms and by festivals at village shrines. Many of the customs of that age can still be recognised. Cherry blossoms still draw crowds in spring. Rice is still planted in early summer. And the white walls of the old castles still rise above their moats, reflecting the soft light of evening.

[long-pause]

So we leave Japan in the last days of 1600, as the first snow settles on the castle roofs.

[pause]

The battle is over, and the roads are quiet again. In the villages, the rice is stored in the barns, and the farmers sit by their hearths. In the castle towns, the samurai lay their armour aside, and the lanterns glow behind the paper doors. Far away in the east, the little town of Edo sleeps beside its bay, not yet knowing that it will become one of the great cities of the world.

Before sleep finally comes, another story from an older world already sits on the screen, with lanterns and quiet roads of its own. Subscribing keeps these slow evenings coming.
