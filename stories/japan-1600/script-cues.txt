Tonight we are going more than four hundred years back, to the islands of Japan, in the spring of the year 1600.

[long-pause]

It is a land of steep green mountains and narrow valleys, of rice fields shining with water, and pine trees bent by the wind, of temples and castles and thatched villages, and of the samurai, the warriors who have ruled it for centuries.

[pause]

And it is a land that has been at war with itself for more than a hundred years, and is about to find peace at last.

[pause]

If you enjoy these slow journeys into other times, you can subscribe to the channel, and there will be more of them waiting for you.

[long-pause]

Japan is a long chain of islands, off the east coast of Asia, curving for more than two thousand kilometres through the Pacific Ocean, from the cold north to the warm south.

[pause]

There are four main islands. The largest, in the middle, is Honshu. To the north is Hokkaido, which in 1600 is still a wild land of forests, home to a people called the Ainu, and hardly part of Japan at all. To the south and west are the smaller islands of Shikoku and Kyushu. And around them, there are thousands of little islands, scattered in the sea.

[long-pause]

Most of Japan is mountains, covered with forests of cedar and pine and cypress and oak and bamboo. The mountains are steep, and wild, and beautiful, with rushing rivers, and waterfalls, and hot springs, bubbling out of the ground, because Japan is a land of volcanoes.

[pause]

The greatest of all the mountains is Mount Fuji, a tall, perfect cone, with snow on its peak for most of the year, rising alone above the plains, near the coast of central Honshu. It is a sacred mountain, home of the gods.

[pause]

{img:mount-fuji-snow-cone-spring-cherry-blossom-foreground}
The people of Japan live in the narrow valleys between the mountains, and on the small plains along the coasts and the rivers. There, every piece of flat land is used for growing rice. In the hills, the rice fields climb up the slopes, in terraces, like great green steps.

[long-pause]

In the spring of 1600, the cherry trees are in blossom.

[pause]

All over Japan, in the gardens of the temples and the castles, and along the rivers, and on the hillsides, the cherry trees are covered with clouds of pale pink and white flowers. For a week or two, everything is pink and white. And then the petals fall, like snow, and drift on the wind, and float on the water of the rivers and the ponds.

[pause]

{img:cherry-blossom-petals-falling-river-temple-gate}
The Japanese love the cherry blossom more than any other flower, because it is so beautiful, and lasts so short a time. It reminds them that life is beautiful, and short, and that everything passes. People go out to sit under the trees, with their families and friends, and eat, and drink rice wine, and write poems, and sing.

[long-pause]

How many people live in Japan, in the year 1600?

[pause]

Historians think there were perhaps twelve to fifteen million, or perhaps more. Most of them are farmers, living in small villages, growing rice. But there are also great cities: Kyoto, the old capital, where the Emperor lives, with perhaps three or four hundred thousand people. And Osaka, a great port and castle town. And Sakai, a rich trading city, by the sea.

[pause]

And there is a little castle town, on the eastern plain, called Edo. In 1600, it is still small, and muddy, and new. But one day, it will become one of the largest cities in the world. Today, we call it Tokyo.

[long-pause]

At the top of Japanese society, there is the Emperor, the Tenno, who lives in his palace in Kyoto.

[pause]

The Japanese believe that the Emperor is descended from the sun goddess, Amaterasu, and that his family has ruled Japan since the beginning of time. He is sacred, and greatly respected. But he has no real power. For hundreds of years, the real rulers of Japan have been the great warriors, the samurai, and their lords.

[pause]

{img:imperial-palace-kyoto-courtyard-white-gravel-red-pillars}
The greatest of these warriors, if he can win enough power, is given the title of shogun, the great general, by the Emperor, and rules the country in the Emperor's name.

[pause]

But in the spring of 1600, there is no shogun. There has not been a real one for many years. Japan has been at war with itself for more than a century.

[long-pause]

{img:sengoku-armies-banners-hills-distant-castle-misty}
The Japanese call the years from about 1467 to 1600 the Sengoku period, which means the age of the warring states.

[pause]

In those years, the power of the shoguns in Kyoto collapsed, and Japan broke up into hundreds of little states, each ruled by a warlord, called a daimyo, which means great name. The daimyo fought each other constantly, for land, and power, and glory, with armies of samurai.

[long-pause]

It was a hard and dangerous time. Castles were built on every hilltop. Villages were burned, and fields trampled. Families were divided. Old lords were overthrown by their own followers. A clever and ruthless man could rise from nothing to become a great lord. And a great lord could lose everything in a single day.

[pause]

But it was also a time of great energy, and change. Trade grew. Towns grew. New ideas came. Great castles and temples were built, and beautiful paintings and pottery and gardens were made.

[pause]

And slowly, toward the end of the sixteenth century, three great men brought the country back together. The Japanese say: Nobunaga mixed the rice cake, Hideyoshi baked it, and Ieyasu ate it.

[long-pause]

The first was Oda Nobunaga.

[pause]

He was a young lord of a small province, in central Japan, near the modern city of Nagoya. He was bold, and clever, and very ruthless. He was one of the first lords in Japan to use guns, in large numbers, which had arrived in Japan only a few years before, brought by the Portuguese.

[pause]

{img:samurai-lord-young-confident-castle-veranda-fan}
One by one, Nobunaga defeated his rivals, and took over most of central Japan, including Kyoto. He built a magnificent castle, at Azuchi, by the shore of Lake Biwa, with a great tower, seven storeys high, decorated with gold, and paintings, unlike anything that had ever been seen in Japan.

[pause]

But in 1582, one of his own generals turned against him, and Nobunaga lost his life, at a temple in Kyoto, called the Honno-ji.

[long-pause]

The second was Toyotomi Hideyoshi.

[pause]

He was one of Nobunaga's generals. And he was born a peasant, the son of a poor foot soldier and farmer. He was small, and ugly, and so like a monkey in his face that Nobunaga called him the little monkey. But he was brilliant, and charming, and a great general, and he rose, step by step, to be Nobunaga's best commander.

[pause]

{img:small-energetic-samurai-general-gold-fan-smiling-court}
After Nobunaga's death, Hideyoshi took his place, and finished his work. By 1590, he had brought the whole of Japan under his rule, for the first time in more than a hundred years.

[long-pause]

Hideyoshi built the greatest castle in Japan, at Osaka, and a great palace in Kyoto, decorated with gold. He loved splendour. He had a tea room, made entirely of gold, which could be taken apart and carried with him. He gave great parties: a tea party for everyone in Kyoto, rich and poor, at the shrine of Kitano, and a cherry blossom party, at the temple of Daigo-ji, for thirteen hundred guests.

[pause]

He did many things to make the country peaceful. He ordered that all the farmers must give up their swords and their weapons, so that only the samurai would be armed. He had all the land of Japan measured, and the rice harvest of every village written down, so that the taxes could be fairly collected.

[pause]

But then, Hideyoshi did a terrible thing. He sent great armies across the sea, to invade Korea, in 1592 and again in 1597, hoping to conquer China. The war was a disaster, and caused great suffering in Korea, and achieved nothing.

[long-pause]

In 1598, Hideyoshi fell ill, and knew that he was dying.

[pause]

He had only one son, Hideyori, a little boy of five. Hideyoshi was afraid that, after his death, his great lords would fight each other, and take the country from his son. So he set up a council of five great lords, the Five Regents, to rule Japan together, until Hideyori was grown up. And he made them all swear to be loyal to his son.

[pause]

{img:old-lord-sickbed-gathered-regents-kneeling-lamplight}
And in September 1598, Toyotomi Hideyoshi passed away, at his castle at Fushimi, near Kyoto. He wrote a last poem: I came like the dew, and I vanish like the dew. All that I have done, Osaka, the castle, is a dream within a dream.

[long-pause]

The most powerful of the Five Regents was the third great man: Tokugawa Ieyasu.

[pause]

And in the spring of 1600, everyone in Japan knows that, sooner or later, there will be a struggle for power, between Ieyasu and the other lords. And that the future of Japan will depend on it.

[long-pause]

{img:tokugawa-ieyasu-middle-aged-lord-calm-robes-castle-room}
In the year 1600, Tokugawa Ieyasu is fifty-seven years old.

[pause]

He is a stout, solid, round-faced man, calm and patient and careful. He is not brilliant and dashing, like Nobunaga, or charming and clever, like Hideyoshi. But he is wise, and cautious, and he knows how to wait. He is the richest and most powerful lord in Japan, after the Toyotomi family itself.

[long-pause]

His life has been long and hard.

[pause]

He was born in 1543, the son of a small lord in the province of Mikawa, on the Pacific coast of central Japan, near Nagoya. When he was a little boy, his family was so weak that he was sent away, as a hostage, to live with a more powerful lord, to make sure that his family stayed loyal. He spent most of his childhood as a hostage, far from home.

[pause]

There is a story that, as a boy, he was once taken to see a great river festival, where two sides of boys threw stones at each other in a mock battle. Everyone thought the larger side would win. But the young Ieyasu said that the smaller side would win, because they were more united, and more determined. And they did.

[long-pause]

As a young man, Ieyasu became an ally of Nobunaga, and fought beside him, for many years. Then, after Nobunaga's death, he fought against Hideyoshi for a while, and then made peace with him, and became his most important ally.

[pause]

In 1590, Hideyoshi gave Ieyasu a great new territory, in the east of Japan, on the wide plain of the Kanto, around a little fishing village and castle called Edo. It was a rich land, but far from Kyoto, and Hideyoshi perhaps hoped to keep Ieyasu far away, where he could do no harm.

[pause]

{img:edo-1590-small-castle-marshes-fishing-village-bay}
But Ieyasu made the best of it. He moved his capital to Edo, and began to build a great castle there, and to drain the marshes, and dig canals, and build a town. And his lands in the east made him richer than ever.

[long-pause]

Ieyasu is famous for his patience.

[pause]

There is a famous saying, about the three great men, and a little bird, a cuckoo, which will not sing. Nobunaga says: if the cuckoo will not sing, kill it. Hideyoshi says: if the cuckoo will not sing, make it want to sing. And Ieyasu says: if the cuckoo will not sing, wait until it does.

[pause]

{img:small-cuckoo-bird-pine-branch-mist-quiet-morning}
And Ieyasu himself wrote, in his old age, a short piece of advice for his descendants: the life of a man is like walking along a long road, carrying a heavy load. Do not hurry. Patience is the root of long life and safety.

[long-pause]

He is careful about his health. He eats simple food: brown rice, and fish, and vegetables, and he does not drink much. He exercises every day. He is very interested in medicine, and makes his own medicines, from herbs, and studies Chinese books on health. He will live to be seventy-three, a great age for those days.

[pause]

He loves falconry, hunting with hawks, and goes out into the fields with his falcons whenever he can. He says it is good exercise, and that it lets him see the countryside, and how the farmers are living.

[pause]

He is careful with money too, and does not like waste. There is a story that he was once seen chasing a piece of paper that the wind had blown away, in his garden, because paper was precious, and should not be wasted.

[long-pause]

He has many children, by several wives and concubines. His eldest son, Nobuyasu, passed away young, in sad circumstances, long ago. His heir now is his third son, Hidetada, who is twenty-one in 1600, a serious and dutiful young man.

[pause]

Ieyasu also has a great band of loyal followers, generals and advisers who have been with him for many years, and would do anything for him. They are some of the finest soldiers in Japan.

[pause]

{img:loyal-generals-kneeling-before-lord-castle-hall-tatami}
And in the spring of 1600, Ieyasu is in Osaka, at the heart of power, as the chief of the Five Regents, looking after the government of Japan, in the name of the little boy, Hideyori.

[pause]

Many people believe that Ieyasu wants to rule Japan himself. And they are right.

[long-pause]

{img:edo-1600-new-castle-town-canals-boats-marsh-reeds}
Let us visit the little town of Edo, in the spring of 1600, ten years after Ieyasu first came there.

[pause]

Edo stands at the head of a wide, shallow bay, where several rivers flow into the sea, on the edge of the great Kanto plain, the largest plain in Japan. When Ieyasu arrived, in 1590, it was a poor place: a small, old castle, half in ruins, on a low hill, and a fishing village of a hundred houses, surrounded by marshes and reed beds, and salt flats, and fields.

[long-pause]

In ten years, it has changed a great deal.

[pause]

Ieyasu has repaired and enlarged the castle. He has dug canals, to bring boats from the bay into the heart of the town, and to bring fresh water from the hills. He has levelled a hill, called Kanda, and used the earth to fill in part of the marshes along the bay, to make new land for houses and streets.

[pause]

{img:workers-digging-canal-earth-baskets-shoulder-poles-sun}
His samurai have come, with their families, from his old lands in the west, and built their houses around the castle. Merchants and craftsmen have followed them, from all over Japan, because wherever there is a great lord, with many samurai, there is work, and trade. Carpenters and builders, especially, are everywhere, because the whole town is being built at once.

[long-pause]

It is a noisy, busy, muddy place: a frontier town, a building site.

[pause]

Boats come and go along the new canals, loaded with timber, and stone, and rice, and salt, and fish. The fish market, by a bridge over the canal, is busy every morning, with fishermen from the villages around the bay bringing their catch. The streets are lined with new wooden shops and houses, with the smell of fresh-cut cedar, and sawdust.

[pause]

{img:early-edo-fish-market-bridge-canal-morning-baskets}
And on a clear day, from the hill of the castle, you can see, far away to the southwest, across the plain, the white cone of Mount Fuji, floating above the haze.

[long-pause]

In 1603, a new bridge will be built over the river, in the middle of the town, called Nihonbashi, the bridge of Japan. All the great roads of the country will begin from this bridge, and all distances in Japan will be measured from it.

[pause]

The bridge is still there today, in the middle of Tokyo, rebuilt in stone, and the distances are still measured from it.

[pause]

{img:wooden-arched-bridge-nihonbashi-canal-boats-crowds}
But in 1600, all this is still to come. Edo is a small, new town, at the edge of the country, far from Kyoto and Osaka. And its lord is far away, at Osaka, watching, and waiting.

[long-pause]

{img:samurai-two-swords-kimono-hakama-castle-courtyard-calm}
Who are the samurai?

[pause]

The word samurai means one who serves. The samurai are the warrior class of Japan. They have been the fighting men, and the rulers, of Japan for about four hundred years, since the twelfth century. They serve their lords, the daimyo, and in return, the lords give them land, or a salary, paid in rice.

[long-pause]

In 1600, perhaps one person in every fifteen or twenty in Japan belongs to a samurai family. They are not all rich, or noble. Some are great lords, with castles, and thousands of followers. Most are ordinary soldiers, and officials, with a small house, and a modest salary of rice. Some are very poor.

[pause]

A samurai is known by his two swords: a long sword, the katana, and a short sword, the wakizashi, which he wears thrust through his sash, on his left side, with the cutting edge facing upward. Only samurai are allowed to wear two swords.

[pause]

The Japanese sword is one of the most beautiful and the most carefully made objects in the world. A swordsmith takes weeks or months to make a single blade, folding and hammering the steel many times, and cooling it in water, in a special way, so that the edge is very hard and sharp, and the back is softer and stronger. The finished blade is polished until it shines like a mirror. A samurai treats his sword with great respect. It is said to be the soul of the samurai.

[long-pause]

But in 1600, the samurai do not fight only with swords.

[pause]

Most battles are fought with long spears, called yari, and with bows and arrows, and above all, with guns, matchlock muskets, which the Japanese call teppo. Guns were first brought to Japan by Portuguese traders, in 1543, and within a few years, Japanese craftsmen were making thousands of them. By 1600, there are more guns in Japan than in almost any country in Europe.

[pause]

{img:ashigaru-foot-soldiers-spears-banners-marching-field}
Most of the fighting is done by foot soldiers, called ashigaru, which means light feet, common men, armed with spears and guns, organized in great companies. The mounted samurai are the officers and the leaders.

[long-pause]

A samurai's armour is beautiful too.

[pause]

It is made of hundreds of small plates of iron or hardened leather, lacquered, so that they shine, and tied together with silk cords, of many colours. It is lighter and more flexible than European armour. On his head, a samurai wears a helmet, often decorated with great crests, in the shape of horns, or crescent moons, or leaves, or animals, so that he can be recognized on the battlefield. And on his back, he often carries a small flag, called a sashimono, with the badge of his lord.

[pause]

{img:samurai-armour-display-lacquered-plates-silk-cords-helmet}
Every great family has its own badge, its mon, a simple, beautiful design, in a circle: three hollyhock leaves, for the Tokugawa. A paulownia flower, for the Toyotomi. These badges are on their flags, and their armour, and their clothes, and their furniture.

[long-pause]

But a samurai is not only a warrior.

[pause]

He is expected to be educated, and cultured. He learns to read and write, Japanese and Chinese, and studies the Chinese classics, the teachings of Confucius, about duty, and loyalty, and the proper relations between people. He learns to write beautiful calligraphy, with a brush, and to write poetry, and to appreciate painting, and gardens, and the tea ceremony.

[pause]

The Japanese say that a samurai should follow the twin ways of the brush and the sword: bun and bu, learning and fighting.

[long-pause]

And a samurai must be loyal.

[pause]

Loyalty to his lord is the most important thing in a samurai's life. He must obey his lord, and serve him, and be ready to give his life for him. In return, the lord must look after his samurai, and their families. In the peaceful centuries that will come after 1600, these ideas will be written down, and called bushido, the way of the warrior.

[pause]

{img:samurai-kneeling-bowing-before-lord-hall-morning-light}
But in the real world of 1600, loyalty is not always so simple. In the age of the warring states, many samurai and many lords changed sides, when it suited them, and betrayed their masters. As we shall see, the great battle of 1600 will be decided, in the end, by a lord who changes sides.

[long-pause]

{img:samurai-house-wooden-veranda-garden-dawn-mist-castle-town}
Let us spend an ordinary day with a samurai, in a castle town, in the spring of 1600.

[pause]

He is not a great lord. He is an ordinary samurai, in the service of a daimyo, with a small house, in the samurai quarter of the town, near the castle, and a salary of rice, enough to keep his family, and a servant or two.

[long-pause]

His house is built of wood, with a roof of tiles, or of thatch, and stands behind a wall, or a hedge, with a gate, and a little garden.

[pause]

Inside, the house is simple, and almost empty, and very clean. The floors are covered with tatami mats, thick mats of woven straw, which are soft to walk on, and to sit on. Everyone takes off their shoes at the door. The rooms are divided by sliding doors, of wood and paper, which can be opened, or taken away, to make the rooms larger, or to open the house to the garden.

[pause]

There is very little furniture: a low table, and cushions to sit on, on the floor, and chests for clothes, and a little alcove, called the tokonoma, where a hanging scroll, with a painting or a poem, and a vase of flowers, are displayed. At night, the family sleeps on thick quilts, futons, spread out on the floor, and folded away in cupboards in the morning.

[long-pause]

Our samurai gets up at dawn.

[pause]

He washes, in cold water, in a basin, outside, in the garden. He combs his hair, and ties it up, in the samurai style, with the top of his head shaved, and the rest of the hair gathered into a knot on top. He dresses in his kimono, and his hakama, wide, pleated trousers, and puts on his two swords, through his sash.

[pause]

{img:samurai-washing-face-wooden-basin-garden-morning-cold}
Then he makes his prayers, at the little Buddhist altar in his house, where the tablets with the names of his ancestors are kept, and at the little Shinto shrine, the kamidana, a shelf high on the wall, for the gods of the household.

[long-pause]

Breakfast is simple: a bowl of rice, or rice mixed with barley, and miso soup, made of fermented soybean paste, with a little tofu, or seaweed, and some pickled vegetables. And green tea.

[pause]

Most Japanese people of this time eat very little meat. Buddhism teaches that it is wrong to kill animals. They eat fish, and shellfish, and tofu, and beans, and vegetables, and rice. Rice is the most important food of all, and the word for a meal, gohan, means cooked rice.

[pause]

Then our samurai goes to work, at the castle.

[long-pause]

What does a samurai do, when there is no war?

[pause]

Many samurai work as officials, for their lord: collecting taxes, and keeping accounts, and managing the lord's lands, and his rice, and his storehouses, and judging disputes, and keeping order in the town, and guarding the castle gates. They write reports, and letters, and keep records, with brush and ink. Some are teachers, or doctors, or engineers.

[pause]

{img:samurai-officials-writing-records-castle-office-scrolls}
And every samurai must practise the arts of war. He goes to the training hall, the dojo, and practises with the sword, with wooden swords, and with the spear, and the bow, and the gun. He rides horses. He practises wrestling, and swimming in armour. He must always be ready, in case war comes.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, if he is free, he might read, or practise his calligraphy, or write poetry, or visit a friend, to drink tea, and talk.

[pause]

Or he might go to the temple, to hear a monk speak, or to sit in meditation, in the Zen way. Zen Buddhism is very popular among the samurai. It teaches calm, and self-control, and concentration, and living fully in the present moment, without fear. These are good qualities for a warrior.

[pause]

Or he might simply sit on his veranda, and look at his garden: the moss, and the stones, and the little pond, and the maple tree, and the bamboo moving in the wind.

[long-pause]

In the evening, he comes home, and has a bath.

[pause]

The Japanese love bathing. In a samurai's house, there is a little bath house, with a deep wooden tub, filled with hot water, heated by a fire underneath. You wash yourself first, outside the tub, with water from a bucket, and only then climb into the hot water, to sit, and soak, and relax, up to your neck.

[pause]

{img:wooden-bathtub-steam-bath-house-evening-lantern-calm}
Then supper, with his family, sitting on the floor, around little trays: rice, and soup, and fish, perhaps, and vegetables, and pickles, and a little warm rice wine, sake.

[long-pause]

After supper, the family sits together, in the lamplight. The children practise their writing. His wife sews, or plays the koto, a long stringed instrument, which lies on the floor. Our samurai reads, or plays a game of go, with a friend, a game of black and white stones on a board, which is very old, and very difficult.

[pause]

And then the sliding doors are closed, and the lamps are put out, and the futons are spread on the floor. And the house is quiet, except for the frogs in the rice fields beyond the town, and the night watchman, walking through the streets, clapping two pieces of wood together, to tell the hours, and to warn people to be careful of fire.

[long-pause]

{img:japanese-maple-leaves-red-temple-steps-autumn-quiet}
The people of Japan, in 1600, live very close to the seasons.

[pause]

The year is full of small festivals and customs, which mark the turning of the seasons, and which everyone, from the Emperor to the poorest farmer, follows and enjoys. Many of them are still followed today.

[long-pause]

The year begins with the New Year, the greatest festival of all, which in 1600 falls in late January or February, because the Japanese use the old lunar calendar, which follows the moon.

[pause]

Before the New Year, every house is cleaned, from top to bottom, to sweep away the dust and the bad luck of the old year. Debts are paid. Pine branches and bamboo are set up on either side of the gate, to welcome the god of the New Year. Special food is prepared: rice cakes, mochi, pounded from sticky rice in great wooden mortars, with wooden mallets, by the men of the family, taking turns, with a rhythmic thudding sound that can be heard all over the village.

[pause]

{img:men-pounding-mochi-wooden-mortar-mallet-winter-yard}
On New Year's Day, everyone puts on new clothes, and visits the shrine, to pray for good fortune in the coming year, and then visits their relatives and their lord, to wish them a happy New Year. Everyone becomes a year older, on New Year's Day, all together, not on their own birthdays.

[long-pause]

In the spring, there are the plum blossoms, first, in February and March, white and pink, with a sweet scent, while it is still cold. And then the cherry blossoms, in April, when everyone goes out to sit under the trees, and have picnics, and drink sake, and write poems.

[pause]

On the third day of the third month, there is the doll festival, for girls, when families set out beautiful dolls, of the Emperor and Empress and their court, on a stand covered with red cloth, with peach blossoms. And on the fifth day of the fifth month, the festival for boys, when families with sons fly great carp-shaped streamers, of cloth or paper, from tall poles, so that the boys will grow up strong and brave, like the carp, which swims up waterfalls.

[pause]

In early summer, there is the rainy season, tsuyu, when it rains softly, almost every day, for weeks. The hydrangeas bloom, blue and purple and pink, in the temple gardens. The frogs sing in the rice fields, all night long. And in the evenings, by the streams, the fireflies come out, in thousands, little green lights, floating over the water. People go out to watch them, and catch them, and keep them in little cages of bamboo and gauze, to light their rooms.

[long-pause]

The summer is hot, and humid. People fan themselves with paper fans, and wear light cotton robes, and sit on their verandas in the evening, to catch the cool air. They hang little glass or metal wind bells from the eaves of their houses, which tinkle in the breeze, and make them feel cooler.

[pause]

In the middle of summer, there is the festival of Obon, when the spirits of the ancestors return to their families. Lanterns are hung at the doors, to guide them home. The graves are cleaned. Food is offered. And on the last night, the people dance the Bon dances, in a great circle, in the village square, under the lanterns, to the music of drums and flutes. And then they float little paper lanterns, with candles inside, down the rivers, or out to sea, to guide the spirits back to their world.

[pause]

{img:paper-lanterns-floating-river-night-candles-glowing}
Autumn is the season of the moon.

[long-pause]

In the eighth month, on the night of the full moon, people hold moon-viewing parties. They sit on their verandas, or by a pond, or on a hill, and look at the great, bright harvest moon, rising over the mountains, and offer it rice dumplings and autumn grasses, and drink sake, and write poems about it, and listen to the insects singing in the grass.

[pause]

The Japanese love the songs of the autumn insects, crickets and bell crickets and pine crickets. Some people keep them in little cages, to hear them sing.

[pause]

{img:moon-viewing-veranda-full-moon-pampas-grass-dumplings}
And in late autumn, the maple leaves turn red and gold, on the hillsides and in the temple gardens, and people go out to look at them, as they look at the cherry blossoms in spring. They call it momijigari, maple-leaf hunting.

[long-pause]

Then the winter comes. In the north, and in the mountains, the snow falls deep, and the villages are buried, for months. In the south, it is cold, and grey, and dry, with clear, bright days.

[pause]

People gather around the hearth, and the charcoal braziers, and wrap themselves in padded robes. They look at the snow, and write poems about it. They drink hot sake, and eat hot food, stews and noodles, cooked in pots on the hearth. And they wait for the plum blossoms, and the New Year, and the spring.

[long-pause]

{img:himeji-castle-white-towers-stone-walls-blue-sky-spring}
Every daimyo has a castle, at the centre of his lands. And in the years around 1600, the castles of Japan are the most magnificent they have ever been.

[pause]

The old castles, in the age of the warring states, were simple forts of wood and earth, on the tops of mountains, hard to reach, and easy to defend. But in the last years of the sixteenth century, after Nobunaga built his great castle at Azuchi, the lords began to build a new kind of castle: on low hills, or on the plains, in the middle of their lands, as centres of government as well as fortresses.

[long-pause]

These new castles stand on great walls of stone, huge blocks, fitted together without mortar, curving gently outward at the bottom, like the sides of a fan, so that they are very strong, and can stand up to earthquakes. Around the walls, there are moats, full of water, and gates, with heavy wooden doors, covered with iron.

[pause]

And on top of the stone walls, there are buildings of wood, plastered white, with roofs of grey tiles, curving gracefully upward at the corners. In the middle, there is the great tower, the tenshu, five or six or seven storeys high, rising up, roof above roof, like a great white bird.

[pause]

{img:castle-stone-walls-fan-curve-moat-water-reflection-pines}
The most beautiful of all these castles, which still stands today, is Himeji Castle, west of Kobe, which is called the White Heron Castle, because it looks like a great white bird, about to fly. After 1600, it will be given to Ieyasu's ally, Ikeda Terumasa, who will rebuild it, over nine years, into the castle we can see today.

[long-pause]

The castle is a maze.

[pause]

To reach the great tower, an enemy would have to pass through many gates, and courtyards, and narrow, winding passages, between high walls, turning this way and that, sometimes leading back in the wrong direction, with places in the walls, above, for archers and gunners to shoot from. It is designed to confuse and slow down any attacker.

[pause]

Inside the castle, there are the lord's residence, with its halls and rooms, decorated with beautiful paintings on the sliding doors and screens, of tigers and cranes and pine trees and flowers, on backgrounds of gold leaf, glowing in the dim light. And there are storehouses, full of rice, and weapons, and gunpowder, and treasures.

[long-pause]

Around the castle, there grows a town, the castle town, jokamachi.

[pause]

Closest to the castle, inside the outer moat, live the samurai, in their houses, behind their walls, the most important ones nearest to the castle. Beyond them, live the merchants and the craftsmen, in their shops and workshops, along the streets: carpenters, and blacksmiths, and potters, and weavers, and dyers, and sake brewers, and tea merchants, and booksellers. And beyond them, the temples and shrines, on the edges of the town.

[pause]

{img:castle-town-street-shops-noren-curtains-merchants-busy}
The greatest castle of all is Osaka Castle, built by Hideyoshi, on a hill above the great river delta, where the Toyotomi family lives. It is enormous, with great moats, and walls, and a towering keep, decorated with gold, on the edges of its roofs. It is said to be impossible to take by force.

[long-pause]

And inside Osaka Castle, in 1600, lives the boy Hideyori, Hideyoshi's son, now seven years old, with his mother, the Lady Yodo, who is proud, and beautiful, and fiercely protective of her son.

[pause]

The Lady Yodo is the niece of Oda Nobunaga. She has lost both her parents, in the wars, when she was a child. She will do anything to keep her son's inheritance safe.

[pause]

{img:osaka-castle-gilded-roofs-huge-stone-walls-moat-evening}
And Ieyasu, as one of the Five Regents, has his own residence in the castle grounds, at Osaka. They are neighbours. But they do not trust each other at all.

[long-pause]

{img:japanese-kitchen-1600-earthen-stove-rice-pot-steam}
What do the people of Japan eat, in the year 1600?

[pause]

The heart of every meal is rice. For the samurai, and the people of the towns, white rice, polished. For the farmers, who must give most of their rice as taxes, rice mixed with barley, or millet, or just millet and barley, with a little rice on special days.

[long-pause]

With the rice, there is soup, usually miso soup, made from fermented soybean paste, with vegetables, or seaweed, or tofu, or little clams. There are pickles: radishes, and cucumbers, and eggplants, and plums, pickled in salt, or in rice bran, or in vinegar, which keep for months, and give flavour to the plain rice.

[pause]

There is fish, fresh, or dried, or salted, or grilled over charcoal: sea bream, and mackerel, and sardines, and bonito, and salmon, and eels, and many others. And shellfish: clams, and oysters, and abalone, and shrimp. And seaweed, many kinds, which the Japanese have eaten since ancient times.

[pause]

{img:grilled-fish-skewers-charcoal-brazier-street-vendor}
There is tofu, bean curd, made from soybeans, soft and white, which the Buddhist monks taught the Japanese to make. There are vegetables: radishes, burdock, lotus roots, bamboo shoots, mushrooms, and wild plants from the mountains. There is fruit: persimmons, and pears, and plums, and peaches, and oranges.

[long-pause]

The Japanese eat with chopsticks, from small bowls and dishes of lacquer and pottery, set out on small individual trays, or little tables, in front of each person, who sits on the floor.

[pause]

There are rules for eating. You must not stick your chopsticks upright into your rice, because that is done only for the dead. You must not pass food from chopsticks to chopsticks. You must say a word of thanks before you eat, and after.

[pause]

The food is simple, and light, and carefully prepared, and beautifully arranged, with attention to colour, and to the season. A few autumn leaves on a plate of fish in autumn. A cherry blossom on a sweet in the spring.

[long-pause]

What about sushi?

[pause]

In 1600, sushi is very different from the sushi we know today. It is a way of preserving fish: the fish is salted, and packed in rice, and left for weeks or months, so that the rice ferments, and the fish keeps. The rice is often thrown away, and only the fish is eaten. It has a strong, sour taste.

[pause]

{img:wooden-sushi-press-box-fermenting-fish-rice-old-style}
The quick, fresh sushi that we know, with raw fish on top of little balls of vinegared rice, will be invented much later, in the street stalls of Edo, in the early nineteenth century, as a kind of fast food.

[long-pause]

There are noodles: soba, made from buckwheat flour, and udon, thick white noodles of wheat. In 1600, soba is often eaten as a kind of dumpling, but in the following years, it will be cut into long thin noodles, and become one of the most popular foods of Edo, eaten hot in broth in winter, and cold, with a dipping sauce, in summer.

[pause]

And there is sake, rice wine, made by fermenting rice with water and a special mould, called koji. The breweries, in the towns, are famous, and the best sake comes from the region around Osaka and Kyoto, where the water is pure. It is drunk warm, in little cups, at festivals and feasts, and in the evenings.

[pause]

{img:sake-brewery-wooden-vats-workers-cedar-ball-sign}
And, of course, there is tea. In 1600, the green powdered tea of the tea ceremony is for the rich. But ordinary people drink a simpler tea, made from roasted leaves, or barley, boiled in a kettle on the hearth. And at the little tea houses along the roads, travellers can sit on a bench, outside, in the shade, and drink a cup of tea, and eat a sweet rice dumpling, and rest their feet, and watch the world go by.

[long-pause]

{img:farmers-planting-rice-seedlings-flooded-paddy-spring-hats}
Most of the people of Japan, in 1600, are farmers. And the most important thing in their lives, and in the life of the whole country, is rice.

[pause]

Rice is food, and it is also money. The wealth of a lord is measured in koku of rice: one koku is about the amount of rice that one person eats in a year. A small lord might have ten thousand koku. Ieyasu has two and a half million. The samurai are paid in rice. And the farmers pay their taxes in rice: often forty or fifty percent of their harvest, or even more.

[long-pause]

Growing rice is hard work, and the whole village must work together.

[pause]

In the early spring, the farmers prepare the fields: they plough them, with oxen, or with hoes, and build up the little walls of earth, around each field, and repair the channels that bring water from the rivers and the streams. Meanwhile, the rice seeds are sown, thickly, in a small seedbed, to sprout.

[pause]

{img:farmer-ploughing-ox-muddy-field-mountains-spring-mist}
Then, in May or June, the fields are flooded with water, and the whole village comes out, men and women and children, to plant the young rice plants, one by one, by hand, in neat rows, in the warm mud, bending over, all day long, with their legs in the water. It is back-breaking work. But they sing as they work, rice-planting songs, to keep the rhythm, and sometimes musicians play drums and flutes, at the edge of the field.

[long-pause]

All through the summer, the farmers look after the rice.

[pause]

They weed the fields, by hand, wading through the water. They watch the water level, letting water in and out. They chase away the birds, and the insects. They pray to the gods of the rice, at the little shrines at the edges of the fields, for good weather, and a good harvest. The rice grows tall and green, and then, in the late summer, golden.

[pause]

In the autumn, it is harvested, with sickles, by hand, and tied into bundles, and hung up on wooden racks, in the fields, to dry in the sun. Then it is threshed, beaten, to separate the grains from the straw. And the best of the rice is packed into straw bags, and carried to the lord's storehouse, as taxes.

[long-pause]

Nothing is wasted. The rice straw is used to make sandals, and raincoats, and hats, and ropes, and mats, and roofs, and bags, and the tatami mats of the houses. The rice husks are used as fuel, and fertilizer.

[pause]

Besides rice, the farmers grow barley and wheat, in the winter, in the dry fields, and millet, and soybeans, and radishes, and turnips, and eggplants, and sweet potatoes. They grow cotton, and mulberry trees, to feed silkworms, and tea bushes, on the hillsides. They keep chickens. And they go into the mountain forests, to gather firewood, and mushrooms, and wild vegetables, and chestnuts.

[pause]

The farmers' houses are made of wood, with thick roofs of thatch, steep, to let the rain and the snow slide off. Inside, there is a large room, with an earth floor, near the door, where the work is done, and a raised floor, of wooden boards, with a fireplace, a square hearth sunk into the floor, called an irori, where the family cooks and eats and warms itself, around the fire, with an iron kettle hanging over it, from a hook on the ceiling.

[long-pause]

In 1588, Hideyoshi ordered the great sword hunt.

[pause]

All the farmers of Japan had to give up their swords, and their spears, and their guns. Hideyoshi said that the metal would be used to make a great statue of the Buddha, in Kyoto, so that the farmers would be blessed, in this life and the next. But the real reason was that he wanted to make sure that the farmers could never rise up against their lords, as they had often done in the age of the warring states.

[pause]

{img:village-festival-shrine-lanterns-drums-farmers-dancing}
From now on, the farmers will be farmers, and the samurai will be samurai, and nobody will be allowed to change from one to the other. A farmer's son must be a farmer. A samurai's son must be a samurai.

[long-pause]

The village is the centre of the farmers' world.

[pause]

Each village has a headman, chosen from the leading families, who is responsible for collecting the taxes, and keeping order, and dealing with the lord's officials. The villagers help each other, at planting and harvest, and in building houses, and in times of trouble. They have their own shrine, to the local god, the ujigami, and their own temple.

[pause]

{img:village-headman-house-gate-persimmon-tree-autumn}
And they have festivals: at the New Year, and in the spring, at planting time, and in the summer, at Obon, the festival of the dead, when the spirits of the ancestors come home, and are welcomed with lanterns, and dances. And in the autumn, at harvest, the greatest festival of all, when the whole village gives thanks, and feasts, and dances, and carries the portable shrine of the god through the village, on the shoulders of the young men, shouting and singing.

[long-pause]

{img:kyoto-street-1600-merchants-shops-noren-crowds-temple}
In the towns and cities of Japan, in 1600, there is a growing world of merchants and craftsmen.

[pause]

Kyoto, the old capital, is the most beautiful city in Japan. It lies in a wide valley, surrounded on three sides by green, wooded hills, with a river, the Kamo, running through it. Its streets are laid out in a neat grid, like a chessboard, copied from the old capitals of China, a thousand years ago.

[long-pause]

Kyoto is full of temples, hundreds of them, with great gates, and halls, and pagodas, and gardens. There is the temple of Kiyomizu, built on a great wooden platform, high on the side of a hill, looking out over the city. There is the Golden Pavilion, a little temple covered with gold leaf, standing by a quiet pond. There are the great temples of the Pure Land Buddhists, with their huge halls, where thousands of people come to pray.

[pause]

And there is the Emperor's palace, and the houses of the court nobles, the kuge, with their ancient families, and their elegant manners, and their poetry, and their music, who live as their ancestors lived, a thousand years ago, poor, but proud.

[long-pause]

But Kyoto is also a city of craftsmen, the finest in Japan.

[pause]

In the district of Nishijin, the weavers make the most beautiful silk cloth in the world, brocades, with patterns of gold and silver, for the kimonos of the rich, and the robes of the priests. There are dyers, and embroiderers, and makers of fans, and lacquerware, and pottery, and dolls, and swords, and armour, and paper, and ink, and brushes.

[pause]

{img:silk-weaver-loom-nishijin-workshop-colourful-threads}
The lacquerware of Japan is very beautiful: boxes and bowls and trays and tables, of wood, covered with many layers of lacquer, the sap of a tree, which dries to a hard, shining surface, black, or red, and decorated with gold, in delicate pictures of flowers and birds and grasses.

[long-pause]

Osaka, nearby, is the great city of trade.

[pause]

Hideyoshi made it his capital, and invited merchants from all over Japan to come and live there. It stands on the delta of a great river, criss-crossed with canals, where boats carry rice, and goods, from all over western Japan, to the great warehouses of the merchants.

[pause]

{img:osaka-canals-boats-rice-bales-warehouses-bridges}
And there is Sakai, a rich port, by the sea, south of Osaka, where the merchants have grown so rich, from the trade with China, and with the Portuguese, that for many years, they ruled the city themselves, like a little republic, with walls and moats, and a council of merchants.

[long-pause]

Merchants are, in theory, at the bottom of Japanese society, below the samurai, and the farmers, and the craftsmen, because they make nothing, and only buy and sell. Many samurai look down on them.

[pause]

But in practice, many merchants are very rich, and very powerful. The lords borrow money from them. They live in fine houses, and wear fine clothes, and collect beautiful things, and give great parties.

[pause]

And in the cities, there is a lively world of entertainment: theatres, where actors perform the old Noh plays, slow and solemn, with masks, and music. And, just a few years after 1600, in 1603, a young woman named Izumo no Okuni will begin to dance, in the dry bed of the river Kamo, in Kyoto, in a new, lively, colourful way, which will become the beginning of Kabuki theatre.

[long-pause]

The towns are also full of travellers: pilgrims, going to the temples and shrines, and merchants, and messengers, and samurai on their lord's business, and monks, and wandering entertainers.

[pause]

They travel on foot, along the great roads, or on horseback, or in a palanquin, a little box, carried on poles by two or four bearers. Along the roads, there are inns, and tea houses, where travellers can rest, and eat, and sleep.

[pause]

{img:travellers-road-tea-house-straw-hats-palanquin-pines}
In the evening, in the cities, the lanterns are lit, in front of the shops and the tea houses, paper lanterns, glowing orange and red and white, and the people walk in the streets, and visit the temples, and eat, and talk, in the warm spring air.

[long-pause]

{img:samurai-wife-kimono-garden-house-arranging-flowers-spring}
What is life like for women in Japan, in the year 1600?

[pause]

It depends very much on who they are: the wife of a great lord, or of a samurai, or of a merchant, or of a farmer. But for all of them, life is shaped by their families, and by their duty to their fathers, their husbands, and their sons.

[long-pause]

A samurai's daughter is brought up carefully. She learns to read and write, and to write poetry, and to play the koto, and to arrange flowers, and to serve tea, and to sew, and to run a household. She also learns to use a weapon, a long spear with a curved blade, called a naginata, so that she can defend her home, if her husband is away at war.

[pause]

She is married young, often at fourteen or fifteen, to a husband chosen by her family, often for political reasons, to make an alliance between two families. She may never have seen him before the wedding.

[pause]

After her marriage, she goes to live in her husband's house, with his family. She must obey her husband, and her mother-in-law. She runs the household: the servants, and the money, and the food, and the clothes, and the children's education. When her husband is away at war, which is often, she is in charge of everything.

[long-pause]

Many samurai women in this age are strong, and brave, and remarkable.

[pause]

There is Nene, Hideyoshi's widow, who was his wife for almost forty years, from the time when he was a poor foot soldier. She was wise, and kind, and much loved, and was a great help to her husband, and many of the great lords respect her greatly. After Hideyoshi's death, she becomes a Buddhist nun, and lives quietly in Kyoto, in a temple she founded. She is a friend of Ieyasu.

[pause]

{img:elderly-noble-woman-nun-temple-garden-kyoto-moss}
And there is the Lady Yodo, the mother of Hideyori, proud and determined, in Osaka Castle.

[long-pause]

There is Hosokawa Tama, the wife of a great lord, Hosokawa Tadaoki, who becomes a Christian, and is baptized with the name Gracia. She is clever, and well educated, and reads Christian books in Japanese and Portuguese and Latin.

[pause]

And there are the women of the farmers and the merchants, who work just as hard as the men: in the fields, planting the rice, and weeding, and harvesting. And at home, spinning, and weaving, and dyeing, and sewing, and cooking, and looking after the children, and the old people. In the merchant families, women often run the shop, and keep the accounts.

[pause]

{img:farm-women-weaving-cotton-loom-house-doorway-sunlight}
The Portuguese and Spanish missionaries in Japan were surprised by how much freedom Japanese women had, compared with women in Europe. A Jesuit priest, Luís Fróis, wrote that in Japan, women could go wherever they liked, without telling their husbands, and that they could read and write, and that many of them managed their own money.

[long-pause]

What do the women of 1600 wear?

[pause]

They wear the kosode, the ancestor of the kimono: a long robe, with small sleeve openings, wrapped around the body, left side over right, and tied with a sash. The kosode is made of silk for the rich, and of cotton or hemp for the poor. In these years, the kosode of the rich are wonderfully beautiful, painted and dyed and embroidered with bold patterns: flowers, and leaves, and waves, and birds, in bright colours, red and white and blue and gold.

[pause]

Women wear their hair long, very long, sometimes down to the ground, hanging loose down their backs, or tied low, at the back of the neck. Long, black, shining hair is thought to be the greatest beauty of a woman.

[long-pause]

Some women, too, became famous as poets, and writers, and artists, in the long history of Japan.

[pause]

The greatest novel of Japanese literature, The Tale of Genji, was written by a woman, Murasaki Shikibu, at the Emperor's court, six hundred years before 1600. It is still read in 1600, by educated women and men, and painted on screens and scrolls, and quoted in poems. It is one of the oldest novels in the world.

[pause]

{img:painted-scroll-court-ladies-reading-genji-gold-clouds}
In the evening, in the houses of Kyoto, women still read it aloud, to each other, by the light of the lamps: the long, sad, beautiful story of Prince Genji and the ladies he loved, a thousand years before.

[long-pause]

{img:small-tea-house-garden-stepping-stones-moss-lantern}
Of all the arts of Japan in the age of the samurai, the most important, and the most strange to us, is the way of tea: chanoyu, the tea ceremony.

[pause]

Tea came to Japan from China, hundreds of years before, brought by Buddhist monks, who drank it to keep awake during their long meditations. It is a special kind of tea, matcha: green tea leaves, ground into a fine powder, which is mixed with hot water, and whisked into a froth, with a little bamboo whisk, in a wide bowl.

[long-pause]

In the sixteenth century, the drinking of tea became an art, almost a religion, followed by the great lords and the samurai and the rich merchants.

[pause]

The greatest of all the tea masters was Sen no Rikyu, a merchant from Sakai, who served as tea master to Nobunaga, and then to Hideyoshi. He taught that the way of tea should be simple, and quiet, and humble. He called it wabi: the beauty of simple, rough, imperfect, old things, and of quiet loneliness.

[pause]

Rikyu passed away in 1591, after a quarrel with Hideyoshi. But his ideas lived on, and his family's schools of tea still exist today.

[long-pause]

The tea is served in a little tea house, in a garden.

[pause]

The tea house is very small, sometimes only big enough for four or five people, with walls of clay and bamboo, and a thatched roof, like a peasant's hut. To reach it, the guests walk along a path of stepping stones, through a little garden, called the roji, the dewy path, with moss, and ferns, and a stone lantern, and a stone basin of water, where they wash their hands and rinse their mouths, to purify themselves.

[pause]

{img:stone-water-basin-bamboo-ladle-moss-garden-path}
The door of the tea house is very low, so that everyone, even a great lord, must bend down, and crawl in, on his knees. Inside, everyone is equal. A samurai must leave his swords outside, on a rack.

[long-pause]

Inside, the room is quiet, and dim, and simple.

[pause]

There is a little alcove, with a hanging scroll, a piece of calligraphy, or a painting, chosen for the season, and a single flower, in a simple vase of bamboo or pottery. In the floor, there is a small hearth, with a charcoal fire, and an iron kettle, singing softly, as the water heats. The guests kneel on the tatami, and look at the scroll, and the flower, in silence.

[pause]

{img:tea-room-alcove-scroll-single-flower-iron-kettle-steam}
Then the host comes in, carrying the tea utensils, one by one, and arranges them, slowly, carefully, with exact, graceful movements, that have been practised for years: the tea bowl, the tea caddy, the bamboo scoop, the whisk, the water jar, and the ladle.

[long-pause]

He wipes each utensil with a silk cloth, folded in a special way. He scoops the green tea powder into the bowl. He ladles hot water from the kettle. He whisks the tea, until it is smooth, and frothy, and bright green. And he places the bowl in front of the first guest.

[pause]

The guest bows, and lifts the bowl, and turns it a little, so as not to drink from the front, and drinks the tea, in three and a half sips, and wipes the rim of the bowl, and admires it, and passes it back.

[pause]

The whole ceremony may take four hours, with a meal, and two kinds of tea. Every movement is fixed. Nothing is hurried. The guests talk quietly, about the scroll, and the flower, and the bowl, and the season.

[long-pause]

The tea bowls are treasures.

[pause]

The most valued are not the perfect, elegant bowls from China, but simple, rough, uneven bowls, made by hand, by Korean or Japanese potters, with irregular shapes, and thick, dripping glazes, in brown and black and grey and white. A great tea bowl could be worth more than a castle. Lords gave them as rewards to their best generals, instead of land.

[pause]

{img:handmade-raku-tea-bowls-black-red-glaze-shelf-soft}
Why did the fierce samurai love the tea ceremony so much? Perhaps because, in a world of war and danger, it was a moment of peace. A quiet room, a cup of tea, a flower, a few friends, and the sound of the kettle. Ichigo ichie, the tea masters said: one time, one meeting. Every meeting is unique, and will never come again. So treasure it.

[long-pause]

{img:shinto-shrine-red-torii-gate-forest-path-morning}
The people of Japan, in 1600, follow two religions at once, and see nothing strange in it: Shinto and Buddhism.

[pause]

Shinto is the old religion of Japan, older than any written history. It is the worship of the kami: the gods and spirits who live in everything, in the mountains, and the rivers, and the waterfalls, and the old trees, and the rocks, and the sea, and the wind, and the rice.

[long-pause]

There are millions of kami. Some are great gods, like Amaterasu, the goddess of the sun, the ancestor of the Emperor, who is worshipped at the great shrine of Ise. Some are the gods of a particular mountain, or village, or family. The spirits of great people, and of ancestors, can become kami too.

[pause]

The kami live in shrines. A Shinto shrine is a simple, beautiful building, of unpainted wood, or painted bright red, with a roof of thatch, or of cypress bark, standing in a grove of tall, old trees. At the entrance, there is a gate, called a torii, two upright posts, and two crossbeams, often painted red, which marks the boundary between the ordinary world and the sacred world of the gods.

[pause]

{img:shrine-visitor-clapping-hands-bowing-rope-bell-quiet}
When you visit a shrine, you wash your hands and your mouth, with water from a stone basin. Then you go to the front of the shrine, and throw a coin into the offering box, and ring the bell, by pulling a thick rope, to call the god, and bow twice, and clap your hands twice, and pray, and bow once more.

[long-pause]

Shinto is about purity, and cleanliness, and harmony with nature, and with the kami. It is about the seasons, and the harvest, and the life of the village, and the family. Its festivals are joyful, with music, and dancing, and processions, and food, and drink.

[pause]

Buddhism came to Japan from India, through China and Korea, about a thousand years before 1600. It teaches that life is full of suffering, because everything changes, and everything passes, and we are always wanting things we cannot keep. And that the way to escape from suffering is to let go of our desires, and to live wisely, and kindly, and to find enlightenment, as the Buddha did.

[pause]

Buddhism in Japan has many schools. There is Zen, which the samurai love, which teaches meditation, and simplicity. There is the Pure Land school, which teaches that anyone, rich or poor, can be reborn in the paradise of the Buddha Amida, simply by calling on his name, with faith: Namu Amida Butsu. It is the most popular among ordinary people. And there are many others.

[long-pause]

The temples are where the Buddhist monks and nuns live, and study, and pray, and where people come to worship, and to remember their dead.

[pause]

A great temple has a gate, with fierce guardian statues on either side, and a great hall, with a golden statue of the Buddha, glowing in the dim light, among the smoke of the incense, and a pagoda, a tall, slender tower of five storeys, and a bell, a huge bronze bell, hanging in its own little building, which is rung by swinging a great wooden beam against it. Its deep, slow sound carries for miles, over the rooftops and the fields, at dawn and at dusk.

[pause]

{img:temple-bell-tower-bronze-bell-wooden-beam-dusk-mist}
On New Year's Eve, the temple bells are rung a hundred and eight times, one for each of the hundred and eight worldly desires, which, the Buddhists say, cause human suffering.

[long-pause]

For most Japanese people, there is no conflict between Shinto and Buddhism. Many shrines and temples stand side by side, and share the same grounds. People go to the shrine for the joys of life: for births, and weddings, and festivals, and harvests. And they go to the temple for the sorrows: for funerals, and to remember their ancestors.

[pause]

The Japanese say that they are born Shinto, and die Buddhist.

[pause]

{img:mountain-path-small-stone-buddhas-moss-red-bibs-forest}
Along the roads and the mountain paths, everywhere in Japan, there are little stone statues of Jizo, a gentle Buddhist saint, who protects travellers, and children. People put little red bibs and caps on them, and leave flowers, and offerings. They are still there today, smiling quietly, by the roadsides, all over Japan.

[long-pause]

{img:battered-dutch-ship-de-liefde-arriving-kyushu-coast-april}
In April 1600, a strange ship appears off the coast of the island of Kyushu, in the southwest of Japan, near the little port of Usuki, in the province of Bungo.

[pause]

It is a Dutch ship, called De Liefde, which means Love, or Charity. It is battered and broken, its sails torn, its hull covered with weed. And on board, there are only about twenty-four men left alive, out of more than a hundred who set out, and most of them are too weak to stand.

[long-pause]

De Liefde left Rotterdam, in the Netherlands, nearly two years earlier, in June 1598, with four other ships, to sail across the Atlantic, and through the stormy Strait of Magellan, at the bottom of South America, and across the Pacific Ocean, to the East Indies, to trade.

[pause]

It was a terrible voyage. The ships were scattered by storms. Many of the men were lost to illness and hunger and cold, and in fights with the Spanish and the Portuguese, the enemies of the Dutch. Of the five ships, only De Liefde reached Japan. And the voyage across the Pacific took more than four months.

[pause]

The Japanese fishermen and villagers of Bungo come out in their boats, and help the sick men ashore, and give them food and shelter. The local lord sends word to Osaka, to the chief of the Five Regents, Tokugawa Ieyasu.

[long-pause]

On board De Liefde, there is an Englishman, the ship's pilot, the chief navigator. His name is William Adams.

[pause]

He is thirty-five years old, from the town of Gillingham, in Kent, in the south of England, near the river Medway. As a boy of twelve, he was apprenticed to a shipbuilder, and learned how to build ships, and how to navigate them. He sailed with the English fleet against the Spanish Armada, in 1588, and then on trading voyages to Africa, and in the Arctic, with Dutch merchants.

[pause]

{img:english-navigator-1600-weathered-face-ship-cabin-charts}
He has a wife, Mary, and a daughter, Deliverance, at home in England. He has not seen them for two years. And he will never see them again.

[long-pause]

The Portuguese Jesuit priests, who are already in Japan, hear about the arrival of the Dutch ship, and are alarmed.

[pause]

The Portuguese have been trading with Japan for more than fifty years, and the Jesuits have been teaching Christianity there, and have made many converts. The Dutch and the English are Protestants, and enemies of Portugal and Spain. The Jesuits tell the Japanese officials that the newcomers are pirates, and should be put to death.

[pause]

{img:jesuit-priests-black-robes-japanese-officials-temple-hall}
But Ieyasu is curious. He wants to see these strangers for himself. He orders that the ship be brought round to Osaka, and that one of the men be sent to him.

[pause]

William Adams goes.

[long-pause]

In May 1600, Adams is brought before Ieyasu, in his residence in Osaka Castle.

[pause]

He kneels before the great lord, in a great hall, with golden screens, and many samurai sitting around. A Portuguese Jesuit interprets. And Ieyasu asks him question after question, for hours, late into the night.

[pause]

He asks him where he comes from, and why they have come to Japan, and what his country is like. He asks him about the wars between England and Holland and Spain and Portugal. He asks him about his religion. He asks him how they came to Japan, and Adams shows him a map of the world, and shows him the route of the voyage, through the Strait of Magellan. Ieyasu is amazed, and fascinated, and thinks Adams is not telling the truth, until he sees it on the map.

[long-pause]

Then Ieyasu sends Adams to prison. But it is not a cruel prison. Adams is well treated, and fed, and he is visited and questioned again, several times.

[pause]

Adams thinks that he will be put to death. But after about six weeks, Ieyasu sends for him again, and talks to him again, and then lets him go, and lets him return to his companions, and the ship.

[pause]

{img:dutch-sailors-small-japanese-house-garden-recovering}
Ieyasu has decided that these strangers are not pirates, and could be useful to him. He likes Adams: his honesty, and his knowledge, and his practical skill. And he has a plan for him.

[pause]

But first, there are more important matters. Because, that summer, war is coming.

[long-pause]

{img:portuguese-carrack-black-ship-nagasaki-harbour-1600}
The Portuguese were the first Europeans to reach Japan.

[pause]

In 1543, a Chinese ship, with three Portuguese traders on board, was blown off course by a storm, and landed on the little island of Tanegashima, off the south of Kyushu. The Portuguese had guns, matchlock muskets. The lord of the island was so impressed that he bought two of them, and ordered his swordsmiths to copy them. Within a few years, guns were being made all over Japan. For many years, the Japanese called guns tanegashima, after the island.

[long-pause]

After that, every year, a great Portuguese ship came to Japan, from the Portuguese trading city of Macau, in southern China.

[pause]

The Japanese call it the black ship, because its hull is painted black. It is a huge carrack, much bigger than any Japanese ship, with high castles at the bow and stern. It brings silk from China, which the Japanese love, and gold, and medicines, and glass, and clocks, and spectacles, and wine, and tobacco, and many strange new things. And it takes back silver, from the mines of Japan, which the Chinese need.

[pause]

The Japanese call the Portuguese nanban, which means southern barbarians, because they come from the south. And they find them very strange, with their big noses, and their baggy trousers, and their tall hats, and their curly hair, and their dark-skinned African and Indian servants, and their habit of eating with their hands, at tables, and eating meat. Japanese artists paint them on great golden folding screens, called nanban screens, arriving in their black ships, and walking in procession through the streets, with their strange clothes and their animals.

[long-pause]

With the Portuguese traders came the Jesuit priests, the missionaries, who wanted to bring Christianity to Japan.

[pause]

The first was Francis Xavier, a Spanish Jesuit, who came in 1549, and spent two years in Japan. After him, many more Jesuits came, and they made many converts. By 1600, there are perhaps three hundred thousand Christians in Japan, especially in Kyushu, in the southwest, including some great lords.

[pause]

{img:small-wooden-church-japanese-village-cross-kyushu-hills}
The Jesuits learned Japanese, and studied Japanese customs, and wrote dictionaries and grammars, and printed books, in Japanese, on a printing press that they brought from Europe. They built churches, and schools, and hospitals. They admired the Japanese very much. Francis Xavier wrote that the Japanese were the best people yet discovered.

[long-pause]

The port of Nagasaki, on the west coast of Kyushu, was a little fishing village, until a Christian lord gave it to the Jesuits, in 1580, as a place for the Portuguese ships to come.

[pause]

Now it is a busy town, with churches, and Portuguese merchants, and Japanese Christians, and warehouses, and a great harbour, surrounded by green hills, where the black ship anchors every summer.

[pause]

{img:nagasaki-bay-green-hills-town-churches-1600-calm}
When the black ship comes in, the whole town celebrates, and merchants come from all over Japan, to buy the silk, and the other goods, and the town is full of noise, and colour, and trade.

[long-pause]

But some Japanese leaders are worried about the Christians.

[pause]

Hideyoshi was suspicious. He feared that the missionaries were the advance guard of a Spanish or Portuguese invasion, as had happened in the Philippines, and in the Americas. In 1587, he ordered the missionaries to leave Japan, although he did not enforce the order very strictly. And in 1597, he had twenty-six Christians put to death in Nagasaki, to show his power.

[pause]

Ieyasu, in 1600, is more relaxed. He wants the trade with the Portuguese, and he is not much interested in religion. But he too is watchful. And that is one reason why he is so interested in the Dutch and English newcomers, who are enemies of Portugal, and want only to trade, and not to make converts.

[long-pause]

The Europeans bring other new things to Japan, too, which the Japanese take up eagerly.

[pause]

Tobacco, and the custom of smoking it, in long, thin pipes. Bread, which the Japanese call pan, from the Portuguese word pão, and is still called pan in Japan today. Sponge cake, called castella, which is still a famous speciality of Nagasaki. And, it is said, a way of frying fish and vegetables in batter, which the Portuguese ate during the fasting days of the church calendar, the tempora. Today, we call it tempura.

[pause]

{img:castella-sponge-cake-tea-lacquer-tray-nagasaki-style}
The Japanese also learn about European maps, and globes, and clocks, and spectacles, and medicine, and astronomy. Japan in 1600 is open to the world, curious, and eager to learn.

[long-pause]

{img:mountain-castle-aizu-north-summer-1600-forests}
In the summer of 1600, the struggle for power in Japan comes out into the open.

[pause]

On one side, there is Tokugawa Ieyasu, the most powerful lord in Japan, with his great lands in the east, and his loyal followers, and many allies among the other lords.

[long-pause]

On the other side, there is a group of lords who are loyal to the Toyotomi family, and to the boy Hideyori, and who believe that Ieyasu wants to take the country for himself.

[pause]

Their leader is Ishida Mitsunari. He is not a great lord, or a great general. He is an administrator, one of Hideyoshi's most trusted officials, a clever, hard-working, honest man, devoted to the memory of Hideyoshi. But he is proud, and cold, and many of the other lords, especially the soldiers, dislike him.

[pause]

There is a story that, when Mitsunari was a young boy, a temple servant, Hideyoshi stopped at his temple, on a hot day, while out hunting, and asked for tea. The boy brought him a large bowl of lukewarm tea first, to quench his thirst. Then a smaller bowl, a little hotter. And then a tiny cup, very hot, to taste. Hideyoshi was so impressed by the boy's thoughtfulness that he took him into his service.

[long-pause]

In the early summer of 1600, Ieyasu marches away from Osaka, with his army, to the northeast, far away, to punish a lord named Uesugi Kagekatsu, who has been building up his castles and his army, in the mountains of the north, and refusing to come to Osaka.

[pause]

Perhaps this is a trap, set by Ieyasu himself. He knows that, when he leaves, Mitsunari will move against him.

[pause]

{img:long-army-column-marching-road-banners-summer-hills}
And he is right. As soon as Ieyasu is gone, Mitsunari gathers the lords of the west, and declares war on Ieyasu, in the name of Hideyori.

[long-pause]

The first thing Mitsunari does is to try to take hostage the wives and families of the lords who have gone with Ieyasu, who are living in Osaka.

[pause]

Some of the families escape. Some are taken. And one of them, the Christian lady, Hosokawa Gracia, refuses to be taken hostage. She will not let her husband's enemies use her against him. And in her house in Osaka, she chooses to end her life, with the help of a loyal servant, rather than be captured. Her house is set alight. It is one of the saddest stories of that summer.

[pause]

After that, Mitsunari stops trying to take hostages. And many lords who might have joined him, angry at what has happened, join Ieyasu instead.

[long-pause]

Then Mitsunari's army attacks Fushimi Castle, near Kyoto, which Ieyasu has left in the care of an old friend and loyal follower, Torii Mototada, with a small garrison of about two thousand men.

[pause]

Before Ieyasu left, he and Torii sat together, at Fushimi, and drank sake, late into the night, and talked about old times. Both of them knew that Torii would be attacked, and could not hold out for long, against a great army. They said goodbye, as old friends, knowing they would not meet again.

[pause]

{img:two-old-samurai-friends-drinking-sake-castle-room-night}
Torii holds the castle for eleven days, against an army of forty thousand. And when the castle falls, at last, he and his men are lost. But the eleven days give Ieyasu time.

[long-pause]

When Ieyasu hears the news, far away in the east, he turns his army around, and marches back toward the west.

[pause]

He leaves his son, Hidetada, with another large army, to come by a different road, through the mountains. And Ieyasu himself goes by the main road, the Tokaido, along the coast, and then inland, toward Kyoto.

[pause]

All through the late summer and autumn of 1600, the lords of Japan must decide which side to join: the east, with Ieyasu, or the west, with Mitsunari. Families are divided. Old friends become enemies. Many lords write secret letters to both sides, waiting to see who will win.

[long-pause]

By the middle of October, the two great armies are approaching each other, in the middle of Japan, near a little village on the main road between the east and the west, in a narrow valley between the mountains, in the province of Mino.

[pause]

The village is called Sekigahara, which means the plain of the barrier, because in ancient times there was a barrier gate there, on the road, where travellers were stopped and checked.

[pause]

{img:narrow-valley-sekigahara-mountains-autumn-rain-village}
On the night of the twentieth of October, 1600, it rains heavily. The two armies march through the rain and the darkness, and take up their positions, on the hills around the little plain. Altogether, there are perhaps a hundred and sixty thousand men, on both sides.

[pause]

And in the morning, there is fog.

[long-pause]

{img:thick-autumn-fog-valley-hills-banners-faint-dawn}
On the morning of the twenty-first of October, 1600, by our calendar, the valley of Sekigahara is filled with thick fog.

[pause]

The soldiers of the two armies cannot see each other, even though they are only a few hundred metres apart. They wait, in the cold and the damp, in their armour, with their banners hanging limp in the still air, listening, and hearing only the sounds of horses, and men, and the dripping of the rain from the trees.

[long-pause]

The army of the west, Mitsunari's army, holds the hills, on the west and the south of the valley, in a strong position, in a great curve, like a crane's wings, with the valley in front of them. It is a good position. If the army of the east comes into the valley, it will be surrounded on three sides.

[pause]

On a high hill, to the south, called Mount Matsuo, stands the army of a young lord, Kobayakawa Hideaki, with about fifteen thousand men. He is only eighteen years old, a nephew of Hideyoshi's wife. He is supposed to be on the side of the west.

[pause]

But Ieyasu has sent him secret letters. And Kobayakawa has not yet decided which side to take.

[long-pause]

At about eight o'clock, the fog begins to lift. And the battle begins.

[pause]

We will not go down into the valley. We will watch from far away, from the hills, as if we were standing on a quiet slope, among the trees, looking down at the valley, where thousands of tiny figures are moving, under their banners, in the morning light.

[pause]

{img:distant-valley-view-tiny-banners-moving-hills-morning-light}
From far away, it would have looked like a great, confused, moving pattern of colours, the flags and banners of hundreds of companies, red and white and black and gold, moving to and fro across the small valley, with smoke drifting over it, from the guns, and the sound, far off, like distant thunder, and drums, and conch shell trumpets.

[long-pause]

For several hours, the battle goes back and forth. Neither side can win.

[pause]

On the hill of Mount Matsuo, young Kobayakawa still waits, and watches, and does nothing. Both sides send messengers to him, begging him to attack. Mitsunari lights a signal fire. Ieyasu waits.

[pause]

It is said that, at last, around midday, Ieyasu grows impatient, and orders his gunners to fire toward Kobayakawa's hill, to force him to make up his mind.

[pause]

And Kobayakawa decides. He orders his army to come down from the hill, and attack the army of the west.

[long-pause]

After that, the battle is quickly over.

[pause]

Other lords of the western army, seeing Kobayakawa change sides, change sides too, or stay where they are, and do nothing. The western army breaks, and scatters, and flees into the mountains. By the middle of the afternoon, it is all over.

[pause]

{img:empty-battlefield-afternoon-quiet-banners-fallen-grass-mist}
Ieyasu has won. In a single day, in a few hours, on a little plain in the middle of Japan, the future of the country for the next two hundred and fifty years has been decided.

[long-pause]

The ancient chronicles say that, after the battle, Ieyasu sat on his camp stool, and took off his helmet, for the first time that day, and put on a light headband. And when his generals came to congratulate him, he said, quietly, an old proverb: after a victory, tighten the cords of your helmet.

[pause]

Meaning: do not relax. The struggle is not over.

[pause]

Ishida Mitsunari escapes into the mountains, but is found a few days later, and taken to Kyoto, and is put to death, with two other leaders of the west. He meets his end calmly, it is said, with great dignity.

[long-pause]

And Ieyasu's son, Hidetada, with his great army, arrives at Sekigahara a few days after the battle. He has been delayed in the mountains, besieging a castle, and has missed everything.

[pause]

Ieyasu is so angry with him that, at first, he refuses to see him.

[pause]

{img:rain-on-mountain-road-army-arriving-late-apologetic}
But in the end, he forgives him. And Hidetada will become the second shogun, and a good one.

[long-pause]

{img:edo-castle-construction-stone-walls-workers-1603-spring}
After Sekigahara, Tokugawa Ieyasu is the master of Japan.

[pause]

He takes the lands of the lords who fought against him, and gives them to his own family, and his loyal followers, and the lords who joined him. He leaves the boy Hideyori in Osaka Castle, with the Lady Yodo, as a great lord, but no longer the ruler of Japan.

[long-pause]

In 1603, the Emperor gives Ieyasu the title of shogun.

[pause]

He is the first of the Tokugawa shoguns. There will be fifteen of them, one after another, from father to son, or to adopted son, for two hundred and sixty-five years, until 1868. Historians call it the Edo period, after the city that becomes the capital of the shoguns. Or the Tokugawa period.

[pause]

And for all those years, there is peace in Japan. No more wars between the lords. No more burned villages, and trampled fields. Two hundred and fifty years of peace. It is one of the longest periods of peace in the history of any country in the world.

[long-pause]

Ieyasu makes Edo his capital, instead of Kyoto, or Osaka.

[pause]

He orders the lords of Japan to help him build a great castle there, the largest in Japan, with great stone walls, and moats, and gates, and towers. Huge blocks of stone are brought by ship, from the mountains of the Izu peninsula, hundreds of them, each one carried by hundreds of men. Hills are dug away, and the earth used to fill in the marshes and the bay. Canals are dug, and rivers moved.

[pause]

{img:huge-stone-block-hauled-by-many-workers-ropes-chanting}
And around the castle, a great city grows up, with astonishing speed. By the end of the seventeenth century, Edo will have about a million people, making it one of the largest cities in the world, larger than London or Paris.

[long-pause]

To keep the peace, the Tokugawa shoguns make strict rules for everyone.

[pause]

The lords must ask permission to repair their castles, or to marry their children. They may have only one castle each. And later, they must spend every other year in Edo, near the shogun, and leave their wives and children there, when they go home. This system is called sankin kotai, alternate attendance.

[pause]

So, every year, great processions of lords and their samurai, hundreds or thousands of men, travel along the great roads of Japan, to and from Edo, with their banners, and their palanquins, and their baggage. The people along the road must kneel, as they pass. The lords spend enormous sums of money on these journeys, and on their great houses in Edo, and so they have little money left to make war.

[long-pause]

The samurai, in the long peace, change too.

[pause]

They have no wars to fight. They become officials, and administrators, and scholars, and teachers. They still wear their two swords, and practise the martial arts, but as a discipline, and a way of life, more than for fighting. They study the Chinese classics, and write books about the way of the warrior, bushido, which describe an ideal samurai, more perfect than any samurai of the age of war had ever been.

[pause]

{img:peaceful-samurai-reading-book-garden-veranda-autumn-maple}
Many samurai, in the long peace, become poor. Their salaries in rice stay the same, while prices rise. Some of them have to take work as teachers, or craftsmen, or even sell their swords. And the merchants, whom they look down on, become rich.

[long-pause]

In 1635, the Tokugawa shoguns make another great decision. They close Japan to the outside world.

[pause]

They are afraid of the Christians, and of the Spanish and the Portuguese, and of foreign ideas, and foreign wars. So they forbid Christianity, and drive out the Portuguese. Japanese people are forbidden to leave Japan, and those who are abroad are forbidden to come home.

[pause]

Only a few foreign traders are allowed to stay: the Chinese, and the Dutch, who are allowed to keep a little trading station, on a tiny, fan-shaped artificial island, in Nagasaki harbour, called Dejima. For more than two hundred years, the little Dutch post at Dejima will be Japan's only window on the Western world.

[long-pause]

This is called sakoku, the closed country.

[pause]

It lasts until 1853, when American warships, the black ships of Commodore Perry, sail into Edo Bay, and force Japan to open its doors again. Fifteen years later, in 1868, the last Tokugawa shogun gives up his power, and the young Emperor Meiji moves from Kyoto to Edo, and renames it Tokyo, the eastern capital.

[pause]

{img:edo-bay-1600s-calm-fishing-boats-castle-distant-sunset}
But all of that is far in the future. In the years after 1600, the long peace is just beginning.

[long-pause]

{img:edo-period-city-street-festival-lanterns-crowds-evening}
The long peace that begins in 1600 changes Japan in many ways.

[pause]

With no more wars, the population grows, from perhaps fifteen million to about thirty million, by the eighteenth century. The farmers clear new land, and dig new canals, and grow more rice. Roads and bridges are built. Trade grows. The cities grow large and rich.

[long-pause]

And a new culture grows up, in the cities, among the merchants and the ordinary townspeople.

[pause]

There are theatres: the Kabuki theatre, with its colourful costumes and its dramatic stories, and the Bunraku puppet theatre, in Osaka, with its great puppets, each worked by three men, in black, on the stage. There are tea houses, and restaurants, and bath houses, and gardens, and festivals.

[pause]

{img:kabuki-theatre-stage-colourful-actors-audience-lanterns}
There are books, printed with wooden blocks, for ordinary people to read: novels, and guidebooks, and cookbooks, and books of jokes, and poems. Many ordinary people, in the cities and even in the villages, learn to read, at small schools, often in Buddhist temples, called terakoya. By the nineteenth century, Japan will have one of the highest rates of reading in the world.

[long-pause]

There is poetry.

[pause]

The most famous poet of the Edo period is Matsuo Basho, who was born in 1644, the son of a samurai, and became a wandering poet, travelling all over Japan, on foot, with his straw hat and his stick, and writing short poems, of seventeen syllables, about what he saw: the seasons, and nature, and the small things of life.

[pause]

We call these poems haiku. One of the most famous is about an old pond: an old pond, a frog jumps in, the sound of water. That is all. But in those few words, there is a whole world of quietness.

[long-pause]

And there are pictures.

[pause]

In the Edo period, artists begin to make colourful prints, with carved wooden blocks, which can be printed again and again, cheaply, so that ordinary people can buy them, and hang them on their walls. They are called ukiyo-e, pictures of the floating world: the world of pleasure, and beauty, and the passing moment.

[pause]

{img:woodblock-print-style-great-wave-mount-fuji-boats}
The prints show actors, and beautiful women, and landscapes, and famous places, and birds, and flowers. Two hundred years after 1600, an old artist called Hokusai will make a series of prints, Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji, including a famous picture of a great wave, curling over little boats, with Mount Fuji far away behind it, small and calm. It will become one of the most famous pictures in the world.

[long-pause]

Life in the long peace is not perfect.

[pause]

The rules are strict, and the shogun's government watches everything, and punishes anyone who breaks the rules. The four classes, samurai, farmers, craftsmen and merchants, are kept apart. The farmers are often poor, and heavily taxed, and there are terrible famines, in bad years. There are earthquakes, and fires, and floods. In Edo, the fires are so frequent that people call them the flowers of Edo.

[pause]

{img:edo-fire-watchtower-wooden-ladder-bell-quiet-night}
But for two hundred and fifty years, there is no war. And the people of Japan, on the whole, live in peace, and grow their rice, and go to their festivals, and look at the cherry blossoms, and the autumn leaves, and the moon, and write their poems.

[pause]

It is perhaps the greatest gift that Tokugawa Ieyasu ever gave to his country.

[long-pause]

{img:william-adams-japanese-clothes-two-swords-ship-yard-ito}
And what became of William Adams, the English pilot of the Dutch ship De Liefde?

[pause]

Ieyasu kept him in Japan. He would not let him go home. But he treated him very well, and gave him an important place at his court.

[long-pause]

At first, Ieyasu asked Adams to build him a ship, in the European style. Adams had been trained as a shipbuilder, as a boy, in England. So, at the little port of Ito, on the Izu peninsula, south of Edo, with Japanese carpenters, he built a small sailing ship, of about eighty tons, the first Western-style ship ever built in Japan.

[pause]

Ieyasu was delighted. He asked Adams to build another, larger ship. And that ship later sailed all the way across the Pacific Ocean, to Mexico.

[pause]

Ieyasu also asked Adams to teach him about mathematics, and geometry, and navigation, and astronomy, and the countries of the world. He liked to talk with him, about Europe, and its wars, and its kings, and its religions.

[long-pause]

Ieyasu made Adams a samurai, one of the very few Europeans ever to become a samurai. He gave him the right to wear two swords, and gave him a Japanese name: Miura Anjin. Anjin means pilot, and Miura is the name of the peninsula, near Edo, where Ieyasu gave him an estate, a village, with farms and farmers, at a place called Hemi.

[pause]

Adams learned to speak Japanese fluently, and dressed in Japanese clothes, and lived in the Japanese way. He married a Japanese woman, Oyuki, the daughter of an official, and they had two children, a son, Joseph, and a daughter, Susanna.

[pause]

{img:english-samurai-family-japanese-house-garden-wife-children}
He also had a house in Edo, in a street that was later called Anjin-cho, the pilot's street, in his honour.

[long-pause]

Adams still longed to go home to England, to see his first wife and his daughter. He asked Ieyasu many times to let him go. But Ieyasu always refused. He valued him too much.

[pause]

In 1613, an English ship, the Clove, at last arrived in Japan, under Captain John Saris, to start trade between England and Japan. Adams helped them to set up an English trading post, at Hirado, a little island port in the far west of Kyushu, and worked for them.

[pause]

{img:hirado-small-port-island-trading-houses-ships-1613}
By then, Ieyasu had at last given Adams permission to go home. But he decided to stay in Japan. Perhaps he felt that Japan was now his home. Perhaps he did not get on with Captain Saris, who thought he had become too Japanese. He sent money to his wife and daughter in England, instead.

[long-pause]

William Adams passed away in 1620, at Hirado, at the age of fifty-five.

[pause]

His grave is still there, on a hill at Hirado, looking out over the sea. And there is a monument to him in Tokyo, at the place where his house once stood, and another at Hemi, on the Miura peninsula, where his estate was. And at Ito, where he built the first Western ship, there is a festival every summer, in his honour, the Anjin festival.

[pause]

And in Gillingham, in Kent, where he was born, there is a monument to him too, a tall clock tower, and every year, the people of Gillingham, and visitors from Japan, remember him together.

[long-pause]

His story has been told many times.

[pause]

In 1975, the American writer James Clavell wrote a famous novel, called Shogun, based on the life of William Adams, but with the names changed, and many adventures added. The Englishman in the novel is called John Blackthorne, and the great lord is called Toranaga. It was made into a television series, in 1980. And in 2024, it was made again, as a new series, which was watched by millions of people all over the world, and won many prizes.

[pause]

{img:quiet-japanese-coast-evening-pine-tree-old-ship-anchored}
So the story of the Englishman who became a samurai, in the year of Sekigahara, is still told today, more than four hundred years later.

[long-pause]

{img:sunpu-castle-mount-fuji-view-old-lord-garden-walk}
Tokugawa Ieyasu is shogun for only two years.

[pause]

In 1605, he gives the title to his son, Hidetada, to show everyone that the office will stay in the Tokugawa family, from father to son. And he retires, to the castle of Sunpu, in the town now called Shizuoka, on the coast, with a fine view of Mount Fuji.

[long-pause]

But he does not really retire. From Sunpu, he still rules Japan, behind the scenes, for another ten years.

[pause]

He works, and reads, and receives visitors, and writes letters, and goes hawking in the fields, and makes his medicines, and practises swimming, even in his old age. He collects books, and has old Japanese and Chinese books printed, with movable type, so that they will not be lost.

[pause]

{img:old-lord-reading-books-sunpu-room-garden-autumn-light}
And he still worries about one thing: the young Hideyori, in Osaka Castle.

[long-pause]

Hideyori grows up to be a tall, handsome, polite young man. Many lords and samurai who are unhappy with the Tokugawa look to him, and to his great castle, and his great wealth.

[pause]

Ieyasu fears that, after his own death, Hideyori might become the centre of a new war. And so, in 1614 and 1615, in his old age, Ieyasu fights one more war: against Osaka Castle.

[pause]

We will tell it only briefly, and from far away. In two great sieges, in the winter of 1614 and the summer of 1615, the Tokugawa armies surround Osaka Castle. And in the summer of 1615, the great castle falls. Hideyori, and his mother, the Lady Yodo, are lost. The Toyotomi family comes to an end.

[pause]

Now there is no one left who can challenge the Tokugawa. The long peace is secure.

[long-pause]

In the spring of 1616, Ieyasu falls ill, perhaps, as the story goes, after eating too much fried sea bream, cooked in the new way, with oil, which was fashionable at that time.

[pause]

He knows that he is dying. He calls his son, Hidetada, and his followers, and gives them his last instructions, for the government of the country. And he passes away peacefully, at Sunpu, in June 1616, at the age of seventy-three.

[pause]

{img:quiet-castle-room-morning-light-folding-screen-flowers}
He asks to be buried first on the mountain of Kuno, near Sunpu, by the sea. And a year later, his remains are moved to Nikko, in the mountains north of Edo, where his grandson will later build a great shrine to him, the Toshogu.

[long-pause]

At the Toshogu shrine, Ieyasu is worshipped as a god, the Tosho Daigongen, the great god who illuminates the east.

[pause]

The shrine is one of the most splendid buildings in Japan, in a forest of great cedar trees, on a mountainside. It is covered with carvings, thousands of them, painted in bright colours, red and green and blue and gold: dragons, and lions, and tigers, and birds, and flowers, and people, and elephants, carved by a sculptor who had never seen an elephant.

[pause]

{img:nikko-toshogu-ornate-gate-carvings-gold-cedar-forest}
There is a famous little carving, over the door of the stable of the sacred horses, of three monkeys. One covers its eyes, one covers its ears, and one covers its mouth. See no evil, hear no evil, speak no evil. They are still there today.

[long-pause]

And the road to Nikko is lined with an avenue of great cedar trees, more than twelve thousand of them, planted by one of Ieyasu's lords, over twenty years, as an offering, because he was too poor to give a stone lantern, like the other lords.

[pause]

The trees are still there today, nearly four hundred years old, tall and dark and silent, making a long green tunnel, for many miles, along the old road.

[long-pause]

{img:sekigahara-battlefield-today-quiet-field-banners-memorial}
Today, more than four hundred years later, you can still visit the places of Japan in the year 1600.

[pause]

The little valley of Sekigahara is still there, in the middle of Japan, between the mountains, with rice fields, and small villages, and a railway line, and a motorway, running through it. On the battlefield, there are little stone markers, with flags, where the lords set up their camps. You can walk from one to another, through the fields and up the wooded hills.

[long-pause]

There is a modern museum at Sekigahara, opened in 2020, where you can learn about the battle, and see armour, and weapons, and banners, and watch films, and even try on a samurai's armour. And from the top of the museum, you can look out over the whole valley, and see the hills where the armies stood.

[pause]

On the twenty-first of October each year, or close to it, there is a festival at Sekigahara, with people dressed in samurai armour, marching through the village, with banners, and drums.

[pause]

Mount Matsuo, where young Kobayakawa waited and watched, is still covered with trees, and you can climb to the top, along a path through the forest, and look down, as he did.

[long-pause]

Himeji Castle still stands, perfect and white, on its hill, the White Heron Castle. It survived the wars, and the earthquakes, and the bombing of the Second World War, when most of the city around it was destroyed. It is a World Heritage Site, and every spring, when the cherry trees around it are in blossom, thousands of people come to see it.

[pause]

Osaka Castle still stands too, rebuilt in concrete, in 1931, on the old stone walls of the castle of Hideyoshi and the Tokugawa. Inside, there is a museum, about the life of Hideyoshi.

[pause]

{img:himeji-castle-cherry-blossoms-visitors-spring-blue-sky}
And in Tokyo, where Edo once stood, the great stone walls and the moats of Edo Castle are still there, in the middle of the modern city, surrounded by skyscrapers. The castle buildings are gone. Today, the Emperor of Japan lives inside the walls, in the Imperial Palace, among the trees. And the people of Tokyo run around the moats, in the evenings, and walk under the cherry trees, along the old walls, in the spring.

[long-pause]

Kyoto is still a city of temples, more than a thousand of them, and gardens, and old wooden houses, and narrow streets. You can still see the Golden Pavilion, shining by its pond, and the wooden stage of Kiyomizu, on its hill. You can still drink tea, in the old tea houses, and watch the tea ceremony, done in the same way as in the time of Rikyu.

[pause]

At Nikko, in the mountains, the great shrine of Ieyasu, the Toshogu, still stands, among the cedar trees, with its carved and painted gates, and its three little monkeys. And the long avenue of cedars still leads to it.

[pause]

{img:kyoto-quiet-lane-wooden-houses-lanterns-evening-rain}
In Hirado, on its island, you can visit the grave of William Adams, on its hill above the sea. And at Ito, every August, the Anjin festival is held, with boat races, and fireworks over the bay, in memory of the English pilot who became a samurai.

[long-pause]

And all over Japan, the old ways of the seasons go on.

[pause]

The cherry blossoms still come every spring, and the people still go out to sit under them, with their families and friends, and eat, and drink, and talk, and take photographs. The rice is still planted in the spring, in the flooded fields, and harvested in the autumn. The lanterns are still floated down the rivers at Obon. The moon is still admired in the autumn. And the maple leaves still turn red on the hillsides.

[pause]

The samurai are gone. Their swords are in museums. But much of the world they knew is still there, in the castles and the temples and the gardens, and in the way the people of Japan still live with the seasons.

[long-pause]

So we leave the islands of Japan, in the year of Sekigahara, as the autumn mist settles over the valleys, and the long peace is about to begin.

[pause]

The farmers in their rice fields, the samurai in their castle towns, the tea master by his singing kettle, and an English pilot, far from home, learning a new name.
