Most of the people of Japan, in 1600, are farmers. And the most important thing in their lives, and in the life of the whole country, is rice.

Rice is food, and it is also money. The wealth of a lord is measured in koku of rice: one koku is about the amount of rice that one person eats in a year. A small lord might have ten thousand koku. Ieyasu has two and a half million. The samurai are paid in rice. And the farmers pay their taxes in rice: often forty or fifty percent of their harvest, or even more.

--

Growing rice is hard work, and the whole village must work together.

In the early spring, the farmers prepare the fields: they plough them, with oxen, or with hoes, and build up the little walls of earth, around each field, and repair the channels that bring water from the rivers and the streams. Meanwhile, the rice seeds are sown, thickly, in a small seedbed, to sprout.

{img:farmer-ploughing-ox-muddy-field-mountains-spring-mist}
Then, in May or June, the fields are flooded with water, and the whole village comes out, men and women and children, to plant the young rice plants, one by one, by hand, in neat rows, in the warm mud, bending over, all day long, with their legs in the water. It is back-breaking work. But they sing as they work, rice-planting songs, to keep the rhythm, and sometimes musicians play drums and flutes, at the edge of the field.

--

All through the summer, the farmers look after the rice.

They weed the fields, by hand, wading through the water. They watch the water level, letting water in and out. They chase away the birds, and the insects. They pray to the gods of the rice, at the little shrines at the edges of the fields, for good weather, and a good harvest. The rice grows tall and green, and then, in the late summer, golden.

In the autumn, it is harvested, with sickles, by hand, and tied into bundles, and hung up on wooden racks, in the fields, to dry in the sun. Then it is threshed, beaten, to separate the grains from the straw. And the best of the rice is packed into straw bags, and carried to the lord's storehouse, as taxes.

--

Nothing is wasted. The rice straw is used to make sandals, and raincoats, and hats, and ropes, and mats, and roofs, and bags, and the tatami mats of the houses. The rice husks are used as fuel, and fertilizer.

Besides rice, the farmers grow barley and wheat, in the winter, in the dry fields, and millet, and soybeans, and radishes, and turnips, and eggplants, and sweet potatoes. They grow cotton, and mulberry trees, to feed silkworms, and tea bushes, on the hillsides. They keep chickens. And they go into the mountain forests, to gather firewood, and mushrooms, and wild vegetables, and chestnuts.

The farmers' houses are made of wood, with thick roofs of thatch, steep, to let the rain and the snow slide off. Inside, there is a large room, with an earth floor, near the door, where the work is done, and a raised floor, of wooden boards, with a fireplace, a square hearth sunk into the floor, called an irori, where the family cooks and eats and warms itself, around the fire, with an iron kettle hanging over it, from a hook on the ceiling.

--

In 1588, Hideyoshi ordered the great sword hunt.

All the farmers of Japan had to give up their swords, and their spears, and their guns. Hideyoshi said that the metal would be used to make a great statue of the Buddha, in Kyoto, so that the farmers would be blessed, in this life and the next. But the real reason was that he wanted to make sure that the farmers could never rise up against their lords, as they had often done in the age of the warring states.

{img:village-festival-shrine-lanterns-drums-farmers-dancing}
From now on, the farmers will be farmers, and the samurai will be samurai, and nobody will be allowed to change from one to the other. A farmer's son must be a farmer. A samurai's son must be a samurai.

--

The village is the centre of the farmers' world.

Each village has a headman, chosen from the leading families, who is responsible for collecting the taxes, and keeping order, and dealing with the lord's officials. The villagers help each other, at planting and harvest, and in building houses, and in times of trouble. They have their own shrine, to the local god, the ujigami, and their own temple.

{img:village-headman-house-gate-persimmon-tree-autumn}
And they have festivals: at the New Year, and in the spring, at planting time, and in the summer, at Obon, the festival of the dead, when the spirits of the ancestors come home, and are welcomed with lanterns, and dances. And in the autumn, at harvest, the greatest festival of all, when the whole village gives thanks, and feasts, and dances, and carries the portable shrine of the god through the village, on the shoulders of the young men, shouting and singing.
