TOPIC: The long age of war and the three great lords
===
IMG: burned-kyoto-temple-ruins-civil-war-smoke | the ruins of burned wooden temples and houses in Kyoto during the age of civil war in Japan, blackened beams, a few townspeople clearing the rubble, a grey sky, quiet sad atmosphere
HEAD: Before 1600, Japan had suffered more than a century of civil war.
BODY: In 1467, a quarrel between powerful families broke out into war in Kyoto. It was called the Onin War. It lasted ten years and left much of the capital in ruins. After that, the central government lost its power. Across Japan, local lords fought each other for land. Historians call this time the Sengoku period, the age of warring states. It lasted for more than a hundred years. Castles were built and destroyed. Families rose and fell. Villages were burned, and fields were trampled by armies. Yet daily life did not stop. Farmers still planted their rice. Merchants still traded. Some regions knew long periods of peace. Even so, the danger of war was never far away. Many people longed for a ruler strong enough to bring the whole country together again.
===
IMG: oda-nobunaga-portrait-style-samurai-lord-armour | a stern samurai lord in rich armour and a dark cloak standing on a hill above a castle under construction in the 1570s, soldiers and workers below, dramatic evening light
HEAD: The first of the three great unifiers was Oda Nobunaga, a bold and ruthless lord.
BODY: Oda Nobunaga came from a small domain in central Japan. As a young man, he was thought to be wild and strange. Yet he proved to be a brilliant general. In 1560, he defeated a much larger army in a surprise attack during a storm. In 1568, he marched into Kyoto. He used guns on a large scale and built a magnificent castle at Azuchi. He welcomed the Portuguese and their Jesuit priests. He also fought the powerful Buddhist temples without mercy. By the early 1580s, he controlled much of central Japan. Then, in 1582, one of his own generals turned against him. Nobunaga was trapped in the Honno-ji temple in Kyoto. The temple was set on fire, and he died there. His body was never found. Historians still debate why his general betrayed him.
===
IMG: toyotomi-hideyoshi-seated-castle-hall-retainers | a small but commanding samurai ruler in rich robes seated in a golden castle hall in the 1590s, retainers kneeling in rows before him, painted screens behind, warm light
HEAD: The second unifier, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, rose from a humble background to rule all of Japan.
BODY: Toyotomi Hideyoshi was born into a poor family in a farming village. He began as a simple foot soldier in Nobunaga's army. Through skill and hard work, he rose to become one of Nobunaga's best generals. After Nobunaga's death, Hideyoshi quickly defeated his rivals. By 1590, he had brought all of Japan under his control. He never became shogun, because of his low birth. Instead, he took other high titles from the emperor. Hideyoshi carried out great reforms. He ordered the survey of the land and the hunt for farmers' swords. He also fixed the social classes more firmly. Yet his last years were troubled. He launched two invasions of Korea, in 1592 and 1597. They cost many lives and failed. Hideyoshi died in 1598, leaving a young son only five years old.
===
IMG: council-of-five-elders-castle-room-tension-1599 | five powerful samurai lords in formal robes kneeling in a tense meeting in a castle room in 1599, a low table with documents between them, painted screens, cool grey light
HEAD: Before he died, Hideyoshi set up a council to rule until his son grew up.
BODY: Hideyoshi worried about the future of his son, Hideyori. So he chose five great lords to govern Japan together. They were known as the Five Elders. He also chose five officials to manage daily affairs. On his deathbed, Hideyoshi asked them all to protect his son. They promised to do so. Yet the balance did not last long. The most powerful of the Five Elders was Tokugawa Ieyasu. He held vast lands in the east, around the town of Edo. Many lords began to look to him for leadership. Others feared that he wanted to rule Japan himself. Historians note that Ieyasu began to arrange marriages between his family and other lords. This had been forbidden by Hideyoshi's rules. Tension grew between Ieyasu and Hideyoshi's loyal official, Ishida Mitsunari.
===
IMG: tokugawa-ieyasu-patient-lord-garden-1600 | an older, calm samurai lord in a dark kimono sitting on a veranda overlooking a quiet castle garden in 1600, a falcon on a perch beside him, autumn trees, thoughtful mood
HEAD: The third great unifier was Tokugawa Ieyasu, a patient and careful lord.
BODY: Tokugawa Ieyasu was born in 1543. As a boy, he was sent as a hostage to other families. He spent many years living in the households of powerful lords. He learned patience and caution. As an adult, he became an ally of Nobunaga and later a follower of Hideyoshi. In 1590, Hideyoshi moved Ieyasu to the eastern plains, far from Kyoto. Some saw this as a punishment. Yet Ieyasu turned it into a strength. He built up the small castle town of Edo. He organised his lands carefully and gathered loyal followers. He loved falconry and was careful about his health. A Japanese saying later compared the three unifiers. Nobunaga mixed the rice cake. Hideyoshi baked it. Ieyasu sat down and ate it. In 1600, he was fifty-seven years old.
===
IMG: ishida-mitsunari-official-writing-letters-night | a serious samurai official writing letters by lamplight in a quiet room at night in 1600, several sealed letters beside him, a messenger kneeling at the door, dark shadows
HEAD: Against Ieyasu stood Ishida Mitsunari, a loyal official of the Toyotomi family.
BODY: Ishida Mitsunari had served Hideyoshi for many years. He was an able administrator. He had organised supplies for the armies in Korea and helped with the land survey. Yet many generals disliked him. They saw him as proud and cold. Some blamed him for problems during the war in Korea. Mitsunari believed that Ieyasu was a threat to Hideyori. He began to gather allies among the western lords. He sent letters across the country, asking lords to join him. In the summer of 1600, Ieyasu marched north to deal with a rebellious lord. While he was away, Mitsunari acted. He raised an army in the west and declared war on Ieyasu. Japan was now divided into two great camps. Historians call them the Eastern Army and the Western Army.
===
IMG: messengers-on-horseback-mountain-road-1600 | samurai messengers on horseback galloping along a mountain road in Japan in summer 1600, dust rising, pine forests and distant peaks, urgent mood
HEAD: In the summer of 1600, messengers raced along the roads as lords chose their side.
BODY: Every lord in Japan now had to make a difficult choice. Should he support Ieyasu, or the Western Army in the name of Hideyori? The wrong choice could cost him his lands, or his life. Messengers galloped along the roads day and night. They carried letters, promises and threats. Some lords made their decision quickly. Others waited to see which side looked stronger. A few secretly promised help to both. Families were sometimes split. A father might join one side and his son the other. That way, the family would survive whatever happened. Historians note that many letters from these months still survive. They show how tense and uncertain the time was. Yet most ordinary people knew little of these plans. They only saw the armies passing along the roads, and hoped the fighting would not reach their village.
===
TOPIC: Ships from the West
===
IMG: portuguese-black-ship-nagasaki-harbour-1600 | a large Portuguese carrack with dark hull and tall masts anchored in the green harbour of Nagasaki in 1600, small Japanese boats around it, hills with houses behind, morning light
HEAD: Each year, a great Portuguese ship sailed into Nagasaki, bringing silk and news from the world.
BODY: The Portuguese had traded with Japan since the 1540s. Their large ships came from Macao, a Portuguese trading post in southern China. The Japanese called them the black ships, because of their dark hulls. The ships brought Chinese silk, which Japanese lords and merchants valued highly. They returned with Japanese silver. This trade made both sides rich. The arrival of the black ship was a great event in Nagasaki. Crowds gathered on the shore to watch. Japanese painters made large folding screens showing the scene. These paintings are called nanban screens. Nanban means southern barbarians, the name the Japanese used for Europeans. Historians note that these screens show tall foreigners with baggy trousers and big hats. Yet the Portuguese were few in number. Japan stayed firmly in Japanese hands.
===
IMG: nanban-screen-painting-europeans-in-port | a Japanese folding screen painting on gold leaf in 1600 showing Portuguese traders in baggy trousers and tall hats walking through a Japanese port with servants carrying goods, a black ship behind them
HEAD: Japanese artists painted the foreign visitors with great curiosity.
BODY: The nanban screens are among the most charming works of art from this period. They were painted on gold leaf, like other fine screens. On one side, a black ship arrives in port. On the other, the Portuguese walk through a Japanese town. Their clothes look strange and colourful. Their servants carry boxes of goods. Jesuit priests in dark robes greet them. Japanese townspeople watch from windows and doorways. The painters had often never seen Europeans themselves. They worked from descriptions and from earlier pictures. So the faces and clothes are sometimes exaggerated. Yet the screens show a real sense of wonder. Rich Japanese families liked to own them. They were a sign of wealth and of interest in the wider world. Several dozen of these screens still survive today in museums and private collections.
===
IMG: jesuit-priest-and-japanese-lord-conversation-room | a Jesuit priest in a black robe kneeling in conversation with a Japanese lord in a tatami room in 1600, a globe and a book on a low table between them, painted screens, soft light
HEAD: Jesuit priests learned Japanese and wrote long letters describing life in Japan.
BODY: The Jesuits were a Catholic religious order. Their missionaries in Japan worked hard to understand the country. Many learned to speak Japanese well. They wrote dictionaries and grammar books. They also sent long letters back to Europe. These letters described Japanese customs, food, houses and beliefs. Historians use them as an important source for this period. Luis Frois lived in Japan for more than thirty years. He wrote a history of the Japanese mission. He also wrote a short book comparing Europe and Japan point by point. Another Jesuit, Joao Rodrigues, became an interpreter for Hideyoshi and later for Ieyasu. He wrote about the tea ceremony in great detail. Yet the Jesuits did not see everything clearly. Their main goal was to win converts. Their letters often praised Christian lords and criticised Buddhist monks.
===
IMG: dutch-ship-de-liefde-battered-arriving-coast-1600 | a battered Dutch sailing ship with torn sails drifting towards a green Japanese coast in April 1600, a few weak sailors on deck, Japanese fishing boats approaching, grey morning light
HEAD: In April 1600, a battered Dutch ship called De Liefde reached the coast of Japan.
BODY: In 1598, five Dutch ships set sail from Rotterdam. They hoped to reach Asia by crossing the Pacific Ocean. The voyage was a disaster. Storms, disease and hunger took many lives. Only one ship, De Liefde, finally reached Japan. Its name means Charity. It arrived in April 1600 off the coast of Kyushu, in the south. Of the more than one hundred men who had started on board, only about two dozen were still alive. Many were too weak to stand. Japanese fishermen helped them ashore. Local officials sent word to Osaka, where Ieyasu was staying. Among the survivors was an English pilot named William Adams. He was about thirty-five years old. The Portuguese in Japan were alarmed by the arrival of these Protestant sailors. They told Ieyasu that the newcomers were pirates.
===
IMG: william-adams-meeting-ieyasu-osaka-castle-1600 | an English sailor in worn European clothes kneeling before an older Japanese lord in a castle room in Osaka in 1600, an interpreter beside them, samurai guards, soft light through paper screens
HEAD: William Adams was taken to meet Tokugawa Ieyasu, who questioned him for many hours.
BODY: Adams was brought to Osaka in the spring of 1600. Ieyasu wanted to know who these strangers were. He questioned Adams through an interpreter. He asked about the voyage, about the Dutch and English, and about their religion. Adams later described these meetings in a letter. He wrote that Ieyasu asked about the wars between European nations. He was especially curious about ships and navigation. Adams answered as well as he could. Ieyasu was impressed by his knowledge. He did not believe the Portuguese claims that the newcomers were pirates. Yet Adams was kept in prison for several weeks while Ieyasu decided what to do. He feared he would be executed. In the end, he was released. Ieyasu kept the guns and cannon from De Liefde, which may have been useful in the war that was coming.
===
IMG: william-adams-building-western-ship-japanese-carpenters | an English shipwright directing Japanese carpenters building a small western-style sailing ship on a beach in Japan, timber frames and tools, a calm bay and green hills, afternoon light
HEAD: Adams later became a trusted adviser to Ieyasu and was given the name Miura Anjin.
BODY: After 1600, William Adams stayed in Japan for the rest of his life. Ieyasu asked him to build ships in the European style. Adams had learned shipbuilding as a young man in England. With Japanese carpenters, he built two ships. Ieyasu was pleased. He made Adams a samurai and gave him an estate at Hemi, near Edo. Adams received a new name, Miura Anjin, which means the pilot of Miura. He married a Japanese woman and had children. He also helped the Dutch and English to set up trading posts in Japan. Historians note that he was one of the very few Europeans to become a samurai. Yet he never forgot England. He asked several times to be allowed to go home. Ieyasu refused. Adams died in Japan in 1620, at the port of Hirado.
===
IMG: japanese-red-seal-ship-sailing-asia-trade | a large Japanese trading ship with mixed Asian and European rigging sailing on a calm sea towards a tropical coast in the early 1600s, sailors on deck, warm light
HEAD: Japanese merchants also sailed abroad, trading across Southeast Asia.
BODY: The trade of Japan did not depend only on foreign ships. Japanese merchants also sailed far from home. They travelled to the Philippines, Vietnam, Cambodia and Siam. Some Japanese settled in these places. Small Japanese communities grew in several Southeast Asian ports. After 1600, Ieyasu gave official licences to trusted traders. Each licence carried a red seal. The ships that carried them were known as red seal ships. Historians estimate that more than three hundred such voyages were made in the following decades. The ships brought back silk, deer skins, sugar and sharkskin, which was used to cover sword handles. Yet this open trade did not last. In the 1630s, the Tokugawa rulers closed the country. Japanese were forbidden to travel abroad, and contact with Europe was limited to a small Dutch post in Nagasaki.

TOPIC: Sekigahara, autumn 1600
===
IMG: armies-marching-mountain-pass-autumn-1600-banners | two long columns of Japanese soldiers with tall banners marching through a narrow mountain valley in autumn 1600, rain clouds over the hills, red and gold leaves, grey light
HEAD: In the autumn of 1600, two huge armies marched towards a narrow valley called Sekigahara.
BODY: By September 1600, the Eastern and Western Armies were moving towards each other. Both sides had gathered tens of thousands of men. Historians estimate that around one hundred and sixty thousand soldiers took part in the coming battle. Some estimates are even higher. The armies met in central Japan, near a village called Sekigahara. Its name means the plain of the barrier. It lay where important roads crossed between the east and the west of the country. Hills surrounded it on several sides. The Western Army arrived first and took positions on the high ground. Ieyasu's Eastern Army came from the east. It had rained heavily for days. The roads were deep in mud. Soldiers were wet, cold and tired. Yet both sides knew that this battle might decide the future of Japan.
===
IMG: misty-battlefield-sekigahara-dawn-fog-banners | thick morning fog over a valley surrounded by hills at dawn in October 1600, the faint shapes of banners and soldiers in the mist, wet grass, silent and tense atmosphere
HEAD: On the morning of the battle, thick fog covered the valley.
BODY: The Battle of Sekigahara took place on the twenty-first of October 1600, by the modern calendar. Before dawn, the two armies faced each other in the darkness. Then a heavy fog rose from the wet ground. Soldiers could see only a few metres ahead. Commanders listened for the sound of drums and gunfire. Around eight in the morning, the fog began to lift. Units from the Eastern Army moved forward and opened fire. The fighting quickly spread across the valley. Guns cracked, and spears clashed. Historians describe the first hours as fierce and evenly matched. The Western Army held strong positions on the hills. Yet some of its units did not move at all. Their commanders waited on the slopes, watching. Ieyasu had secretly been in contact with several of them. He was waiting too.
===
IMG: kobayakawa-troops-on-hill-waiting-battle-below | samurai troops with banners standing still on a wooded hillside, watching a battle in the valley below in October 1600, smoke from guns rising, their commander on horseback hesitating, grey light
HEAD: The battle turned when a young lord on a hillside changed sides.
BODY: On Mount Matsuo, overlooking the battlefield, stood the army of Kobayakawa Hideaki. He was a young lord of about nineteen. He had joined the Western Army. Yet he had secretly promised to help Ieyasu. Through the morning, he did nothing. His troops watched the battle from the hill. According to later accounts, Ieyasu grew impatient. He ordered his guns to fire towards Kobayakawa's hill as a warning. Historians still debate whether this really happened. Around midday, Kobayakawa made his choice. His army came down the hill and attacked the Western Army from the side. Several other lords then joined him. The Western Army began to collapse. Its soldiers were caught between enemies on two sides. By the afternoon, the battle was over. The Eastern Army had won.
===
IMG: ieyasu-camp-after-battle-helmet-evening | an older samurai commander in armour seated on a camp stool behind cloth curtains with his family crest after a battle in October 1600, putting on his helmet, retainers kneeling, evening light
HEAD: After the victory, Ieyasu is said to have tightened the cords of his helmet.
BODY: There is a famous story about Ieyasu after the battle. His generals gathered around him to celebrate. Instead of removing his helmet, Ieyasu tied its cords more tightly. He is said to have spoken an old warrior saying. After victory, tighten the cords of your helmet. It meant that a wise man should never relax too soon. Historians cannot be sure the story is true. Yet it fits what is known about him. Ieyasu then held a ceremony to inspect the heads of defeated enemy commanders. This was an old custom of war in Japan. It may seem harsh. It was a way to confirm who had fallen and to reward those who had fought. After the battle, Ieyasu treated the lords who had changed sides generously. Those who had opposed him to the end were punished.
===
IMG: kyoto-autumn-execution-ground-quiet-crowd | a quiet crowd of townspeople in kimono gathered at a riverside in Kyoto in November 1600, samurai guards with spears, autumn trees, grey sky, solemn mood
HEAD: Ishida Mitsunari fled from the battlefield but was soon captured.
BODY: After the defeat, Ishida Mitsunari escaped into the mountains. For several days, he hid in the countryside. He was tired and ill. Then he was found and captured. He was taken to Kyoto. There, in November 1600, he was executed along with two other leaders of the Western Army. Their heads were displayed at a bridge over the Kamo River. This was a warning to anyone who might still resist. A story is told about Mitsunari's last days. On the way to his execution, he was offered a persimmon. He refused it, saying it would be bad for his stomach. A guard laughed, because he would soon be dead anyway. Mitsunari answered that a man with great plans must care for his health until the very end. Historians cannot confirm the story. Yet it is still told in Japan.
===
IMG: lords-bowing-before-ieyasu-castle-hall-1600 | many samurai lords in formal dress bowing deeply before an older lord seated at the end of a large castle hall in late 1600, rows of retainers, painted screens, solemn light
HEAD: After Sekigahara, Ieyasu reshaped the map of Japan, rewarding friends and punishing enemies.
BODY: The victory at Sekigahara made Ieyasu the most powerful man in Japan. In the months that followed, he redistributed land on a huge scale. Lords who had fought against him lost their domains, or saw them greatly reduced. Lords who had supported him received new land. Historians estimate that land producing several million koku of rice changed hands. Ieyasu placed his most loyal followers in key positions. They guarded important roads and castles. He moved less trusted lords to distant regions, far from Edo and Kyoto. This careful arrangement would help keep the peace for a very long time. Yet one great question remained. Toyotomi Hideyori still lived in Osaka Castle. He was only a boy of seven. Many people still saw him as Hideyoshi's rightful heir.
===
IMG: autumn-village-after-war-farmers-returning-fields | Japanese farmers returning to their village and fields after the war in late autumn 1600, carrying tools and baskets, rice stubble in the fields, smoke from cooking fires, calm golden light
HEAD: For ordinary people, the end of the fighting brought a slow return to normal life.
BODY: After the battle, the soldiers went home. Many were farmers who had been taken away from their fields. They returned to their villages in time for the end of the harvest. Some found their homes damaged or their crops trampled. Others came back wounded, or did not come back at all. Families mourned their dead and repaired what had been broken. Yet there was also hope. People sensed that the long age of war might truly be ending. A single strong ruler now held power. Roads would become safer. Taxes would be more regular. Historians note that peace did not come all at once. There would be one more great conflict, at Osaka, in 1614 and 1615. Even so, for most villages, the autumn of 1600 marked the beginning of a quieter world.

TOPIC: The peace of Edo
===
IMG: ieyasu-becomes-shogun-imperial-envoy-1603 | an imperial envoy in court robes presenting a scroll to an older samurai lord in formal dress in a ceremonial hall in 1603, courtiers and samurai kneeling, solemn golden light
HEAD: In 1603, the emperor named Tokugawa Ieyasu shogun, the military ruler of Japan.
BODY: The title of shogun was very old. It meant the great general who subdues the barbarians. For centuries, shoguns had ruled Japan in the name of the emperor. The title could only be given by the emperor himself. Toyotomi Hideyoshi had never received it. In 1603, three years after Sekigahara, the emperor gave the title to Ieyasu. With it came the right to govern the whole country. Ieyasu chose to rule from Edo, his own castle town in the east. The emperor stayed in Kyoto, with great dignity but little power. Only two years later, Ieyasu passed the title to his son Hidetada. This showed that the office would stay in the Tokugawa family. Yet Ieyasu kept real control until his death. He continued to guide his son from his retirement castle at Sunpu.
===
IMG: edo-castle-construction-workers-stones-1600s | thousands of workers dragging huge stones on wooden sledges to build the walls of Edo Castle in the early 1600s, samurai overseers, scaffolding and a bay in the distance, bright daylight
HEAD: Edo was a small town when Ieyasu arrived, but it grew quickly into a great castle city.
BODY: When Ieyasu first came to Edo in 1590, it was a modest place. There was a small old castle, some fishing villages and marshy land along a bay. Ieyasu began to change it. He had hills levelled and marshes filled in. Canals were dug to bring goods into the town. After he became shogun, the work grew much larger. Lords from across Japan were ordered to help build Edo Castle. They sent workers, stones and money. Huge blocks of stone were shipped from distant regions. Edo Castle became the largest castle in Japan. Around it, the city spread in every direction. Historians estimate that by the 1700s Edo had about one million people. It may then have been the largest city in the world. Edo would later be renamed Tokyo, meaning eastern capital.
===
IMG: daimyo-procession-road-edo-porters-banners | a long procession of a Japanese lord travelling to Edo in the early 1600s, samurai with spears, porters with lacquered boxes, a covered palanquin, villagers kneeling by the road, pine trees and mountains, morning light
HEAD: Under the new rulers, great lords had to spend part of their time in Edo.
BODY: The Tokugawa rulers found clever ways to keep the lords under control. One of the most important was a system of alternate attendance. It developed over the following decades. Each lord had to live in Edo for part of the time, often every other year. When he returned to his own domain, his wife and heir stayed behind in Edo. The journeys between Edo and the domains were long and costly. Great lords travelled with hundreds or even thousands of followers. Their processions moved slowly along the roads. Villagers knelt as they passed. Historians note that this system had many effects. The lords spent much of their wealth on travel and on their houses in Edo. They had little left to raise armies. Yet the journeys also helped trade, inns and towns along the roads to grow.
===
IMG: peaceful-castle-town-street-evening-lanterns-1600s | a peaceful street in a Japanese castle town in the early 1600s at evening, paper lanterns glowing outside shops, townspeople strolling, a samurai buying fans, children playing, warm calm light
HEAD: The peace that began around 1600 lasted for more than two and a half centuries.
BODY: The Tokugawa family ruled Japan from 1603 until 1868. That is more than two hundred and sixty years. Historians often call it the Edo period. For most of that time, Japan saw no major wars. This was a remarkable change after the long age of fighting. In the peace, towns grew and trade flourished. Roads and bridges were improved. Many new crafts and arts developed. Kabuki theatre began in Kyoto only a few years after 1600. It soon became hugely popular. Woodblock prints, poetry and new kinds of novels followed later. Many samurai became officials and scholars rather than fighting men. Yet the peace came at a price. Society was strictly controlled. Each class had its own rules. People were not free to change their position, to travel abroad or to follow forbidden beliefs.
===
IMG: samurai-reading-books-study-lamp-peace | a samurai in a plain kimono reading a bound book by lamplight in a quiet study in the early 1600s, shelves of books and scrolls, his sword resting on a stand nearby, soft warm light
HEAD: In the long peace, the samurai slowly changed from warriors into officials and scholars.
BODY: After 1600, there were few battles left to fight. Yet the samurai remained the ruling class. They still wore their two swords. They still trained in martial arts. But most of their work now took place in offices. They kept records, collected taxes, judged disputes and managed towns. Many became deeply interested in learning. Schools for samurai sons spread across the country. Confucian teachings became very important. They taught loyalty, duty, respect for parents and good government. Historians note that the idea of bushido, the way of the warrior, was written down mainly in these peaceful centuries. Some writers looked back with longing to the age of war. Others believed that a true samurai should now serve with the brush. Yet the old pride remained. A samurai was still expected to be ready to give his life for his lord.
===
IMG: ieyasu-last-years-sunpu-castle-garden-old-man | an elderly Japanese lord in a simple kimono walking slowly in the garden of a castle with a view of Mount Fuji in the distance, around 1615, a servant following, quiet spring light
HEAD: Ieyasu spent his last years at Sunpu Castle, within sight of Mount Fuji.
BODY: After handing the title of shogun to his son, Ieyasu retired to Sunpu Castle. It stood in a town that is now called Shizuoka. From there, he could see Mount Fuji. Ieyasu stayed active. He met foreign visitors, including William Adams. He collected books and supported scholars. He also kept a careful eye on politics. In 1614 and 1615, his armies besieged Osaka Castle. Toyotomi Hideyori and his mother Lady Yodo died when the castle fell. With that, the last challenge to Tokugawa rule was gone. Ieyasu died the following year, in 1616, at the age of seventy-three. He was buried and later honoured as a god. A great shrine was built for him at Nikko, in the mountains north of Edo. Its gates and halls are covered in carvings and gold.
===
IMG: cherry-blossoms-castle-moat-spring-calm-evening | cherry blossoms reflected in the still water of a castle moat at dusk in Japan, a white castle keep on its stone walls behind, petals floating on the water, soft pink and blue evening light, calm and peaceful
HEAD: The year 1600 is remembered as the moment when Japan turned from war towards a long peace.
BODY: For Japanese historians, the year 1600 is a turning point. Before it lay more than a century of war. After it came the long peace of the Edo period. The Battle of Sekigahara is often called the battle that decided the realm. Yet daily life in 1600 was shaped by much more than battles. It was shaped by the planting of rice and the turning of the seasons. It was shaped by the craft of potters and swordsmiths, by the quiet of tea rooms and by festivals at village shrines. Many of the customs of that age can still be recognised. Cherry blossoms still draw crowds in spring. Rice is still planted in early summer. And the white walls of the old castles still rise above their moats, reflecting the soft light of evening.
