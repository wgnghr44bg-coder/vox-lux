TOPIC: Rice, fish and tea: food in 1600
===
IMG: simple-meal-tray-rice-miso-soup-pickles | a small lacquered tray on a tatami floor in 1600 with a bowl of white rice, a bowl of miso soup, pickled vegetables and a piece of grilled fish, chopsticks resting beside it, soft daylight
HEAD: A typical meal in 1600 was simple: rice, soup and pickled vegetables.
BODY: Food in Japan was plain and carefully prepared. At the centre of almost every meal was a bowl of rice. With it came a bowl of soup, often made with miso, a paste of fermented soybeans. Small dishes of pickled vegetables added flavour. Radish, cucumber and plums were pickled in salt or rice bran. For those who could afford it, there was also a piece of fish. Many people ate two main meals a day. Historians note that a third meal became common only later. Each person ate from a small tray, using chopsticks. European visitors were surprised by this. The Jesuit Luis Frois noted that Japanese people ate with two sticks, while Europeans used their fingers. Yet white rice was not eaten by everyone. Many farmers mixed their rice with barley or millet to make it last.
===
IMG: fishermen-boats-nets-coast-morning-1600 | Japanese fishermen in small wooden boats pulling nets near a rocky green coast in 1600, a fishing village with thatched roofs on the shore, calm sea, early morning light
HEAD: Fish was the most important source of meat, and fishing villages lined the coasts.
BODY: Japan is an island country, surrounded by rich seas. Along its coasts lay many fishing villages. Fishermen went out in small wooden boats before dawn. They used nets, lines and traps. They caught sardines, mackerel, sea bream, squid and many other fish. Seaweed and shellfish were also gathered from the shore. Fresh fish did not keep for long. So much of the catch was dried, salted or pickled. Dried fish could travel far inland, to mountain villages that never saw the sea. Sea bream was a fish for celebrations. Its red colour was thought to bring good fortune. Even so, fish was a luxury for many farmers. In poorer homes, a small piece of dried fish might be shared by the whole family. It was saved for festivals and special days.
===
IMG: buddhist-temple-vegetarian-food-tofu-vegetables | a Buddhist monk in a plain robe preparing a meal of tofu, mountain vegetables and rice in a simple temple kitchen with a wooden counter and steam rising, quiet morning light
HEAD: Buddhist teachings meant that most people ate very little meat from animals.
BODY: For many centuries, Buddhism had taught respect for living creatures. As a result, eating the meat of four-legged animals was avoided by most people. Fish and birds were usually allowed. Monks in many temples ate no meat at all. They developed a careful cooking style based on vegetables, beans and grains. Tofu, made from soybeans, was an important part of this food. It was rich in protein and easy to prepare. Mountain vegetables, mushrooms and seaweed added flavour. Temple cooking spread slowly to the rest of the country. Yet the rules were not followed everywhere. Hunters in the mountains ate deer and wild boar. Soldiers on long campaigns sometimes did the same. They often called it by another name, such as mountain whale, so that it sounded less like meat.
===
IMG: sake-brewery-wooden-barrels-workers-steam | workers in a sake brewery in 1600 stirring rice in large wooden barrels, steam rising in a dim timber building with heavy beams, straw-wrapped barrels stacked along the wall, warm light
HEAD: Rice was also used to brew sake, which was drunk at festivals and celebrations.
BODY: Sake is an alcoholic drink made from rice, water and a special mould called koji. Brewing it was skilled work. The rice was polished, washed and steamed. Then the koji turned its starch into sugar. Yeast changed the sugar into alcohol. The whole process took many weeks, usually in the cold months of winter. By 1600, some towns were famous for their sake. Temples had brewed it for centuries. Merchants now sold it in many cities. Sake was offered to the gods at shrines. It was shared at weddings, festivals and New Year. Samurai drank it after a victory. Historians note that drinking together was an important way to build trust. Yet sake was not cheap. Ordinary families drank it mainly on special days, poured warm from small flasks into tiny cups.
===
IMG: portuguese-and-japanese-cooks-frying-batter-kitchen | a Japanese cook watching a Portuguese cook fry vegetables in batter in an iron pan over a fire in a busy port kitchen in 1600, baskets of fish and vegetables, warm light
HEAD: New foods had also arrived with the Portuguese, including sweets and food fried in batter.
BODY: The Portuguese traders brought more than guns to Japan. They also brought new foods and ways of cooking. Sugar became better known through their trade. Sponge cake, called castella, was first made in Nagasaki in this period. It was based on a Portuguese recipe. Small hard sweets also came with the foreign ships. Historians believe that frying food in batter was another Portuguese influence. It later developed into the famous Japanese dish of tempura. The Portuguese also brought sweet potatoes, chilli peppers and tobacco to the region. Some of these spread slowly across Japan. Tobacco became popular surprisingly fast. Within a few years, many people were smoking long thin pipes. Yet for most Japanese, daily food changed very little. Rice, soup, pickles and fish remained at the heart of every meal.
===
IMG: green-tea-picking-hillside-terraces-women | women in straw hats picking green tea leaves on neat hillside rows of tea bushes in spring in 1600, wooden baskets on their backs, mountains and mist in the distance, soft light
HEAD: Green tea had become a daily drink in many homes, though the finest tea was still costly.
BODY: Tea had come to Japan from China many centuries earlier. Buddhist monks first used it to stay awake during long hours of meditation. Slowly, tea drinking spread to the warrior class and then to ordinary people. By 1600, tea was grown on hillsides in several regions. The Uji district near Kyoto was the most famous. In spring, the young leaves were picked by hand. They were steamed, dried and stored carefully. The finest tea was ground into a bright green powder. It was whisked with hot water in a bowl. This was the tea used in the formal tea ceremony. Ordinary people drank simpler tea, made from coarser leaves. They brewed it in pots or kettles. Yet both kinds were valued. A cup of tea was a sign of welcome in almost every Japanese home.
===
IMG: kitchen-hearth-rice-pot-steam-farmhouse-woman | a woman cooking rice in a heavy iron pot with a wooden lid over a wood fire in a farmhouse kitchen with an earthen floor in 1600, steam rising, baskets of vegetables, soft light from the doorway
HEAD: Cooking was done over open fires, in heavy pots of iron and clay.
BODY: Japanese kitchens in 1600 were simple and practical. In farmhouses, cooking was done on a raised clay stove or over the open hearth. Wood and charcoal were the main fuels. Rice was cooked in a heavy iron pot with a thick wooden lid. Cooking rice well was a skill. The fire had to be strong at first and then gentle. The lid was not lifted until the rice was ready. Water came from a well or a nearby stream. It was carried in wooden buckets and stored in large jars. Knives were sharp and finely made, like small swords. Food was cut into neat pieces before cooking. Historians note that Japanese cooking used little oil. Most food was boiled, steamed or grilled. Yet a good meal was not only about taste. It was also arranged with care, so that it looked pleasing on the tray.
===
TOPIC: Towns, merchants and craftsmen
===
IMG: kyoto-street-1600-shops-curtains-crowds | a busy street in Kyoto in 1600 with two-storey wooden shops, cloth curtains hanging in the doorways, merchants, porters with loads on poles and townspeople in kimono, a temple roof in the distance, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Kyoto, the old capital, was one of the largest cities in the world in 1600.
BODY: Kyoto had been the home of the emperor for about eight hundred years. In the age of war, much of the city had burned. By 1600, it had been rebuilt and was busy again. Historians estimate that a few hundred thousand people lived there. Its streets were laid out in a grid, an ancient pattern copied from China. Shops lined the main roads. Cloth curtains hung in their doorways, printed with the name or sign of the business. Behind the shops lay narrow houses with small inner gardens. Kyoto was famous for fine crafts. Silk weavers, potters, lacquer makers and fan makers worked there. Temples and shrines stood in every district. Yet the emperor had almost no real power. He lived quietly in his palace, while warlords fought for control of the country.
===
IMG: sakai-port-merchants-warehouses-ships-1600 | a busy harbour at Sakai in 1600 with large wooden warehouses, merchants inspecting bales and boxes, small sailing ships at the quay, porters carrying goods, clear morning light over the water
HEAD: The port of Sakai was a rich trading town, run for many years by its own merchants.
BODY: Sakai lay on the coast near Osaka. During the age of war, it grew rich from trade with China and Southeast Asia. Its merchants imported silk, medicine, books and fine pottery. They sold copper, silver, swords and lacquer ware. For a time, the merchants governed the town themselves. They dug moats around it and hired guards. A Jesuit visitor compared it to Venice, a wealthy city of traders in Europe. Sakai was also a centre of gun making. Many of the guns used in the wars were made there. Some Sakai merchants became famous masters of the tea ceremony. Sen no Rikyu himself came from a merchant family in Sakai. Yet the town lost much of its freedom under the great warlords. By 1600, it served the rulers of Japan like every other town.
===
IMG: craftsman-lacquer-workshop-bowls-brushes | a Japanese craftsman applying shining red and black lacquer to wooden bowls with a fine brush in a quiet workshop in 1600, finished bowls drying on shelves, soft light through paper windows
HEAD: Japanese craftsmen were famous for lacquer, a hard and shining coating made from tree sap.
BODY: Lacquer is made from the sap of the lacquer tree. When it dries in a damp place, it becomes very hard and glossy. Craftsmen painted it onto wooden bowls, trays, boxes and armour. They applied many thin layers, one after another. Each layer had to dry before the next was added. A fine piece could take months to make. The raw sap could cause painful rashes, so the work needed great care. The best pieces were decorated with gold and silver powder. This technique was called maki-e, which means sprinkled picture. Portuguese traders loved Japanese lacquer. They ordered chests, desks and boxes to take back to Europe. Some of these objects can still be seen in European museums. Yet most lacquer was made for daily use in Japan, for eating, drinking and storing small treasures.
===
IMG: potter-wheel-kiln-workshop-tea-bowls | a Japanese potter shaping a rough tea bowl on a slow wooden wheel in a workshop beside a climbing kiln on a hillside in 1600, finished bowls on a shelf, smoke rising, afternoon light
HEAD: Potters made the simple, uneven tea bowls that tea masters had come to love.
BODY: Pottery had a long history in Japan. By 1600, new styles were becoming popular. Tea masters admired bowls that looked simple, rough and a little uneven. They believed such bowls had a quiet beauty. One famous style was raku. It was made by a family of potters in Kyoto. Their bowls were shaped by hand, without a wheel. After the wars in Korea in the 1590s, many Korean potters were brought to Japan. Some came by force. They set up new kilns in the south of the country. Their skills changed Japanese pottery for centuries. Historians note that this is a painful part of the story. Yet it also explains why some Japanese pottery towns still trace their origins to Korean craftsmen. A good tea bowl could become more valuable than land.
===
IMG: porters-and-travellers-on-road-inn-pine-trees | travellers, porters carrying loads on poles and a samurai on horseback on a road lined with tall pine trees in Japan in 1600, a small wooden inn with a lantern beside the road, soft evening light
HEAD: Roads connected the towns of Japan, and travellers stopped at inns along the way.
BODY: Most people travelled on foot. They walked along roads that linked the castle towns and the old capital. The most famous road ran along the eastern coast. Pine trees were planted along the roads to give shade. At regular distances, there were small towns with inns and teahouses. Travellers could rest, eat and sleep there. Porters carried goods on poles across their shoulders. Wealthy people were carried in small wooden chairs called kago. Only higher samurai rode horses. A traveller could walk perhaps thirty or forty kilometres a day. Rivers often had no bridges. Ferrymen carried people across, sometimes on their backs. Historians note that roads became much safer after 1600. Yet in the age of war, bandits and soldiers had made every journey a risk.
===
IMG: market-day-town-square-stalls-vegetables-fish | a lively market in a Japanese town square in 1600 with stalls of vegetables, dried fish, straw goods and cloth under simple awnings, townspeople bargaining, a temple gate behind, morning light
HEAD: Markets brought farmers, merchants and townspeople together on fixed days each month.
BODY: In many towns, markets were held on set days. Farmers brought vegetables, firewood and straw goods. Fishermen brought dried fish and seaweed. Merchants sold cloth, pots, tools and medicine. Customers paid with coins, rice or silver. Coins in Japan were often old Chinese coins, with a square hole in the middle. They were strung together on cords. Gold and silver were used for larger sums. The great lords had begun to mine more silver. Historians estimate that Japan produced a large share of the world's silver around this time. Much of it went to China in exchange for silk. Markets were also places for news and gossip. Storytellers and entertainers performed there. Yet merchants had a low position in society. In theory, they ranked below farmers, because they produced nothing themselves.
===
IMG: town-bathhouse-steam-wooden-tubs-1600 | the steamy interior of a simple public bathhouse in a Japanese town in 1600, wooden tubs and buckets, a small window letting in light, townspeople washing quietly, warm hazy atmosphere
HEAD: Bathing was an important part of daily life, and towns had public bathhouses.
BODY: The Japanese were known for their love of cleanliness. European visitors often remarked on it. They noted that Japanese people washed far more often than people in Europe. In towns, public bathhouses were becoming common. Some used steam, rather like a sauna. Others had large tubs of hot water. People washed themselves first, outside the tub, and then soaked in the warm water. The bath was also a place to meet friends and share news. In the countryside, families heated water in a wooden tub at home. Hot springs were valued for health. Lords and samurai sometimes travelled to them to rest after a campaign. Historians note that the first public bathhouse in Edo is said to have opened in 1591. Yet hot water was costly to heat. In many homes, the whole family used the same bath in turn.
===
TOPIC: Women, children and family life
===
IMG: samurai-wife-managing-household-keys-storeroom | a samurai wife in a long kimono with a set of keys at her sash checking supplies of rice and cloth in a wooden storeroom in 1600, a maid beside her, soft light from a small window
HEAD: In samurai families, the wife managed the household and its money.
BODY: In a samurai household, the husband served his lord. His wife ran the home. She managed the rice, the stores and the servants. She often kept the keys to the storerooms. She raised the children and looked after the family's elderly members. In wealthy houses, this was a large task with many people to direct. Historians note that women of the warrior class were expected to be strong and loyal. Some were trained to use a long curved spear called the naginata. In times of war, they might defend the house while the men were away. Yet women had fewer rights than men. Marriages were arranged by the families. A daughter often married to build an alliance between two clans. She left her parents' home and joined the household of her husband.
===
IMG: noble-ladies-castle-room-painting-screens | noblewomen in layered silk kimono sitting in a quiet castle room in 1600, painted folding screens, one woman reading a scroll and another arranging flowers, soft daylight through paper doors
HEAD: Some women of great families held real power, even in the age of war.
BODY: The lives of a few women from this period are well known. Lady Yodo was the mother of Hideyoshi's son. After Hideyoshi died, she lived in Osaka Castle and guarded her son's interests. Another famous woman was Nene, Hideyoshi's widow. She was respected by many lords. Historians believe she had influence over important decisions. Other women ran estates when their husbands were away. Some sent letters that helped to arrange peace between families. Many of these letters still survive. They were written in flowing Japanese script, often on fine paper. Yet such women were exceptions. Most women's lives went unrecorded. They worked in the fields, in shops and in kitchens. Their names were rarely written down, though their work kept the country fed and clothed.
===
IMG: farm-woman-weaving-loom-house-1600 | a farm woman weaving cotton cloth on a wooden loom in a simple farmhouse in 1600, a small child playing beside her on the floor, a basket of thread, soft light from the open door
HEAD: Farm women worked in the fields and also spun, wove and sewed for the whole family.
BODY: In farming families, women worked as hard as men. They planted and harvested rice. They cared for vegetables and animals. At home, they cooked, washed and looked after the children. They also made the family's clothes. In 1600, cotton was spreading across Japan. Before, most ordinary clothes were made from hemp. Cotton was softer and warmer. Women spun the thread and wove the cloth on simple wooden looms. They dyed it, often with indigo, which gave a deep blue colour. Blue cotton became the everyday colour of working people. Historians note that weaving brought extra income to many families. Cloth could be sold at the market. Yet the work never seemed to end. Many women sat at the loom long after the rest of the family had gone to sleep.
===
IMG: children-playing-village-lane-tops-kites | Japanese children in simple cotton kimono playing with spinning tops and a paper kite in a village lane in 1600, thatched houses and a stone wall, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Children played with tops, kites and balls, and helped their parents from a young age.
BODY: Japanese children in 1600 had simple toys. They spun wooden tops and flew paper kites. They played with balls made of cloth. Girls played with dolls made of paper or clay. European visitors noticed how kindly Japanese parents treated their children. Luis Frois wrote that Japanese children were rarely beaten. He thought they were raised with words rather than punishment. Yet childhood was short. Farm children helped in the fields from an early age. They looked after younger brothers and sisters. They gathered firewood and carried water. Samurai boys began training with wooden swords. They also learned to read and write. Many children did not survive to adulthood. Illness was common, and medicine was limited. Families often visited shrines to pray for their children's health and long life.
===
IMG: wedding-procession-bride-white-kimono-lanterns | a quiet wedding procession in Japan in 1600, a bride in a white kimono and hood walking beside lanterns carried by family members, a wooden house gate ahead, evening light
HEAD: Marriages were arranged by families, and the bride often wore white.
BODY: In 1600, most marriages were arranged by parents. A go-between helped the two families meet and agree. For samurai, marriage was often a matter of politics. It could tie two houses together. For farmers and merchants, it joined two families in work and property. On the wedding day, the bride left her parents' house. She often wore a white kimono. White was the colour of purity, and also of leaving one family for another. A procession carried her belongings to her new home. The ceremony was usually held there. Bride and groom drank sake from shared cups, in a fixed order. Historians note that this exchange of cups was the heart of the wedding. Yet a bride's new life could be lonely. She had to win the trust of her husband's mother.
===
IMG: elderly-grandmother-telling-stories-children-hearth | an elderly Japanese grandmother telling stories to small children around a sunken hearth in a farmhouse in 1600, an iron kettle steaming, firelight on their faces, night outside
HEAD: Grandparents were respected, and many lived with their children and grandchildren.
BODY: In Japanese families, several generations often lived under one roof. Grandparents were treated with respect. They gave advice and helped to raise the children. In samurai families, the head of the house sometimes retired while still alive. He passed his duties to his son. He might then shave his head and live a quieter life, with time for poetry and prayer. Many retired men took Buddhist names. Grandmothers had a special place in the home. They looked after the youngest children. They passed on songs, stories and old customs. Historians note that care for parents was seen as a basic duty. This idea came partly from Confucian teaching, which was growing more important in Japan. Yet not every old person was well cared for. In hard years, the poorest families struggled to feed everyone.
===
IMG: kimono-dyer-workshop-indigo-cloth-hanging | a Japanese dyer lifting long strips of cloth from vats of deep blue indigo in a workshop yard in 1600, dyed cloth hanging to dry on bamboo poles, bright morning light
HEAD: Clothing showed a person's place in society, from fine silk to plain cotton.
BODY: In 1600, clothes told others who a person was. Lords and wealthy samurai wore silk. Their robes were dyed in rich colours and patterns. Some were embroidered with gold thread. Ordinary townspeople and farmers wore cotton or hemp. Their clothes were simple and often blue, from indigo dye. Everyone wore a garment shaped like the kimono. It was wrapped left over right and held with a sash. In summer, people wore thin, light robes. In winter, they wore several layers, some padded with cotton. Footwear was simple. People wore straw sandals or wooden clogs called geta. Indoors, they went barefoot or wore split-toed socks. Historians note that the rulers sometimes made laws about clothing. These laws forbade lower classes from wearing silk. Yet merchants who had grown rich often found quiet ways to wear fine cloth beneath plain outer robes.
===
TOPIC: Tea, gardens and the quiet arts
===
IMG: small-tea-house-garden-path-stone-lantern | a small rustic tea house with a thatched roof at the end of a mossy garden path with stepping stones and a stone lantern in 1600, bamboo fence, maple trees, soft morning mist
HEAD: The tea ceremony was one of the most admired arts in Japan in 1600.
BODY: The tea ceremony is a careful way of preparing and serving green tea. In 1600, it was loved by lords, samurai and rich merchants. Guests walked along a garden path to a small tea house. The path was meant to leave the busy world behind. Stepping stones, moss and a stone lantern lined the way. Guests washed their hands at a stone basin. Then they entered the tea room through a very low door. Everyone had to bow to pass through, even a great lord. Inside, the room was small and simple. A scroll hung in an alcove, beside a single flower. The host prepared the tea in silence. Each movement followed fixed rules. Yet the ceremony was not only about rules. It was meant to bring calm, respect and a sense of the present moment.
===
IMG: tea-master-whisking-green-tea-bowl-iron-kettle | a tea master in a plain dark kimono whisking bright green powdered tea in a rough bowl with a bamboo whisk, an iron kettle steaming over a sunken hearth, a small tea room with a scroll, soft light
HEAD: The greatest tea master of the age was Sen no Rikyu, who died nine years before 1600.
BODY: Sen no Rikyu was born into a merchant family in Sakai. He became the tea master of Oda Nobunaga and later of Toyotomi Hideyoshi. Rikyu shaped the tea ceremony that is still practised in Japan. He favoured a simple style called wabi. It valued plain materials, quiet rooms and rough, natural objects. He built tea rooms only a few mats wide. In 1591, Hideyoshi ordered Rikyu to end his own life. The exact reason is still unknown. Historians have suggested a quarrel over politics, money or pride. Rikyu was about seventy years old. Yet his ideas did not die with him. His students and grandsons carried on his teaching. In 1600, the great lords still competed to own tea bowls, kettles and jars that Rikyu had once admired.
===
IMG: zen-temple-rock-garden-raked-gravel-monk | a Zen temple rock garden with raked white gravel in wave patterns and a few large stones surrounded by moss, a monk in a dark robe sitting quietly on the wooden veranda, early morning light
HEAD: Zen temples were known for their gardens of stone and raked gravel.
BODY: Some of the most famous gardens in Japan were made in Zen Buddhist temples. They were very different from flower gardens. A few large stones stood in a bed of white gravel. Monks raked the gravel into lines that looked like water. Moss grew around the stones. These gardens were made for looking, not for walking. Monks sat on the wooden veranda and studied them quietly. Each viewer could see something different. The stones might be islands, mountains or animals. Historians note that many of these gardens were made in the century before 1600. The garden of Ryoan-ji in Kyoto is the best known. Nobody knows exactly who designed it. Yet its simple beauty has been admired for centuries. Raking the gravel was itself part of the monks' daily practice.
===
IMG: noh-theatre-stage-masked-actor-pine-backdrop | a Noh theatre performance in 1600 on a wooden stage with a painted pine tree backdrop, an actor in a pale mask and rich silk robes moving slowly, musicians with drums and a flute, audience in kimono, lantern light
HEAD: Noh theatre, with its slow movements and masks, was a favourite of the samurai.
BODY: Noh is one of the oldest forms of theatre in the world. It began in the fourteenth century. By 1600, it was greatly loved by the warrior class. Toyotomi Hideyoshi enjoyed it so much that he performed on stage himself. A Noh play was slow and solemn. The main actor wore a carved wooden mask and rich silk robes. He moved with great control, sometimes taking only a step at a time. A small group of musicians played drums and a flute. A chorus sang parts of the story. Many plays told of ghosts, gods and warriors from the past. Behind the stage stood a painting of a single pine tree. Historians note that the masks were made to show different feelings as the actor tilted his head. Yet the plays were not meant to be exciting. They were meant to move the heart quietly.
===
IMG: calligraphy-master-brush-large-paper-ink | an elderly calligraphy master writing large flowing characters with a big brush on a sheet of paper laid on the floor, an inkstone and water jar beside him, a quiet room with paper doors, soft light
HEAD: Writing with a brush was an art, and fine calligraphy was greatly prized.
BODY: In Japan, beautiful writing was considered an art form. It was called shodo, the way of writing. Calligraphers used brushes of animal hair and ink made from soot and glue. The ink was ground by hand on a stone with a little water. Grinding it slowly was thought to calm the mind before writing. A good calligrapher could show strength, grace or sadness in a single character. Scrolls of fine writing were hung in tea rooms and halls. Poems and Buddhist sayings were the most common subjects. Many samurai and monks practised calligraphy every day. Historians note that letters from this period are often admired for their writing as much as their words. Yet good calligraphy took years to learn. Each stroke had to be made in one movement, without any correction.
===
IMG: cherry-blossom-viewing-picnic-hillside-1600 | people in colourful kimono sitting on mats under blooming cherry trees on a hillside in spring in 1600, sharing food and sake, a temple roof among the trees, soft pink light
HEAD: In spring, people gathered under the cherry trees to enjoy the blossoms.
BODY: Cherry blossom viewing is an old custom in Japan. It is called hanami. In spring, people gathered under the flowering trees. They shared food and sake. They wrote poems and sang songs. The blossoms lasted only a short time, often about a week. Their quick beauty was a reminder that all things pass. In the spring of 1598, Toyotomi Hideyoshi held a famous blossom party at the Daigo temple near Kyoto. Hundreds of guests were invited. About seven hundred cherry trees were planted for the occasion. Women of his household wore new kimono, changing them during the day. It was one of the grandest parties of the age. Yet Hideyoshi died only a few months later. Many people saw a lesson in this. Even the most powerful life was as brief as the cherry blossom.
===
IMG: ikebana-flower-arrangement-alcove-scroll | a simple flower arrangement of a single branch and a few blossoms in a bronze vase in the alcove of a quiet room in 1600, a hanging scroll above it, soft light through paper screens
HEAD: Arranging flowers was also an art, practised in temples and in the homes of the wealthy.
BODY: Flower arranging in Japan grew out of the custom of offering flowers at Buddhist altars. Over time, it became an art of its own. It was called ikebana, which means living flowers. Arrangements were placed in the alcove of a room, beside a hanging scroll. Some were tall and grand, built of many branches. They stood for mountains, valleys and the whole natural world. Others were very simple. A single branch or flower in a plain vase could be enough. Tea masters preferred this quiet style. Flowers were chosen to match the season. Plum blossom suited late winter. Iris suited early summer. Maple leaves and chrysanthemums suited autumn. Historians note that teachers of flower arranging passed on their methods within families, as in other arts. Yet the aim was always the same. A small arrangement was meant to bring the feeling of nature into the room.
===
TOPIC: Gods, temples and festivals
===
IMG: shinto-shrine-red-torii-gate-forest-path | a Shinto shrine in a forest in Japan in 1600 with a red torii gate, a gravel path, tall cedar trees, stone lanterns and a simple wooden hall with a thatched roof, morning mist and light
HEAD: Shinto shrines honoured the kami, the spirits found in nature and in the land.
BODY: Shinto is the oldest religion of Japan. It teaches that spirits, called kami, live in many places. A kami might live in a mountain, a waterfall, an old tree or a large rock. Some kami were the spirits of ancestors or great heroes. Shrines were built to honour them. A shrine was often entered through a gate called a torii. It marked the boundary between the everyday world and a sacred place. Visitors washed their hands and mouth before approaching. They bowed, clapped their hands and said a short prayer. Shrines stood in villages, towns and deep in the forests. Some were very old. The great shrine at Ise was rebuilt every twenty years, an old custom that had been interrupted by the wars. Yet Shinto had no single holy book. It lived mainly in customs, festivals and prayers.
===
IMG: buddhist-temple-hall-monks-chanting-incense | Buddhist monks in robes chanting before a golden statue in a dim temple hall in 1600, incense smoke drifting, candles glowing, wooden pillars and a high ceiling
HEAD: Buddhism had been in Japan for about a thousand years, and temples were found everywhere.
BODY: Buddhism came to Japan from Korea and China in the sixth century. By 1600, there were many Buddhist schools. Some taught meditation, like Zen. Others taught that faith in the Buddha Amida would bring rebirth in a pure land. Temples served as places of prayer and learning. They also kept the records of families and held funerals. Most Japanese people followed both Shinto and Buddhism. They saw no conflict between them. A family might visit a shrine for a birth and a temple for a funeral. Some Buddhist temples had been very powerful in the age of war. They owned land and even kept armies. Historians note that Oda Nobunaga destroyed the temple complex on Mount Hiei in 1571. Yet Buddhism remained deeply rooted in daily life. Temple bells rang across the towns every morning and evening.
===
IMG: jesuit-church-nagasaki-japanese-christians | Japanese Christians in kimono gathered outside a small wooden church with a cross in Nagasaki in 1600, a Jesuit priest in a dark robe speaking with them, a harbour and hills behind, morning light
HEAD: Christianity had also come to Japan, and by 1600 there were many thousands of Japanese Christians.
BODY: In 1549, the Jesuit missionary Francis Xavier arrived in Japan. Other priests followed him. They preached in the south and west of the country. Some great lords became Christians. Their people often followed them. Historians estimate that by 1600 there were about three hundred thousand Japanese Christians. Nagasaki became the centre of the Christian community. Churches, schools and a printing press were built there. Yet the faith also caused fear among the rulers. In 1587, Hideyoshi ordered the missionaries to leave, though the order was not fully enforced. In 1597, twenty-six Christians were executed in Nagasaki. Among them were Japanese believers and foreign priests. In 1600, Christians could still practise their faith quietly. In later decades, the religion would be banned completely and driven underground.

IMG: obon-lanterns-floating-river-night-summer | paper lanterns floating on a calm river at night during the summer festival of Obon in Japan in 1600, families standing quietly on the bank, a wooden bridge and soft lantern glow on the water
HEAD: In summer, families welcomed the spirits of their ancestors during the festival of Obon.
BODY: Obon was a Buddhist festival held in late summer. People believed that the spirits of their ancestors returned home for a few days. Families cleaned the graves and placed offerings of food and flowers. They lit small fires or lanterns to guide the spirits to the house. At night, villagers danced in a circle. These dances were called bon odori. They were joyful and simple, with drums and songs. On the last evening, the spirits were sent back. In some places, lanterns were floated down rivers or out to sea. The small lights drifted slowly into the dark. Historians note that Obon has been celebrated in Japan for more than a thousand years. Yet it was not a sad festival. It was a time for families to feel close to those they had lost.

IMG: new-year-preparations-pine-decorations-rice-cakes | a Japanese family in 1600 pounding rice into rice cakes with a large wooden mallet in a courtyard, pine and bamboo decorations beside the gate, winter morning, light snow
HEAD: New Year was the most important festival, a time for cleaning, rice cakes and new beginnings.
BODY: The New Year was the greatest celebration of the year in Japan. In 1600, it followed the old lunar calendar. It usually fell in late January or February. Before the New Year, families cleaned their houses from top to bottom. Debts were paid where possible. Pine branches and bamboo were placed by the gates to welcome the year's god. Families pounded steamed rice into sticky cakes called mochi. They used a large wooden mallet and a mortar. It was hard work, often done by the men while the women turned the rice. On New Year's morning, people dressed in their best clothes. They visited shrines and temples to pray for good fortune. Historians note that everyone became one year older at New Year, rather than on their own birthday. Yet the festival was also quiet. Many shops closed for several days.

IMG: mountain-pilgrims-white-clothes-temple-steps | pilgrims in white clothes with straw hats and walking staffs climbing long stone steps through a cedar forest to a mountain temple in Japan in 1600, morning mist, quiet atmosphere
HEAD: Pilgrims walked long distances to visit holy mountains, shrines and temples.
BODY: Pilgrimage was an important part of religious life in Japan. People walked to sacred places to pray, to give thanks or to ask for help. Some climbed holy mountains, where they believed the gods lived. Others followed routes that linked many temples. On the island of Shikoku, pilgrims walked a long circle of eighty-eight temples. The route was linked to the great monk Kukai. Pilgrims often wore white clothes and carried a walking staff. They slept in temples, inns or the homes of kind villagers. Giving food or shelter to a pilgrim was considered a good deed. Historians note that pilgrimage became much more popular after 1600. Roads grew safer during the long peace. Yet even in the age of war, many people had made these journeys. Some never returned home.

IMG: autumn-moon-viewing-veranda-family-1600 | a Japanese family sitting on a wooden veranda in autumn in 1600, looking at a full moon above dark hills, offerings of rice dumplings and pampas grass on a small stand, soft silver moonlight
HEAD: In autumn, families gathered on their verandas to admire the full moon.
BODY: Moon viewing was a quiet autumn custom. It took place on the night of the full moon in the eighth month of the old calendar. Families placed small rice dumplings and stalks of pampas grass on a stand. These were offerings of thanks for the harvest. Then they sat on the veranda and looked at the moon rising over the hills. Some wrote poems. Others simply talked softly, or sat in silence. The custom had come from China many centuries earlier. At first, it was enjoyed mainly by the court. By 1600, it had spread to samurai, merchants and farmers. Historians note that the moon appears in countless Japanese poems. It was a symbol of beauty and of things that change and return. Some temples held moon viewing gatherings beside their ponds, where the moon could be seen twice, once in the sky and once on the still water. Boats were sometimes rowed out onto the lakes for a better view. Yet moon viewing asked for nothing grand. A clear sky, a quiet place and a little time were all that it needed.
