TOPIC: A samurai's day in 1600
===
IMG: samurai-house-morning-tatami-sliding-doors-1600 | a simple samurai house in Japan in 1600 at dawn, tatami mats, paper sliding doors open to a small garden with a pine tree, a samurai in a plain kimono kneeling calmly, soft morning light and mist
HEAD: In 1600, the day of a samurai began early, in a quiet wooden house near his lord's castle.
BODY: The day of a samurai began before sunrise. Most samurai did not live in grand halls. They lived in modest wooden houses close to the castle of their lord. The floors were covered with thick mats of woven straw, called tatami. Light came in through sliding doors of thin paper on wooden frames. In the morning, the bedding was folded and put away in a cupboard. The same room then served for meals, for work and for receiving guests. A samurai washed his face and hands with cold water from a well. He dressed in a kimono and wide trousers called hakama. A servant or his wife helped him tie his hair in the old warrior style. Yet the house was simple, almost bare. Furniture was rare, and a few objects were kept with great care. This simplicity was not a sign of poverty. It was the way a well-ordered home was meant to look.
===
IMG: samurai-tying-topknot-wife-helping-morning | a samurai wife in a long kimono helping her husband tie his hair in a topknot in a quiet room with tatami mats and paper screens, early morning light
HEAD: A samurai wore his hair in a topknot, and the front of his head was often shaved.
BODY: The hairstyle of a samurai was a sign of his rank. Many men shaved the front and top of the head. The long hair at the back was oiled, gathered and folded into a small knot. This style was called the chonmage. Historians believe it began in times of war. A shaved head stayed cooler under a heavy iron helmet. By 1600, the topknot had become part of daily life, even in times of peace. A barber or a family member kept it neat every few days. European visitors found the style very strange. The Jesuit Luis Frois wrote a long list of differences between Europe and Japan. He noted that Europeans grew their hair, while Japanese men carefully plucked and shaved it. Even so, the topknot was worn with pride. To cut it off was a deep disgrace. It meant that a man had left the world of the warrior.
===
IMG: samurai-two-swords-sash-kimono-hakama-garden | a samurai standing in a small garden in 1600 wearing kimono and hakama with a long and a short sword thrust through his sash, calm expression, pine trees and stone lantern, morning light
HEAD: Before leaving the house, a samurai placed two swords through his sash.
BODY: Every samurai carried two swords on his left side. The long sword was the katana. The short sword was the wakizashi. Both were pushed through the sash, with the cutting edge facing upward. This allowed a sword to be drawn and used in one smooth movement. The pair of swords was called the daisho, meaning large and small. In 1600, the right to wear two swords was becoming a mark of the samurai class. A few years earlier, the great ruler Toyotomi Hideyoshi had ordered farmers to hand in their swords. Officials travelled from village to village to collect them. Yet in daily life, the swords were rarely drawn. Inside a house, the long sword was laid on a rack by the door. The short sword stayed in the sash. It was a quiet reminder of who its owner was.
===
IMG: samurai-walking-to-castle-gate-morning-street | samurai in kimono and hakama walking along a quiet street of wooden houses towards a large castle gate with stone walls in 1600, early morning, light mist
HEAD: Most samurai spent their days serving their lord as officials, guards and clerks.
BODY: In 1600, many samurai worked more with brush and paper than with swords. A great lord, called a daimyo, needed many officials. Some samurai collected taxes in rice. Others kept records of land and harvests. Some guarded the gates of the castle or trained young soldiers. Others carried messages across the country. Each morning, men walked from their houses to the castle to begin their duties. Their rank decided where they lived, what they wore and how much rice they received. Historians note that a samurai's income was measured in koku. One koku was the amount of rice thought to feed one person for a year. A great lord might hold many thousands of koku. An ordinary samurai might receive only a few dozen. Even so, every samurai was bound to his lord by loyalty. That bond shaped every part of his life.
===
IMG: samurai-practising-archery-courtyard-target | samurai practising archery with tall wooden bows in a castle courtyard in 1600, straw target in the distance, white walls and curved tile roofs, clear morning light
HEAD: Part of every day was spent in training, with the sword, the spear and the bow.
BODY: A samurai was expected to stay ready for war. Many spent part of each day in practice. They trained with the sword, the spear and the long bow. The Japanese bow was taller than a man and made of bamboo and wood. Archers held it below the middle, which gave it a special shape. Riding was also important. Older warriors taught young men to shoot arrows from a moving horse. In 1600, the spear and the gun were the most important weapons on the battlefield. Yet the sword kept a special place. Schools of swordsmanship were growing in number. Teachers passed on their methods to a small circle of students. Training was not only about fighting. It was meant to build calm, patience and self-control. Those qualities were thought to matter in peace as much as in war.
===
IMG: samurai-writing-with-brush-low-desk-scroll | a samurai kneeling at a low wooden desk writing with an ink brush on a paper scroll, inkstone beside him, paper screens and soft afternoon light in a quiet room
HEAD: Many samurai were also expected to read, write and enjoy poetry.
BODY: In the afternoon, a samurai might sit at a low desk with an ink brush. Writing in Japan used thousands of Chinese characters, together with simpler Japanese signs. Learning them took many years. Samurai boys began their lessons young, often at a Buddhist temple. They copied old texts again and again until the brush moved easily. Educated warriors also read Chinese classics and books on strategy. Many wrote short poems. Some great generals left poems written just before their death. Historians note that the ideal warrior was skilled in both the brush and the sword. This idea was very old in Japan. Even so, not every samurai was well educated in 1600. Years of war had left little time for study. In the long peace that followed, learning would become far more common among the samurai class.
===
IMG: samurai-family-evening-meal-lantern-house | a samurai family kneeling on tatami at an evening meal in 1600, small lacquered trays with rice and soup, a paper lantern glowing, sliding doors open to a dark garden
HEAD: In the evening, the family ate together, and the house grew quiet early.
BODY: When the day's work was done, a samurai returned home before dark. Each person ate from a small tray placed on the floor. A simple evening meal included rice, soup, pickled vegetables and perhaps some fish. Light came from small oil lamps or candles inside paper lanterns. Lamp oil and candles were costly, so most families went to bed soon after dark. Thick bedding was laid out on the tatami mats. In winter, a small charcoal brazier gave a little warmth. The paper walls let in the sounds of the night. Crickets sang in the garden in summer. In autumn, the wind moved through the pine trees. A night watchman might pass along the street, striking two wooden blocks together. The sound told the town that all was well. Then the house slept until dawn.
===
TOPIC: Swords, armour and the first guns
===
IMG: swordsmith-forging-blade-workshop-sparks | a Japanese swordsmith in white clothes hammering a glowing blade on an anvil in a dim wooden workshop in 1600, sparks rising, a helper with a hammer beside him, firelight
HEAD: A Japanese sword was made by hand, over many weeks, by a master swordsmith.
BODY: Making a sword was slow and careful work. The swordsmith heated steel in a charcoal fire until it glowed. Then he hammered it, folded it and hammered it again. This was repeated many times. Folding helped to remove impurities and spread the carbon evenly through the steel. The smith often made the blade from steels of different hardness. A softer core kept the sword from breaking. A harder edge allowed it to stay very sharp. Before the final cooling, the blade was coated with clay. A thin layer covered the edge and a thicker layer covered the back. When the hot blade was plunged into water, the edge cooled faster. This gave the sword its gentle curve. Yet the work was not finished. A polisher then spent days bringing out the line of the hardened edge.
===
IMG: polished-katana-blade-on-stand-lamp | a polished Japanese sword blade resting on a wooden stand in a quiet room, the wavy line of the hardened edge visible in soft lamp light, a folded silk cloth beside it
HEAD: A good sword was treated as a family treasure and passed down through the generations.
BODY: A finished blade could be worth more than a house. The best swords came from famous schools of smiths in a few regions. Some blades were already centuries old by 1600. They had been carried by grandfathers and great-grandfathers. A sword was cleaned with care, oiled and wrapped in silk. Some were given as rewards by great lords. Others were offered as gifts to shrines and temples. Hideyoshi collected famous swords, and so did Tokugawa Ieyasu. Many of the blades they owned still survive. The idea that the sword was the soul of the samurai became popular in later centuries. Yet even in 1600, a sword was more than a weapon. It stood for the honour of the family. Losing it, or using it badly, brought shame on the whole house.
===
IMG: samurai-armour-displayed-on-stand-lacquered-plates | a full suit of samurai armour displayed on a wooden stand in a castle room, lacquered plates laced with coloured silk cords, a helmet with a crest, soft daylight through paper screens
HEAD: Samurai armour was made of many small plates, laced together with silk cords.
BODY: Japanese armour looked very different from the armour of European knights. It was made from many small plates of iron or hardened leather. The plates were lacquered to protect them from rain. Then they were laced together with coloured cords of silk or leather. This made the armour flexible and lighter than it looked. By 1600, many suits used larger solid plates on the chest, which could stop a musket ball better. The helmet often carried a crest. Some crests were simple. Others were shaped like horns, moons or flames. They helped soldiers recognise their commanders in battle. A good suit of armour was costly, and it was kept with great care. Even so, ordinary foot soldiers wore much simpler armour. It was often a plain chest plate and a light helmet shaped like a cone.
===
IMG: foot-soldiers-ashigaru-spears-banners-field | lines of Japanese foot soldiers in simple armour with long spears and tall narrow banners on their backs standing in a green field in 1600, mountains behind, morning light
HEAD: By 1600, most soldiers in Japanese armies were foot soldiers called ashigaru.
BODY: The armies of 1600 were very large. Most of the men were not mounted warriors. They were foot soldiers, called ashigaru, which means light feet. Many came from farming families. They fought in tight rows with very long spears. Some spears were more than five metres long. Others carried bows or guns. On their backs, many soldiers wore tall narrow banners. The banners showed the crest of their lord. On a crowded battlefield, they helped commanders see where each unit stood. Historians note that the rise of the foot soldier changed warfare in Japan. Battles were no longer won by a few brave warriors. They were won by discipline, numbers and good supplies. Yet the mounted samurai still led the armies. They gave the orders, and they took the greatest honours.
===
IMG: portuguese-matchlock-gun-tanegashima-island-1543 | a Japanese craftsman examining an early matchlock gun beside a Portuguese merchant on a beach on a green island with a small sailing ship offshore, sixteenth century, warm light
HEAD: Guns had arrived in Japan about sixty years earlier, on a small southern island.
BODY: In 1543, a Chinese ship with a few Portuguese traders on board landed on the island of Tanegashima. The Portuguese carried matchlock guns. The local lord was so impressed that he bought two of them. He ordered his smiths to study them and make copies. Within a few years, Japanese craftsmen were making guns of their own. For a long time, the Japanese simply called guns tanegashima, after the island. By 1600, there were probably tens of thousands of guns in Japan. Historians believe Japan had more guns than many countries in Europe at that time. A matchlock was slow to load. Rain could put out its burning cord. Yet in large numbers, fired in turns, guns could break a charge of horsemen. This new weapon helped bring the long age of war to an end.
===
IMG: samurai-on-horseback-crest-banner-field | a mounted samurai in lacquered armour with a crested helmet riding a sturdy horse across a field of tall grass in 1600, a small banner on his back, distant mountains, soft evening light
HEAD: Horses were important to the samurai, though Japanese horses were small and sturdy.
BODY: For centuries, the samurai had fought as mounted archers. By 1600, horses were still a sign of rank. Only higher samurai rode into battle. Japanese horses were smaller than the great war horses of Europe. Archaeologists who studied horse bones from this period found animals about the size of ponies. They were strong and sure-footed on mountain paths. Horses were not usually shod with iron. Instead, they sometimes wore sandals woven from straw. Riders sat in deep wooden saddles with wide stirrups. In peace, horses were used for travel and for ceremonies. Riding well was part of a samurai's education. Yet horses were costly to keep. A poor samurai often had to walk, even on long journeys. Only the wealthy could afford a stable, grooms and fodder for the winter.

TOPIC: Castles and castle towns
===
IMG: white-castle-keep-stone-base-curved-roofs-1600 | a tall white Japanese castle keep with several layers of curved grey tile roofs rising from a massive sloping stone base, surrounded by a moat and pine trees, clear blue sky, 1600
HEAD: In 1600, the castle was the heart of every great lord's domain.
BODY: The great castles of Japan were built in the last decades of the age of war. They stood on hills or in the middle of plains, close to rivers and roads. The main tower, called the tenshu, rose many floors high. Its walls were covered in white plaster. Its roofs were made of grey clay tiles with curved edges. Below it lay huge walls of fitted stone. Some stones weighed many tons and were moved without machines. Thousands of workers dragged them on sledges and rollers. Around the castle ran moats, gates and narrow paths. They were designed to confuse and slow down any attacker. Yet the castle was also a home and an office. The lord lived there with his family and servants. From its windows, he could look out over the land he ruled.
===
IMG: castle-stone-walls-moat-gate-bridge | massive curved stone walls of a Japanese castle rising from a still moat, a wooden bridge leading to a heavy gate with iron fittings, pine trees leaning over the water, soft morning light
HEAD: The stone walls of a castle were built with great skill and curved gently upward.
BODY: Japanese castle walls had a special shape. They sloped steeply and then curved upward near the top, like the line of a fan. This shape made them very strong. It also made them hard to climb. Builders fitted large stones together without mortar. Smaller stones filled the gaps behind them. Walls built in this way could survive earthquakes, which were common in Japan. The work was done by skilled teams of stonemasons. Some groups of masons were famous across the country. Lords sometimes marked their stones with small carved signs. They showed which family had supplied each block. Many of these marks can still be seen. Yet the walls were only the first line of defence. Behind them lay a maze of courtyards, gates and towers.
===
IMG: castle-interior-painted-screens-gold-leaf-hall | a large hall inside a Japanese castle in 1600 with tatami floors and sliding doors painted with pine trees and tigers on gold leaf, a lord seated at the far end, retainers kneeling in rows, soft light
HEAD: Inside, the halls of the greatest castles shone with gold and painted screens.
BODY: The rooms where a lord received his guests were richly decorated. Sliding doors and folding screens were covered in gold leaf. On them, painters showed pine trees, flowering plums, tigers, cranes and mountains. The most famous painters of the time worked for the great lords. The Kano school of painters was especially admired. In the hall, everyone sat on the floor in a fixed order. The lord sat at the far end, on a raised level. His most important followers sat closest to him. Others knelt further back, according to their rank. A visitor bowed deeply on entering. Every movement followed careful rules. Yet behind the golden halls, the living rooms were much simpler. There, the lord's family lived quietly, among plain wood, paper and straw mats.
===
IMG: castle-town-streets-shops-wooden-houses-busy | a busy street in a Japanese castle town in 1600 with rows of wooden shops with cloth curtains, merchants, porters and samurai walking, the white castle tower visible above the roofs, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Around each castle grew a town of samurai, merchants and craftsmen.
BODY: A castle needed people to serve it. So around each castle, a town slowly grew. Lords often ordered merchants and craftsmen to move there. The town was carefully planned. Samurai lived closest to the castle, in streets of walled houses. Further out lived the townspeople. Carpenters, smiths, dyers and cooks often had their own streets. Temples were placed along the edge of the town. In an attack, their strong walls and wide grounds could help defend it. Historians call these places castle towns. Many of Japan's large cities began in this way. Osaka, Nagoya and Kanazawa all grew around castles. Even so, in 1600 many of these towns were still young. Some were only a few years old. The sound of hammers and saws filled their streets from morning to evening.
===
IMG: osaka-castle-1600-great-keep-black-gold | the great castle of Osaka in 1600 with a tall dark keep decorated with gold ornaments, high stone walls and wide moats, the town spreading out below, river boats, golden evening light
HEAD: The largest castle in Japan in 1600 was Osaka Castle, built by Toyotomi Hideyoshi.
BODY: Toyotomi Hideyoshi began building Osaka Castle in 1583. He wanted a fortress that showed his power to the whole country. Tens of thousands of workers took part. Stones were brought by boat from far away. The main tower was decorated with gold. Its outer walls were dark, with golden ornaments shining in the sun. Hideyoshi died in 1598. His young son Hideyori lived in the castle with his mother, Lady Yodo. In 1600, Osaka was the richest city in Japan. Merchants from across the land traded rice, cloth, oil and salt there. Ships came and went along its rivers. Yet the castle's future was uncertain. Many lords wondered who would truly rule Japan now that Hideyoshi was gone. The answer would come later that same year, on a misty battlefield.
===
IMG: castle-garden-pond-stone-bridge-maple | a quiet garden inside a Japanese castle with a pond, carp, a small stone bridge, raked gravel and maple trees turning red, a stone lantern, soft autumn light
HEAD: Many castles also had peaceful gardens, made for walking, resting and quiet thought.
BODY: Behind the walls of many castles lay gardens. They were designed to feel calm and natural. A garden might hold a pond with carp, small islands and stone bridges. Paths of stepping stones wound between trees and mossy rocks. Stone lanterns stood among the plants. Some gardens copied famous landscapes in miniature. A small hill might stand for a mountain. A stretch of raked gravel might stand for the sea. Lords walked in these gardens to rest. They also received guests there for tea or poetry. Gardeners worked every day to keep each plant in shape. Historians note that many garden ideas came from Zen Buddhist temples. Yet castle gardens were also a sign of wealth. Only a powerful lord could set aside so much land for beauty alone.
===
IMG: castle-guards-night-lanterns-gate-watch | samurai guards with spears standing beside paper lanterns at a castle gate at night in 1600, stone walls and a curved roof above them, quiet and calm, moonlight
HEAD: At night, guards kept watch at the gates, and the castle fell silent.
BODY: When darkness came, the castle gates were closed. Guards took their places by the entrances and on the walls. They carried spears and paper lanterns. Every few hours, a new group of men took over. Fire was the greatest fear in a castle built of wood. Watchmen walked along the paths to check that no lamp or brazier had been left burning. Water was stored in large barrels near the buildings. In the town below, the streets grew quiet. Only the night watchman passed by, calling out and clapping his wooden blocks. Historians note that many castles burned down in later years. Lightning, accidents and war all took their toll. Yet in the calm of a peaceful night, the castle stood dark and still above the sleeping town, its white walls pale in the moonlight.

TOPIC: Farmers and the year of rice
===
IMG: farming-village-thatched-houses-rice-paddies | a Japanese farming village in 1600 with thatched wooden farmhouses among green rice paddies, a small stream, farmers in straw hats working in the fields, wooded hills behind, soft morning light
HEAD: Most people in Japan in 1600 were farmers, living in small villages among the rice fields.
BODY: In 1600, Japan probably had between twelve and eighteen million people. Historians believe that about four out of five were farmers. They lived in villages scattered across the valleys and plains. Farmhouses were built of wood, with thick roofs of thatch. Inside, there was often an earthen floor for work and cooking. A raised wooden floor served for eating and sleeping. In the middle of the house was a sunken hearth. Smoke rose slowly through the thatch, which helped to keep insects away. Around the village lay the rice fields. Every family knew each field, each ditch and each path by heart. Villages were close communities. Neighbours worked together at planting and harvest. Yet life was hard. A bad year could bring hunger to the whole valley.
===
IMG: rice-planting-early-summer-women-line-flooded-field | a row of women in straw hats and tied-back sleeves planting rice seedlings by hand in a flooded paddy field in early summer, mountains reflected in the water, soft light
HEAD: Rice was planted by hand in early summer, in fields flooded with water.
BODY: The year of rice began in spring. First, seeds were sown in small, sheltered beds. When the young plants were strong, they were moved to the fields. By then, the fields had been flooded with water from rivers and ponds. Planting took place in early summer, often during the rainy season. Rows of farmers waded through the mud, pressing the seedlings into the ground by hand. Women did much of this work. In many villages, planting was a festival as well as a task. Drums and flutes played at the edge of the fields. Songs helped the workers keep time. Historians note that this custom is very old in Japan. Yet the work was exhausting. Bending all day in the water hurt the back. Leeches clung to the legs of the planters.
===
IMG: terraced-rice-fields-mountain-slope-water-channels | green rice terraces climbing a mountain slope in Japan in 1600, stone and earth walls, water flowing through small channels from field to field, a farmer walking along a narrow path, mist on the peaks
HEAD: In the hills, farmers built terraces, so that rice could grow even on steep slopes.
BODY: Japan is a land of mountains. Flat land was precious. So in many places, farmers built terraces on the hillsides. Each terrace was a narrow flat field held up by a wall of earth or stone. Water flowed from the highest field down to the next, and then to the next. Small channels and gates controlled its flow. Keeping the water moving correctly was a shared duty. Villages chose leaders to settle disputes over water. A quarrel over water could divide a village for years. Some terraces had been built centuries earlier. Each generation repaired the walls after heavy rain. Historians note that rice fields covered a large part of the farmland of Japan. Even so, farmers also grew barley, wheat, millet, beans and vegetables, often on drier land beside the paddies.
===
IMG: rice-harvest-autumn-sickles-drying-racks | farmers cutting golden rice with sickles in autumn and hanging the bundles on wooden drying racks along the edge of a field, a village and red maple trees in the background, warm afternoon light
HEAD: In autumn, the ripe rice was cut with sickles and hung on racks to dry.
BODY: By autumn, the rice fields had turned from green to gold. The water was drained away, and the harvest began. Farmers cut the stalks by hand with small curved sickles. They tied them in bundles. Then they hung the bundles upside down on long wooden racks. There the rice dried in the sun and the wind. After drying, the grains were separated from the stalks. The straw was not wasted. It was used for roofs, sandals, raincoats, mats and rope. Every family worked in the fields at harvest time, even the children. When the work was finished, villages gave thanks at the local shrine. Yet the harvest did not belong only to the farmer. A large share had to be handed over as tax. The lord's officials came to measure it carefully.
===
IMG: officials-measuring-rice-tax-bales-village | samurai officials with brushes and record books watching farmers stack straw bales of rice in a village courtyard in 1600, a storehouse with a thick thatched roof, autumn light
HEAD: Farmers paid their taxes in rice, often almost half of everything they grew.
BODY: The whole system of power in Japan rested on rice. Lords received their income from the land they controlled. Farmers paid it in rice, packed in straw bales. The tax was often between forty and fifty percent of the harvest. Sometimes it was even higher. Toyotomi Hideyoshi had ordered a great survey of the land in the years before 1600. Officials measured fields across the country. They recorded how much rice each one should produce. This survey set the tax for many years to come. The village as a whole was responsible for paying. If one family could not pay, the others had to help. Historians note that this made villages very close, and very careful. Yet in bad years, the tax could leave families with too little to eat until spring.
===
IMG: farmhouse-winter-hearth-family-straw-work | a farming family sitting around a sunken hearth inside a thatched farmhouse in winter in 1600, weaving straw sandals and baskets, a kettle hanging over the fire, snow outside the door, warm firelight
HEAD: In winter, farm families stayed close to the hearth and made things from straw.
BODY: When winter came, the fields lay empty. In the north and in the mountains, snow could cover the villages for months. Families gathered around the sunken hearth in the middle of the house. A kettle hung above the fire on a long hook. The warmth reached only a little way into the room. Even so, winter was not a time of rest. Farmers used the dark months to make things. They wove straw into sandals, baskets, mats and raincoats. They repaired their tools. Some made paper, cloth or charcoal to sell. Children listened to old stories by the fire. Grandparents told tales of foxes, mountain spirits and brave heroes. Historians note that many folk tales were passed on in this way. In the spring, when the snow melted, the year of rice would begin again.
===
IMG: village-shrine-harvest-festival-lanterns-drums | villagers gathered at a small Shinto shrine with a red torii gate for an autumn harvest festival in 1600, paper lanterns, a drum, offerings of rice and sake, evening light through tall cedar trees
HEAD: Villages held festivals at their local shrine to pray for rain and to give thanks for the harvest.
BODY: Every village had its own shrine. It honoured the kami, the spirits of the land, the mountains and the rice. Farmers believed these spirits could bring good weather or bad. So in spring, they prayed for rain and a good year. In autumn, they gave thanks for the harvest. Offerings of rice, sake and fruit were placed before the shrine. Drums were beaten, and people danced. Sometimes a portable shrine was carried through the village on the shoulders of young men. Festivals were among the happiest days of the year. They brought the whole village together. Old quarrels were set aside. People ate, drank and sang late into the night. Yet the next morning, the work began again. The rhythm of the year was always set by the rice and the seasons.
