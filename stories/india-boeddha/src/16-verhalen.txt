The Buddha, and his followers, loved stories, and told many of them, to explain the teaching, in simple ways. Some of the most beloved are the Jataka tales: stories of the Buddha's earlier lives, before he was born as Siddhartha, when, the stories say, he was born, again, and again, as a man, or an animal, and each time, practised kindness, and wisdom, and courage.

Let us listen to two of them.

--

Long ago, the first story says, the Buddha-to-be was born as a monkey, the king of a great troop of eighty thousand monkeys, who lived in a great mango tree, beside the river Ganges, in the Himalayas, and ate its wonderful, sweet mangoes.

One day, a mango fell into the river, and floated, all the way down, to Varanasi, where the king of Varanasi was bathing, and he tasted it, and it was the most delicious fruit he had ever eaten. And he went, with his soldiers, up the river, to find the tree. And when he found it, full of monkeys, he ordered his archers to surround it, so that, in the morning, they could take all the mangoes.

{img:monkey-king-stretched-between-trees-troop-escaping-across-river-dawn}
The monkey king saw the danger. And he climbed, to the top of the tree, and leapt, a great leap, across the river, to a tree, on the other side, and tied a long bamboo cane to his foot, and leapt back. But the cane was a little too short. So he held onto a branch, of the mango tree, with his hands, and stretched his body, across the gap, so that it became a bridge. And all eighty thousand monkeys ran, across his back, one by one, to safety, on the other side.

--

The king of Varanasi watched, from below, in the moonlight, and was amazed. In the morning, he had the tired monkey king gently brought down, and laid on a soft bed, and cared for, and asked him: Why did you do this, for them? And the monkey king said: I am their king, and they trusted me. A king must care for his people, more than for himself.

And the king of Varanasi never forgot it, and ruled his own people, from that day, with kindness, and care.

{img:full-moon-hare-silhouette-on-moon-forest-night-gentle}
The second story is about a hare.

--

Long ago, the Buddha-to-be was born as a hare, who lived in a forest, with three friends: a monkey, and a jackal, and an otter. And on a holy day, they decided that they would each give a gift of food, to any traveller who came by.

The otter found some fish. The jackal found some meat, and a pot of curds. The monkey picked some mangoes. But the hare only ate grass, and had nothing to give. And he thought: if a traveller comes, I will give him myself.

And the god Sakka, the king of the gods, heard this, and wanted to test him. So he came to the forest, disguised as a poor, old, hungry Brahmin. And the hare said: Light a fire, good Brahmin, and I will give you my body, to eat. And the Brahmin lit a fire, and the hare jumped into it, joyfully.

{img:hare-sitting-moonlit-grass-friends-otter-monkey-jackal-forest}
But the fire was cool, like snow, and did not burn him. And the old Brahmin turned back into the god, Sakka, who said: Little hare, your kindness will be remembered, forever. And he took a mountain, and squeezed it, and with its juice, he drew the picture of the hare, on the face of the moon, so that everyone, in the world, would see it, and remember.

--

And that is why, in India, and in many countries of Asia, when people look up at the full moon, they do not see a man in the moon. They see a hare.

Tonight, if the moon is up, you can look for him, too: the little hare, sitting, in the moon, with his long ears, shining, softly, over the forests, and the rivers, of India.
