Let us go to the city of Shravasti, the great city of the kingdom of Kosala.

It is one of the largest cities of India, in the time of the Buddha, with perhaps tens of thousands of people, surrounded by walls, of earth, and brick, with a moat, and gates, and watchtowers, beside the river Rapti.

--

Inside, the streets are crowded, and noisy, and colourful. There are houses, of brick, and wood, two, and three storeys high, with balconies, and carved wooden doors, and windows, with lattices, and roofs, of tiles, and thatch. There are palaces, and gardens, and parks, with lotus pools. There are shops, opening onto the streets, selling everything: cloth, and spices, and perfumes, and jewellery, and pots, and pans, and grain, and sweets, and flowers.

And the streets are full of people, and animals: bullock carts, creaking, and horses, and chariots, and elephants, swaying slowly through the crowds, with their riders, and porters, carrying loads, on their heads, and on poles, across their shoulders, and merchants, and monks, and soldiers, and beggars, and dancers, and acrobats, and snake charmers, and storytellers.

{img:craftsmen-street-guild-ivory-carvers-goldsmiths-weavers-workshops}
The craftsmen of the city are organised, in guilds: the weavers, and the potters, and the carpenters, and the smiths, and the goldsmiths, and the ivory carvers, and the perfume makers, and the garland makers, each with its own street, or quarter, and its own leaders, and its own rules. The guilds are powerful, and rich, and the sons learn the craft of their fathers, from generation to generation.

--

And there is money.

In these years, in India, the first coins are being made. They are small, flat pieces of silver, of a fixed weight, cut, and stamped, with little symbols, punched into the metal, one by one: a sun, and a wheel, and an elephant, and a tree, and a mountain, and a bull. They are called punch-marked coins, and they are among the first coins in the world. And with them, it is much easier to buy, and sell, and travel, and trade.

{img:punch-marked-silver-coins-ancient-india-symbols-sun-wheel-close}
The merchants of Shravasti are very rich. The richest of them all is a man called Sudatta, who is so generous, that he is called Anathapindika, which means, the one who feeds the poor. Every day, he gives food, to hundreds of poor people, at his house.

--

One day, Anathapindika hears the Buddha teach, at Rajagriha, and is so moved, that he invites him, to come, and stay, at Shravasti, during the rainy season. And he looks for a place, for him, and his monks, to stay: a quiet grove, near the city, but not too near.

He finds a beautiful park, belonging to a prince, Prince Jeta. But the prince does not want to sell it, and says, jokingly, that he will only sell it, for as many gold coins as it takes to cover the ground. And Anathapindika says yes, and brings cartloads of gold coins, and begins to cover the ground with them.

{img:jetavana-grove-monastery-shravasti-trees-paths-monks-evening}
The prince is so amazed, that he gives the rest of the land for nothing, and gives the trees, too, as his own gift. And so, the Jetavana, the grove of Jeta, becomes the great monastery, where the Buddha will spend more rainy seasons than anywhere else, about twenty of them, and where he will give many of his teachings.

--

Today, at Shravasti, in the state of Uttar Pradesh, you can still walk, among the ruins of the Jetavana, among the old brick foundations, of the monasteries, and the stupas, under the great trees, where pilgrims, from Sri Lanka, and Thailand, and Myanmar, and Japan, and all over the world, sit, quietly, in the shade, and chant, and meditate.
