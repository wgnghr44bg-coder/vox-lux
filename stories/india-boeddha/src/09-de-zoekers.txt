In the time of the Buddha, the roads, and the forests, of India are full of wanderers.

They are called shramanas, which means, those who strive. They are men, and some women, who have left their homes, and their families, and their possessions, and their work, to search for the truth, and for freedom from suffering.

--

They live very simply. They wear only a simple robe, or a loincloth, or, some of them, nothing at all. They own almost nothing: a bowl, for begging, and perhaps a water pot, and a staff. They sleep under trees, or in caves, or in little huts, of leaves. And they live on what people give them: every morning, they walk, silently, into the villages, and stand, at the doors, and the people put a little food into their bowls.

Some of them practise great hardships: fasting, for days, and weeks, and standing, on one leg, and sitting, in the burning sun, or the freezing cold, and holding their breath, for a long time, believing that, by mastering the body, they can free the spirit.

{img:meditating-hermit-cave-mouth-hills-dawn-stillness-simple-life}
Others practise meditation: sitting, still, and silent, for hours, with their legs crossed, and their eyes closed, calming the mind, and looking deep within.

--

There are many different teachers, and many different schools, with their followers. And they travel, from place to place, and meet, in the parks, outside the cities, and in special halls, built by the kings, and the merchants, for them, and they discuss, and debate, the great questions, before the crowds, who come, to listen, as if to a great show.

One of the greatest teachers of the time is Vardhamana, called Mahavira, the great hero, a prince, like the Buddha, who has left his home, and become a wanderer, and taught a path of great gentleness, and great self-discipline. His followers, the Jains, do not harm any living thing, not even the smallest insect. Some of them sweep the path in front of them, with a soft brush, as they walk, so as not to step on anything alive, and wear a cloth over their mouths, so as not to breathe in tiny creatures. The Jain religion is still alive, in India, today.

{img:jain-monks-walking-soft-brush-sweeping-path-white-robes-gentle}
The people of India respect the wanderers, and give them food, gladly, believing that it brings them good fortune, and that the seekers are doing something important, for everyone.

--

And the kings, too, honour them. King Bimbisara, of Magadha, likes to visit the forest sages, and the wanderers, and sit with them, and ask them questions, about life, and death, and the right way to live, and to rule.

So the young prince, Siddhartha, when he leaves his home, will not be alone. He will join a great company of seekers, walking the roads of India, under the hot sun, and the monsoon rain, with their bowls, and their questions, looking for the answer.
