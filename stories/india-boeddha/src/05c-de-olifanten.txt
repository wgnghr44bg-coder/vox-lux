In the forests of India, in the time of the Buddha, there are elephants.

They live in great herds, in the forests, and the jungles, between the villages, and in the foothills of the Himalayas: the mothers, and the aunts, and the grandmothers, and the young ones, led by the oldest, and wisest, female, walking, slowly, through the trees, eating leaves, and grass, and bamboo, and fruit, and coming down to the rivers, in the evening, to drink, and bathe, and play.

--

The people of India love, and respect, the elephants. They are strong, and wise, and gentle, and they never forget. In the old stories, the elephants are often noble, and kind, and the Buddha-to-be was born, several times, the stories say, as a great white elephant. And on the night before the Buddha was born, his mother, Queen Maya, dreamed that a white elephant, with six tusks, came down from the sky, and entered her side, a sign that her son would be great.

The kings catch wild elephants, in the forests, and tame them, and train them, for work, and for war, and for ceremonies. The elephant trainers, called mahouts, spend their whole lives, with their elephants, from when they are young, and know them, and talk to them, and sing to them, and the elephants know them, and trust them.

{img:mahout-washing-elephant-river-scrubbing-boy-laughing-evening}
The tame elephants work hard: carrying great logs, from the forests, and pulling carts, and lifting stones, for the walls, and carrying the kings, and the nobles, on their backs, in the processions, in great howdahs, decorated with gold, and silk, and jewels. And every evening, the mahouts take their elephants down to the river, and wash them, scrubbing their great grey backs, with stones, and coconut husks, while the elephants lie, happily, in the water, and spray themselves, with their trunks.

--

There is a story, about the Buddha, and an elephant. At Rajagriha, there was a great, fierce elephant, called Nalagiri, who was drunk, and angry, and was let loose, in the street, where the Buddha was walking, on his alms round. And the people ran, and screamed, and hid, as the great elephant came charging, down the street, toward the Buddha.

But the Buddha stood still, calmly, and looked at the elephant, with great kindness, and love, and spoke to him, gently. And the great elephant slowed down, and stopped, in front of him, and lowered his head, and the Buddha stroked his forehead, softly, with his hand. And the elephant knelt down, quietly, and was calm.

{img:elephant-kneeling-before-monk-gentle-street-crowd-watching-rajgir}
The people, watching, from their windows, and their roofs, were amazed. And this story is carved, and painted, in temples, all over Asia: the great elephant, kneeling, quietly, before the calm, gentle figure, in the street.

--

Today, there are still wild elephants, in the forests of India, in the foothills of the Himalayas, and in the south, and in the east, though far fewer than in the Buddha's time. And in the evenings, in some places, you can still see them, coming down to the rivers, to drink, and bathe, as the sun sets, as they have done, for thousands of years.
