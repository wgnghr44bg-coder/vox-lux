At first, the Buddha's monks were only men. But women, too, wanted to follow the path.

The stories tell that, some years after his awakening, the Buddha's aunt, and foster-mother, Mahaprajapati, who had brought him up, after his own mother died, came to him, and asked to be allowed to become a nun. And at first, he hesitated.

--

But Mahaprajapati did not give up. She, and a group of other women, of the Shakya clan, many of them widows, or women whose husbands had become monks, cut off their hair, and put on simple robes, and walked, barefoot, a long way, to the city of Vaishali, where the Buddha was staying, and stood, at the gate, with their feet swollen, and dusty, and their faces streaked with tears.

The Buddha's faithful attendant, and cousin, Ananda, saw them, and went to the Buddha, and asked him: Lord, can a woman, too, reach awakening, if she follows the path? And the Buddha said: Yes, Ananda, she can. And Ananda said: Then, Lord, let them be ordained. And the Buddha agreed.

{img:buddhist-nuns-grove-meditating-together-ancient-india-peaceful-light}
And so, Mahaprajapati became the first Buddhist nun, and the order of nuns, the bhikkhunis, began, one of the first communities of women, devoted to a spiritual life, in the history of the world.

--

Thousands of women joined it: princesses, and queens, and merchants' daughters, and farmers' wives, and servants, and courtesans, and widows, and mothers, who had lost their children.

And some of them wrote poems, or spoke them, which were remembered, and collected, in a book, called the Therigatha, the verses of the elder nuns. It is one of the oldest collections of poems by women, anywhere in the world.

{img:elder-nun-sitting-forest-hut-evening-light-peaceful-face}
In these poems, the nuns speak, simply, and honestly, of their lives: of their hardships, and their sorrows, and their freedom. One nun, Mutta, wrote: So free am I, so gloriously free, free from three crooked things: from the grinding stone, from the mortar, and from my crooked husband.

--

And another, an old nun, called Ambapali, who had been a famous, and beautiful, courtesan, in the city of Vaishali, and had given her mango grove, to the Buddha, and his monks, wrote a poem, about growing old: how her hair, once black, and soft, and scented, is now like bark, and her eyes, once bright, like jewels, are now dim, and her voice, once sweet, like a cuckoo's, now falters. And at the end of each verse, she says, gently: the words of the truthful one are not false. All things change.

Many other women supported the Buddha, too, as lay followers. One of the greatest was Visakha, a rich, and generous, and wise woman, of Shravasti, who built a great monastery, for the monks, the Eastern Park, and gave food, and robes, and medicine, and was a friend, and adviser, to the Buddha, and the whole community, for many years.
