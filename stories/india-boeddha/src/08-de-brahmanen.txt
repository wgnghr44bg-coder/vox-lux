In the India of the Buddha's time, there are the Brahmins.

The Brahmins are the priests, and the teachers, and the keepers of the sacred knowledge. Their knowledge is the Veda: a great collection of hymns, and prayers, and chants, and rituals, in the ancient language, Sanskrit, which are very old, already more than a thousand years old, some of them, and which have never been written down, but are learned, by heart, and passed on, from teacher to pupil, word for word, sound for sound.

--

A young Brahmin boy goes to live with his teacher, at about the age of eight, and stays with him, for twelve years, or more, learning the Veda, by heart, listening, and repeating, again, and again, every syllable, every sound, every tone, perfectly, until he knows thousands of verses, from memory. And because of this careful method, the hymns of the Veda have been passed down, without changing, for more than three thousand years, and are still chanted, in the same way, today.

The Brahmins perform the great sacrifices, for the kings, and the rich, and the small, daily rituals, for the families: at the sacred fire, which burns, in every Brahmin household, and into which they pour offerings, of ghee, and milk, and grain, and chant the ancient hymns, to the gods: to Agni, the god of fire, and Indra, the king of the gods, with his thunderbolt, and Surya, the sun, and Soma, and Varuna, and many others.

{img:teacher-pupils-forest-hermitage-learning-veda-by-heart-sitting-circle}
Society, in the old teachings, is divided into four great orders, called varnas: the Brahmins, the priests, and the teachers; the Kshatriyas, the warriors, and the rulers; the Vaishyas, the farmers, and the merchants; and the Shudras, the servants, and the workers. And within these, there are many smaller groups, of families, and trades, and clans.

--

{img:people-of-many-walks-ancient-india-market-crowd-merchants-farmers-priests}
In the time of the Buddha, these divisions are important, but not yet as strict as they will become, later. The Buddha himself is a Kshatriya, of the warrior order, and he will say, often, that a person is not noble, or low, by birth, but by their actions, and their heart. And his followers will come from every order, and every kind of family: Brahmins, and princes, and merchants, and farmers, and barbers, and sweepers.

And in these years, too, some of the Brahmins themselves are asking new, deep questions, beyond the rituals. In the forests, and the hermitages, the teachers, and their pupils, sit together, and discuss the meaning of everything: the self, and the world, and what is real, and what lies behind all things, and what happens to us, when we die.

{img:forest-sage-teaching-young-students-under-tree-quiet-afternoon-light}
Their teachings are collected, in the Upanishads, which means, sitting down near: sitting near the teacher, to listen. They speak of the deep, true self, within every person, and of the great reality, behind the whole world, and say, in a famous phrase: that thou art. You, yourself, are that.

--

And they speak of a great cycle, of birth, and death, and rebirth, in which every living being is born, again, and again, in a new body, according to its deeds, its karma, in the life before, and of the hope, of finding release, one day, from this endless wheel, into a peace beyond all change.

These ideas are in the air, in the India of the Buddha's time, discussed, and debated, by the wise, in the forests, and the courts, and the parks, of the cities, and the young prince, Siddhartha, will hear them, and think about them, and go his own way.
