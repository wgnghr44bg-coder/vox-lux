Most of the people of India, in the time of the Buddha, live in villages.

A village is a little world, of perhaps thirty, or fifty, or a hundred families, living close together, in a cluster of houses, among the fields, and the groves, with a pond, or a well, and a great tree, a banyan, or a pipal, in the middle, where the people meet, and talk, and the old men sit, in the shade, and the children play.

--

The houses are made of mud, and wood, and bamboo, with thatched roofs, of straw, and grass, and palm leaves. They are built around little courtyards, swept clean, every morning, and smeared with fresh cow dung, mixed with water, which dries hard, and smooth, and keeps away the dust, and the insects. On the walls, the women paint patterns, in white, and red, for festivals, and good luck.

The cattle are the wealth of the village. The cows give milk, and butter, and curds, and ghee, the clarified butter, which is used in cooking, and in offerings, to the gods. The oxen pull the ploughs, and the carts. And the dung is dried, in round flat cakes, stuck to the walls, and used as fuel, for the cooking fires. The cattle are loved, and cared for, and given names, and decorated, at the festivals.

{img:farmer-ploughing-paddy-oxen-wooden-plough-iron-tip-water-reflections}
Around the village, there are the fields. The most important crop is rice, which grows in flooded fields, called paddies, in the rainy season. The farmers plough the wet fields, with their oxen, and the women plant the young rice seedlings, one by one, in long rows, in the water, and mud, bending over, all day long, and singing.

--

In the dry season, they grow barley, and wheat, and millet, and lentils, and beans, and sesame, for oil, and mustard, and sugar cane, which they crush, to make a kind of brown sugar, and cotton, which the women spin, and weave.

Each village has its craftsmen: a carpenter, who makes the ploughs, and the carts, and the houses, and a blacksmith, and a potter, at his wheel, and a weaver, and a barber, and a washerman, who washes the clothes, at the river, beating them on a flat stone. And the villagers pay them, not with money, but with a share of the harvest, every year.

{img:village-headman-elders-under-banyan-tree-meeting-afternoon-shade}
The village has a headman, who settles quarrels, and collects the king's share of the harvest, a sixth part, usually, and the elders, who meet, under the great tree, and decide things, together.

--

And the life of the village follows the seasons: the hot season, when the earth is dry, and cracked, and the air shimmers, and the people rest, in the shade, through the long afternoons; the rainy season, when the monsoon comes, and everything turns green, and the rice is planted; and the cool season, when the harvest is gathered, and the festivals, and the weddings, are held, and the nights are cold, and clear, and full of stars.
