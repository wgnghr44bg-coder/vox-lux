After the Buddha's death, his followers met, the stories say, at Rajagriha, in a great council, of five hundred monks, and recited, together, all his teachings, so that they would be remembered, exactly, and passed on.

For hundreds of years, the teachings were passed on, by heart, from teacher, to pupil, as the Vedas had been, until, at last, about four hundred years later, in Sri Lanka, they were written down, on palm leaves.

--

The kingdom of Magadha grew, and grew, and about two hundred years after the Buddha, it became the centre of the first great empire of India, the Maurya Empire, stretching across almost the whole of the subcontinent. And its greatest emperor was Ashoka.

Ashoka was a fierce conqueror, at first. But after a terrible war, in the land of Kalinga, he was filled with sorrow, at the suffering he had caused, and turned to the teaching of the Buddha, and decided to rule, from then on, by kindness, and justice, and peace.

{img:ashoka-edict-carved-rock-brahmi-script-inscription-sunlit-surface}
He had his messages, his edicts, carved on rocks, and on great, polished stone pillars, all over his empire, for everyone to read: messages about kindness to animals, and respect for all religions, and care for the poor, and the sick, and the old, and honesty, and generosity. He built hospitals, for people, and for animals, and planted trees, along the roads, for shade, and dug wells, and built rest houses, for travellers.

--

And he visited the places of the Buddha's life, as a pilgrim. At Lumbini, he set up a pillar, with an inscription, saying: Here, the Buddha was born. And that pillar is still there, today.

He sent messengers, and monks, to other lands, to share the teaching: to Sri Lanka, and to the south of India, and to the lands of the Greeks, in the west, and, the stories say, to Burma, and beyond. And from there, over the centuries, the teaching of the Buddha spread, peacefully, along the trade routes, and the sea routes, to China, and Korea, and Japan, and Tibet, and Thailand, and Cambodia, and Vietnam, and much of Asia.

{img:monks-travelling-silk-road-caravan-mountains-scrolls-china-bound}
In India itself, over many centuries, Buddhism slowly faded, and the great monasteries, and universities, like Nalanda, near Rajagriha, which had thousands of students, from all over Asia, became ruins, and the places of the Buddha's life were forgotten, and lost, in the jungles, and the fields.

--

But in the nineteenth century, a British scholar, James Prinsep, worked out how to read the old script of Ashoka's inscriptions, in 1837, and an archaeologist, Alexander Cunningham, using the travel diaries of Chinese pilgrims, who had visited India, more than a thousand years before, found the places again, one by one: Bodh Gaya, and Sarnath, and Kushinagar, and Shravasti. And in 1896, in the jungle, near the border of Nepal, the pillar of Ashoka, at Lumbini, was found, with its inscription: Here, the Buddha was born.
