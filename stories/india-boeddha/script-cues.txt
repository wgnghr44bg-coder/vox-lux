Tonight we are going about two and a half thousand years back, to the great plain of the river Ganges, in northern India, around the year 500 BC.

[long-pause]

It is early morning. The sun is rising, red, and huge, through the mist, over a wide, slow river, and its light spreads, across the water, in a long, golden path. On the banks, there are villages, among mango trees, and palms, and the green fields of rice, and the smoke of the morning fires is rising, into the still air. A boat is crossing the river, slowly, the boatman pushing it along, with a long pole. And on the steps, down to the water, people are bathing, and praying, facing the rising sun.

[pause]

Somewhere, a bird is calling, a koel, its clear, rising call, over and over, from the trees.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

This is India, in the time of the Buddha.

[pause]

{img:indian-village-500bc-thatched-huts-banyan-tree-cattle-morning-women-water}
It is a time of great change. All across the plain of the Ganges, forests are being cleared, and new fields planted, and villages are growing into towns, and towns into cities, with walls, and gates, and markets, and palaces. New kingdoms are rising, and old ones are falling. Merchants travel, with their caravans, from city to city, and the first coins are being made.

[pause]

And it is a time of great questions. Many people, rich, and poor, kings, and merchants, and farmers, are asking: What is the meaning of life? Why is there suffering? What happens after death? How can we find peace? And some of them leave their homes, and their families, and go out, into the forests, to look for the answers.

[long-pause]

One of them is a young prince, from a small country, at the foot of the Himalayas. His name is Siddhartha Gautama. And after many years of searching, he will find an answer, sitting, one night, under a great tree, by a river, and he will become known, as the Buddha, the one who has woken up.

[pause]

Tonight, we are going to travel through the India of his time. We will walk through the villages, and the rice fields, and the great cities, of Rajagriha, and Shravasti, and Varanasi. We will sit with the farmers, and the merchants, and the kings, and the forest sages. We will watch the monsoon come, and spend the rainy season, in a quiet grove. And we will follow the Buddha, gently, from his birth, in a garden, to his last journey.

[pause]

{img:bodhi-tree-pipal-leaves-heart-shaped-sunlight-quiet-grove}
We will not try to teach, or to persuade, but simply to look, and listen, quietly, at a world, long ago, and the people who lived in it.

[long-pause]

So let us walk down to the river, in the early morning, with the mist on the water, and the sound of the koel, and the bells of the cattle, and the smell of woodsmoke, and wet earth, and begin.

[long-pause]

{img:ganges-river-wide-sandbanks-boats-sails-afternoon-light-kites-flying}
The river Ganges rises far to the north, in the high mountains of the Himalayas, from an ice cave, at the foot of a great glacier, and rushes down, through the gorges, and the valleys, and comes out, onto the plains, and flows, east, for more than two thousand kilometres, across the whole of northern India, to the sea, at the Bay of Bengal.

[pause]

Along the way, it is joined by many other great rivers, flowing down from the mountains: the Yamuna, and the Gandak, and the Ghaghara, and the Son, and many more, so that it becomes wider, and wider, and slower, and slower, until, in places, you can hardly see the other side.

[long-pause]

For the people of India, the Ganges is not just a river. It is a goddess, Ganga, sacred, and pure, who came down from heaven, to the earth, to bless it. To bathe in her waters is to be cleansed, and to die beside her, is a blessing.

[pause]

The plain of the Ganges is one of the most fertile lands in the world. Every year, in the rainy season, the rivers flood, and spread their rich, soft mud, over the land, and the soil is deep, and dark, and good. Two harvests can be grown, every year, sometimes three.

[pause]

{img:ancient-forest-clearing-iron-axes-farmers-new-fields-ganges-plain}
In earlier times, much of this plain was covered with thick forest, full of wild animals: elephants, and tigers, and rhinoceros, and deer, and wild buffalo. But now, in the time of the Buddha, the forests are being cleared, with iron axes, and the land is being ploughed, with iron ploughshares, pulled by oxen, and new villages are being founded, everywhere.

[long-pause]

Iron has changed everything. It makes better axes, and ploughs, and sickles, and tools, so that the farmers can clear more land, and grow more food, and so there are more people, and more villages, and more towns.

[pause]

Still, the forests are never far away. Between the villages, and the fields, there are groves, of mango, and banyan, and sal, and woods, and jungles, where the wild elephants roam, and the tigers hunt, and the monkeys chatter, in the trees, and the peacocks call, at dusk, and the hermits, and the wandering seekers, live, in little huts, of leaves.

[pause]

{img:peacock-dancing-feathers-spread-grove-monsoon-clouds-village-edge}
And in the north, on clear days, after the rain, you can see them, far away, on the horizon: the Himalayas, the abode of snow, white, and shining, high above the green plain, like a wall of clouds, the highest mountains in the world.

[long-pause]

This great plain, of rivers, and fields, and forests, and villages, and cities, is the world in which the Buddha lived, and walked, and taught, for all of his long life, from the foothills of the mountains, in the north, to the banks of the Ganges, in the south.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-indian-city-wooden-palisade-gate-watchtowers-moat-500bc}
In the time of the Buddha, northern India is divided into many countries, sixteen great ones, the old texts say, called the mahajanapadas, the great lands, and many smaller ones.

[pause]

Some of them are kingdoms, ruled by kings, in their palaces, with their armies, and their ministers. Others are republics, ruled by assemblies, of the heads of the great families, who meet, in a hall, and discuss, and debate, and vote, and choose a leader, for a time.

[long-pause]

The two greatest kingdoms are Magadha, and Kosala.

[pause]

Magadha lies in the east, south of the Ganges, in what is now the state of Bihar. It is rich, with fertile land, and forests, full of elephants, and timber, and mines, of iron. Its king, in the time of the Buddha, is Bimbisara, and its capital is Rajagriha, the house of the king, a city, surrounded by five hills.

[pause]

{img:kosala-city-shravasti-palace-gardens-river-rapti-ancient-india}
Kosala lies to the north-west, along the rivers flowing down from the mountains. Its king is Prasenajit, and its great city is Shravasti, a rich trading city, on the river Rapti.

[long-pause]

And there are republics, like the Vajji confederacy, in the north, with its capital, Vaishali, ruled by the Licchavis, a great clan, whose assembly meets in a hall, in the city, and is famous for its fairness, and its harmony. And there are the Mallas, and the Shakyas, smaller republics, near the foothills of the Himalayas.

[pause]

The Shakyas are a proud, ancient clan, ruling a small country, around the town of Kapilavastu, at the foot of the mountains, in what is now the border of India, and Nepal. Their chief, in the time of our story, is called Shuddhodana. And it is into his family that the Buddha will be born.

[pause]

{img:assembly-hall-republic-elders-seated-debate-vaishali-pillars}
The kingdoms, and the republics, are rivals. They make alliances, and marriages, between their royal families, and sometimes, quarrels, and wars. Slowly, over the years, Magadha will grow stronger, and stronger, and swallow up its neighbours, one by one, until, two hundred years later, it will become the centre of the first great empire of India.

[long-pause]

But in the time of the Buddha, the kings, and the republics, still live side by side, and the roads between them are open, to merchants, and pilgrims, and wanderers, and the Buddha will travel freely, from one country to another, for forty-five years, and be welcomed, by kings, and by the people, wherever he goes.

[long-pause]

{img:rajagriha-valley-five-hills-stone-walls-city-ancient-india-morning}
Let us visit Rajagriha, the capital of Magadha.

[pause]

It lies in a valley, surrounded, on all sides, by five rocky hills, covered with forest, so that the hills are like the walls of a great fortress. And along the tops of the hills, there are walls of stone, great, rough, unmortared stones, piled up, for kilometres, around the whole valley, with gates, in the passes. Parts of these walls are still there, today, among the trees, among the oldest stone walls in India.

[long-pause]

Inside, there is the city: the king's palace, and the houses of the nobles, and the merchants, and the craftsmen, built of wood, and brick, and clay, with thatched roofs, and tiled roofs, and balconies, and courtyards, and gardens, and markets, and streets, full of people, and bullock carts, and elephants, and horses.

[pause]

Around the valley, among the hills, there are hot springs, where the people come to bathe, in the warm water, and there are caves, in the hills, where the hermits live, and meditate. And on one of the hills, there is a rocky peak, shaped like a vulture, called Vulture Peak, where the Buddha, later, will often stay, and teach, looking out, over the valley, and the plains.

[pause]

{img:vulture-peak-rajgir-rocky-summit-sunset-plains-below-quiet}
Outside the city walls, there is a beautiful grove of bamboo, cool, and green, and rustling, in the wind, with a pond, where the squirrels play. The king will give this grove, the Bamboo Grove, to the Buddha, and his followers, as a place to stay, the first of the great monasteries.

[long-pause]

And there is a mango grove, belonging to the king's doctor, Jivaka, the most famous physician of his time, who has studied medicine, for seven years, at the great city of Taxila, far away, in the north-west, and who looks after the king, and the Buddha, and his followers, and the poor, without payment.

[pause]

There is a story that, when Jivaka finished his studies, his teacher sent him out, into the forest, around Taxila, and told him to find a plant that had no use in medicine. And Jivaka searched, for a long time, and came back, and said: Teacher, I have not found a single plant that cannot be used as medicine. And the teacher said: Then you are ready.

[pause]

{img:physician-jivaka-mango-grove-preparing-herbal-medicine-mortar-patients}
Rajagriha is a busy, rich, noisy city, the capital of a growing kingdom, with traders, and soldiers, and officials, and craftsmen, coming, and going, all day long. But it is also a place where the wise, and the seekers, come, to the caves, and the groves, around the hills, and the king, Bimbisara, likes to visit them, and listen to them, and ask them questions.

[long-pause]

Today, the place is called Rajgir, a small town, in the state of Bihar, still surrounded by its five hills, with the remains of the old walls, on their slopes, and the hot springs, still flowing, and visitors come, from all over the world, to climb Vulture Peak, at sunrise, or sunset, and sit, quietly, where the Buddha sat, and look out, over the green valley, and the plains.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-indian-village-mud-houses-thatch-courtyard-cow-dung-cakes-drying}
Most of the people of India, in the time of the Buddha, live in villages.

[pause]

A village is a little world, of perhaps thirty, or fifty, or a hundred families, living close together, in a cluster of houses, among the fields, and the groves, with a pond, or a well, and a great tree, a banyan, or a pipal, in the middle, where the people meet, and talk, and the old men sit, in the shade, and the children play.

[long-pause]

The houses are made of mud, and wood, and bamboo, with thatched roofs, of straw, and grass, and palm leaves. They are built around little courtyards, swept clean, every morning, and smeared with fresh cow dung, mixed with water, which dries hard, and smooth, and keeps away the dust, and the insects. On the walls, the women paint patterns, in white, and red, for festivals, and good luck.

[pause]

The cattle are the wealth of the village. The cows give milk, and butter, and curds, and ghee, the clarified butter, which is used in cooking, and in offerings, to the gods. The oxen pull the ploughs, and the carts. And the dung is dried, in round flat cakes, stuck to the walls, and used as fuel, for the cooking fires. The cattle are loved, and cared for, and given names, and decorated, at the festivals.

[pause]

{img:farmer-ploughing-paddy-oxen-wooden-plough-iron-tip-water-reflections}
Around the village, there are the fields. The most important crop is rice, which grows in flooded fields, called paddies, in the rainy season. The farmers plough the wet fields, with their oxen, and the women plant the young rice seedlings, one by one, in long rows, in the water, and mud, bending over, all day long, and singing.

[long-pause]

In the dry season, they grow barley, and wheat, and millet, and lentils, and beans, and sesame, for oil, and mustard, and sugar cane, which they crush, to make a kind of brown sugar, and cotton, which the women spin, and weave.

[pause]

Each village has its craftsmen: a carpenter, who makes the ploughs, and the carts, and the houses, and a blacksmith, and a potter, at his wheel, and a weaver, and a barber, and a washerman, who washes the clothes, at the river, beating them on a flat stone. And the villagers pay them, not with money, but with a share of the harvest, every year.

[pause]

{img:village-headman-elders-under-banyan-tree-meeting-afternoon-shade}
The village has a headman, who settles quarrels, and collects the king's share of the harvest, a sixth part, usually, and the elders, who meet, under the great tree, and decide things, together.

[long-pause]

And the life of the village follows the seasons: the hot season, when the earth is dry, and cracked, and the air shimmers, and the people rest, in the shade, through the long afternoons; the rainy season, when the monsoon comes, and everything turns green, and the rice is planted; and the cool season, when the harvest is gathered, and the festivals, and the weddings, are held, and the nights are cold, and clear, and full of stars.

[long-pause]

{img:monsoon-clouds-gathering-over-indian-plain-dark-sky-village-waiting}
In India, the most important event of the year is the coming of the rain.

[pause]

From March, to June, it is the hot season. The sun burns down, from a white sky, day after day. The earth dries, and cracks. The rivers shrink. The ponds dry up. The leaves fall from the trees, and the grass turns brown, and the dust blows, in hot winds, across the plain. Everyone waits.

[long-pause]

And then, in June, the clouds begin to come, from the south-west, from the sea. At first, a few, high, white clouds. Then more, and more, building up, in great towers, dark, and heavy, and the air becomes thick, and still, and the birds fall silent.

[pause]

And then, one afternoon, the wind rises, and the sky turns dark, almost black, and there is a crack of thunder, and the rain comes.

[pause]

It does not rain, as it rains in other places. It pours, in great, warm, heavy sheets of water, roaring, on the thatched roofs, and the leaves, and the fields, so that, in a few minutes, the lanes are rivers, and the courtyards are ponds, and the whole world is water, and noise.

[long-pause]

And everyone comes out, into the rain, laughing, and the children dance, and shout, and splash, and the old people stand, in their doorways, and lift their faces, to the sky, and the peacocks spread their tails, and dance, and scream, with joy, and the frogs come out, by thousands, and sing, all night long.

[pause]

The smell of the first rain, on the dry earth, is one of the most wonderful smells in the world, rich, and sweet, and earthy, and the people of India love it.

[pause]

{img:green-rice-paddies-monsoon-sky-farmers-planting-seedlings-rows}
In a few days, the whole land turns green. The grass grows, the trees put out new leaves, the rivers rise, and the ponds fill, and the farmers go out, into the flooded fields, with their oxen, to plough, and plant the rice.

[long-pause]

The monsoon lasts for about four months, from June to September. It does not rain all the time. There are storms, and then sunshine, and then more storms. But the roads become mud, and the rivers flood, and travel is difficult, and dangerous.

[pause]

And so, in the time of the Buddha, the wandering seekers, and the monks, who travel, from place to place, all through the year, stop, for the rainy season, and stay in one place, for three months, so that they do not have to walk through the mud, and trample on the young rice, and the tiny creatures, which come out, everywhere, in the wet.

[pause]

{img:monk-shelter-grove-rain-falling-meditation-hut-thatch-quiet}
This is the rainy season retreat. And it is the beginning of the great monasteries of Buddhism: places, in groves, given by kings, and merchants, where the monks could stay, together, through the rains, and listen to the teaching, and practise, and live, in peace, while the rain fell, softly, on the roofs, and the leaves, all around them.

[long-pause]

{img:wild-elephants-herd-forest-river-ancient-india-dawn-mist}
In the forests of India, in the time of the Buddha, there are elephants.

[pause]

They live in great herds, in the forests, and the jungles, between the villages, and in the foothills of the Himalayas: the mothers, and the aunts, and the grandmothers, and the young ones, led by the oldest, and wisest, female, walking, slowly, through the trees, eating leaves, and grass, and bamboo, and fruit, and coming down to the rivers, in the evening, to drink, and bathe, and play.

[long-pause]

The people of India love, and respect, the elephants. They are strong, and wise, and gentle, and they never forget. In the old stories, the elephants are often noble, and kind, and the Buddha-to-be was born, several times, the stories say, as a great white elephant. And on the night before the Buddha was born, his mother, Queen Maya, dreamed that a white elephant, with six tusks, came down from the sky, and entered her side, a sign that her son would be great.

[pause]

The kings catch wild elephants, in the forests, and tame them, and train them, for work, and for war, and for ceremonies. The elephant trainers, called mahouts, spend their whole lives, with their elephants, from when they are young, and know them, and talk to them, and sing to them, and the elephants know them, and trust them.

[pause]

{img:mahout-washing-elephant-river-scrubbing-boy-laughing-evening}
The tame elephants work hard: carrying great logs, from the forests, and pulling carts, and lifting stones, for the walls, and carrying the kings, and the nobles, on their backs, in the processions, in great howdahs, decorated with gold, and silk, and jewels. And every evening, the mahouts take their elephants down to the river, and wash them, scrubbing their great grey backs, with stones, and coconut husks, while the elephants lie, happily, in the water, and spray themselves, with their trunks.

[long-pause]

There is a story, about the Buddha, and an elephant. At Rajagriha, there was a great, fierce elephant, called Nalagiri, who was drunk, and angry, and was let loose, in the street, where the Buddha was walking, on his alms round. And the people ran, and screamed, and hid, as the great elephant came charging, down the street, toward the Buddha.

[pause]

But the Buddha stood still, calmly, and looked at the elephant, with great kindness, and love, and spoke to him, gently. And the great elephant slowed down, and stopped, in front of him, and lowered his head, and the Buddha stroked his forehead, softly, with his hand. And the elephant knelt down, quietly, and was calm.

[pause]

{img:elephant-kneeling-before-monk-gentle-street-crowd-watching-rajgir}
The people, watching, from their windows, and their roofs, were amazed. And this story is carved, and painted, in temples, all over Asia: the great elephant, kneeling, quietly, before the calm, gentle figure, in the street.

[long-pause]

Today, there are still wild elephants, in the forests of India, in the foothills of the Himalayas, and in the south, and in the east, though far fewer than in the Buddha's time. And in the evenings, in some places, you can still see them, coming down to the rivers, to drink, and bathe, as the sun sets, as they have done, for thousands of years.

[long-pause]

{img:indian-woman-dawn-courtyard-grinding-grain-clay-hearth-fire-500bc}
Let us spend a day with a family, in a village, near the city of Varanasi, beside the Ganges.

[pause]

The father is a farmer, with a few fields of rice, and barley, and a pair of oxen, and two cows. The mother runs the house, and looks after the children, and the animals, and works in the fields, at the planting, and the harvest. There are three children, and the father's parents, and his younger brother, and his wife, all living together, in a house, of several rooms, around a courtyard.

[long-pause]

They wake before dawn, when the first birds begin to sing, and the sky grows pale, in the east. The father, and the grandfather, go down to the river, to bathe, and pour water, from their cupped hands, toward the rising sun, and say a prayer.

[pause]

The mother sweeps the courtyard, and smears the floor with fresh cow dung, and water, and draws a little pattern, at the door, with white rice flour, for good luck. Then she lights the fire, in the clay hearth, and milks the cows, and boils the milk, and makes the morning meal: rice, boiled, or a thin rice porridge, with milk, and a little sugar, from the sugar cane, or curds, and some fruit.

[pause]

{img:farmer-leading-oxen-to-fields-dawn-boy-following-mango-trees}
Then the father, and his brother, go out, to the fields, with the oxen, and the eldest son, who is ten, goes with them, to look after the cattle, and drive them, out to the grazing land, by the river, where he will sit, under a tree, all day, and watch them, and play his bamboo flute.

[long-pause]

The mother, and her sister-in-law, and the grandmother, work, at home: grinding grain, on a flat stone, with a round stone, rolling, back, and forth, and husking rice, in a wooden mortar, with a long pestle, pounding, and spinning cotton, on a little spindle, and fetching water, from the well, in clay pots, carried on their heads, and on their hips.

[pause]

The younger children play, in the courtyard, and the lane, with the other children of the village: with clay toys, and carts, and balls, and tops, and little bows, and arrows, and with the dogs, and the goats, and the calves, and they climb the mango trees, and swing on the roots of the banyan.

[pause]

{img:family-midday-meal-courtyard-leaf-plates-rice-lentils-sitting-floor}
At midday, the men come home, to eat. They sit on the floor, in the shade, on mats, and eat from leaf plates, or brass bowls, with their right hands: rice, and lentils, and vegetables, cooked with spices, ginger, and turmeric, and pepper, and cumin, and a little ghee, and curds, and pickles, and fruit. Then, in the heat of the afternoon, everyone rests.

[long-pause]

In the late afternoon, the men go back to the fields, and the women go to the river, to wash the clothes, and fetch water, and talk, with the other women, on the bank, while the children swim, and play, in the shallows.

[pause]

And in the evening, as the sun sets, the cattle come home, from the grazing land, raising a cloud of golden dust, along the lane, with their bells ringing, and the boy, walking behind them, with his flute. This time of day, in India, is called the cow-dust hour.

[pause]

{img:cow-dust-hour-cattle-returning-village-sunset-golden-dust-bells}
The family eats the evening meal, together, by the light of the fire, and a little clay lamp, burning mustard oil. And afterwards, they sit in the courtyard, under the stars, and the grandfather tells the children stories: stories of the gods, and the heroes, and the clever animals, the monkey, and the crocodile, and the jackal, and the lion, and the wise old elephant.

[long-pause]

And then they sleep, on string beds, called charpoys, in the courtyard, or on the roof, in the hot weather, or inside, in the rains, and the cool season, while the frogs sing, in the pond, and the jackals howl, far away, in the fields, and the night watchman, walking through the village, calls out, now and then, that all is well.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-indian-kitchen-clay-pots-spices-turmeric-ginger-rice-steaming}
What do the people of India eat, in the time of the Buddha?

[pause]

Above all, rice. Rice is the great food, of the plain of the Ganges: boiled, and steamed, and made into porridge, and flattened, and puffed, and ground into flour, for little cakes, and sweets. The finest rice, fragrant, and white, is called shali, and is eaten by the rich, and offered to the gods, and the monks.

[long-pause]

With the rice, there are lentils, and beans, and peas, of many kinds, cooked into soups, and stews, called dal, and vegetables: gourds, and pumpkins, and aubergines, and cucumbers, and radishes, and leafy greens, and lotus roots, and bamboo shoots. And there is milk, and curds, and butter, and ghee, from the cows, and the buffaloes.

[pause]

And there are spices. The food of India is already full of flavour: ginger, and turmeric, and black pepper, and long pepper, and cumin, and coriander, and mustard seed, and cardamom, and cloves, and cinnamon, and asafoetida, and sesame. There are no chillies yet. They will come, from America, two thousand years later. But the food is spicy, and warm, and fragrant, all the same.

[pause]

{img:fruit-market-ancient-india-mangoes-bananas-jackfruit-coconuts-baskets}
There is fruit, everywhere: mangoes, above all, the king of fruits, in many varieties, sweet, and golden, and juicy, in the hot season. And bananas, and jackfruit, and coconuts, and dates, and grapes, and pomegranates, and the little sour fruits, of the jujube tree, and the wood apple, and the rose apple.

[long-pause]

And there are sweets. The people of India love sweet things. They make a kind of sugar, from the juice of the sugar cane, boiled, until it becomes thick, and brown, and sticky, and then hard, like toffee. In fact, the word sugar, and the word candy, come, in the end, from the old languages of India. With it, and with honey, they make sweets, and cakes, of rice flour, and sesame, and milk, and ghee, and coconut.

[pause]

Meat, and fish, are eaten, by many people, though some, especially among the Brahmins, and the Jains, and later the Buddhists, eat little, or none, because they do not want to harm living creatures.

[pause]

{img:wedding-feast-ancient-india-guests-sitting-rows-banana-leaf-plates}
At a feast, or a wedding, the guests sit, in long rows, on the floor, or on low seats, and the food is served, on great banana leaves, or plates, made of leaves, stitched together, with little twigs, many dishes, one after another: rice, and dal, and vegetables, and curds, and pickles, and fried cakes, and sweets, and fruit, and milk, sweetened with sugar, and flavoured with cardamom.

[long-pause]

And everyone eats with their right hand, carefully, and neatly, mixing the rice, and the dal, with their fingers, into little balls. And after the meal, they wash their hands, and their mouths, and chew a little leaf, of the betel vine, wrapped around a piece of areca nut, and a little lime, and spices, which freshens the breath, and helps the digestion, and turns the mouth red, as people in India still do, today.

[long-pause]

{img:shravasti-ancient-city-street-merchants-shops-bullock-carts-elephant}
Let us go to the city of Shravasti, the great city of the kingdom of Kosala.

[pause]

It is one of the largest cities of India, in the time of the Buddha, with perhaps tens of thousands of people, surrounded by walls, of earth, and brick, with a moat, and gates, and watchtowers, beside the river Rapti.

[long-pause]

Inside, the streets are crowded, and noisy, and colourful. There are houses, of brick, and wood, two, and three storeys high, with balconies, and carved wooden doors, and windows, with lattices, and roofs, of tiles, and thatch. There are palaces, and gardens, and parks, with lotus pools. There are shops, opening onto the streets, selling everything: cloth, and spices, and perfumes, and jewellery, and pots, and pans, and grain, and sweets, and flowers.

[pause]

And the streets are full of people, and animals: bullock carts, creaking, and horses, and chariots, and elephants, swaying slowly through the crowds, with their riders, and porters, carrying loads, on their heads, and on poles, across their shoulders, and merchants, and monks, and soldiers, and beggars, and dancers, and acrobats, and snake charmers, and storytellers.

[pause]

{img:craftsmen-street-guild-ivory-carvers-goldsmiths-weavers-workshops}
The craftsmen of the city are organised, in guilds: the weavers, and the potters, and the carpenters, and the smiths, and the goldsmiths, and the ivory carvers, and the perfume makers, and the garland makers, each with its own street, or quarter, and its own leaders, and its own rules. The guilds are powerful, and rich, and the sons learn the craft of their fathers, from generation to generation.

[long-pause]

And there is money.

[pause]

In these years, in India, the first coins are being made. They are small, flat pieces of silver, of a fixed weight, cut, and stamped, with little symbols, punched into the metal, one by one: a sun, and a wheel, and an elephant, and a tree, and a mountain, and a bull. They are called punch-marked coins, and they are among the first coins in the world. And with them, it is much easier to buy, and sell, and travel, and trade.

[pause]

{img:punch-marked-silver-coins-ancient-india-symbols-sun-wheel-close}
The merchants of Shravasti are very rich. The richest of them all is a man called Sudatta, who is so generous, that he is called Anathapindika, which means, the one who feeds the poor. Every day, he gives food, to hundreds of poor people, at his house.

[long-pause]

One day, Anathapindika hears the Buddha teach, at Rajagriha, and is so moved, that he invites him, to come, and stay, at Shravasti, during the rainy season. And he looks for a place, for him, and his monks, to stay: a quiet grove, near the city, but not too near.

[pause]

He finds a beautiful park, belonging to a prince, Prince Jeta. But the prince does not want to sell it, and says, jokingly, that he will only sell it, for as many gold coins as it takes to cover the ground. And Anathapindika says yes, and brings cartloads of gold coins, and begins to cover the ground with them.

[pause]

{img:jetavana-grove-monastery-shravasti-trees-paths-monks-evening}
The prince is so amazed, that he gives the rest of the land for nothing, and gives the trees, too, as his own gift. And so, the Jetavana, the grove of Jeta, becomes the great monastery, where the Buddha will spend more rainy seasons than anywhere else, about twenty of them, and where he will give many of his teachings.

[long-pause]

Today, at Shravasti, in the state of Uttar Pradesh, you can still walk, among the ruins of the Jetavana, among the old brick foundations, of the monasteries, and the stupas, under the great trees, where pilgrims, from Sri Lanka, and Thailand, and Myanmar, and Japan, and all over the world, sit, quietly, in the shade, and chant, and meditate.

[long-pause]

{img:caravan-bullock-carts-forest-road-ancient-india-merchants-dust}
Between the cities of India, along the roads, and the rivers, the merchants travel, with their caravans.

[pause]

A great caravan may have hundreds of bullock carts, in a long line, creaking slowly along the dusty roads, loaded with goods: cloth, from Varanasi, which is famous for its fine cotton, and later, silk, and salt, and spices, and sugar, and iron, and copper, and pottery, and sandalwood, and perfumes, and precious stones, and horses, from the north-west.

[long-pause]

The caravans travel slowly, a few kilometres a day, from village to village, and town to town, stopping at night, in a circle, with the carts around them, and fires burning, to keep away the wild animals, and guards, watching, through the night.

[pause]

They cross forests, and deserts, and rivers, and mountains. There are dangers: wild elephants, and tigers, and snakes, and bandits, and floods, and droughts. In the old stories, the caravan leaders must be wise, and brave, and careful, and know where to find water, in the desert, and how to travel, at night, by the stars.

[pause]

{img:caravan-camp-night-circle-carts-fires-guards-stars-forest-edge}
There is an old story, told among the Buddhists, of a caravan, crossing a great desert, at night, by the stars, guided by a desert pilot. And on the last night, the pilot falls asleep, and the oxen turn round, and walk back, all night, the way they came, and in the morning, the caravan finds itself where it started, with no more water, and no more wood.

[long-pause]

But the leader is calm, and wise, and does not despair. He walks around, in the desert, until he finds a patch of grass, growing, and he thinks: there must be water, under here. And he orders the men to dig, and they dig, and dig, until they find a great rock, and they are about to give up. But the leader climbs down, and puts his ear to the rock, and hears the sound of water, flowing, underneath. And he gives a hammer, to a young servant, and tells him to strike the rock. And the boy strikes, and the rock breaks, and the water rises up, cool, and clear, and saves them all.

[pause]

The story says: do not give up, too soon.

[pause]

{img:river-trade-boats-ganges-cargo-boatmen-sails-sunset-merchants}
Many goods also travel by river. Along the Ganges, and its great tributaries, there are boats, large, and small, carrying goods, and passengers, up, and down, the river, from city to city, and down to the sea, at the mouth of the Ganges, where ships sail, to the south of India, and to Sri Lanka, and across the sea, to Burma, and beyond.

[long-pause]

And with the merchants, travel ideas, and stories, and news, and religions. The merchants are among the first, and most generous, followers of the Buddha, because his teaching, of peace, and honesty, and moderation, suits them, and because the monks, too, are travellers, on the same roads, and the merchants, and the monks, become friends, and companions, on the long, dusty roads of India.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-varanasi-riverbank-steps-temples-boats-dawn-500bc}
On the banks of the Ganges, where the river curves, in a great crescent, toward the north, there is the city of Varanasi, also called Kashi, the city of light.

[pause]

It is one of the oldest cities in the world, where people have lived, without stopping, for three thousand years, or more. And already, in the time of the Buddha, it is old, and famous, and holy.

[long-pause]

The city stands on the western bank, on a high ridge, above the river, so that it is safe from the floods, and every morning, the people of Varanasi go down to the river, along paths, and steps, to bathe, in the sacred water, and pray, facing the sun, as it rises, over the flat, sandy shore, on the other side.

[pause]

Varanasi is a city of learning. Brahmin teachers live here, with their pupils, and wise men, and wandering seekers, come here, to debate, and teach. It is no accident that the Buddha came here, to give his first teaching, in the deer park, at Sarnath, just outside the city, where seekers had always gathered.

[pause]

{img:weavers-varanasi-ancient-looms-fine-cotton-cloth-courtyard-sunlight}
And Varanasi is a city of weavers. Its fine cotton cloth, soft, and light, and white, or dyed in beautiful colours, is famous, all over India, and is sold, by the merchants, in every city. The stories of the Buddha's youth say that his clothes, as a prince, were made of the finest cloth of Kashi. And, two thousand years later, the weavers of Varanasi will become famous, all over the world, for their silk saris, woven with gold, and silver, thread.

[long-pause]

There are markets, full of cloth, and perfumes, and sandalwood, and ivory, and jewellery, and spices, and boats, along the river, coming, and going, with goods, from up, and down, the Ganges.

[pause]

And many of the old stories, the Jataka tales, begin with the words: Long ago, when Brahmadatta was king of Varanasi. So that, for the people of India, Varanasi is the city of once upon a time, the city of all the old stories.

[pause]

{img:evening-lamps-floating-ganges-varanasi-leaf-boats-flowers-dusk}
In the evening, at Varanasi, people light little lamps, of clay, filled with oil, with a wick, and set them, on leaves, with flowers, and float them, on the river, as an offering, so that the dark water is covered with hundreds of tiny, flickering lights, drifting, slowly, downstream, in the dusk.

[long-pause]

Today, Varanasi is a great, busy, crowded city, of more than a million people, with its famous ghats: great stone steps, leading down to the river, all along the bank, for kilometres, built in later centuries, with temples, and palaces, above them. And every evening, at sunset, on the ghats, there is a ceremony, called the Ganga aarti, with priests, swinging great lamps, of fire, and bells ringing, and chanting, and conch shells blowing, and thousands of people watching, from the steps, and the boats, on the river.

[pause]

And every morning, at dawn, as in the time of the Buddha, the people of Varanasi still go down to the river, to bathe, and pray, facing the rising sun, with the mist on the water, and the boats, gliding past, and the bells ringing, from the temples.

[long-pause]

{img:brahmin-priests-fire-altar-vedic-ritual-ghee-offering-chanting-dawn}
In the India of the Buddha's time, there are the Brahmins.

[pause]

The Brahmins are the priests, and the teachers, and the keepers of the sacred knowledge. Their knowledge is the Veda: a great collection of hymns, and prayers, and chants, and rituals, in the ancient language, Sanskrit, which are very old, already more than a thousand years old, some of them, and which have never been written down, but are learned, by heart, and passed on, from teacher to pupil, word for word, sound for sound.

[long-pause]

A young Brahmin boy goes to live with his teacher, at about the age of eight, and stays with him, for twelve years, or more, learning the Veda, by heart, listening, and repeating, again, and again, every syllable, every sound, every tone, perfectly, until he knows thousands of verses, from memory. And because of this careful method, the hymns of the Veda have been passed down, without changing, for more than three thousand years, and are still chanted, in the same way, today.

[pause]

The Brahmins perform the great sacrifices, for the kings, and the rich, and the small, daily rituals, for the families: at the sacred fire, which burns, in every Brahmin household, and into which they pour offerings, of ghee, and milk, and grain, and chant the ancient hymns, to the gods: to Agni, the god of fire, and Indra, the king of the gods, with his thunderbolt, and Surya, the sun, and Soma, and Varuna, and many others.

[pause]

{img:teacher-pupils-forest-hermitage-learning-veda-by-heart-sitting-circle}
Society, in the old teachings, is divided into four great orders, called varnas: the Brahmins, the priests, and the teachers; the Kshatriyas, the warriors, and the rulers; the Vaishyas, the farmers, and the merchants; and the Shudras, the servants, and the workers. And within these, there are many smaller groups, of families, and trades, and clans.

[long-pause]

{img:people-of-many-walks-ancient-india-market-crowd-merchants-farmers-priests}
In the time of the Buddha, these divisions are important, but not yet as strict as they will become, later. The Buddha himself is a Kshatriya, of the warrior order, and he will say, often, that a person is not noble, or low, by birth, but by their actions, and their heart. And his followers will come from every order, and every kind of family: Brahmins, and princes, and merchants, and farmers, and barbers, and sweepers.

[pause]

And in these years, too, some of the Brahmins themselves are asking new, deep questions, beyond the rituals. In the forests, and the hermitages, the teachers, and their pupils, sit together, and discuss the meaning of everything: the self, and the world, and what is real, and what lies behind all things, and what happens to us, when we die.

[pause]

{img:forest-sage-teaching-young-students-under-tree-quiet-afternoon-light}
Their teachings are collected, in the Upanishads, which means, sitting down near: sitting near the teacher, to listen. They speak of the deep, true self, within every person, and of the great reality, behind the whole world, and say, in a famous phrase: that thou art. You, yourself, are that.

[long-pause]

And they speak of a great cycle, of birth, and death, and rebirth, in which every living being is born, again, and again, in a new body, according to its deeds, its karma, in the life before, and of the hope, of finding release, one day, from this endless wheel, into a peace beyond all change.

[pause]

These ideas are in the air, in the India of the Buddha's time, discussed, and debated, by the wise, in the forests, and the courts, and the parks, of the cities, and the young prince, Siddhartha, will hear them, and think about them, and go his own way.

[long-pause]

{img:wandering-ascetics-shramanas-forest-path-begging-bowls-simple-robes}
In the time of the Buddha, the roads, and the forests, of India are full of wanderers.

[pause]

They are called shramanas, which means, those who strive. They are men, and some women, who have left their homes, and their families, and their possessions, and their work, to search for the truth, and for freedom from suffering.

[long-pause]

They live very simply. They wear only a simple robe, or a loincloth, or, some of them, nothing at all. They own almost nothing: a bowl, for begging, and perhaps a water pot, and a staff. They sleep under trees, or in caves, or in little huts, of leaves. And they live on what people give them: every morning, they walk, silently, into the villages, and stand, at the doors, and the people put a little food into their bowls.

[pause]

Some of them practise great hardships: fasting, for days, and weeks, and standing, on one leg, and sitting, in the burning sun, or the freezing cold, and holding their breath, for a long time, believing that, by mastering the body, they can free the spirit.

[pause]

{img:meditating-hermit-cave-mouth-hills-dawn-stillness-simple-life}
Others practise meditation: sitting, still, and silent, for hours, with their legs crossed, and their eyes closed, calming the mind, and looking deep within.

[long-pause]

There are many different teachers, and many different schools, with their followers. And they travel, from place to place, and meet, in the parks, outside the cities, and in special halls, built by the kings, and the merchants, for them, and they discuss, and debate, the great questions, before the crowds, who come, to listen, as if to a great show.

[pause]

One of the greatest teachers of the time is Vardhamana, called Mahavira, the great hero, a prince, like the Buddha, who has left his home, and become a wanderer, and taught a path of great gentleness, and great self-discipline. His followers, the Jains, do not harm any living thing, not even the smallest insect. Some of them sweep the path in front of them, with a soft brush, as they walk, so as not to step on anything alive, and wear a cloth over their mouths, so as not to breathe in tiny creatures. The Jain religion is still alive, in India, today.

[pause]

{img:jain-monks-walking-soft-brush-sweeping-path-white-robes-gentle}
The people of India respect the wanderers, and give them food, gladly, believing that it brings them good fortune, and that the seekers are doing something important, for everyone.

[long-pause]

And the kings, too, honour them. King Bimbisara, of Magadha, likes to visit the forest sages, and the wanderers, and sit with them, and ask them questions, about life, and death, and the right way to live, and to rule.

[pause]

So the young prince, Siddhartha, when he leaves his home, will not be alone. He will join a great company of seekers, walking the roads of India, under the hot sun, and the monsoon rain, with their bowls, and their questions, looking for the answer.

[long-pause]

{img:taxila-ancient-city-hills-north-west-teachers-students-courtyard}
Far away, in the north-west of India, in what is now Pakistan, beyond the river Indus, there is a famous city of learning: Taxila.

[pause]

It stands in a green valley, among the hills, on the great road, which runs, from the plain of the Ganges, to the north-west, through the mountains, to the lands of Persia, and beyond. And in the time of the Buddha, it is famous, all over India, for its teachers.

[long-pause]

Young men come, to Taxila, from all over India, from Rajagriha, and Varanasi, and Shravasti, and further, walking, for months, along the great road, to study with the famous teachers. They study the Vedas, and grammar, and law, and mathematics, and astronomy, and medicine, and archery, and the art of war, and the care of elephants, and many other things.

[pause]

There is no great university building, with halls, and libraries. Each teacher lives in his own house, with his pupils, who live with him, as part of his family, and work for him, in the house, and the fields, and the forest, and learn from him, by heart, sitting at his feet.

[pause]

{img:students-fetching-firewood-water-teacher-house-forest-dawn}
The pupils who are rich pay their teacher, at the beginning, with a gift of money. Those who are poor work for him, during the day, fetching water, and firewood, and caring for his cattle, and study, at night, by the light of the fire.

[long-pause]

The Buddha's friend, the physician Jivaka, studied medicine at Taxila, for seven years. And the stories say that some of the princes of the kingdoms of India studied there, too, and became friends, as students, and remembered each other, all their lives.

[pause]

Many years later, about a hundred, or two hundred, years after the Buddha, one of the greatest scholars of ancient India, Panini, from near Taxila, will write a grammar, of the Sanskrit language, so complete, and so clever, with nearly four thousand short rules, that scholars still study it, and admire it, today, as one of the greatest works of language, ever made.

[pause]

{img:ancient-scholar-writing-birch-bark-grammar-rules-lamp-night}
And when Alexander the Great comes, to India, about two hundred years after the Buddha, he will stop, at Taxila, and meet its wise men, and argue with them, and admire them.

[long-pause]

Today, the ruins of Taxila, in Pakistan, are a World Heritage Site, with the remains of three cities, built, one after another, over many centuries, and of great Buddhist monasteries, and stupas, built later, on the hills around them, where monks, from all over Asia, came, to study, and pray, and live, in peace, among the green hills.

[long-pause]

{img:lumbini-garden-sal-trees-blossom-queen-maya-spring-gentle-light}
The story of the Buddha's life was told, and retold, by his followers, for many centuries, before it was written down, and it has many wonderful, and miraculous, details, which grew, over the years. But the heart of the story is simple, and human.

[pause]

He was born, the stories say, in a garden, called Lumbini, in a grove of sal trees, in blossom, in the spring, at the foot of the Himalayas.

[long-pause]

His mother, Queen Maya, was travelling, from Kapilavastu, the town of the Shakyas, to her own family's home, to give birth, as was the custom. And on the way, she stopped, in the garden, at Lumbini, to rest, among the flowering trees. And there, standing, holding a branch of a sal tree, she gave birth to a son.

[pause]

His father, Shuddhodana, the chief of the Shakyas, was overjoyed. They named the baby Siddhartha, which means, the one who achieves his aim. His family name was Gautama.

[pause]

{img:infant-prince-wise-old-sage-asita-holding-baby-palace-courtyard}
The stories say that a wise old sage, called Asita, came down from the mountains, to see the baby, and held him, in his arms, and smiled, and then wept. And when the king asked him why, he said that he smiled, because the child would become a great teacher, who would show the world the way to freedom. And he wept, because he himself was old, and would not live to hear him.

[long-pause]

Sadly, a few days after the birth, Queen Maya died. And Siddhartha was brought up, lovingly, by her sister, Mahaprajapati, who became his second mother, and who, many years later, would become the first Buddhist nun.

[pause]

He grew up, in comfort, and luxury, in his father's house, at Kapilavastu. The stories say that his father had three palaces built for him, one for the hot season, one for the cool season, and one for the rains, and that he wore clothes, of the finest cloth, from Varanasi, and had music, and dancing, and gardens, with lotus pools, and everything he could wish for.

[pause]

{img:young-prince-siddhartha-sitting-under-rose-apple-tree-meditating-field-ploughing}
There is a lovely story, from his childhood. One day, his father took him, to the ploughing festival, at the beginning of the planting season, where the king, himself, ploughed the first furrow. And the little boy was left, sitting, in the shade, of a rose-apple tree, while everyone else watched the ploughing. And sitting there, quietly, watching the ploughs, and the oxen, and the birds, he fell, naturally, into a deep, calm, peaceful state of meditation, all by himself. And when the nurses came back, they found the shadow of the tree had not moved, to protect him.

[long-pause]

He learned everything a prince should learn: to ride, and to use a bow, and to read, and to govern. He was handsome, and clever, and kind, and everyone loved him. At sixteen, he married a beautiful cousin, called Yashodhara, and, years later, they had a son, called Rahula.

[pause]

And yet, the stories say, in the middle of all this comfort, and happiness, the young prince felt that something was missing.

[long-pause]

{img:prince-chariot-ride-city-streets-kapilavastu-charioteer-morning}
The old stories tell that, one day, the young prince Siddhartha went out, from the palace, in his chariot, with his charioteer, Channa, to see the town.

[pause]

And on the road, he saw an old man, bent, and grey, and shaking, leaning on a stick. And he asked Channa: What is wrong with this man? And Channa said: He is old, my lord. Everyone who lives grows old. And the prince was troubled.

[long-pause]

Another day, he saw a man who was ill, lying by the road, in pain. And Channa said: He is sick, my lord. Everyone can fall sick. And another day, he saw a funeral procession, carrying a body, to the river, with the family, weeping. And Channa said: He has died, my lord. Everyone who is born must die.

[pause]

And the prince went home, and could not forget what he had seen. All the comfort, and the music, and the gardens, of the palace could not take away the thought: everyone I love, and I myself, will grow old, and fall sick, and die. Is there no way beyond this?

[pause]

And then, one more day, he saw a fourth sight: a wandering monk, with a shaven head, and a simple robe, and a bowl, walking, calmly, peacefully, along the road, with a quiet, serene face. And the prince asked Channa: Who is this man? And Channa said: He is one who has left his home, to search for the truth.

[long-pause]

And the prince knew, then, what he must do.

[pause]

That night, the stories say, when he was twenty-nine years old, he went, quietly, to the room, where his wife, Yashodhara, was sleeping, with their baby son, Rahula, in her arms. And he stood in the doorway, and looked at them, for a long time, in the lamplight. He wanted to hold his son, one last time, but he was afraid of waking them. And so he turned, and went away.

[pause]

{img:white-horse-night-ride-moonlight-prince-charioteer-leaving-city-gate}
He woke Channa, and told him to saddle his white horse, Kanthaka. And they rode out, through the sleeping town, and through the gate, in the moonlight, and on, through the night, until they came to a river, at dawn.

[long-pause]

There, on the riverbank, the prince got down from his horse, and took off his jewels, and his fine clothes, and gave them to Channa, and cut off his long hair, with his sword. And he exchanged his princely clothes, with a poor hunter, who was passing, for a simple, rough robe.

[pause]

And he told Channa to take the horse, and go home, and tell his father, and his wife, that he had gone, not because he did not love them, but to find a way beyond suffering, for them, and for everyone. And that he would come back, when he had found it.

[pause]

{img:monk-alone-riverbank-dawn-shaven-head-simple-robe-path-ahead}
The stories say that Channa wept, and the faithful horse, Kanthaka, would not leave, and looked back, and back, at his master, as he was led away.

[long-pause]

And Siddhartha, the prince, now a wanderer, with nothing but his robe, and a bowl, turned, and walked away, alone, along the road, toward the south, toward the great kingdom of Magadha, where the famous teachers lived, in the forests, and the hills, to begin his search.

[pause]

It is called, by Buddhists, the Great Going Forth. And it is remembered, and painted, and carved, in temples, all over Asia, to this day: the prince, on his white horse, riding out, through the gate, in the moonlight, into the night.

[long-pause]

{img:young-wanderer-alms-round-rajagriha-street-people-watching-calm-dignity}
When Siddhartha left his home, and became a wanderer, he walked, south, for many days, until he came to Rajagriha, the capital of Magadha.

[pause]

And there, early one morning, he walked into the city, with his bowl, for the first time, on his alms round, as the wanderers did, going, quietly, from door to door, and standing, silently, waiting, for whatever the people would give.

[long-pause]

The stories say that the people of Rajagriha noticed him, at once. He was young, and tall, and handsome, and he walked, so calmly, and with such grace, and dignity, looking down, at the road, in front of him, that they thought he must be someone very special. And the news spread, through the streets, and reached the palace, and the ears of the king.

[pause]

King Bimbisara went up, onto the roof of his palace, and looked down, and saw the young wanderer, walking, through the streets, below, and he too was amazed, and he sent his servants, to follow him, and see where he went.

[pause]

{img:king-bimbisara-visiting-young-ascetic-rock-shelter-hill-retinue}
Siddhartha collected his food, and walked out of the city, to one of the five hills, around Rajagriha, and sat down, in the shade of a rock, and ate his meal, quietly. And the servants came back, and told the king. And the king got into his royal carriage, and went out, with his court, to the hill, and walked, up to the rock, where the young man was sitting.

[long-pause]

He greeted him, and sat down, beside him, and talked with him. And he was so impressed, by the young man's manner, and his words, that he said: You are young, and noble, and you seem to be born to rule. Stay here, in my kingdom. I will give you wealth, and land, and an army, to lead, and you can live a great, and happy, life.

[pause]

But Siddhartha thanked him, and said, gently, that he was the son of the chief of the Shakyas, and that he had left all of that, a palace, and wealth, and power, and everything he could want, behind him, freely, because he was looking for something else, something that no palace, and no kingdom, could give: a way beyond old age, and sickness, and death, and sorrow.

[pause]

{img:king-and-wanderer-parting-hillside-sunset-rajagriha-valley-below}
The king understood, and did not try to persuade him any more. But he made him promise one thing: that if he found what he was looking for, he would come back, to Rajagriha, and teach it to him, first.

[long-pause]

And Siddhartha promised. And he went on, his way, to the forests, and the teachers, and the long years of searching.

[pause]

And years later, after the night under the Bodhi tree, and the first teaching, in the deer park, the Buddha remembered his promise, and came back, to Rajagriha, with his monks. And the king, now older, came out, with his whole court, to meet him, and heard the teaching, and became his follower, and gave him the Bamboo Grove.

[long-pause]

They remained friends, the king, and the teacher, for many years. And in the evenings, the stories say, the king would sometimes go, quietly, out of the city, to the Vulture Peak, or the Bamboo Grove, to sit with the Buddha, and listen, and talk, in the cool of the evening, as the sun went down, over the hills, of Rajagriha.

[long-pause]

{img:ascetic-siddhartha-forest-five-companions-austerities-thin-meditating}
For six years, Siddhartha searched.

[pause]

First, he went to Rajagriha, and learned from two of the greatest teachers of meditation, of his time. He learned everything they could teach him, quickly, and reached the deepest states of calm, and stillness, that they knew. But he was not satisfied. These states were peaceful, but when he came out of them, the old questions, and the suffering, were still there.

[long-pause]

So he went on, alone, to a quiet place, near a village called Uruvela, beside a river, with a forest grove, and he joined five other seekers, and began to practise the hardest austerities of all.

[pause]

He fasted, eating only a few grains of rice, or a single bean, a day, until he became so thin, that his ribs stood out, like the beams of an old barn, and his eyes sank deep into his head, like the reflection of stars, in a deep well. He sat, in the heat, and the cold, and held his breath, and went without sleep. And the five companions admired him, and waited, for him to find the truth.

[pause]

But one day, nearly fainting, he realised that this, too, was not the way. Tormenting the body did not free the mind. It only weakened it. And he remembered the day, as a child, under the rose-apple tree, when he had sat, peacefully, and calmly, and felt a deep, natural happiness. Perhaps, he thought, that is the way.

[long-pause]

And so, he accepted a bowl of rice, cooked in milk, from a young woman of the village, called Sujata, who brought it to him, as an offering. And he ate it, and bathed in the river, and felt his strength come back.

[pause]

His five companions were disappointed, and thought that he had given up, and they left him, and went away, to Varanasi.

[pause]

{img:bodhi-tree-night-full-moon-meditator-seated-calm-river-niranjana}
And Siddhartha walked, to a great pipal tree, near the river, and sat down beneath it, on a seat of grass, given to him by a grass-cutter, facing east, and made a promise to himself: I will not get up from this place, until I have found the answer.

[long-pause]

It was the evening of a full moon, in the month of Vaishakha, in May.

[pause]

And through that night, the stories say, he sat, under the tree, in meditation, calmly, steadily, going deeper, and deeper. The stories tell of a great battle, that night, with Mara, the tempter, who tried to frighten him, and distract him, and make him give up, with storms, and armies, and beautiful daughters, and doubts. But Siddhartha did not move. He touched the earth, with his right hand, to call the earth itself as his witness. And the earth trembled, and Mara fled.

[pause]

{img:dawn-under-bodhi-tree-morning-star-peaceful-figure-golden-light}
And through the watches of the night, his mind became clear, and calm, and bright, like a still lake. He saw, the stories say, the whole chain of his past lives, and the lives of all beings, and how they are born, and die, and are reborn, according to their actions. And he saw, at last, clearly, the cause of suffering, and the way to end it.

[long-pause]

And as the morning star rose, in the east, at dawn, he became the Buddha: the one who is awake.

[pause]

He was thirty-five years old. The tree, under which he sat, is called the Bodhi tree, the tree of awakening. And the place, today, is called Bodh Gaya.

[long-pause]

{img:pipal-tree-heart-shaped-leaves-close-light-through-breeze-trembling}
The tree under which the Buddha sat, on the night of his awakening, was a pipal tree, a kind of fig tree, which grows, wild, all over India.

[pause]

It is a beautiful tree, tall, and wide, with a smooth, grey trunk, and great spreading branches, and leaves, shaped like hearts, with long, thin tips, like little tails, which tremble, and flutter, and rustle, in the slightest breeze, so that, even on a still day, the tree seems to be whispering.

[long-pause]

The pipal tree was already sacred, in India, before the Buddha. People worshipped it, and tied threads around its trunk, and left offerings, at its roots. And after the Buddha's awakening, the tree, at Bodh Gaya, became the most sacred tree in the world, for his followers.

[pause]

They came, from far away, to sit under it, and to honour it, with flowers, and lamps, and water. And in the early years, before there were any statues of the Buddha, the artists, who carved the stories of his life, did not show him, as a person, at all. Instead, they showed an empty seat, under a pipal tree, or a footprint, or a wheel, to show that he was there.

[pause]

{img:nun-sanghamitta-ship-sapling-golden-vase-sea-voyage-sri-lanka}
About two hundred and fifty years after the Buddha, the emperor Ashoka's daughter, Sanghamitta, who had become a nun, sailed, across the sea, to the island of Sri Lanka, carrying, the stories say, a branch, of the Bodhi tree, in a golden vase. And the king of Sri Lanka received it, wading out, into the sea, up to his neck, to meet the ship, and planted it, in his capital, Anuradhapura, with great ceremony.

[long-pause]

And that tree is still alive, today. It is called the Sri Maha Bodhi, and it is more than two thousand two hundred years old, the oldest tree, planted by people, with a known date, in the whole world. It stands, on a terrace, surrounded by railings, of gold, and propped up, with golden supports, under its old branches, and it is cared for, day, and night, by monks, and pilgrims come, from all over Sri Lanka, and the world, to see it, and sit beside it.

[pause]

The original tree, at Bodh Gaya, died, and was replaced, several times, over the centuries. And the tree that grows there today was planted, in the nineteenth century, from a cutting of the tree in Sri Lanka, so that the daughter has come home, to the place of the mother.

[pause]

{img:anuradhapura-sri-maha-bodhi-ancient-tree-golden-railings-pilgrims-white}
And from these trees, cuttings have been taken, and planted, at temples, and monasteries, all over the world, so that, today, there are Bodhi trees, descended from the tree at Bodh Gaya, growing, in Asia, and in Europe, and America, and Australia, and Africa.

[long-pause]

And in the evening, when the breeze comes up, the heart-shaped leaves of all these trees tremble, and whisper, together, softly, in the fading light, all around the world.

[long-pause]

{img:sarnath-deer-park-buddha-five-companions-first-teaching-trees-deer}
After his awakening, the stories say, the Buddha stayed under the Bodhi tree, and near it, for several weeks, in peace, and joy, and quiet.

[pause]

And he wondered whether he should try to teach what he had found. It was so deep, and so subtle, he thought, that perhaps nobody would understand it. But then, the stories say, he thought of all the people in the world, like lotus flowers, in a pond, some still deep under the water, some just reaching the surface, and some standing, above the water, ready to open, to the sun. And he decided that, for those who were ready, he would teach.

[long-pause]

He thought first of his five old companions, and he walked, more than two hundred kilometres, to find them, at a deer park, at a place called Isipatana, now called Sarnath, near the city of Varanasi, beside the Ganges.

[pause]

When they saw him coming, they said to each other: here comes Gautama, who gave up the struggle. Let us not stand up to greet him. But as he came closer, they saw something in his face, and his walk, so calm, and bright, and peaceful, that they stood up, without meaning to, and welcomed him, and gave him a seat, and water, to wash his feet.

[pause]

{img:spotted-deer-grazing-grove-sarnath-evening-light-peaceful}
And there, in the deer park, in the evening, with the deer grazing, nearby, under the trees, he gave his first teaching. It is called, by Buddhists, the turning of the wheel of the teaching.

[long-pause]

He spoke, first, of the middle way: that a person searching for the truth should avoid both extremes, the life of pleasure, and comfort, which he had known as a prince, and the life of hardship, and self-torment, which he had known as an ascetic, and follow, instead, a middle path, between them.

[pause]

And then, he spoke of four noble truths. That life contains suffering, dissatisfaction, which everyone feels. That suffering has a cause: craving, and clinging, wanting things to be other than they are. That suffering can end, when craving ends. And that there is a path, to its end: a way of living, with right understanding, and right intention, and right speech, and right action, and right livelihood, and right effort, and right mindfulness, and right concentration.

[pause]

{img:dharma-wheel-carved-stone-eight-spokes-sunlight-ancient}
Simple words. But the five companions listened, and understood, and one of them, Kondanna, first, and then the others, became his first followers, the first monks. And so, the community of the Buddha's followers, the Sangha, began.

[long-pause]

The wheel, with eight spokes, for the eight parts of the path, became the symbol of the teaching. Two hundred years later, a great emperor will put a pillar, at Sarnath, with four lions, on top, standing back to back, above a wheel. And today, those four lions are the national emblem of India, and the wheel is in the middle of the flag of India.

[pause]

And at Sarnath, today, there is a great, round, brick tower, a stupa, called the Dhamek stupa, rising out of the green park, among the ruins of old monasteries, where pilgrims walk, slowly, round, and round, and the deer still graze, in a park, nearby, under the trees.

[long-pause]

{img:bamboo-grove-veluvana-monks-sitting-shade-pond-squirrels-rajagriha}
After the first teaching, the Buddha travelled, and taught, and more, and more people came to listen, and many of them decided to follow him, and become monks.

[pause]

He went to Rajagriha, the capital of Magadha. And King Bimbisara, who had met Siddhartha, years before, when he first passed through the city, as a young wanderer, and had asked him to come back, if he found what he was looking for, came out, with his whole court, to greet him, and listened to his teaching, and became his follower, and friend.

[long-pause]

And the king gave him the Bamboo Grove, outside the city, a quiet, cool place, with a pond, as a place for the Buddha, and his monks, to stay. It was the first gift of land to the Sangha.

[pause]

Soon, there were hundreds of monks, and later, thousands. They came from every kind of family: the sons of Brahmins, and princes, and merchants, and farmers, and craftsmen, and servants. One of the most respected of all, Upali, had been a barber, at the court of the Shakyas. In the Sangha, the old divisions of birth did not matter. The order of seniority depended only on who had joined first.

[pause]

{img:monks-walking-in-line-alms-round-village-morning-bowls-silent}
The monks lived very simply. They shaved their heads, and wore simple robes, made of pieces of cloth, sewn together, like the patchwork of rice fields, dyed in the colours of earth, and bark, and saffron. They owned almost nothing: three robes, a bowl, a needle, a razor, a belt, and a water strainer, to strain out the tiny creatures, from their drinking water.

[long-pause]

They did not work, or handle money. Every morning, they walked into the villages, and the towns, in a quiet line, with their bowls, and stood, silently, at the doors, and the people put food in their bowls, and the monks ate it, before midday, and did not eat again, until the next morning.

[pause]

The rest of the day, they meditated, and studied, and listened to the teaching, and discussed it, and cared for each other, and walked, slowly, up and down, under the trees, in walking meditation.

[pause]

{img:monastery-grove-evening-monks-gathered-listening-teacher-lamps}
For most of the year, they wandered, from place to place, teaching, and sleeping under trees, and in caves, and groves. But in the rainy season, they stayed together, for three months, in one place, in the groves, and parks, given to them, by kings, and merchants, and ordinary people, which, over time, became the first monasteries, with huts, and halls, and walking paths, and wells.

[long-pause]

The Buddha himself lived the same simple life, as his monks, walking, all over the plain of the Ganges, for forty-five years, barefoot, with his bowl, teaching, every day, to kings, and farmers, and merchants, and Brahmins, and wanderers, and outcasts, and anyone who came to him, in simple language, with stories, and examples, from everyday life, that everyone could understand.

[pause]

And he had followers who did not become monks: men, and women, who lived at home, with their families, and their work, and supported the monks, and tried to live by the teaching: not to kill, not to steal, not to lie, not to harm others, and to be generous, and kind, and mindful.

[long-pause]

{img:young-monk-waking-dawn-hut-grove-folding-robe-birds}
Let us spend a day with a young monk, in a grove, near the city of Shravasti, during the rainy season.

[pause]

He wakes before dawn, in his little hut, of bamboo, and thatch, in the grove, with the rain dripping, softly, from the leaves, and the birds beginning to sing. He sits up, on his mat, and folds his legs, and closes his eyes, and meditates, for a while, in the dark, breathing, slowly, and calmly, and watching his breath, going in, and going out.

[long-pause]

As the light grows, he gets up, and sweeps the path, in front of his hut, and fetches water, from the well, and strains it, through his cloth, and washes his face, and his feet. Then, he puts on his outer robe, carefully, over one shoulder, and takes his bowl, and joins the other monks, walking, in a quiet line, along the muddy road, to the city, for the morning alms round.

[pause]

He walks slowly, mindfully, looking down, at the ground, a few steps ahead of him, not looking around, at the shops, and the people. He stops, at each door, silently, for a moment, and if someone comes out, and puts food in his bowl, he accepts it, with a nod, and walks on. If no one comes, he walks on, just the same. He does not ask, or choose. He takes whatever is given: rice, and curry, and lentils, and a piece of fruit, a sweet, or a little bread.

[pause]

{img:monk-eating-alms-food-quietly-under-tree-grove-before-noon}
Back at the grove, he sits, under a tree, and eats, slowly, and quietly, thinking of the people who gave the food, and of all the work that went into it, the farmers, and the cooks, and the rain, and the sun. Then he washes his bowl, and dries it, and puts it away.

[long-pause]

In the heat of the day, he sits, in meditation, again, in the shade, or walks, slowly, back, and forth, along a path, under the trees, for an hour, or more, paying attention to each step, lifting, moving, placing, the foot, on the earth.

[pause]

In the afternoon, he studies with his teacher, an older monk, learning the teachings, by heart, verse by verse, as there are no books, and asking questions, and listening. And he helps to mend his robe, with his needle, and thread, sitting, cross-legged, on the porch of his hut.

[pause]

{img:monks-gathered-hall-evening-buddha-teaching-oil-lamps-rain-outside}
And in the evening, as the light fades, and the rain falls, on the roof of the hall, all the monks gather, together, in the great hall, of the grove, and sit, in rows, in silence, and the Buddha, or one of the senior monks, gives a teaching, quietly, by the light of the oil lamps, and the monks listen, and ask questions, and then sit, together, in silence, for a long time, listening to the rain.

[long-pause]

Then, the young monk walks back, to his hut, through the dark, wet grove, with the frogs singing, and the fireflies, flickering, among the trees, and lies down, on his right side, on his mat, as the Buddha taught, with his mind calm, and clear, and goes to sleep, to the sound of the rain, on the leaves.

[long-pause]

{img:mahaprajapati-women-shaved-heads-robes-walking-to-vaishali-dusty-road}
At first, the Buddha's monks were only men. But women, too, wanted to follow the path.

[pause]

The stories tell that, some years after his awakening, the Buddha's aunt, and foster-mother, Mahaprajapati, who had brought him up, after his own mother died, came to him, and asked to be allowed to become a nun. And at first, he hesitated.

[long-pause]

But Mahaprajapati did not give up. She, and a group of other women, of the Shakya clan, many of them widows, or women whose husbands had become monks, cut off their hair, and put on simple robes, and walked, barefoot, a long way, to the city of Vaishali, where the Buddha was staying, and stood, at the gate, with their feet swollen, and dusty, and their faces streaked with tears.

[pause]

The Buddha's faithful attendant, and cousin, Ananda, saw them, and went to the Buddha, and asked him: Lord, can a woman, too, reach awakening, if she follows the path? And the Buddha said: Yes, Ananda, she can. And Ananda said: Then, Lord, let them be ordained. And the Buddha agreed.

[pause]

{img:buddhist-nuns-grove-meditating-together-ancient-india-peaceful-light}
And so, Mahaprajapati became the first Buddhist nun, and the order of nuns, the bhikkhunis, began, one of the first communities of women, devoted to a spiritual life, in the history of the world.

[long-pause]

Thousands of women joined it: princesses, and queens, and merchants' daughters, and farmers' wives, and servants, and courtesans, and widows, and mothers, who had lost their children.

[pause]

And some of them wrote poems, or spoke them, which were remembered, and collected, in a book, called the Therigatha, the verses of the elder nuns. It is one of the oldest collections of poems by women, anywhere in the world.

[pause]

{img:elder-nun-sitting-forest-hut-evening-light-peaceful-face}
In these poems, the nuns speak, simply, and honestly, of their lives: of their hardships, and their sorrows, and their freedom. One nun, Mutta, wrote: So free am I, so gloriously free, free from three crooked things: from the grinding stone, from the mortar, and from my crooked husband.

[long-pause]

And another, an old nun, called Ambapali, who had been a famous, and beautiful, courtesan, in the city of Vaishali, and had given her mango grove, to the Buddha, and his monks, wrote a poem, about growing old: how her hair, once black, and soft, and scented, is now like bark, and her eyes, once bright, like jewels, are now dim, and her voice, once sweet, like a cuckoo's, now falters. And at the end of each verse, she says, gently: the words of the truthful one are not false. All things change.

[pause]

Many other women supported the Buddha, too, as lay followers. One of the greatest was Visakha, a rich, and generous, and wise woman, of Shravasti, who built a great monastery, for the monks, the Eastern Park, and gave food, and robes, and medicine, and was a friend, and adviser, to the Buddha, and the whole community, for many years.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-indian-wedding-procession-musicians-drums-flowers-torches-evening}
Life in ancient India is full of festivals, and celebrations.

[pause]

There are the festivals of the seasons: at the beginning of the ploughing, when the king himself ploughs the first furrow, with a golden plough, and at the harvest, and at the coming of the rains, and in the spring, when the trees blossom, and the young people go out, into the parks, and the gardens, to play, and sing, and swing on swings, hung from the branches.

[long-pause]

There are the festivals of the stars, and the moon, when the whole city celebrates, for days, with music, and dancing, and feasting, and people wear their best clothes, and garlands of flowers, and visit each other, and the streets are decorated, with flags, and banners, and the houses, with lamps.

[pause]

In the old stories, there is a festival, in the city of Shravasti, called the festival of the stars, and the people are so busy, celebrating, for a whole week, that a poor man, who wanted to take part, but had nothing, worked, for days, to earn enough money, to buy a garland, of flowers, so that he, too, could join in.

[pause]

{img:village-women-singing-dancing-circle-festival-courtyard-lamps-colours}
And there are the great family celebrations: the birth of a child, and the naming ceremony, and the first time the baby eats rice, and the first haircut, and the beginning of school, and, above all, the wedding.

[long-pause]

A wedding, in ancient India, is a great event, which lasts for several days. The families have arranged it, carefully, often consulting the astrologers, to find the right day, and hour. The bride is bathed, and dressed, in fine clothes, of red, and gold, and adorned, with jewellery, of gold, and silver, and pearls, and flowers, in her hair, and the women paint patterns on her hands, and feet.

[pause]

The groom comes, in a procession, with his family, and friends, on horses, and elephants, and in carts, with music, of drums, and flutes, and conch shells, and torches, and dancers, through the streets, to the bride's house. And there, before the sacred fire, the bride, and groom, walk around the fire, together, seven times, holding hands, while the priest chants the ancient verses, and the families throw rice, and flowers, over them.

[pause]

{img:bride-groom-walking-around-sacred-fire-priest-chanting-families-flowers}
And then there is a great feast, for all the guests, and music, and dancing, late into the night, and the next day, the bride goes, with her husband, to her new home, in his family's house, in a covered cart, decorated with flowers, and the women of the village sing songs, of farewell, and some of them weep, a little.

[long-pause]

Many of these customs, the seven steps around the fire, the red, and gold, the painted hands, the processions, the music, and the flowers, are still part of Indian weddings, today, more than two and a half thousand years later.

[pause]

And on festival nights, in the towns, and the villages, of the Ganges plain, the little clay lamps are lit, in their hundreds, along the walls, and the roofs, and the doorways, and the river banks, and the whole land glows, with tiny, flickering flames, under the stars.

[long-pause]

{img:monkey-king-bridge-body-river-monkeys-crossing-mango-tree-moonlight}
The Buddha, and his followers, loved stories, and told many of them, to explain the teaching, in simple ways. Some of the most beloved are the Jataka tales: stories of the Buddha's earlier lives, before he was born as Siddhartha, when, the stories say, he was born, again, and again, as a man, or an animal, and each time, practised kindness, and wisdom, and courage.

[pause]

Let us listen to two of them.

[long-pause]

Long ago, the first story says, the Buddha-to-be was born as a monkey, the king of a great troop of eighty thousand monkeys, who lived in a great mango tree, beside the river Ganges, in the Himalayas, and ate its wonderful, sweet mangoes.

[pause]

One day, a mango fell into the river, and floated, all the way down, to Varanasi, where the king of Varanasi was bathing, and he tasted it, and it was the most delicious fruit he had ever eaten. And he went, with his soldiers, up the river, to find the tree. And when he found it, full of monkeys, he ordered his archers to surround it, so that, in the morning, they could take all the mangoes.

[pause]

{img:monkey-king-stretched-between-trees-troop-escaping-across-river-dawn}
The monkey king saw the danger. And he climbed, to the top of the tree, and leapt, a great leap, across the river, to a tree, on the other side, and tied a long bamboo cane to his foot, and leapt back. But the cane was a little too short. So he held onto a branch, of the mango tree, with his hands, and stretched his body, across the gap, so that it became a bridge. And all eighty thousand monkeys ran, across his back, one by one, to safety, on the other side.

[long-pause]

The king of Varanasi watched, from below, in the moonlight, and was amazed. In the morning, he had the tired monkey king gently brought down, and laid on a soft bed, and cared for, and asked him: Why did you do this, for them? And the monkey king said: I am their king, and they trusted me. A king must care for his people, more than for himself.

[pause]

And the king of Varanasi never forgot it, and ruled his own people, from that day, with kindness, and care.

[pause]

{img:full-moon-hare-silhouette-on-moon-forest-night-gentle}
The second story is about a hare.

[long-pause]

Long ago, the Buddha-to-be was born as a hare, who lived in a forest, with three friends: a monkey, and a jackal, and an otter. And on a holy day, they decided that they would each give a gift of food, to any traveller who came by.

[pause]

The otter found some fish. The jackal found some meat, and a pot of curds. The monkey picked some mangoes. But the hare only ate grass, and had nothing to give. And he thought: if a traveller comes, I will give him myself.

[pause]

And the god Sakka, the king of the gods, heard this, and wanted to test him. So he came to the forest, disguised as a poor, old, hungry Brahmin. And the hare said: Light a fire, good Brahmin, and I will give you my body, to eat. And the Brahmin lit a fire, and the hare jumped into it, joyfully.

[pause]

{img:hare-sitting-moonlit-grass-friends-otter-monkey-jackal-forest}
But the fire was cool, like snow, and did not burn him. And the old Brahmin turned back into the god, Sakka, who said: Little hare, your kindness will be remembered, forever. And he took a mountain, and squeezed it, and with its juice, he drew the picture of the hare, on the face of the moon, so that everyone, in the world, would see it, and remember.

[long-pause]

And that is why, in India, and in many countries of Asia, when people look up at the full moon, they do not see a man in the moon. They see a hare.

[pause]

Tonight, if the moon is up, you can look for him, too: the little hare, sitting, in the moon, with his long ears, shining, softly, over the forests, and the rivers, of India.

[long-pause]

{img:old-buddha-walking-slowly-ananda-road-sal-forest-evening-light}
For forty-five years, the Buddha walked, and taught, across the plain of the Ganges.

[pause]

And when he was eighty years old, an old man now, tired, and frail, he set out, on one last journey, from Rajagriha, to the north, with his faithful attendant, Ananda, and a group of monks.

[long-pause]

They walked slowly, from village to village, crossing the Ganges, by boat, and stopping, at Vaishali, where he stayed, in the mango grove, of Ambapali, and spent the last rainy season. And there, he fell ill. But he recovered, and went on.

[pause]

He stood, on a hill, outside Vaishali, and looked back at the city, for a long time, and said to Ananda: How lovely is Vaishali, Ananda. This is the last time that the Buddha will look upon it.

[pause]

At last, they came to a little town, called Kushinagar, in the country of the Mallas, and to a grove of sal trees, outside the town. And there, the Buddha lay down, between two great sal trees, on his right side, with his head to the north, resting on his hand, calm, and peaceful.

[long-pause]

The stories say that, although it was not the season, the sal trees burst into blossom, and the flowers fell, softly, on him, like rain.

[pause]

Ananda was very sad, and went away, and leaned against a doorpost, and wept. And the Buddha called for him, and said, gently: Do not grieve, Ananda. Have I not told you, that everything we love must change, and pass away? You have looked after me, so well, and so kindly, for so many years. Keep going, and you, too, will soon be free.

[pause]

{img:monks-gathered-quietly-sal-grove-night-lamps-reverent-stillness}
He asked the monks, three times, if any of them had any doubts, or questions, that he could answer, while there was still time. And they were all silent.

[long-pause]

And then, he spoke his last words: All things that are made will pass away. Strive on, with care.

[pause]

And then, quietly, peacefully, in the night, under the sal trees, he passed away, into the final peace, which Buddhists call parinirvana.

[pause]

We will leave him there, gently, under the blossoming trees, in the quiet grove, at Kushinagar, with the monks sitting, in silence, around him, and the lamps burning, and the night, soft, and still.

[pause]

{img:parinirvana-stupa-kushinagar-today-white-dome-pilgrims-flowers}
Today, at Kushinagar, there is a white stupa, and a temple, with a great statue, of the Buddha, lying, on his right side, peacefully, covered with a golden cloth, which pilgrims bring, and lay over him, and the sal trees still grow, in the park, around it, and blossom, every spring.

[long-pause]

{img:ashoka-pillar-polished-stone-lion-capital-ancient-india-road-sunlight}
After the Buddha's death, his followers met, the stories say, at Rajagriha, in a great council, of five hundred monks, and recited, together, all his teachings, so that they would be remembered, exactly, and passed on.

[pause]

For hundreds of years, the teachings were passed on, by heart, from teacher, to pupil, as the Vedas had been, until, at last, about four hundred years later, in Sri Lanka, they were written down, on palm leaves.

[long-pause]

The kingdom of Magadha grew, and grew, and about two hundred years after the Buddha, it became the centre of the first great empire of India, the Maurya Empire, stretching across almost the whole of the subcontinent. And its greatest emperor was Ashoka.

[pause]

Ashoka was a fierce conqueror, at first. But after a terrible war, in the land of Kalinga, he was filled with sorrow, at the suffering he had caused, and turned to the teaching of the Buddha, and decided to rule, from then on, by kindness, and justice, and peace.

[pause]

{img:ashoka-edict-carved-rock-brahmi-script-inscription-sunlit-surface}
He had his messages, his edicts, carved on rocks, and on great, polished stone pillars, all over his empire, for everyone to read: messages about kindness to animals, and respect for all religions, and care for the poor, and the sick, and the old, and honesty, and generosity. He built hospitals, for people, and for animals, and planted trees, along the roads, for shade, and dug wells, and built rest houses, for travellers.

[long-pause]

And he visited the places of the Buddha's life, as a pilgrim. At Lumbini, he set up a pillar, with an inscription, saying: Here, the Buddha was born. And that pillar is still there, today.

[pause]

He sent messengers, and monks, to other lands, to share the teaching: to Sri Lanka, and to the south of India, and to the lands of the Greeks, in the west, and, the stories say, to Burma, and beyond. And from there, over the centuries, the teaching of the Buddha spread, peacefully, along the trade routes, and the sea routes, to China, and Korea, and Japan, and Tibet, and Thailand, and Cambodia, and Vietnam, and much of Asia.

[pause]

{img:monks-travelling-silk-road-caravan-mountains-scrolls-china-bound}
In India itself, over many centuries, Buddhism slowly faded, and the great monasteries, and universities, like Nalanda, near Rajagriha, which had thousands of students, from all over Asia, became ruins, and the places of the Buddha's life were forgotten, and lost, in the jungles, and the fields.

[long-pause]

But in the nineteenth century, a British scholar, James Prinsep, worked out how to read the old script of Ashoka's inscriptions, in 1837, and an archaeologist, Alexander Cunningham, using the travel diaries of Chinese pilgrims, who had visited India, more than a thousand years before, found the places again, one by one: Bodh Gaya, and Sarnath, and Kushinagar, and Shravasti. And in 1896, in the jungle, near the border of Nepal, the pillar of Ashoka, at Lumbini, was found, with its inscription: Here, the Buddha was born.

[long-pause]

{img:mahabodhi-temple-bodh-gaya-today-tower-pilgrims-prayer-flags-dawn}
Today, more than two and a half thousand years later, the places of the Buddha's life are visited by millions of people, every year, from all over the world.

[pause]

At Bodh Gaya, in the state of Bihar, there is a great temple, the Mahabodhi Temple, with a tall, pointed tower, of brick, carved, all over, with little niches, and figures, rising high, above the town. It is a World Heritage Site. And beside it, behind the temple, there is a great pipal tree, with its wide, spreading branches, and its heart-shaped leaves, trembling, in the breeze: the Bodhi tree.

[long-pause]

It is not the same tree, under which the Buddha sat. But it is its descendant, grown from a cutting, of a cutting, of the original tree, more than two thousand years old, which grows, still, at Anuradhapura, in Sri Lanka, brought there, the stories say, by Ashoka's daughter, the nun Sanghamitta.

[pause]

Under the Bodhi tree, at Bodh Gaya, all day long, and into the night, pilgrims sit, in silence, or chant, softly, in many languages: monks, in saffron robes, from Thailand, and Sri Lanka, and Myanmar, and in maroon robes, from Tibet, and Ladakh, and in grey robes, from Korea, and in black robes, from Japan, and ordinary people, from India, and all over the world. They light little butter lamps, and lay flowers, and prostrate themselves, on the stone, and walk, slowly, round, and round, the temple, in the evening light.

[pause]

{img:bodhi-tree-leaves-butter-lamps-evening-pilgrims-meditating-bodh-gaya}
At Lumbini, in Nepal, the pillar of Ashoka still stands, and there is a temple, over the place of the birth, and a pool, and a sacred garden, and monasteries, built by many countries. At Sarnath, the Dhamek stupa rises, out of the green park, and the deer still graze, nearby. At Kushinagar, the Buddha lies, on his right side, in his temple, under his golden cloth.

[long-pause]

At Rajgir, the visitors climb Vulture Peak, at sunset, and the hot springs still flow. At Shravasti, the pilgrims sit, under the trees, of the Jetavana, among the ruins. At Vaishali, a pillar of Ashoka still stands, with a single lion, on the top, looking north, toward Kushinagar.

[pause]

And the plain of the Ganges is still there, green, and wide, and full of life, with its villages, and its rice fields, and its mango groves, and its cattle, coming home, at the cow-dust hour, and its river, flowing, slowly, toward the sea, past the ghats, of Varanasi, where people still come, at dawn, to bathe, and pray, facing the rising sun.

[pause]

{img:varanasi-ghats-dawn-ganges-boats-bathers-lamps-mist-today}
So we leave the India of the Buddha, tonight, as the evening falls.

[long-pause]

The sun is setting, red, and golden, over the plain, and the river. The cattle are coming home, along the lanes, in a cloud of golden dust, with their bells ringing. The smoke of the evening fires is rising, from the villages, and the little clay lamps are being lit, in the doorways. In a quiet grove, outside the city, the monks are gathering, in the hall, and sitting, in silence, as the first stars come out, and the frogs begin to sing. And under a great pipal tree, beside a slow river, the heart-shaped leaves are trembling, softly, in the evening breeze, as the full moon rises, with a little hare, on its face, over the quiet land.
