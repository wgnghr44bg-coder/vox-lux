{img:ganges-plain-500bc-river-dawn-mist-boats-village-palms-rice-fields}
Tonight we go about two and a half thousand years back. We go to the great plain of the river Ganges, in the north of India, around the year 500 before Christ.

[long-pause]

It is early morning. The sun rises red and huge through the mist over a wide slow river. Its light spreads across the water in a long golden path. On the banks there are villages among mango trees and palms and green rice fields. Smoke from the morning fires rises into the still air. A boat crosses the river slowly. The boatman pushes it along with a long pole. And on the steps down to the water, people are bathing and praying, facing the rising sun.

[pause]

Somewhere a bird is calling. Its clear rising call comes again and again from the trees.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

This is India in the time of the Buddha.

[pause]

{img:indian-village-500bc-thatched-huts-banyan-tree-cattle-morning-women-water}
It is a time of great change. All across the plain, forests are being cleared and new fields planted. Villages are growing into towns. Towns are growing into cities with walls and gates and markets and palaces. New kingdoms are rising and old ones are falling. Traders travel from city to city. And the first coins are being made.

[pause]

And it is a time of great questions. Many people are asking them. Kings and traders and farmers. What is the meaning of life? Why do we suffer? What happens after death? How can we find peace? Some of them leave their homes and families. They go out into the forests to look for the answers.

[long-pause]

One of them is a young prince from a small country at the foot of the Himalaya mountains. His name is Siddhartha Gautama. He will search for many years. Many times he will be close to giving up. And then one night, sitting under a great tree by a river, he will find an answer. He will become known as the Buddha. It means the one who has woken up.

[pause]

Tonight we will travel through the India of his time. We will walk through villages and rice fields and great cities. We will sit with farmers and traders and kings and forest wise men. We will watch the great rains come and spend the rainy season in a quiet grove. And we will follow the Buddha gently, from his birth in a garden, to his last journey.

[pause]

{img:bodhi-tree-pipal-leaves-heart-shaped-sunlight-quiet-grove}
We will not try to teach or persuade. We will simply look and listen quietly at a world long ago, and the people who lived in it.

[long-pause]

So let us walk down to the river in the early morning. There is mist on the water and a bird singing. Cattle bells ring. It smells of wood smoke and wet earth. And let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:ganges-river-wide-sandbanks-boats-sails-afternoon-light-kites-flying}
The river Ganges begins far to the north, high in the Himalaya mountains. It starts from an ice cave at the foot of a great glacier. It rushes down through gorges and valleys and comes out onto the plains. Then it flows east for more than two thousand kilometres, across the whole of northern India, to the sea.

[pause]

Along the way, many other great rivers join it from the mountains. So it gets wider and wider and slower and slower. In some places you can hardly see the other side.

[long-pause]

For the people of India, the Ganges is not just a river. It is a goddess. She is holy and pure. She came down from heaven to bless the earth. To bathe in her water is to be made clean.

[pause]

The Ganges plain is one of the richest lands in the world. Every year in the rainy season the rivers flood. They spread rich soft mud over the land. The soil is deep and dark and good. Two harvests can grow every year, sometimes three.

[pause]

{img:ancient-forest-clearing-iron-axes-farmers-new-fields-ganges-plain}
In earlier times, thick forest covered much of this plain. It was full of wild animals. Elephants and tigers and rhinos and deer and wild buffalo. But now, in the time of the Buddha, the forests are being cleared with iron axes. The land is being ploughed with iron ploughs pulled by oxen. And new villages are being started everywhere.

[long-pause]

Iron has changed everything. It makes better axes and ploughs and tools. Farmers can clear more land and grow more food. So there are more people and more villages and more towns.

[pause]

Still, the forests are never far away. Between the villages and fields there are groves of mango and banyan trees. There are woods and jungles. Wild elephants wander there. Tigers hunt. Monkeys chatter in the trees. Peacocks call at dusk. And hermits and seekers live there in little huts made of leaves.

[pause]

{img:peacock-dancing-feathers-spread-grove-monsoon-clouds-village-edge}
And in the north, on clear days after the rain, you can see them far away on the horizon. The Himalayas. White and shining high above the green plain, like a wall of clouds. The highest mountains in the world.

[long-pause]

This great plain of rivers and fields and forests and villages and cities is the Buddha's world. He lived and walked and taught here all his long life. From the foot of the mountains in the north, to the banks of the Ganges in the south.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-indian-city-wooden-palisade-gate-watchtowers-moat-500bc}
In the time of the Buddha, northern India is divided into many countries. The old books say there are sixteen great ones, and many smaller ones.

[pause]

Some are kingdoms, ruled by kings in their palaces, with armies and ministers. Others are republics. There, the heads of the great families meet in a hall. They talk and argue and vote. And they choose a leader for a time.

[long-pause]

The two greatest kingdoms are Magadha and Kosala.

[pause]

Magadha lies in the east, south of the Ganges. It is rich, with good land and forests full of elephants and timber. It has iron mines. Its king in the time of the Buddha is called Bimbisara. His capital is Rajagriha, a city ringed by five hills.

[pause]

{img:kosala-city-shravasti-palace-gardens-river-rapti-ancient-india}
Kosala lies to the north-west, along the rivers that come down from the mountains. Its king is called Prasenajit. Its great city is Shravasti, a rich trading city on a river.

[long-pause]

And there are republics. In one of them, the great families meet in a hall in the city. They are famous for being fair and getting along. There are also smaller republics near the foot of the mountains. One of them belongs to a proud old clan called the Shakyas.

[pause]

The Shakyas rule a small country around the town of Kapilavastu. It lies at the foot of the mountains, where India and Nepal meet today. Their chief in our story is called Shuddhodana. And it is into his family that the Buddha will be born.

[pause]

{img:assembly-hall-republic-elders-seated-debate-vaishali-pillars}
The kingdoms and republics are rivals. They make deals and marriages between their royal families. And sometimes they quarrel and fight. Slowly, over the years, Magadha will grow stronger and stronger. It will swallow its neighbours one by one. Two hundred years later it will be the heart of the first great empire of India. The small countries can feel it coming.

[long-pause]

But in the time of the Buddha, the kings and the republics still live side by side. The roads between them are open to traders and pilgrims and wanderers. And the Buddha will travel freely from one country to another for forty-five years. Kings and ordinary people will welcome him wherever he goes.

[long-pause]

{img:rajagriha-valley-five-hills-stone-walls-city-ancient-india-morning}
Let us visit Rajagriha, the capital of Magadha.

[pause]

It lies in a valley with five rocky hills all around it, covered with forest. The hills are like the walls of a great fortress. And along the tops of the hills there are real walls too. They are made of great rough stones piled up for kilometres around the whole valley. Gates stand in the gaps between the hills. Parts of these walls are still there today among the trees. They are among the oldest stone walls in India.

[long-pause]

Inside is the city. There is the king's palace. There are houses of nobles and traders and craftsmen. They are built of wood and brick and clay, with straw or tile roofs. They have balconies and courtyards and gardens. There are markets and streets full of people and ox carts and elephants and horses.

[pause]

Around the valley, among the hills, there are hot springs. People come to bathe in the warm water. There are caves in the hills where hermits live and sit in silence. And on one hill there is a rocky peak shaped like a vulture. Later the Buddha will often stay there and teach. From the top he can look out over the valley and the plains.

[pause]

{img:vulture-peak-rajgir-rocky-summit-sunset-plains-below-quiet}
Outside the city walls there is a beautiful bamboo grove. It is cool and green and rustles in the wind. There is a pond where squirrels play. The king will give this grove to the Buddha and his followers as a place to stay. It will be the first of the great monasteries.

[long-pause]

There is also a mango grove. It belongs to the king's doctor, Jivaka. He is the most famous doctor of his time. He studied medicine for seven years in a great city far to the north-west. He looks after the king and the Buddha and his followers. And he looks after the poor for free.

[pause]

There is a story about his last test at school. His teacher sent him into the forest. Find a plant that has no use as medicine, the teacher said. Jivaka searched for a long time. Days went by. Then he came back. Teacher, he said, I have not found a single plant that cannot be used as medicine. And the teacher smiled. Then you are ready, he said.

[pause]

{img:physician-jivaka-mango-grove-preparing-herbal-medicine-mortar-patients}
Rajagriha is a busy rich noisy city. Traders and soldiers and officials come and go all day long. But it is also a place where wise men and seekers come, to the caves and groves around the hills. King Bimbisara likes to visit them and listen to them and ask them questions.

[long-pause]

Today the place is a small town in the north-east of India. It is still ringed by its five hills. The remains of the old walls are still on their slopes. The hot springs still flow. And visitors come from all over the world to climb the vulture peak at sunrise or sunset. They sit quietly where the Buddha sat and look out over the green valley and the plains.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-indian-village-mud-houses-thatch-courtyard-cow-dung-cakes-drying}
Most people in India in the time of the Buddha live in villages.

[pause]

A village is a little world. Perhaps thirty or fifty or a hundred families live close together among the fields and groves. There is a pond or a well. And in the middle stands a great tree. People meet and talk under it. Old men sit in its shade. And children play around it.

[long-pause]

The houses are made of mud and wood and bamboo. The roofs are straw and grass and palm leaves. Each house has a little courtyard. It is swept clean every morning and smeared with fresh cow dung mixed with water. It dries hard and smooth and keeps away the dust and insects. For festivals and good luck, the women paint patterns on the walls in white and red.

[pause]

The cattle are the wealth of the village. The cows give milk and butter and yogurt. The oxen pull the ploughs and the carts. The dung is dried in flat round cakes stuck to the walls. It is burned as fuel for cooking. The cattle are loved and cared for and given names. At festivals they are decorated.

[pause]

{img:farmer-ploughing-paddy-oxen-wooden-plough-iron-tip-water-reflections}
Around the village are the fields. The most important crop is rice. It grows in fields flooded with water in the rainy season. The farmers plough the wet fields with their oxen. The women plant the young rice plants one by one in long rows in the water and mud. They bend over all day long. And they sing.

[long-pause]

In the dry season they grow barley and wheat and lentils and beans. They grow sesame for oil and sugar cane, which they crush to make brown sugar. They grow cotton, which the women spin and weave.

[pause]

Each village has its craftsmen. A carpenter makes the ploughs and carts and houses. There is a blacksmith and a potter at his wheel. There is a weaver and a barber. There is a washerman who washes clothes at the river, beating them on a flat stone. The villagers do not pay them with money. They give them a share of the harvest every year.

[pause]

{img:village-headman-elders-under-banyan-tree-meeting-afternoon-shade}
The village has a headman. He settles quarrels. He collects the king's share of the harvest, usually a sixth part. And the elders meet under the great tree to decide things together.

[long-pause]

The life of the village follows the seasons. In the hot season the earth is dry and cracked and the air shimmers. People rest in the shade through the long afternoons. Then the great rains come. Everything turns green and the rice is planted. And in the cool season, the harvest is gathered. There are festivals and weddings. And the nights are cold and clear and full of stars.

[long-pause]

{img:monsoon-clouds-gathering-over-indian-plain-dark-sky-village-waiting}
In India, the most important event of the year is the coming of the rain. It is called the monsoon.

[pause]

From March to June it is the hot season. The sun burns down from a white sky day after day. The earth dries and cracks. The rivers shrink. The ponds dry up. Leaves fall from the trees. The grass turns brown. Hot winds blow dust across the plain. And everyone waits. If the rains come late, the rice cannot be planted. And then there will be hunger.

[long-pause]

Then in June the clouds begin to come from the sea in the south-west. At first a few high white clouds. Then more and more. They build up in great towers, dark and heavy. The air becomes thick and still. And the birds go quiet.

[pause]

And then one afternoon the wind rises. The sky turns dark, almost black. There is a crack of thunder. And the rain comes.

[pause]

It does not rain the way it rains in other places. It pours. Great warm heavy sheets of water roar on the roofs and the leaves and the fields. In a few minutes the lanes are rivers. The courtyards are ponds. The whole world is water and noise.

[long-pause]

And everyone comes out into the rain, laughing. The children dance and shout and splash. The old people stand in their doorways and lift their faces to the sky. The peacocks spread their tails and dance and cry out with joy. Thousands of frogs come out and sing all night long.

[pause]

The smell of the first rain on the dry earth is one of the most wonderful smells in the world. It is rich and sweet and earthy. And the people of India love it.

[pause]

{img:green-rice-paddies-monsoon-sky-farmers-planting-seedlings-rows}
In a few days the whole land turns green. The grass grows. The trees put out new leaves. The rivers rise and the ponds fill. The farmers go out into the flooded fields with their oxen to plough and plant the rice.

[long-pause]

The monsoon lasts about four months. It does not rain all the time. There are storms, then sunshine, then more storms. But the roads turn to mud. The rivers flood. Travel is hard and dangerous.

[pause]

So the wandering seekers and monks stop for the rainy season. All through the rest of the year they walk from place to place. But in the rains they stay in one place for three months. That way they do not have to walk through the mud. And they will not step on the young rice or the tiny creatures that come out everywhere in the wet.

[pause]

{img:monk-shelter-grove-rain-falling-meditation-hut-thatch-quiet}
This is the rainy season retreat. It is the start of the great Buddhist monasteries. Kings and traders gave groves where the monks could stay together through the rains. There they listened to the teaching and practised and lived in peace. And the rain fell softly on the roofs and the leaves all around them.

[long-pause]

{img:wild-elephants-herd-forest-river-ancient-india-dawn-mist}
In the forests of India in the time of the Buddha, there are elephants.

[pause]

They live in great herds in the forests and jungles between the villages, and at the foot of the mountains. The mothers and aunts and grandmothers and the young ones walk together. The oldest and wisest female leads them. They walk slowly through the trees, eating leaves and grass and bamboo and fruit. In the evening they come down to the rivers to drink and bathe and play.

[long-pause]

The people of India love and respect the elephants. They are strong and wise and gentle. And they never forget. In the old stories, elephants are often noble and kind. Before the Buddha was born, the story goes, his mother had a dream. She dreamed that a white elephant came down from the sky. It was a sign that her son would be great.

[pause]

The kings catch wild elephants in the forests and tame them. They train them for work and for ceremonies. The elephant keepers spend their whole lives with their elephants, from when they are young. They talk to them and sing to them. And the elephants know them and trust them.

[pause]

{img:mahout-washing-elephant-river-scrubbing-boy-laughing-evening}
The tame elephants work hard. They carry big logs from the forests and pull carts and lift stones. They carry kings and nobles on their backs in processions, in seats decorated with gold and silk. And every evening the keepers take their elephants down to the river to wash them. They scrub their great grey backs with stones and coconut husks. The elephants lie happily in the water and spray themselves with their trunks.

[long-pause]

There is a story about the Buddha and an elephant. In Rajagriha there was a big wild elephant called Nalagiri. One day he was let loose in the street where the Buddha was walking. People ran and screamed and hid. The great elephant came charging down the street, straight toward the Buddha. Everyone held their breath.

[pause]

But the Buddha stood still and calm. He looked at the elephant with great kindness. And he spoke to him gently. The great elephant slowed down. He stopped right in front of the Buddha. He lowered his head. The Buddha softly stroked his forehead. And the elephant knelt down, quiet and calm.

[pause]

{img:elephant-kneeling-before-monk-gentle-street-crowd-watching-rajgir}
The people watching from their windows and roofs were amazed. This story is carved and painted in temples all over Asia. The great elephant kneeling quietly before the calm gentle man in the street.

[long-pause]

Today there are still wild elephants in the forests of India, though far fewer than in the Buddha's time. In some places in the evening, you can still see them coming down to the rivers to drink and bathe as the sun sets. Just as they have done for thousands of years.

[long-pause]

{img:indian-woman-dawn-courtyard-grinding-grain-clay-hearth-fire-500bc}
Let us spend a day with a family in a village by the Ganges.

[pause]

The father is a farmer. He has a few fields of rice and barley, a pair of oxen and two cows. The mother runs the house and looks after the children and the animals. She works in the fields too, at planting and harvest. There are three children and the father's parents. His younger brother and his wife live there too. They all share a house of several rooms around a courtyard.

[long-pause]

They wake before dawn, when the first birds start to sing and the sky grows pale in the east. The father and the grandfather go down to the river to bathe. They pour water from their cupped hands toward the rising sun and say a prayer.

[pause]

The mother sweeps the courtyard and smears the floor with fresh cow dung and water. She draws a little pattern at the door with white rice flour, for good luck. Then she lights the fire in the clay stove and milks the cows. She boils the milk and makes breakfast. Rice porridge with milk and a little sugar. Or yogurt and some fruit.

[pause]

{img:farmer-leading-oxen-to-fields-dawn-boy-following-mango-trees}
Then the father and his brother go out to the fields with the oxen. The eldest son is ten. He takes the cattle to the grazing land by the river. He sits under a tree all day and watches them. He plays his bamboo flute. And he keeps an eye on the edge of the forest. Sometimes a big cat comes near the herd at dusk.

[long-pause]

The women work at home. They grind grain on a flat stone, rolling a round stone back and forth. They pound rice in a wooden bowl with a long stick to get the husks off. They spin cotton. And they fetch water from the well in clay pots, carried on their heads and hips.

[pause]

The younger children play in the courtyard and the lane with the other village children. They play with clay toys and carts and balls and tops and little bows. They play with the dogs and goats and calves. They climb the mango trees and swing on the hanging roots of the great tree.

[pause]

{img:family-midday-meal-courtyard-leaf-plates-rice-lentils-sitting-floor}
At midday the men come home to eat. They sit on mats on the floor in the shade. They eat from leaf plates with their right hands. Rice and lentils and vegetables cooked with spices. Ginger and pepper and cumin and a little butter. Yogurt and pickles and fruit. Then in the heat of the afternoon, everyone rests.

[long-pause]

In the late afternoon the men go back to the fields. The women go to the river to wash clothes and fetch water. They talk with the other women on the bank. And the children swim and play in the shallows.

[pause]

In the evening, as the sun sets, the cattle come home from the grazing land. They raise a cloud of golden dust along the lane. Their bells ring. The boy walks behind them with his flute. In India, this time of day is called the cow-dust hour.

[pause]

{img:cow-dust-hour-cattle-returning-village-sunset-golden-dust-bells}
The family eats supper together by the light of the fire and a little clay oil lamp. Afterwards they sit in the courtyard under the stars. The grandfather tells the children stories. Stories of the gods and heroes. Stories of clever animals. The monkey and the crocodile. The jackal and the lion. And the wise old elephant.

[long-pause]

Then they sleep on beds woven of string. In hot weather they sleep in the courtyard or on the roof. In the rains and the cool season they sleep inside. The frogs sing in the pond. Far away in the fields the jackals howl. And the night watchman walks through the village, calling out now and then that all is well.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-indian-kitchen-clay-pots-spices-turmeric-ginger-rice-steaming}
What do people in India eat in the time of the Buddha?

[pause]

Above all, rice. Rice is the great food of the Ganges plain. It is boiled and steamed. It is made into porridge. It is flattened and puffed. It is ground into flour for little cakes and sweets. The finest rice is white and smells sweet. The rich eat it. And it is offered to the gods and the monks.

[long-pause]

With the rice there are lentils and beans and peas of many kinds. They are cooked into thick soups. There are vegetables. Pumpkins and cucumbers and radishes and greens. Lotus roots and bamboo shoots. And there is milk and yogurt and butter from the cows and buffaloes.

[pause]

And there are spices. The food of India is already full of flavour. Ginger and turmeric and black pepper. Cumin and coriander and mustard seed. Cardamom and cloves and cinnamon. There are no chillies yet. They will come from America two thousand years later. But the food is spicy and warm and smells wonderful all the same.

[pause]

{img:fruit-market-ancient-india-mangoes-bananas-jackfruit-coconuts-baskets}
There is fruit everywhere. Mangoes above all, the king of fruits. In the hot season they are sweet and golden and juicy. There are bananas and coconuts and dates and grapes and pomegranates. And many small sour fruits from the forest trees.

[long-pause]

And there are sweets. The people of India love sweet things. They boil the juice of sugar cane until it turns thick and brown and sticky. Then it hardens, like toffee. In fact, the words sugar and candy come in the end from old languages of India. With sugar and honey they make sweets and cakes of rice flour and sesame and milk and coconut.

[pause]

Many people eat meat and fish. But some eat little or none. They do not want to harm living creatures.

[pause]

{img:wedding-feast-ancient-india-guests-sitting-rows-banana-leaf-plates}
At a feast or a wedding, the guests sit in long rows on the floor. The food is served on big banana leaves, or plates made of leaves stitched together with little twigs. Many dishes come one after another. Rice and lentils and vegetables. Yogurt and pickles and fried cakes. Sweets and fruit. And milk sweetened with sugar and flavoured with cardamom.

[long-pause]

Everyone eats with their right hand, carefully and neatly. They mix the rice and lentils with their fingers into little balls. After the meal they wash their hands and mouths. Then they chew a little leaf wrapped around a piece of nut and spices. It freshens the breath and helps the stomach. And it turns the mouth red. People in India still do this today.

[long-pause]

{img:shravasti-ancient-city-street-merchants-shops-bullock-carts-elephant}
Let us go to the city of Shravasti, the great city of the kingdom of Kosala.

[pause]

It is one of the biggest cities in India in the time of the Buddha. Perhaps tens of thousands of people live there. It has walls of earth and brick, a moat, gates and watchtowers. It stands beside a river.

[long-pause]

Inside, the streets are crowded and noisy and colourful. There are houses of brick and wood, two and three floors high. They have balconies and carved wooden doors and windows with wooden screens. There are palaces and gardens and parks with lotus pools. Shops open onto the streets. They sell everything. Cloth and spices and perfumes. Jewellery and pots and grain and sweets and flowers.

[pause]

The streets are full of people and animals. Ox carts creak along. Horses and chariots pass by. Elephants sway slowly through the crowds with their riders. Porters carry loads on their heads and on poles across their shoulders. There are traders and monks and soldiers. There are beggars and dancers and acrobats. There are snake charmers and storytellers.

[pause]

{img:craftsmen-street-guild-ivory-carvers-goldsmiths-weavers-workshops}
The city's craftsmen work together in groups called guilds. Weavers, potters, carpenters and goldsmiths. Ivory carvers and perfume makers and garland makers. Each guild has its own street and its own leaders and rules. The guilds are rich and powerful. Sons learn the craft of their fathers, generation after generation.

[long-pause]

And there is money.

[pause]

In these years, the first coins in India are being made. They are small flat pieces of silver of a fixed weight. Little symbols are punched into the metal one by one. A sun, a wheel, an elephant, a tree, a mountain, a bull. They are among the first coins in the world. With them it is much easier to buy and sell and travel and trade.

[pause]

{img:punch-marked-silver-coins-ancient-india-symbols-sun-wheel-close}
The traders of Shravasti are very rich. The richest of them all is a man so generous that people give him a special name. It means the one who feeds the poor. Every day he gives food to hundreds of poor people at his house.

[long-pause]

One day this rich man hears the Buddha teach. He is so moved that he invites the Buddha to come and stay at Shravasti for the rainy season. He looks for a place for the Buddha and his monks. A quiet grove near the city, but not too near.

[pause]

He finds a beautiful park. It belongs to a prince called Jeta. But the prince does not want to sell it. He makes a joke. I will only sell it, he says, for as many gold coins as it takes to cover the ground. Surely nobody would pay that much. But the rich man says yes. He brings cartloads of gold coins. And he begins to cover the ground with them, coin by coin.

[pause]

{img:jetavana-grove-monastery-shravasti-trees-paths-monks-evening}
The prince is so amazed that he gives the rest of the land for free. He gives the trees too, as his own gift. And so Jeta's grove becomes the great monastery. The Buddha will spend more rainy seasons there than anywhere else. About twenty of them. And he will give many of his teachings there.

[long-pause]

Today at Shravasti you can still walk among the ruins of Jeta's grove. Among the old brick walls of the monasteries, under the great trees. Pilgrims from all over the world sit quietly in the shade. They chant and sit in silence.

[long-pause]

{img:caravan-bullock-carts-forest-road-ancient-india-merchants-dust}
Between the cities of India, along the roads and rivers, traders travel in caravans. A caravan is a long line of travellers and carts going together.

[pause]

A great caravan may have hundreds of ox carts. They creak slowly along the dusty roads in a long line. They are loaded with goods. Fine cotton cloth from the holy city by the Ganges. Salt and spices and sugar. Iron and copper and pottery. Sandalwood and perfumes and precious stones. And horses from the north-west.

[long-pause]

The caravans travel slowly, a few kilometres a day, from village to village. At night they stop and put the carts in a circle. They burn fires to keep wild animals away. And guards watch through the night.

[pause]

They cross forests and deserts and rivers and mountains. There are dangers. Wild elephants and tigers and snakes. Robbers and floods and droughts. In the old stories, a caravan leader must be wise and brave and careful. He must know where to find water in the desert. And he must know how to travel at night by the stars.

[pause]

{img:caravan-camp-night-circle-carts-fires-guards-stars-forest-edge}
There is an old story about a caravan crossing a great desert at night. A guide leads them by the stars. But on the last night, the guide falls asleep. The oxen turn round. All night they walk back the way they came. In the morning the caravan is back where it started. And there is no more water and no more wood.

[long-pause]

But the leader stays calm. He does not give up. He walks around the desert until he finds a small patch of grass. There must be water under here, he thinks. He tells the men to dig. They dig and dig. Then they hit a great rock. They are ready to give up. But the leader climbs down. He puts his ear to the rock. And he hears the sound of water flowing underneath. He gives a hammer to a young servant. Strike the rock, he says. The boy strikes. The rock breaks. And the water rises up, cool and clear, and saves them all.

[pause]

The story has a lesson. Do not give up too soon.

[pause]

{img:river-trade-boats-ganges-cargo-boatmen-sails-sunset-merchants}
Many goods travel by river too. Boats big and small carry goods and passengers up and down the Ganges, from city to city. They go down to the sea. From there, ships sail to the south of India and across the sea to other lands.

[long-pause]

With the traders travel ideas and stories and news and new beliefs. The traders are among the first and most generous followers of the Buddha. His teaching of peace and honesty and not too much of anything suits them. And the monks are travellers too, on the same roads. So traders and monks become friends and companions on the long dusty roads of India.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-varanasi-riverbank-steps-temples-boats-dawn-500bc}
Where the Ganges bends in a great curve, there is the city of Varanasi. People also call it the city of light.

[pause]

It is one of the oldest cities in the world. People have lived there without stopping for three thousand years or more. Already in the time of the Buddha, it is old and famous and holy.

[long-pause]

The city stands on a high ridge above the river. That keeps it safe from the floods. Every morning the people of Varanasi go down to the river along paths and steps. They bathe in the holy water. And they pray, facing the sun as it rises over the flat sandy shore on the other side.

[pause]

Varanasi is a city of learning. Teachers live there with their pupils. Wise men and seekers come there to debate and to teach. It is no accident that the Buddha came near here to give his first teaching. It was in a deer park just outside the city, where seekers had always gathered.

[pause]

{img:weavers-varanasi-ancient-looms-fine-cotton-cloth-courtyard-sunlight}
And Varanasi is a city of weavers. Its fine cotton cloth is soft and light, white or dyed in beautiful colours. It is famous all over India and sold in every city. The stories say that the Buddha wore clothes of this cloth when he was a prince. Two thousand years later, the weavers of Varanasi will become famous all over the world for their silk, woven with gold and silver thread.

[long-pause]

There are markets full of cloth and perfumes and sandalwood and jewellery and spices. Boats come and go along the river with goods from up and down the Ganges.

[pause]

Many of the old stories begin with the words, long ago, when a king ruled in Varanasi. So for the people of India, Varanasi is the city of once upon a time. The city of all the old stories.

[pause]

{img:evening-lamps-floating-ganges-varanasi-leaf-boats-flowers-dusk}
In the evening at Varanasi, people light little clay lamps filled with oil. They set them on leaves with flowers. And they float them on the river as a gift. Soon the dark water is covered with hundreds of tiny flickering lights. They drift slowly downstream in the dusk.

[long-pause]

Today Varanasi is a big busy crowded city of more than a million people. Great stone steps lead down to the river along the bank for kilometres. Temples and palaces stand above them. Every evening at sunset there is a ceremony on the steps. Priests swing great lamps of fire. Bells ring. People chant and blow sea shells like trumpets. Thousands watch from the steps and from boats on the river.

[pause]

And every morning at dawn, just as in the time of the Buddha, the people of Varanasi still go down to the river. They bathe and pray, facing the rising sun. There is mist on the water. Boats glide past. And the bells ring from the temples.

[long-pause]

{img:brahmin-priests-fire-altar-vedic-ritual-ghee-offering-chanting-dawn}
In the India of the Buddha's time there are the Brahmins.

[pause]

The Brahmins are the priests and the teachers. They keep the holy knowledge. This knowledge is called the Veda. It is a great collection of hymns and prayers and rituals in the old language, Sanskrit. Some of it is already more than a thousand years old. It has never been written down. It is learned by heart and passed from teacher to pupil, word for word, sound for sound.

[long-pause]

A young Brahmin boy goes to live with his teacher at about eight years old. He stays with him for twelve years or more. He learns the Veda by heart. He listens and repeats again and again, every sound perfectly. In the end he knows thousands of verses from memory. One mistake, and he must start the verse again. Because of this careful way of learning, the hymns have been passed down without changing for more than three thousand years. They are still chanted the same way today.

[pause]

The Brahmins hold great ceremonies for the kings and the rich. And they hold small daily ones for families. Every Brahmin home has a holy fire. They pour gifts of butter and milk and grain into it. And they chant the old hymns to the gods. To the god of fire. To the king of the gods with his thunderbolt. To the sun. And to many others.

[pause]

{img:teacher-pupils-forest-hermitage-learning-veda-by-heart-sitting-circle}
In the old teachings, society is divided into four great groups. The priests and teachers. The warriors and rulers. The farmers and traders. And the servants and workers. Within these there are many smaller groups of families and trades.

[long-pause]

{img:people-of-many-walks-ancient-india-market-crowd-merchants-farmers-priests}
In the time of the Buddha, these groups matter. But they are not yet as strict as they will become later. The Buddha himself comes from the warrior group. He will often say that a person is not noble or low because of birth. A person is noble or low because of what they do and what is in their heart. His followers will come from every group and every kind of family. Priests and princes and traders. Farmers and barbers and sweepers.

[pause]

In these years, some Brahmins are also asking new deep questions. In the forests, teachers and pupils sit together. They talk about the meaning of everything. The self and the world. What is real. What lies behind all things. And what happens to us when we die.

[pause]

{img:forest-sage-teaching-young-students-under-tree-quiet-afternoon-light}
Their teachings are collected in books called the Upanishads. The name means sitting down near. Sitting near the teacher, to listen. They speak of a deep true self inside every person. And of a great reality behind the whole world. One famous phrase says, that thou art. You yourself are that.

[long-pause]

They also speak of a great circle of birth and death and birth again. Every living being is born again and again in a new body. What happens depends on what it did in the life before. This is called karma. And they speak of the hope of one day being freed from this endless wheel, into a peace beyond all change.

[pause]

These ideas are in the air in the Buddha's time. Wise men discuss them in the forests and the courts and the city parks. The young prince Siddhartha will hear them and think about them. And then he will go his own way.

[long-pause]

{img:wandering-ascetics-shramanas-forest-path-begging-bowls-simple-robes}
In the time of the Buddha, the roads and forests of India are full of wanderers.

[pause]

They are called shramanas. It means those who strive. They are men, and some women, who have left their homes and families and possessions and work. They are searching for the truth. And for freedom from suffering.

[long-pause]

They live very simply. They wear only a simple robe or cloth. They own almost nothing. A bowl for food, perhaps a water pot and a walking stick. They sleep under trees or in caves or in little huts of leaves. And they live on what people give them. Every morning they walk silently into the villages. They stand at the doors. And people put a little food into their bowls.

[pause]

Some of them put themselves through great hardships. They go without food for days and weeks. They stand on one leg. They sit in the burning sun or the freezing cold. They believe that if they master the body, they can free the spirit. Some become so thin they can barely stand.

[pause]

{img:meditating-hermit-cave-mouth-hills-dawn-stillness-simple-life}
Others meditate. They sit still and silent for hours with their legs crossed and their eyes closed. They calm the mind and look deep inside.

[long-pause]

There are many different teachers and many different schools. They travel from place to place. They meet in parks outside the cities and in special halls built by kings and traders. They discuss and debate the great questions. Crowds come to listen, as if to a great show.

[pause]

One of the greatest teachers of the time is a prince called Mahavira. That name means the great hero. Like the Buddha, he left his home to become a wanderer. He teaches a path of great gentleness. His followers do not harm any living thing, not even the smallest insect. Some sweep the path in front of them with a soft brush as they walk. That way they do not step on anything alive. This faith is called Jainism. It is still alive in India today.

[pause]

{img:jain-monks-walking-soft-brush-sweeping-path-white-robes-gentle}
The people of India respect the wanderers. They give them food gladly. They believe it brings good luck. And they believe the seekers are doing something important for everyone.

[long-pause]

The kings honour them too. King Bimbisara likes to visit the forest wise men and the wanderers. He sits with them and asks them questions. About life and death. And about the right way to live and to rule.

[pause]

So when the young prince Siddhartha leaves his home, he will not be alone. He will join a great company of seekers. They walk the roads of India under the hot sun and the monsoon rain, with their bowls and their questions. Looking for the answer.

[long-pause]

{img:taxila-ancient-city-hills-north-west-teachers-students-courtyard}
Far away in the north-west of India, in what is now Pakistan, there is a famous city of learning. It is called Taxila.

[pause]

It stands in a green valley among the hills. A great road runs past it. The road goes from the Ganges plain through the mountains to Persia and beyond. In the time of the Buddha, Taxila is famous all over India for its teachers.

[long-pause]

Young men come to Taxila from all over India. They walk for months along the great road to study with the famous teachers. They study the holy books and grammar and law. They study maths and the stars and medicine. They study archery and the care of elephants and many other things.

[pause]

There is no big university building with halls and libraries. Each teacher lives in his own house with his pupils. The pupils live with him as part of his family. They work in his house and fields and forest. And they learn from him by heart, sitting at his feet.

[pause]

{img:students-fetching-firewood-water-teacher-house-forest-dawn}
Rich pupils pay their teacher with a gift of money at the start. Poor pupils work for him during the day instead. They fetch water and firewood and look after his cattle. And they study at night by the light of the fire, though their eyes are heavy.

[long-pause]

The Buddha's friend, the doctor Jivaka, studied medicine at Taxila for seven years. The stories say some princes of India studied there too. They became friends as students and remembered each other all their lives.

[pause]

Much later, a great scholar from near Taxila will write a grammar of the Sanskrit language. It has nearly four thousand short rules. It is so complete and so clever that scholars still study and admire it today. It is one of the greatest works about language ever made.

[pause]

{img:ancient-scholar-writing-birch-bark-grammar-rules-lamp-night}
And when Alexander the Great comes to India, about two hundred years after the Buddha, he will stop at Taxila. He will meet its wise men and argue with them. And he will admire them.

[long-pause]

Today the ruins of Taxila are a World Heritage Site. There are the remains of three cities, built one after another over many centuries. And on the hills around them there are great Buddhist monasteries built later. Monks from all over Asia came there to study and pray and live in peace, among the green hills.

[long-pause]

{img:lumbini-garden-sal-trees-blossom-queen-maya-spring-gentle-light}
The Buddha's followers told the story of his life again and again for many centuries before it was written down. Over the years it gained many wonderful details. But the heart of the story is simple and human.

[pause]

He was born, the stories say, in a garden called Lumbini. It was a grove of flowering trees in spring, at the foot of the Himalayas.

[long-pause]

His mother, Queen Maya, was travelling to her own family's home to give birth, as was the custom. On the way she stopped in the garden to rest among the flowering trees. And there, standing and holding a branch, she gave birth to a son.

[pause]

His father, the chief of the Shakyas, was overjoyed. They named the baby Siddhartha. It means the one who reaches his goal. His family name was Gautama.

[pause]

{img:infant-prince-wise-old-sage-asita-holding-baby-palace-courtyard}
The stories say a wise old man came down from the mountains to see the baby. He held him in his arms and smiled. And then he cried. The king asked him why. The old man said he smiled because the child would become a great teacher. He would show the world the way to freedom. And he cried because he himself was old. He would not live to hear him.

[long-pause]

Sadly, a few days after the birth, Queen Maya passed away. Her sister lovingly brought up the boy. She became his second mother. Many years later she would become the first Buddhist nun.

[pause]

He grew up in comfort and luxury in his father's house. The stories say his father built him three palaces. One for the hot season, one for the cool season, and one for the rains. He wore the finest cloth. He had music and dancing and gardens with lotus pools. He had everything he could wish for. His father wanted it that way. He wanted his son to become a great ruler, not a wandering teacher. So he tried to keep every sad sight away from him.

[pause]

{img:young-prince-siddhartha-sitting-under-rose-apple-tree-meditating-field-ploughing}
There is a lovely story from his childhood. One day his father took him to the ploughing festival. The king himself ploughed the first line in the field. The little boy was left sitting in the shade of a tree while everyone watched. And sitting there quietly, watching the oxen and the birds, he fell all by himself into a deep calm peace. When his nurses came back, they found him still sitting there, quiet and happy.

[long-pause]

He learned everything a prince should learn. To ride and shoot a bow and read and rule. He was handsome and clever and kind. Everyone loved him. At sixteen he married a beautiful cousin called Yashodhara. Years later they had a son called Rahula.

[pause]

And yet, the stories say, in the middle of all this comfort and happiness, the young prince felt something was missing. He could not say what it was. But it would not go away.

[long-pause]

{img:prince-chariot-ride-city-streets-kapilavastu-charioteer-morning}
The old stories tell that one day the young prince went out of the palace in his chariot. His driver, Channa, went with him. They went to see the town.

[pause]

On the road the prince saw an old man, bent and grey and shaking, leaning on a stick. What is wrong with this man, the prince asked. He is old, my lord, said Channa. Everyone who lives grows old. And the prince was troubled.

[long-pause]

Another day he saw a man who was ill, lying by the road in pain. He is sick, my lord, said Channa. Anyone can fall sick. And another day he saw a family carrying someone who had passed away to the river. They were crying. Everyone who is born must one day die, said Channa.

[pause]

The prince went home. And he could not forget what he had seen. All the comfort and music and gardens of the palace could not take away one thought. Everyone I love, and I myself, will grow old and fall sick and die. Is there no way beyond this?

[pause]

Then one more day he saw a fourth sight. A wandering monk with a shaved head and a simple robe and a bowl. He walked calmly along the road with a quiet peaceful face. Who is this man, the prince asked. He is one who has left his home to search for the truth, said Channa.

[long-pause]

And then the prince knew what he must do.

[pause]

That night, when he was twenty-nine, he went quietly to the room where his wife was sleeping. Their baby son lay in her arms. He stood in the doorway in the lamplight and looked at them for a long time. He wanted to hold his son one last time. But he was afraid of waking them. If they woke, could he still go? So he turned and went away.

[pause]

{img:white-horse-night-ride-moonlight-prince-charioteer-leaving-city-gate}
He woke Channa and told him to saddle his white horse. They rode out through the sleeping town and through the gate in the moonlight. They rode on through the night, until they came to a river at dawn.

[long-pause]

On the river bank the prince got down from his horse. He took off his jewels and fine clothes and gave them to Channa. He cut off his long hair with his sword. And he swapped his princely clothes with a poor hunter for a simple rough robe.

[pause]

He told Channa to take the horse home. Tell my father and my wife that I have not gone because I do not love them, he said. I have gone to find a way beyond suffering, for them and for everyone. And I will come back when I have found it.

[pause]

{img:monk-alone-riverbank-dawn-shaven-head-simple-robe-path-ahead}
The stories say Channa cried. And the faithful white horse would not leave. It looked back and back at its master as it was led away.

[long-pause]

And Siddhartha, the prince who was now a wanderer, turned and walked away alone along the road. He had nothing but his robe and a bowl. He walked south toward the great kingdom where the famous teachers lived in the forests and hills. His search had begun.

[pause]

Buddhists call this the Great Going Forth. It is painted and carved in temples all over Asia to this day. The prince on his white horse, riding out through the gate in the moonlight, into the night.

[long-pause]

{img:young-wanderer-alms-round-rajagriha-street-people-watching-calm-dignity}
After leaving home, Siddhartha walked south for many days. At last he came to Rajagriha, the capital of Magadha.

[pause]

Early one morning he walked into the city with his bowl for the first time. He went quietly from door to door, as the wanderers did. He stood silently at each door and waited for whatever people would give.

[long-pause]

The stories say the people noticed him at once. He was young and tall and handsome. He walked so calmly and with such grace, looking down at the road in front of him. They thought he must be someone very special. The news spread through the streets. It reached the palace and the ears of the king.

[pause]

King Bimbisara went up onto the palace roof and looked down. He saw the young wanderer walking through the streets below. He too was amazed. He sent his servants to follow him and see where he went.

[pause]

{img:king-bimbisara-visiting-young-ascetic-rock-shelter-hill-retinue}
Siddhartha collected his food. He walked out of the city to one of the five hills. He sat down in the shade of a rock and ate his meal quietly. The servants came back and told the king. And the king got into his royal carriage and went out with his court to the hill. He walked up to the rock where the young man was sitting.

[long-pause]

He greeted him and sat down beside him and talked with him. He liked the young man very much. You are young and noble, the king said. You seem born to rule. Stay here in my kingdom. I will give you wealth and land and an army to lead. You can live a great and happy life.

[pause]

It was a big offer. But Siddhartha thanked him gently. He said he was the son of the chief of the Shakyas. He had already left a palace and wealth and power behind him. He had left it freely. Because he was looking for something no palace and no kingdom could give. A way beyond old age and sickness and death and sorrow.

[pause]

{img:king-and-wanderer-parting-hillside-sunset-rajagriha-valley-below}
The king understood. He did not try to persuade him any more. But he asked for one promise. If you find what you are looking for, he said, come back to Rajagriha. And teach it to me first.

[long-pause]

Siddhartha promised. And he went on his way, to the forests and the teachers and the long years of searching.

[pause]

Years later, after the night under the great tree and the first teaching, the Buddha remembered his promise. He came back to Rajagriha with his monks. The king, older now, came out with his whole court to meet him. He heard the teaching and became his follower. And he gave him the bamboo grove.

[long-pause]

The king and the teacher stayed friends for many years. In the evenings, the stories say, the king would sometimes go quietly out of the city to the bamboo grove. He would sit with the Buddha and listen and talk in the cool of the evening, as the sun went down over the hills.

[long-pause]

{img:ascetic-siddhartha-forest-five-companions-austerities-thin-meditating}
For six years, Siddhartha searched.

[pause]

First he learned from two of the greatest teachers of meditation of his time. He learned everything they could teach him, very quickly. He reached the deepest calm and stillness they knew. But he was not satisfied. Those states were peaceful. But when he came out of them, the old questions were still there. And so was the suffering.

[long-pause]

So he went on alone to a quiet place by a river with a forest grove. Five other seekers were there. He joined them. And he began the hardest path of all.

[pause]

He almost stopped eating. A few grains of rice a day. A single bean. He grew so thin that his ribs stood out like the beams of an old barn. His eyes sank deep into his head. He sat in the heat and the cold. He held his breath and went without sleep. The five companions admired him. Surely, they thought, he will find the truth soon.

[pause]

But one day, nearly fainting, he realised this was not the way either. Hurting the body did not free the mind. It only made it weaker. Then he remembered the day as a child under the tree. He had sat there peacefully and felt a deep natural happiness. Perhaps that is the way, he thought.

[long-pause]

So he accepted a bowl of rice cooked in milk. A young woman from the village brought it to him as a gift. He ate it and bathed in the river. And slowly his strength came back.

[pause]

His five companions were disappointed. They thought he had given up. They left him and went away.

[pause]

{img:bodhi-tree-night-full-moon-meditator-seated-calm-river-niranjana}
Now Siddhartha was alone. He walked to a great tree near the river. A grass cutter gave him some grass to sit on. He sat down beneath the tree, facing east. And he made a promise to himself. I will not get up from this place until I have found the answer.

[long-pause]

It was the evening of a full moon in May.

[pause]

All through that night, the stories say, he sat under the tree. Calm and steady, he went deeper and deeper. The stories tell of a great struggle that night. A tempter called Mara tried to make him give up. He sent storms and doubts and fears. He sent beautiful visions to distract him. But Siddhartha did not move. He touched the earth with his right hand, asking the earth itself to be his witness. And the earth trembled. And the tempter fled.

[pause]

{img:dawn-under-bodhi-tree-morning-star-peaceful-figure-golden-light}
Through the hours of the night his mind became clear and calm and bright, like a still lake. He saw, the stories say, how all beings are born and die and are born again. And at last he saw clearly what causes suffering. And he saw the way to end it.

[long-pause]

And as the morning star rose in the east at dawn, he became the Buddha. The one who is awake.

[pause]

He was thirty-five years old. The tree he sat under is called the Bodhi tree, the tree of waking up. And the place today is called Bodh Gaya.

[long-pause]

{img:pipal-tree-heart-shaped-leaves-close-light-through-breeze-trembling}
The tree the Buddha sat under on that night was a pipal tree. It is a kind of fig tree that grows wild all over India.

[pause]

It is a beautiful tree, tall and wide, with a smooth grey trunk and great spreading branches. Its leaves are shaped like hearts, with long thin tips like little tails. They tremble and flutter and rustle in the slightest breeze. Even on a still day, the tree seems to be whispering.

[long-pause]

The pipal tree was already holy in India before the Buddha. People tied threads around its trunk and left gifts at its roots. After the Buddha's waking up, the tree at Bodh Gaya became the holiest tree in the world for his followers.

[pause]

They came from far away to sit under it and honour it with flowers and lamps and water. In the early years there were no statues of the Buddha at all. When artists carved the stories of his life, they did not show him as a person. Instead they showed an empty seat under a tree. Or a footprint. Or a wheel. To show that he was there.

[pause]

{img:nun-sanghamitta-ship-sapling-golden-vase-sea-voyage-sri-lanka}
About two hundred and fifty years after the Buddha, a great emperor's daughter became a nun. She sailed across the sea to the island of Sri Lanka. The stories say she carried a branch of the Bodhi tree in a golden pot. The long sea journey was risky. Would the little branch survive? It did. The king of Sri Lanka waded into the sea up to his neck to meet the ship. And he planted the branch in his capital with great ceremony.

[long-pause]

That tree is still alive today. It is more than two thousand two hundred years old. It is the oldest tree in the world planted by people with a known date. It stands on a terrace with golden railings. Golden posts hold up its old branches. Monks care for it day and night. And pilgrims come from all over the world to see it and sit beside it.

[pause]

The first tree at Bodh Gaya died and was replaced several times over the centuries. The tree that grows there today was planted about a hundred and fifty years ago. It came from a cutting of the tree in Sri Lanka. So the daughter has come home to the place of the mother.

[pause]

{img:anuradhapura-sri-maha-bodhi-ancient-tree-golden-railings-pilgrims-white}
And cuttings from these trees have been planted at temples all over the world. Today there are Bodhi trees from the tree at Bodh Gaya growing in Asia and Europe and America and Australia and Africa.

[long-pause]

And in the evening, when the breeze comes up, the heart-shaped leaves of all these trees tremble and whisper together softly in the fading light, all around the world.

[long-pause]

{img:sarnath-deer-park-buddha-five-companions-first-teaching-trees-deer}
After his waking up, the stories say, the Buddha stayed near the tree for several weeks, in peace and joy and quiet.

[pause]

He wondered if he should try to teach what he had found. It was so deep, he thought. Perhaps nobody would understand it. But then he thought of all the people in the world. They were like lotus flowers in a pond. Some were still deep under the water. Some were just reaching the surface. And some stood above the water, ready to open to the sun. For those who were ready, he decided, he would teach.

[long-pause]

He thought first of his five old companions. He walked more than two hundred kilometres to find them. They were at a deer park near Varanasi.

[pause]

When they saw him coming, they said to each other, here comes Gautama, who gave up. Let us not stand up to greet him. But as he came closer, they saw something in his face and in the way he walked. He was so calm and bright and peaceful. Without meaning to, they stood up. They welcomed him and gave him a seat and water to wash his feet.

[pause]

{img:spotted-deer-grazing-grove-sarnath-evening-light-peaceful}
And there in the deer park, in the evening, with deer grazing nearby under the trees, he gave his first teaching. Buddhists call it the turning of the wheel of the teaching.

[long-pause]

First he spoke of the middle way. A person looking for the truth should avoid two extremes. One is the life of pleasure and comfort, which he had known as a prince. The other is the life of hardship and hurting yourself, which he had known in the forest. Instead, he said, follow a middle path between them.

[pause]

Then he spoke of four noble truths. Life has suffering in it. Everyone feels it. Suffering has a cause. It is wanting and grasping, wishing things were other than they are. Suffering can end, when the grasping ends. And there is a path that leads there. A way of living with right understanding and right speech and right action. With kindness and effort and a calm, watchful mind.

[pause]

{img:dharma-wheel-carved-stone-eight-spokes-sunlight-ancient}
Simple words. But the five companions listened. And they understood. One by one they became his first followers, the first monks. And so the community of the Buddha's followers began.

[long-pause]

A wheel with eight spokes became the sign of the teaching. One spoke for each part of the path. Two hundred years later, a great emperor will set up a pillar here. On top stand four lions back to back, above a wheel. Today those four lions are the national symbol of India. And the wheel is in the middle of India's flag.

[pause]

At this place today there is a great round brick tower. It rises from the green park among the ruins of old monasteries. Pilgrims walk slowly round and round it. And in a park nearby, deer still graze under the trees.

[long-pause]

{img:bamboo-grove-veluvana-monks-sitting-shade-pond-squirrels-rajagriha}
After the first teaching, the Buddha travelled and taught. More and more people came to listen. Many decided to follow him and become monks.

[pause]

He went to Rajagriha. King Bimbisara came out with his whole court to greet him. He listened to the teaching and became his follower and friend.

[long-pause]

The king gave him the bamboo grove outside the city. It was quiet and cool, with a pond. It was the first gift of land to the community of monks.

[pause]

Soon there were hundreds of monks, and later thousands. They came from every kind of family. Sons of priests and princes and traders. Sons of farmers and craftsmen and servants. One of the most respected monks had been a barber at the court. Among the monks, birth did not matter. Whoever joined first was the most senior.

[pause]

{img:monks-walking-in-line-alms-round-village-morning-bowls-silent}
The monks lived very simply. They shaved their heads. They wore simple robes made of pieces of cloth sewn together, like a patchwork of rice fields. The robes were dyed in earthy colours. The monks owned almost nothing. Three robes, a bowl, a needle, a razor, a belt. And a cloth to strain tiny creatures out of their drinking water.

[long-pause]

They did not work for money or handle money. Every morning they walked into the villages in a quiet line with their bowls. They stood silently at the doors. People put food in their bowls. The monks ate before midday. And they did not eat again until the next morning.

[pause]

The rest of the day they sat in meditation and studied. They listened to the teaching and talked about it. They cared for each other. And they walked slowly up and down under the trees, paying attention to every step.

[pause]

{img:monastery-grove-evening-monks-gathered-listening-teacher-lamps}
For most of the year they wandered from place to place. They taught and slept under trees and in caves. But in the rainy season they stayed together for three months in one place. Kings and traders and ordinary people gave them groves and parks. Over time, these became the first monasteries, with huts and halls and walking paths and wells.

[long-pause]

The Buddha himself lived the same simple life as his monks. For forty-five years he walked barefoot all over the Ganges plain with his bowl. Every day he taught. Kings and farmers and traders and priests. Wanderers and outcasts. Anyone who came. He used simple words and stories from everyday life that everyone could understand.

[pause]

He also had followers who did not become monks. Men and women who lived at home with their families and work. They supported the monks. And they tried to live by the teaching. Not to kill or steal or lie. Not to harm others. To be generous and kind and aware.

[long-pause]

{img:young-monk-waking-dawn-hut-grove-folding-robe-birds}
Let us spend a day with a young monk in a grove near Shravasti, in the rainy season.

[pause]

He wakes before dawn in his little hut of bamboo and straw. Rain drips softly from the leaves. The birds begin to sing. He sits up on his mat and folds his legs. He closes his eyes and sits quietly in the dark. He breathes slowly and calmly. He watches his breath going in and going out.

[long-pause]

As the light grows, he gets up. He sweeps the path in front of his hut. He fetches water from the well and strains it through his cloth. He washes his face and feet. Then he puts on his outer robe carefully over one shoulder. He takes his bowl. And he joins the other monks walking in a quiet line along the muddy road to the city.

[pause]

He walks slowly, looking down at the ground a few steps ahead. He does not look around at the shops and the people. At each door he stops silently for a moment. If someone comes out and puts food in his bowl, he accepts it with a nod and walks on. If no one comes, he walks on just the same. He does not ask and he does not choose. He takes whatever is given. Rice and lentils, a piece of fruit, a sweet. Some days his bowl is full. Some days it is nearly empty.

[pause]

{img:monk-eating-alms-food-quietly-under-tree-grove-before-noon}
Back at the grove he sits under a tree and eats slowly and quietly. He thinks of the people who gave the food. He thinks of all the work that went into it. The farmers and the cooks, the rain and the sun. Then he washes his bowl and dries it and puts it away.

[long-pause]

In the heat of the day he sits in meditation again in the shade. Or he walks slowly back and forth along a path under the trees for an hour or more. He pays attention to each step. Lifting, moving, placing the foot on the earth.

[pause]

In the afternoon he studies with his teacher, an older monk. There are no books. So he learns the teachings by heart, verse by verse. He asks questions and listens. And he mends his robe with needle and thread, sitting cross-legged on the porch of his hut.

[pause]

{img:monks-gathered-hall-evening-buddha-teaching-oil-lamps-rain-outside}
In the evening, as the light fades, the rain falls on the roof of the great hall. All the monks gather there. They sit in rows in silence. The Buddha, or one of the older monks, gives a teaching quietly by the light of the oil lamps. The monks listen and ask questions. Then they sit together in silence for a long time, listening to the rain.

[long-pause]

Then the young monk walks back to his hut through the dark wet grove. The frogs are singing. Fireflies flicker among the trees. He lies down on his right side on his mat, as the Buddha taught. His mind is calm and clear. And he falls asleep to the sound of the rain on the leaves.

[long-pause]

{img:mahaprajapati-women-shaved-heads-robes-walking-to-vaishali-dusty-road}
At first, only men became the Buddha's monks. But women wanted to follow the path too.

[pause]

The stories tell that some years after his waking up, the Buddha's aunt came to him. She was the woman who had raised him after his mother passed away. She asked if she could become a nun. And at first, he hesitated.

[long-pause]

But she did not give up. She and a group of other women cut off their hair. They put on simple robes. And they walked barefoot a long way to the city where the Buddha was staying. They stood at the gate. Their feet were swollen and dusty. Their faces were streaked with tears. Would they be turned away again?

[pause]

The Buddha's faithful helper and cousin, Ananda, saw them. He went to the Buddha and asked him a question. Lord, can a woman also wake up, if she follows the path? And the Buddha said, yes, Ananda, she can. Then, Lord, said Ananda, let them join. And the Buddha agreed.

[pause]

{img:buddhist-nuns-grove-meditating-together-ancient-india-peaceful-light}
So the Buddha's aunt became the first Buddhist nun. The order of nuns began. It was one of the first communities of women in the world given to a spiritual life.

[long-pause]

Thousands of women joined. Princesses and queens. Traders' daughters and farmers' wives. Servants and widows. And mothers who had lost their children.

[pause]

Some of them made poems. The poems were remembered and collected in a book. It is one of the oldest collections of poems by women anywhere in the world.

[pause]

{img:elder-nun-sitting-forest-hut-evening-light-peaceful-face}
In these poems, the nuns speak simply and honestly about their lives. About their hard times and their sorrows and their freedom. One nun wrote that she was so free, so wonderfully free. Free from the grinding stone, free from the cooking pot, and free from her grumpy husband.

[long-pause]

Another old nun had once been a famous beauty in the city. She had given her mango grove to the Buddha and his monks. She wrote a poem about growing old. Her hair, once black and soft and sweet-smelling, is now like bark. Her eyes, once bright like jewels, are now dim. Her voice, once sweet like a cuckoo, now shakes. And at the end of each verse she says gently, the words of the truthful one are true. All things change.

[pause]

Many other women helped the Buddha as followers who lived at home. One of the greatest was a rich and wise woman of Shravasti. She built a great monastery for the monks. She gave food and robes and medicine. And for many years she was a friend and adviser to the Buddha and the whole community.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-indian-wedding-procession-musicians-drums-flowers-torches-evening}
Life in ancient India is full of festivals and celebrations.

[pause]

There are festivals of the seasons. When the ploughing begins, the king himself ploughs the first line with a golden plough. There are festivals at harvest and when the rains come. In spring, when the trees blossom, young people go out to the parks and gardens. They play and sing and swing on swings hung from the branches.

[long-pause]

There are festivals of the stars and the moon. The whole city celebrates for days, with music and dancing and feasting. People wear their best clothes and flower garlands. They visit each other. The streets are hung with flags and the houses with lamps.

[pause]

An old story tells of a festival in Shravasti that lasted a whole week. A poor man wanted to join in. But he had nothing. So he worked for days to earn enough money for a garland of flowers. At last he had it. And then he too could join the festival.

[pause]

{img:village-women-singing-dancing-circle-festival-courtyard-lamps-colours}
And there are the great family celebrations. The birth of a child. The naming day. The first time the baby eats rice. The first haircut. The first day of school. And above all, the wedding.

[long-pause]

A wedding in ancient India is a big event. It lasts several days. The families have arranged it carefully. They often ask star readers to find the right day and hour. The bride is bathed and dressed in fine red and gold clothes. She wears jewellery of gold and silver and pearls, and flowers in her hair. The women paint patterns on her hands and feet.

[pause]

The groom comes in a procession with his family and friends. They come on horses and elephants and in carts. There is music of drums and flutes, and torches and dancers. They go through the streets to the bride's house. There, before the holy fire, the bride and groom walk around the fire together seven times, holding hands. The priest chants the old verses. And the families throw rice and flowers over them.

[pause]

{img:bride-groom-walking-around-sacred-fire-priest-chanting-families-flowers}
Then there is a great feast for all the guests, with music and dancing late into the night. The next day, the bride goes with her husband to her new home in his family's house. She rides in a covered cart decorated with flowers. The women of the village sing songs of goodbye. And some of them cry a little.

[long-pause]

Many of these customs are still part of Indian weddings today. The seven steps around the fire. The red and gold. The painted hands. The processions and music and flowers. More than two and a half thousand years later.

[pause]

And on festival nights in the towns and villages, little clay lamps are lit in their hundreds. Along the walls and roofs and doorways and river banks. And the whole land glows with tiny flickering flames under the stars.

[long-pause]

{img:monkey-king-bridge-body-river-monkeys-crossing-mango-tree-moonlight}
The Buddha and his followers loved stories. They told many of them to explain the teaching in simple ways. Some of the most loved are stories of the Buddha's earlier lives. The stories say he was born again and again, as a man or an animal. And each time he was kind and wise and brave.

[pause]

Let us listen to two of them.

[long-pause]

Long ago, the first story says, the Buddha-to-be was born as a monkey. He was the king of a great troop of eighty thousand monkeys. They lived in a huge mango tree beside the Ganges, high in the mountains. They ate its wonderful sweet mangoes.

[pause]

One day a mango fell into the river. It floated all the way down to Varanasi. The king of Varanasi was bathing there. He tasted it. It was the most delicious fruit he had ever eaten. So he went up the river with his soldiers to find the tree. When he found it, full of monkeys, he told his archers to surround it. In the morning, they would take all the mangoes. The monkeys were trapped.

[pause]

{img:monkey-king-stretched-between-trees-troop-escaping-across-river-dawn}
The monkey king saw the danger. He climbed to the top of the tree. He made a great leap across the river to a tree on the other side. He tied a long bamboo cane to his foot and leapt back. But the cane was a little too short. So he held onto a branch of the mango tree with his hands. And he stretched his own body across the gap, like a bridge. All eighty thousand monkeys ran across his back, one by one, to safety on the other side.

[long-pause]

The king of Varanasi watched from below in the moonlight. He was amazed. In the morning he had the tired monkey king gently brought down. He laid him on a soft bed and cared for him. Why did you do this for them, he asked. And the monkey king said, I am their king, and they trusted me. A king must care for his people more than for himself.

[pause]

The king of Varanasi never forgot it. From that day on, he ruled his own people with kindness and care.

[pause]

{img:full-moon-hare-silhouette-on-moon-forest-night-gentle}
The second story is about a hare.

[long-pause]

Long ago, the Buddha-to-be was born as a hare. He lived in a forest with three friends. A monkey, a jackal and an otter. On a holy day they decided to each give a gift of food to any traveller who came by.

[pause]

The otter found some fish. The jackal found some meat and a pot of yogurt. The monkey picked some mangoes. But the hare only ate grass. He had nothing to give. So he made a brave decision. If a traveller comes, he thought, I will give him myself.

[pause]

The king of the gods heard this. He wanted to test the hare. So he came to the forest dressed as a poor hungry old man. The hare said, light a fire, good sir, and I will give you myself to eat. The old man lit a fire. And the hare jumped toward it, gladly.

[pause]

{img:hare-sitting-moonlit-grass-friends-otter-monkey-jackal-forest}
But the fire was cool, like snow. It did not hurt him at all. The old man turned back into the king of the gods. Little hare, he said, your kindness will be remembered forever. He took a mountain and squeezed it. With its juice he drew a picture of the hare on the face of the moon. So everyone in the world would see it and remember.

[long-pause]

That is why, in India and many lands of Asia, people look up at the full moon and do not see a man in the moon. They see a hare.

[pause]

Tonight, if the moon is up, you can look for him too. The little hare sitting in the moon with his long ears. Shining softly over the forests and rivers of India.

[long-pause]

{img:old-buddha-walking-slowly-ananda-road-sal-forest-evening-light}
For forty-five years, the Buddha walked and taught across the Ganges plain.

[pause]

When he was eighty years old, he was tired and frail. He set out on one last journey to the north. His faithful helper Ananda went with him, and a group of monks.

[long-pause]

They walked slowly from village to village. They crossed the Ganges by boat. They stopped at a city where he stayed in a mango grove for the last rainy season. There he fell ill. Everyone was worried. But he got better, and went on.

[pause]

He stood on a hill outside the city and looked back at it for a long time. How lovely this city is, Ananda, he said. This is the last time I will look at it.

[pause]

At last they came to a small town and a grove of trees outside it. There the Buddha lay down between two great trees. He lay on his right side with his head resting on his hand. He was calm and peaceful.

[long-pause]

The stories say that even though it was not the season, the trees burst into blossom. And the flowers fell softly on him, like rain.

[pause]

Ananda was very sad. He went away and leaned against a doorpost and cried. The Buddha called for him. Do not be sad, Ananda, he said gently. Have I not told you that everything we love must change and pass away? You have looked after me so well and so kindly for so many years. Keep going. And you too will soon be free.

[pause]

{img:monks-gathered-quietly-sal-grove-night-lamps-reverent-stillness}
He asked the monks three times if any of them had a question he could still answer, while there was time. They were all silent.

[long-pause]

Then he spoke his last words. All things that are made will pass away. Keep going, with care.

[pause]

And then, quietly and peacefully, in the night under the flowering trees, he passed away. Buddhists call it the final peace.

[pause]

We will leave him there, gently, under the blossoming trees in the quiet grove. The monks sit in silence around him. The lamps burn. And the night is soft and still.

[pause]

{img:parinirvana-stupa-kushinagar-today-white-dome-pilgrims-flowers}
Today at that place there is a white dome and a temple. Inside is a great statue of the Buddha lying peacefully on his right side. Pilgrims bring golden cloth and lay it over him. And the same kind of trees still grow in the park around it. Every spring, they blossom.

[long-pause]

{img:ashoka-pillar-polished-stone-lion-capital-ancient-india-road-sunlight}
After the Buddha passed away, the stories say, five hundred monks met at Rajagriha. Together they recited all his teachings. That way they would be remembered exactly and passed on.

[pause]

For hundreds of years the teachings were passed on by heart, from teacher to pupil. About four hundred years later, in Sri Lanka, they were finally written down on palm leaves.

[long-pause]

The kingdom of Magadha grew and grew. About two hundred years after the Buddha, it became the centre of the first great empire of India. It stretched across almost the whole land. And its greatest emperor was Ashoka.

[pause]

At first Ashoka was a fierce conqueror. But after one terrible war, he was filled with sorrow at all the suffering he had caused. He turned to the Buddha's teaching. From then on, he decided to rule with kindness and justice and peace.

[pause]

{img:ashoka-edict-carved-rock-brahmi-script-inscription-sunlit-surface}
He had his messages carved on rocks and on great polished stone pillars all over his empire, for everyone to read. Messages about kindness to animals. Respect for all faiths. Care for the poor and the sick and the old. Honesty and generosity. He built hospitals for people and for animals. He planted trees along the roads for shade. He dug wells and built rest houses for travellers.

[long-pause]

He visited the places of the Buddha's life as a pilgrim. At the garden where the Buddha was born, he set up a pillar. Its words say, here the Buddha was born. That pillar is still there today.

[pause]

He sent monks to other lands to share the teaching. To Sri Lanka and the south of India and lands in the west. From there, over the centuries, the teaching spread peacefully along the trade roads and sea roads. To China and Korea and Japan. To Tibet and Thailand and Cambodia and Vietnam. And to much of Asia.

[pause]

{img:monks-travelling-silk-road-caravan-mountains-scrolls-china-bound}
But in India itself, over many centuries, Buddhism slowly faded. The great monasteries and schools became ruins. One of them once had thousands of students from all over Asia. And the places of the Buddha's life were forgotten. They were lost in the jungles and the fields.

[long-pause]

Then, nearly two hundred years ago, a British scholar worked out how to read the old writing on Ashoka's pillars. An archaeologist used the diaries of Chinese pilgrims who had visited India more than a thousand years before. With their help he found the places again, one by one. The tree of waking up. The deer park. The place of the final peace. And then, about a hundred and thirty years ago, in the jungle near Nepal, someone found Ashoka's pillar in the garden. Its words were still clear. Here the Buddha was born.

[long-pause]

{img:mahabodhi-temple-bodh-gaya-today-tower-pilgrims-prayer-flags-dawn}
Today, more than two and a half thousand years later, millions of people visit the places of the Buddha's life every year, from all over the world.

[pause]

At Bodh Gaya there is a great temple with a tall pointed tower of brick. It is carved all over with little figures and rises high above the town. It is a World Heritage Site. And behind the temple there is a great pipal tree. Its branches spread wide. Its heart-shaped leaves tremble in the breeze. This is the Bodhi tree.

[long-pause]

It is not the very same tree the Buddha sat under. But it grew from a cutting of a cutting of the old tree. That old tree still grows in Sri Lanka. A nun brought it there long ago.

[pause]

Under the Bodhi tree, all day long and into the night, pilgrims sit in silence or chant softly in many languages. Monks in orange robes from Thailand and Sri Lanka. Monks in dark red robes from Tibet. Monks in grey robes from Korea and black robes from Japan. And ordinary people from India and all over the world. They light little lamps and lay down flowers. They bow down on the stone. And they walk slowly round and round the temple in the evening light.

[pause]

{img:bodhi-tree-leaves-butter-lamps-evening-pilgrims-meditating-bodh-gaya}
In the birth garden in Nepal, Ashoka's pillar still stands. There is a temple over the place of the birth, and a pool, and monasteries built by many countries. In the deer park, the great brick tower still rises from the green grass, and deer still graze nearby. And in the place of the final peace, the Buddha still lies on his right side in his temple, under his golden cloth.

[long-pause]

Visitors still climb the vulture peak at sunset, and the hot springs still flow. Pilgrims still sit under the trees of Jeta's grove among the ruins.

[pause]

And the Ganges plain is still there, green and wide and full of life. Its villages and rice fields and mango groves are still there. Cattle still come home at the cow-dust hour. And the river still flows slowly toward the sea, past the great steps of Varanasi. People still come there at dawn to bathe and pray, facing the rising sun.

[pause]

{img:varanasi-ghats-dawn-ganges-boats-bathers-lamps-mist-today}
So we leave the India of the Buddha tonight, as the evening falls.

[long-pause]

The sun sets red and golden over the plain and the river. The cattle are coming home along the lanes in a cloud of golden dust, their bells ringing. Smoke from the evening fires rises from the villages. Little clay lamps are lit in the doorways. In a quiet grove outside the city, the monks gather in the hall. They sit in silence as the first stars come out and the frogs begin to sing. And under a great pipal tree beside a slow river, the heart-shaped leaves tremble softly in the evening breeze. The full moon rises, with a little hare on its face, over the quiet land.
