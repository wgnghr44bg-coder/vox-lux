Tonight we travel back more than a hundred and fifty years, to the autumn of 1868. We go to the Highlands of Scotland, to a wide green valley among the hills, where a fast, clear river flows over the stones. It is called the river Dee. And beside the river, among the trees, there stands a castle of pale grey granite, with towers and turrets, like a castle in a fairy tale. It is Balmoral. And it is the favourite home of Queen Victoria.

[long-pause]

It is early morning, in September. A soft mist lies over the river. The birch trees on the hillsides are turning gold. The heather on the hills is purple, fading to brown. The bracken is turning red. The air is cool and fresh, and smells of pine trees and woodsmoke. Somewhere up on the hill, a stag is roaring, a deep, wild sound, that echoes across the valley. And the first sunlight is touching the tops of the mountains.

[pause]

In the castle, the servants are already up. Fires are being lit in the rooms. Hot water is being carried upstairs. Breakfast is being prepared in the kitchens. In the stables, the ponies are being brushed and saddled. And outside the castle, a tall, bearded Highlander in a kilt is standing by the door, waiting. He is the queen's personal servant. His name is John Brown. And he is waiting for the queen, who will soon come out for her morning walk.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:queen-victoria-1868-black-dress-white-cap-walking-garden-balmoral-dogs}
Queen Victoria is forty-nine years old. She has been queen of Britain for more than thirty years. She rules over a vast empire, stretching all around the world. But she is also a widow. Her beloved husband, Prince Albert, passed away seven years ago. And she is still in mourning. She wears black every day. And she spends as much time as she can here, at Balmoral, in the Highlands, which she and Albert loved more than anywhere else in the world.

[pause]

Tonight, we will spend an autumn at Balmoral, in 1868. We will see the castle and its rooms, full of tartan. We will follow the queen on her walks, her drives and her picnics in the hills. We will meet her children, her servants, and the people who live on the estate. And especially, we will follow a family of Highlanders, who live in a little cottage near the castle. A ghillie, his wife, and their two children.

[pause]

{img:highland-cottage-crathie-stone-walls-thatch-smoke-family-door-autumn}
We will see the Highland games, where strong men throw tree trunks. The deer on the hills, and the salmon in the river. The little church where the queen goes on Sundays. The new lodge she has built, by a lonely loch in the mountains. And the great ball for the servants and the ghillies, at the end of the season, where everyone dances reels until midnight, and the queen watches, and smiles.

[long-pause]

{img:river-dee-autumn-golden-birches-salmon-pool-hills-misty-highlands}
It was a world of great differences. A queen, and her poor tenants. A castle, and little stone cottages. But it was also a world of beautiful landscapes, of old traditions, and of a surprising friendship, between a lonely queen and the people of the Highlands. So let us begin, on a misty September morning, in the valley of the Dee, as the sun rises over the hills, and the stags roar, and the queen comes out of her castle, for her morning walk.

[long-pause]

{img:young-queen-victoria-coronation-1838-crown-robes-westminster-abbey}
Victoria became queen in 1837, when she was only eighteen years old. Before that, she had a lonely and strict childhood, at Kensington Palace, in London. Her mother kept her apart from other children, and watched her every moment. So when she became queen, she was very happy to be free. She was small, lively and strong-willed. And from the very first day, she was determined to be a good queen.

[long-pause]

In 1840, she married her cousin, Prince Albert, a German prince, from a small duchy called Saxe-Coburg. She was deeply in love with him. And he was a remarkable man. Clever, serious, hardworking, interested in science, art, music and everything new. He helped Victoria with her work, and became her closest adviser. Together, they had nine children. Their marriage was very happy.

[pause]

Albert was interested in everything. He organised the Great Exhibition of 1851, in London, in a huge building of glass and iron, called the Crystal Palace, where inventions and products from all over the world were shown. He designed buildings, and gardens, and farms. He loved music, and played the organ. He was very interested in education and in improving the lives of working people. And he loved nature, walking and hunting, especially in Scotland.

[pause]

{img:queen-victoria-and-prince-albert-young-family-children-garden-osborne}
Victoria and Albert first visited Scotland in 1842, and fell in love with it. The mountains, the lochs, the forests and the people reminded Albert of the mountains of Germany, where he grew up. They came back again and again. And in 1852, they bought the estate of Balmoral, in the Highlands, and built a new castle there. It became their favourite home. They called it their dear paradise.

[pause]

But in December 1861, Albert fell ill, with a fever. He was only forty-two. And after a short illness, he passed away, at Windsor Castle. Victoria was devastated. Her grief was enormous. She shut herself away, and would not appear in public, for years. She wore black, every day, for the rest of her life. She kept Albert's rooms exactly as he had left them. And every evening, his clothes were laid out, and hot water brought for his shaving, as if he were still alive.

[long-pause]

{img:widow-queen-victoria-black-mourning-dress-writing-desk-albert-portrait}
In 1868, it is seven years since Albert died. Victoria is still in mourning. She still wears black. She still avoids London as much as she can. Many people in Britain are unhappy about this. They think that the queen should be seen more, and do more of her public duties. Some newspapers criticise her. People call her the Widow of Windsor. But slowly, she is beginning to come out of her grief, a little.

[pause]

And the place that helps her most is Balmoral. In the Highlands, she feels close to Albert. Every path, every hill, every view reminds her of him, and of the happy times they had together. She spends about four months a year there, in the spring and the autumn. She walks, and rides, and drives in her carriage through the hills, and sketches, and writes her journal. And slowly, the mountains and the fresh air, and the kindness of the Highland people, help her to heal.

[pause]

{img:queen-victoria-small-figure-pony-carriage-highland-road-hills-autumn}
The queen is small, only about one metre fifty tall. She is a little round, now, and her face is serious. She has blue eyes, which can be very cold, or very warm. She can be stern and demanding. She does not like to be contradicted. But she can also be kind, generous and funny. When she laughs, her whole face changes. And with the Highland people, she is often warm and relaxed, in a way she never is in London.

[long-pause]

She has a famous phrase, which she is said to have used. We are not amused. Nobody is sure if she ever really said it. And in fact, the people who knew her best said that she laughed a great deal, especially at funny stories, and at the things that children said. At Balmoral, in the autumn of 1868, with her children and her servants around her, in the hills she loves, she is perhaps as happy as she can be.

[long-pause]

{img:balmoral-castle-scottish-baronial-towers-turrets-granite-lawn-river-sunny}
When Victoria and Albert bought the Balmoral estate, in 1852, there was already a small old castle there. But it was too small for the royal family and all their servants. So Albert decided to build a new one. He designed it himself, with an architect from Aberdeen, called William Smith. And it was built just beside the old castle, which was then pulled down.

[long-pause]

The new castle was finished in 1856. It is built of granite, a hard, pale grey stone, from a quarry nearby, which sparkles when the sun shines on it. It is in a style called Scottish Baronial, which is like the old castles of the Scottish lords, with round towers, pointed turrets, stepped gables and little battlements. In the middle, there is a tall square tower, with a clock, and a round turret at one corner, with a flag pole. When the queen is at Balmoral, her flag flies from it.

[pause]

Albert designed everything. Not only the castle, but also the gardens, the paths, the cottages for the workers, the bridges, and even the farm buildings. He planted thousands of trees. He laid out walks through the woods and along the river. He built a dairy, and a school for the children of the estate. And he made the whole estate bigger, buying the land around it, until it covered many thousands of hectares of hills, forests and moors.

[pause]

{img:balmoral-drawing-room-tartan-carpets-curtains-chairs-thistle-wallpaper}
Inside, the castle is decorated in a very special way. With tartan. Everywhere. Albert designed his own tartan, called the Balmoral tartan, grey, black and red. And Victoria designed another, the Victoria tartan. The carpets are tartan. The curtains are tartan. The chairs are covered with tartan. Even the linoleum on the floors is tartan. There are thistles, the flower of Scotland, painted on the wallpaper. And antlers of stags on the walls of the hall.

[pause]

Some visitors thought it was too much. One government minister, who stayed at Balmoral, joked that the tartan made him dizzy. Another said that the drawing room was the ugliest room he had ever seen. But Victoria loved it. It was what Albert had made. And to her, it was beautiful. After his death, she would not let anything be changed. Everything stayed exactly as he had designed it.

[long-pause]

{img:prince-albert-statue-memorial-balmoral-woods-granite-pedestal-autumn}
After Albert died, Victoria built memorials to him all over the Balmoral estate. Statues, stone crosses, and cairns, which are piles of stones, built on the tops of hills. On a hill above the castle, there is a great pyramid-shaped cairn, built of granite, in his memory, with an inscription. And near the castle, there is a large bronze statue of Albert, in Highland dress, with a dog and a gun, looking out over the valley.

[pause]

The estate is full of places that remind Victoria of Albert. The paths he laid out. The trees he planted. The views he loved. The rocks where they had picnics together. The hills where he went stalking deer. She visits them all, again and again, in her carriage, or on her pony. And she remembers. And sometimes, she weeps. But slowly, the memories become a comfort, as well as a sorrow.

[pause]

{img:queen-victoria-on-pony-led-by-highlander-hill-path-cairn-view-autumn}
Albert also made Balmoral a working estate. With farms, and forests, and deer, and grouse, and salmon in the river. Many people live and work on the estate. Farmers, foresters, gamekeepers, ghillies, shepherds, gardeners, and their families. They live in cottages, scattered through the valley. Albert built many new cottages for them, better than the old ones, with stone walls, slate roofs and proper chimneys. And Victoria knows many of them by name.

[long-pause]

The queen often says that Balmoral is the place where she feels most at home. More than Windsor, more than Buckingham Palace, more than Osborne, her house by the sea, on the Isle of Wight. At Balmoral, she says, she can breathe freely. She can be herself. Away from London, and politics, and the crowds, and the ceremonies. In the hills, with the heather and the deer and the clear river, she feels close to Albert. And that is all she wants.

[long-pause]

{img:lochnagar-mountain-dark-corrie-heather-moor-autumn-light-highlands}
The Balmoral estate lies in the heart of the Scottish Highlands, in a region called Royal Deeside. It is one of the most beautiful landscapes in Britain. A wide valley, with the fast, clear river Dee running through it, between forests of pine and birch. And all around, the hills and mountains, rising up, wild and bare, covered with heather and rocks, to the sky.

[long-pause]

Above Balmoral, to the south, there is a great mountain, called Lochnagar. It is more than eleven hundred metres high, with a dark, steep cliff on its northern side, around a little loch. In winter, it is covered with snow. In summer, the heather on its slopes is purple. The poet Lord Byron, who lived nearby as a boy, wrote a famous poem about it, called Dark Lochnagar. Victoria and Albert climbed it, together, on ponies, more than once.

[pause]

The hills are covered with heather. In August and September, the heather blooms, and the whole landscape turns purple and pink, for kilometres and kilometres. It is a wonderful sight. The bees hum in the heather, making honey. The grouse, plump brown birds, run through it, calling go-back, go-back. And in the autumn, the heather fades to a soft, rusty brown, and the bracken turns red and gold.

[pause]

{img:red-deer-stag-roaring-autumn-rut-misty-glen-heather-antlers-calm-distance}
On the hills, there are red deer. Thousands of them. The largest wild animals in Britain. The stags have great branching antlers, and a rough mane around their necks. In the autumn, in September and October, it is the rutting season, when the stags gather the hinds, and fight each other, and roar. Their roaring echoes across the hills, day and night. A deep, hoarse, wild bellow, that you can hear for kilometres. It is the sound of the Highland autumn.

[pause]

In the forests, there are red squirrels, with their tufted ears. Pine martens. Capercaillies, great black birds like turkeys. Owls. And in the river, there are salmon, which swim up from the sea, every year, to lay their eggs in the gravel of the upper river. In the autumn, you can see them leaping, out of the water, at the waterfalls, trying to get upstream. They are silver, and strong, and very beautiful.

[long-pause]

{img:salmon-leaping-waterfall-river-dee-autumn-spray-rocks-pine-trees}
The weather in the Highlands changes very quickly. One moment, the sun is shining, and the hills are bright and clear. The next moment, the clouds come down, and it rains, or hails, or snows. Then the sun comes out again, and there is a rainbow, over the valley. The light is always changing. The colours of the hills change from purple to gold to grey to blue, as the clouds pass over them. Victoria loved to sketch and paint them, in watercolours.

[pause]

In the valley, there are farms and little villages. Small stone cottages, with thatched or slate roofs, and smoke rising from their chimneys. Fields of oats and barley, and pastures with sheep and cattle. Highland cattle, with long shaggy red coats and long curved horns. There is a village called Crathie, near the castle, with a little church. And further along the river, there is the village of Braemar, in the mountains, and the town of Ballater, down the valley.

[pause]

{img:highland-cattle-shaggy-red-coats-long-horns-pasture-river-cottages}
The people of the valley speak English, with a strong Scottish accent. And some of the older people still speak Gaelic, the old Celtic language of the Highlands. They are farmers, shepherds, gamekeepers, ghillies, foresters and craftsmen. Many of them work for the royal estate, or for the other great estates in the valley. They are proud, independent, hardworking people, who know the hills and the river better than anyone.

[long-pause]

Victoria loves the Highland people. She thinks they are honest, simple, brave and loyal. More honest and loyal, she says, than the courtiers and politicians of London. She visits their cottages, talks to them, and learns their names and their stories. She goes to their weddings and their funerals. And she writes about them in her journal, with great affection. They, in turn, respect her, and are a little in awe of her. But many of them also come to love her, in their own quiet way.

[long-pause]

{img:highland-ghillie-1868-kilt-tweed-jacket-bonnet-beard-standing-river-bank}
In a little stone cottage, about a kilometre from the castle, on the edge of the woods, lives a family. The father is called Angus Grant. He is a ghillie. A ghillie is a Highland servant, who works outdoors. He guides people on the hills, helps with the deer stalking and the salmon fishing, leads the ponies, carries things, and knows every path, every stream and every hill on the estate. Angus has worked at Balmoral since he was a boy.

[long-pause]

Angus is about forty years old. He is tall and lean, with a red beard, going grey, and blue eyes, wrinkled from looking at the hills in all weathers. He wears a kilt of grey tweed, a jacket, thick woollen stockings, heavy boots, and a flat bonnet on his head. He speaks English with a soft Highland accent, and Gaelic with his mother. He is quiet, patient and strong. He can walk all day on the hills, and never seem tired.

[pause]

His wife is called Kirsty. She is a small, cheerful woman, with dark hair and rosy cheeks. She runs the cottage, cooks, bakes, spins, knits, looks after the hens and the cow, and works in the little garden. She also helps in the castle laundry, sometimes, when the queen is in residence, and they need extra hands. She has a beautiful singing voice, and sings old Gaelic songs while she works.

[pause]

{img:highland-mother-kitchen-cottage-baking-oatcakes-girdle-fire-children}
They have two children. Morag, who is thirteen, and Ewan, who is nine. Morag is tall for her age, with her father's red hair and her mother's dark eyes. She is clever and quiet, and loves to read. She goes to the little school on the estate, which Prince Albert built. And she helps her mother at home. She knows all the plants and birds of the woods, and she can bake oatcakes better than anyone in the family.

[pause]

Ewan is small and freckled, and always running. He wants to be a ghillie, like his father. He follows Angus everywhere, when he is allowed to, and asks endless questions. He can already tie a fishing fly, and he knows where all the deer go, on the hill behind the cottage. He has a little dog, a scruffy terrier called Bran, who goes everywhere with him.

[long-pause]

{img:boy-and-terrier-dog-running-highland-path-birch-woods-autumn-sunny}
There is also a grandmother, Angus's mother, who lives with them. She is very old, about eighty, and speaks mostly Gaelic. She sits by the fire, knitting stockings, and telling stories, about the old days in the Highlands, before the castle was built, when the valley belonged to the old lords, and the people lived in little black houses of stone and turf. She remembers when the queen first came, as a young woman, with Prince Albert. She says that they were the handsomest couple she had ever seen.

[pause]

The cottage is small but solid. It is made of granite, with a slate roof, and was built by Prince Albert for the estate workers. It has two rooms downstairs, a kitchen and a parlour, and a loft upstairs, where the children sleep. In the kitchen, there is a big fireplace, where Kirsty cooks, over a fire of peat and wood. There is a box bed, in an alcove in the wall, where the grandmother sleeps, warm by the fire.

[pause]

{img:highland-cottage-kitchen-box-bed-peat-fire-dresser-plates-grandmother-knitting}
Outside, there is a little garden, with potatoes, kale, turnips and onions. A hen house. A shed for the cow. A peat stack, against the wall. And a little burn, a stream, running past the cottage, down to the river, where Ewan catches small trout. Behind the cottage, the birch woods rise up the hill, and above them, the heather moor, and the high hills, where the deer live.

[long-pause]

Life is simple, and sometimes hard, in the cottage. There is not much money. The winters are long and cold. But the family is happy. They have a warm house, enough food, good work, and each other. And they live in one of the most beautiful places in the world. In the evenings, they sit by the fire, and the grandmother tells stories, and Kirsty sings, and Angus mends his fishing rods, and the children listen. And the stags roar on the hill, in the autumn dark.

[long-pause]

{img:balmoral-castle-entrance-hall-antlers-stags-heads-tartan-carpet-staircase}
Inside Balmoral Castle, everything is Scottish. Albert designed it that way, and Victoria has kept it exactly as he left it. When you come through the front door, into the entrance hall, you see stags' antlers on the walls, and stuffed deer heads, from the deer that Albert shot. There are tartan carpets on the floor. And a great staircase, leading up to the rooms above.

[long-pause]

On the ground floor, there are the rooms where the queen and her family live during the day. A dining room, with a long table, and paintings of Highland scenes on the walls. A drawing room, with tartan curtains and tartan chairs, and a piano. A library, full of books. A billiard room. And the queen's own sitting room, where she works, at her desk, by the window, looking out over the lawn and the river to the hills.

[pause]

On the floor above, there are the bedrooms of the queen and her children, and the rooms of the ladies who attend her, called ladies-in-waiting. The queen's bedroom has a view of the hills. In it, there is a photograph of Albert, on the pillow beside her. And there are many more of his pictures, and his things, all over the castle. The queen keeps them close, always.

[pause]

{img:queen-victoria-sitting-room-desk-red-despatch-boxes-window-view-hills}
The castle is cold. Very cold. The queen loves fresh air, and cold rooms. She thinks they are healthy. She keeps the windows open, even in October, and does not like big fires. Her ladies-in-waiting and her ministers suffer terribly. They wear thick shawls and woollen underclothes, and huddle together, shivering. One visitor wrote that he had never been so cold in his life, as in the drawing room at Balmoral. The queen did not seem to notice.

[pause]

There are many servants in the castle, when the queen is in residence. More than a hundred. Cooks, maids, footmen, pages, dressers, housekeepers, coachmen, grooms and many more. Most of them come with the queen, from Windsor, on the train. Others are local Highlanders, who work on the estate. The servants live and work in the back part of the castle, with its own kitchens, laundries, stores and bedrooms. They must be quiet, and never be seen in the wrong place.

[long-pause]

{img:balmoral-kitchens-1868-cooks-copper-pans-range-servants-busy-steam}
The kitchens are huge, with great ranges, rows of copper pans, and a team of cooks. The queen eats a great deal, and her meals are rich, and many courses. Soup, fish, meat, game, puddings and cakes. Some of the food comes from London, on the train. But much of it comes from the estate. Venison from the deer. Salmon and trout from the river. Grouse from the moors. Vegetables and fruit from the gardens. Milk, cream and butter from the dairy.

[pause]

There is also something special, in the castle, every evening. A piper. A Highland bagpiper, in his kilt, who walks around the outside of the castle, playing his pipes, every morning at nine o'clock, to wake the queen. And every evening, during dinner, he walks around the dining table, playing. The sound of the pipes is very loud, in a small room. Some guests find it unbearable. But the queen loves it.

[pause]

{img:highland-piper-kilt-playing-bagpipes-castle-lawn-morning-mist-tradition}
Morag sometimes goes to the castle, with her mother, to help in the laundry, when they need extra hands. The laundry is in a separate building, behind the castle, full of steam and soap and hot water. Morag folds sheets, irons tablecloths, and carries baskets. And sometimes, through the windows, she catches a glimpse of the life inside the castle. Footmen in red coats. Ladies in silk dresses. And once, a small figure in black, walking slowly along a corridor, with a little dog. The queen.

[long-pause]

In the evening, when the dinner is over, the castle grows quiet. The piper stops playing. The candles are put out, one by one. The servants go to their beds. And the queen sits alone, in her sitting room, writing her journal, by the light of a lamp, as she does every night of her life. Outside, the river murmurs in the darkness, and the stags roar on the hills. And the flag on the tower flaps softly, in the night wind.

[long-pause]

{img:queen-victoria-breakfast-tent-lawn-balmoral-morning-ladies-tea-outdoors}
A day at Balmoral, for the queen, follows a regular pattern. She gets up at about eight o'clock, when the piper plays under her window. She has breakfast, sometimes outside, in a little tent on the lawn, even when it is cold. Then she goes for a walk, in the grounds, with her dogs and a lady-in-waiting, and with John Brown, a few steps behind, in case she needs anything.

[long-pause]

After her walk, the queen works. Even at Balmoral, far from London, she is still the queen, and there is always work to do. Every day, red leather boxes arrive, by messenger, from London. They are called despatch boxes. Inside, there are letters, reports and documents from her ministers, which she must read and sign. She reads every one, carefully, and writes long letters back, giving her opinions. She is a very hardworking queen.

[pause]

In the afternoon, if the weather is good, or even if it is not very good, the queen goes out. She drives in her carriage, a little open carriage pulled by two ponies, along the roads and tracks of the estate, through the hills and glens. Or she rides on her pony, a sturdy Highland pony, led by John Brown. She goes for hours, sometimes, far up into the hills, to visit her favourite places, and to look at the views.

[pause]

{img:queen-victoria-open-carriage-ponies-glen-road-hills-autumn-afternoon}
She often takes her sketchbook, and her watercolours, and stops to paint. A view of the river. A mountain, with clouds on top. A cottage, with smoke from the chimney. A group of Highlanders. She is quite a good painter. She has painted all her life, since she was a girl. Her sketchbooks are full of the landscapes of the Highlands, and the faces of the people she met there.

[pause]

At about five o'clock, she comes back to the castle, for tea. Tea is a very important meal, at Balmoral. There are scones, oatcakes, shortbread, cakes, jam and cream. The queen loves cake. Sometimes, she has tea outside, in the hills, during her drive, at a little cottage, or a hut, where a fire has been lit, and a kettle boiled, by the ghillies. She calls these her little teas. They are her favourite moments of the day.

[long-pause]

{img:tea-in-highland-hut-queen-ladies-kettle-fire-scones-ghillies-outside}
Dinner is at about nine o'clock in the evening. It is a formal meal, in the dining room, with the queen, her children, her ladies-in-waiting, and any guests, such as government ministers, who have come to see her. Everyone must wear their best clothes. Nobody may speak, unless the queen speaks to them first. And the queen eats very fast. As soon as she has finished a course, everyone's plate is taken away, even if they have not finished. Some guests leave hungry.

[pause]

After dinner, the queen sits in the drawing room, and talks with her guests, for a little while. Or someone plays the piano, or reads aloud. Then, at about eleven, she goes to her sitting room, and writes her journal. She writes in it every single day, about everything she has done, everyone she has seen, and everything she has thought. She has done this since she was thirteen years old. By the end of her life, it will fill a hundred and twenty-two volumes.

[pause]

{img:queen-writing-journal-lamplight-evening-sitting-room-black-dress-alone}
On Sundays, the routine is different. There is no work, and no driving out. In the morning, the queen goes to church, at the little church in Crathie, near the castle, with her family and her household. In the afternoon, she walks, or visits people on the estate. And in the evening, there is a quiet dinner, and early to bed. The queen is very religious, in a simple way. She believes in God, and in kindness, and in duty.

[long-pause]

The people of the estate know the queen's routine very well. They know when she will drive past their cottages, and they stand at their doors, and bow, or curtsy, as she passes. Sometimes she stops, and talks to them. She asks about their families, their health, their children. She remembers everything they tell her. And the next time she passes, she asks about it again. The Highlanders are always amazed by her memory.

[long-pause]

{img:john-brown-highland-servant-kilt-tall-bearded-holding-pony-bridle-queen}
Wherever the queen goes, at Balmoral, there is one man always close by. A tall, strong, bearded Highlander, in a kilt, with a stern face and a deep voice. His name is John Brown. He is the queen's personal servant, her Highland attendant. And he is the only person in the world who speaks to the queen as if she were an ordinary woman.

[long-pause]

John Brown was born in 1826, near Balmoral, the son of a farmer. He worked as a stable boy, and then as a ghillie, on the estate. Prince Albert noticed him, and liked him, and chose him to be his own ghillie, on his stalking and walking expeditions. After Albert died, the queen remembered how much Albert had trusted Brown. And in 1864, she asked him to come to her, and lead her pony, and look after her, at Balmoral and wherever she went.

[pause]

John Brown is a big, strong man, about forty-two years old in 1868, with curly reddish-brown hair and a beard, going grey. He is very loyal to the queen. He is also very blunt. He says exactly what he thinks, to everyone, including the queen. He calls her woman, sometimes, which shocks everyone who hears it. If she is cold, he tells her to put on her shawl. If she is wrong, he tells her so. And she does not mind. She likes it.

[pause]

{img:john-brown-adjusting-queens-shawl-carriage-hills-wind-blunt-kindly}
The queen trusts John Brown completely. He is always there. He leads her pony on the hills. He sits on the box of her carriage. He carries her shawls, her cushions and her sketchbook. He makes the fire, and boils the kettle, for her little teas in the hills. He looks after her, like a protective older brother. And he is never afraid of anything, or anyone.

[pause]

Many people at court do not like John Brown. The queen's children are jealous of him. The ladies-in-waiting think he is rude. The ministers think he has too much influence. The newspapers make jokes about him, and even call the queen Mrs Brown. But the queen does not care. She says that he is the most honest, loyal and devoted servant she has ever had. And that he helped her, more than anyone, to bear her grief for Albert.

[long-pause]

{img:highland-servant-and-ghillie-talking-by-stable-door-evening-pipes-friends}
John Brown is a friend of Angus Grant, Morag's father. They grew up together, as boys, on the estate. They worked together, as ghillies, for Prince Albert. And sometimes, in the evenings, John Brown comes to the Grants' cottage, for a dram of whisky and a chat by the fire. In the cottage, he is not the queen's famous servant. He is just John, an old friend, who laughs loudly, tells stories, and teases the children.

[pause]

Morag is a little afraid of him, at first. He is so big, and so loud, and so important. But one evening, he sits down beside her by the fire, and asks her what she is reading. It is a book of poems, by Robert Burns. And John Brown, to her amazement, begins to recite one of the poems, from memory, in his deep voice, in broad Scots. Morag stares at him. And from that day on, she is not afraid of him any more.

[pause]

{img:big-highlander-reciting-poem-fireside-cottage-girl-listening-amazed}
Brown tells the children stories about the queen. Not secrets. He never tells secrets. But funny little stories. How the queen once got her feet wet, crossing a stream, and laughed about it. How she loves cake, and always has a second piece. How she once got lost in the fog, on the hill, and he had to find the way home by feeling the path with his feet. The children laugh. They have never thought of the queen as a real person, who gets wet feet, and gets lost.

[long-pause]

John Brown served the queen for nineteen years, until he passed away in 1883. The queen was heartbroken. She said that she had lost her best and truest friend. She put up a statue of him, at Balmoral, and kept his room exactly as he had left it, as she had with Albert. Many years later, in 1997, a film was made about their friendship, called Mrs Brown. It showed how a lonely widow and a blunt Highland servant became the closest of friends.

[long-pause]

{img:leaves-from-the-journal-book-1868-cover-highlands-victorian-bookshop-window}
At the beginning of 1868, something unusual happened. The queen published a book. It was called Leaves from the Journal of Our Life in the Highlands. It contained parts of her own journal, the diary she had kept, about her happy times at Balmoral and in the Highlands, with Albert, from 1848 to 1861. And it was a sensation.

[long-pause]

Nobody had ever seen anything like it. A queen, writing about her own life, in her own simple words, for ordinary people to read. Not about politics, or wars, or ceremonies. But about picnics, and walks, and pony rides, and the weather, and the people she met. About getting wet in the rain, and lost in the fog, and climbing mountains, and eating oatcakes in a cottage. About her love for Albert, and for the Highlands.

[pause]

The book was a huge success. It sold more than a hundred thousand copies, in a few months. Everyone was reading it. In the cities, in the villages, in the drawing rooms and the kitchens. People were charmed by it. They saw a different side of their queen. Not the stern widow in black, but a happy young wife, enjoying simple pleasures in the countryside. Many people who had criticised her began to feel warmly toward her again.

[pause]

{img:victorian-family-reading-queens-book-parlour-fireside-1868-smiling}
In the book, the queen writes about many of the people of Balmoral. The ghillies, the gamekeepers, the farmers and their wives. She writes about John Brown, and about Angus Grant, and many others, by name. She describes their kindness, their skill, their loyalty and their good humour. For the people of the estate, it is a strange feeling, to be written about, in a book that the whole country is reading.

[pause]

Angus Grant is mentioned in the book, twice. Once, on an expedition to the hills, when he carried the queen's sketching things, and showed her the way through a bog. And once, when he caught a big salmon, and Prince Albert was delighted. Kirsty has a copy of the book, which the queen gave to every family on the estate. She keeps it on the shelf above the fire, wrapped in a cloth. And sometimes, in the evenings, Morag reads it aloud.

[long-pause]

{img:girl-reading-book-aloud-cottage-fireside-father-listening-proud-shy}
When Morag reads the parts about her father, Angus looks at the floor, and goes red, and says that the queen was too kind. But the children are very proud. Ewan tells everyone at school that his father is in the queen's book. And the grandmother, who cannot read English, asks Morag to read the parts about her son again and again, and nods, and smiles, and says that he was always a good boy.

[pause]

The book is written in a simple, lively way. The queen describes the views, the weather, the food and the people, with great enthusiasm. She uses many exclamation marks. She calls things delightful, charming, lovely and beautiful. Some sophisticated people in London laughed at the book, and said it was too simple. One famous writer made fun of it, privately. But ordinary people loved it, because it was honest and warm.

[pause]

{img:queen-and-albert-young-picnic-highland-hill-memory-sketch-style-1850s}
Reading the book, you can feel how happy the queen was, in the Highlands, with Albert. She writes about their first visit to Balmoral, and how they fell in love with it. About the expeditions they made together, on ponies, to the tops of the mountains, and the lochs in the hills. About the evenings by the fire, and the Highland dances, and the singing of the ghillies. It is full of light and joy. And knowing what happened later, it is also very sad.

[long-pause]

Many years later, in 1884, the queen published a second book, More Leaves, about her life in the Highlands after Albert's death. It was dedicated to the memory of John Brown. Both books are still read today. And they are still the best way to understand what Balmoral meant to Queen Victoria. Not a palace, or a duty. But a home. A place of happiness, of memory, and of healing, in the hills she loved.

[long-pause]

{img:princess-louise-princess-beatrice-prince-leopold-1868-family-group-balmoral}
Queen Victoria had nine children. Four sons and five daughters. By 1868, most of them are grown up. Some are married, with children of their own, and live in other countries. The eldest daughter, Vicky, is married to the crown prince of Prussia, in Germany. The eldest son, Bertie, the Prince of Wales, is married to a Danish princess, and lives in London. But three of the younger children are still with their mother, at Balmoral, in the autumn of 1868.

[long-pause]

Princess Louise is twenty. She is the most beautiful and the most artistic of the queen's daughters. She loves sculpture, painting and drawing, and wants to be an artist. She is clever, lively and independent, and sometimes argues with her mother. She is a little bored at Balmoral, with its routines and its cold rooms. But she loves sketching the hills, and the people, and she is very good at it. She will later become a real sculptor.

[pause]

Prince Leopold is fifteen. He is clever, kind and gentle, and loves books and music. But he is often ill. He has a disease called haemophilia, which means that his blood does not clot properly. A small cut or bump can be very dangerous for him. So his mother keeps him very close, and very protected. He is not allowed to play rough games, or ride fast, or climb. He finds this very frustrating. He wants to be like other boys.

[pause]

{img:young-prince-reading-by-window-balmoral-quiet-bookish-tartan-room}
Princess Beatrice is eleven. She is the youngest of the queen's children, and her mother's favourite. The queen calls her Baby, even though she is eleven. She is shy, quiet and sweet-natured. She goes everywhere with her mother, on her walks and drives. She will stay with her mother all her life, as her companion and secretary, even after she marries. She loves music, and plays the piano for her mother in the evenings.

[pause]

The royal children have a very different life from the children of the estate. They have governesses and tutors, who teach them languages, history, music, drawing and good manners. They have ponies, and dogs, and fine clothes. But they also have strict rules, and very little freedom. They must always behave perfectly. They must always do what their mother says. And they are often lonely, with no friends of their own age.

[long-pause]

{img:princess-and-highland-girl-meeting-river-path-shy-smiles-autumn}
One afternoon, Princess Beatrice, walking with her governess near the river, meets Morag, who is collecting firewood in the woods. The two girls look at each other, shyly. The governess wants to walk on. But Beatrice asks Morag her name, and what she is doing. Morag curtsies, as she has been taught, and answers. Beatrice asks if she can help. And for a few minutes, the princess and the ghillie's daughter gather sticks together, by the river, while the governess watches, disapprovingly.

[pause]

Beatrice asks Morag about her life. About school, and her family, and her little brother, and his dog. Morag tells her. And Beatrice listens, wide-eyed. She has never been to a school. She has never had a friend of her own age to play with. She has never collected firewood. She asks if Morag is happy. Morag says yes, very happy. Beatrice is quiet for a moment. Then the governess calls her, and she must go.

[pause]

{img:royal-girl-waving-goodbye-walking-away-governess-woods-path-autumn-light}
After that, whenever Beatrice drives past the Grants' cottage, in her mother's carriage, she waves to Morag. And Morag waves back. And once, at Christmas, a small parcel arrives at the cottage, from the castle, addressed to Miss Morag Grant. Inside, there is a little book of Scottish fairy tales, with a card, signed in careful handwriting. From Beatrice. Morag keeps it all her life.

[long-pause]

The queen was a loving mother, in her way. But she was also demanding and controlling. Her children loved her, but were also afraid of her. Some of them found it hard to live their own lives, under her watchful eye. But at Balmoral, in the hills, things were a little easier. There was more freedom, more fresh air, and more fun, than in the palaces of London and Windsor. And the children, too, came to love the Highlands, as their parents had.

[long-pause]

{img:red-stag-roaring-misty-highland-glen-autumn-rut-golden-bracken}
In the autumn, the hills around Balmoral are full of a strange, deep sound. It echoes across the glens, in the early morning and the evening. A long, rough, roaring call, like a cow, or a lion, far away. It is the sound of the red stags, in the rut. Every autumn, the male deer roar, to show how strong they are, and to call the females. It is one of the great sounds of the Highlands.

[pause]

The red deer are the largest wild animals in Britain. A big stag can stand as tall as a man's shoulder, with great branching antlers. In the summer, the deer live high in the hills, in the cool air. In the autumn, they come down a little lower, and the stags gather their groups of hinds. They fight, sometimes, clashing their antlers together. But mostly they just roar, and stare at each other, and the weaker one walks away.

[long-pause]

{img:ghillie-and-gentleman-stalking-deer-heather-hillside-crouching-telescope}
For the Victorian gentlemen who visited the Highlands, deer stalking was a great sport. It was very popular, and very fashionable. Prince Albert loved it. He went out stalking almost every day, in the autumn, with his ghillies. And many rich men bought or rented Highland estates, just for the deer stalking. It brought money and work to the Highlands. But it also changed the land, and moved people from it.

[pause]

A stalk is a long, slow, quiet business. The stalker, the gentleman, goes out with a ghillie, who knows the hills and the deer. They walk for hours, across the moors. The ghillie looks through his long brass telescope, searching the hillsides for deer. When he sees a good stag, they begin to creep toward it, very slowly. Always against the wind, so the deer cannot smell them. Crawling through the heather, and the bogs, and along the burns.

[pause]

Angus Grant is one of the best stalkers on the estate. He can see a deer on a far hillside, where other men see only rocks and heather. He knows which way the wind will turn, and where the deer will go. He can move across the hill without a sound. The gentlemen who go out with him say he is like a fox. Angus says it is just practice. He has been doing it since he was a boy, with his own father.

[pause]

{img:highland-pony-carrying-deer-down-hill-ghillie-leading-evening}
A stalk can take all day. Sometimes, after hours of creeping, the wind changes, and the deer smell them, and run away. Then the gentleman goes home with nothing, wet and tired. Angus does not mind this. He thinks the deer should have a fair chance. When a stag is taken, it is brought down the hill on the back of a strong Highland pony, which is called a garron. And there is venison for the castle kitchen, and for the families on the estate.

[pause]

The queen herself did not stalk. But she liked to watch, through a telescope, from the hillside. And sometimes she made sketches of the deer and the hills. Her daughter Louise went out with the stalkers once or twice, and drew the ghillies resting in the heather, with their telescopes and their dogs. Those drawings still exist, today.

[long-pause]

{img:hinds-and-young-deer-grazing-quiet-glen-mist-lifting-peaceful}
Most of the time, the deer are left in peace. They graze in the glens, in the early morning mist, and lie in the heather in the afternoon. The young deer, born in early summer, follow their mothers everywhere. The children of the estate see them often, on their walks. Ewan and his dog Bran once came around a corner, by the burn, and found a hind and her calf drinking from the water. They all looked at each other, for a long moment. Then the deer turned and trotted calmly away, up the hill. Bran did not even bark. He just watched them go, with his ears up.

[pause]

In the evenings, when the light fades, the roaring of the stags comes down the glens, again and again. In the cottages, the families hear it, as they sit by the fire. It is the sound of the autumn, in the Highlands. And of the wild hills, all around, in the dark.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-royal-picnic-highland-hillside-ponies-tartan-blankets-loch-view}
One of the queen's greatest pleasures at Balmoral is going on expeditions. Long drives and rides into the hills, to see the views, the lochs, and the waterfalls. She calls them her great expeditions. With Albert, she went on many of them, sometimes riding all day on ponies, over the mountains, to places very few visitors had ever seen. Now, she goes on smaller ones, in her carriage, with her daughters and her ladies.

[pause]

On a fine autumn morning, word comes from the castle to the ghillies' cottages. The queen is going to Loch Muick today, for a picnic. Angus and John Brown will go with her. The ponies are saddled, and the carriage is made ready. The kitchen staff pack baskets with food. Cold chicken, and ham, and bread, and cake, and fruit, and bottles of wine and tea. And of course, plenty of warm shawls and rugs, in case it gets cold, which it usually does.

[long-pause]

The little procession sets off, along the road up the valley. The queen and Princess Louise in the open carriage, with a lady in waiting. John Brown sits on the box, next to the driver. Angus walks beside the ponies, which carry the baskets. The road winds up the valley, beside the river, through the woods of birch and pine, glowing gold and green in the autumn sunshine. The air is clear and cool, and smells of pine.

[pause]

{img:carriage-on-highland-road-birch-woods-autumn-gold-river-mountains}
After an hour or so, the road ends, and they continue on foot and on ponies, up a rough track, into the hills. The queen rides a quiet grey pony, led by John Brown. The views open up around them, the great brown and purple hills, rolling away in every direction, under the wide sky. They stop to rest by a waterfall, and the queen takes out her sketchbook, and draws it, while the others wait.

[pause]

At last, they come over a ridge, and below them lies Loch Muick. A long, narrow loch, deep and dark, between steep hills. The water reflects the sky, and the clouds, and the hills. It is very quiet, and very beautiful. There is no house in sight, no road, no sound but the wind, and the call of a bird far away. The queen sits for a long time, just looking. She says it is one of her favourite places in the whole world.

[long-pause]

{img:ghillies-lighting-fire-kettle-picnic-lochside-servants-busy-mist}
The ghillies make a fire of heather and sticks, by the shore, and boil a kettle for tea. The rugs are spread on the grass, and the food is laid out. The queen and her daughter and her lady eat, sitting on the rugs, wrapped in their shawls. The ghillies eat a little further away, sitting on the rocks. Angus has bread and cheese, and a piece of cold mutton, and a drink of water from the loch.

[pause]

On the hilltops around Balmoral, there are many stone cairns. Piles of stones, built to mark special events. The queen and Albert built one together, on the hill of Craig Gowan, the day they took possession of Balmoral, in 1852. Everyone in the household added a stone to it, and there was dancing and whisky afterward. Since then, more cairns have been built, for the marriages of the royal children, and other events. There is a big one, in the shape of a pyramid, in memory of Albert.

[pause]

{img:stone-cairn-pyramid-hilltop-balmoral-heather-wide-view-autumn-sky}
On the way home, the weather changes, as it so often does in the Highlands. Clouds come over the hills, and soon there is a fine, soaking rain. The queen does not mind. She is used to it. She pulls her shawl tighter and laughs. Princess Louise is less pleased, and grumbles. By the time they get back to the castle, in the dusk, they are all wet through. But the queen writes in her journal that evening that it was a delightful day.

[pause]

And Angus walks home to his cottage in the rain, to his warm fire, and his supper, and his family. He takes off his wet boots, and sits by the fire, and Kirsty brings him a bowl of hot soup. The children ask him about the day. He tells them about the loch, and the waterfall, and the picnic, and the rain. And Ewan asks if the queen ate cake. And Angus says yes, two pieces.

[long-pause]

{img:braemar-gathering-1860s-field-crowd-tartan-pipers-hills-behind}
In the late summer and early autumn, there is a great event in the valley. The Highland Games at Braemar, a village a few miles up the river from Balmoral. They are called the Braemar Gathering. People come from all over the district, and from far away, to watch. The ghillies, the farmers, the shepherds, the gentlemen from the estates, and their families. And sometimes, the queen herself.

[pause]

The Highland Games are very old. People say they began hundreds of years ago, when the Scottish kings held contests to find the strongest and fastest men, to be their soldiers and messengers. In the nineteenth century, they became popular again, partly because of the queen's love of the Highlands. She first came to the Braemar Gathering in 1848, the year she first came to Balmoral. And the royal family has supported it ever since.

[long-pause]

On the morning of the games, the whole valley is on the move. Families walk along the road to Braemar, in their best clothes. The men in their kilts, the women in their shawls, the children running ahead. Carts full of people, and riders on ponies. There are stalls selling food and drink, and ribbons, and toys. And everywhere the sound of bagpipes, as the pipers tune up and practise, in little groups, here and there.

[pause]

{img:caber-toss-strong-highlander-kilt-lifting-tree-trunk-crowd-watching}
The games themselves take place on a big flat field, with the hills all around. There are many different contests. The most famous is tossing the caber. The caber is a long, heavy pole, made from a tree trunk, as long as two men. A strong man lifts it upright, holding the bottom end in his hands. He runs forward with it, and then throws it, so that it flips over, end over end, and lands pointing straight away from him. It is very hard, and the crowd cheers when it is done well.

[pause]

There are other contests of strength. Throwing a heavy stone, as far as possible. Throwing a heavy iron hammer, on a wooden handle, swinging it around the head. And tug of war, between teams of men, pulling on a thick rope, with their heels dug into the grass. There are races, too, on foot. And the hill race, which is the hardest of all, straight up a steep hill and down again. The runners come back red-faced and muddy, and gasping.

[long-pause]

{img:highland-dancers-swords-on-ground-piper-playing-judges-watching}
And there is music and dancing. The pipers play in competition, one by one, walking slowly up and down, in front of the judges, playing the old tunes. Marches, and laments, and reels. The dancers dance the Highland fling, and the sword dance, stepping lightly between two crossed swords on the ground, without touching them. It takes great skill, and years of practice.

[pause]

This year, Angus is in the tug of war, with the other men from the Balmoral estate. They are against the men from the Invercauld estate, their neighbours. It is a long, hard pull. The two teams strain and heave, and the rope barely moves, for a long time. The crowd shouts and cheers. Kirsty and the children shout loudest of all. Then, slowly, the Balmoral men begin to win. Step by step, they pull the others over the line. And Ewan jumps up and down, and Bran barks, and Morag hugs her mother.

[pause]

{img:tug-of-war-teams-straining-rope-kilts-crowd-cheering-highland-field}
In the afternoon, the queen arrives, in her carriage, with Princess Louise and Princess Beatrice. She sits in a special pavilion, and watches the games for an hour or two. Everyone stands and cheers when she arrives. The prizes are given out, and some of the winners are presented to her. She speaks to each of them for a moment, kindly. Then she drives home, before the evening.

[pause]

The games go on until late in the afternoon. Then the families begin the long walk home, tired and happy, in the golden evening light. The children are sleepy, and Ewan has to be carried part of the way, on his father's shoulders. The hills glow purple and gold in the setting sun. And far behind them, the sound of a lone piper, still playing, drifts across the valley, as the light fades.

[pause]

The Braemar Gathering still takes place today, every year, on the first Saturday in September. And the royal family still goes to watch, just as Queen Victoria did, more than a hundred and fifty years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:highland-harvest-field-oats-stooks-families-working-autumn-sun}
In September and October, it is harvest time in the valley. On the small farms along the river, the families gather in their crops. Oats and barley, mostly, and potatoes, and turnips. The summer in the Highlands is short, and the harvest comes late. Everyone must work quickly, while the weather is dry. Men, women and children all help in the fields, from early morning until the light fades.

[pause]

The oats are cut by hand, with a sickle or a scythe. The men cut, and the women follow behind, gathering the stalks into bundles, and tying them with a twist of straw. The bundles, called sheaves, are stood up together in little groups, called stooks, to dry in the wind and sun. The fields are dotted with stooks, golden in the autumn light. The children help, and play, and run between them.

[long-pause]

When the last sheaf of the harvest is cut, there is a little ceremony. The last sheaf is called the maiden, if the harvest is early, or the old woman, the cailleach, if it is late. It is dressed up in ribbons, and carried home, and hung up in the kitchen until next year. It is an old custom, and nobody quite remembers why. But it brings good luck, the grandmother says. And it means that the hard work is over, for another year.

[pause]

{img:harvest-home-dance-barn-lanterns-fiddler-families-celebrating}
Then there is the harvest home. A big supper and a dance, in the barn of the biggest farm. Everyone comes. There is food, and whisky, and music, and dancing until late in the night. The fiddler plays reels and strathspeys, and the floor shakes with the stamping of feet. The children fall asleep in the corner, on piles of coats. It is one of the happiest nights of the year.

[pause]

At Balmoral, there is another kind of party, in the autumn. The ghillies' ball. The queen gives it every year, for the servants and the workers of the estate, and their families. It is held in the castle itself, in the big ballroom. Everyone comes in their best clothes. The ghillies in their kilts, the maids in their best dresses, the farmers and their wives. And the royal family comes too, and dances with them.

[long-pause]

{img:ghillies-ball-balmoral-ballroom-royal-family-dancing-reel-servants-kilts}
For Kirsty and Angus, the ghillies' ball is a great event. Kirsty has made herself a new dress, of dark blue wool, and Morag has helped her to sew it. Angus wears his best kilt and jacket, with silver buttons, and his sporran. Morag is old enough to go this year, for the first time. She is very excited, and very nervous. She has been practising her dancing in the kitchen for weeks.

[pause]

The ballroom is lit by hundreds of candles, and decorated with tartan, and antlers, and heather. The pipers play, and the dancing begins. The reels, the strathspeys, the eightsome reel, and the Highland schottische. The queen does not dance any more, since Albert's death, but she watches, and smiles, and taps her foot. Her children dance with the ghillies and the maids. And Prince Leopold, who is not allowed to dance, sits beside his mother and watches longingly.

[pause]

{img:young-girl-dancing-reel-first-ball-candlelight-shy-happy-tartan-sash}
Morag dances with her father first, and then with one of the young ghillies, and then, to her great surprise, with Prince Arthur, the queen's third son, who is visiting. He is very polite, and dances well. Morag is so nervous that she nearly forgets the steps. But she remembers, just in time. Afterward, she cannot stop smiling, all the way home through the dark.

[pause]

The ball goes on until late. Then the families walk home under the stars, with lanterns, through the cold autumn night. The music still rings in their ears. In the cottage, Kirsty and Angus sit by the last of the fire, for a moment, before going to bed. Kirsty says it was a lovely evening. And Angus agrees. Then they bank the fire, and blow out the candle, and go to sleep.

[long-pause]

{img:crathie-church-small-old-kirk-1860s-river-dee-hills-sunday-morning}
On Sundays, everyone on the estate goes to church. The little church at Crathie, just across the river from the castle. It is a plain, simple building, of grey stone, with a small bell tower, and a graveyard around it, full of old stones. It is a church of the Church of Scotland, the Presbyterian church, which is very different from the Church of England, with its bishops and its grand ceremonies.

[pause]

The queen goes to church at Crathie every Sunday, when she is at Balmoral. This surprised and upset some people in England, at first. The queen is the head of the Church of England. Should she really go to a Scottish Presbyterian church? But the queen did not mind. She liked the simple services, and the plain words, and the sincere preaching. She said she felt closer to God there than in many grand cathedrals.

[long-pause]

On Sunday morning, the families walk to church, along the road beside the river. In their best clothes, the men in their dark coats, the women in their shawls and bonnets. The children are scrubbed clean, and told to behave. Bran stays at home, and howls when they leave. Angus and Kirsty walk slowly, talking quietly with the neighbours. The bell rings out across the valley, calling them in.

[pause]

{img:plain-kirk-interior-wooden-pews-congregation-minister-pulpit-high}
Inside, the church is very simple. White walls, plain wooden pews, and a tall wooden pulpit, where the minister stands to preach. There are no statues, no pictures, no candles, no stained glass. The royal family has its own pew, in a gallery at the side. The families of the estate sit below, in their usual places. Everyone stands when the queen comes in. Then the service begins.

[pause]

The service is long. There are prayers, and readings from the Bible, and psalms. The psalms are sung without any organ or instruments, because the Church of Scotland did not allow them in those days. A man called the precentor stands at the front, and sings each line first, and the congregation sings it after him. It is a slow, strong, solemn sound, rising and falling, filling the little church. Some of the old people sing the psalms in Gaelic, quietly, in their own words.

[pause]

Then the minister preaches. The sermon goes on for a long time, often for an hour or more. The minister talks about God, and sin, and the duty of a good Christian. The children find it very hard to sit still. Ewan counts the beams in the roof, and the buttons on the coat of the man in front. Morag tries to listen, but her mind drifts away, to the ball, and to Prince Arthur. The grandmother listens to every word, nodding.

[long-pause]

{img:highland-graveyard-old-stones-church-autumn-leaves-quiet-family-visiting}
After the service, the families stand in the graveyard for a while, talking to friends and neighbours. This is an important part of the Sunday. They share news, about the harvest, the weather, the animals, the births and the weddings, and the people who have passed away. The young people look at each other shyly. The children run between the stones, until they are told to stop.

[pause]

Angus and Kirsty visit the grave of Angus's father, who passed away five years ago. He was a ghillie too, at Balmoral, before the castle was built. He knew Prince Albert well. They stand together for a moment, by the plain grey stone, with its carved name. Kirsty puts a little bunch of heather on the grass. Then they walk home together, slowly, in the autumn sunshine.

[pause]

{img:sunday-afternoon-cottage-family-resting-bible-reading-quiet-window-light}
Sunday afternoon is quiet. No work is done on the Sabbath, in the Highlands. No digging, no sewing, no washing, no games. Not even a walk, just for pleasure, in some strict families. The day is for rest and for God. Angus reads the Bible aloud, by the window. The grandmother dozes in her chair. The children sit quietly, and look at books, or out of the window. Sometimes they are allowed to walk to the river, if they promise to be good.

[pause]

The church at Crathie was rebuilt in the 1890s, in a grander style, with stones given by the royal family. The queen laid the foundation stone herself. It is still the church where the royal family goes, every Sunday, when they are at Balmoral. And in the churchyard, the old stones are still there, under the trees.

[long-pause]

{img:queen-victoria-visiting-highland-cottage-old-woman-doorway-gift-basket}
One of the things the queen loves most at Balmoral is visiting the cottages of the people on the estate. Especially the old women, and the poor, and the sick. She goes in her carriage, or on her pony, with one of her daughters or a lady in waiting, and John Brown. She brings gifts. A warm petticoat, or a shawl, or tea, or a little money. And she sits and talks with them, for a while.

[pause]

In her journal, she wrote about these visits often. She liked the old women especially. They were poor, but proud and dignified. They spoke to her plainly and simply, not like the courtiers in London. Some of them only spoke Gaelic, and someone had to translate. The queen was always touched by their kindness, their faith, and their patience in hard times.

[long-pause]

One afternoon, the queen comes to the cottage of the Grants. Kirsty has had a message in the morning, and the whole family has been cleaning all day. The floor is swept, the table scrubbed, the pots polished. The grandmother is in her best black dress and her white cap. The children are washed and brushed. Bran has been shut in the byre, so that he does not jump up at the queen. He is not happy about it.

[pause]

{img:highland-cottage-interior-royal-visitor-seated-by-fire-grandmother-curtsy}
The carriage stops outside, and the queen comes in, with Princess Beatrice. Everyone curtsies and bows. The cottage seems very small, with the queen inside. She sits in the best chair, by the fire, and Beatrice stands beside her, and smiles at Morag. The queen asks after the grandmother's health, and the children's schooling, and Angus's work. Kirsty answers, nervously at first, then more easily.

[pause]

The queen speaks to the grandmother, who answers in Gaelic. Morag translates. The grandmother tells the queen that she remembers the old days, before the castle, when she was a girl, and the old laird lived at Balmoral, in a smaller house. And she says that the valley is a better place now. More work, and more money, and better houses. And she thanks the queen for her kindness to the people. The queen is very moved.

[long-pause]

The queen has brought a gift for the grandmother. A thick, warm shawl, of soft grey wool. And for the children, a little book each, and a bag of sweets. And for Kirsty, a pound of tea, which is a great luxury. Then she sees Ewan looking at the door of the byre, and asks what is in there. Ewan tells her, very seriously, that it is his dog Bran, who is not allowed to meet the queen. The queen laughs, and says she would like very much to meet Bran.

[pause]

{img:small-terrier-meeting-queen-victoria-cottage-boy-holding-dog-laughter}
So Bran is brought out, wriggling with excitement, and held tightly by Ewan. The queen pats him on the head, and says he is a fine little dog. She loves dogs, and has many of her own. Bran licks her glove. Ewan is so proud he can hardly speak. For the rest of his life, he will tell the story of the day his dog met the queen.

[pause]

The visit lasts about twenty minutes. Then the queen rises, and says goodbye, and goes out to her carriage. The family stands at the door and watches her drive away, down the track, between the birch trees. Then Kirsty sits down suddenly, on the bench, and laughs, with relief. The grandmother wraps herself in her new shawl, and says it is the warmest thing she has ever owned.

[pause]

{img:carriage-driving-away-birch-track-family-at-cottage-door-waving-autumn}
These visits were not always easy for the people. Some of them found it strange and uncomfortable, to have the queen in their little homes. But most of them were pleased, and proud. And the queen's kindness was real. She remembered the names of the people, and their children, and their troubles. And when someone on the estate was ill or in need, she sent help.

[long-pause]

{img:glassalt-shiel-lodge-loch-muick-shore-steep-hills-waterfall-1868}
In the autumn of 1868, something new was finished, on the shore of Loch Muick. A small house, built of grey granite, standing alone by the water, at the foot of a steep hillside, beside a waterfall. It was called the Glassalt Shiel. A shiel is an old Scottish word for a hut, or a shelter in the hills. But this was more than a hut. It was a little lodge, with a dozen rooms, built for the queen.

[pause]

After Albert died, the queen found it hard to stay at some of the places where they had been happy together. There had been an older little house by Loch Muick, called the Hut, where they had often stayed together. Now, she wanted somewhere new. A place of her own, far from everyone, where she could be quiet, and remember, and rest. So she had the Glassalt Shiel built, at the far end of the loch, where the road ends.

[long-pause]

The Glassalt Shiel was very remote. No road went there, only a rough track, and the loch itself. The queen called it the widow's house. She came there for a few days at a time, with a very small household. A lady, a maid, a cook, and John Brown, and a few ghillies. There were no visitors, no ministers, no messengers. Just the loch, and the hills, and the sound of the waterfall.

[pause]

{img:victorian-lodge-sitting-room-simple-fire-window-loch-view-dusk-quiet}
In October 1868, the queen came to stay at the Glassalt Shiel for the first time. There was a little housewarming party. The ghillies and servants gathered in the kitchen, and there was whisky and toddy, and dancing, and singing. The queen watched for a while, and then went to bed. Angus Grant was there, to help with the boats, and he danced a reel with the cook, and sang an old Gaelic song, which made the queen's maid cry.

[pause]

At the Glassalt Shiel, the queen lived very simply. She walked by the loch, and sketched, and wrote her letters and her journal. She went out on the water in a little rowing boat, with John Brown at the oars. She sat by the fire in the evenings, and read, and looked out at the dark loch, and the stars. She said she felt nearer to Albert there than anywhere else.

[long-pause]

{img:rowing-boat-on-dark-loch-muick-two-figures-misty-evening-hills}
The loch is very beautiful, but also wild and lonely. The hills rise steeply from the water, dark and silent. In bad weather, the wind comes down the glen and whips the water into waves. The rain beats against the windows, for days. The mist comes down and hides everything. But on a calm evening, the loch is like a mirror, perfectly still, reflecting the hills and the sky, the golden light, and the first stars.

[pause]

The ghillies who worked at the Glassalt Shiel lived in a small cottage nearby. In the evenings, after the queen had gone to bed, they sat outside, by the water, smoking their pipes, and talking quietly in Gaelic. Sometimes, someone played a tune on a fiddle, or a chanter. The sound drifted out over the loch, in the dark. And far up the hill, a stag roared, and another answered him.

[pause]

{img:night-loch-muick-stars-reflection-small-lit-window-lodge-silhouette}
The queen came to the Glassalt Shiel many times over the years. It became one of her favourite places. She wrote about it lovingly in her journal, and in her second book. She called it a perfect place of rest. After her death, her family used it only rarely, and the house was later closed up. It still stands today, by the loch, among the trees. Walkers can pass it on the path along the shore, and stop, and imagine the queen, sitting by the window, looking out at the water.

[pause]

The waterfall still tumbles down the hillside beside it, into the loch, as it always has. And in the autumn, the stags still roar in the hills around. And the loch is still, and dark, and quiet, as the evening falls.

[long-pause]

{img:ballater-railway-station-1868-steam-train-arriving-platform-victorian}
In 1866, the railway came to Ballater, a small town a few miles down the river from Balmoral. Before that, the queen and her household had to travel the last part of the journey from the south by carriage, on the bumpy roads, for many hours. Now, the train brought them almost all the way. It was a great change, for the queen, and for the whole valley.

[pause]

The royal journey from Windsor to Balmoral is a long one. It takes about a day and a night, on the train. The queen has her own royal train, with a special saloon carriage for herself. It has soft chairs, and a bed, and a little sitting room, with curtains and carpets. But the queen does not like trains to go fast. She is nervous of speed. She insists that the royal train never goes faster than about forty miles an hour. And it stops at night, so that she can sleep, and in the morning, for her breakfast.

[long-pause]

{img:royal-train-saloon-interior-victorian-plush-chairs-curtains-window-hills}
When the royal train arrives at Ballater, there is always a big welcome. The station is decorated with flags and flowers. The local people gather on the platform, and in the street, to see the queen. A guard of honour of soldiers stands in line. The queen steps down from the train, in her black dress and bonnet, and gets into her carriage. And the procession drives off, along the road to Balmoral, past the cheering people.

[pause]

The railway has changed life in the valley in many ways. It has brought tourists. People come from Aberdeen, and from England, to see the Highlands, and the queen's castle. They walk in the hills, and fish in the river, and stay in the new hotels in Ballater. The town has grown, with new shops and houses. And there is more work, and more money, for the people of the valley.

[pause]

{img:victorian-tourists-walking-ballater-street-shops-hills-summer-hats}
For the families on the estate, the railway means that things from the cities are easier to get. Tea, sugar, cloth, tools, lamps, newspapers. Letters arrive faster. People can travel to Aberdeen and back in a day, to visit family, or go to market. The young people can go away more easily, to find work in the cities, or to emigrate to Canada or America. Some of them never come back.

[pause]

Morag has been to Aberdeen on the train once. With her mother, to visit her aunt. She will never forget it. The noise of the engine, the smoke, the speed, the fields and trees rushing past the window. And the city itself, with its tall grey granite buildings, and its crowded streets, and its harbour full of ships. She liked it, but she was glad to come home, to the quiet valley.

[long-pause]

The queen would not allow the railway to come any closer to Balmoral than Ballater. There was a plan to extend it further up the valley, to Braemar. But the queen did not want trains near her castle. She wanted Balmoral to stay quiet, and private, and peaceful. So the line ended at Ballater, and the last few miles were always by road.

[pause]

{img:old-railway-line-ballater-disused-path-trees-autumn-quiet-walkers}
The railway to Ballater was used for almost a hundred years. Kings and queens, prime ministers and foreign visitors, all arrived at Ballater station on their way to Balmoral. But in 1966, the line was closed. Today, the old railway track is a footpath, for walking and cycling, along the river. And the old station has been restored, with a copy of the queen's royal carriage, so that visitors can see how she travelled, all those years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:first-snow-on-lochnagar-peaks-autumn-birches-yellow-below-balmoral}
By the end of October, the autumn is turning toward winter. The birch trees have lost most of their golden leaves. The bracken on the hills has turned brown and dry. The days are getting short, and the nights long and cold. One morning, the families wake up to see the first snow, on the top of Lochnagar. A thin white cap, shining in the morning sun, high above the brown hills.

[pause]

The queen usually stays at Balmoral until the middle or end of November. Then she goes south, to Windsor, for the winter, and Christmas. Her ministers in London are always glad when she leaves Balmoral. It is so far away, and so hard to reach. When there is important government business, a minister must travel all the way up to Scotland, for a day or two, to see her. Most of them hate it. They say Balmoral is too cold, too dull, and too far.

[long-pause]

{img:servants-packing-trunks-castle-corridor-autumn-departure-busy}
Before the queen leaves, the castle is full of activity. The servants pack the trunks, and the boxes, and the baskets. Clothes, books, papers, pictures, and all the things the queen needs for her journey and for the winter. The horses and carriages are made ready. The dogs are brushed. There are farewells to be said, and gifts to be given to the people of the estate, before the long winter.

[pause]

On the last day, the queen drives around the estate, to say goodbye. She stops at some of the cottages, and speaks to the old people, and the families. She gives the ghillies their presents. A warm coat, or a new pair of boots, or a silver brooch. Then she drives to Ballater, and boards the royal train. And the people of the valley stand along the road, and wave, as she goes by.

[pause]

After the queen leaves, the valley is very quiet. Most of the household goes south with her. The castle is closed up for the winter, the furniture covered with sheets, and the shutters closed. Only a few servants stay, to look after it. The ghillies and the gamekeepers go back to their winter work. Mending fences, cutting wood, feeding the deer, and keeping watch over the estate.

[pause]

{img:highland-cottage-winter-evening-peat-stack-snow-hills-smoke-chimney}
For the families in the cottages, the winter is a long, hard time. The snow comes, and the frost, and the cold winds from the north. Sometimes the snow is so deep that the roads are blocked for days, and nobody can get in or out of the valley. The families must have enough food stored up, and enough peat and wood for the fire, to last through the winter.

[long-pause]

The Grants are well prepared. The peat stack beside the cottage is tall and neat. There are sacks of oatmeal and potatoes in the store, and salted meat, and cheese, and butter. There is a barrel of salt herring, from the east coast. The cow is in the byre, and the hens are safe in their little house. And the roof has been mended, with new thatch, so that it will not leak.

[pause]

{img:family-by-fire-winter-night-spinning-wheel-grandmother-children-stories}
In the long winter evenings, the family sits around the fire. Kirsty sews and mends, and the grandmother spins wool on her spinning wheel. Angus mends his boots, or carves a new handle for a tool. Morag reads aloud from the queen's book, or the Bible. Ewan plays with Bran on the floor. And the grandmother tells the old stories, in Gaelic, of the fairies, and the giants, and the heroes of long ago. The fire glows, and the wind blows outside, and the snow falls softly on the roof.

[pause]

It is a hard life, in some ways, and a simple one. But it is also a warm one, full of family, and friendship, and old traditions. And when the spring comes, the snow will melt, and the birds will come back, and the queen will come again, and it will all begin again.

[long-pause]

{img:older-queen-victoria-1880s-balmoral-terrace-black-dress-pony-cart}
Queen Victoria came to Balmoral twice a year, every year, for the rest of her life. In the spring, for a few weeks, and in the autumn, for two or three months. She came for more than fifty years. As she grew older, she found it harder to walk, and she was driven around the estate in a little pony carriage. But she still loved the Highlands, and the hills, and the people.

[pause]

Over the years, she slowly came out of her deep grief for Albert. She began to appear in public again, to open buildings and visit cities. In 1876, she was given a new title, Empress of India. And in 1887, she celebrated her Golden Jubilee, fifty years on the throne, with great celebrations across the country and the empire. The crowds cheered her in the streets of London. She had become popular again.

[long-pause]

{img:balmoral-bonfire-jubilee-hilltop-crowd-celebrating-night-sparks}
At Balmoral, too, there were celebrations for the Golden Jubilee. Bonfires were lit on the tops of the hills, all around the valley, and there was a great party for the people of the estate. Ten years later, in 1897, there was the Diamond Jubilee, for sixty years on the throne. No British king or queen had ever reigned so long. Again, there were bonfires on the hills, and celebrations, and dancing.

[pause]

Many of the queen's children married, and had children of their own. By the end of her life, she had forty-two grandchildren, spread across all the royal families of Europe. Her grandchildren and great-grandchildren became kings and queens of Britain, Germany, Russia, Spain, Norway, Greece, Romania, and Sweden. She was called the grandmother of Europe. Many of them came to visit her at Balmoral, and some of them grew to love it, as she had.

[pause]

{img:large-royal-family-group-photograph-balmoral-1890s-grandchildren-tartan}
Prince Leopold, the gentle, sickly son, married a German princess, and had two children. But in 1884, when he was only thirty, he had a fall, and passed away, because of his illness. The queen was heartbroken again. Princess Louise married the Marquess of Lorne, a Scottish nobleman, and became a famous sculptor. Princess Beatrice married a German prince, Henry of Battenberg. But she stayed with her mother, and they lived with the queen, as she had always wanted.

[pause]

John Brown passed away in 1883, and the queen missed him terribly. Later, another servant became her close companion. A young Indian man called Abdul Karim, who taught her a little Hindustani, and became her teacher, her Munshi. He came to Balmoral with her too, and some of the courtiers were just as jealous of him, as they had been of John Brown. But the queen did not care.

[long-pause]

And the families of the estate grew older too. Angus Grant became the head ghillie, after many years, and then retired, and lived in his cottage by the river until he was very old. Kirsty kept her copy of the queen's book on the shelf, all her life. Morag married a young gamekeeper from Glen Muick, and had five children. And Ewan went away to Canada, and became a farmer on the prairies. But he always kept a little picture of Balmoral on his wall.

[pause]

{img:old-ghillie-sitting-cottage-doorway-pipe-river-dee-evening-grandchildren}
In the winter of 1901, in January, Queen Victoria passed away, at her house on the Isle of Wight. She was eighty-one years old. She had reigned for sixty-three years, longer than any British monarch before her. When the news came to Balmoral, the people of the valley were very sad. Many of them had known her all their lives. And in the little church at Crathie, they held a service for her, and the old people wept.

[pause]

She left instructions for her funeral. Among the things to be placed with her in her coffin were a photograph of John Brown, and a lock of his hair, which her doctor put in her hand, hidden by flowers. And beside her, also, was Albert's dressing gown, and a cast of his hand. She was buried beside Albert, at last, at Frogmore, near Windsor, in the mausoleum she had built for them both.

[long-pause]

{img:balmoral-castle-early-1900s-edward-vii-shooting-party-motor-cars}
After the queen's death, Balmoral passed to her son, Bertie, who became King Edward the Seventh. He was very different from his mother. He loved parties, and fine food, and horse racing, and shooting. At Balmoral, he brought in new comforts, and new guests, and new fun. He even had motor cars, which drove along the valley road, frightening the ponies. But he still kept many of his mother's traditions, like the ghillies' ball, and the Braemar Gathering.

[pause]

Since then, every king and queen of Britain has come to Balmoral, every year, in the late summer and autumn. King George the Fifth, King Edward the Eighth for a short time, King George the Sixth, and Queen Elizabeth the Second. For all of them, it has been a private place, a home, and a place to rest, away from the work and the crowds of London. Just as it was for Victoria and Albert.

[long-pause]

{img:balmoral-autumn-castle-river-dee-mist-morning-timeless-granite}
Queen Elizabeth the Second loved Balmoral, perhaps even more than her great-great-grandmother did. She came every summer, for more than seventy years. She walked her dogs in the hills, rode her ponies, had picnics by the lochs, and went to church at Crathie on Sundays. She said that it was the place where she felt most at home. And it was at Balmoral that she passed away, peacefully, in September 2022, at the age of ninety-six.

[pause]

The castle itself has changed very little since Victoria's time. The white granite walls, the tall tower, the turrets and the gardens are all just as they were. Inside, there are new comforts, like electric light and central heating. But much of the old furniture is still there, and the tartan, and the paintings, and the antlers on the walls. And the old Balmoral tartan, designed by Albert, is still used, only by the royal family.

[pause]

The estate is still a working estate, with farms, and forests, and deer, and fishing. Ghillies and gamekeepers still work there, as they did in Victoria's time. They take visitors stalking, and fishing on the River Dee. They look after the deer, and the moors, and the forests. Some of them come from families who have worked on the estate for generations, for a hundred years or more.

[pause]

{img:modern-ghillie-river-dee-fishing-autumn-waders-salmon-rod}
The Highlands have changed a great deal, of course. Many of the old cottages are empty, or have become holiday homes. The old Gaelic language has almost disappeared from the valley. Very few people speak it now. The young people go away to the cities to work, as they did in Victoria's time. But the hills, the river, the forests and the lochs are still there, as wild and beautiful as ever.

[long-pause]

The queen's books, Leaves from the Journal and More Leaves, are still read today. They are important historical records, of the life of the queen and her family. But also of the life of the Highlands, in the nineteenth century. The ghillies, the cottages, the games, the harvest, and the old customs. Because of them, we know the names and stories of many ordinary people, who would otherwise have been forgotten.

[pause]

{img:later-old-victorian-photograph-album-highland-ghillies-portraits-sepia}
There are also many photographs, from Victoria's time at Balmoral. The queen loved photography, which was a new invention. She had photographs taken of her family, her servants, her ghillies, and her dogs. Many of these photographs still exist, in the royal collection. They show the ghillies in their kilts, with their long beards and serious faces. The maids in their aprons. The ponies, the dogs, and the cottages. Looking at them, we can see the faces of the people of Balmoral, in 1868.

[pause]

And in the hills around Balmoral, the cairns still stand. Albert's pyramid, on its hilltop, and the others, for the marriages and the jubilees. Walkers climb up to them, and look out at the view. The same view that Victoria and Albert saw, more than a hundred and fifty years ago. The purple hills, the dark forests, the silver river, winding through the valley.

[long-pause]

{img:today-balmoral-castle-visitors-gardens-summer-tourists-walking}
Today, Balmoral is still owned by the royal family. It is a private home, not a palace of the state. But for a few months every year, in the spring and early summer, the gardens and grounds are open to visitors. People come from all over the world to see them. They walk through the gardens, and along the river, and visit the ballroom, which is the only room in the castle that the public can see.

[pause]

In the ballroom, there is an exhibition, about the history of Balmoral, and the royal family's life there. There are paintings and photographs, and furniture, and clothes. There are things that belonged to Queen Victoria, and to Albert. Some of the queen's drawings and watercolours. And pictures of the ghillies' balls, which still take place there every year, in the autumn, just as they did in Victoria's time.

[long-pause]

{img:today-crathie-kirk-granite-church-river-dee-hills-autumn-visitors}
Nearby, the church at Crathie welcomes visitors too. It is a little bigger and grander than it was in 1868, but it is still simple and peaceful inside. The royal family still goes there, every Sunday, when they are at Balmoral. And in the churchyard, there are the graves of many people who worked at Balmoral, over the years. Among them, the grave of John Brown, with a stone that the queen had carved for him.

[pause]

All around Balmoral, there are paths and trails for walkers. Some go up into the hills, to the cairns, and to the top of Lochnagar, the great mountain that rises above the estate. Others go along the river, through the old pine forests, where red squirrels live. And the path along Loch Muick, past the Glassalt Shiel, is one of the most beautiful walks in Scotland. In the autumn, walkers can still hear the stags roaring in the hills around.

[pause]

The area around Balmoral is now part of the Cairngorms National Park, the largest national park in Britain. It is a protected place, of mountains, moors, forests and rivers. There are golden eagles, ospreys, red deer, mountain hares, wildcats, and pine martens. And there are many people working to protect the old Caledonian pine forest, and help it grow again, after hundreds of years of being cut down.

[pause]

{img:today-loch-muick-path-walkers-autumn-hills-mist-glassalt-distant}
In Ballater, the little town down the river, visitors can see the restored railway station, where the royal train used to arrive. There is a copy of Queen Victoria's royal railway carriage, to show how she travelled. And in the shops along the main street, there are royal crests above the doors. They show that the shop has supplied goods to the royal family, for many years. The butcher, the baker, and the grocer.

[long-pause]

And every year, on the first Saturday in September, the Braemar Gathering still takes place, just as it did in Victoria's time. Thousands of people come to watch. The pipers still play, the dancers still dance, and the strong men still toss the caber. The hill runners still race up the steep hillside and down again. And the royal family still comes to watch, from the royal pavilion, just as the queen did, all those years ago.

[pause]

{img:today-braemar-gathering-crowd-caber-toss-pipe-band-september}
Some things have changed, and some have stayed the same. The valley is busier now, with cars, and tourists, and holiday homes. But the hills, the river, the forests and the lochs are still the same. The autumn still turns the birches gold, and the heather purple. The first snow still falls on Lochnagar in October. And in the evenings, the mist still rises from the River Dee, and drifts across the valley, as the light fades.

[pause]

If you visit Balmoral today, and walk along the river, in the autumn, you can still feel what Victoria felt there. The peace, the quiet, and the wild beauty of the Highlands. It is easy to understand why she loved it so much. And why she called it her dear paradise in the Highlands.

So we leave Balmoral tonight, in the autumn of 1868, as the evening falls. The sun has gone down behind the hills, and the sky is turning from gold to pale green, and then to deep blue. The first stars are appearing, one by one. The mist is rising from the River Dee, white and soft, drifting across the meadows and between the trees. And the air is cold, and still, and smells of woodsmoke and pine.

[pause]

In the castle, the lamps are being lit, one by one, in the tall windows. The queen has finished her dinner, and sits in her sitting room, by the fire, with her journal. She writes about the day. The weather, the walk, the people she saw. Princess Beatrice plays the piano softly in the next room. Princess Louise is sketching, by the lamp. And Prince Leopold is reading a book, curled up in a chair.

Angus Grant walks home along the track, by the river, with his lantern swinging in his hand. His long day on the hill is over. He is tired, and his legs ache, but he is content. Ahead of him, through the trees, he can see the warm glow of the cottage window. And smoke rising from the chimney, straight up into the still evening air. And he can hear Bran barking, because the dog has heard him coming.

[pause]

In the cottage, the fire is burning bright. The pot of soup is bubbling. Kirsty is laying the table, and the grandmother is sitting in her chair, wrapped in the queen's grey shawl. Morag is helping her mother. Ewan is sitting on the floor, with Bran. When Angus comes in, they all look up and smile. He hangs up his coat, and sits down by the fire. And the family has supper together.

[long-pause]

{img:highland-cottage-family-supper-candlelight-peat-fire-dog-sleeping-cosy}
After supper, Morag reads a little from the queen's book. Then the grandmother sings an old Gaelic song, very softly, a lullaby that her own mother sang to her. Ewan falls asleep by the fire, with his head on Bran. Angus carries him to his bed in the box bed by the wall. Then the rest of the family go to bed too. The fire is banked, the candle blown out, and the cottage is quiet.

[pause]

Outside, the night is very still. The stars are bright above the dark hills. Far away, up the glen, a stag roars, and then another. The river runs on, murmuring over its stones, as it has done for thousands of years. An owl calls from the pine woods. And the mist lies softly on the valley, like a blanket, over the castle, and the cottages, and the sleeping hills.
