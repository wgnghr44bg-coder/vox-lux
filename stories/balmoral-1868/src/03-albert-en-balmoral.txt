When Victoria and Albert bought the Balmoral estate, in 1852, there was already a small old castle there. But it was too small for the royal family and all their servants. So Albert decided to build a new one. He designed it himself, with an architect from Aberdeen, called William Smith. And it was built just beside the old castle, which was then pulled down.

--

The new castle was finished in 1856. It is built of granite, a hard, pale grey stone, from a quarry nearby, which sparkles when the sun shines on it. It is in a style called Scottish Baronial, which is like the old castles of the Scottish lords, with round towers, pointed turrets, stepped gables and little battlements. In the middle, there is a tall square tower, with a clock, and a round turret at one corner, with a flag pole. When the queen is at Balmoral, her flag flies from it.

Albert designed everything. Not only the castle, but also the gardens, the paths, the cottages for the workers, the bridges, and even the farm buildings. He planted thousands of trees. He laid out walks through the woods and along the river. He built a dairy, and a school for the children of the estate. And he made the whole estate bigger, buying the land around it, until it covered many thousands of hectares of hills, forests and moors.

{img:balmoral-drawing-room-tartan-carpets-curtains-chairs-thistle-wallpaper}
Inside, the castle is decorated in a very special way. With tartan. Everywhere. Albert designed his own tartan, called the Balmoral tartan, grey, black and red. And Victoria designed another, the Victoria tartan. The carpets are tartan. The curtains are tartan. The chairs are covered with tartan. Even the linoleum on the floors is tartan. There are thistles, the flower of Scotland, painted on the wallpaper. And antlers of stags on the walls of the hall.

Some visitors thought it was too much. One government minister, who stayed at Balmoral, joked that the tartan made him dizzy. Another said that the drawing room was the ugliest room he had ever seen. But Victoria loved it. It was what Albert had made. And to her, it was beautiful. After his death, she would not let anything be changed. Everything stayed exactly as he had designed it.

--

{img:prince-albert-statue-memorial-balmoral-woods-granite-pedestal-autumn}
After Albert died, Victoria built memorials to him all over the Balmoral estate. Statues, stone crosses, and cairns, which are piles of stones, built on the tops of hills. On a hill above the castle, there is a great pyramid-shaped cairn, built of granite, in his memory, with an inscription. And near the castle, there is a large bronze statue of Albert, in Highland dress, with a dog and a gun, looking out over the valley.

The estate is full of places that remind Victoria of Albert. The paths he laid out. The trees he planted. The views he loved. The rocks where they had picnics together. The hills where he went stalking deer. She visits them all, again and again, in her carriage, or on her pony. And she remembers. And sometimes, she weeps. But slowly, the memories become a comfort, as well as a sorrow.

{img:queen-victoria-on-pony-led-by-highlander-hill-path-cairn-view-autumn}
Albert also made Balmoral a working estate. With farms, and forests, and deer, and grouse, and salmon in the river. Many people live and work on the estate. Farmers, foresters, gamekeepers, ghillies, shepherds, gardeners, and their families. They live in cottages, scattered through the valley. Albert built many new cottages for them, better than the old ones, with stone walls, slate roofs and proper chimneys. And Victoria knows many of them by name.

--

The queen often says that Balmoral is the place where she feels most at home. More than Windsor, more than Buckingham Palace, more than Osborne, her house by the sea, on the Isle of Wight. At Balmoral, she says, she can breathe freely. She can be herself. Away from London, and politics, and the crowds, and the ceremonies. In the hills, with the heather and the deer and the clear river, she feels close to Albert. And that is all she wants.
