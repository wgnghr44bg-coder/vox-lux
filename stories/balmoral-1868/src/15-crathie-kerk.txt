On Sundays, everyone on the estate goes to church. The little church at Crathie, just across the river from the castle. It is a plain, simple building, of grey stone, with a small bell tower, and a graveyard around it, full of old stones. It is a church of the Church of Scotland, the Presbyterian church, which is very different from the Church of England, with its bishops and its grand ceremonies.

The queen goes to church at Crathie every Sunday, when she is at Balmoral. This surprised and upset some people in England, at first. The queen is the head of the Church of England. Should she really go to a Scottish Presbyterian church? But the queen did not mind. She liked the simple services, and the plain words, and the sincere preaching. She said she felt closer to God there than in many grand cathedrals.

--

On Sunday morning, the families walk to church, along the road beside the river. In their best clothes, the men in their dark coats, the women in their shawls and bonnets. The children are scrubbed clean, and told to behave. Bran stays at home, and howls when they leave. Angus and Kirsty walk slowly, talking quietly with the neighbours. The bell rings out across the valley, calling them in.

{img:plain-kirk-interior-wooden-pews-congregation-minister-pulpit-high}
Inside, the church is very simple. White walls, plain wooden pews, and a tall wooden pulpit, where the minister stands to preach. There are no statues, no pictures, no candles, no stained glass. The royal family has its own pew, in a gallery at the side. The families of the estate sit below, in their usual places. Everyone stands when the queen comes in. Then the service begins.

The service is long. There are prayers, and readings from the Bible, and psalms. The psalms are sung without any organ or instruments, because the Church of Scotland did not allow them in those days. A man called the precentor stands at the front, and sings each line first, and the congregation sings it after him. It is a slow, strong, solemn sound, rising and falling, filling the little church. Some of the old people sing the psalms in Gaelic, quietly, in their own words.

Then the minister preaches. The sermon goes on for a long time, often for an hour or more. The minister talks about God, and sin, and the duty of a good Christian. The children find it very hard to sit still. Ewan counts the beams in the roof, and the buttons on the coat of the man in front. Morag tries to listen, but her mind drifts away, to the ball, and to Prince Arthur. The grandmother listens to every word, nodding.

--

After the service, the families stand in the graveyard for a while, talking to friends and neighbours. This is an important part of the Sunday. They share news, about the harvest, the weather, the animals, the births and the weddings, and the people who have passed away. The young people look at each other shyly. The children run between the stones, until they are told to stop.

Angus and Kirsty visit the grave of Angus's father, who passed away five years ago. He was a ghillie too, at Balmoral, before the castle was built. He knew Prince Albert well. They stand together for a moment, by the plain grey stone, with its carved name. Kirsty puts a little bunch of heather on the grass. Then they walk home together, slowly, in the autumn sunshine.

{img:sunday-afternoon-cottage-family-resting-bible-reading-quiet-window-light}
Sunday afternoon is quiet. No work is done on the Sabbath, in the Highlands. No digging, no sewing, no washing, no games. Not even a walk, just for pleasure, in some strict families. The day is for rest and for God. Angus reads the Bible aloud, by the window. The grandmother dozes in her chair. The children sit quietly, and look at books, or out of the window. Sometimes they are allowed to walk to the river, if they promise to be good.

The church at Crathie was rebuilt in the 1890s, in a grander style, with stones given by the royal family. The queen laid the foundation stone herself. It is still the church where the royal family goes, every Sunday, when they are at Balmoral. And in the churchyard, the old stones are still there, under the trees.
