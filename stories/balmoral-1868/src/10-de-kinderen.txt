Queen Victoria had nine children. Four sons and five daughters. By 1868, most of them are grown up. Some are married, with children of their own, and live in other countries. The eldest daughter, Vicky, is married to the crown prince of Prussia, in Germany. The eldest son, Bertie, the Prince of Wales, is married to a Danish princess, and lives in London. But three of the younger children are still with their mother, at Balmoral, in the autumn of 1868.

--

Princess Louise is twenty. She is the most beautiful and the most artistic of the queen's daughters. She loves sculpture, painting and drawing, and wants to be an artist. She is clever, lively and independent, and sometimes argues with her mother. She is a little bored at Balmoral, with its routines and its cold rooms. But she loves sketching the hills, and the people, and she is very good at it. She will later become a real sculptor.

Prince Leopold is fifteen. He is clever, kind and gentle, and loves books and music. But he is often ill. He has a disease called haemophilia, which means that his blood does not clot properly. A small cut or bump can be very dangerous for him. So his mother keeps him very close, and very protected. He is not allowed to play rough games, or ride fast, or climb. He finds this very frustrating. He wants to be like other boys.

{img:young-prince-reading-by-window-balmoral-quiet-bookish-tartan-room}
Princess Beatrice is eleven. She is the youngest of the queen's children, and her mother's favourite. The queen calls her Baby, even though she is eleven. She is shy, quiet and sweet-natured. She goes everywhere with her mother, on her walks and drives. She will stay with her mother all her life, as her companion and secretary, even after she marries. She loves music, and plays the piano for her mother in the evenings.

The royal children have a very different life from the children of the estate. They have governesses and tutors, who teach them languages, history, music, drawing and good manners. They have ponies, and dogs, and fine clothes. But they also have strict rules, and very little freedom. They must always behave perfectly. They must always do what their mother says. And they are often lonely, with no friends of their own age.

--

{img:princess-and-highland-girl-meeting-river-path-shy-smiles-autumn}
One afternoon, Princess Beatrice, walking with her governess near the river, meets Morag, who is collecting firewood in the woods. The two girls look at each other, shyly. The governess wants to walk on. But Beatrice asks Morag her name, and what she is doing. Morag curtsies, as she has been taught, and answers. Beatrice asks if she can help. And for a few minutes, the princess and the ghillie's daughter gather sticks together, by the river, while the governess watches, disapprovingly.

Beatrice asks Morag about her life. About school, and her family, and her little brother, and his dog. Morag tells her. And Beatrice listens, wide-eyed. She has never been to a school. She has never had a friend of her own age to play with. She has never collected firewood. She asks if Morag is happy. Morag says yes, very happy. Beatrice is quiet for a moment. Then the governess calls her, and she must go.

{img:royal-girl-waving-goodbye-walking-away-governess-woods-path-autumn-light}
After that, whenever Beatrice drives past the Grants' cottage, in her mother's carriage, she waves to Morag. And Morag waves back. And once, at Christmas, a small parcel arrives at the cottage, from the castle, addressed to Miss Morag Grant. Inside, there is a little book of Scottish fairy tales, with a card, signed in careful handwriting. From Beatrice. Morag keeps it all her life.

--

The queen was a loving mother, in her way. But she was also demanding and controlling. Her children loved her, but were also afraid of her. Some of them found it hard to live their own lives, under her watchful eye. But at Balmoral, in the hills, things were a little easier. There was more freedom, more fresh air, and more fun, than in the palaces of London and Windsor. And the children, too, came to love the Highlands, as their parents had.
