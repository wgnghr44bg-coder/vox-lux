Tonight we go nearly two thousand years back. We go to ancient China, to the great city of Luoyang, around the year 150. It is the time of the Han dynasty. And the New Year is coming.

[long-pause]

It is a cold winter morning. Snow lies thin on the curved grey roofs. Smoke rises from thousands of chimneys into a pale sky. Outside the city wall, the river Luo is half frozen. Its edges are white with ice. Long walls of beaten earth run around the city. Gates with tall wooden towers stand in the walls. And inside, the streets are already busy.

[pause]

People hurry past with bundles on their backs. Carts creak through the frozen mud. Market sellers blow on their cold hands. Everyone seems to be carrying something home. A basket of dried fish. A jar of wine. A bundle of peach wood sticks. Because in a few weeks, the old year will end. And a new one will begin.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:han-family-courtyard-house-lanterns-winter-evening-luoyang-snow}
The New Year is the most important time of the whole year in China. It is a time to clean the house and settle debts. It is a time to honour the ancestors and visit the family. It is a time to eat well and drink warm wine and wish each other a long life. And it is a time to chase away the bad luck of the old year, so the new year can start fresh.

[pause]

People have celebrated the turn of the year in China for a very long time. But many of the customs we still see today first appear in the time of the Han. Lucky charms on the doors. Popping bamboo in the fire. Special wine for a long life. Children bowing to their grandparents. And the twelve animals of the years.

[long-pause]

{img:han-dynasty-street-market-luoyang-new-year-shoppers-lanterns}
Tonight we will spend the turning of the year in Luoyang. We will meet a family in their house with its quiet courtyard. We will visit the busy markets and the great palace. We will watch a parade of masked dancers who chase the old year out of the city. We will sit by the fire on the last night of the year and listen to the bamboo pop. And we will wake up on New Year's morning with the whole family.

[pause]

There will be worries too. Will the family be ready in time? Will the bad luck really leave? Will the new year be a good one?

[pause]

{img:han-pottery-house-model-tomb-figurine-courtyard-watchtower-museum}
How do we know all this? Some of it comes from old books written in the Han time. And some comes from the Han people themselves. They buried little clay models with their dead. Models of houses and farms and wells. Models of cooks and musicians and dogs. Thousands of these little figures have been found. And they show us, in clay, how people lived.

[long-pause]

So let us walk through the cold streets of Luoyang, in the last weeks of the old year. The snow crunches under our feet. Our breath turns white in the air. And from somewhere nearby comes the smell of something warm, cooking.

[long-pause]

{img:han-empire-landscape-great-wall-mountains-rivers-fields-vast}
The Han dynasty is one of the great ages of China. A dynasty is a family of rulers, one after another. The Han ruled China for about four hundred years. They started a little more than two hundred years before Christ.

[pause]

Before them, China had been split into many warring kingdoms. Then a fierce king joined them all together. He made himself the first emperor of China. He built roads and walls. But he was harsh, and people suffered. After he passed away, his empire fell apart quickly. There were years of fighting. And then a man of humble birth won. He was a village official who had become a rebel leader. He became the first emperor of the Han.

[long-pause]

The Han learned from what had gone wrong. They kept the big empire together. But they tried to rule more gently. They chose officials who had studied the old books. They lowered taxes in good years. And they followed the teachings of a wise man who had lived long before. His name was Confucius.

[pause]

Confucius taught that a good life is built on kindness and respect. Children should honour their parents. The young should honour the old. Rulers should be fair. And everyone should behave well toward everyone else. In the Han time, his teachings become the heart of Chinese life. And they shape the New Year too. Because the New Year is above all about family.

[pause]

{img:confucian-scholar-han-dynasty-robes-reading-bamboo-strips-study}
The Han empire is huge. It reaches from the cold grasslands in the north to the warm green hills of the south. It reaches from the sea in the east to the deserts in the far west. Perhaps fifty or sixty million people live under the Han. That is about as many as live in the whole Roman Empire at the same time. Far away to the west, Rome is at its height too. The two great empires know a little about each other. But they will never meet.

[long-pause]

Our story takes place in the second half of the Han time. The capital has moved east, to Luoyang. Things are mostly peaceful. Farms are busy. Trade is growing. But at court, there are rivals and whispers. Powerful families and palace servants compete for the emperor's ear. Not everyone is sure the peace will last.

[pause]

{img:han-chinese-farmers-rice-millet-fields-water-buffalo-village}
Most people, though, do not live at court. Nine out of ten people in Han China are farmers. They grow millet and wheat in the north, and rice in the south. They keep pigs and chickens and oxen. They pay taxes in grain and cloth. And every year, when the harvest is in and the cold comes, they turn their thoughts to the New Year.

[long-pause]

{img:luoyang-han-city-aerial-walls-gates-palaces-grid-streets-winter}
Luoyang is the capital of the Han empire. It is one of the biggest cities in the world.

[pause]

It lies in a wide valley between hills, beside the river Luo. That is how it got its name. Luo-yang means the sunny side of the Luo. The city is shaped like a long box. Thick walls of pounded earth surround it. They are about ten metres high. You could walk around the whole wall in half a day. Twelve gates lead in and out. Each gate has a name and a tower on top.

[long-pause]

Inside, there are two great palaces, one in the north and one in the south. A covered walkway joins them. The emperor can walk between his palaces without ever stepping into the street. Around the palaces are the offices of the government, the houses of nobles, the barracks of the guards, and the homes of ordinary people.

[pause]

The streets are straight and wide. They cross each other like the lines on a game board. Rows of trees line the main streets. At night the gates of each neighbourhood are closed. Watchmen walk the streets with drums. Nobody should be out after dark without a good reason.

[pause]

{img:han-luoyang-street-wooden-houses-tiled-roofs-carts-people-snow}
The houses are built of wood and pounded earth. Their roofs are covered with grey clay tiles. The tiles curve down at the edges. At the end of each row of tiles is a round disk with a pattern or a lucky word. Some say long life. Some say ten thousand years of joy.

[pause]

Perhaps half a million people live in and around Luoyang. Officials and soldiers. Traders and craftsmen. Scholars and servants. And people from far away. Travellers from the western deserts with camels. Monks from India. Envoys from the north and the south. Luoyang is the centre of the world, as far as the Han are concerned.

[long-pause]

{img:luoyang-markets-han-dynasty-stalls-silk-pottery-crowds-cold}
The city has three great markets. The biggest is in the south-west. It is a large square surrounded by walls. Inside are rows of shops, each row for one kind of goods. A tall drum tower stands in the middle. When the drum sounds in the morning, the market opens. When it sounds in the evening, the market closes. Officials walk around and check the prices and the scales. Anyone caught cheating will be in trouble.

[pause]

In the last month of the year, the markets are busier than ever. Everyone is buying for the New Year. And the drum tower can hardly keep up.

[long-pause]

The father of our family walks through this market every day on his way to work. Today he stops at a stall that sells writing brushes. He looks at a fine brush with a handle of red lacquer. It would make a good New Year gift for his son. He asks the price. It is too much. He puts it back and walks on. But he keeps thinking about it all day.

[long-pause]

{img:han-imperial-palace-luoyang-red-pillars-tiled-roofs-courtyard-guards}
In the north of Luoyang stands the emperor's palace.

[pause]

It is a whole world inside walls. There are great halls with red pillars and roofs of shining tiles. There are courtyards paved with stone and gardens with ponds and bridges. There are store rooms and kitchens and stables. And there are thousands of people. Officials and guards. Cooks and musicians. Doctors and writers. And the ladies of the palace with their servants.

[long-pause]

The grandest building is the hall where the emperor meets his officials. It stands on a high platform of earth. Wide steps lead up to it. Its roof is huge and heavy, held up by rows of thick wooden pillars painted red. Inside it is dim and cold in winter. Braziers of glowing charcoal stand along the walls. They give a little warmth.

[pause]

The emperor sits at the far end, on a raised seat. He wears a robe of black and yellow silk. On his head is a flat board with strings of jade beads hanging down in front and behind. The beads hide his eyes a little. Nobody looks straight at the emperor. People bow low to the ground before him.

[pause]

{img:han-palace-officials-bowing-hall-emperor-distant-dais-incense}
The emperor is called the Son of Heaven. People believe heaven gives him the right to rule. But only if he rules well. If he is cruel or careless, heaven may send warnings. Floods. Droughts. Earthquakes. Strange lights in the sky. Then people whisper that heaven is unhappy. So the court watches the sky and the seasons very closely. And the New Year matters a great deal. If the year starts well, it is a good sign for everyone.

[long-pause]

In the last month of the year, the palace is very busy. The halls must be swept and polished. New robes must be sewn for the ceremonies. Food and wine must be gathered for great feasts. Gifts arrive from every corner of the empire. Silk and lacquer and jade. Horses and fruit and rare birds.

[pause]

{img:han-palace-kitchen-cooks-steaming-baskets-new-year-feast-preparing}
The palace kitchens work day and night. Cooks steam bread and boil meat. They make sauces from beans and salt fish. They stack food in tall lacquered boxes, red and black. There are hundreds of mouths to feed. And on New Year's Day, every guest at court will expect a feast.

[long-pause]

{img:han-astronomers-observing-sky-bronze-instrument-tower-night-stars}
When does the new year begin? That is not as simple as it sounds.

[pause]

The Chinese count months by the moon. A new month begins at each new moon, when the moon is dark. Twelve moons make about one year. But not exactly. Twelve moons are about eleven days shorter than a year of the sun. So after a few years, the months drift away from the seasons. Spring festivals start to fall in winter. Something must be done.

[long-pause]

So every two or three years, the astronomers add an extra month. That brings the calendar back in step with the sun. It is careful, difficult work. The court astronomers watch the sun and the moon and the stars night after night. They measure the shadow of a tall pole at noon. The shortest shadow means midsummer. The longest shadow means midwinter.

[pause]

In earlier times, the year began at different moments. Some rulers started it in winter. Some started it in autumn. It was confusing. Then, about two hundred and fifty years before our story, a Han emperor ordered a new calendar. The new year would begin on the second new moon after midwinter. That is somewhere between late January and the middle of February.

[pause]

{img:han-calendar-bamboo-slips-written-characters-desk-brush-ink}
That choice has lasted almost two thousand years. It is why Chinese New Year still falls in late January or February today. When people all over the world celebrate it now, they follow a rule made by a Han emperor and his astronomers.

[long-pause]

The calendar matters to everyone. Farmers need to know when to plough and when to sow. Officials need to know when to hold ceremonies. Families need to know which days are lucky for weddings and journeys. So every year the court sends out the new calendar to every corner of the empire. It is written on strips of bamboo or wood. Officials read it out in the towns. And everyone waits to hear when the new year will begin.

[pause]

{img:han-village-official-reading-calendar-to-farmers-winter-square}
This year, the astronomers have done their work. The extra month was added last year. The calendar is right. The date is known. In a few weeks, on the night of the new moon, the old year will end. And all over China, people are getting ready.

[long-pause]

{img:han-family-portrait-grandparents-parents-children-courtyard-winter}
Let us meet a family in Luoyang.

[pause]

The father is a small official. He works in one of the government offices near the palace. He keeps records and writes letters. He is not rich. But he is respected. He studied the old books when he was young. He can read and write well. And he hopes his son will one day do even better.

[long-pause]

The mother runs the house. She manages the money and the servants and the food. She weaves silk on a loom in a room off the courtyard. She is clever and careful. In the New Year weeks, she is the busiest person in the family. Everything must be ready in time. And everything depends on her.

[pause]

There are three children. The eldest is a boy of twelve. He studies the old books every morning with a teacher. He finds them hard. There is a girl of nine. She helps her mother and learns to weave and sew. And there is a little boy of four. He mostly runs around and gets in everyone's way.

[pause]

{img:han-grandmother-sitting-mat-children-listening-brazier-warm-room}
And there are the grandparents. The father's mother and father live with them. In China, that is the right way. The oldest son takes care of his parents. The grandfather is seventy. He has a long white beard and walks with a stick. The grandmother is sixty-five. She knows every old custom and every old story. And she makes sure nobody forgets them.

[long-pause]

There are also two servants. A cook and a young girl who helps with the cleaning. And there is a dog that sleeps by the kitchen fire. And a cat that sleeps wherever it likes.

[pause]

{img:han-mother-checking-store-room-jars-grain-new-year-list}
This year the New Year worries the mother a little. The harvest was not good in the countryside. Prices in the market have gone up. Rice and wine and meat cost more than last year. She counts the coins in the family box. She looks at the store room. Will there be enough for a proper New Year? Enough food for the family and the guests? Enough for the gifts to the ancestors? She is not sure. But she does not say anything yet. There are still a few weeks to go.

[long-pause]

{img:han-courtyard-house-gate-wall-trees-well-rooms-around-yard}
The family's house is built around a courtyard.

[pause]

From the street, you see only a wall and a gate. The gate has two wooden doors painted black. Above it is a little roof of grey tiles. Inside the gate there is a short wall. It stands right in front of you, so you have to walk around it. People say bad spirits can only move in straight lines. So the little wall keeps them out.

[long-pause]

Then you step into the courtyard. It is a square of beaten earth with a well in one corner. A small tree grows in the middle. In summer it gives shade. Now its branches are bare. Rooms stand on all four sides. The best room faces south, toward the sun. That is where the grandparents live. The father and mother have rooms on the east side. The children sleep on the west side. The kitchen and the store rooms and the servants' rooms are near the gate.

[pause]

The rooms have wooden floors raised a little above the ground. People sit on mats on the floor. They kneel, sitting back on their heels. There are no chairs yet in China. Low tables stand in front of them. When guests come, the best mats are rolled out. The most honoured guest sits facing east.

[pause]

{img:han-room-interior-low-table-mats-folding-screen-lamp-winter}
In winter the house is cold. The walls are thin and the windows are covered with paper or cloth. People wear many layers of padded clothes. They warm their hands over small bronze braziers full of glowing charcoal. In the north, some houses have a raised brick bed with a fire underneath. It is called a kang. The whole family gathers on it on the coldest nights.

[long-pause]

At night the rooms are lit by little lamps. Some are made of bronze. One famous Han lamp is shaped like a kneeling girl holding a lantern. The smoke goes up through her arm and into her body. That way the room stays clean. Most families have simpler lamps of clay. They burn oil and give a soft yellow light.

[pause]

{img:han-bronze-lamp-kneeling-figure-glowing-soft-light-dark-room}
Every corner of the house has its own small spirit. The spirit of the gate. The spirit of the well. The spirit of the stove. In the New Year weeks, all of them must be honoured. If one is forgotten, people believe, the new year could bring trouble. So the grandmother keeps a list in her head. And she checks it every day.

[long-pause]

{img:han-twelfth-month-winter-fields-bare-village-hunters-returning}
The last month of the year is called the La month. La is the name of a great winter offering.

[pause]

Long ago, at the end of the year, people went hunting. They offered the animals they caught to their ancestors and to the gods of the fields. They thanked them for the harvest. And they asked for a good year to come. In the Han time, the La offering is still one of the biggest days of the year. It falls a few days after midwinter.

[long-pause]

On La day, families honour their ancestors at home. They lay out meat and grain and wine on a table. They light incense. They bow and say the names of the dead. In the villages, people gather to thank the spirits of the soil and the grain. There is music and drinking and dancing. Farmers who have worked hard all year can rest a little.

[pause]

A special porridge is cooked in this month in many places. It is made of grains and beans and nuts and dried fruit, all boiled together. Later it will be called La-month porridge. It is thick and sweet and warm. It fills you up on a cold day. Families share it with neighbours and give some to the poor.

[pause]

{img:han-family-cooking-grain-porridge-big-pot-kitchen-steam-winter}
In the La month, people also try to settle what is left over from the old year. Debts must be paid. Borrowed things must be given back. Quarrels must be made up. Nobody wants to carry old troubles into the new year. So people knock on doors with money in their hands. They return a borrowed pot or a borrowed book. They send small gifts to neighbours they have argued with.

[long-pause]

In our family, the father has a small debt. In the summer, he borrowed money from a colleague to fix the roof after a storm. Now he must pay it back before the year ends. He counts the coins carefully. It will leave the family with less for the New Year. But he does not want to start the year owing money. That would be bad luck. And bad manners. So one cold evening he walks to his colleague's house and pays it all. His colleague smiles and pours him a cup of warm wine. And the father walks home lighter, even though his purse is lighter too.

[pause]

{img:han-man-walking-home-snowy-street-evening-lantern-relieved}
At home, the mother has been thinking too. She has decided to sell a roll of the silk she wove this year. It is fine silk, the best she has made. She was saving it for her daughter. But a good New Year matters more right now. Tomorrow she will take it to the market. She hopes she will get a fair price.

[long-pause]

{img:han-kitchen-stove-clay-offering-bowl-incense-stove-god-shrine}
In every Chinese kitchen there is a stove. And on every stove, people believe, lives a little god.

[pause]

He is the god of the stove. He lives in the kitchen all year long. He sees everything that happens in the house. Who is kind and who is lazy. Who quarrels and who helps. And at the end of the year, he goes up to heaven. There he reports to the great gods on how the family has behaved.

[long-pause]

So in the La month, families honour the stove god. They clean the stove carefully. They set out a little food and wine for him. They light incense. And they bow and ask him to speak well of them in heaven.

[pause]

There is an old story from the Han time about the stove god. A man called Yin was very poor. But he was kind and faithful. Every year he honoured the stove god, even when he had almost nothing. One La morning, while he was cooking, the stove god appeared to him. Yin was so surprised he almost dropped his pot. Then he bowed. He had only a yellow dog. So he offered the dog to the god as a gift. The god smiled and blessed him. And from that year on, the story says, Yin's family grew rich. They had fields and horses and servants. And they never forgot to honour the stove god.

[pause]

{img:han-family-bowing-before-stove-small-offering-evening-candle}
People told this story every year. It reminded them to be grateful even when times are hard. Later, people will put sticky sweets on the stove god's mouth before he leaves. That way he can only say sweet things about the family. That custom comes later. But the idea is already here. The family wants the stove god's report to be good.

[long-pause]

In our family, the grandmother leads the little ceremony. The kitchen is swept. The stove is wiped clean of soot. The cook places a bowl of rice and a cup of wine on the edge of the stove. Everyone stands in a row. The grandmother lights the incense. The smoke curls up toward the roof. And the whole family bows.

[pause]

{img:incense-smoke-curling-kitchen-rafters-han-house-soft-light}
The little boy of four does not understand it all. But he bows very low, because everyone else does. Then he whispers something to the stove. What did you say, his sister asks. I told him I was good this year, he says. Mostly. The grandmother laughs. And even the cook smiles.

[long-pause]

{img:great-nuo-ceremony-masked-dancer-bearskin-four-eyes-palace-torches}
On the last day of the year, there is a great event at the palace in Luoyang. It is called the Great Nuo. It is a parade to chase out the bad spirits of the old year.

[pause]

People believe that sickness and bad luck gather over the year like dust. Before the new year can begin, all of it must be swept away. So at the palace, a huge ceremony is held. And the whole city waits for it.

[long-pause]

It begins in the evening. One hundred and twenty boys gather in the palace. They are between ten and twelve years old. Each wears a red cap and a black tunic. Each carries a big drum shaped like a hand drum with little beads that rattle. They are the helpers.

[pause]

Then the leader appears. He is called the exorcist. He wears a bearskin over his shoulders. His face is covered with a golden mask with four eyes. He wears a black coat and a red skirt. In one hand he holds a spear and in the other a shield. He looks strange and fierce. But he is on the people's side. His job is to frighten away whatever should not stay.

[pause]

{img:han-boys-red-caps-drums-parade-palace-courtyard-torches-night}
Behind him come twelve dancers dressed as animals. They wear masks and fur and feathers. Each one stands for a spirit that chases away a different kind of trouble. One eats bad dreams. One eats sickness. One eats bad luck. And so on.

[long-pause]

The parade moves through every hall of the palace. The boys beat their drums and shout. The exorcist stamps and waves his spear into every corner. They sing a song together. It tells the bad spirits to leave right now, or the twelve animals will catch them. The song is loud and a little silly. Some of the officials watching try not to smile.

[pause]

Then the whole parade goes out of the palace gates with flaming torches. Riders take the torches and gallop out of the city. They throw them into the river Luo. The flames hiss out in the dark water. And that is the sign. The bad spirits have been carried far away. The city is clean.

[pause]

{img:horsemen-carrying-torches-galloping-toward-river-night-sparks}
In the streets of Luoyang, people crowd to watch the torches race past. Children sit on their fathers' shoulders. Some of the smaller ones hide their faces when the masked dancers come by. But the grown-ups laugh and cheer.

[long-pause]

In the villages too, people hold their own small Nuo. Young men put on masks and go from house to house, beating drums. They chase the old year out of every courtyard. And families give them food and a few coins.

[pause]

{img:village-nuo-masked-young-men-drums-house-to-house-winter-dusk}
In some parts of China, these masked dances are still performed today, almost two thousand years later.

[long-pause]

{img:han-family-sweeping-house-courtyard-brooms-dust-winter-sun}
In the last days of the year, every house in Luoyang is cleaned from top to bottom.

[pause]

People sweep the floors and the courtyard. They brush the dust from the rafters with long brooms. They wash the walls and wipe the lamps. They take the mats outside and beat them. They wash the curtains and the bedding. It is hard work in the cold. But it must be done. People believe the dust of the old year holds the bad luck of the old year. Sweep it out, and the bad luck goes with it.

[long-pause]

But you must be careful. On New Year's Day itself, you must not sweep at all. If you sweep on that day, people say, you sweep the new good luck right out of the door. So everything must be clean before the year ends. After that, the brooms are put away.

[pause]

In our family, everyone helps. The father climbs a ladder to clean the roof beams. The mother and the servant girl wash the floors. The eldest son carries out the mats. The daughter polishes the bronze lamp until it shines. Even the little boy is given a small broom. He sweeps the same corner of the courtyard again and again, very proudly.

[pause]

{img:han-children-helping-clean-polishing-bronze-lamp-carrying-mats}
And there is the shopping. So much to buy. New clothes for the children, if the family can afford them. Meat and fish. Rice and millet and flour. Wine and vinegar and salt. Pepper for the special New Year wine. Peach wood for the door charms. Incense and candles and paper. Gifts for the grandparents and for friends.

[long-pause]

The next morning, the mother goes to the market with her roll of silk. She wraps it carefully in a cloth and carries it under her arm. Her daughter walks beside her. The market is packed. Sellers shout. Buyers argue about prices. The mother walks to the silk sellers' row. She unwraps her silk and shows it.

[pause]

The first seller offers a low price. The mother shakes her head and wraps it up again. The second seller offers a little more. Still too little. She walks on. Her daughter looks worried. What if nobody pays a fair price? Then the mother comes to an old woman's stall at the end of the row. The old woman takes the silk and holds it up to the light. She runs her fingers over it. She looks at the mother. This is good work, she says. And she names a price. It is a fair one.

[pause]

{img:han-market-silk-seller-old-woman-holding-silk-to-light-buyer}
The mother bows and accepts. She puts the coins safely in her sleeve. On the way home, she buys pork and a fish and a jar of good wine. And for her daughter, a little red ribbon. The daughter ties it in her hair at once. And she smiles all the way home.

[long-pause]

{img:han-market-row-stalls-ceramics-lacquer-bronze-mirrors-buyers}
Let us walk through the great market of Luoyang in the last days of the year.

[pause]

The market is surrounded by walls with gates on each side. Inside, the shops stand in long straight rows. Each row sells one kind of thing. There is a row for grain and a row for meat. A row for cloth and a row for pots. A row for iron tools and a row for medicine. Little flags hang over the shops to show what they sell.

[long-pause]

In the row for lacquer, there are bowls and cups and boxes painted red and black. Lacquer is made from the sap of a tree. It is painted on in many thin layers. When it dries, it is hard and shiny and waterproof. Han lacquer is beautiful. It is decorated with clouds and dragons and swirling lines. A good lacquer box can cost more than a bronze one.

[pause]

In the row for bronze, there are mirrors. They are round and flat. One side is polished so smoothly that you can see your face in it. The other side is decorated with patterns and lucky words. Long life. Wealth and honour. Many children. People give mirrors as gifts at New Year.

[pause]

{img:han-bronze-mirror-back-pattern-lucky-inscription-close-detail}
In the row for food, there is everything. Sacks of millet and rice and beans. Strings of dried fish and dried meat. Jars of sauce made from fermented beans. Pickled vegetables. Dates and chestnuts and dried persimmons. Little cakes of sesame and honey. And sellers with steaming baskets of buns, calling out to the hungry shoppers.

[long-pause]

There are entertainers too, near the gates. Acrobats who balance on poles. Jugglers who throw balls and knives. Musicians with drums and bells. Storytellers who sit on a mat and tell old tales to a crowd. The Han love these shows. Little clay figures of them have been found in tombs. One famous figure is a storyteller with a drum. He has a big round belly and a huge happy grin. He is laughing so hard his eyes are closed.

[pause]

{img:han-pottery-storyteller-figure-drum-laughing-belly-museum-light}
And there are people from far away. Traders from the western deserts with strange hats and long coats. They sell horses and jade and grapes and glass beads. Some have come along the long road through the desert for many months. Children stare at their camels with their two humps.

[long-pause]

As the light fades, the drum on the market tower begins to sound. Boom, boom, boom. It is closing time. The sellers pack up their goods. The shoppers hurry home with their arms full. And the market gates swing shut, until tomorrow.

[long-pause]

{img:han-silk-weaving-loom-woman-courtyard-room-threads-colours}
In the weeks before New Year, there is one sound you hear in many houses of Luoyang. It is the soft clack and thump of the loom.

[pause]

China is the land of silk. For thousands of years, Chinese families have kept silkworms. They are small white caterpillars that eat only the leaves of the mulberry tree. When they are fully grown, each one spins a little case around itself, made of one very long, very fine thread. This case is called a cocoon. One cocoon can hold a thread almost a kilometre long.

[long-pause]

To get the silk, the cocoons are dropped into hot water. That loosens the thread. Then a woman finds the end of the thread and gently unwinds it. Several threads are twisted together to make one stronger thread. It is slow and careful work. One thread breaks, and you must start that part again.

[pause]

Then the thread is dyed and woven on a loom. Han silk can be plain and light. Or it can be thick and heavy, with patterns of clouds and animals and words woven into it. Some silk is so fine that a whole robe weighs less than fifty grams. That is less than a small apple.

[pause]

{img:mulberry-trees-silkworms-trays-leaves-han-farm-spring-women}
Silk is very precious. It is worn by the rich. It is given as gifts and paid as taxes. It is even used as money. Rolls of silk of a fixed length can buy grain or horses or land. And it travels far. Along the roads to the west, through the deserts, all the way to Rome. The Romans love Chinese silk. They do not know how it is made. Some of them think it grows on trees.

[long-pause]

The Chinese keep the secret carefully. Taking silkworms out of China is strictly forbidden. For hundreds of years, nobody outside China knows how silk is made. It is one of the best-kept secrets in history.

[pause]

{img:roman-lady-wearing-silk-robe-far-away-villa-garden-later}
In our family, the mother weaves silk in the evenings and in the winter months. The loom stands in a room by the courtyard. Her daughter sits beside her and watches. Sometimes she is allowed to throw the shuttle, the small wooden piece that carries the thread across the loom. Her hands are getting quicker. Next year, her mother says, you will weave your first piece on your own. The daughter is excited and a little nervous. What if she makes a mistake? Then you start again, says her mother. That is how everyone learns.

[long-pause]

{img:han-papermaking-workshop-vats-bark-pulp-screens-drying-sheets}
In the Han time, something new is changing the way people write. Paper.

[pause]

For many centuries, the Chinese have written on strips of bamboo or wood. Each strip holds one line of characters. The strips are tied together with string and rolled up. A long book is a heavy bundle of bamboo. One story says a famous emperor read so many reports every day that they had to be carried in on carts.

[long-pause]

Rich people sometimes write on silk. It is light and beautiful. But it is very costly. Only the most important letters and books are written on silk.

[pause]

Then, about fifty years before our story, a palace official called Cai Lun found a better way. People had made rough paper before. But he made it much better. He took the bark of mulberry trees, old rags, fishing nets and bits of hemp. He soaked them and beat them into a soft pulp in water. Then he scooped up a thin layer of the pulp on a fine screen. He let the water drain away. And he dried the thin layer in the sun.

[pause]

{img:cai-lun-official-holding-sheet-of-paper-workshop-light-han}
What was left was a sheet of paper. Light and smooth and cheap. You could write on it with a brush and ink. You could fold it and carry it. The emperor was delighted. Cai Lun was given a title and a reward. And slowly, over the next centuries, paper spread all over China.

[long-pause]

In the Han time, paper is still new. Bamboo strips are still common. But paper is becoming more and more popular. It is used for letters and for wrapping. And it is used for something else at New Year. Some families write lucky words on paper or cloth and stick them on their doors.

[pause]

Paper will travel west very slowly. It will take more than a thousand years to reach Europe. And when it does, it will change the world there too. Books and newspapers and letters. Without paper, none of them would be the same.

[pause]

{img:han-boy-practising-characters-brush-ink-paper-low-table-lamp}
In our family, the eldest son practises his writing every evening. He has a few sheets of paper for his best work. For practice, he uses a smooth board that he can wipe clean. He dips his brush in black ink and paints the characters slowly. Each stroke must be in the right order. He is trying to write the words for spring and good luck. He wants to hang them on the door at New Year. His father looks over his shoulder. That one is crooked, he says. Try again. The boy sighs. And tries again.

[long-pause]

{img:han-new-years-eve-family-gathered-room-lamps-low-tables-food}
At last it is the last evening of the old year.

[pause]

The house is clean. The food is ready. The ancestors have been honoured. The debts are paid. Now the whole family gathers together. This night is called the night of keeping the year. Nobody goes to sleep early. Everyone stays awake to see the old year out and the new year in.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the family eats a big meal together. They sit on their mats around the low tables in the warmest room. The grandparents sit in the place of honour. The lamps are lit, more than on any other night. Braziers glow in the corners. Outside, the night is cold and dark and very quiet. But inside it is warm and bright.

[pause]

The food comes in bowl after bowl. Pork cooked with ginger. A whole fish, steamed. Bowls of millet and rice. Soup with meat and vegetables. Pickles and sauces. Little cakes with honey. The mother has managed it after all. There is enough for everyone. She looks around the table and lets out a quiet breath. She did not tell anyone how worried she was. And now she does not need to.

[pause]

{img:han-steamed-fish-whole-platter-lacquer-table-family-meal-lamplight}
After the meal, the family stays together. The grandfather tells stories from his youth. About a great flood when he was a boy. About the year he walked all the way to the capital to see the emperor's parade. The children have heard these stories before. But they listen anyway.

[long-pause]

Then the grandmother tells the old stories. About the first emperors who taught people to farm. About the hero who shot down nine of the ten suns, when the earth was burning. About the goddess who lives on the moon with a white rabbit. The little boy curls up on her lap. He is trying very hard to stay awake.

[pause]

{img:han-grandmother-telling-stories-child-on-lap-brazier-glow-night}
The older children play games. One game is called liubo. Two players throw sticks like dice and move little pieces on a board. It is a game of luck and a little skill. The grandfather and the eldest son play. The grandfather wins. He always wins. The boy suspects he cheats a little. But he never catches him.

[long-pause]

Outside, from other houses, the sound of voices drifts over the walls. Someone is singing. Someone is laughing. Somewhere a drum is beating. All over Luoyang, families are doing the same. Sitting together. Keeping the year.

[pause]

{img:luoyang-night-new-years-eve-glowing-windows-rooftops-quiet-cold}
And the night goes on, slowly. The little boy falls asleep at last, his head on his grandmother's knee. Nobody wakes him. He has done his best.

[long-pause]

{img:bamboo-stalks-burning-courtyard-fire-popping-sparks-new-year-night}
In the middle of the night, before the new year arrives, there is a loud and happy noise. Pop! Crack! Bang!

[pause]

It comes from the courtyards all over the city. People are throwing pieces of bamboo onto fires.

[long-pause]

Bamboo is a hollow plant. Its stem is divided into sections, like little rooms, each one sealed off. When you put a piece of green bamboo in a fire, the air inside each section heats up. It has nowhere to go. It pushes and pushes. And then the bamboo bursts with a loud crack. Sparks fly up into the dark.

[pause]

People believe the noise frightens away bad spirits. Bad spirits do not like loud noises or bright lights. So on New Year's night, everyone makes as much noise and light as they can. The whole city crackles and pops.

[pause]

{img:han-children-watching-bamboo-burst-courtyard-fire-hands-over-ears}
Much later, a later book will mention this custom. People get up early on New Year's morning. And first of all, they burn bamboo in the courtyard to scare away mountain spirits. This custom is older than the book. Burning bamboo at New Year goes back a long way.

[long-pause]

Hundreds of years later, people in China will invent gunpowder. Then they will fill bamboo tubes with it, and later paper tubes. These will be the first firecrackers. That is why the Chinese word for firecrackers still means exploding bamboo. Every time you hear firecrackers at Chinese New Year today, you are hearing the great-great-grandchild of a piece of bamboo popping in a Han courtyard.

[pause]

In our family, the father builds a small fire in the courtyard. He has bought a bundle of fresh bamboo. The children stand back with their mother. The eldest son is allowed to throw the first piece. He tosses it into the flames. Nothing happens. Everyone waits. Has it gone wrong? Then, crack! It bursts with a loud bang. The girl screams and then laughs. The little boy wakes up on the grandmother's lap with a start. And then he laughs too.

[pause]

{img:sparks-rising-night-sky-luoyang-courtyards-many-fires-glow}
They throw piece after piece. Crack. Pop. Bang. Sparks fly up into the cold night. From the other courtyards comes the same sound, near and far, all over the city. And above the curved roofs, in the dark sky, the stars are shining.

[long-pause]

{img:han-gate-peach-wood-boards-door-gods-shentu-yulu-painted-figures}
Before the new year comes, the front gate of the house must be protected.

[pause]

For this, people use peach wood. The peach tree is special in China. Its fruit is a sign of long life. And its wood, people believe, has power over bad spirits. They are afraid of it.

[long-pause]

So at New Year, families hang two boards of peach wood on their gate. One on each door. On the boards, they paint or carve two figures. They are two brothers, the door gods. Their names are Shentu and Yulü.

[pause]

An old Han story explains why. Far out in the eastern sea, there is a mountain. On the mountain stands a huge peach tree. Its branches spread for many kilometres. Between its branches, in the north-east, is a gate. Through that gate, all the spirits come and go. And at the gate stand two brothers, Shentu and Yulü. They watch every spirit that passes through. If a spirit has done harm to people, they tie it up with a rope of reeds. And they feed it to a tiger.

[pause]

{img:giant-mythical-peach-tree-mountain-sea-gate-two-guardians-misty}
So people paint the two brothers on their gates. They hang a rope of reeds there too. And sometimes they paint a tiger on the wall nearby. That way no harmful spirit dares to come in.

[long-pause]

It is a fierce story. But for the people of Luoyang, it is a comfort. It means the house is safe. The two brothers stand guard all year. They look stern. But they are on the family's side.

[pause]

Later in Chinese history, other door gods will take their place. Famous generals painted in bright colours. And much later, people will hang red paper with lucky words on each side of the door. That custom grows out of the old peach wood boards. Even today, at Chinese New Year, millions of front doors have red paper and painted guardians. And the idea started here, with two brothers and a peach tree.

[pause]

{img:han-father-hanging-peach-boards-gate-son-holding-ladder-evening}
In our family, the father and the eldest son hang up the new peach boards on New Year's Eve. The old ones from last year come down. The boy has painted the faces himself this year. He took a lot of care. But one brother looks a bit cross-eyed. The father looks at it for a long time. Will he make him do it again? Then he smiles. It will scare the spirits even more, he says. And they hang it up.

[long-pause]

{img:han-new-years-morning-courtyard-dawn-family-new-clothes-bowing}
And then it is New Year's morning. The first day of the first month.

[pause]

Everyone gets up very early, before dawn. The family washes and dresses in their best clothes. The children have new clothes if possible. The daughter wears a new silk jacket, sewn by her mother. The eldest son has a new belt. The little boy has new cloth shoes. He keeps looking down at them.

[long-pause]

First, the family honours the ancestors. In the main room there is a small altar. On it stand wooden tablets with the names of the ancestors. Grandfather's father and grandfather's grandfather, and those before them. The family lays out food and wine before the tablets. The grandfather lights the incense. And everyone bows deeply, all the way to the floor. They tell the ancestors that a new year has begun. And they ask them to watch over the family.

[pause]

Then comes the most important part of the morning. The children and grandchildren greet the grandparents. The grandparents sit side by side on their mats. One by one, starting with the oldest son, everyone kneels before them and bows. They wish them a long life. And the grandparents bless them in return.

[pause]

{img:han-children-kneeling-bowing-to-grandparents-new-year-greeting}
Then the family drinks the New Year wine. It is a special wine, mixed with pepper and the leaves of a certain tree. People call it pepper wine. They believe it brings a long life and keeps sickness away.

[long-pause]

But there is a funny rule about who drinks first. Usually in China, the oldest person always goes first. But with the New Year wine, it is the other way around. The youngest drinks first. And the oldest drinks last.

[pause]

Why? An old Han writer explains it. The young are gaining a year. So we congratulate them first. The old are losing a year. So they drink last. It is a gentle joke about growing old. The grandfather pretends to be sad about it every year. Everyone laughs.

[pause]

{img:little-boy-sipping-small-cup-pepper-wine-family-smiling-morning}
So the little boy of four drinks first. He takes a tiny sip from a little cup. His face screws up. It is spicy and strange. Everyone laughs. Then his sister drinks, and his brother. Then the mother and father. And last of all, the grandmother and the grandfather. The grandfather lifts his cup high. To another year, he says. And he drinks it all in one go.

[long-pause]

Outside, the sun is coming up. Its first light falls on the courtyard and the peach boards on the gate. The new year has begun. And so far, everything has gone well.

[long-pause]

{img:han-ancestral-hall-tablets-altar-offerings-incense-quiet-light}
For a Han family, the ancestors are always close.

[pause]

People believe that when someone passes away, their spirit lives on. The spirit still cares for the family. It can help them or, if it is forgotten, it can be unhappy. So the family keeps the memory of the dead alive. They say their names. They give them food and drink. And they tell them the family news.

[long-pause]

Rich families have a special hall for the ancestors. Ordinary families have a small altar in the main room. On it stand the wooden name tablets. Every new moon and full moon, the family lights incense there. And at New Year, the ancestors are honoured most of all.

[pause]

Han people also take great care with graves. A family grave is often on a hillside outside the city, facing south. Rich families build big tombs underground, with rooms like a house. They fill them with things the dead might need. Food and wine. Clothes and lamps. And the little clay models.

[pause]

{img:han-tomb-chamber-painted-walls-pottery-models-servants-animals}
That is why we know so much about Han life. In the tombs, archaeologists have found models of everything. Houses with courtyards and towers. Farms with pigs in their pens. Wells with little buckets. Kitchens with cooks chopping fish. Musicians and dancers. Even little dogs with curly tails. They were made so the dead would not be lonely. And they tell us how the living lived.

[long-pause]

Some tombs also have paintings on the walls. They show feasts and journeys and the stars. In one famous Han tomb, a great painting on silk was found. It shows the world of heaven at the top, with the sun and the moon. In the middle is the noble lady who was buried there. She leans on a stick and walks with her servants. And at the bottom is the world under the earth. The painting is more than two thousand years old. But its colours are still bright.

[pause]

{img:han-silk-painting-heaven-sun-moon-lady-walking-servants-museum}
Honouring the ancestors is not only about the dead. It is about the living too. It teaches children who they are. Where their family came from. What their grandparents did. It ties the whole family together across many generations. And at New Year, when the family bows before the tablets, the little ones feel it. They are part of something long and old.

[long-pause]

In our family, after the ceremony, the grandfather takes the little boy by the hand. He shows him the tablets one by one and tells him the names. This was my father, he says. He was a farmer. He could carry two sacks of grain at once. This was my grandfather. He was a soldier on the northern wall. The little boy listens with big eyes. One day, says the grandfather, my name will be here too. And you will tell your grandchildren about me. The boy thinks about this. Then he nods, very seriously.

[long-pause]

{img:han-new-years-day-court-audience-officials-rows-palace-hall-dawn}
On New Year's morning, there is a great ceremony at the palace. It is the New Year's court.

[pause]

Before dawn, thousands of officials gather outside the palace gates. They have come from every office in the city. Some have travelled from far provinces. Princes are there, and generals, and envoys from foreign lands. They all wear their finest robes. Each robe has a colour and a badge to show their rank. Their hats are tall and black. And they stand in the cold, waiting.

[long-pause]

The father of our family is there too. He is only a small official. He stands far back in the crowd, near the gate. He has been told where to stand and when to bow. He is nervous. He has never been to the New Year's court before. If he bows at the wrong moment, everyone around him will notice.

[pause]

At dawn, drums and bells sound. The gates open. Guards in armour line the way. The officials walk in slowly, in long lines, by rank. The highest go first. They cross the great courtyard and climb the steps. The hall is vast and dim. Torches and lamps burn along the walls. And at the far end, high on his seat, sits the emperor.

[pause]

{img:emperor-han-seated-dais-jade-bead-crown-silk-robes-distant-hall}
The officials kneel in their rows and bow to the floor. A herald calls out their names and gifts. Each province sends gifts of its finest goods. Silk from one place. Lacquer from another. Rare fruit, horses, jade. The envoys from far countries bring strange things. Furs from the north. Pearls and spices from the south. The emperor watches and nods.

[long-pause]

Then the emperor gives gifts in return. Rolls of silk, cups of wine. And there is music. Bells and stone chimes and drums and flutes play the slow solemn music of the court. Then there are dancers and acrobats. Some play the parts of animals. One great show has a huge fish that turns into a dragon in front of everyone. The officials gasp.

[pause]

{img:han-court-musicians-bronze-bells-stone-chimes-playing-hall}
At last there is a great feast. The officials sit in rows and are served food and wine. Our father sits far at the back. He can barely see the emperor. But he has bowed at the right moments. Nobody has looked at him strangely. He breathes out slowly. And he lifts his cup of wine.

[long-pause]

In the evening he comes home tired and happy. The children crowd around him. Did you see the emperor? What was he like? What did you eat? The father sits down on his mat. Well, he says. I saw a very tall hat. And a lot of backs. And a fish that turned into a dragon. The children laugh. And he tells them everything.

[pause]

And then he reaches into his sleeve. He takes out a small wrapped parcel and gives it to his eldest son. The boy unwraps it slowly. Inside is a fine writing brush with a handle of red lacquer. The boy stares at it. Then he bows to his father, very low. He does not say anything. He does not need to.

[long-pause]

{img:han-feast-table-lacquer-dishes-meats-grains-wine-cups-guests}
What do people eat in Han China?

[pause]

In the north, the main food is millet and wheat. Millet is a small round grain. It is boiled into porridge or steamed. Wheat is ground into flour. In the Han time, people are learning to grind flour much better, with stone mills turned by hand or by animals. With flour, they make noodles and steamed buns and flat cakes. In the south, the main food is rice.

[long-pause]

With the grain, people eat vegetables. Cabbage and turnips and onions and garlic. Bamboo shoots and mushrooms and beans. They eat tofu, or something like it. They make sauces from fermented beans and salt. And they pickle everything, so it lasts through the winter.

[pause]

Meat is for special days, for most people. Pork above all. Chickens and ducks and geese. Fish from the rivers. Rich people eat beef and lamb and dog and pheasant and deer. One Han tomb held a list of the dishes buried with a lady. There were stews of beef and turnip, of deer and fish, of chicken and bamboo shoots. There were dumplings and cakes. The list goes on and on.

[pause]

{img:han-kitchen-model-cooks-chopping-fish-steaming-pot-stove}
People eat with chopsticks and spoons. Chopsticks are already old in China. They are made of bamboo or wood, or bronze for the rich. Food is cut into small pieces in the kitchen, so it can be picked up easily.

[long-pause]

There is a famous legend about New Year food from the Han time. It is about a doctor called Zhang Zhongjing. He was one of the greatest doctors of ancient China. One winter, the story goes, he came home to his village. It was bitterly cold. He saw that many poor people had sore frozen ears. Their ears were so cold they were cracked and red.

[pause]

So the doctor made a soup. He cooked lamb and pepper and warming herbs. He chopped them up and wrapped them in little pieces of dough. He folded each piece into the shape of an ear. He boiled them in the soup. And he gave a bowl to every poor person in the village, every day, until New Year. They grew warm. Their ears healed.

[pause]

{img:han-doctor-giving-bowls-soup-dumplings-poor-villagers-winter}
People say that is how dumplings began. Whether the story is true, nobody knows. But today, in the north of China, families still make dumplings together at New Year. They fold them into little shapes. And some people still say they look like ears.

[long-pause]

In our family, on New Year's Day, there is a big meal again. And the grandmother teaches her granddaughter to fold little dough parcels around a filling of meat and cabbage. The girl's first ones fall apart in the water. The second ones hold. By the end, she has made twenty. She counts them proudly. And she gives the first one to her grandfather.

[long-pause]

{img:twelve-zodiac-animals-han-style-carved-circle-rat-ox-tiger-rabbit}
Every Chinese New Year belongs to an animal. The year of the rat. The year of the ox. The year of the tiger. There are twelve animals. They take turns, one year each. After twelve years, the circle starts again.

[pause]

When did this begin? The twelve animals are very old. But the first time we find them all written down together is in the Han time.

[long-pause]

A Han writer called Wang Chong wrote a long book full of questions about the world. He liked to argue and think for himself. In his book he lists the animals that belong to the twelve hours of the day and the twelve directions. Rat, ox, tiger, rabbit. Dragon, snake, horse, sheep. Monkey, rooster, dog, pig. It is almost exactly the list people still use today.

[pause]

The twelve animals are also linked to the twelve double hours of the day. The hour of the rat is around midnight. The hour of the horse is around noon. People use them to tell the time and to choose lucky days.

[pause]

{img:wang-chong-han-scholar-writing-bamboo-strips-study-thinking}
There is an old story about how the animals got their order. It is told in many ways, and most of it comes from later times. But it goes something like this. The great ruler of heaven called all the animals to a race. The first twelve to arrive would each get a year.

[long-pause]

The animals had to cross a wide river. The ox was strong and steady. He swam across slowly. But the clever little rat climbed onto the ox's back without him noticing. Just as the ox reached the other bank, the rat jumped off and ran ahead. So the rat came first and the ox came second.

[pause]

The tiger came third, wet and tired from the strong current. The rabbit hopped across on stones and a floating log. The dragon came fifth. He could have flown and won. But he stopped on the way to make rain for some thirsty villages. The other animals came one by one. And last of all came the pig. He had stopped for a meal and a nap on the way.

[pause]

{img:ox-swimming-river-rat-on-back-other-animals-bank-folk-tale-scene}
And what about the cat? The story says the rat promised to wake the cat on the morning of the race. But he forgot. Or perhaps he did not forget. The cat slept late and missed the race. And that, people say, is why cats and rats have not been friends ever since.

[long-pause]

In our family, the children ask the grandmother which animal they are. You are a rooster, she tells the girl. Early to rise and proud. You are a dog, she tells the eldest son. Loyal and honest. And you are a pig, she tells the little boy. He frowns. A pig? The pig is lucky, says his grandmother. He enjoys his food and his sleep and everyone loves him. The little boy thinks about this. Then he nods. That sounds right.

[long-pause]

{img:han-family-visiting-relatives-gate-bowing-gifts-new-year-snow}
In the first days of the new year, everyone goes visiting.

[pause]

On the second and third day, families visit their relatives. The married daughters come home to see their parents. Uncles and aunts and cousins come by. Friends call on each other. Officials visit their superiors and their teachers. The streets of Luoyang are full of people in their best clothes, carrying small gifts.

[long-pause]

At each gate, people bow and wish each other a good new year. They say things like, may you have ten thousand years of joy. Or, may your house be full of good fortune. Then they go inside and sit on the mats. They drink a cup of warm wine and eat a little cake. They talk about the old year and the new one. Then they bow again and go on to the next house.

[pause]

It is tiring. Some people visit ten or twenty houses in a day. Their feet are cold and their faces are red from the wine. But it is important. The New Year visits keep friendships warm. A family that is not visited feels forgotten. And a person who does not visit seems rude.

[pause]

{img:han-guests-sitting-mats-warm-wine-cups-cakes-host-bowing-room}
In our family, the eldest daughter of the grandparents comes home on the second day. She is the father's older sister. She married a farmer in a village outside the city. She has walked half a day through the snow with her husband and their two small children. The grandmother has been waiting at the gate since morning. When she sees them coming down the street, she hurries out. She takes her daughter's cold hands in hers. And she does not let go for a long time.

[long-pause]

The cousins play together in the courtyard. They throw a feathered ball to each other with their feet, trying to keep it in the air. They play hide and seek among the rooms. They fight over the last honey cake. The grown-ups sit inside and talk and talk.

[pause]

{img:han-children-kicking-feather-shuttlecock-courtyard-cousins-laughing}
There is music too. The aunt's husband plays a small stringed instrument called a qin. He plucks it softly. The notes are low and sweet. The grandfather closes his eyes and listens. When the song ends, nobody says anything for a moment. Then the grandfather opens his eyes and says, again. And the aunt's husband plays it again.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the aunt and her family stay the night. The house is crowded. Children sleep three to a mat. But nobody minds. Outside, the snow begins to fall again, softly, on the curved roofs and the quiet courtyard. And inside, the whole family is together.

[pause]

The grandmother cannot sleep. She lies awake and listens to the soft breathing of all her children and grandchildren in the house. She counts them in her head. All of them are here. All of them are well. It has been a hard year. But it has ended well. And she closes her eyes at last and smiles in the dark.

[long-pause]

{img:han-imperial-academy-luoyang-students-courtyard-scholars-robes}
In Luoyang there is a great school. It is called the Imperial Academy.

[pause]

It was founded long ago by a Han emperor. He wanted the best young men of the empire to study the old books together. And then to serve the government. In the time of our story, the Academy is huge. Perhaps thirty thousand students live and study there. That makes it one of the biggest schools in the ancient world.

[long-pause]

The students study the five classics. These are very old books of poems and history and rituals and sayings. They are written in an old style that is hard to understand. The students learn them by heart. They study what the old teachers said about them. And they argue about what every line means.

[pause]

The best students take examinations. If they pass, they can become officials. That is the dream of many families. A son who becomes an official brings honour to his parents and his ancestors. And a steady income too.

[pause]

{img:stone-classics-tablets-row-scholars-copying-texts-han-courtyard}
In the years after our story, there will be a famous project at the Academy. The classics will be carved in stone, on great flat tablets. They will be set up in a long row in front of the Academy. That way everyone can read the true words, without mistakes from copying. Scholars will come from all over China to copy them. So many people will come that the streets will be blocked with carts.

[long-pause]

At New Year, many students go home to their families. But some live too far away. They stay in Luoyang. They celebrate together, far from home. They share what little food they have. They write poems and letters to their families. And they wonder how things are back in their villages.

[pause]

The eldest son of our family dreams of going to the Academy one day. But the studying is hard. Some days he wonders if he is clever enough. His father went there for two years when he was young. But he never passed the highest examination. He became a small official instead. He does not want his son to feel the same pressure he felt.

[pause]

{img:father-and-son-walking-past-academy-gate-evening-snow-talking}
On the fourth day of the new year, the father takes his son for a walk past the Academy. They stand at the gate and look in at the courtyards and the halls. Do you want to study here one day, the father asks. The boy thinks for a long time. I do not know, he says honestly. I want to. But I am afraid I will fail. The father puts his hand on the boy's shoulder. Then we will find out together, he says. And they walk home through the snow.

[long-pause]

{img:han-lantern-festival-first-full-moon-lanterns-glowing-city-night}
On the fifteenth day of the first month, there is the first full moon of the new year. And on that night, the city glows with lanterns.

[pause]

This is the last night of the New Year celebrations. After this, everyone goes back to work. So people want to make it special.

[long-pause]

In later centuries, the Lantern Festival will be one of the great festivals of China. People will hang lanterns of every shape and colour all over the cities. Lanterns shaped like fish and rabbits and dragons. There will be riddles written on the lanterns. People will walk the streets all night.

[pause]

In the Han time, the festival is just beginning. An old story says it started with a Han emperor. He had heard about the Buddha from far away. On the night of the first full moon, he ordered that lamps be lit in the palace and in the temples, to honour the Buddha. And the people of the city lit lamps too.

[pause]

{img:white-horse-temple-luoyang-han-lamps-monks-night-full-moon}
Whether that story is true, nobody knows. But the Buddha's teaching really did arrive in Luoyang in the Han time. The story says that a Han emperor once dreamed of a golden figure flying over his palace. His advisers told him it must be the Buddha, a holy man from the west. So the emperor sent messengers to find out more. They came back with two monks from India. And the monks came with a white horse carrying holy books.

[long-pause]

The emperor built them a temple outside Luoyang. It was called the White Horse Temple, after the horse. It is said to be the first Buddhist temple in China. It still stands near Luoyang today. And every year, thousands of visitors walk through its gates.

[pause]

On the night of the first full moon, the people of Luoyang go out. They walk through the streets in the cold air. Lamps glow in doorways and windows. The full moon rises huge and white over the city walls. Children carry little lamps of their own.

[pause]

{img:han-children-carrying-small-lamps-street-full-moon-snow-parents}
In our family, the children each have a little clay lamp. The father lights them from the kitchen fire. The family walks together through the neighbourhood. The little boy holds his lamp very carefully with both hands. He walks slowly so it will not go out. A gust of wind comes down the street. His flame flickers. He stops. He holds his breath. The flame bends and shrinks. And then it stands up straight again. He looks up at his mother with a huge smile.

[long-pause]

They walk until their feet are cold. Then they go home. The moon follows them all the way. The New Year is over now. Tomorrow, life will go back to normal. But tonight, the whole city is shining.

[long-pause]

{img:silk-road-caravan-camels-desert-dunes-han-traders-sunset}
Far to the west of Luoyang, beyond the mountains and the deserts, runs a long road. It is the road that carries Chinese silk to the rest of the world. Much later, people will call it the Silk Road.

[pause]

It began in the Han time. About two hundred and eighty years before our story, a Han official called Zhang Qian was sent west. The emperor wanted to find friends against the horse-riding peoples of the north. Zhang Qian travelled for years. He was captured and held prisoner for ten years. He escaped. He reached lands nobody in China had seen before. And thirteen years after he left, he came home. Of the hundred men who set out, only two came back.

[long-pause]

He did not find the friends the emperor wanted. But he brought back something else. Knowledge. He told the emperor about kingdoms in the far west. About cities and markets and great horses. About grapes and new kinds of plants. After that, the Han sent trade missions west. Soldiers guarded the road. Little forts were built along the desert edge. And the caravans began to move.

[pause]

{img:han-desert-watchtower-beacon-fort-great-wall-western-end}
The road goes west from China, through the long narrow land between the mountains and the desert. Then it goes around a huge sandy desert. It is so dry and so dangerous that people say, you go in, but you do not come out. The caravans travel from oasis to oasis. An oasis is a green place in the desert with water and trees. Each one is a little town where travellers can rest and trade.

[long-pause]

From China, the caravans carry silk and lacquer and iron. From the west, they bring horses and jade and glass and wool and spices. They bring grapes and walnuts and new musical instruments. They bring ideas and stories and faiths. The Buddha's teaching travels along this road too.

[pause]

Hardly anyone travels the whole road. Goods pass from trader to trader, from city to city. A roll of silk from Luoyang might change hands ten times before it reaches Rome. By then it costs many times more than at the start.

[pause]

{img:oasis-town-tarim-basin-poplars-water-channels-traders-resting}
At New Year, the traders on the road far from home think of their families. In the little oasis towns they gather in the inns. They drink a cup of wine together and wish each other a good year. One day, they hope, they will be back in Luoyang for the New Year. With money in their purses and stories to tell.

[long-pause]

{img:han-envoy-gan-ying-standing-shore-western-sea-looking-out}
There is one more story from the road. About fifty years before our story, a Han general sent an envoy called Gan Ying to find the great empire in the far west. The Chinese called it Da Qin. We call it Rome. Gan Ying travelled for months through deserts and mountains. At last he reached the shore of a great sea. Perhaps it was the sea near Persia. The sailors there told him the crossing could take three months, or even two years. Many men got homesick on the way, they said. Some never came back. Gan Ying stood on the shore and looked out at the water for a long time. Then he turned around and went home. So close. Rome and China never met. But silk still found its way, roll by roll, all the way across.

[long-pause]

{img:zhang-heng-han-scholar-astronomer-star-map-armillary-sphere}
In the time of our story, Luoyang is home to some of the cleverest people in the world. One of them is a man called Zhang Heng.

[pause]

He has passed away not long before our story. But everyone in Luoyang still knows his name. He was a poet and a painter. He was a mathematician and an astronomer. He was the head of the court astronomers. And he was an inventor.

[long-pause]

Zhang Heng studied the stars. He made a map of the sky with more than two thousand five hundred stars. He built a great bronze globe with rings around it. It showed the movements of the stars and the sun. Water turned it slowly, so it kept pace with the real sky. He understood that the moon does not make its own light. It shines because the sun lights it up.

[pause]

And he built a famous machine to detect earthquakes. Earthquakes happen often in China. They can be terrible. The court wanted to know about them quickly, even when they happened far away. So Zhang Heng built a great bronze jar about two metres wide. Around the outside were eight dragons, facing eight directions. Each dragon held a small bronze ball in its mouth. Below each dragon sat a bronze toad with its mouth open.

[pause]

{img:han-seismoscope-bronze-jar-eight-dragons-toads-balls-palace-room}
When the earth shook, a weight inside the jar moved. It pushed a little lever. And one dragon dropped its ball. Clang! The ball fell into the toad's mouth below. The direction of that dragon showed where the earthquake had come from.

[long-pause]

At first, nobody believed it worked. One day, a ball dropped. Clang. But nobody in Luoyang had felt anything. The officials laughed. Zhang Heng's machine is broken, they said. Then, a few days later, a messenger arrived on a tired horse. He came from a province far to the west. There had been an earthquake there. In exactly the direction the dragon had pointed. After that, nobody laughed.

[pause]

The machine is lost now. But people have tried to rebuild it from the old descriptions. And it is still one of the most famous inventions of ancient China.

[pause]

{img:han-astronomers-night-sky-luoyang-tower-new-year-stars-cold}
On New Year's night, the court astronomers are on their tower as always. They watch the sky. They look for anything unusual. A new star, a comet, a strange light. Anything that might be a message from heaven about the year ahead. This year the sky is clear and calm. The stars are in their places. The astronomers write it down carefully. A good sign, they report to the emperor. And everyone in the palace is glad.

[long-pause]

{img:han-dynasty-end-luoyang-autumn-palace-quiet-fallen-leaves}
The New Year in our story is a peaceful one. But the Han dynasty will not last forever.

[pause]

In the years after our story, troubles grow at court. Powerful families and palace servants fight for power. The emperors are young and weak. In the countryside, floods and bad harvests make people poor and hungry. Many lose their land. Some begin to follow new teachers who promise a better world.

[long-pause]

About thirty-five years after our story, a great rising breaks out. The rebels wear yellow scarves on their heads. The Han armies put the rising down. But the generals who did it become too powerful. They start to fight each other. And in the year 220, the last Han emperor gives up his throne. After four hundred years, the Han dynasty is over.

[pause]

China splits into three kingdoms. Then into many more. For almost four hundred years, there will be war and division. We will leave those hard years there, gently, in the past.

[pause]

{img:three-kingdoms-later-china-misty-mountains-river-boats-quiet}
But the Han are never forgotten. Even today, most people in China call themselves the Han people. They call the Chinese characters Han characters. The Han time is remembered as a golden age. A time when China was united and strong and full of new ideas.

[long-pause]

And many of the New Year customs we saw tonight lived on. Through all the troubles and changes of the next two thousand years, families kept them going. They cleaned their houses before the new year. They honoured the stove god and the ancestors. They hung guardians on their gates. They burned bamboo, and later firecrackers. They stayed up on the last night of the year. They bowed to their grandparents in the morning. They made dumplings. And they lit lamps on the first full moon.

[pause]

The Han calendar lived on too. The new year still begins on the second new moon after midwinter. And the twelve animals still take their turns, year after year.

[pause]

{img:chinese-family-reunion-dinner-generations-round-table-later-lamps}
In later times, the festival grew even bigger. Lanterns and red paper and lion dances and great fireworks. But at its heart it stayed the same as in the Han time. A family, together, at the turning of the year. Hoping that the new year will be better than the old.

[long-pause]

{img:modern-chinese-new-year-red-lanterns-street-night-crowds-today}
Today, Chinese New Year is celebrated by more than a billion people all over the world.

[pause]

In China it is called the Spring Festival. It is the biggest holiday of the year. Hundreds of millions of people travel home to their families. Trains and buses and planes are packed for weeks. It is the largest yearly journey of people on earth.

[long-pause]

In cities all over the world, from Beijing to London to Sydney to San Francisco, the streets fill with red lanterns. People hang red paper with lucky words beside their doors. Lion dancers leap and shake their heads to the beat of drums. Firecrackers pop and bang. And families sit down together for a great dinner on New Year's Eve.

[pause]

And today Luoyang is still there. It is a big modern city in the middle of China, beside the river Luo. In spring, it is famous for its peonies. Big soft flowers in pink and red and white. In the fields outside the city lie the ruins of the old Han capital. Low mounds of earth that were once walls. Patches of ground where palaces once stood. And near the city, the White Horse Temple still welcomes visitors, as it has for almost two thousand years.

[pause]

{img:luoyang-today-white-horse-temple-gate-stone-horses-morning-visitors}
In museums, you can see the little clay figures from the Han tombs. The cooks and the musicians and the dogs. The laughing storyteller with his drum. The bronze lamp shaped like a kneeling girl. They look back at us across two thousand years. And they remind us that people then were not so different from us.

[long-pause]

So we leave Luoyang tonight, in the Han time, on the night of the first full moon of the new year.

[pause]

The lamps are going out one by one in the windows. The streets are quiet now. Snow lies thin on the curved roofs and on the peach wood boards by the gates. In the courtyards, the ashes of the bamboo fires have gone cold. In the palace, the emperor sleeps behind his red pillars. On their tower, the astronomers watch the clear calm sky. And in a small house near the palace, a family is asleep. The grandparents in the south room. The parents in the east room. The children in the west room, under their padded quilts. The little boy holds his clay lamp, now cold, in his sleep. And over the whole sleeping city, the full moon shines, white and round and quiet. With a little rabbit on its face.

[pause]
