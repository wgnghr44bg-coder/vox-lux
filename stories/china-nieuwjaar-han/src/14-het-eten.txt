What do people eat in Han China?

In the north, the main food is millet and wheat. Millet is a small round grain. It is boiled into porridge or steamed. Wheat is ground into flour. In the Han time, people are learning to grind flour much better, with stone mills turned by hand or by animals. With flour, they make noodles and steamed buns and flat cakes. In the south, the main food is rice.

--

With the grain, people eat vegetables. Cabbage and turnips and onions and garlic. Bamboo shoots and mushrooms and beans. They eat tofu, or something like it. They make sauces from fermented beans and salt. And they pickle everything, so it lasts through the winter.

Meat is for special days, for most people. Pork above all. Chickens and ducks and geese. Fish from the rivers. Rich people eat beef and lamb and dog and pheasant and deer. One Han tomb held a list of the dishes buried with a lady. There were stews of beef and turnip, of deer and fish, of chicken and bamboo shoots. There were dumplings and cakes. The list goes on and on.

{img:han-kitchen-model-cooks-chopping-fish-steaming-pot-stove}
People eat with chopsticks and spoons. Chopsticks are already old in China. They are made of bamboo or wood, or bronze for the rich. Food is cut into small pieces in the kitchen, so it can be picked up easily.

--

There is a famous legend about New Year food from the Han time. It is about a doctor called Zhang Zhongjing. He was one of the greatest doctors of ancient China. One winter, the story goes, he came home to his village. It was bitterly cold. He saw that many poor people had sore frozen ears. Their ears were so cold they were cracked and red.

So the doctor made a soup. He cooked lamb and pepper and warming herbs. He chopped them up and wrapped them in little pieces of dough. He folded each piece into the shape of an ear. He boiled them in the soup. And he gave a bowl to every poor person in the village, every day, until New Year. They grew warm. Their ears healed.

{img:han-doctor-giving-bowls-soup-dumplings-poor-villagers-winter}
People say that is how dumplings began. Whether the story is true, nobody knows. But today, in the north of China, families still make dumplings together at New Year. They fold them into little shapes. And some people still say they look like ears.

--

In our family, on New Year's Day, there is a big meal again. And the grandmother teaches her granddaughter to fold little dough parcels around a filling of meat and cabbage. The girl's first ones fall apart in the water. The second ones hold. By the end, she has made twenty. She counts them proudly. And she gives the first one to her grandfather.
