The New Year in our story is a peaceful one. But the Han dynasty will not last forever.

In the years after our story, troubles grow at court. Powerful families and palace servants fight for power. The emperors are young and weak. In the countryside, floods and bad harvests make people poor and hungry. Many lose their land. Some begin to follow new teachers who promise a better world.

--

About thirty-five years after our story, a great rising breaks out. The rebels wear yellow scarves on their heads. The Han armies put the rising down. But the generals who did it become too powerful. They start to fight each other. And in the year 220, the last Han emperor gives up his throne. After four hundred years, the Han dynasty is over.

China splits into three kingdoms. Then into many more. For almost four hundred years, there will be war and division. We will leave those hard years there, gently, in the past.

{img:three-kingdoms-later-china-misty-mountains-river-boats-quiet}
But the Han are never forgotten. Even today, most people in China call themselves the Han people. They call the Chinese characters Han characters. The Han time is remembered as a golden age. A time when China was united and strong and full of new ideas.

--

And many of the New Year customs we saw tonight lived on. Through all the troubles and changes of the next two thousand years, families kept them going. They cleaned their houses before the new year. They honoured the stove god and the ancestors. They hung guardians on their gates. They burned bamboo, and later firecrackers. They stayed up on the last night of the year. They bowed to their grandparents in the morning. They made dumplings. And they lit lamps on the first full moon.

The Han calendar lived on too. The new year still begins on the second new moon after midwinter. And the twelve animals still take their turns, year after year.

{img:chinese-family-reunion-dinner-generations-round-table-later-lamps}
In later times, the festival grew even bigger. Lanterns and red paper and lion dances and great fireworks. But at its heart it stayed the same as in the Han time. A family, together, at the turning of the year. Hoping that the new year will be better than the old.

Over the centuries, new customs were added to the old ones. People began to hang red paper strips with lucky words on both sides of their doors. Children received red envelopes with a little money inside. Families made dumplings together on New Year's Eve, folding them into the shape of old silver coins. But the oldest customs stayed too. The cleaning, the honouring of the ancestors, the family meal and the bowing to the grandparents. They have been passed down from the Han time until today.

In many Chinese families today, the grandparents still tell the old stories at New Year. They tell about the kitchen god and the door gods and the creature afraid of loud noises. The children listen, just as children listened in Luoyang so long ago. And so the stories travel on, from one generation to the next, like a lantern passed from hand to hand.
