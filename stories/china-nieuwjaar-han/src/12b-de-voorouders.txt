For a Han family, the ancestors are always close.

People believe that when someone passes away, their spirit lives on. The spirit still cares for the family. It can help them or, if it is forgotten, it can be unhappy. So the family keeps the memory of the dead alive. They say their names. They give them food and drink. And they tell them the family news.

--

Rich families have a special hall for the ancestors. Ordinary families have a small altar in the main room. On it stand the wooden name tablets. Every new moon and full moon, the family lights incense there. And at New Year, the ancestors are honoured most of all.

Han people also take great care with graves. A family grave is often on a hillside outside the city, facing south. Rich families build big tombs underground, with rooms like a house. They fill them with things the dead might need. Food and wine. Clothes and lamps. And the little clay models.

{img:han-tomb-chamber-painted-walls-pottery-models-servants-animals}
That is why we know so much about Han life. In the tombs, archaeologists have found models of everything. Houses with courtyards and towers. Farms with pigs in their pens. Wells with little buckets. Kitchens with cooks chopping fish. Musicians and dancers. Even little dogs with curly tails. They were made so the dead would not be lonely. And they tell us how the living lived.

--

Some tombs also have paintings on the walls. They show feasts and journeys and the stars. In one famous Han tomb, a great painting on silk was found. It shows the world of heaven at the top, with the sun and the moon. In the middle is the noble lady who was buried there. She leans on a stick and walks with her servants. And at the bottom is the world under the earth. The painting is more than two thousand years old. But its colours are still bright.

{img:han-silk-painting-heaven-sun-moon-lady-walking-servants-museum}
Honouring the ancestors is not only about the dead. It is about the living too. It teaches children who they are. Where their family came from. What their grandparents did. It ties the whole family together across many generations. And at New Year, when the family bows before the tablets, the little ones feel it. They are part of something long and old.

--

In our family, after the ceremony, the grandfather takes the little boy by the hand. He shows him the tablets one by one and tells him the names. This was my father, he says. He was a farmer. He could carry two sacks of grain at once. This was my grandfather. He was a soldier on the northern wall. The little boy listens with big eyes. One day, says the grandfather, my name will be here too. And you will tell your grandchildren about me. The boy thinks about this. Then he nods, very seriously.
