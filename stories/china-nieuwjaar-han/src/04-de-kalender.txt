When does the new year begin? That is not as simple as it sounds.

The Chinese count months by the moon. A new month begins at each new moon, when the moon is dark. Twelve moons make about one year. But not exactly. Twelve moons are about eleven days shorter than a year of the sun. So after a few years, the months drift away from the seasons. Spring festivals start to fall in winter. Something must be done.

--

So every two or three years, the astronomers add an extra month. That brings the calendar back in step with the sun. It is careful, difficult work. The court astronomers watch the sun and the moon and the stars night after night. They measure the shadow of a tall pole at noon. The shortest shadow means midsummer. The longest shadow means midwinter.

In earlier times, the year began at different moments. Some rulers started it in winter. Some started it in autumn. It was confusing. Then, about two hundred and fifty years before our story, a Han emperor ordered a new calendar. The new year would begin on the second new moon after midwinter. That is somewhere between late January and the middle of February.

{img:han-calendar-bamboo-slips-written-characters-desk-brush-ink}
That choice has lasted almost two thousand years. It is why Chinese New Year still falls in late January or February today. When people all over the world celebrate it now, they follow a rule made by a Han emperor and his astronomers.

--

The calendar matters to everyone. Farmers need to know when to plough and when to sow. Officials need to know when to hold ceremonies. Families need to know which days are lucky for weddings and journeys. So every year the court sends out the new calendar to every corner of the empire. It is written on strips of bamboo or wood. Officials read it out in the towns. And everyone waits to hear when the new year will begin.

{img:han-village-official-reading-calendar-to-farmers-winter-square}
This year, the astronomers have done their work. The extra month was added last year. The calendar is right. The date is known. In a few weeks, on the night of the new moon, the old year will end. And all over China, people are getting ready.

In the family's house, the grandmother keeps a small calendar of her own, in her head. She knows exactly how many days are left until the New Year. Every morning at breakfast, she tells the children. Twenty days, she says, and then nineteen, and then eighteen. The little boy counts on his fingers, but he runs out of fingers too soon. So his sister lends him hers. And they count together, very seriously, until they lose count and start to laugh.

In the countryside, the farmers also follow the calendar closely. It tells them when the cold will end and when to start ploughing again. In the long winter weeks, they mend their tools and their fences. They twist new rope from straw and sharpen their hoes. The women weave cloth and sew warm clothes for the children. And everyone looks forward to the New Year, when the work stops for a while and the families can rest together.
