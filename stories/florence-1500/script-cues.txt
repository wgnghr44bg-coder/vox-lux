Tonight we travel more than five hundred years back in time. We go to Italy, to a city of red roofs and stone towers in a green valley. The year is around 1500. And the city is called Florence.

[long-pause]

It is early morning in spring. A soft golden light is spreading over the hills around the city. The hills are covered with olive trees and vineyards, with tall dark cypress trees and little stone farmhouses. In the valley below, the river Arno winds slowly through the city. Its water is calm and green, and a light mist lies on it. Four stone bridges cross the river, one after another.

[pause]

And in the middle of the city, above all the red-tiled roofs, there is an enormous dome. It is red too, with white stone ribs, and a little lantern of white marble on the top. It belongs to the cathedral of Florence. It is the biggest dome in the world. The first rays of the sun touch it, and it glows warm and golden. Church bells begin to ring, all over the city. And Florence wakes up.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:florence-street-1500-stone-palaces-shops-people-renaissance-clothes-morning}
Florence around the year 1500 is one of the most remarkable cities in the world. It is not very big, perhaps sixty or seventy thousand people. But it is rich and busy and full of new ideas. It is a city of merchants and bankers, of weavers and goldsmiths. And it is a city of artists. Some of the greatest artists who ever lived are working here, at the same time, in these narrow streets.

[pause]

This time is called the Renaissance. The word means rebirth. The people of Florence believe that they are bringing back to life the art and learning of ancient Greece and Rome. They study old books and old statues. They try to understand the world in new ways. They paint pictures that look real, with depth and light and shadow. And they carve statues that seem almost alive.

[pause]

Tonight we will spend some years in Florence, with an ordinary family. We will meet a stonemason who works in the great workshop of the cathedral. We will meet his wife, who weaves silk on a loom at home. We will meet their son, who is learning to paint in an artist's workshop. And we will meet their daughter, who wants to see everything and understand everything.

[long-pause]

We will walk through the markets and along the river. We will watch the painters mixing their colours, and the sculptors carving marble. We will meet Leonardo da Vinci, painting a portrait of a quiet young woman. And we will meet a young sculptor called Michelangelo, carving a giant out of a block of marble. We will see how the whole city came out to watch, when that giant was moved through the streets.

{img:young-girl-looking-up-at-cathedral-dome-florence-piazza-morning-wonder}
So let us begin in the square in front of the cathedral, on a spring morning around 1500. The great doors of the baptistery are glowing golden in the sun. Pigeons are strutting across the stones. Shopkeepers are opening their shutters. And a girl of eleven is standing in the middle of the square, with her head tipped back, looking up at the great red dome. She does this every morning. And every morning, it takes her breath away.

[pause]

In the streets, the shops are opening one by one. A baker carries a tray of fresh round loaves on his head. A goldsmith opens the wooden shutters of his little workshop. A woman sets out baskets of eggs and cheese on a stone bench. A boy leads a donkey loaded with firewood through the narrow street. And the smell of bread and wood smoke drifts through the cool morning air.

[pause]

In the hills around the city, the farmers are already at work in the olive groves and vineyards. Oxen pull wooden ploughs slowly through the red earth. Shepherds lead their sheep out to the meadows. Little church bells ring in the villages. And from the hills, the farmers can see the great dome in the valley, rising above the city, as they have every morning of their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:palazzo-vecchio-florence-1500-tall-tower-piazza-della-signoria-crowd}
Florence is not ruled by a king or a duke. It is a republic. That means it is ruled by its own citizens, or at least by some of them.

[pause]

The government meets in a great stone palace with a tall tower, in the main square of the city. The palace is called the Palazzo della Signoria. Today we call it the Palazzo Vecchio, the old palace. Every two months, a new group of leaders is chosen, by drawing names from bags. They live in the palace for those two months and make decisions for the city. In 1502, the city also chooses a leader for life, called the gonfaloniere. His name is Piero Soderini.

[long-pause]

The city is organised into guilds. A guild is a group of people who do the same kind of work. There is a guild of wool merchants and a guild of silk merchants. There is a guild of bankers and a guild of doctors and spice sellers. There is a guild of stonemasons and carpenters. Each guild has its own rules, its own meeting hall and its own saint. The guilds are very important. Only members of the guilds can take part in the government.

[pause]

{img:guild-hall-florence-merchants-meeting-wooden-benches-banners-saints}
For most of the last sixty years, Florence was really ruled by one rich family, the Medici. They were bankers, the richest in Europe. The most famous of them was Lorenzo de' Medici, called Lorenzo the Magnificent. He loved art and poetry, and helped many artists. But in 1494, two years after Lorenzo passed away, the Medici were sent away from Florence. Now the city is a republic again, and the people are proud of it.

[long-pause]

A few years before our story, there was a strict preacher in Florence called Savonarola. He gave fiery sermons in the cathedral, and told people to give up their luxuries. For a few years, many people followed him. People even burned fine clothes, paintings and books in a great fire in the main square. But later, the city turned against him, and his time came to an end. Now, around 1500, Florence is calmer again. People are glad to enjoy art and music once more.

[pause]

{img:florentine-merchant-counting-coins-desk-ledger-gold-florins-window}
Florence is rich because of trade and banking. The merchants of Florence buy wool from England and Spain. They make it into fine cloth, and sell it all over Europe. They also make silk, beautiful and very expensive. Florentine bankers lend money to kings and popes. They have offices in London and Bruges, Rome and Venice. The gold coin of Florence is called the florin. It is trusted all over Europe.

[long-pause]

The city has strong walls all around it, with tall gates and towers. Inside the walls, there are narrow streets of stone, with tall houses and grand palaces. The palaces of the rich families are made of big rough stones at the bottom and smoother stones above. They have big wooden doors and courtyards inside. Each family wants its palace to be grander than the others.

[pause]

{img:florence-city-walls-gate-1500-farmers-carts-entering-morning-hills}
Outside the walls, the countryside is beautiful. There are farms and villas, olive groves and vineyards. Many rich families have a villa in the hills, where they go in summer to escape the heat. Farmers bring their produce into the city every morning, through the gates. They bring wine and olive oil, vegetables and cheese, chickens and eggs. And the city feeds on the rich land of Tuscany all around it.

[pause]

Florence has many beautiful churches, built by different religious orders and rich families. There are big churches with plain brick fronts, and churches covered with white and green marble. Inside, they are full of paintings and statues, chapels and tombs. Each neighbourhood has its own church, and each church has its own feast day. The bells of all these churches fill the city with sound.

[pause]

Every evening, when the sun goes down, the gates of the city are closed. Guards stand at each gate through the night. Nobody may come in or go out until the morning. Inside the walls, the streets become dark and quiet. Watchmen walk through the city with lanterns, calling out the hours. And the people of Florence sleep safely, behind their strong walls.

[long-pause]

{img:florence-cathedral-dome-close-red-tiles-white-ribs-marble-lantern-blue-sky}
The great dome of the cathedral is the pride of Florence. Every Florentine loves it. And it has a wonderful story.

[pause]

The cathedral was begun about two hundred years before our story. The people of Florence wanted it to be the most beautiful cathedral in the world. They built the walls and the long nave. They built the base for a great dome, an eight-sided opening, high above the floor. But then they had a problem. The opening was so wide that nobody knew how to build a dome over it. For many years, the cathedral stood with a great hole in its roof.

[long-pause]

In those days, domes and arches were built using a wooden frame underneath, to hold up the stones until they were finished. But this opening was too wide and too high for any wooden frame. There were not enough trees in all of Tuscany to build one. So, in 1418, the city held a competition. Anyone who had a good idea for building the dome could come and explain it.

[pause]

{img:brunelleschi-architect-plans-models-workshop-candles-1420-thinking}
The winner was a goldsmith and clockmaker called Filippo Brunelleschi. He was a small, clever, stubborn man. He had studied the old Roman buildings in Rome for years. He had a plan to build the dome without any wooden frame at all. He would build it in two shells, one inside the other, lighter than a single thick dome. And he would lay the bricks in a special pattern, like the bones of a fish, so that each ring of bricks held itself up.

[long-pause]

Many people thought his idea was impossible. But Brunelleschi was given the job. The building took sixteen years. Hundreds of workers climbed up and down every day. Brunelleschi invented new machines to lift the heavy stones and bricks, pulled by oxen walking round and round. He even had little kitchens built high up on the dome, so the workers would not waste time climbing down for lunch. And in 1436, the great dome was finished.

[pause]

{img:workers-building-dome-scaffolding-ox-hoist-bricks-herringbone-1430s}
In the year 1500, the dome has stood for more than sixty years. It is the tallest building in Florence, and the biggest dome in the world. It can be seen from far away, from every hill around the city. Travellers coming to Florence see it first, rising above everything. They say it is so big that it covers all the people of Tuscany with its shadow.

[long-pause]

Inside the cathedral, the dome is so high that people look like little ants beneath it. The light comes in through round windows of coloured glass. In the evening, the inside is dim and quiet and cool. People come in to pray, or just to sit and rest. Some of them light candles. Some of them just look up into the great dome, and wonder how anyone could have built it.

[pause]

{img:inside-florence-cathedral-dome-above-small-people-below-coloured-windows-light}
Next to the cathedral there is a tall, elegant bell tower, covered in white, green and pink marble. It was designed by the great painter Giotto, long ago. And in front of the cathedral, there is the baptistery, a smaller eight-sided building where babies are baptised. Its doors are made of bronze, covered with gold. They show stories from the Bible, so beautifully made that people call them the Gates of Paradise.

[pause]

On the top of the dome, there is a great copper ball, covered with gold, with a cross on top. It was put there about thirty years before our story. A young apprentice helped to make it and lift it into place. His name was Leonardo da Vinci. He was only about twenty years old. He watched the clever machines that lifted the heavy ball, and he made drawings of them in his notebook.

[pause]

Bianca often asks her father about the dome. How did they lift the bricks so high? How did they know it would not fall down? Piero tells her what he knows. He says that the old stonemasons of the cathedral workshop still tell stories about Brunelleschi. They say he was clever and stubborn, and that he never gave up. Bianca thinks she would like to be like that.

[long-pause]

{img:florentine-stonemason-father-1500-leather-apron-chisel-dusty-hands-kind}
Let us meet a family who lives in Florence, in a narrow street not far from the cathedral.

[pause]

The father is called Piero. He is a stonemason, about forty years old. He works in the great workshop of the cathedral, called the Opera del Duomo. The workshop looks after the cathedral and all its buildings. Piero cuts and shapes stone for repairs. He carves decorations and steps and window frames. His hands are strong and covered with little scars, and his hair is always grey with stone dust. He is a calm, patient man who loves his work.

[pause]

The mother is called Caterina. She is a silk weaver. She has a loom in the main room of the house, where she weaves silk cloth for a merchant. The merchant brings her the silk thread, and she weaves it into beautiful fabric, with patterns of flowers and leaves. It is slow, careful work. She is paid for each length of cloth she weaves. She also runs the house and looks after the family.

[long-pause]

They have two children. The son is called Matteo, and he is thirteen. He is an apprentice in the workshop of a painter, near the river. He grinds colours, prepares wooden panels and sweeps the floor. He is learning to draw and to paint, little by little. He dreams of becoming a great painter one day, like the famous masters of Florence.

[pause]

{img:boy-apprentice-painter-workshop-grinding-pigments-stone-slab-1500}
The daughter is called Bianca, and she is eleven. She is curious about everything. She asks questions all day long. Why is the sky blue? How do birds fly? How did they build the dome? She helps her mother with the silk, winding the thread onto little bobbins. But she would much rather be outside, exploring the city. She loves to visit her father at the cathedral workshop and watch the stonemasons at work.

[long-pause]

The grandmother lives with them too. She is Caterina's mother, and her name is Lucia. Everyone calls her Nonna. She is about seventy, small and round, with bright eyes and white hair under a linen cap. She was once a silk weaver too, and she taught Caterina everything she knows. Now her fingers are stiff, but she still spins a little and sews. And she tells the best stories in the whole street.

[pause]

{img:italian-grandmother-1500-linen-cap-spinning-window-sunlight-smiling}
Nonna Lucia remembers the old days, when Lorenzo the Magnificent ruled Florence. She saw him once, riding through the streets on a fine horse, with his friends and his servants. She remembers the great festivals of those years, with parades and music and fireworks. She remembers when the baptistery doors were new and shining. And she remembers the day the great dome was finished, when she was a little girl, sitting on her father's shoulders.

[long-pause]

The family is not rich, but they are not poor either. Piero earns a steady wage from the cathedral workshop. Caterina earns a little from her weaving. They have enough to eat, and a little to spare. On feast days, they can buy meat and good wine. And Piero has been able to pay for Matteo to become an apprentice. That is something he is very proud of.

[pause]

{img:florentine-family-supper-1500-table-bread-wine-soup-candles-evening}
In the evenings, the family eats together at a wooden table, by the light of an oil lamp. There is bread and soup, beans with olive oil, and sometimes a little cheese or fish. Piero tells them about his day at the cathedral. Matteo tells them about the painter's workshop. Bianca asks a hundred questions. And Nonna Lucia tells a story about the old days, while Caterina clears the table and listens with a smile.

[pause]

The family also has a cat, a fat tabby cat called Moro. He spends his days sleeping in the sun on the loggia, and his nights hunting mice in the courtyard. He likes to sit on Caterina's silk when nobody is looking, which makes her cross. He is especially fond of Nonna Lucia, who gives him little pieces of cheese. In the evenings, he sleeps curled up on her lap by the fire.

[pause]

On Sundays, the family goes to mass at their parish church, a little old church at the end of the street. After mass, they walk along the river, or visit relatives. Sometimes they walk out of the city gate, into the countryside. They pick wild flowers and watch the farmers at work. They have a picnic of bread and cheese under an olive tree. It is the best day of the week.

[long-pause]

{img:florence-narrow-street-1500-tall-stone-house-wooden-shutters-washing}
The family lives in a tall, narrow house in a little street near the cathedral. The house is made of stone and plaster, three storeys high, with a roof of red clay tiles.

[pause]

On the ground floor, there is a small room facing the street, which the family rents to a shoemaker. He sits in the doorway all day, making and mending shoes. He is a cheerful old man who whistles while he works. Behind his shop, there is a little courtyard with a well, shared with the neighbours. Washing hangs on lines across the courtyard.

[long-pause]

The family lives on the first and second floors. A narrow stone staircase leads up from the street. On the first floor, there is the main room, called the sala. It is the biggest room in the house, with a fireplace and a big window looking onto the street. Caterina's silk loom stands by the window, where the light is best. There is a wooden table and some benches, and a big wooden chest for linen.

[pause]

{img:silk-loom-window-light-woman-weaving-patterned-silk-wooden-room-1500}
Next to the sala, there is a small kitchen, with a fireplace for cooking and a stone sink. Pots and pans hang from hooks on the wall. There are shelves with jars of olive oil, flour, beans and dried herbs. Strings of garlic and onions hang from the ceiling. There is no running water. Bianca fetches water from the well in the courtyard every morning.

[long-pause]

On the second floor, there are two bedrooms. Piero and Caterina sleep in one, in a big wooden bed with curtains around it. In winter, the curtains keep out the cold. The children and Nonna Lucia sleep in the other. Bianca shares a bed with her grandmother, who tells her stories every night until she falls asleep. Matteo has a little bed in the corner, though he is often too tired to talk.

[pause]

{img:bedroom-1500-wooden-bed-curtains-chest-small-window-candle-night}
On the wall of the main room, there is a small painting of the Virgin Mary and the baby Jesus. It is not by a famous painter. It was bought from a workshop near the river, many years ago. But the family loves it. Below it, there is a little shelf with a candle, which Nonna lights every evening. The painting has soft colours, blue and red and gold. Bianca thinks Mary has a kind face.

[long-pause]

At the very top of the house, there is a little open loggia under the roof. A loggia is a kind of covered balcony, open on one side. Many houses in Florence have them. Caterina dries her washing there, and grows herbs in pots. In the summer evenings, the family sometimes sits up there, to catch the cool breeze.

[pause]

{img:rooftop-loggia-florence-evening-family-view-dome-red-roofs-sunset}
From the loggia, you can see over the red roofs of the whole city. You can see the great dome of the cathedral, so close that it seems you could almost touch it. You can see the tower of the palace of the government, and the hills beyond the river. In the evenings, when the sun goes down, the whole city glows red and gold. And Bianca sits beside her grandmother, watching the swallows dive and swoop over the rooftops, until the first stars come out.

[pause]

In winter, the house can be cold. The stone walls hold the chill, and the wind comes through the shutters. The family gathers around the fireplace in the evenings. Caterina warms bricks in the fire, wraps them in cloth, and puts them in the beds before everyone goes to sleep. Nonna Lucia sits with a little pot of hot coals under her skirts, to keep her feet warm.

[pause]

The neighbours in the street all know each other very well. They share the well in the courtyard. They help each other when someone is ill. They borrow salt and flour and candles. The women talk at the well and at their windows, across the narrow street. The men talk in the evenings, sitting on the stone benches outside the houses. Nobody in the street is ever really alone.

[long-pause]

{img:wool-dyers-workshop-arno-river-vats-coloured-cloth-hanging-1500}
Much of the wealth of Florence comes from cloth. The Florentines make some of the finest wool and silk in all of Europe.

[pause]

Making wool cloth is a long process, with many steps. First, the raw wool arrives from England or Spain, in big bales. It is washed and cleaned. Then it is combed and carded, to make the fibres straight. Then it is spun into thread. Then it is woven into cloth on big looms. Then the cloth is washed and beaten and stretched. And last of all, it is dyed in beautiful colours. Thousands of people in Florence work in the wool trade, each doing one of these steps.

[long-pause]

Along the banks of the river Arno, there are many dyers' workshops. They need lots of water for their work. They have big vats, heated by fires, full of coloured dye. The dyers dip the cloth into the vats, again and again, until it is the right colour. Then they hang it up to dry on long poles, in the sun. The riverbank is a riot of colour: red and blue, green and yellow, purple and brown.

[pause]

{img:dyed-cloth-drying-poles-riverbank-red-blue-yellow-sunlight-workers}
The most precious colour is a deep red, called crimson. It is made from tiny insects, dried and crushed. A very good blue is made from a plant called woad. Yellow comes from another plant, called weld. The dyers keep their recipes secret. They have stained hands, red or blue or green, that never quite come clean. People can tell what colour a dyer works with, just by looking at his hands.

[long-pause]

Silk is even more precious than wool. It comes from silkworms, which make little cocoons of very fine thread. In Florence, silk is woven into the most beautiful fabrics. There is plain silk and patterned silk, velvet and brocade. Brocade has patterns woven into it with gold and silver thread. Rich people wear clothes of silk and velvet, to show how wealthy they are. Churches use silk for the robes of the priests and the cloths on the altars.

[pause]

{img:woman-weaving-silk-brocade-gold-thread-loom-pattern-flowers-close}
Caterina weaves silk at home, for a silk merchant called Messer Giovanni. He brings her the coloured silk threads, and a drawing of the pattern he wants. She sets up the loom and weaves, thread by thread, line by line. A good length of patterned silk can take her weeks. She must count every thread and follow the pattern exactly. One mistake can spoil the whole piece. She works slowly and carefully, from morning till evening, by the window.

[long-pause]

Bianca helps her mother. She winds the silk thread onto little bobbins, ready for the loom. She learns the names of the colours and the patterns. Sometimes, her mother lets her weave a few lines herself. Bianca is careful, but she is not very patient. Her mind wanders, and she looks out of the window at the street. Then her mother gently reminds her to count. One, two, three, over. One, two, three, under.

[pause]

{img:mother-daughter-winding-silk-bobbins-loom-window-afternoon-light}
When a piece of silk is finished, Messer Giovanni comes to collect it. He unrolls it on the table and looks at it very carefully, in the light from the window. He checks every flower and every leaf. Then he nods and smiles. Beautiful, Monna Caterina, he says. Monna is how people in Florence politely address a married woman. He pays her in silver coins, and leaves another bundle of thread for the next piece. Caterina puts the coins in a little wooden box on the shelf. And she starts again.

[pause]

The wool workers of Florence are the poorest workers in the city. They work long hours for low pay. They comb and card the wool, and spin it, and do the heavy washing. Many of them live in the poorer parts of the city, near the walls. Their lives are hard. Long ago, they rose up to ask for fairer treatment. Some things improved for a while. But life for the wool workers remains difficult.

[pause]

Silk weaving, though, is a respected craft. Many silk weavers are women, working at home, like Caterina. They are skilled and proud of their work. Their beautiful silks are sold to kings and queens, popes and cardinals, all over Europe. Caterina likes to imagine where her silk goes after it leaves her loom. Perhaps to a palace in France, she thinks, or a church in Spain.

[long-pause]

{img:florence-dawn-1500-mother-lighting-kitchen-fire-family-waking-bells}
Let us spend an ordinary day with the family, in the spring of 1501.

[pause]

The day begins early, with the bells of the cathedral ringing for the first prayers. Caterina gets up and lights the fire in the kitchen. Bianca goes down to the courtyard to fetch water from the well. The neighbours are there too, filling their jugs and talking about the weather. The shoemaker is already in his doorway, whistling.

[long-pause]

Breakfast is simple: bread, a little cheese, and watered wine. Piero eats quickly and goes off to the cathedral workshop. Matteo goes off to the painter's workshop near the river. Both of them work from dawn until dusk, six days a week. Caterina sits down at her loom by the window and begins to weave. Nonna Lucia sits beside her, sewing.

[pause]

{img:father-son-leaving-house-morning-florence-street-tools-bag-bells}
Bianca has errands to do. She goes to the baker for bread. The baker has a big oven in the back of his shop, and the smell of fresh bread fills the whole street. Then she goes to the market for vegetables and a little fish. She knows all the stall holders, and they know her. They give her a fig or a few cherries to eat on the way home.

[long-pause]

In the middle of the morning, Bianca takes a basket with bread and cheese to her father at the cathedral workshop. This is her favourite part of the day. The workshop is a big courtyard behind the cathedral, full of stone and marble. There are stonemasons and carvers working everywhere, under wooden roofs. The air is full of white dust and the sound of chisels tapping on stone. Tap, tap, tap. All day long.

[pause]

{img:cathedral-workshop-courtyard-stonemasons-carving-marble-dust-chisels-sunlight}
Piero is working on a new stone step for the cathedral. He is shaping it with a chisel and a wooden mallet, slowly and carefully. When he sees Bianca, he smiles and puts down his tools. They sit together on a block of stone and eat the bread and cheese. Piero shows her how to hold a chisel, and how to tap it gently with the mallet. Bianca tries, and a little chip of stone flies off. She laughs with delight.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, Bianca helps her mother wind the silk. Then she goes out to play in the little square with her friends. They play with balls and hoops, and skip with ropes. They play a game with little stones, throwing and catching them. They chase each other around the fountain. The pigeons fly up in clouds whenever the children come running.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-small-florence-square-fountain-pigeons-afternoon-sun}
In the evening, Piero and Matteo come home, tired and dusty. Piero is covered with white stone dust. Matteo has paint on his fingers and his face, blue and red and yellow. They wash at the well in the courtyard. Then the family eats supper together, by lamplight. Afterwards, Nonna tells a story, and Caterina sings a little song. And one by one, the family goes to bed, as the bells of the city ring out the evening hour.

[pause]

Sometimes, on her way home, Bianca stops at the baptistery to look at the golden doors. They are covered with little scenes from the Bible, made of bronze and gold. She looks at Adam and Eve in the garden, and Noah and his ark, and Joseph and his brothers. Each scene is like a little world, with people and buildings and trees. She finds something new every time she looks.

[pause]

Before bed, Caterina brushes Bianca's long hair and braids it for the night. They talk quietly about the day. Bianca tells her mother about the stonemasons and the chisel. Caterina tells her about the silk merchant and the new pattern she will weave. Then she kisses her daughter goodnight. And Bianca falls asleep to the sound of the bells and her grandmother's soft breathing.

[long-pause]

{img:mercato-vecchio-florence-1500-market-stalls-vegetables-crowds-tower-houses}
In the middle of Florence, there is a big busy square called the Mercato Vecchio, which means the old market. It is the place where Florentines buy their food every day.

[pause]

The square is crowded with stalls and shops. There are farmers selling vegetables and fruit from the countryside. There are butchers and fishmongers, bakers and cheese sellers. There are sellers of eggs and chickens, herbs and spices. There are little taverns where people eat and drink. And there are crowds of people everywhere, buying and selling, talking and arguing.

[long-pause]

In spring, the market is full of fresh green things. There are artichokes and peas, broad beans and lettuces. There are bunches of herbs: parsley and basil, rosemary and sage. In summer, there are melons and figs, peaches and plums, cucumbers and onions. There are no tomatoes yet, because they will only come later, from the Americas. In autumn, there are grapes and chestnuts, mushrooms and apples.

[pause]

{img:vegetable-stall-1500-artichokes-beans-herbs-farmer-woman-girl-buying}
The Florentines love their bread. It is made without salt, which is unusual. Some people say this is because salt was once very expensive in Florence, because of a heavy tax. Bread without salt tastes a little plain on its own. But it is very good with salty cheese and ham, or dipped in olive oil, or in soup. Florentines still make their bread this way today.

[long-pause]

Near the market, there is a famous old bridge over the river, called the Ponte Vecchio, the old bridge. It has little shops built along both sides of it, like a street over the water. In 1500, most of the shops on the bridge belong to butchers and tanners. They throw their scraps into the river below. The bridge is busy and noisy, and the smell is rather strong. Much later, the butchers will be replaced by goldsmiths and jewellers, as they are today.

[pause]

{img:ponte-vecchio-1500-shops-on-bridge-butchers-arno-river-morning}
Bianca goes to the market every few days, with her mother or with Nonna. They carry a big basket and a little purse of coins. They walk slowly from stall to stall, looking at everything. Caterina chooses carefully, and bargains a little. The stall holders know her, and give her a fair price. Sometimes they add a little extra, a bunch of parsley or a few cherries, for Bianca.

[long-pause]

At the edge of the market, there are people who are not selling food. There are storytellers and singers, who perform for a few coins. There are men who write letters for people who cannot write. There are barbers who shave men and pull teeth. There are sellers of little holy pictures and candles. And there are beggars, poor people who ask for a coin or a piece of bread. Caterina always gives a little to an old blind man who sits by the church door.

[pause]

{img:storyteller-market-square-crowd-listening-children-1500-afternoon}
Bianca's favourite is a storyteller who sits on a stone bench near the fountain. He is an old man with a long grey beard and a deep voice. He tells stories of knights and ladies, of giants and magic, of the old heroes of Florence. Children and grown-ups gather around him to listen. When he gets to the exciting parts, he stands up and waves his arms. At the end, people drop coins into his hat. Bianca always wishes she had more coins to give him.

[pause]

At the market, there are also stalls that sell things from faraway lands. There are spices from the East: pepper and cinnamon, ginger and cloves. There is sugar from the islands of the Mediterranean, very expensive. There are oranges and lemons from the south of Italy. And there are little bags of saffron, the most precious spice of all, which turns food bright yellow. Caterina buys a tiny pinch of saffron once a year, for a special feast.

[pause]

At the end of the morning, the market slowly empties. The farmers pack up their carts and drive back to the countryside. The butchers wash down their tables. The fishmongers throw the last scraps to the cats. Poor women come to collect leftover vegetables and leaves. And by the afternoon, the square is quiet again, except for the pigeons and the children playing around the fountain.

[long-pause]

{img:renaissance-painters-workshop-bottega-master-apprentices-panels-easels-light}
Let us visit the workshop where Matteo is learning to paint. In Florence, a painter's workshop is called a bottega.

[pause]

The bottega is a big room on the ground floor of a house near the river. It has wide doors and big windows, to let in plenty of light. Inside, there are wooden panels leaning against the walls, some blank and some half painted. There are easels and tables, pots and jars, brushes of every size. There is a strong smell of oil, paint and glue. And there are several people working at once.

[long-pause]

The master of the workshop is a painter called Maestro Andrea. He is about fifty years old, with a grey beard and paint on his sleeves. He is not one of the most famous painters of Florence. But he is a good painter, and an honest one. He paints pictures for churches and for rich families: saints and madonnas, angels and stories from the Bible. He has three apprentices and two assistants.

[pause]

{img:apprentice-grinding-colours-marble-slab-muller-pigments-jars-window}
Matteo is the youngest apprentice. He started two years ago, when he was eleven. At first, he only swept the floor and ran errands. Now he does more important jobs. He grinds the colours. The colours come from minerals and plants and earth. They arrive as lumps or powder. Matteo grinds them on a flat slab of stone, with a smooth stone called a muller. He grinds them for a long time, until they are as fine as dust. Then he mixes them with egg yolk or with oil, to make paint.

[long-pause]

The most precious colour is a deep blue called ultramarine. It is made from a stone called lapis lazuli, which comes from the mountains of Afghanistan, very far away. It is more expensive than gold. Maestro Andrea uses it only for the robe of the Virgin Mary, in his most important paintings. He keeps it in a little locked box. Only he is allowed to touch it.

[pause]

{img:master-painter-teaching-boy-drawing-silverpoint-paper-table-window}
Matteo also prepares the wooden panels for painting. He covers them with layers of white chalk and glue, called gesso. When it is dry, he smooths it with a knife and a piece of stone, until it is as smooth as glass. Then the master draws the picture on it. Sometimes, the master lets Matteo paint small parts of the picture: a bit of sky, a rock, a tree in the background.

[long-pause]

Every day, Matteo also practises drawing. Maestro Andrea says that drawing is the most important skill of all. Matteo draws with a silver stick on special paper, which makes fine grey lines. He draws hands and feet, faces and folds of cloth. He copies drawings by the master. He draws the other apprentices while they work. Sometimes the master looks at his drawings, and nods. Sometimes he shakes his head and says: Again.

[pause]

{img:apprentices-eating-lunch-workshop-bench-laughing-paint-on-hands}
At midday, the apprentices eat their lunch together on a bench in the workshop. They eat bread and cheese, and drink watered wine. They laugh and joke and talk about the famous painters of Florence. They talk about who is the best: Botticelli or Ghirlandaio, Leonardo or Perugino. And they talk about a young sculptor called Michelangelo, who has just come back to Florence. Everyone is talking about him. They say he has been given a giant block of marble to carve.

[pause]

Apprentices live a hard life, but a happy one. They work long hours, and they must do what the master says. But they learn a wonderful craft. And they have each other. In the workshop, Matteo has made good friends with the other apprentices. They help each other, tease each other, and share their food. On feast days, they go out together to see the processions.

[pause]

Sometimes rich customers come to the workshop to see how their painting is going. They come in fine clothes of velvet and silk, with servants behind them. Maestro Andrea shows them the painting and explains what he is doing. The customers often want changes: more gold here, a different saint there, their own face in the crowd. The master listens patiently, and nods, and makes the changes.

[long-pause]

{img:leonardo-da-vinci-1503-long-beard-workshop-notebooks-drawings-calm}
One of the most famous men in Florence around 1500 is the painter Leonardo da Vinci. He is about fifty years old, tall and handsome, with long hair and a long beard. He wears a short rose-coloured cloak, which is unusual in Florence. People turn to look at him when he walks through the streets.

[pause]

Leonardo was born in a little village called Vinci, in the hills not far from Florence. That is why he is called Leonardo da Vinci, which means Leonardo from Vinci. As a young man, he learned to paint in a famous workshop in Florence. Then he went to Milan, where he worked for the duke for many years. Now he has come back to Florence.

[long-pause]

Leonardo is not only a painter. He is curious about everything in the world. He studies the flight of birds and the flow of water. He studies plants and rocks, clouds and light. He studies the human body, to understand how it works. He designs machines: flying machines, bridges, water pumps, even a kind of diving suit. He writes all his ideas and drawings in little notebooks, which he carries everywhere.

[pause]

{img:leonardo-notebook-pages-drawings-birds-flight-machines-mirror-writing-close}
He writes in a strange way, from right to left, backwards. You need a mirror to read it easily. Nobody is quite sure why. Perhaps because he was left-handed, and it was easier not to smudge the ink. Perhaps he wanted to keep his ideas a little secret. His notebooks are full of thousands of pages of drawings and notes. Many of them have survived until today.

[long-pause]

In 1503, Leonardo begins a portrait of a young woman. She is called Lisa, and she is the wife of a silk merchant called Francesco del Giocondo. She is about twenty-four. Leonardo paints her sitting calmly, with her hands folded, and a gentle smile. Behind her, there is a strange, misty landscape of rocks and water. He paints the picture very slowly, with tiny soft brushstrokes, layer upon layer. He works on it for many years.

[pause]

{img:portrait-painting-young-woman-gentle-smile-misty-landscape-easel-studio}
To keep Lisa happy while she sits for the portrait, people say that Leonardo has musicians play for her. He wants her to look calm and content. Today, this painting is called the Mona Lisa. Mona is short for Madonna, which means my lady. It is probably the most famous painting in the whole world. Millions of people come to see it every year.

[long-pause]

Matteo has seen Leonardo once, in the street near the river. He was walking slowly, looking up at the birds flying over the Arno. He stopped and took out his little notebook and drew something quickly. Then he walked on. Matteo watched him until he turned the corner. He told the other apprentices about it at lunch. They all wanted to know what Leonardo had drawn. But Matteo had not dared to look.

[pause]

{img:leonardo-sketching-birds-riverbank-arno-notebook-boy-watching-distance}
Leonardo also loves animals. People say that he sometimes buys caged birds at the market, only to open the cages and set the birds free. He watches them fly away, and studies how they move their wings. He dreams that one day, people will fly too. He designs flying machines with wings like a bird or a bat. None of them ever flies. But four hundred years later, people do learn to fly. And Leonardo's drawings are still admired by engineers all over the world.

[pause]

Leonardo also loves music. He plays the lyre and sings beautifully. When he was young in Milan, he even made a silver lyre in the shape of a horse's head. He also loves riddles and jokes, and he writes them in his notebooks. He loves beautiful clothes and fine horses. And he is a vegetarian, which is very unusual in his time. He does not like to eat animals, because he loves them too much.

[pause]

In Florence, Leonardo also works on many other projects. He makes maps of the river Arno and the country around it. He makes plans to change the course of the river, to help the boats. He studies the flight of birds on the hills above the city. People say he is always starting new things, and does not always finish them. But everything he touches, he makes more interesting.

[long-pause]

{img:young-michelangelo-1501-twenty-six-dark-hair-broken-nose-intense-eyes-workshop}
In the summer of 1501, a young sculptor comes back to Florence from Rome. His name is Michelangelo Buonarroti. He is twenty-six years old. And everyone in Florence is talking about him.

[pause]

Michelangelo grew up in Florence. As a boy, he was taken in by Lorenzo the Magnificent, who saw that he was very talented. He lived in the Medici palace and studied the old Roman statues in the Medici garden. When he was very young, another boy broke his nose in a quarrel. So his nose was always a little flat and crooked.

[long-pause]

Then he went to Rome. There, he carved a beautiful statue of the Virgin Mary holding her son Jesus, in white marble. It is called the Pietà. People who saw it could hardly believe that such a young man had made it. The marble looks soft, like real skin and cloth. Michelangelo became famous. And now, the city of Florence wants him to make something for them.

[pause]

{img:huge-marble-block-giant-lying-cathedral-yard-weeds-old-damaged-1501}
In the yard of the cathedral workshop, where Piero works, there is a huge block of marble. It is more than five metres long. It came from the famous quarries of Carrara, in the mountains, about forty years earlier. Two other sculptors had started to carve it, long ago, but they had given up. They had started to cut it, and then stopped. The block was left lying in the yard, out in the rain and the sun, for many years. The workers call it the Giant.

[long-pause]

Many people think the block is spoiled. It is tall and thin, and there are cuts in it in the wrong places. But the leaders of the cathedral workshop want someone to carve a great statue from it. They ask Michelangelo if he can do it. He studies the block for a long time. He measures it carefully. And he says yes. He will carve a statue of David, the young hero from the Bible.

[pause]

{img:michelangelo-measuring-marble-block-compass-cathedral-yard-workers-watching}
David was a shepherd boy, in the old story. A great giant called Goliath threatened his people. Nobody dared to face him. But young David stepped forward, with only a sling and a few stones. And with one stone, he won. For the people of Florence, David is a symbol of their city. Florence is small, but brave and free, and it stands up to bigger and more powerful enemies.

[long-pause]

Piero is there on the day that Michelangelo starts work. It is early in the morning, in September 1501. The young sculptor comes into the yard with his hammer and chisels. He climbs up onto the block and knocks off a little piece of marble from the chest. The stonemasons stop working and watch. It is the beginning of something. They can feel it.

[pause]

{img:sculptor-first-strike-chisel-marble-block-dawn-yard-stonemasons-watching}
That evening, Piero tells his family about it at supper. He has seen the young man up close, he says. He is small and serious, with dark hair and dusty clothes. He did not speak to anyone. He just looked at the marble for a long time, and then began. Bianca asks a hundred questions. What will the statue look like? How long will it take? Can she come and watch? Piero laughs. He says that nobody is allowed to watch. But perhaps, one day, she will see it.

[pause]

Michelangelo does not care about fine clothes or comfort. He eats simple food, often just bread and a little wine. He sleeps little and works a lot. He is quick-tempered and easily offended. He does not have many friends. But when he works, he is completely focused. He loves marble more than anything. He says that he drank in his love of stone with the milk of his nurse, who was the wife of a stonecutter.

[pause]

Michelangelo comes from a family that is not rich, but proud. His father did not want him to become a sculptor. He thought it was dirty, hard work, not fit for a gentleman. But Michelangelo would not listen. He drew and carved whenever he could. And in the end, his father gave up, and let him follow his heart. Now the whole city is proud of him.

[long-pause]

{img:wooden-shed-around-marble-block-cathedral-yard-sculptor-working-inside-light}
For more than two years, Michelangelo works on the statue of David, in the yard of the cathedral workshop.

[pause]

He has a wooden shed built around the great block of marble, so that nobody can see what he is doing. Inside the shed, there is a wooden scaffold, so he can reach every part of the tall block. There is a little window high up, to let in the light. He works there almost every day, from early in the morning until late in the evening. Sometimes he works at night, by the light of a candle fixed to his cap.

[long-pause]

Carving marble is very hard and very slow. Michelangelo uses a heavy hammer and different kinds of chisels. First he uses a pointed chisel, to knock off big pieces. Then he uses a chisel with little teeth, like a comb, to shape the forms more carefully. Then he uses a flat chisel, to make smooth surfaces. Last of all, he uses files and rough stones and sand, to polish the marble until it shines.

[pause]

{img:sculptor-chisel-hammer-marble-chips-flying-dust-candle-cap-night}
Michelangelo said that the statue was already inside the block of marble. His job was only to take away the stone that was not part of it, to set the figure free. He worked from the front of the block toward the back, slowly, as if he were lowering the water in a bath. Bit by bit, the figure of David appeared, rising out of the stone.

[long-pause]

He does not like visitors. He does not like people watching him. He wants to be alone with the marble. He works so hard that he often forgets to eat. He sleeps in his clothes and boots, so he can start again early in the morning. He is often covered from head to toe in white marble dust, like a ghost. The other workers in the yard are a little afraid of him. But they respect him very much.

[pause]

{img:stonemason-father-bringing-bread-water-sculptor-shed-door-friendly}
Piero works in the same yard, on his own stone. Sometimes he hears the steady tap, tap, tap of Michelangelo's hammer coming from the shed. It goes on all day and into the night. Piero sometimes leaves a jug of water and a piece of bread at the door of the shed, when he goes home in the evening. In the morning, it is always gone. One day, Michelangelo comes out of the shed and nods to him. Thank you, he says. That is all. But Piero is very pleased.

[long-pause]

Michelangelo makes David different from all other statues of David. In the older statues, David is shown after he has won, with the giant's head at his feet. But Michelangelo shows David before the fight. He stands calmly, with his sling over his shoulder. He is looking into the distance, at the giant who is coming. He is thinking. He is a little worried, but brave and ready. It is the moment just before everything happens.

[pause]

{img:marble-david-half-finished-scaffold-shed-light-through-high-window}
The statue is enormous, more than five metres tall. That is as tall as three men standing on each other's shoulders. And yet, it looks alive. You can see the veins in David's hands, the muscles in his arms, the curls in his hair. His face is calm and serious, with his brows drawn together. It seems as if, at any moment, he might move.

[pause]

In the city, people hear rumours about the statue. Some say it will be the most beautiful statue ever made. Some say Michelangelo has spoiled the marble, and it will never be finished. Everybody is curious. Everybody wants to see. But the door of the shed stays closed.

[pause]

Marble is a beautiful stone. The marble of Carrara is very white and very fine. When it is polished, it glows softly, almost like skin. But marble can also be difficult. It can have little cracks or veins hidden inside. If the sculptor hits it in the wrong way, a piece can break off. And then it cannot be put back. So the sculptor must be very careful, and very sure of what he is doing.

[pause]

Sometimes, late at night, Piero walks past the cathedral yard on his way home. He can see a little light flickering through the cracks in the wooden shed. And he can hear the tap, tap, tap of the hammer. He stops and listens for a while in the dark street. It is like the heartbeat of the city, he thinks. Then he walks on home, to his sleeping family.

[long-pause]

{img:florentine-children-1500-playing-ball-street-hoops-dogs-afternoon}
What is life like for children in Florence around 1500?

[pause]

Children of working families start to work young. By the age of ten or twelve, many boys become apprentices, to learn a trade. They learn to be goldsmiths and weavers, carpenters and painters, bakers and shoemakers. They live with their master, or go home in the evenings, as Matteo does. Girls usually learn at home, from their mothers. They learn to spin and weave, to sew and cook, and to run a house.

[long-pause]

Some children go to school. Florence has many little schools, where boys learn to read and write, and to do arithmetic. Merchants need to be good at numbers, to keep their accounts. So the sons of merchants go to special schools, called abacus schools, where they learn to calculate. A few girls go to school too, especially in richer families. Some girls learn at home, from a tutor or from their mothers.

[pause]

{img:abacus-school-1500-boys-wooden-benches-teacher-counting-board-numbers}
Bianca cannot go to school. But Nonna Lucia has taught her to read a little, from a small prayer book. And Matteo, in the evenings, is teaching her to draw. He shows her how to hold a piece of charcoal, how to look carefully at things, how to draw what she sees. Bianca draws the cat, the window, Nonna's hands, the dome from the loggia. She is quite good. Matteo says she has a good eye.

[long-pause]

Children in Florence love to play. They play with balls, hoops and spinning tops. They play hide and seek in the narrow streets, and tag in the squares. Girls play with dolls made of wood and cloth. Boys fight pretend battles with wooden swords. In summer, they swim in the river Arno, in the shallow places, and catch little fish with their hands.

[pause]

{img:children-swimming-arno-river-shallows-summer-boys-splashing-bridge}
There is also a game called calcio, which is a little like football and a little like rugby. Teams of young men play it in the big squares, especially at carnival time. They kick and carry and throw a ball, and push each other around. It is rough and exciting, and big crowds come to watch. Boys play their own little games of calcio in the streets, with a ball made of rags.

[long-pause]

On feast days, the children of Florence take part in processions. They dress in white and carry candles and flowers. They sing hymns as they walk through the streets to the churches. Some children belong to special clubs, called confraternities, run by the churches. There, they learn songs and prayers, and help with good works. They also put on little plays, about saints and stories from the Bible.

[pause]

{img:children-procession-white-robes-candles-flowers-singing-street-feast-day}
Bianca's best friend is a girl called Ginevra, who lives across the street. Ginevra's father is a baker. The two girls play together every afternoon. They sit on the steps of the little church in their street, and tell each other stories. They plan what they will do when they grow up. Ginevra wants to marry a rich merchant and live in a palace. Bianca wants to travel to faraway lands, like the merchants and explorers she hears about. Ginevra laughs and says girls cannot do that. Bianca says, we will see.

[pause]

Babies in Florence are often sent to live with a nurse in the countryside, for their first year or two. The nurse is a farmer's wife, who looks after them and feeds them. Then they come back to the city, to live with their families. Bianca was sent to a nurse in a village in the hills. She does not remember it. But her mother tells her that she was a happy baby, who loved to laugh at the chickens.

[pause]

In the evenings, the children of the street gather around the oldest neighbour, an old man who was once a soldier. He tells them about the battles of his youth, and the cities he has seen. He tells them about Rome and Venice and Naples. He shows them an old scar on his arm and a coin from a faraway land. The children listen with their mouths open, until their mothers call them in.

[long-pause]

{img:feast-of-san-giovanni-florence-procession-banners-candles-june-crowds}
The most important festival of the year in Florence is the feast of San Giovanni, Saint John the Baptist. He is the patron saint of Florence. His feast day is on the twenty-fourth of June, in the middle of summer. And the whole city celebrates for days.

[pause]

For weeks before, the city gets ready. The streets are cleaned and decorated. The shops display their finest goods outside. The rich families hang beautiful cloths and tapestries from their windows. People prepare costumes and banners and floats for the processions.

[long-pause]

On the days before the feast, there are great processions through the streets. The guilds march with their banners and their saints. The clergy march in their finest robes, carrying holy relics and candles. Groups of young people dress up in costumes, as angels and saints and figures from old stories. There are floats with scenes from the Bible, pulled through the streets. Music plays everywhere.

[pause]

{img:florence-1500-decorated-street-tapestries-windows-crowds-music-summer}
On the feast day itself, there are gifts for Saint John. People from all the towns and villages around Florence come to bring candles and offerings to the baptistery. The great square in front of the cathedral is covered with a huge blue cloth, decorated with gold lilies, the symbol of Florence. It gives shade from the hot summer sun.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, there is a horse race through the streets of the city. It is called the palio. The horses race without saddles, from one gate of the city to another, through the narrow streets. The crowds line the way and cheer. The winner receives a great banner of red silk, called the palio. It is a very exciting race, and everyone has a favourite.

[pause]

{img:palio-horse-race-florence-street-crowds-cheering-red-silk-banner-afternoon}
And in the evening, there are fireworks. They are set off from the tower of the palace of the government, and from the towers of the cathedral. Rockets go up into the summer sky, and burst into stars of gold and silver. The whole city comes out to watch. People crowd into the squares and onto the bridges. The light of the fireworks is reflected in the river Arno.

[long-pause]

The family goes to the festival together, in their best clothes. Caterina has made Bianca a new dress of blue linen, with a little silk ribbon. Piero wears his good brown coat. Matteo has a new red cap. Nonna walks slowly, holding Piero's arm. They watch the processions and the horse race. They buy little honey cakes from a stall. And in the evening, they stand on a bridge over the Arno to watch the fireworks.

[pause]

{img:family-on-bridge-watching-fireworks-arno-reflections-night-june-1500}
Bianca holds her grandmother's hand. The rockets go up, one after another, and burst over the river. The crowd cries out with delight. Nonna tells Bianca that she has seen the fireworks of San Giovanni for almost seventy years. And they are still as beautiful as ever, she says. Bianca looks up at the sky, full of golden stars. She thinks that when she is old, she will tell her grandchildren about these fireworks too.

[pause]

During the festival, there are also games and contests in the squares. There are jousts, where knights on horseback ride at each other with long wooden lances. There are races for young men on foot. There are music contests and dances. Rich families hold great feasts in their palaces, and give food to the poor at their doors. For a few days, all of Florence is one big celebration.

[pause]

During the feast days, the poor of Florence are not forgotten. The guilds and the churches give out bread and wine and meat. Rich families open their doors and give food to anyone who comes. Prisoners who owe small debts are set free, as a gift to Saint John. It is a time of generosity and kindness. Everyone in the city should have a share in the celebration.

[long-pause]

{img:lorenzo-de-medici-magnificent-1480s-palace-garden-artists-poets-gathered}
The Renaissance in Florence did not happen by itself. It was helped along by rich families who paid for art. The most famous of these families was the Medici.

[pause]

The Medici began as merchants and bankers. About seventy years before our story, the head of the family was a man called Cosimo de' Medici. He became the richest man in Florence, and perhaps in all of Europe. He used his money to pay for beautiful buildings and works of art. He paid for churches and libraries, monasteries and palaces. He collected old books from Greece and Rome. And he gave work to many artists and scholars.

[long-pause]

His grandson was Lorenzo de' Medici, called Lorenzo the Magnificent. He led the city for more than twenty years, until 1492. He was not handsome, and he had a strange, rough voice. But he was clever and charming, and he loved art and poetry. He wrote poems himself. He invited artists, poets and thinkers to his palace and his villas in the hills. They talked late into the night about beauty and ideas.

[pause]

{img:medici-palace-courtyard-1480s-statues-columns-young-artists-studying}
Lorenzo opened a garden near his palace, full of old Roman statues. Young artists could come there to study and to draw. One of these young artists was Michelangelo, when he was only fourteen. Lorenzo saw how talented the boy was. He invited him to live in the Medici palace, and to eat at his table with his own sons. Michelangelo never forgot it.

[long-pause]

Lorenzo also helped many other artists. One of them was a painter called Sandro Botticelli. Botticelli painted beautiful, dreamy pictures, full of grace and colour. One of them shows the goddess Venus, standing on a great seashell, floating to the shore. Another shows a group of figures dancing in a garden full of flowers, in the springtime. People still come from all over the world to see these paintings today.

[pause]

{img:botticelli-style-painting-studio-goddess-shell-sea-flowers-canvas-workshop}
Around 1500, Botticelli is still living in Florence. He is an old man now, about fifty-five. His painting has changed, and become more serious and sad. He is not as famous as he was. But young painters still admire him. Matteo's master, Maestro Andrea, once worked with him for a while. He tells the apprentices that Botticelli was the most graceful painter he ever knew.

[long-pause]

The Medici were sent away from Florence in 1494, after Lorenzo's death. Their palace was taken over by the city. But they will come back later, in 1512. And in later years, they will become dukes of Florence, and even popes in Rome. For three hundred years, they will be one of the most powerful families in Italy. And their great art collection will one day be given to the people of Florence, forever.

[pause]

{img:nonna-telling-story-lorenzo-medici-children-fireside-evening-memories}
Nonna Lucia tells Bianca about Lorenzo the Magnificent. She remembers the grand festivals he used to organise, with parades and costumes, songs and dancing. She remembers the carnival songs he wrote, which everyone sang in the streets. One of them begins: How beautiful is youth, which flies away so fast. Let those who want to be happy, be happy now, for tomorrow is uncertain. Nonna sings it softly, in her old voice. And Bianca learns it by heart.

[pause]

The Medici also loved nature and gardens. Their villas in the hills had beautiful gardens, with fountains and statues, flower beds and fruit trees. They grew lemons and oranges in big pots, which were carried indoors in winter. They kept rare animals too: lions and parrots, and once even a giraffe, given to Lorenzo as a gift by the sultan of Egypt. The whole city came out to see it.

[pause]

Many of the artists who worked for Lorenzo came from simple families. They were the sons of shoemakers, barbers and farmers. Lorenzo did not care where they came from. He cared only about their talent. He gave them money and work and a place to learn. And because of him, many of them became some of the greatest artists in history.

[long-pause]

{img:florence-bookshop-1500-printed-books-manuscripts-scholars-browsing-shelves}
Florence around 1500 is a city of books and ideas. It is one of the most important centres of learning in Europe.

[pause]

About fifty years before our story, a new invention arrived in Europe: the printing press. Before then, every book had to be copied out by hand, letter by letter. It took months to copy one book. Books were very rare and very expensive. Now, with the printing press, hundreds of copies of a book can be made quickly. Books become cheaper, and more people can read them.

[long-pause]

In Florence, there are now several printing shops. They print books of poetry and history, prayers and sermons, and old books from Greece and Rome. There are bookshops near the cathedral, where people can browse and buy. Rich people still also have beautiful handwritten books, made by skilled scribes and decorated with tiny paintings in gold and colour.

[pause]

{img:scholars-humanists-reading-greek-latin-texts-library-wooden-desks-light}
The scholars of Florence are called humanists. They study the books of ancient Greece and Rome. They learn Latin and Greek. They read the old poets and philosophers, historians and scientists. They believe that these old books contain great wisdom, which had been forgotten for a long time. And they believe that people should use their own minds, to understand the world and to live good lives.

[long-pause]

One of the most interesting people working in the government of Florence around 1500 is a man called Niccolò Machiavelli. He is about thirty years old. He works as a secretary and diplomat for the city. He travels to other cities and countries, to talk with kings and dukes. He watches them closely and thinks about how power works. Later, he will write a famous book about it. It is called The Prince. People still read it and argue about it today.

[pause]

{img:machiavelli-1500-official-desk-palazzo-letters-quill-thinking-window}
There are also new ideas about the world itself. In 1492, a sailor called Christopher Columbus sailed west across the Atlantic and found new lands. Another explorer from Florence, called Amerigo Vespucci, sailed there too, a few years later. He wrote letters about these lands, saying they were a new world, not part of Asia. His letters were printed and read all over Europe. And a mapmaker named the new lands after him: America.

[long-pause]

Bianca hears about this from her father, who heard it from a merchant at the cathedral. A man from Florence has sailed to a new world, he says, across the great ocean. There are lands there that nobody in Europe knew about. There are strange animals and plants, and peoples who live in different ways. Bianca's eyes grow wide. She asks if she can go there one day. Her father laughs. Perhaps, he says. The world is getting bigger every day.

[pause]

{img:girl-looking-at-new-world-map-merchant-shop-window-florence-curious}
On the way home from the market, Bianca stops at a little shop that sells maps and prints. In the window, there is a new map of the world. It shows Europe and Africa and Asia, and, far to the west, a strange new coastline. Bianca stands and looks at it for a long time. The shopkeeper comes out and tells her about the new lands. Bianca listens to every word. And that night, she dreams of ships and oceans and faraway shores.

[pause]

The great library of the Medici was full of beautiful old books. Cosimo de' Medici had collected them from all over the world. He sent people to search for old Greek and Latin books in monasteries and libraries far away. Many of these books had been almost forgotten. Thanks to him and others, they were saved and copied. Some of them would otherwise have been lost forever.

[pause]

In the evenings, the scholars of Florence meet in each other's houses and gardens. They read poems aloud and discuss ideas. They talk about Plato and the meaning of beauty. They talk about the stars and the planets, about the soul and about love. Sometimes there is music, played on a lute or a harp. These evenings can go on late into the night, by the light of candles.

[long-pause]

{img:marble-david-finished-shed-opened-committee-artists-looking-up-january-1504}
In January 1504, after more than two years of work, the statue of David is almost finished. And now a big question must be answered. Where should it stand?

[pause]

The statue was first meant to stand high up on the cathedral, on one of the roof buttresses. But now that it is made, everyone can see that it is too beautiful to be put so high, where nobody can see it properly. So the leaders of the cathedral workshop call a meeting. They invite the most important artists and craftsmen of Florence, to give their opinions.

[long-pause]

Around thirty people come to the meeting. There are painters and sculptors, goldsmiths and architects, carpenters and stonemasons. There are some of the most famous artists in the world. Leonardo da Vinci is there. Botticelli is there. So are many other masters. They gather in the cathedral workshop and look at the great statue. Then they discuss where it should go.

[pause]

{img:artists-meeting-discussion-workshop-leonardo-botticelli-standing-statue-background}
Some say it should stand in front of the cathedral. Some say it should stand under the great arched loggia beside the palace of the government, where it would be safe from the rain. Some say it should stand in front of the palace itself, in the main square of the city. Each person gives a reason. The discussion goes on for a long time. A scribe writes down every word. The record of the meeting still exists today.

[long-pause]

In the end, it is decided that David will stand in front of the palace of the government, in the main square of Florence, the Piazza della Signoria. There, he will be a symbol of the free republic of Florence, standing guard at the door of its government. Michelangelo agrees. He thinks it is a good place. And so the plans begin, to move the giant statue across the city.

[pause]

{img:piazza-della-signoria-1504-palazzo-vecchio-door-empty-place-crowds-square}
Piero is in the yard on the day of the meeting. He does not go inside, of course. But he hears all about it from the other workers. That evening, he tells his family. Leonardo was there, he says, and Botticelli. They talked for hours. And now the David will stand in the main square, in front of the palace. Bianca jumps up and down. Then everyone will be able to see it, she says. Even me.

[long-pause]

But moving the statue will not be easy. It is more than five metres tall and weighs about six tons. It is made of marble, which can crack if it is bumped or dropped. It must be carried from the cathedral to the main square, through narrow streets, about six hundred metres. Nobody has ever moved such a big statue before. The engineers of the cathedral workshop think very hard about how to do it.

[pause]

{img:engineers-planning-wooden-frame-ropes-rollers-drawings-workshop-table}
They come up with a clever plan. The statue will stand upright inside a tall wooden frame. It will hang from ropes inside the frame, so that it can swing a little, gently, without bumping into anything. The frame will rest on big wooden rollers, greased with fat. And men will pull it along slowly, with ropes and winches. Piero is chosen to be one of the workers. He is very proud. And a little nervous too.

[pause]

During the weeks of preparation, everyone in the city talks about the move. In the markets and the taverns, at the wells and in the churches, people wonder how it will be done. Will the statue break? Will the frame hold? How long will it take? Some people make bets. The children of Florence play at moving giants, with dolls on little carts made of sticks.

[pause]

Michelangelo also prepares the statue for the journey. He checks every part of it carefully, to make sure there are no hidden cracks. He wraps the most delicate parts in cloth and straw. He talks with the engineers about the ropes and the frame. He does not say much. But everyone can see that he is worried. The statue is the most important thing in his life.

[long-pause]

{img:david-statue-moving-wooden-frame-rollers-narrow-street-night-crowds-torches-may-1504}
On the evening of the fourteenth of May, 1504, the great move begins. It will be one of the most exciting events in the history of Florence.

[pause]

The workers have taken down a part of the wall of the cathedral yard, to make an opening big enough for the statue. The giant wooden frame stands ready, with David hanging inside it on strong ropes. About forty men are ready to pull. Crowds of people have come to watch, even though it is evening.

[long-pause]

Slowly, very slowly, the frame begins to move. The men pull on the ropes. The rollers creak and turn. The workers carry the rollers from the back of the frame to the front, again and again, so that the frame always has rollers under it. Inside the frame, the great white statue sways gently in its ropes, like a sleeping giant being carried home.

[pause]

{img:workers-pulling-ropes-greased-rollers-wooden-frame-statue-inside-lanterns}
The move goes very slowly. The streets of Florence are narrow and uneven. The frame must be steered carefully around the corners. Sometimes it moves only a few metres in an hour. The workers do not hurry. They know that if the statue falls, it could break, and two years of work would be lost forever. Piero is pulling on one of the ropes. His arms ache, but he pulls steadily, with the others, in rhythm.

[long-pause]

The move goes on for four days. The statue travels during the day and through the nights. Crowds follow it everywhere. People lean out of windows and stand on balconies to watch. Children run beside it. Old people come out of their houses to see. Nobody in Florence has ever seen anything like it. The giant is coming through the streets.

[pause]

{img:crowds-windows-balconies-watching-giant-statue-pass-narrow-street-day}
On one of the nights, something unpleasant happens. Some young men throw stones at the statue, in the dark. Nobody knows exactly why. Perhaps they were friends of the Medici, and did not like a symbol of the republic. Perhaps they were just being foolish. Luckily, the stones do no harm to the marble. Guards are put around the frame for the rest of the journey. The people who threw the stones are found and punished.

[long-pause]

Bianca and Matteo and their mother come to watch every day. Bianca brings bread and water for her father, and hands it to him through the crowd. Piero is tired and dusty, but he smiles at her. On the third day, Bianca gets close to the frame, and looks up through the wooden beams. And for the first time, she sees David. His great marble feet. His strong legs. His hand, holding the sling. And his calm, serious face, high up, looking out over the city.

[pause]

{img:girl-looking-up-through-wooden-beams-at-marble-statue-face-wonder-day}
She stands there with her mouth open, for a long time. She has never seen anything so beautiful. He looks as if he is alive, she thinks. As if he could step down out of the frame and walk through the streets. Matteo stands beside her, looking up too. He does not say anything. But he takes out a little piece of paper and a stick of charcoal, and begins to draw.

[pause]

On the morning of the eighteenth of May, the frame reaches the main square of the city. The crowd cheers. The giant has arrived. The workers let go of the ropes and sit down on the ground, exhausted. And Piero, covered in dust, finds his family in the crowd, and hugs them all at once.

[pause]

At night, while the statue rests in the street, the workers take turns to sleep beside the frame. They wrap themselves in blankets on the stones. Guards walk around with lanterns. The people of the street bring them soup and bread and wine. Piero sleeps there one night too. He lies on his back and looks up at the white marble figure above him in the moonlight. He thinks he will never forget it.

[long-pause]

{img:david-statue-piazza-della-signoria-palazzo-vecchio-door-unveiled-crowd-sunny}
After the statue arrives in the main square, there is still work to do. It must be lifted onto its stone base, in front of the door of the palace. That takes several more weeks. Then Michelangelo does some final work on it, polishing and finishing. And a little gilded decoration is added, though it will later be lost.

[long-pause]

At last, on the eighth of September, 1504, everything is ready. It is a feast day. The wooden scaffolding is taken away. And the statue of David stands alone in the main square of Florence, for everyone to see. It is white and shining in the late summer sun. It towers over the people below, calm and strong, looking out across the square.

[pause]

{img:people-gathering-around-david-statue-square-looking-up-amazed-1504}
The people of Florence come from all over the city to see it. They stand in the square and look up at the giant. Some of them are silent. Some of them talk excitedly. Some of them cry. They have never seen a statue like this. It is so big, and so beautiful, and so alive. And it is theirs. It belongs to the city of Florence.

[long-pause]

The family comes to see it together, in their best clothes, on the first Sunday. Piero walks slowly around the statue, looking at every detail. He knows how hard the marble is, and how much work it took. He shakes his head in wonder. Caterina holds Nonna Lucia's arm. The old woman looks up at the statue for a long time. In all my seventy years, she says at last, I have never seen anything so beautiful. And I never thought I would.

[pause]

{img:family-standing-before-david-statue-grandmother-looking-up-sunday-afternoon}
Matteo has brought his drawing paper. He sits on the steps of the loggia beside the palace, and draws David. He draws the head, the hands, the feet. He draws the whole figure, from the front and from the side. He draws until the light begins to fade. Many other young artists are there too, drawing. In the years to come, artists from all over Europe will come to Florence to draw David.

[long-pause]

The giant stands in front of the palace of the government, looking south across the square. People say that he is watching over the city. Watching out for any enemy who might come to take away its freedom. For the people of Florence, David is a promise: a small city can be brave and free, like a young shepherd standing up to a giant.

[pause]

{img:sunset-david-statue-square-golden-light-palazzo-tower-pigeons-calm}
Michelangelo is proud of his work, though he does not show it much. He is already thinking about his next work. He is a restless man, always moving on. But for the rest of his long life, he will be known as the man who made the David. And on summer evenings in Florence, the people walk through the main square, and look up at the giant, glowing white and gold in the last light of the setting sun.

[pause]

Soon, people start to write poems about David and leave them on the base of the statue. Some of the poems are funny, and some are serious. Some praise Michelangelo, and some praise the city. Every morning, there are new poems. People stop to read them, and laugh, and add their own. It becomes a kind of game, for the whole city.

[pause]

For weeks after the statue is unveiled, people come from all over Tuscany to see it. Farmers come in from the countryside in their carts. Merchants come from other cities. Priests and monks come from the monasteries. They all stand in the main square and look up. And they all go home and tell their families about the giant shepherd of Florence.

[long-pause]

{img:great-council-hall-palazzo-vecchio-1504-empty-walls-scaffold-two-painters}
In the same year, 1504, something else exciting happens in Florence. The two greatest artists of the city are asked to paint in the same room, at the same time.

[pause]

In the palace of the government, there is a huge new hall, called the Hall of the Great Council. It was built a few years before, for the meetings of the citizens of the republic. It is very big, with tall bare walls. The leaders of the city want to decorate the walls with great paintings, showing famous victories of Florence in the past.

[long-pause]

They ask Leonardo da Vinci to paint one wall. He will paint a famous battle, the Battle of Anghiari, with horsemen fighting over a flag. And they ask Michelangelo to paint the opposite wall. He will paint another battle, the Battle of Cascina. In his picture, soldiers are bathing in a river, and are suddenly called to arms. They are scrambling up the riverbank, pulling on their clothes.

[pause]

{img:leonardo-drawing-horses-cartoon-large-paper-studio-1504-sketches-energy}
Everyone in Florence is excited. It is like a competition between the two greatest artists in the world. Leonardo is fifty-two, the famous old master. Michelangelo is twenty-nine, the brilliant young sculptor. The two men do not like each other very much. They are very different. Leonardo is elegant, gentle and curious. Michelangelo is proud and fierce and passionate. People take sides, and argue about who will win.

[long-pause]

Both artists begin by making huge drawings, the same size as the paintings will be. These drawings are called cartoons. Leonardo draws horses and riders, twisting and turning, full of energy and movement. Michelangelo draws the soldiers' bodies, strong and muscular, in every possible position. When the cartoons are shown, young artists come from everywhere to study and copy them. People call them the school of the world.

[pause]

{img:young-artists-copying-large-drawings-hall-crowd-sketching-paper-light}
Matteo goes with his master to see the cartoons. He stands in front of them for hours, with his drawing paper. He has never seen anything like them. Leonardo's horses seem to gallop right out of the paper. Michelangelo's men seem to climb out of the wall. Matteo copies a horse's head, and a soldier's back. He takes the drawings home and shows them to Bianca, very proudly.

[long-pause]

But the great paintings are never finished. Leonardo tries a new way of painting on the wall, with a special mixture. It does not work well. The colours run and fade. He grows frustrated, and later leaves Florence. Michelangelo is called to Rome by the pope, before he can even begin painting. So the two great paintings remain unfinished. Later, the cartoons are cut into pieces and lost.

[pause]

{img:copy-drawing-battle-horses-riders-old-paper-museum-case-later}
Today, we know these works only from copies and sketches, made by other artists who saw them. Still, people say that the two cartoons changed art forever. Young artists learned from them for many years. And the people of Florence never forgot the year when the two greatest artists in the world worked side by side, in the great hall of their city.

[pause]

While they work on their cartoons, both artists have large workshops. Leonardo works in a big hall at a monastery near the old church of Santa Maria Novella. Michelangelo works in a room at a hospital. Each has assistants helping him. Each works in his own way. And all of Florence waits, eagerly, to see the results.

[pause]

Bianca asks her brother which of the two great artists is the best. Matteo thinks about it for a long time. Then he says that Leonardo is the best at making things look alive and full of movement. And Michelangelo is the best at making people look strong and beautiful. They are both the best, he says. Bianca says that is not a proper answer. Matteo laughs and says it is the only answer.

[long-pause]

{img:sistine-chapel-ceiling-michelangelo-painting-scaffold-rome-candles-later}
After 1504, the great artists of Florence went on to do many more wonderful things, in Florence and elsewhere.

[pause]

Michelangelo went to Rome, where the pope asked him to paint the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel. He did not want to, at first. He said he was a sculptor, not a painter. But he did it, lying on his back on a high scaffold, for four years. The ceiling shows the story of the creation of the world, from the Bible. It is one of the greatest works of art ever made. Later, he also designed the great dome of Saint Peter's Basilica in Rome. He lived until he was almost eighty-nine.

[long-pause]

Leonardo da Vinci went on travelling, to Milan and Rome. He kept the portrait of Lisa with him, and went on working on it for years. In the end, he went to France, invited by the young French king, Francis the First. The king admired him very much. Leonardo lived his last years in a little manor house in France, near the king's castle. He passed away there in 1519, at the age of sixty-seven. The Mona Lisa stayed in France. Today, it hangs in the Louvre museum in Paris.

[pause]

{img:leonardo-old-manor-house-france-garden-notebooks-gentle-last-years-later}
Florence went through many changes. The Medici family came back in 1512. Later, they became dukes, and ruled Florence and Tuscany for more than two hundred years. They built new palaces and gardens, and collected even more art. When the last of the Medici family passed away, in 1743, she left all the family's art to the city of Florence, on one condition. It must never leave Florence. And it never has.

[long-pause]

The Renaissance spread from Florence all over Europe. Artists from other countries came to Florence to study. They took the new ideas home with them, to France and Spain, to Germany and the Netherlands, to England and beyond. Painters learned to paint with depth and light. Architects learned to build with domes and columns. Scholars learned to read the old books and to think for themselves. In many ways, the modern world began in those narrow streets beside the Arno.

[pause]

{img:david-statue-moved-indoors-1873-academy-gallery-tribune-skylight-later}
The statue of David stood in the main square of Florence for more than three hundred and sixty years. It stood in the sun and the rain, the wind and the snow. It was damaged a little once, by a bench thrown from a window during a quarrel. Its arm broke into three pieces, but the pieces were saved and put back. In 1873, it was moved indoors, to a museum called the Accademia, to protect it. A copy now stands in the square, where the original once stood.

[long-pause]

What happened to the family? We can only imagine. Perhaps Piero worked at the cathedral workshop for the rest of his life, and was proud that he had helped to move the giant. Perhaps Caterina went on weaving silk, and taught Bianca everything she knew. Perhaps Matteo became a good painter, with his own workshop near the river, and taught apprentices of his own.

[pause]

{img:old-woman-bianca-showing-map-and-drawing-grandchildren-florence-window-later}
And perhaps Bianca, the girl who wanted to see the world, married a merchant who travelled to faraway lands. Perhaps she went with him, at least once, across the sea. And perhaps, when she was old, she sat by a window in Florence, with her grandchildren around her. She showed them an old drawing of a giant marble shepherd, made by her brother long ago. And she told them about the spring when the giant was carried through the streets, and she saw his face for the first time.

[pause]

Florence also became famous for many other things in later years. The scientist Galileo Galilei worked there, about a hundred years after our story. He looked at the moon and the planets through a telescope, and made great discoveries. Florentine craftsmen became famous for their leather work and their paper. And people from all over the world came to Florence to study its art and to learn its language.

[pause]

Florence was also the home of a new kind of music. About a hundred years after our story, a group of musicians and poets in Florence created the first operas. Operas are plays in which the actors sing their words, with music. The first operas told old stories from Greece. Soon, opera spread to Venice and Rome, and then all over Europe. It is still loved all over the world today.

[long-pause]

{img:florence-today-duomo-dome-view-from-piazzale-michelangelo-sunset-today}
Today, Florence is one of the most beautiful and most visited cities in the world. Millions of people come every year to see its art and its buildings.

[pause]

The great dome of the cathedral still rises above the red roofs of the city. Visitors can climb up inside it, between the two shells that Brunelleschi built. There are more than four hundred steps. At the top, there is a wonderful view over the whole city, and the green hills around it. The bell tower of Giotto still stands beside the cathedral, and the golden Gates of Paradise still shine on the baptistery.

[long-pause]

The statue of David stands in the Accademia, in a special hall built just for him. He stands under a round skylight, so the soft daylight falls on him from above. Visitors walk slowly toward him, down a long gallery. And when they reach him, most of them stop and look up, silent, for a long time. He is still as beautiful, and still as alive, as on the day he was finished.

[pause]

{img:david-statue-accademia-today-skylight-visitors-looking-up-quiet-today}
In the main square, the Piazza della Signoria, the copy of David stands in front of the old palace, where the original once stood. The palace itself is now the town hall of Florence. Visitors can go inside and see the great Hall of the Great Council. The walls are covered with later paintings now. But some people believe that part of Leonardo's lost painting may still be hidden underneath.

[long-pause]

The Medici family's art collection is now in a great museum called the Uffizi. It is beside the river, in a long building that was once the offices of the Medici government. Inside, there are paintings by Botticelli and Leonardo, Michelangelo and many others. The goddess on her seashell still floats to the shore, and the figures still dance in the spring garden. People come from all over the world to see them.

[pause]

{img:uffizi-gallery-today-visitors-botticelli-paintings-long-corridor-light-today}
The Ponte Vecchio still crosses the river Arno, with its little shops built on it. But the butchers are long gone. Since about a hundred years after our story, the shops on the bridge have been goldsmiths and jewellers. Their windows sparkle with gold and silver and precious stones. In the evenings, musicians play on the bridge, and people stand and watch the sun set over the river.

[long-pause]

And in the narrow streets of the city, there are still craftsmen at work. There are leather workers and goldsmiths, paper makers and bookbinders, painters and restorers. In little workshops near the river, they still work by hand, as their ancestors did. There are still silk weavers in Florence too, weaving on old wooden looms, in the old patterns.

[pause]

{img:florence-evening-today-ponte-vecchio-arno-sunset-musicians-people-today}
In the evenings, people still walk through the streets of Florence, and sit in the squares. They eat ice cream, and drink wine, and talk. Children play around the fountains, and the pigeons fly up in clouds. The bells of the cathedral ring out across the city. And as the sun goes down, the great dome glows red and gold above the rooftops, just as it did five hundred years ago.

[pause]

On the twenty-fourth of June, Florence still celebrates the feast of San Giovanni. There is a parade in old costumes, and fireworks over the river in the evening. And there is a game of calcio storico, the old Florentine football, played in a sandy square in front of a great church. Teams from the four old quarters of the city play each other. Thousands of people come to watch and cheer.

[pause]

In the hills around Florence, the olive groves and vineyards are still there, with their cypress trees and stone farmhouses. Every autumn, the farmers still pick the olives and press them for oil. They still pick the grapes and make wine. And from the hills, they can still see the great dome in the valley below, rising above the red roofs of the city, just as their ancestors did.

[long-pause]

{img:florence-1504-evening-sunset-dome-red-roofs-arno-golden-light-swallows}
So we leave Florence tonight, around the year 1500, as the evening falls.

[pause]

The sun is setting behind the hills to the west. The sky turns gold, then pink, then a deep soft blue. The great dome of the cathedral glows red and gold above the rooftops. The river Arno shines like a ribbon of light, flowing slowly under its bridges. Swallows dive and swoop over the roofs. And the bells of the city ring for evening prayers, one after another, from every church tower.

[long-pause]

In the main square, the people are walking slowly in the evening air. They stop to look up at David, standing white and tall in front of the old palace. He is glowing softly in the last light of the sun. Pigeons settle on his shoulders and his head. And he goes on looking out across the square, calm and patient, as if he is waiting for something.

[pause]

{img:florence-house-evening-family-loom-lamp-grandmother-children-drawing}
In the family's house in the narrow street, the lamps are being lit. Caterina is finishing the last lines of her silk for the day. Piero is sitting by the fire, with his tired hands in his lap. Matteo is at the table, with his drawings spread out in front of him. He is still working on his drawing of David, adding little details by the light of the lamp.

[long-pause]

Up on the loggia, Bianca is sitting beside Nonna Lucia, wrapped in a shawl. They are watching the sky over the rooftops. The first stars are coming out above the great dome. Nonna is humming the old carnival song of Lorenzo the Magnificent, very softly. How beautiful is youth, which flies away so fast. Bianca leans her head on her grandmother's shoulder and listens.

[pause]

{img:girl-and-grandmother-rooftop-loggia-dusk-stars-dome-humming-calm}
Bianca thinks about all the things she has seen. The great dome and the golden doors. The silk on her mother's loom. The painters' workshop and the old storyteller. The fireworks over the river, and the new map of the world. And the giant marble shepherd, carried through the streets, with his calm and serious face. She thinks she is very lucky, to live in such a city, at such a time.

[long-pause]
