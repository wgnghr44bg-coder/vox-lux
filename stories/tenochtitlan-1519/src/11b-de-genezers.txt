When someone falls ill in Tenochtitlan, they go to a healer, a ticitl.

The healers of the Mexica, men and women, know a great deal about the plants of Mexico, and their powers. In Moctezuma's gardens, at Oaxtepec, and elsewhere, medicinal plants are grown, from all over the empire, and the healers study them, and try them, and learn which ones help which illnesses.

--

In the market of Tlatelolco, there is a whole street of sellers of medicinal herbs, roots, barks, leaves, flowers, and seeds, hundreds of kinds, in baskets and bundles, with healers who will tell you which one to take.

There are herbs for fevers, and for coughs, and for headaches, and for stomach aches, and for wounds, and for burns, and for sleeplessness, and for sadness. Some of them really work, and are still used in Mexico today, in traditional medicine. Some are used in modern medicines too.

{img:herb-market-stall-dried-plants-roots-baskets-healer}
The healers also use the steam bath, the temazcalli, to cure illnesses, and to help women before and after giving birth. They set broken bones, with splints. They clean and stitch wounds, with human hair, and cover them with healing herbs, and with the sap of plants. The Spanish soldiers, when they were wounded, often went to the Mexica healers, because they were better than their own doctors.

--

The Mexica believe that illness can have many causes: natural causes, like cold, or bad food, or injury. But also supernatural ones: the anger of a god, or a broken rule, or a bad omen, or the loss of part of the soul. So the healer may also pray, and make offerings, and read the sacred calendar, to find out the cause of the illness, and the cure.

{img:healer-praying-offering-incense-small-altar-patient-family}
In 1552, thirty years after the conquest, two Mexica scholars at the college of Santa Cruz, in Tlatelolco, made a little book about the medicinal plants of Mexico. One of them, Martín de la Cruz, an old healer, wrote it in Nahuatl. The other, Juan Badiano, translated it into Latin. It has beautiful coloured pictures of the plants. It is the oldest book of medicine written in the Americas.

--

{img:old-herbal-book-painted-plants-pages-museum-case}
The little book was sent to Spain, as a gift for the king. Then it disappeared, for centuries, and was found again in the Vatican Library, in Rome, in 1929. And in 1990, the Pope gave it back to Mexico.

Today, it is kept in the National Museum of Anthropology, in Mexico City: a little book of plants and healing, made by two Mexica scholars, at the end of their world, so that the knowledge of their healers would not be lost.
