After the fall of Tenochtitlan, Cortés decides to build a new Spanish city, on the same place, on the ruins of the old one.

He calls it Mexico, after the Mexica. It becomes the capital of New Spain, the great Spanish colony that will stretch from Central America to California, for three hundred years.

--

The Spanish build their city on top of the Mexica city, using its stones. The main square of the new city is built on the old sacred precinct, next to the ruins of the Great Temple. A great cathedral rises there, built partly with stones from the temples. The palace of the Spanish viceroy is built on the site of the palace of Moctezuma.

The streets of the Spanish city follow the old Mexica streets and causeways, many of which are still the main streets of Mexico City today.

{img:cathedral-construction-stone-workers-plaza-colonial-1560}
The Spanish do not understand the lake, or the chinampas, or the canals. Over the following centuries, they slowly drain the lakes, to stop the floods, which often damage the city, and to make new land. By the twentieth century, the great lakes of the Valley of Mexico are almost all gone. Only a small part remains, in the south, at Xochimilco, with its canals and its chinampas.

--

Many Spanish friars come to Mexico, to teach the people Christianity.

Some of them are deeply interested in the Mexica, and their language, and their history, and their customs. They learn Nahuatl, and write it down in the Latin alphabet, and teach the young Mexica nobles to read and write in it.

The greatest of them is a Franciscan friar, named Bernardino de Sahagún, who comes to Mexico in 1529, and spends sixty years there. With the help of Mexica scholars, and old men who remembered the world before the conquest, he writes a great encyclopedia of the Mexica world, in twelve books, in Nahuatl and Spanish, with more than two thousand pictures, painted by Mexica artists.

--

It is called the General History of the Things of New Spain, or the Florentine Codex, because it is now kept in a library in Florence, in Italy.

It describes everything: the gods, and the festivals, and the calendar, and the rulers, and the merchants, and the craftsmen, and the plants, and the animals, and medicine, and the speeches of the old ones, and the story of the conquest, told by the Mexica themselves. Without it, we would know very little about the world of Tenochtitlan.

{img:codex-pages-pictures-text-columns-library-florence-light}
Much of what we have told tonight comes, in the end, from the work of Sahagún, and his Mexica helpers, who wanted to save the memory of their world.

--

The people of Mexico, Native and Spanish, slowly mix together, over the centuries, and become the Mexican people.

The old festivals blend with the Christian ones. The Day of the Dead becomes a festival of All Saints and All Souls, with marigolds and candles and offerings, as in the old days. The Virgin of Guadalupe, who, the story says, appeared to a Native man, Juan Diego, in 1531, on the hill of Tepeyac, where there had once been a temple of a Mexica mother goddess, becomes the most beloved symbol of Mexico.

{img:marigold-altar-candles-family-day-of-dead-gentle-glow}
And in 1821, when Mexico wins its independence from Spain, the new country chooses, for its flag, the old symbol of Tenochtitlan: the eagle, sitting on a cactus, on a rock, in a lake, with a snake in its beak. It is still on the flag of Mexico today.
