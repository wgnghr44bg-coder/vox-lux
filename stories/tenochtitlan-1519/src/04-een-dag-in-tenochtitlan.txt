Let us spend an ordinary day in Tenochtitlan, in the year 1519, with an ordinary family.

The day begins before dawn. From the tops of the temples, the priests blow great conch shells, deep, booming notes, and beat drums, to greet the rising sun, and to wake the city. The sun is the god Tonatiuh, and the Mexica believe that he must be honoured every day, so that he will continue his journey across the sky.

--

Our family lives in a small house, in one of the neighbourhoods of the city, near a canal.

The house is built of adobe, sun-dried mud bricks, plastered white, with a flat roof. It has a few rooms, around a little courtyard, where the family works, and cooks, and keeps a few turkeys, and a little dog. There is a garden too, with flowers, and chili plants, and herbs, and perhaps a few maize plants.

Inside, there is little furniture. The family sits and sleeps on mats, woven of reeds, called petates, on the floor. There are baskets and pottery jars for storing things, and a little shrine, with small clay figures of the household gods, where the family offers flowers, and food, and incense, every day.

--

The mother is the first to get up.

She lights the fire, on the hearth, which is made of three stones, set in a triangle, on the floor. And she begins to make tortillas, as every woman in Mexico does, every morning.

{img:woman-kneeling-metate-grinding-maize-hearth-three-stones}
The day before, she soaked the dried maize kernels in water mixed with lime, to soften them. Now she kneels in front of the metate, a flat stone, slightly sloping, and grinds the maize, with a long stone roller, called a mano, back and forth, back and forth, into a soft dough. It takes hours, every day.

Then she pats the dough into thin, round tortillas, between her hands, clap, clap, clap, and cooks them on a flat clay griddle, called a comal, over the fire. The sound of patting tortillas is the sound of every morning in Mexico.

--

The family eats breakfast together: tortillas, and perhaps a bowl of atole, a warm, thick drink, made of maize, mixed with water, and sometimes honey or chili. Then the father goes to work.

He is a craftsman, perhaps, a potter, or a stone cutter, or a weaver of mats. Or a farmer, who goes out by canoe, to work in his garden plots, on the edge of the lake. Or a fisherman. Or a porter, who carries goods on his back, across the city. Or a soldier.

{img:father-paddling-canoe-canal-morning-tools-baskets}
Every man in Tenochtitlan, except the priests, is a soldier too, when he is needed. He has trained as a boy, at school, and when the ruler calls, he must go to war.

--

The children help their parents.

The boys go with their father, and learn his trade. The girls stay with their mother, and learn to grind maize, and cook, and spin, and weave. Mexica parents are strict, but loving. They teach their children to be hard-working, and honest, and polite, and modest, and to respect their elders, and the gods.

Every girl, and every woman, spins and weaves. Women spin cotton, or maguey fibre, with a spindle, twirling in a little bowl, and weave cloth on a backstrap loom: one end of the loom is tied to a post, and the other to a strap around the weaver's back, and she leans back, to keep the threads tight. The cloth is used for clothing, and blankets, and to pay tribute, and even as money.

--

In the middle of the day, the main meal: more tortillas, and beans, and chili sauce, and perhaps squash, or tomatoes, or greens, and sometimes fish, or a little turkey, or duck.

In the afternoon, the work goes on. The mother goes to the market, to buy and sell. The children play: with balls, and dolls, and little clay animals on wheels, which are toys, the only wheels in all of Mexico. The Mexica know the wheel, but use it only for toys, because they have no animals to pull carts.

{img:children-playing-clay-toy-animals-wheels-courtyard-sun}
In the evening, as the sun goes down behind the western mountains, the conch shells sound again, from the temples. The family eats a light supper, and sits together, in the courtyard, and talks, as the stars come out. The father, or the grandfather, tells the children stories, about the gods, and the heroes, and the history of their people.

--

And then they lie down to sleep, on their mats, wrapped in their cotton blankets, for the nights are cool, at that height.

All night, the priests keep watch on the temples, and tend the sacred fires, and blow the conch shells at certain hours, to mark the passing of the night. And the city sleeps, on its island, in the middle of the dark lake, under the stars.
