When a baby is born in Tenochtitlan, the midwife welcomes it with beautiful words.

The Mexica have many speeches like this, called huehuetlatolli, the words of the old ones, which are spoken at important moments in life: at births, and weddings, and when children grow up, and when rulers are chosen. They are full of wisdom, and kindness, and poetry. Some of them were written down by a Spanish friar, Bernardino de Sahagún, a few years after the conquest, and we can still read them today.

--

To a newborn baby girl, the midwife says: my little one, my precious necklace, my precious feather, you have arrived. You will be in the house, like the heart in the body. You will be the ashes that cover the fire of the hearth.

And to a baby boy, she says that he belongs to the sun, and to the battlefield, and that his home is only a nest, and that he will go out into the world.

A few days later, the parents ask a priest, who reads the sacred calendar, to choose a lucky day for the naming ceremony. Children are often named after the day of their birth in the calendar: One Reed, or Seven Flower, or Two Rabbit. Or they are given names from nature: Precious Flower, or Smoking Mirror, or Jade Snake.

--

Mexica parents are strict, and teach their children carefully.

Fathers give their sons long speeches of advice. Be humble, they say. Be respectful to your elders. Do not mock the old, or the sick, or the poor. Do not gossip. Do not be lazy. Work hard. Walk calmly in the street, not too fast, and not too slow. Speak gently. Eat slowly. Be grateful for what you have.

{img:father-advising-son-sitting-courtyard-evening-gentle}
And mothers say to their daughters: take care of yourself, my daughter. Be clean. Wash your face, and your hands, and your mouth. Learn to spin and weave well, and to cook well. Do not be proud. Be kind to everyone. And if anyone is kind to you, be grateful.

--

All Mexica children go to school, boys and girls. This is very unusual in the world of 1519. In Europe, at that time, most children, especially girls, never went to school at all.

There are two kinds of schools. Most children go to the telpochcalli, the house of youth, in their own neighbourhood. There, the boys learn to be soldiers, and good citizens. They learn the history of their people, and the songs and dances of the gods, and how to work, building and digging and carrying. They learn to fight, with the club and the shield and the spear.

{img:boys-school-courtyard-training-shields-wooden-clubs-teacher}
The children of nobles, and some clever children of ordinary families, go to the calmecac, the school of the temple, where the priests teach them to read and write the picture-writing of the Mexica, and the calendar, and astronomy, and history, and law, and poetry, and the speeches, and the songs of the gods. They are trained to be priests, and judges, and officials, and leaders. The discipline is very strict. The students must get up in the night to pray, and bathe in cold water, and fast.

--

Girls go to their own schools, too, attached to the temples, where they learn the songs and dances of the gods, and how to serve in the temples. Some girls become priestesses.

Every evening, in every neighbourhood, there is a house of song, the cuicacalli, where the young men and women go to learn singing and dancing, from teachers, for the festivals of the gods. Music and dance are very important to the Mexica. Every festival has its own songs and dances, and everyone must know them.

The music is played on drums: a tall upright drum, called the huehuetl, played with the hands, and a two-toned wooden drum, the teponaztli, played with sticks. And on flutes, of clay and bone and reed, and rattles, and conch shells, and little bells.

--

When a young man is about twenty, and a young woman about sixteen, they marry.

The marriage is arranged by their families, with the help of old women who act as matchmakers. The wedding takes place in the evening, at the house of the groom. The bride is carried there on the back of a matchmaker, with a procession of her family, carrying torches. The bride and groom sit together on a mat, by the fire, and their cloaks are tied together, in a knot, as a sign that they are now joined.

{img:wedding-couple-cloaks-tied-together-hearth-torches-family}
Then there is a feast, and the old people give the young couple long speeches of advice, about how to live together, and be good to each other, and work hard, and raise their children well.
