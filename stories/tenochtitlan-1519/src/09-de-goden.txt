The Mexica live in a world full of gods.

There are gods of the sun, and the moon, and the stars, and the rain, and the wind, and the maize, and the water, and the earth, and fire, and death, and flowers, and music, and merchants, and many more. Hundreds of them. Every god has his or her own temple, and priests, and festivals, and colours, and symbols.

--

The greatest gods of Tenochtitlan are the two who share the top of the Great Temple: Huitzilopochtli, the hummingbird of the left, the god of the sun and of war, the special god of the Mexica, who led them from Aztlan. And Tlaloc, the god of rain, with his round goggle eyes, and his long fangs, who lives in the mountains, and sends the rain that makes the maize grow.

There is Quetzalcoatl, the feathered serpent, the god of wind, and of learning, and of priests, and of the morning star. He is a gentle, wise god, who, the stories say, once ruled as a king in the ancient city of Tollan, and taught people how to grow maize, and to make things, and to write, and to measure time. And then he went away, across the eastern sea, and promised one day to return.

There is Tezcatlipoca, the smoking mirror, the god of the night sky, and of fate, and of kings, powerful and mysterious, who sees everything in his magic mirror of black obsidian. There is Chalchiuhtlicue, she of the jade skirt, the goddess of lakes and rivers and springs. There is Xochiquetzal, the precious flower, the goddess of flowers, and beauty, and love, and weaving. And there is Centeotl, the young god of the maize.

--

The Mexica believe that the world has been created and destroyed four times before. Each world, each age, was called a sun. And each sun ended in disaster: by jaguars, by wind, by fire, and by flood.

Now, they live in the fifth sun. And the fifth sun, too, will one day come to an end, in earthquakes.

{img:great-round-stone-calendar-carving-sun-face-plaza}
They believe that the sun, and the gods, need to be fed and strengthened, so that the sun will keep moving across the sky, and the world will not end. The gods gave their own lives, at the beginning of the fifth sun, to set the sun in motion. And so the people must give back to the gods, in return.

--

They give offerings every day: flowers, and food, and incense, and rubber, and paper, and precious things, and songs, and dances. And the priests offer drops of their own blood, from little cuts on their ears and their legs, made with the thorns of the maguey plant.

And, on the great festivals, the Mexica also offer human lives to the gods. Most of those offered are prisoners of war, captured in battle.

{img:temple-steps-distant-evening-smoke-quiet-plaza-empty}
This is the hardest part of the story of the Mexica, and the part that horrified the Spanish when they came. We will not describe it. It is enough to know that it happened, that the Mexica believed, deeply and sincerely, that it was necessary, to keep the world alive, and that it was part of their religion, and their world, which is very far from ours. Many other ancient peoples, in many parts of the world, did similar things.

--

The priests are very important in Tenochtitlan.

There are thousands of them, men and women, serving in the temples. They wear black robes, and paint their bodies black, and let their hair grow long, and never cut it. They live strict lives, of fasting, and prayer, and study. They tend the sacred fires, which must never go out, and sweep the temples, and make the offerings, and sing and dance at the festivals.

The priests also keep the sacred books, and the calendar, and study the stars, and know the history of the Mexica, and the stories of the gods, and the songs. They are the scholars and the teachers of Tenochtitlan.

--

And the Mexica believe in an afterlife.

Most people, when they die, go to Mictlan, the land of the dead, in the north, far beneath the earth. The journey is long, four years, through nine levels, past mountains, and rivers, and winds of knives, and at the end, the soul simply fades away, and rests.

{img:marigold-flowers-candles-small-altar-offerings-soft-glow}
But warriors who die in battle, and women who die in childbirth, go to the paradise of the sun, and accompany the sun across the sky, every day, singing. After four years, they come back to earth as hummingbirds and butterflies, and drink the nectar of the flowers.

And those who die by water, by drowning, or by lightning, go to the paradise of Tlaloc, a green and pleasant land, full of flowers and fruits and gentle rain, where they live happily forever.
