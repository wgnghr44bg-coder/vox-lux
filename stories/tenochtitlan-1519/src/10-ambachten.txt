Tenochtitlan is a city of wonderful craftsmen.

The most admired of all are the featherworkers, the amanteca, who live in their own neighbourhood of the city, and have their own god, and their own festivals. They make pictures, and shields, and headdresses, and cloaks, and fans, out of feathers, in brilliant colours: the shimmering green of the quetzal, and the turquoise of the cotinga, and the red and yellow of parrots, and the iridescent colours of hummingbirds, which change as the light moves.

--

The featherworkers cut the feathers carefully, and glue them, one by one, onto a backing of paper or cloth, in layers, like a mosaic, or tie them, with thread, onto a net, to make cloaks and headdresses that move and shimmer in the wind.

Feathers are the most precious things in the Mexica world, more precious than gold. The long green tail feathers of the quetzal bird, which lives in the cloud forests of the south, are worth more than anything else.

There is a famous feather headdress, said to have belonged to Moctezuma, made of more than four hundred quetzal feathers, with gold. It was sent to Europe after the conquest, and is now in a museum in Vienna, in Austria. It is one of the very few Mexica featherworks that still exist.

--

There are goldsmiths too, who make beautiful jewellery: earrings, and lip plugs, and necklaces, and pendants, in the shapes of animals, and gods, and flowers, using the lost wax method. They make a little model in wax, and cover it in clay, and heat it, so that the wax melts away, leaving a hollow mould, and pour in the molten gold.

The German artist Albrecht Dürer saw some of the Mexica gold work, in 1520, in Brussels, when it had been sent to Europe by Cortés. He wrote in his diary that he had never seen anything in all his life that rejoiced his heart so much, and that he marvelled at the subtle genius of men in distant lands.

{img:gold-jewellery-animal-pendants-lip-plug-earrings-cloth}
Sadly, almost all the Mexica gold work was melted down by the Spanish, into bars of gold, to send to Spain. Only a few pieces survive, mostly those that were buried, and found later.

--

There are stone carvers, who make great statues of the gods, and carvings for the temples, in basalt, and other hard stones, using only tools of harder stone, and sand, and water, and patience. They carve the great round calendar stones, and serpents' heads, and jaguars, and eagles, and figures of gods, some of them huge, weighing many tons.

And they carve little things too, from precious stones: jade, and turquoise, and crystal, and obsidian, the black volcanic glass, which can be polished to make mirrors, or chipped into blades sharper than any steel.

{img:stone-carver-basalt-jaguar-sculpture-tools-courtyard}
There are mosaic makers, who cover masks and knives and skulls and shields with tiny pieces of turquoise, and shell, and jade, fitted together so closely that the surface looks like a single, shining stone. Some of these turquoise masks are in the British Museum, in London, today.

--

And there are the painters of books, the tlacuilo.

The Mexica do not have an alphabet, like ours. They write with pictures, and symbols, and signs for sounds, painted in bright colours, on long strips of deerskin, or bark paper, folded like a screen, or an accordion. These books are called codices.

They record history, and the calendar, and the stories of the gods, and the tribute lists, and maps, and laws, and the family trees of the rulers. The scribes are highly respected. They are trained at the calmecac, the temple school. The Mexica say that a good scribe is wise, and careful, and has God in his heart, and in his hand.

--

Sadly, almost all the Mexica books were destroyed after the conquest, by the Spanish, who thought they were the work of the devil. Only a few survive, from before the conquest, and some more painted by Mexica scribes in the years just after, at the request of the Spanish, explaining their history and customs.

One of the most beautiful is the Codex Mendoza, made in about 1541, for the Spanish viceroy, which shows the founding of Tenochtitlan, the eagle on the cactus, and the tribute of the empire, and the life of the Mexica from birth to old age. It is now in the Bodleian Library, in Oxford, in England.

{img:old-codex-page-eagle-cactus-founding-scene-colours}
And there are the potters, and the weavers, and the mat makers, and the carpenters, and the makers of musical instruments, and paper, and rope, and sandals. Every craft has its own neighbourhood, and its own gods, and its own festivals, and passes its skills from father to son, and mother to daughter.
