What do the people of Tenochtitlan wear?

Their clothes are simple, and made of woven cloth, without sewing, or cutting, or buttons. Every piece of clothing is a rectangle of cloth, woven on the backstrap loom, and wrapped, or tied, around the body.

--

A man wears a loincloth, called a maxtlatl, a long strip of cloth, wrapped around his waist and between his legs, with the ends hanging down, in front and behind. And over it, a cloak, a tilmatli, a large rectangle of cloth, tied in a knot over one shoulder.

A woman wears a skirt, a cueitl, a long piece of cloth wrapped around her waist, and held up by a belt, and a blouse, a huipilli, a simple, loose tunic, like a long shirt, with a hole for the head, often beautifully embroidered, around the neck and the hem. The huipil is still worn, by many women in Mexico and Guatemala, today.

{img:women-embroidered-huipil-blouses-skirts-market-chatting}
Ordinary people wear clothes made of the fibre of the maguey plant, a kind of agave, which is rough, and plain, and white, or brown. And sandals, also of maguey fibre, or of leather. Only the nobles, and the rich, may wear cotton, which is softer, and more expensive.

--

There are very strict laws about clothing, made by Moctezuma, and the rulers before him.

Commoners may not wear cotton cloaks, or cloaks that are long, below the knee, unless they have been wounded in the legs in battle. They may not wear jewellery of gold, or precious stones. They may not wear certain colours, or patterns. Anyone who breaks these rules can be severely punished.

The nobles wear beautiful cloaks of fine cotton, dyed in bright colours, red, and blue, and yellow, and green, and decorated with patterns: borders of geometric designs, and pictures of flowers, and shells, and animals, and the symbols of the gods. Some cloaks are decorated with feathers, or with rabbit fur, which is soft and warm.

--

The colours come from plants and insects and shells.

The most precious red, a deep crimson, comes from a tiny insect, the cochineal, which lives on the prickly pear cactus. The insects are collected, by hand, and dried, and crushed, into a red powder. It takes about seventy thousand insects to make half a kilogram of dye. After the conquest, the Spanish sent cochineal to Europe, where it became one of the most valuable things that came from the Americas, after silver. It was used to dye the red coats of the British soldiers, and the robes of cardinals, and it is still used today, to colour foods and lipsticks.

{img:cochineal-insects-on-prickly-pear-cactus-pads-red-dye-bowl}
Blue comes from the indigo plant. Yellow from flowers, and from the wood of certain trees. Purple from a sea snail, on the Pacific coast.

--

Jewellery tells everyone who you are.

The nobles and the great warriors wear earplugs, large round ornaments, through holes in their earlobes, of jade, or obsidian, or gold, or shell, or crystal. And lip plugs, through a hole below the lower lip, of gold, or crystal, or obsidian, sometimes in the shape of an eagle's head, or a serpent. And necklaces, and bracelets, and armbands, and anklets, of gold and jade and turquoise and shell.

{img:warrior-eagle-costume-feathers-shield-proud-calm-portrait}
The warriors, too, wear special costumes, according to how many prisoners they have captured in battle. The greatest of them, the eagle warriors and the jaguar warriors, wear costumes like eagles, with feathered helmets shaped like eagles' heads, or like jaguars, with spotted skins, and helmets like jaguars' heads, so that their faces look out from the open jaws.

--

And everyone, rich and poor, loves flowers, and wears them: in their hair, and around their necks, and carried in their hands.

Women often paint their faces with a yellow cream, made from a kind of earth, and dye their teeth red with cochineal, and their hair with dark mud, to make it shine. They wear their hair long, and on special occasions, braid it, with ribbons, and wind the braids around their heads, with two little points at the front, like horns.
