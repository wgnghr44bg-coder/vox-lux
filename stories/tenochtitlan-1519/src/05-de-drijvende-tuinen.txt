All around the edges of Tenochtitlan, and in the southern lakes, there are the chinampas: the so-called floating gardens.

They do not really float. They are long, narrow fields, built up out of the shallow water of the lake, separated from each other by canals, like long green islands, side by side, with water all around them.

--

The farmers build them by hand. They mark out a long rectangle, in the shallow water, with wooden stakes, and weave branches and reeds between them, like a fence. Then they fill the space inside with layers of mud, scooped up from the bottom of the lake, and rotting water plants, and earth, until it rises above the water.

They plant willow trees along the edges. The roots of the willows grow down into the lake bed, and hold the chinampa together, and make it strong. The tall, thin willows, standing in long rows, along the edges of the canals, are one of the most beautiful sights of the lake.

{img:farmer-scooping-mud-from-canal-onto-chinampa-canoe}
The chinampas are wonderfully fertile. The soil is rich, and always moist, because the water of the lake seeps into it from below. The farmers can grow several crops a year, without ever having to water them. Every few months, they scoop new mud from the bottom of the canal, and spread it over the field, to keep it rich.

--

On the chinampas, the farmers grow maize, the most important crop of all, and beans, and squash, and chili peppers, and tomatoes, and amaranth, a grain with tiny seeds, and herbs, and vegetables.

And flowers: marigolds, and dahlias, and many others, which the Mexica love, and use for decorating their homes and temples, and for offerings, and for festivals, and just for their beauty.

They start the young plants in little seedbeds, made of blocks of mud, cut into small cubes, each with a seed in it. When the seedlings are ready, they move the cubes to the field. It is a very clever way of farming, and it produces a great deal of food, enough to feed a large part of the city.

--

The chinampa farmers live on their fields, in little houses of reeds and mud, with thatched roofs, among the willows. They go everywhere by canoe, along the canals: to their fields, to their neighbours, to the market in the city.

In the morning, the canals are busy, with canoes loaded with vegetables and flowers, going to the market. The farmers stand in the stern, pushing the canoes along with long poles. There is the sound of water lapping, and birds singing in the willows, and people calling to each other across the canals.

{img:canoes-loaded-vegetables-flowers-poled-canal-morning-mist}
Around the chinampas, the farmers catch fish, and frogs, and little shrimps, and water insects, and collect the eggs of the water flies, which they eat. And they hunt the ducks and geese, with nets, and with throwing sticks.

--

The lake gives the Mexica many other foods too.

There is a little green algae, called tecuitlatl, which grows on the surface of the lake. The Mexica scoop it up, with fine nets, and dry it in the sun, into cakes, and eat it with tortillas. It is very nutritious. Today, it is called spirulina, and sold in health food shops all over the world.

{img:women-drying-green-algae-cakes-sun-lake-shore}
And there is a strange little animal in the lake, a kind of salamander, which stays in the water all its life, and never grows up, keeping its feathery gills, and its little smile. The Mexica call it the axolotl, which means water dog, or water monster, after their god Xolotl. They eat it, roasted. Today, the axolotl is one of the most beloved animals in Mexico, and is found almost nowhere else in the world, except in the last canals of the old lake.

--

In the evenings, the chinampa farmers sit by their houses, and watch the sun set over the lake, and the mountains turn purple, and the snow on the volcanoes glow pink and gold.

The frogs begin to sing. Fireflies drift over the canals. The lamps of the city shine across the water. And the farmers go to sleep, among their willows, to the sound of the water lapping against the edges of their gardens.
