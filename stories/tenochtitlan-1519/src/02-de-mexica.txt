The Mexica believed that they came from a place far to the north, called Aztlan, the place of the white herons. That is why we call them Aztecs, the people from Aztlan.

Nobody knows where Aztlan was, or if it was a real place. The Mexica said that it was an island, in a lake, where they lived, long ago. And that one day, their god, Huitzilopochtli, the hummingbird of the left, the god of the sun and of war, told them to leave, and to go south, to find a new home.

--

So they wandered, for many years, for generations, the stories say. They carried the image of their god with them, in a bundle, on the back of a priest. They stopped in many places, and planted crops, and moved on.

At last, in the thirteenth century, they came into the Valley of Mexico. But the valley was already full of people, and cities, and kingdoms. Nobody wanted the Mexica. They were poor, and rough, and they had a reputation as fierce fighters. They were pushed from one place to another, from the shores of the lake, into the marshes.

For a time, they served the king of a city called Culhuacan, as soldiers, and were allowed to live on his land, on a bare, rocky place, full of snakes. The king hoped the snakes would drive them away. But the Mexica, the story says, caught the snakes, and cooked them, and ate them.

--

Then, their god spoke to them again, through his priests.

He told them to look for a sign: an eagle, sitting on a prickly pear cactus, growing out of a rock, in the middle of the lake, eating a snake. And there, where they saw the eagle, they would build their city, and it would become great and powerful, and rule over all the lands.

{img:eagle-on-prickly-pear-cactus-rock-lake-reeds-golden}
And one day, the story says, on a little island in the marshes of Lake Texcoco, they saw it: the eagle on the cactus. And they knelt down, and wept with joy, and began to build.

The year, according to their tradition, was 1325.

--

They named their city Tenochtitlan, which means the place of the prickly pear cactus on the rock.

At first, it was just a little village of huts made of reeds and mud, on a small, muddy island, with a little temple to their god. They lived by fishing, and catching birds, and gathering plants from the lake, and trading them with the cities on the shore, for stone and wood, to build with.

{img:early-village-reed-huts-small-temple-island-lake}
Slowly, they made the island bigger, by building up the mud, and planting gardens, and driving stakes into the lake bed, and filling the space behind them with earth.

--

For about a hundred years, the Mexica were not very important. They paid tribute to the powerful city of Azcapotzalco, on the western shore of the lake, and served its king as soldiers.

Then, in 1428, under a great leader named Itzcoatl, the Mexica made an alliance with two other cities, Texcoco and Tlacopan, and together they defeated Azcapotzalco, and took its lands. The three cities, the Triple Alliance, became the most powerful force in the valley.

And over the next ninety years, the armies of the Triple Alliance conquered city after city, and land after land, far beyond the valley, east to the Gulf of Mexico, and south to the Pacific, and into the mountains and the tropical forests. And the Mexica, the poor wanderers, became the greatest power in Mexico.

--

The conquered peoples were not ruled directly. They kept their own kings, and their own gods, and their own ways. But they had to pay tribute to Tenochtitlan, every year, or every few months: maize, and beans, and cotton cloth, and cacao, and feathers, and gold, and jade, and jaguar skins, and turquoise, and rubber, and copal incense, and warriors' costumes, and many other things.

{img:tribute-bearers-carrying-goods-road-to-city-sunlit}
Long lines of porters carried the tribute along the roads to Tenochtitlan, on their backs, because the people of Mexico had no horses, or oxen, or wheeled carts. And the tribute made Tenochtitlan rich.

But it also made the conquered peoples resentful. And that, in 1519, will be very important.
