The Mexica measure time in a very special way.

They have two calendars, running side by side, like two wheels, turning together.

--

The first is a sacred calendar, of two hundred and sixty days, called the tonalpohualli, the count of days.

It is made of twenty day signs, each with its own picture: crocodile, wind, house, lizard, serpent, death, deer, rabbit, water, dog, monkey, grass, reed, jaguar, eagle, vulture, movement, flint knife, rain, and flower. And thirteen numbers, from one to thirteen. The numbers and the signs turn together, so that the days go: one crocodile, two wind, three house, and so on, until thirteen reed. Then the numbers start again, but the signs go on: one jaguar, two eagle, and so on.

Each day has its own character, lucky or unlucky, and its own gods. The priests read the sacred calendar, in their painted books, to decide the best days for everything: for naming a child, or for a wedding, or for starting a journey, or for planting, or for going to war.

--

The second calendar is a solar calendar, of three hundred and sixty-five days, called the xiuhpohualli, the count of years.

It has eighteen months, of twenty days each, which makes three hundred and sixty days. And then there are five extra days at the end of the year, called the nemontemi, the empty days. These five days are thought to be very unlucky. People stay at home, and do as little as possible, and wait for the new year to begin.

{img:quiet-city-empty-streets-five-unlucky-days-grey-sky}
Each of the eighteen months has its own great festival, for one or more of the gods, with processions, and dances, and songs, and feasts, and offerings, and the whole city takes part.

--

There are festivals for the rain gods, in the dry season, to ask for rain. There are festivals for the young maize, in the spring, when the girls dance with green maize stalks in their hands, and their hair loose. There are festivals of flowers, when the temples and the houses and the streets are covered with flowers, and everyone wears garlands.

There are festivals for the dead, in the late summer and autumn, when families remember their ancestors, and make offerings of food and flowers to them. The modern Day of the Dead, in Mexico, which is celebrated every year at the beginning of November, with marigolds, and candles, and sugar skulls, comes partly from these old festivals.

{img:festival-procession-flowers-garlands-dancers-street-sun}
There are festivals for the merchants, and the featherworkers, and the salt makers, and the fishermen, each with its own god.

--

The two calendars turn together, and every fifty-two years, they come back to the same starting point, at the same time.

This great cycle of fifty-two years is called a bundle of years. And at the end of every bundle, the Mexica fear that the world might end.

On the last night of the cycle, every fire in the whole Valley of Mexico is put out. The people break their old pots, and clean their houses, and throw away their old things, and wait, in the darkness, in silence, on the roofs of their houses, looking toward a hill, called the Hill of the Star, near the city.

There, on the top of the hill, the priests watch the stars, waiting for the Pleiades, the little group of stars, to pass the highest point of the sky, at midnight. If they do, the world will go on. And at that moment, the priests light a new fire, on the top of the hill, with a fire drill.

--

And when the people see the new fire, burning on the hilltop, far away in the darkness, they cheer, and weep with joy. Runners light torches from the new fire, and carry them, running, to the Great Temple in Tenochtitlan, and to all the temples, in all the towns of the valley, and from there to every house. And the fires are lit again, and the world goes on, for another fifty-two years.

The last New Fire ceremony was held in 1507, in the reign of Moctezuma. The next would have been in 1559.

{img:runners-carrying-torches-causeway-night-lake-city}
It never came.
