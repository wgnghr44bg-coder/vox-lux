The story of what happened next is a very sad one. We will tell it gently, and from a distance, as if we were watching from the shore of the lake, far away.

In the spring of 1520, Cortés has to leave Tenochtitlan for a while, to go back to the coast, to fight against another Spanish army, sent by the governor of Cuba to arrest him. He leaves some of his men in the city, under one of his captains, Pedro de Alvarado.

--

While Cortés is away, during a great festival, in the sacred precinct, Alvarado and his men turn against the Mexica nobles, who are dancing, unarmed, in honour of their god. Many are lost.

The people of Tenochtitlan rise up, in anger, and surround the Spanish in their palace. When Cortés comes back, with more men, he finds the city in revolt. In the struggle that follows, Moctezuma, the great speaker, passes away, at the end of June, 1520. The Spanish say that he was struck by stones thrown by his own people, when he tried to calm them. The Mexica say that the Spanish took his life. We do not know for certain.

Then, on a dark, rainy night, at the end of June, the Spanish try to escape from the city, along the western causeway, carrying as much gold as they can. The Mexica discover them, and attack them, from the causeway, and from canoes, on the lake. Many of the Spanish, and of their allies, are lost, in the water, in the darkness. The Spanish call it the Noche Triste, the sad night.

--

The survivors escape, and go back to Tlaxcala, to their allies, to rest and recover.

And then, in the autumn of 1520, something else comes to Tenochtitlan, more terrible than any army. Smallpox.

{img:quiet-city-houses-closed-doors-smoke-grey-autumn-sorrow}
Smallpox is a disease that had never existed in the Americas before the Europeans came. The people of Mexico had no protection against it at all. It came with a man on one of the Spanish ships, and spread through the country like a fire.

In Tenochtitlan, in the autumn of 1520, thousands and thousands of people fall ill, and pass away, including the new ruler, Cuitlahuac, Moctezuma's brother, after only eighty days as ruler. Perhaps a third, or even half, of the people of the Valley of Mexico are lost, in a few months.

--

In the spring of 1521, Cortés comes back, with his Spanish soldiers, and tens of thousands of Native allies, from Tlaxcala, and Texcoco, and many other cities.

He has thirteen small ships built, in pieces, in Tlaxcala, and carried over the mountains, by thousands of porters, and put together on the shore of the lake. And with the ships, and the armies, he surrounds Tenochtitlan, and cuts off its food and its water.

{img:small-sailing-brigantines-lake-distant-city-smoke-haze}
The new ruler of the Mexica is a young man, about twenty-five years old, named Cuauhtemoc, which means descending eagle. He is brave, and determined, and he and his people defend their city, for almost three months, street by street, canal by canal, with great courage, against the Spanish and their allies, and against hunger, and illness.

--

But on the thirteenth of August, 1521, it is over. Cuauhtemoc is captured, in a canoe, on the lake, trying to escape. And the great city of Tenochtitlan has fallen.

Much of it is in ruins. The temples are broken. The canals are filled with rubble. The people who survive leave the city, along the causeways, thin and tired, and go to live in the towns around the lake.

A Mexica poet, a few years later, wrote a sad song about it: Broken spears lie in the roads. We have torn our hair in our grief. The houses are roofless now, and their walls are red. We have pounded our hands in despair, against the adobe walls, for our inheritance, our city, is lost and dead.

--

So, in a little more than two years after the Spanish first saw it, from the pass between the volcanoes, the great city on the lake is gone.

It is one of the great tragedies of history. A city of two hundred thousand people, one of the most beautiful in the world, with its temples and its gardens and its canals and its markets and its schools and its poets, destroyed, by war, and by illness, and by the meeting of two worlds that had never known each other.

{img:single-heron-standing-still-lake-shore-evening-reeds}
But the people of Mexico did not disappear. Millions of them survived. Their language, Nahuatl, is still spoken today, by more than a million and a half people. And their descendants are still there, in the Valley of Mexico, and all over Mexico, today.
