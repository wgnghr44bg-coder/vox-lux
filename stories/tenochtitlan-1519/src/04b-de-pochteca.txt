Among the people of Tenochtitlan, there is a special group of merchants, called the pochteca, who travel far beyond the valley, to trade.

They live in their own neighbourhoods, especially in Tlatelolco, and have their own god, Yacatecuhtli, the lord of the nose, the lord who goes ahead, and their own laws, and their own festivals. Being a pochteca is passed from father to son.

--

The pochteca travel for months, sometimes years, on long journeys, to the far corners of the empire, and beyond: to the hot lands of the Gulf coast, and the Pacific coast, and south, to the lands of the Maya, and to Guatemala, and further.

They travel on foot, because there are no horses, or other animals to carry loads, in Mexico. Long lines of porters, called tlamemes, carry the goods on their backs, in baskets or frames, held by a strap, a tumpline, across their foreheads. A porter can carry about twenty-three kilograms, for about twenty or twenty-five kilometres a day, along the mountain paths, and the forest trails.

{img:porters-resting-trail-shade-baskets-tropical-forest}
The merchants take with them the goods of the city: fine cotton cloth, and embroidered cloaks, and obsidian blades, and mirrors, and gold jewellery, and copper bells, and needles, and herbs, and dyes. And they bring back the treasures of the hot lands: cacao, and quetzal feathers, and jaguar skins, and jade, and amber, and turquoise, and gold dust, and tropical shells, and rubber.

--

Their journeys are dangerous. They pass through the lands of peoples who are not friendly to the Mexica. Sometimes they are attacked. So the merchants travel together, in large groups, and carry weapons.

Sometimes, they disguise themselves as local people, and learn the local languages, and act as spies for the ruler of Tenochtitlan, bringing back news of the lands they visit, and of the riches there, and of how strong or weak the local people are. They are the eyes and ears of the empire.

{img:merchant-in-disguise-local-market-tropical-town-listening}
Before they leave on a journey, the merchants gather at the house of the leader, and pray to their god, and make offerings, and cut their hair. And while they are away, their families must not wash their heads, or comb their hair, as a sign of their concern, until they return.

--

When they come home, the merchants are careful not to show off their wealth.

They come back into the city at night, quietly, in canoes, so that nobody will see how much they have brought. They hide their treasures in the houses of their relatives. They wear plain clothes, and walk humbly, with their heads down. Because the nobles and the warriors of Tenochtitlan look down on merchants, and are jealous of their wealth, and might take it from them.

{img:canoe-arriving-night-canal-lanterns-quiet-unloading}
But among themselves, the merchants give great feasts, to celebrate their safe return, with chocolate, and food, and flowers, and singing, and dancing, all night long. Their feasts are among the most splendid in the city.

--

Thanks to the pochteca, the market of Tlatelolco is full of the goods of half a continent. And the people of Tenochtitlan know about lands and peoples far away.

And it was perhaps the pochteca, travelling on the Gulf coast, who first brought to Moctezuma the news of the strange ships with white sails.
