The Mexica, and their neighbours in the Valley of Mexico, love poetry.

They call it in xochitl, in cuicatl: flower and song. Poems are sung, to the music of drums and flutes, at the courts of the rulers, and in the houses of the nobles, and at festivals. Poets are honoured, and kings themselves write poems.

--

The most famous poet of all is Nezahualcoyotl, which means hungry coyote. He was the king of Texcoco, the city on the eastern shore of the lake, the ally of Tenochtitlan, and he lived from 1402 to 1472, about fifty years before the time of our story.

He had a hard youth. When he was a boy, his father, the king of Texcoco, was killed by enemies, and Nezahualcoyotl had to flee, and hide, and live in exile, for years. But he came back, and won back his kingdom, with the help of the Mexica, and became a great and wise king.

He made Texcoco a city of learning, and art, and law, with a great library, and schools, and a council of music and poetry, which judged the poets and the musicians. Texcoco was called the Athens of the Americas.

--

Nezahualcoyotl was also a great builder. He built a great dyke, across the lake, ten miles long, to separate the salty water of the eastern lake from the fresh water around Tenochtitlan, and to protect the city from floods.

And on a hill near Texcoco, called Texcotzinco, he built a wonderful garden, with terraces, and stairways, and pools, and baths, cut into the living rock of the hill, and fed by an aqueduct, with water flowing down from pool to pool. The remains of the baths are still there today.

{img:rock-cut-baths-stone-steps-hillside-garden-water-flowing}
And he wrote poems, many of which were remembered, and written down after the conquest, and can still be read today.

--

His poems are often sad, and thoughtful, and full of the beauty and the shortness of life.

He writes: Is it true that we really live on the earth? Not forever on earth, only a little while here. Though it be jade, it will break. Though it be gold, it will melt. Though it be quetzal feathers, they will tear apart. Not forever on earth, only a little while here.

{img:single-flower-hand-sunlight-garden-quiet-thoughtful}
And he writes about flowers, and songs, and friends, and how they are the only things that make life on earth worth living, and the only things that last.

He writes: I, Nezahualcoyotl, ask this: is it true that we really live with roots in the earth? Let us at least have flowers. Let us at least have songs.

--

Other poets write about friendship, and about the gods, and about war, and about the beauty of the world.

One poet writes: my friends, let us be happy. Let us embrace each other. We walk upon the flowered earth. Nobody can put an end to these flowers, to these songs. They last, in the house of the giver of life.

These poems are sung at gatherings of friends, in the evening, in the gardens and the courtyards, with the scent of flowers, and the sound of drums and flutes, and the cups of chocolate passing from hand to hand.

--

The Mexica also have a great love of flowers.

Flowers are everywhere: in the gardens of the palaces, and the roofs of the houses, and the chinampas, and the markets, and the temples. People carry bunches of flowers in their hands, and smell them, as they walk. They make garlands and necklaces of flowers, and give them as gifts. A noble, it is said, always carries a bunch of flowers, and a pipe of tobacco, when he goes out.

{img:nobles-walking-carrying-flower-bouquets-street-smiling}
For the Mexica, flowers are the symbol of beauty, and of joy, and of the shortness of life: beautiful for a day, and then gone. Just like the cherry blossoms in Japan, on the other side of the world, at the same time.
