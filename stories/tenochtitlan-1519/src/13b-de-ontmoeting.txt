On the eighth of November, 1519, the Spanish come down from the mountains, and walk along the southern causeway, across the lake, toward Tenochtitlan.

Bernal Díaz del Castillo, a young soldier, was there. Many years later, when he was an old man, living in Guatemala, he wrote a book about everything he had seen. And he remembered that morning, more than fifty years later, as if it were yesterday.

--

He wrote: when we saw so many cities and villages built in the water, and other great towns on dry land, and that straight and level causeway going toward Mexico, we were amazed, and said that it was like the enchantments they tell of in the book of Amadis, on account of the great towers and temples and buildings rising from the water, and all built of masonry. And some of our soldiers even asked whether the things that we saw were not a dream.

He wrote: I do not know how to describe it, seeing things never heard of, nor seen, nor even dreamed of, as we did.

The causeway is crowded with people, who have come out to see the strangers. The lake is crowded with canoes. The roofs of the houses, and the tops of the temples, are full of people, watching.

--

At the place where the southern causeway meets another causeway, near the entrance to the city, Moctezuma comes out to meet them.

He comes in a litter, with a great company of nobles, in their finest clothes, with feathers and gold. Then he gets down from the litter, and walks, under a canopy of green feathers and gold and silver and pearls, held over him by four lords. He wears sandals with golden soles, set with precious stones. The nobles sweep the ground in front of him, and lay down cloaks, so that his feet will not touch the earth.

{img:moctezuma-canopy-green-feathers-nobles-causeway-meeting}
Cortés gets down from his horse, and goes forward to embrace him. But the lords stop him. Nobody may touch the great speaker. Instead, Cortés places a necklace of glass beads around Moctezuma's neck. And Moctezuma gives Cortés two necklaces of shells, with golden shrimps hanging from them.

--

Then Moctezuma speaks. According to a Mexica account, written down years later, he says something like this:

Our lord, you are weary. You have come to your land, to your city. You have come to sit on your mat, on your seat, which I have kept for you. The rulers who went before me have gone. Now, you have come. I have seen you in dreams, and now I see you with my own eyes. Welcome. Rest. Come into your palace.

{img:two-leaders-facing-each-other-causeway-interpreter-between}
Did Moctezuma really say this? Was he being polite, in the very formal way of the Mexica, as a host greeting a guest? Or did he really think that Cortés was a god, or a messenger of the gods? Historians still argue about it.

--

Moctezuma takes the Spanish into the city, and lodges them in the palace of his father, Axayacatl, a great building, near the Great Temple, with many rooms and courtyards. He gives them food, and gifts, and servants. He treats them as honoured guests.

In the following days, Moctezuma shows them the city. He takes them to the market of Tlatelolco, and up to the top of the Great Temple, from where they look out over the city, and the lake, and the causeways, and the towns around the shore. Bernal Díaz says that from there they could see the whole city, and the canoes, and the bridges, and it was a wonderful sight.

For a few days, Tenochtitlan is at peace, and the strangers walk through it, amazed, looking at everything.

--

But it does not last.

After about a week, Cortés, fearing that the Mexica might turn against him, takes Moctezuma prisoner, in his own city, and keeps him under guard, in the palace of Axayacatl. Moctezuma goes on ruling, in name, and receiving his nobles, but he is a prisoner.

{img:palace-courtyard-guards-quiet-evening-lamps-tension}
And for the next months, the city waits, uneasily, while the strangers live in the palace, at its heart.
