Tonight we are going about five hundred years back, to a great city built on an island in a shining lake, high in the mountains of Mexico, in the year 1519.

[long-pause]

The city is called Tenochtitlan. It is the capital of the Mexica people, whom we often call the Aztecs. White temples and palaces rise out of the blue water, with canoes gliding along its canals, and gardens floating around its edges, and snow-capped volcanoes standing guard on the horizon.

[pause]

It is one of the largest and most beautiful cities in the world. And in this year, strangers from across the sea are about to see it for the first time.

[pause]

You are welcome to subscribe to the channel, if you like falling asleep to slow stories from faraway times.

[long-pause]

Let us imagine the Valley of Mexico, as it was in 1519.

[pause]

It is a great high valley, more than two thousand metres above the sea, surrounded on all sides by mountains. To the southeast, there are two great volcanoes, side by side, their tops covered with snow all year round: Popocatepetl, the smoking mountain, which often sends a thin plume of smoke into the sky, and Iztaccihuatl, the white woman, which looks like a sleeping woman, lying on her back, covered with a white blanket of snow.

[long-pause]

In the middle of the valley, there is not one lake, but a chain of five lakes, joined together, covering a great part of the valley floor. In the rainy season, they join into one great lake. Some of the lakes are salty, and some are fresh. Around them, there are marshes, and reed beds, full of birds: ducks, and geese, and herons, and egrets, thousands and thousands of them.

[pause]

The air is thin and clear and fresh, because the valley is so high. The sun is bright and strong. The days are warm, and the nights are cool. The sky, in the dry season, is a deep, clear blue.

[pause]

{img:lake-texcoco-reeds-herons-ducks-clear-morning-mountains}
Around the shores of the lakes, there are many towns and cities, more than fifty of them, with their temples and their markets and their fields. Perhaps one or two million people live in the valley. It is one of the most crowded places on earth.

[long-pause]

And in the western part of the great salt lake, which is called Lake Texcoco, there is an island. And on the island, there is the city of Tenochtitlan.

[pause]

It is a wonderful sight. The city seems to float on the water. From the shore, you can see its white walls, shining in the sun, and the great pyramids of its temples, rising above the roofs, with their shrines on top, painted in bright colours, and the smoke of incense rising from them.

[pause]

Three great causeways, roads built of stone and earth, run across the water, from the island to the shore: to the north, to the west, and to the south. They are wide enough for eight horsemen to ride side by side, the Spanish will later say. Along them, all day long, people walk to and from the city, carrying goods on their backs.

[long-pause]

And the lake around the city is full of canoes.

[pause]

Thousands of them, long and narrow, carved from tree trunks, gliding over the water, carrying people and goods to and from the city: maize, and beans, and vegetables, and flowers, and fish, and stone, and wood, and cloth. Some say there were fifty thousand canoes on the lake.

[pause]

{img:many-dugout-canoes-lake-goods-flowers-paddlers-sunlight}
The canoes come right into the city itself, along the canals, which run between the houses, like streets of water, so that, like Venice, much of the city can be reached by boat.

[long-pause]

How many people live in Tenochtitlan?

[pause]

Historians think that perhaps two hundred thousand people lived in the city, and its twin city of Tlatelolco, on the northern part of the island. That would make it one of the largest cities in the world, at that time, larger than any city in Spain, larger than London, or Rome, about the same size as Paris, or Constantinople, or Venice.

[pause]

It is the capital of a great empire, which stretches from the Gulf of Mexico to the Pacific Ocean, and rules over millions of people, of many different nations and languages, who pay tribute to the Mexica every year.

[long-pause]

And yet, the city is not very old.

[pause]

Less than two hundred years before, there was nothing on this island but reeds, and marshes, and a few rocks, and snakes, and birds. The Mexica came here, a poor, wandering people, and built their city from nothing, in the middle of a lake.

[pause]

{img:reed-marsh-island-small-huts-ancient-lake-early-morning}
How they did it, and how they became the masters of Mexico, is one of the most remarkable stories in history.

[long-pause]

{img:wandering-people-walking-desert-carrying-bundles-ancient}
The Mexica believed that they came from a place far to the north, called Aztlan, the place of the white herons. That is why we call them Aztecs, the people from Aztlan.

[pause]

Nobody knows where Aztlan was, or if it was a real place. The Mexica said that it was an island, in a lake, where they lived, long ago. And that one day, their god, Huitzilopochtli, the hummingbird of the left, the god of the sun and of war, told them to leave, and to go south, to find a new home.

[long-pause]

So they wandered, for many years, for generations, the stories say. They carried the image of their god with them, in a bundle, on the back of a priest. They stopped in many places, and planted crops, and moved on.

[pause]

At last, in the thirteenth century, they came into the Valley of Mexico. But the valley was already full of people, and cities, and kingdoms. Nobody wanted the Mexica. They were poor, and rough, and they had a reputation as fierce fighters. They were pushed from one place to another, from the shores of the lake, into the marshes.

[pause]

For a time, they served the king of a city called Culhuacan, as soldiers, and were allowed to live on his land, on a bare, rocky place, full of snakes. The king hoped the snakes would drive them away. But the Mexica, the story says, caught the snakes, and cooked them, and ate them.

[long-pause]

Then, their god spoke to them again, through his priests.

[pause]

He told them to look for a sign: an eagle, sitting on a prickly pear cactus, growing out of a rock, in the middle of the lake, eating a snake. And there, where they saw the eagle, they would build their city, and it would become great and powerful, and rule over all the lands.

[pause]

{img:eagle-on-prickly-pear-cactus-rock-lake-reeds-golden}
And one day, the story says, on a little island in the marshes of Lake Texcoco, they saw it: the eagle on the cactus. And they knelt down, and wept with joy, and began to build.

[pause]

The year, according to their tradition, was 1325.

[long-pause]

They named their city Tenochtitlan, which means the place of the prickly pear cactus on the rock.

[pause]

At first, it was just a little village of huts made of reeds and mud, on a small, muddy island, with a little temple to their god. They lived by fishing, and catching birds, and gathering plants from the lake, and trading them with the cities on the shore, for stone and wood, to build with.

[pause]

{img:early-village-reed-huts-small-temple-island-lake}
Slowly, they made the island bigger, by building up the mud, and planting gardens, and driving stakes into the lake bed, and filling the space behind them with earth.

[long-pause]

For about a hundred years, the Mexica were not very important. They paid tribute to the powerful city of Azcapotzalco, on the western shore of the lake, and served its king as soldiers.

[pause]

Then, in 1428, under a great leader named Itzcoatl, the Mexica made an alliance with two other cities, Texcoco and Tlacopan, and together they defeated Azcapotzalco, and took its lands. The three cities, the Triple Alliance, became the most powerful force in the valley.

[pause]

And over the next ninety years, the armies of the Triple Alliance conquered city after city, and land after land, far beyond the valley, east to the Gulf of Mexico, and south to the Pacific, and into the mountains and the tropical forests. And the Mexica, the poor wanderers, became the greatest power in Mexico.

[long-pause]

The conquered peoples were not ruled directly. They kept their own kings, and their own gods, and their own ways. But they had to pay tribute to Tenochtitlan, every year, or every few months: maize, and beans, and cotton cloth, and cacao, and feathers, and gold, and jade, and jaguar skins, and turquoise, and rubber, and copal incense, and warriors' costumes, and many other things.

[pause]

{img:tribute-bearers-carrying-goods-road-to-city-sunlit}
Long lines of porters carried the tribute along the roads to Tenochtitlan, on their backs, because the people of Mexico had no horses, or oxen, or wheeled carts. And the tribute made Tenochtitlan rich.

[pause]

But it also made the conquered peoples resentful. And that, in 1519, will be very important.

[long-pause]

{img:tenochtitlan-sacred-precinct-twin-temple-pyramid-plaza-sunlight}
Let us walk into Tenochtitlan, along the southern causeway, on a spring morning, in the year 1519.

[pause]

The causeway runs straight across the shining water, for several miles, from the shore to the city. It is built of stone and earth, wide and smooth. At intervals, there are gaps in it, with wooden bridges over them, so that the water can flow through, and canoes can pass. The bridges can be taken up, in time of war, to protect the city.

[long-pause]

All around us, there is the lake, blue and calm, with canoes gliding over it, and birds flying low over the water. Ahead of us, the city grows larger and larger, its white walls shining in the sun.

[pause]

As we come into the city, the causeway becomes a broad street, running straight toward the centre, lined with houses, and gardens, and temples. The houses are low, one or two storeys, with flat roofs, built of stone or adobe, mud brick, plastered and painted white, or red. Many have gardens on their roofs, full of flowers.

[pause]

The city is laid out in a careful plan, with straight streets and canals, crossing each other at right angles, and divided into four great quarters, and many smaller neighbourhoods, called calpolli, each with its own temple, and school, and market, and leaders.

[long-pause]

It is very clean. This surprises the Spanish, when they come, because the cities of Europe at that time are dirty and smelly.

[pause]

In Tenochtitlan, a thousand men are employed to sweep and wash the streets every day. Waste is collected, and carried away by canoe, to be used as fertiliser in the fields. Every house has a toilet, and the waste is collected too, and used, or sold. People bathe often, every day if they can, and many houses have a little steam bath, called a temazcalli, a small dome of stone, heated by a fire, where people sit in the steam, and wash.

[pause]

{img:small-stone-steam-bath-dome-courtyard-house-garden}
There is fresh water too, brought to the city from springs in the hills of Chapultepec, on the western shore, along a great aqueduct of stone and clay, with two channels, so that one can be cleaned while the other is used. The water flows into fountains and pools in the city, and the people fetch it in jars. Water sellers paddle canoes through the canals, selling fresh water to the houses.

[long-pause]

And in the centre of the city, there is the great sacred precinct.

[pause]

It is a huge square, surrounded by a wall, carved with serpents' heads. Inside, there are dozens of temples, and shrines, and schools for priests, and a ball court, and houses for the priests, and pools for ritual bathing. The ground is paved with smooth stone, white and gleaming.

[pause]

And rising above everything, in the middle, there is the Great Temple, which the Mexica call Huey Teocalli, and which we call the Templo Mayor.

[long-pause]

The Great Temple is a pyramid, about sixty metres high, with four great terraces, one above the other, and a great double staircase, running up the front, with more than a hundred steps.

[pause]

On the top, there are two shrines, side by side. One, painted red and white, is for Huitzilopochtli, the god of the sun and of war, the god of the Mexica. The other, painted blue and white, is for Tlaloc, the god of rain, who makes the maize grow. Sun and rain. War and farming. The two things that the Mexica need most.

[pause]

{img:great-temple-twin-shrines-red-blue-steps-incense-sky}
From the top of the Great Temple, the whole city can be seen, spread out below, and the lake, and the causeways, and the towns around the shore, and the mountains, and the volcanoes.

[long-pause]

Next to the sacred precinct, there are the palaces.

[pause]

The palace of the ruler, Moctezuma, is enormous, with hundreds of rooms, around great courtyards, with gardens, and fountains, and pools. There are rooms for his councillors, and his judges, and his warriors, and his scribes, and his guests, and his treasures. There are storehouses, full of tribute. There are workshops, for the finest craftsmen.

[pause]

And around the palaces, there are the houses of the nobles, large and beautiful, of stone, with gardens and courtyards, and painted walls.

[long-pause]

To the north of Tenochtitlan, on the same island, is the twin city of Tlatelolco.

[pause]

Tlatelolco was once a separate city, founded by a group of Mexica who split off from the others, soon after the founding of Tenochtitlan. But in 1473, it was conquered by Tenochtitlan, and now it is part of the capital.

[pause]

{img:tlatelolco-plaza-temple-market-stalls-distance-busy}
Tlatelolco has its own great temple, and its own sacred precinct. And it has the greatest market in all of the Americas, where every day, tens of thousands of people come to buy and sell.

[pause]

We shall visit it soon.

[long-pause]

{img:dawn-temple-priest-blowing-conch-shell-city-waking}
Let us spend an ordinary day in Tenochtitlan, in the year 1519, with an ordinary family.

[pause]

The day begins before dawn. From the tops of the temples, the priests blow great conch shells, deep, booming notes, and beat drums, to greet the rising sun, and to wake the city. The sun is the god Tonatiuh, and the Mexica believe that he must be honoured every day, so that he will continue his journey across the sky.

[long-pause]

Our family lives in a small house, in one of the neighbourhoods of the city, near a canal.

[pause]

The house is built of adobe, sun-dried mud bricks, plastered white, with a flat roof. It has a few rooms, around a little courtyard, where the family works, and cooks, and keeps a few turkeys, and a little dog. There is a garden too, with flowers, and chili plants, and herbs, and perhaps a few maize plants.

[pause]

Inside, there is little furniture. The family sits and sleeps on mats, woven of reeds, called petates, on the floor. There are baskets and pottery jars for storing things, and a little shrine, with small clay figures of the household gods, where the family offers flowers, and food, and incense, every day.

[long-pause]

The mother is the first to get up.

[pause]

She lights the fire, on the hearth, which is made of three stones, set in a triangle, on the floor. And she begins to make tortillas, as every woman in Mexico does, every morning.

[pause]

{img:woman-kneeling-metate-grinding-maize-hearth-three-stones}
The day before, she soaked the dried maize kernels in water mixed with lime, to soften them. Now she kneels in front of the metate, a flat stone, slightly sloping, and grinds the maize, with a long stone roller, called a mano, back and forth, back and forth, into a soft dough. It takes hours, every day.

[pause]

Then she pats the dough into thin, round tortillas, between her hands, clap, clap, clap, and cooks them on a flat clay griddle, called a comal, over the fire. The sound of patting tortillas is the sound of every morning in Mexico.

[long-pause]

The family eats breakfast together: tortillas, and perhaps a bowl of atole, a warm, thick drink, made of maize, mixed with water, and sometimes honey or chili. Then the father goes to work.

[pause]

He is a craftsman, perhaps, a potter, or a stone cutter, or a weaver of mats. Or a farmer, who goes out by canoe, to work in his garden plots, on the edge of the lake. Or a fisherman. Or a porter, who carries goods on his back, across the city. Or a soldier.

[pause]

{img:father-paddling-canoe-canal-morning-tools-baskets}
Every man in Tenochtitlan, except the priests, is a soldier too, when he is needed. He has trained as a boy, at school, and when the ruler calls, he must go to war.

[long-pause]

The children help their parents.

[pause]

The boys go with their father, and learn his trade. The girls stay with their mother, and learn to grind maize, and cook, and spin, and weave. Mexica parents are strict, but loving. They teach their children to be hard-working, and honest, and polite, and modest, and to respect their elders, and the gods.

[pause]

Every girl, and every woman, spins and weaves. Women spin cotton, or maguey fibre, with a spindle, twirling in a little bowl, and weave cloth on a backstrap loom: one end of the loom is tied to a post, and the other to a strap around the weaver's back, and she leans back, to keep the threads tight. The cloth is used for clothing, and blankets, and to pay tribute, and even as money.

[long-pause]

In the middle of the day, the main meal: more tortillas, and beans, and chili sauce, and perhaps squash, or tomatoes, or greens, and sometimes fish, or a little turkey, or duck.

[pause]

In the afternoon, the work goes on. The mother goes to the market, to buy and sell. The children play: with balls, and dolls, and little clay animals on wheels, which are toys, the only wheels in all of Mexico. The Mexica know the wheel, but use it only for toys, because they have no animals to pull carts.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-clay-toy-animals-wheels-courtyard-sun}
In the evening, as the sun goes down behind the western mountains, the conch shells sound again, from the temples. The family eats a light supper, and sits together, in the courtyard, and talks, as the stars come out. The father, or the grandfather, tells the children stories, about the gods, and the heroes, and the history of their people.

[long-pause]

And then they lie down to sleep, on their mats, wrapped in their cotton blankets, for the nights are cool, at that height.

[pause]

All night, the priests keep watch on the temples, and tend the sacred fires, and blow the conch shells at certain hours, to mark the passing of the night. And the city sleeps, on its island, in the middle of the dark lake, under the stars.

[long-pause]

{img:pochteca-merchants-caravan-porters-tumplines-mountain-road}
Among the people of Tenochtitlan, there is a special group of merchants, called the pochteca, who travel far beyond the valley, to trade.

[pause]

They live in their own neighbourhoods, especially in Tlatelolco, and have their own god, Yacatecuhtli, the lord of the nose, the lord who goes ahead, and their own laws, and their own festivals. Being a pochteca is passed from father to son.

[long-pause]

The pochteca travel for months, sometimes years, on long journeys, to the far corners of the empire, and beyond: to the hot lands of the Gulf coast, and the Pacific coast, and south, to the lands of the Maya, and to Guatemala, and further.

[pause]

They travel on foot, because there are no horses, or other animals to carry loads, in Mexico. Long lines of porters, called tlamemes, carry the goods on their backs, in baskets or frames, held by a strap, a tumpline, across their foreheads. A porter can carry about twenty-three kilograms, for about twenty or twenty-five kilometres a day, along the mountain paths, and the forest trails.

[pause]

{img:porters-resting-trail-shade-baskets-tropical-forest}
The merchants take with them the goods of the city: fine cotton cloth, and embroidered cloaks, and obsidian blades, and mirrors, and gold jewellery, and copper bells, and needles, and herbs, and dyes. And they bring back the treasures of the hot lands: cacao, and quetzal feathers, and jaguar skins, and jade, and amber, and turquoise, and gold dust, and tropical shells, and rubber.

[long-pause]

Their journeys are dangerous. They pass through the lands of peoples who are not friendly to the Mexica. Sometimes they are attacked. So the merchants travel together, in large groups, and carry weapons.

[pause]

Sometimes, they disguise themselves as local people, and learn the local languages, and act as spies for the ruler of Tenochtitlan, bringing back news of the lands they visit, and of the riches there, and of how strong or weak the local people are. They are the eyes and ears of the empire.

[pause]

{img:merchant-in-disguise-local-market-tropical-town-listening}
Before they leave on a journey, the merchants gather at the house of the leader, and pray to their god, and make offerings, and cut their hair. And while they are away, their families must not wash their heads, or comb their hair, as a sign of their concern, until they return.

[long-pause]

When they come home, the merchants are careful not to show off their wealth.

[pause]

They come back into the city at night, quietly, in canoes, so that nobody will see how much they have brought. They hide their treasures in the houses of their relatives. They wear plain clothes, and walk humbly, with their heads down. Because the nobles and the warriors of Tenochtitlan look down on merchants, and are jealous of their wealth, and might take it from them.

[pause]

{img:canoe-arriving-night-canal-lanterns-quiet-unloading}
But among themselves, the merchants give great feasts, to celebrate their safe return, with chocolate, and food, and flowers, and singing, and dancing, all night long. Their feasts are among the most splendid in the city.

[long-pause]

Thanks to the pochteca, the market of Tlatelolco is full of the goods of half a continent. And the people of Tenochtitlan know about lands and peoples far away.

[pause]

And it was perhaps the pochteca, travelling on the Gulf coast, who first brought to Moctezuma the news of the strange ships with white sails.

[long-pause]

{img:chinampas-floating-gardens-canals-willows-maize-flowers}
All around the edges of Tenochtitlan, and in the southern lakes, there are the chinampas: the so-called floating gardens.

[pause]

They do not really float. They are long, narrow fields, built up out of the shallow water of the lake, separated from each other by canals, like long green islands, side by side, with water all around them.

[long-pause]

The farmers build them by hand. They mark out a long rectangle, in the shallow water, with wooden stakes, and weave branches and reeds between them, like a fence. Then they fill the space inside with layers of mud, scooped up from the bottom of the lake, and rotting water plants, and earth, until it rises above the water.

[pause]

They plant willow trees along the edges. The roots of the willows grow down into the lake bed, and hold the chinampa together, and make it strong. The tall, thin willows, standing in long rows, along the edges of the canals, are one of the most beautiful sights of the lake.

[pause]

{img:farmer-scooping-mud-from-canal-onto-chinampa-canoe}
The chinampas are wonderfully fertile. The soil is rich, and always moist, because the water of the lake seeps into it from below. The farmers can grow several crops a year, without ever having to water them. Every few months, they scoop new mud from the bottom of the canal, and spread it over the field, to keep it rich.

[long-pause]

On the chinampas, the farmers grow maize, the most important crop of all, and beans, and squash, and chili peppers, and tomatoes, and amaranth, a grain with tiny seeds, and herbs, and vegetables.

[pause]

And flowers: marigolds, and dahlias, and many others, which the Mexica love, and use for decorating their homes and temples, and for offerings, and for festivals, and just for their beauty.

[pause]

They start the young plants in little seedbeds, made of blocks of mud, cut into small cubes, each with a seed in it. When the seedlings are ready, they move the cubes to the field. It is a very clever way of farming, and it produces a great deal of food, enough to feed a large part of the city.

[long-pause]

The chinampa farmers live on their fields, in little houses of reeds and mud, with thatched roofs, among the willows. They go everywhere by canoe, along the canals: to their fields, to their neighbours, to the market in the city.

[pause]

In the morning, the canals are busy, with canoes loaded with vegetables and flowers, going to the market. The farmers stand in the stern, pushing the canoes along with long poles. There is the sound of water lapping, and birds singing in the willows, and people calling to each other across the canals.

[pause]

{img:canoes-loaded-vegetables-flowers-poled-canal-morning-mist}
Around the chinampas, the farmers catch fish, and frogs, and little shrimps, and water insects, and collect the eggs of the water flies, which they eat. And they hunt the ducks and geese, with nets, and with throwing sticks.

[long-pause]

The lake gives the Mexica many other foods too.

[pause]

There is a little green algae, called tecuitlatl, which grows on the surface of the lake. The Mexica scoop it up, with fine nets, and dry it in the sun, into cakes, and eat it with tortillas. It is very nutritious. Today, it is called spirulina, and sold in health food shops all over the world.

[pause]

{img:women-drying-green-algae-cakes-sun-lake-shore}
And there is a strange little animal in the lake, a kind of salamander, which stays in the water all its life, and never grows up, keeping its feathery gills, and its little smile. The Mexica call it the axolotl, which means water dog, or water monster, after their god Xolotl. They eat it, roasted. Today, the axolotl is one of the most beloved animals in Mexico, and is found almost nowhere else in the world, except in the last canals of the old lake.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, the chinampa farmers sit by their houses, and watch the sun set over the lake, and the mountains turn purple, and the snow on the volcanoes glow pink and gold.

[pause]

The frogs begin to sing. Fireflies drift over the canals. The lamps of the city shine across the water. And the farmers go to sleep, among their willows, to the sound of the water lapping against the edges of their gardens.

[long-pause]

{img:tlatelolco-great-market-thousands-people-stalls-arcades}
The greatest market in the Americas is the market of Tlatelolco, in the northern part of the island of Tenochtitlan.

[pause]

Every day, it is said, twenty or thirty thousand people come to buy and sell there. And every fifth day, on the great market day, sixty thousand or more, from all over the valley, and beyond.

[long-pause]

The market is a great open square, surrounded by arcades, with the great temple of Tlatelolco rising on one side. It is laid out very carefully, in rows and streets, and every kind of goods has its own place, its own street, so that you know exactly where to find what you need.

[pause]

When the Spanish soldiers see it, later in 1519, they are amazed. One of them, Bernal Díaz del Castillo, writes, many years later, that some of the soldiers had been in Constantinople, and in Rome, and all over Italy, and had never seen a market so large, so well ordered, and so full of people.

[pause]

Let us walk through it, slowly, and see what there is.

[long-pause]

In one part of the market, there are the sellers of food.

[pause]

Maize, in great heaps, of many colours: white, and yellow, and red, and blue, and black. Beans, of many kinds. Chili peppers, fresh and dried, red and green and yellow and brown, dozens of kinds. Squash, and pumpkins. Tomatoes, red and green. Avocados. Sweet potatoes. Amaranth seeds, and chia seeds. Honey, from the wild bees. Salt, from the salty lake.

[pause]

{img:market-stall-chili-peppers-maize-colours-baskets-heaps}
There are fruits: guavas, and papayas, and pineapples, and sapotes, and the fruit of the prickly pear cactus. There are turkeys, and ducks, and rabbits, and deer, and little dogs, which are raised to be eaten. There are fish from the lake, and from the sea, carried by runners. There are frogs, and lake shrimp, and algae cakes.

[pause]

And there are cooked foods, ready to eat: tortillas, and tamales, steamed in maize husks, with fillings of beans, or meat, or chili. And bowls of stew, and sauces.

[long-pause]

In another part, there are the sellers of cloth: cotton cloth, white and dyed in many colours, and clothes, cloaks and skirts and blouses and loincloths, and blankets, plain and beautifully embroidered.

[pause]

There are sellers of pottery: cooking pots, and water jars, and plates, and cups, and griddles, and incense burners, and little figures of the gods. There are sellers of baskets, and mats, and rope, and sandals, made of maguey fibre.

[pause]

{img:pottery-sellers-jars-plates-incense-burners-market-shade}
There are sellers of wood, and charcoal, and stone, and lime, and building materials. There are sellers of medicinal herbs, hundreds of kinds, with healers who will tell you which herb to take for any illness. There are barbers, who will cut your hair. There are sellers of paper, made from the bark of the fig tree, and of paints, and dyes, and ink.

[long-pause]

And there are the sellers of luxury goods, for the rich: gold and silver jewellery, and precious stones, jade, and turquoise, and emeralds. Feathers, the brilliant green feathers of the quetzal bird, the most precious thing of all, and the feathers of parrots, and hummingbirds, and herons. Jaguar skins, and deer skins. Cacao beans. Tobacco, to be smoked in little tubes.

[pause]

There is no money, as we know it. People exchange goods. But some goods are used like money: cacao beans, for small amounts. A turkey hen might cost a hundred cacao beans. A tomato, one bean. And for larger amounts, cotton cloaks, called quachtli, and little copper axe-heads, and quills filled with gold dust.

[pause]

Some clever people make false cacao beans, by taking the inside out of the shell, and filling it with earth. So the sellers must be careful.

[long-pause]

The market is carefully watched.

[pause]

There are judges, who sit in a house in the market, every day, to settle disputes, and punish cheats. There are officials, who walk through the market, checking the goods, and the prices, and the measures. Anyone who cheats is punished severely.

[pause]

{img:market-judges-seated-house-officials-calm-orderly}
And the market is not only a place to buy and sell. It is a place to meet friends, and hear the news, and gossip, and see the world. People come from far away, from the coast, and the mountains, and the forests, speaking many languages. The noise of the market, Bernal Díaz says, could be heard more than a league away.

[long-pause]

{img:mexica-baby-cradle-mother-midwife-house-gentle-light}
When a baby is born in Tenochtitlan, the midwife welcomes it with beautiful words.

[pause]

The Mexica have many speeches like this, called huehuetlatolli, the words of the old ones, which are spoken at important moments in life: at births, and weddings, and when children grow up, and when rulers are chosen. They are full of wisdom, and kindness, and poetry. Some of them were written down by a Spanish friar, Bernardino de Sahagún, a few years after the conquest, and we can still read them today.

[long-pause]

To a newborn baby girl, the midwife says: my little one, my precious necklace, my precious feather, you have arrived. You will be in the house, like the heart in the body. You will be the ashes that cover the fire of the hearth.

[pause]

And to a baby boy, she says that he belongs to the sun, and to the battlefield, and that his home is only a nest, and that he will go out into the world.

[pause]

A few days later, the parents ask a priest, who reads the sacred calendar, to choose a lucky day for the naming ceremony. Children are often named after the day of their birth in the calendar: One Reed, or Seven Flower, or Two Rabbit. Or they are given names from nature: Precious Flower, or Smoking Mirror, or Jade Snake.

[long-pause]

Mexica parents are strict, and teach their children carefully.

[pause]

Fathers give their sons long speeches of advice. Be humble, they say. Be respectful to your elders. Do not mock the old, or the sick, or the poor. Do not gossip. Do not be lazy. Work hard. Walk calmly in the street, not too fast, and not too slow. Speak gently. Eat slowly. Be grateful for what you have.

[pause]

{img:father-advising-son-sitting-courtyard-evening-gentle}
And mothers say to their daughters: take care of yourself, my daughter. Be clean. Wash your face, and your hands, and your mouth. Learn to spin and weave well, and to cook well. Do not be proud. Be kind to everyone. And if anyone is kind to you, be grateful.

[long-pause]

All Mexica children go to school, boys and girls. This is very unusual in the world of 1519. In Europe, at that time, most children, especially girls, never went to school at all.

[pause]

There are two kinds of schools. Most children go to the telpochcalli, the house of youth, in their own neighbourhood. There, the boys learn to be soldiers, and good citizens. They learn the history of their people, and the songs and dances of the gods, and how to work, building and digging and carrying. They learn to fight, with the club and the shield and the spear.

[pause]

{img:boys-school-courtyard-training-shields-wooden-clubs-teacher}
The children of nobles, and some clever children of ordinary families, go to the calmecac, the school of the temple, where the priests teach them to read and write the picture-writing of the Mexica, and the calendar, and astronomy, and history, and law, and poetry, and the speeches, and the songs of the gods. They are trained to be priests, and judges, and officials, and leaders. The discipline is very strict. The students must get up in the night to pray, and bathe in cold water, and fast.

[long-pause]

Girls go to their own schools, too, attached to the temples, where they learn the songs and dances of the gods, and how to serve in the temples. Some girls become priestesses.

[pause]

Every evening, in every neighbourhood, there is a house of song, the cuicacalli, where the young men and women go to learn singing and dancing, from teachers, for the festivals of the gods. Music and dance are very important to the Mexica. Every festival has its own songs and dances, and everyone must know them.

[pause]

The music is played on drums: a tall upright drum, called the huehuetl, played with the hands, and a two-toned wooden drum, the teponaztli, played with sticks. And on flutes, of clay and bone and reed, and rattles, and conch shells, and little bells.

[long-pause]

When a young man is about twenty, and a young woman about sixteen, they marry.

[pause]

The marriage is arranged by their families, with the help of old women who act as matchmakers. The wedding takes place in the evening, at the house of the groom. The bride is carried there on the back of a matchmaker, with a procession of her family, carrying torches. The bride and groom sit together on a mat, by the fire, and their cloaks are tied together, in a knot, as a sign that they are now joined.

[pause]

{img:wedding-couple-cloaks-tied-together-hearth-torches-family}
Then there is a feast, and the old people give the young couple long speeches of advice, about how to live together, and be good to each other, and work hard, and raise their children well.

[long-pause]

{img:mexica-clothing-cotton-cloak-tied-shoulder-loincloth-sandals}
What do the people of Tenochtitlan wear?

[pause]

Their clothes are simple, and made of woven cloth, without sewing, or cutting, or buttons. Every piece of clothing is a rectangle of cloth, woven on the backstrap loom, and wrapped, or tied, around the body.

[long-pause]

A man wears a loincloth, called a maxtlatl, a long strip of cloth, wrapped around his waist and between his legs, with the ends hanging down, in front and behind. And over it, a cloak, a tilmatli, a large rectangle of cloth, tied in a knot over one shoulder.

[pause]

A woman wears a skirt, a cueitl, a long piece of cloth wrapped around her waist, and held up by a belt, and a blouse, a huipilli, a simple, loose tunic, like a long shirt, with a hole for the head, often beautifully embroidered, around the neck and the hem. The huipil is still worn, by many women in Mexico and Guatemala, today.

[pause]

{img:women-embroidered-huipil-blouses-skirts-market-chatting}
Ordinary people wear clothes made of the fibre of the maguey plant, a kind of agave, which is rough, and plain, and white, or brown. And sandals, also of maguey fibre, or of leather. Only the nobles, and the rich, may wear cotton, which is softer, and more expensive.

[long-pause]

There are very strict laws about clothing, made by Moctezuma, and the rulers before him.

[pause]

Commoners may not wear cotton cloaks, or cloaks that are long, below the knee, unless they have been wounded in the legs in battle. They may not wear jewellery of gold, or precious stones. They may not wear certain colours, or patterns. Anyone who breaks these rules can be severely punished.

[pause]

The nobles wear beautiful cloaks of fine cotton, dyed in bright colours, red, and blue, and yellow, and green, and decorated with patterns: borders of geometric designs, and pictures of flowers, and shells, and animals, and the symbols of the gods. Some cloaks are decorated with feathers, or with rabbit fur, which is soft and warm.

[long-pause]

The colours come from plants and insects and shells.

[pause]

The most precious red, a deep crimson, comes from a tiny insect, the cochineal, which lives on the prickly pear cactus. The insects are collected, by hand, and dried, and crushed, into a red powder. It takes about seventy thousand insects to make half a kilogram of dye. After the conquest, the Spanish sent cochineal to Europe, where it became one of the most valuable things that came from the Americas, after silver. It was used to dye the red coats of the British soldiers, and the robes of cardinals, and it is still used today, to colour foods and lipsticks.

[pause]

{img:cochineal-insects-on-prickly-pear-cactus-pads-red-dye-bowl}
Blue comes from the indigo plant. Yellow from flowers, and from the wood of certain trees. Purple from a sea snail, on the Pacific coast.

[long-pause]

Jewellery tells everyone who you are.

[pause]

The nobles and the great warriors wear earplugs, large round ornaments, through holes in their earlobes, of jade, or obsidian, or gold, or shell, or crystal. And lip plugs, through a hole below the lower lip, of gold, or crystal, or obsidian, sometimes in the shape of an eagle's head, or a serpent. And necklaces, and bracelets, and armbands, and anklets, of gold and jade and turquoise and shell.

[pause]

{img:warrior-eagle-costume-feathers-shield-proud-calm-portrait}
The warriors, too, wear special costumes, according to how many prisoners they have captured in battle. The greatest of them, the eagle warriors and the jaguar warriors, wear costumes like eagles, with feathered helmets shaped like eagles' heads, or like jaguars, with spotted skins, and helmets like jaguars' heads, so that their faces look out from the open jaws.

[long-pause]

And everyone, rich and poor, loves flowers, and wears them: in their hair, and around their necks, and carried in their hands.

[pause]

Women often paint their faces with a yellow cream, made from a kind of earth, and dye their teeth red with cochineal, and their hair with dark mud, to make it shine. They wear their hair long, and on special occasions, braid it, with ribbons, and wind the braids around their heads, with two little points at the front, like horns.

[long-pause]

{img:moctezuma-ruler-feather-headdress-turquoise-crown-throne-calm}
The ruler of Tenochtitlan, in 1519, is Moctezuma Xocoyotzin, Moctezuma the younger.

[pause]

His title is huey tlatoani, which means great speaker, because the ruler is the one who speaks for his people. He is about fifty years old. He has ruled since 1502, for seventeen years.

[long-pause]

Moctezuma is a slim man, of medium height, with a thin, dark beard, and long hair. He is serious, and dignified, and religious, and very proud. Before he became ruler, he was a priest, and a great warrior. When the nobles came to tell him that he had been chosen as ruler, it is said, they found him sweeping the floor of a temple.

[pause]

He is very clean, Bernal Díaz says, and bathes every afternoon. He changes his clothes four times a day, and never wears the same clothes twice. He wears a cloak of fine white cotton, and sandals with golden soles, and a crown of turquoise.

[pause]

When he goes out, he is carried in a litter, covered with feathers and gold, and nobles walk before him, sweeping the ground, and laying cloths for him to walk on. Nobody may look him in the face. Everyone must bow, with their eyes cast down, when he passes.

[long-pause]

Moctezuma lives in a magnificent palace, near the Great Temple.

[pause]

It has hundreds of rooms, and halls, and courtyards, with walls of stone, carved and painted, and floors of polished plaster, and ceilings of fragrant cedar wood. There are gardens, full of flowers, and medicinal plants, and fountains, and pools. There are rooms for his wives, and his children, and his servants, and his guests, and his councillors.

[pause]

{img:palace-hall-cedar-ceiling-painted-walls-feather-hangings}
Every day, three hundred cooks prepare his meals. More than three hundred dishes are brought to him, on little warming dishes, with fires underneath, so that he can choose what he wants. He eats alone, behind a gilded wooden screen, so that nobody can see him eat, while musicians play, and jesters and dwarfs and acrobats entertain him.

[pause]

And after the meal, he drinks chocolate, foaming, in golden cups, and smokes tobacco, in a painted tube.

[long-pause]

And Moctezuma has a zoo.

[pause]

It is the first great zoo in the Americas, perhaps in the world. There are hundreds of birds, from every part of the empire: eagles, and hawks, and parrots, and quetzals, and hummingbirds, and herons, and flamingos, and ducks, in cages, and in great pools, of fresh water and salt water. Three hundred people work just to look after the birds. When the birds moult, their feathers are collected, and used by the featherworkers.

[pause]

{img:aviary-courtyard-colourful-birds-parrots-quetzal-pools}
There are animals too: jaguars, and pumas, and wolves, and foxes, and snakes, in a special house. Bernal Díaz says that the noise of the animals, the roaring and the howling and the hissing, was frightening to hear.

[long-pause]

Moctezuma has gardens, too, outside the city, in the hills, at places like Chapultepec, and Oaxtepec, with beautiful flowers, and trees, and medicinal plants, brought from every part of the empire, and pools, and pavilions, where he goes to rest, and enjoy the beauty of nature.

[pause]

He has a library of painted books, and scribes, and astronomers, and wise men, who advise him.

[pause]

And he is a powerful ruler. He has made the empire stronger, and larger. He has made the nobles more important, and separated them more from the common people. He has fought many wars, and conquered many cities. But he has also made enemies.

[long-pause]

To the east of the valley, beyond the mountains, there is a small, independent republic, called Tlaxcala, which the Mexica have never been able to conquer.

[pause]

For many years, the Mexica and the Tlaxcalans have fought each other, in a strange kind of war, called the flower war, arranged by both sides, not to conquer land, but to capture warriors. The Tlaxcalans hate the Mexica, more than anything.

[pause]

{img:mountain-ridge-tlaxcala-town-walls-distant-valley-haze}
And many of the peoples who pay tribute to the Mexica resent them, and would be glad to see them overthrown.

[pause]

In the spring of 1519, Moctezuma does not know that this resentment will soon be used by strangers from across the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:ball-court-i-shaped-stone-walls-rings-players-crowd}
In the sacred precinct of Tenochtitlan, there is a ball court. And all over Mexico and Central America, in almost every city, there are ball courts too, for one of the oldest games in the world.

[pause]

The Mexica call it ullamaliztli. It has been played for more than three thousand years, by the Olmecs, and the Maya, and the Toltecs, and many other peoples, long before the Mexica.

[long-pause]

The court is shaped like a capital letter I: a long, narrow alley, between two sloping or straight walls of stone, with wider areas at each end. High up, in the middle of each side wall, there is a stone ring, set vertically, like a hoop turned on its side.

[pause]

The ball is made of solid rubber, heavy and bouncy, about the size of a large grapefruit, or a little bigger. The Mexica get the rubber from the rubber trees of the hot lands, on the Gulf coast, as tribute. In fact, the Spanish had never seen rubber before they came to Mexico. They were amazed by the way the ball bounced, as if it were alive.

[pause]

{img:solid-rubber-ball-close-player-hip-padding-leather}
The players may not touch the ball with their hands, or their feet. They must hit it with their hips, and their knees, and their elbows, and their buttocks, and keep it in the air, and send it to the other side. They wear thick pads of leather on their hips, and their knees, to protect themselves, because the ball is heavy, and hard.

[long-pause]

The rules are not entirely known to us. But it seems that the teams score points when the other team lets the ball touch the ground in the wrong place, or fails to send it back. And if a player manages to send the ball through one of the stone rings, high on the wall, which is very difficult, and very rare, his team wins the game at once. And by custom, the lucky player may claim the cloaks and jewellery of all the spectators. So, when this happens, the spectators run away, laughing, as fast as they can.

[pause]

{img:stone-ring-high-on-wall-ball-court-sunlight-shadow}
The game is very fast, and very exciting, and very difficult. The best players are famous, and admired. The nobles love to watch, and bet on the games: gold, and jewels, and cloaks, and feathers, and even houses and land.

[long-pause]

The ball game also has a sacred meaning.

[pause]

The Mexica believe that the ball court is like the sky, and the ball is like the sun, or the moon, moving across it. The game is a picture of the struggle between light and darkness, day and night. On certain festivals, the game is played as a ceremony, in honour of the gods.

[pause]

{img:spanish-court-1528-indigenous-players-demonstrating-game}
In 1528, Cortés took some Mexica ball players with him to Spain, to show the game to the Emperor Charles the Fifth. A German artist, Christoph Weiditz, who was at the Spanish court, drew pictures of them playing, with their leather hip pads, which still exist today.

[long-pause]

And the game is still played today, in a few villages in the northwest of Mexico, in the state of Sinaloa, where it is called ulama. The players still hit the heavy rubber ball with their hips, on a long, narrow court of earth, just as their ancestors did, thousands of years ago.

[pause]

It is one of the oldest team sports in the world. And every time a child kicks a rubber ball, anywhere in the world, they are using something that the people of ancient Mexico gave us.

[long-pause]

{img:temple-priests-incense-burners-copal-smoke-shrine-dawn}
The Mexica live in a world full of gods.

[pause]

There are gods of the sun, and the moon, and the stars, and the rain, and the wind, and the maize, and the water, and the earth, and fire, and death, and flowers, and music, and merchants, and many more. Hundreds of them. Every god has his or her own temple, and priests, and festivals, and colours, and symbols.

[long-pause]

The greatest gods of Tenochtitlan are the two who share the top of the Great Temple: Huitzilopochtli, the hummingbird of the left, the god of the sun and of war, the special god of the Mexica, who led them from Aztlan. And Tlaloc, the god of rain, with his round goggle eyes, and his long fangs, who lives in the mountains, and sends the rain that makes the maize grow.

[pause]

There is Quetzalcoatl, the feathered serpent, the god of wind, and of learning, and of priests, and of the morning star. He is a gentle, wise god, who, the stories say, once ruled as a king in the ancient city of Tollan, and taught people how to grow maize, and to make things, and to write, and to measure time. And then he went away, across the eastern sea, and promised one day to return.

[pause]

There is Tezcatlipoca, the smoking mirror, the god of the night sky, and of fate, and of kings, powerful and mysterious, who sees everything in his magic mirror of black obsidian. There is Chalchiuhtlicue, she of the jade skirt, the goddess of lakes and rivers and springs. There is Xochiquetzal, the precious flower, the goddess of flowers, and beauty, and love, and weaving. And there is Centeotl, the young god of the maize.

[long-pause]

The Mexica believe that the world has been created and destroyed four times before. Each world, each age, was called a sun. And each sun ended in disaster: by jaguars, by wind, by fire, and by flood.

[pause]

Now, they live in the fifth sun. And the fifth sun, too, will one day come to an end, in earthquakes.

[pause]

{img:great-round-stone-calendar-carving-sun-face-plaza}
They believe that the sun, and the gods, need to be fed and strengthened, so that the sun will keep moving across the sky, and the world will not end. The gods gave their own lives, at the beginning of the fifth sun, to set the sun in motion. And so the people must give back to the gods, in return.

[long-pause]

They give offerings every day: flowers, and food, and incense, and rubber, and paper, and precious things, and songs, and dances. And the priests offer drops of their own blood, from little cuts on their ears and their legs, made with the thorns of the maguey plant.

[pause]

And, on the great festivals, the Mexica also offer human lives to the gods. Most of those offered are prisoners of war, captured in battle.

[pause]

{img:temple-steps-distant-evening-smoke-quiet-plaza-empty}
This is the hardest part of the story of the Mexica, and the part that horrified the Spanish when they came. We will not describe it. It is enough to know that it happened, that the Mexica believed, deeply and sincerely, that it was necessary, to keep the world alive, and that it was part of their religion, and their world, which is very far from ours. Many other ancient peoples, in many parts of the world, did similar things.

[long-pause]

The priests are very important in Tenochtitlan.

[pause]

There are thousands of them, men and women, serving in the temples. They wear black robes, and paint their bodies black, and let their hair grow long, and never cut it. They live strict lives, of fasting, and prayer, and study. They tend the sacred fires, which must never go out, and sweep the temples, and make the offerings, and sing and dance at the festivals.

[pause]

The priests also keep the sacred books, and the calendar, and study the stars, and know the history of the Mexica, and the stories of the gods, and the songs. They are the scholars and the teachers of Tenochtitlan.

[long-pause]

And the Mexica believe in an afterlife.

[pause]

Most people, when they die, go to Mictlan, the land of the dead, in the north, far beneath the earth. The journey is long, four years, through nine levels, past mountains, and rivers, and winds of knives, and at the end, the soul simply fades away, and rests.

[pause]

{img:marigold-flowers-candles-small-altar-offerings-soft-glow}
But warriors who die in battle, and women who die in childbirth, go to the paradise of the sun, and accompany the sun across the sky, every day, singing. After four years, they come back to earth as hummingbirds and butterflies, and drink the nectar of the flowers.

[pause]

And those who die by water, by drowning, or by lightning, go to the paradise of Tlaloc, a green and pleasant land, full of flowers and fruits and gentle rain, where they live happily forever.

[long-pause]

{img:painted-codex-pages-calendar-day-signs-colourful-screenfold}
The Mexica measure time in a very special way.

[pause]

They have two calendars, running side by side, like two wheels, turning together.

[long-pause]

The first is a sacred calendar, of two hundred and sixty days, called the tonalpohualli, the count of days.

[pause]

It is made of twenty day signs, each with its own picture: crocodile, wind, house, lizard, serpent, death, deer, rabbit, water, dog, monkey, grass, reed, jaguar, eagle, vulture, movement, flint knife, rain, and flower. And thirteen numbers, from one to thirteen. The numbers and the signs turn together, so that the days go: one crocodile, two wind, three house, and so on, until thirteen reed. Then the numbers start again, but the signs go on: one jaguar, two eagle, and so on.

[pause]

Each day has its own character, lucky or unlucky, and its own gods. The priests read the sacred calendar, in their painted books, to decide the best days for everything: for naming a child, or for a wedding, or for starting a journey, or for planting, or for going to war.

[long-pause]

The second calendar is a solar calendar, of three hundred and sixty-five days, called the xiuhpohualli, the count of years.

[pause]

It has eighteen months, of twenty days each, which makes three hundred and sixty days. And then there are five extra days at the end of the year, called the nemontemi, the empty days. These five days are thought to be very unlucky. People stay at home, and do as little as possible, and wait for the new year to begin.

[pause]

{img:quiet-city-empty-streets-five-unlucky-days-grey-sky}
Each of the eighteen months has its own great festival, for one or more of the gods, with processions, and dances, and songs, and feasts, and offerings, and the whole city takes part.

[long-pause]

There are festivals for the rain gods, in the dry season, to ask for rain. There are festivals for the young maize, in the spring, when the girls dance with green maize stalks in their hands, and their hair loose. There are festivals of flowers, when the temples and the houses and the streets are covered with flowers, and everyone wears garlands.

[pause]

There are festivals for the dead, in the late summer and autumn, when families remember their ancestors, and make offerings of food and flowers to them. The modern Day of the Dead, in Mexico, which is celebrated every year at the beginning of November, with marigolds, and candles, and sugar skulls, comes partly from these old festivals.

[pause]

{img:festival-procession-flowers-garlands-dancers-street-sun}
There are festivals for the merchants, and the featherworkers, and the salt makers, and the fishermen, each with its own god.

[long-pause]

The two calendars turn together, and every fifty-two years, they come back to the same starting point, at the same time.

[pause]

This great cycle of fifty-two years is called a bundle of years. And at the end of every bundle, the Mexica fear that the world might end.

[pause]

On the last night of the cycle, every fire in the whole Valley of Mexico is put out. The people break their old pots, and clean their houses, and throw away their old things, and wait, in the darkness, in silence, on the roofs of their houses, looking toward a hill, called the Hill of the Star, near the city.

[pause]

There, on the top of the hill, the priests watch the stars, waiting for the Pleiades, the little group of stars, to pass the highest point of the sky, at midnight. If they do, the world will go on. And at that moment, the priests light a new fire, on the top of the hill, with a fire drill.

[long-pause]

And when the people see the new fire, burning on the hilltop, far away in the darkness, they cheer, and weep with joy. Runners light torches from the new fire, and carry them, running, to the Great Temple in Tenochtitlan, and to all the temples, in all the towns of the valley, and from there to every house. And the fires are lit again, and the world goes on, for another fifty-two years.

[pause]

The last New Fire ceremony was held in 1507, in the reign of Moctezuma. The next would have been in 1559.

[pause]

{img:runners-carrying-torches-causeway-night-lake-city}
It never came.

[long-pause]

{img:featherworker-workshop-quetzal-feathers-mosaic-shield-light}
Tenochtitlan is a city of wonderful craftsmen.

[pause]

The most admired of all are the featherworkers, the amanteca, who live in their own neighbourhood of the city, and have their own god, and their own festivals. They make pictures, and shields, and headdresses, and cloaks, and fans, out of feathers, in brilliant colours: the shimmering green of the quetzal, and the turquoise of the cotinga, and the red and yellow of parrots, and the iridescent colours of hummingbirds, which change as the light moves.

[long-pause]

The featherworkers cut the feathers carefully, and glue them, one by one, onto a backing of paper or cloth, in layers, like a mosaic, or tie them, with thread, onto a net, to make cloaks and headdresses that move and shimmer in the wind.

[pause]

Feathers are the most precious things in the Mexica world, more precious than gold. The long green tail feathers of the quetzal bird, which lives in the cloud forests of the south, are worth more than anything else.

[pause]

There is a famous feather headdress, said to have belonged to Moctezuma, made of more than four hundred quetzal feathers, with gold. It was sent to Europe after the conquest, and is now in a museum in Vienna, in Austria. It is one of the very few Mexica featherworks that still exist.

[long-pause]

There are goldsmiths too, who make beautiful jewellery: earrings, and lip plugs, and necklaces, and pendants, in the shapes of animals, and gods, and flowers, using the lost wax method. They make a little model in wax, and cover it in clay, and heat it, so that the wax melts away, leaving a hollow mould, and pour in the molten gold.

[pause]

The German artist Albrecht Dürer saw some of the Mexica gold work, in 1520, in Brussels, when it had been sent to Europe by Cortés. He wrote in his diary that he had never seen anything in all his life that rejoiced his heart so much, and that he marvelled at the subtle genius of men in distant lands.

[pause]

{img:gold-jewellery-animal-pendants-lip-plug-earrings-cloth}
Sadly, almost all the Mexica gold work was melted down by the Spanish, into bars of gold, to send to Spain. Only a few pieces survive, mostly those that were buried, and found later.

[long-pause]

There are stone carvers, who make great statues of the gods, and carvings for the temples, in basalt, and other hard stones, using only tools of harder stone, and sand, and water, and patience. They carve the great round calendar stones, and serpents' heads, and jaguars, and eagles, and figures of gods, some of them huge, weighing many tons.

[pause]

And they carve little things too, from precious stones: jade, and turquoise, and crystal, and obsidian, the black volcanic glass, which can be polished to make mirrors, or chipped into blades sharper than any steel.

[pause]

{img:stone-carver-basalt-jaguar-sculpture-tools-courtyard}
There are mosaic makers, who cover masks and knives and skulls and shields with tiny pieces of turquoise, and shell, and jade, fitted together so closely that the surface looks like a single, shining stone. Some of these turquoise masks are in the British Museum, in London, today.

[long-pause]

And there are the painters of books, the tlacuilo.

[pause]

The Mexica do not have an alphabet, like ours. They write with pictures, and symbols, and signs for sounds, painted in bright colours, on long strips of deerskin, or bark paper, folded like a screen, or an accordion. These books are called codices.

[pause]

They record history, and the calendar, and the stories of the gods, and the tribute lists, and maps, and laws, and the family trees of the rulers. The scribes are highly respected. They are trained at the calmecac, the temple school. The Mexica say that a good scribe is wise, and careful, and has God in his heart, and in his hand.

[long-pause]

Sadly, almost all the Mexica books were destroyed after the conquest, by the Spanish, who thought they were the work of the devil. Only a few survive, from before the conquest, and some more painted by Mexica scribes in the years just after, at the request of the Spanish, explaining their history and customs.

[pause]

One of the most beautiful is the Codex Mendoza, made in about 1541, for the Spanish viceroy, which shows the founding of Tenochtitlan, the eagle on the cactus, and the tribute of the empire, and the life of the Mexica from birth to old age. It is now in the Bodleian Library, in Oxford, in England.

[pause]

{img:old-codex-page-eagle-cactus-founding-scene-colours}
And there are the potters, and the weavers, and the mat makers, and the carpenters, and the makers of musical instruments, and paper, and rope, and sandals. Every craft has its own neighbourhood, and its own gods, and its own festivals, and passes its skills from father to son, and mother to daughter.

[long-pause]

{img:cacao-pods-tree-tropical-forest-yellow-red-ripe}
Among all the gifts that the people of Mexico gave to the world, perhaps the most beloved is chocolate.

[pause]

The cacao tree grows in the hot, wet lowlands of southern Mexico and Central America, far from the cool Valley of Mexico. It is a small tree, which grows in the shade of taller trees, with big, shiny leaves. Its fruits, the cacao pods, grow straight out of the trunk and the branches, large and oval, yellow and orange and red and purple.

[long-pause]

Inside each pod, there are thirty or forty seeds, the cacao beans, wrapped in a sweet white pulp. The beans are taken out, and left to ferment for a few days, and then dried in the sun, and roasted, and ground on a metate, into a thick, dark paste.

[pause]

The people of Mexico and Central America have been making chocolate from cacao for thousands of years. The Olmecs, and the Maya, and then the Mexica.

[pause]

{img:woman-grinding-roasted-cacao-metate-paste-dark}
The Mexica cannot grow cacao in the high valley. So they get it as tribute, and by trade, from the hot lands of the south. It is so valuable that cacao beans are used as money. Moctezuma's storehouses, the Spanish said, contained more than forty thousand loads of cacao beans.

[long-pause]

The Mexica do not eat chocolate as we do. They drink it, cold, or at room temperature, as a foaming drink, called xocolatl.

[pause]

To make it, they mix the cacao paste with water, and flavour it with chili, or vanilla, or flowers, or honey, or maize. Then they pour it from one cup to another, from a height, again and again, to make a thick, rich foam on the top. The foam is the best part.

[pause]

It is a drink for the rich, and for warriors, and for feasts. Moctezuma drinks it, it is said, from golden cups, many times a day. Ordinary people drink it only on special occasions.

[long-pause]

After the conquest, the Spanish learned to drink chocolate too. At first, they did not like its bitter taste. But they added sugar, and cinnamon, and drank it hot, and soon, it became very popular in Spain, and then all over Europe, as a fashionable drink, in the chocolate houses of London and Paris and Amsterdam.

[pause]

The word chocolate comes from the Nahuatl language, the language of the Mexica.

[pause]

{img:european-chocolate-house-seventeenth-century-cups-pot}
And so do many other words that we still use today: tomato, from tomatl. Avocado, from ahuacatl. Chili, from chilli. Chia, and coyote, and ocelot, and axolotl, and guacamole, from ahuacamolli, avocado sauce.

[long-pause]

Many of the foods that the whole world eats today first came from Mexico and the Americas.

[pause]

Maize, which is now grown all over the world. Beans, many kinds. Squash, and pumpkins. Tomatoes, which the Italians would later make into their sauces. Chili peppers, which would travel to India, and Thailand, and China, and Hungary, and change the cooking of half the world. Avocados. Vanilla. Turkeys. Peanuts. Sweet potatoes. And chocolate.

[pause]

{img:market-basket-tomatoes-chilis-avocados-maize-beans-squash}
It is hard to imagine the food of the world without them. Before 1519, nobody in Europe or Asia or Africa had ever tasted any of them.

[long-pause]

{img:poet-king-texcoco-garden-flowers-hillside-baths-evening}
The Mexica, and their neighbours in the Valley of Mexico, love poetry.

[pause]

They call it in xochitl, in cuicatl: flower and song. Poems are sung, to the music of drums and flutes, at the courts of the rulers, and in the houses of the nobles, and at festivals. Poets are honoured, and kings themselves write poems.

[long-pause]

The most famous poet of all is Nezahualcoyotl, which means hungry coyote. He was the king of Texcoco, the city on the eastern shore of the lake, the ally of Tenochtitlan, and he lived from 1402 to 1472, about fifty years before the time of our story.

[pause]

He had a hard youth. When he was a boy, his father, the king of Texcoco, was killed by enemies, and Nezahualcoyotl had to flee, and hide, and live in exile, for years. But he came back, and won back his kingdom, with the help of the Mexica, and became a great and wise king.

[pause]

He made Texcoco a city of learning, and art, and law, with a great library, and schools, and a council of music and poetry, which judged the poets and the musicians. Texcoco was called the Athens of the Americas.

[long-pause]

Nezahualcoyotl was also a great builder. He built a great dyke, across the lake, ten miles long, to separate the salty water of the eastern lake from the fresh water around Tenochtitlan, and to protect the city from floods.

[pause]

And on a hill near Texcoco, called Texcotzinco, he built a wonderful garden, with terraces, and stairways, and pools, and baths, cut into the living rock of the hill, and fed by an aqueduct, with water flowing down from pool to pool. The remains of the baths are still there today.

[pause]

{img:rock-cut-baths-stone-steps-hillside-garden-water-flowing}
And he wrote poems, many of which were remembered, and written down after the conquest, and can still be read today.

[long-pause]

His poems are often sad, and thoughtful, and full of the beauty and the shortness of life.

[pause]

He writes: Is it true that we really live on the earth? Not forever on earth, only a little while here. Though it be jade, it will break. Though it be gold, it will melt. Though it be quetzal feathers, they will tear apart. Not forever on earth, only a little while here.

[pause]

{img:single-flower-hand-sunlight-garden-quiet-thoughtful}
And he writes about flowers, and songs, and friends, and how they are the only things that make life on earth worth living, and the only things that last.

[pause]

He writes: I, Nezahualcoyotl, ask this: is it true that we really live with roots in the earth? Let us at least have flowers. Let us at least have songs.

[long-pause]

Other poets write about friendship, and about the gods, and about war, and about the beauty of the world.

[pause]

One poet writes: my friends, let us be happy. Let us embrace each other. We walk upon the flowered earth. Nobody can put an end to these flowers, to these songs. They last, in the house of the giver of life.

[pause]

These poems are sung at gatherings of friends, in the evening, in the gardens and the courtyards, with the scent of flowers, and the sound of drums and flutes, and the cups of chocolate passing from hand to hand.

[long-pause]

The Mexica also have a great love of flowers.

[pause]

Flowers are everywhere: in the gardens of the palaces, and the roofs of the houses, and the chinampas, and the markets, and the temples. People carry bunches of flowers in their hands, and smell them, as they walk. They make garlands and necklaces of flowers, and give them as gifts. A noble, it is said, always carries a bunch of flowers, and a pipe of tobacco, when he goes out.

[pause]

{img:nobles-walking-carrying-flower-bouquets-street-smiling}
For the Mexica, flowers are the symbol of beauty, and of joy, and of the shortness of life: beautiful for a day, and then gone. Just like the cherry blossoms in Japan, on the other side of the world, at the same time.

[long-pause]

{img:healer-herbs-bundles-woman-patient-mat-courtyard-gentle}
When someone falls ill in Tenochtitlan, they go to a healer, a ticitl.

[pause]

The healers of the Mexica, men and women, know a great deal about the plants of Mexico, and their powers. In Moctezuma's gardens, at Oaxtepec, and elsewhere, medicinal plants are grown, from all over the empire, and the healers study them, and try them, and learn which ones help which illnesses.

[long-pause]

In the market of Tlatelolco, there is a whole street of sellers of medicinal herbs, roots, barks, leaves, flowers, and seeds, hundreds of kinds, in baskets and bundles, with healers who will tell you which one to take.

[pause]

There are herbs for fevers, and for coughs, and for headaches, and for stomach aches, and for wounds, and for burns, and for sleeplessness, and for sadness. Some of them really work, and are still used in Mexico today, in traditional medicine. Some are used in modern medicines too.

[pause]

{img:herb-market-stall-dried-plants-roots-baskets-healer}
The healers also use the steam bath, the temazcalli, to cure illnesses, and to help women before and after giving birth. They set broken bones, with splints. They clean and stitch wounds, with human hair, and cover them with healing herbs, and with the sap of plants. The Spanish soldiers, when they were wounded, often went to the Mexica healers, because they were better than their own doctors.

[long-pause]

The Mexica believe that illness can have many causes: natural causes, like cold, or bad food, or injury. But also supernatural ones: the anger of a god, or a broken rule, or a bad omen, or the loss of part of the soul. So the healer may also pray, and make offerings, and read the sacred calendar, to find out the cause of the illness, and the cure.

[pause]

{img:healer-praying-offering-incense-small-altar-patient-family}
In 1552, thirty years after the conquest, two Mexica scholars at the college of Santa Cruz, in Tlatelolco, made a little book about the medicinal plants of Mexico. One of them, Martín de la Cruz, an old healer, wrote it in Nahuatl. The other, Juan Badiano, translated it into Latin. It has beautiful coloured pictures of the plants. It is the oldest book of medicine written in the Americas.

[long-pause]

{img:old-herbal-book-painted-plants-pages-museum-case}
The little book was sent to Spain, as a gift for the king. Then it disappeared, for centuries, and was found again in the Vatican Library, in Rome, in 1929. And in 1990, the Pope gave it back to Mexico.

[pause]

Today, it is kept in the National Museum of Anthropology, in Mexico City: a little book of plants and healing, made by two Mexica scholars, at the end of their world, so that the knowledge of their healers would not be lost.

[long-pause]

{img:comet-night-sky-over-lake-city-temples-silhouette}
In the years before 1519, the old chronicles say, there were strange signs in Tenochtitlan.

[pause]

These stories were written down after the conquest, by Mexica scholars and Spanish friars, and some of them may have been told, or changed, afterward, to explain what happened. But they are part of the story that the Mexica themselves told about the end of their world.

[long-pause]

They say that, ten years before the Spanish came, a great fiery light appeared in the night sky, in the east, like a flame, or a great ear of fire, which shone every night, for a year, from midnight to dawn. The people were afraid, and wept.

[pause]

They say that the temple of Huitzilopochtli caught fire, by itself, and burned, and that the more water the people threw on it, the more it burned.

[pause]

{img:lake-waves-rising-calm-day-strange-wind-city-shore}
They say that the temple of Xiuhtecuhtli, the god of fire, was struck by lightning, without any thunder, on a day of light rain. That a comet fell, in daylight, in three parts, from west to east, with long tails of sparks. That the lake boiled up, and rose in great waves, on a day without wind, and flooded the houses.

[long-pause]

And they say that, at night, a woman was heard weeping, in the streets, crying out: oh my children, we must go far away. Oh my children, where shall I take you?

[pause]

And that some fishermen caught a strange bird, like a crane, grey, with a mirror on its head. And they took it to Moctezuma. And when he looked into the mirror, he saw the night sky, and the stars. And when he looked again, he saw a great crowd of men, coming toward him, riding on animals like deer, armed for war. And when he called his wise men to look, the bird vanished.

[pause]

These are the omens that the Mexica remembered.

[long-pause]

Moctezuma, the stories say, was deeply troubled. He called his wise men, and his priests, and asked them what the signs meant. And when they could not tell him, he was angry.

[pause]

There is a story, too, that Moctezuma remembered the old legend of Quetzalcoatl, the feathered serpent god, who had gone away across the eastern sea, long ago, and promised to return, in a year called One Reed. And 1519 was a year One Reed.

[pause]

{img:eastern-sea-coast-dawn-horizon-waves-calm-waiting}
Some historians today doubt this story. They think that it was invented after the conquest, by the Spanish, or by the Mexica themselves, to explain why Moctezuma did not fight the Spanish at once. We do not really know what Moctezuma believed.

[long-pause]

But we do know that, in the years before 1519, news began to come to Tenochtitlan, from the coast, of strange things.

[pause]

The Spanish had arrived in the Caribbean in 1492, with Christopher Columbus. They had settled on the islands, on Hispaniola and Cuba. And from there, they had begun to explore the coasts of the mainland. In 1517 and 1518, two Spanish expeditions sailed along the coast of the Gulf of Mexico, and the Yucatán, and met the Maya, and other peoples.

[pause]

{img:large-sailing-ships-white-sails-gulf-coast-distant-watchers}
And messengers came to Moctezuma, with paintings, made by his scribes on the coast, showing great houses floating on the sea, with white sails, like clouds, and strange men, with white faces, and long beards, and clothes of metal, and strange weapons.

[pause]

Moctezuma ordered his people on the coast to watch, and to report everything.

[long-pause]

{img:spanish-ships-anchored-gulf-coast-sand-dunes-1519}
In April 1519, eleven Spanish ships anchor off the coast of the Gulf of Mexico, near the place that is now the city of Veracruz.

[pause]

On board, there are about five hundred Spanish soldiers, and a hundred sailors, and sixteen horses, and some cannons, and a few dogs. Their leader is a man named Hernán Cortés.

[long-pause]

Cortés is thirty-four years old, from a small town in Extremadura, in the dry west of Spain. He studied law for a while, at the University of Salamanca, and then went to the Caribbean, as a young man, looking for his fortune. He has been living in Cuba, as a landowner, and an official.

[pause]

He is clever, and charming, and persuasive, and very ambitious, and very bold. The governor of Cuba sent him to explore the coast, and to trade. But Cortés has decided to do much more: to find the great kingdom that he has heard about, in the mountains, and to conquer it.

[pause]

On the coast, Moctezuma's officials come to meet the strangers, with gifts: beautiful things of gold and feathers and cloth. They ask the strangers to go away. But the gifts only make the Spanish more eager to go on.

[long-pause]

Cortés has a remarkable helper.

[pause]

On the way along the coast, in the land of the Maya, in the Tabasco region, the local people gave the Spanish twenty young women, as a gift. One of them was a young Nahua woman, named Malintzin, who had been born in a noble family, in the lands of the Mexica, but had been sold, or given away, as a child, to the Maya, and had grown up among them.

[pause]

{img:young-indigenous-woman-interpreter-standing-between-groups}
So Malintzin speaks both Nahuatl, the language of the Mexica, and Maya. And among the Spanish, there is a man named Gerónimo de Aguilar, who was shipwrecked on the coast of Yucatán, years before, and lived among the Maya, and learned their language. So, at first, Malintzin translates from Nahuatl to Maya, and Aguilar from Maya to Spanish. Soon, Malintzin learns Spanish too.

[long-pause]

The Spanish call her Doña Marina. She becomes Cortés's interpreter, and his adviser, and his companion. She is clever, and brave, and calm, and she understands the ways of the Mexica, and the other peoples of Mexico, and helps Cortés to understand them.

[pause]

In Mexico today, people have very mixed feelings about her. For a long time, she was seen as a traitor, who helped the Spanish conquer her own people. But today, many people see her differently: as a young woman, who had been sold into slavery as a child, and who did what she had to do, to survive, in a terrible time. And as the mother of one of the first children of mixed Spanish and Native blood, the beginning of the Mexican people.

[pause]

{img:interpreter-woman-speaking-calmly-council-chiefs-listening}
The Mexica call Cortés Malinche, after her, because he is always with her.

[long-pause]

Cortés founds a little town on the coast, which he calls Villa Rica de la Vera Cruz, the rich town of the true cross. And then, so that none of his men can turn back, he has his ships run aground, and stripped, and sunk.

[pause]

Then, in August 1519, the Spanish march inland, toward the mountains, and Tenochtitlan.

[pause]

On the way, they meet the Totonac people, of the coast, who are tired of paying tribute to the Mexica, and become their allies. And then, in the mountains, they come to Tlaxcala, the independent republic, the old enemy of the Mexica.

[long-pause]

At first, the Tlaxcalans fight the Spanish. There are several battles. But then they make peace, and become their allies, against the Mexica. Thousands of Tlaxcalan warriors join the Spanish.

[pause]

This is the secret of the conquest. It is not only a few hundred Spanish soldiers who will defeat the Mexica. It is the Spanish, together with tens of thousands of Native warriors, from the peoples who hated Mexica rule: Tlaxcalans, and Totonacs, and others, who see the strangers as useful allies, in their own long struggle against Tenochtitlan.

[pause]

{img:tlaxcalan-warriors-banners-meeting-spanish-camp-hills}
At the beginning of November 1519, the Spanish and their allies climb up into the high pass between the two great volcanoes, Popocatepetl and Iztaccihuatl, through pine forests, and snow, and cold winds.

[pause]

And from the top of the pass, they look down, for the first time, on the Valley of Mexico, with its lakes, and its cities, shining in the sun.

[long-pause]

{img:view-from-mountain-pass-down-valley-lakes-cities-shining}
On the eighth of November, 1519, the Spanish come down from the mountains, and walk along the southern causeway, across the lake, toward Tenochtitlan.

[pause]

Bernal Díaz del Castillo, a young soldier, was there. Many years later, when he was an old man, living in Guatemala, he wrote a book about everything he had seen. And he remembered that morning, more than fifty years later, as if it were yesterday.

[long-pause]

He wrote: when we saw so many cities and villages built in the water, and other great towns on dry land, and that straight and level causeway going toward Mexico, we were amazed, and said that it was like the enchantments they tell of in the book of Amadis, on account of the great towers and temples and buildings rising from the water, and all built of masonry. And some of our soldiers even asked whether the things that we saw were not a dream.

[pause]

He wrote: I do not know how to describe it, seeing things never heard of, nor seen, nor even dreamed of, as we did.

[pause]

The causeway is crowded with people, who have come out to see the strangers. The lake is crowded with canoes. The roofs of the houses, and the tops of the temples, are full of people, watching.

[long-pause]

At the place where the southern causeway meets another causeway, near the entrance to the city, Moctezuma comes out to meet them.

[pause]

He comes in a litter, with a great company of nobles, in their finest clothes, with feathers and gold. Then he gets down from the litter, and walks, under a canopy of green feathers and gold and silver and pearls, held over him by four lords. He wears sandals with golden soles, set with precious stones. The nobles sweep the ground in front of him, and lay down cloaks, so that his feet will not touch the earth.

[pause]

{img:moctezuma-canopy-green-feathers-nobles-causeway-meeting}
Cortés gets down from his horse, and goes forward to embrace him. But the lords stop him. Nobody may touch the great speaker. Instead, Cortés places a necklace of glass beads around Moctezuma's neck. And Moctezuma gives Cortés two necklaces of shells, with golden shrimps hanging from them.

[long-pause]

Then Moctezuma speaks. According to a Mexica account, written down years later, he says something like this:

[pause]

Our lord, you are weary. You have come to your land, to your city. You have come to sit on your mat, on your seat, which I have kept for you. The rulers who went before me have gone. Now, you have come. I have seen you in dreams, and now I see you with my own eyes. Welcome. Rest. Come into your palace.

[pause]

{img:two-leaders-facing-each-other-causeway-interpreter-between}
Did Moctezuma really say this? Was he being polite, in the very formal way of the Mexica, as a host greeting a guest? Or did he really think that Cortés was a god, or a messenger of the gods? Historians still argue about it.

[long-pause]

Moctezuma takes the Spanish into the city, and lodges them in the palace of his father, Axayacatl, a great building, near the Great Temple, with many rooms and courtyards. He gives them food, and gifts, and servants. He treats them as honoured guests.

[pause]

In the following days, Moctezuma shows them the city. He takes them to the market of Tlatelolco, and up to the top of the Great Temple, from where they look out over the city, and the lake, and the causeways, and the towns around the shore. Bernal Díaz says that from there they could see the whole city, and the canoes, and the bridges, and it was a wonderful sight.

[pause]

For a few days, Tenochtitlan is at peace, and the strangers walk through it, amazed, looking at everything.

[long-pause]

But it does not last.

[pause]

After about a week, Cortés, fearing that the Mexica might turn against him, takes Moctezuma prisoner, in his own city, and keeps him under guard, in the palace of Axayacatl. Moctezuma goes on ruling, in name, and receiving his nobles, but he is a prisoner.

[pause]

{img:palace-courtyard-guards-quiet-evening-lamps-tension}
And for the next months, the city waits, uneasily, while the strangers live in the palace, at its heart.

[long-pause]

{img:lake-city-distant-grey-sky-storm-clouds-gathering-1520}
The story of what happened next is a very sad one. We will tell it gently, and from a distance, as if we were watching from the shore of the lake, far away.

[pause]

In the spring of 1520, Cortés has to leave Tenochtitlan for a while, to go back to the coast, to fight against another Spanish army, sent by the governor of Cuba to arrest him. He leaves some of his men in the city, under one of his captains, Pedro de Alvarado.

[long-pause]

While Cortés is away, during a great festival, in the sacred precinct, Alvarado and his men turn against the Mexica nobles, who are dancing, unarmed, in honour of their god. Many are lost.

[pause]

The people of Tenochtitlan rise up, in anger, and surround the Spanish in their palace. When Cortés comes back, with more men, he finds the city in revolt. In the struggle that follows, Moctezuma, the great speaker, passes away, at the end of June, 1520. The Spanish say that he was struck by stones thrown by his own people, when he tried to calm them. The Mexica say that the Spanish took his life. We do not know for certain.

[pause]

Then, on a dark, rainy night, at the end of June, the Spanish try to escape from the city, along the western causeway, carrying as much gold as they can. The Mexica discover them, and attack them, from the causeway, and from canoes, on the lake. Many of the Spanish, and of their allies, are lost, in the water, in the darkness. The Spanish call it the Noche Triste, the sad night.

[long-pause]

The survivors escape, and go back to Tlaxcala, to their allies, to rest and recover.

[pause]

And then, in the autumn of 1520, something else comes to Tenochtitlan, more terrible than any army. Smallpox.

[pause]

{img:quiet-city-houses-closed-doors-smoke-grey-autumn-sorrow}
Smallpox is a disease that had never existed in the Americas before the Europeans came. The people of Mexico had no protection against it at all. It came with a man on one of the Spanish ships, and spread through the country like a fire.

[pause]

In Tenochtitlan, in the autumn of 1520, thousands and thousands of people fall ill, and pass away, including the new ruler, Cuitlahuac, Moctezuma's brother, after only eighty days as ruler. Perhaps a third, or even half, of the people of the Valley of Mexico are lost, in a few months.

[long-pause]

In the spring of 1521, Cortés comes back, with his Spanish soldiers, and tens of thousands of Native allies, from Tlaxcala, and Texcoco, and many other cities.

[pause]

He has thirteen small ships built, in pieces, in Tlaxcala, and carried over the mountains, by thousands of porters, and put together on the shore of the lake. And with the ships, and the armies, he surrounds Tenochtitlan, and cuts off its food and its water.

[pause]

{img:small-sailing-brigantines-lake-distant-city-smoke-haze}
The new ruler of the Mexica is a young man, about twenty-five years old, named Cuauhtemoc, which means descending eagle. He is brave, and determined, and he and his people defend their city, for almost three months, street by street, canal by canal, with great courage, against the Spanish and their allies, and against hunger, and illness.

[long-pause]

But on the thirteenth of August, 1521, it is over. Cuauhtemoc is captured, in a canoe, on the lake, trying to escape. And the great city of Tenochtitlan has fallen.

[pause]

Much of it is in ruins. The temples are broken. The canals are filled with rubble. The people who survive leave the city, along the causeways, thin and tired, and go to live in the towns around the lake.

[pause]

A Mexica poet, a few years later, wrote a sad song about it: Broken spears lie in the roads. We have torn our hair in our grief. The houses are roofless now, and their walls are red. We have pounded our hands in despair, against the adobe walls, for our inheritance, our city, is lost and dead.

[long-pause]

So, in a little more than two years after the Spanish first saw it, from the pass between the volcanoes, the great city on the lake is gone.

[pause]

It is one of the great tragedies of history. A city of two hundred thousand people, one of the most beautiful in the world, with its temples and its gardens and its canals and its markets and its schools and its poets, destroyed, by war, and by illness, and by the meeting of two worlds that had never known each other.

[pause]

{img:single-heron-standing-still-lake-shore-evening-reeds}
But the people of Mexico did not disappear. Millions of them survived. Their language, Nahuatl, is still spoken today, by more than a million and a half people. And their descendants are still there, in the Valley of Mexico, and all over Mexico, today.

[long-pause]

{img:colonial-mexico-city-1550s-churches-plaza-lake-shrinking}
After the fall of Tenochtitlan, Cortés decides to build a new Spanish city, on the same place, on the ruins of the old one.

[pause]

He calls it Mexico, after the Mexica. It becomes the capital of New Spain, the great Spanish colony that will stretch from Central America to California, for three hundred years.

[long-pause]

The Spanish build their city on top of the Mexica city, using its stones. The main square of the new city is built on the old sacred precinct, next to the ruins of the Great Temple. A great cathedral rises there, built partly with stones from the temples. The palace of the Spanish viceroy is built on the site of the palace of Moctezuma.

[pause]

The streets of the Spanish city follow the old Mexica streets and causeways, many of which are still the main streets of Mexico City today.

[pause]

{img:cathedral-construction-stone-workers-plaza-colonial-1560}
The Spanish do not understand the lake, or the chinampas, or the canals. Over the following centuries, they slowly drain the lakes, to stop the floods, which often damage the city, and to make new land. By the twentieth century, the great lakes of the Valley of Mexico are almost all gone. Only a small part remains, in the south, at Xochimilco, with its canals and its chinampas.

[long-pause]

Many Spanish friars come to Mexico, to teach the people Christianity.

[pause]

Some of them are deeply interested in the Mexica, and their language, and their history, and their customs. They learn Nahuatl, and write it down in the Latin alphabet, and teach the young Mexica nobles to read and write in it.

[pause]

The greatest of them is a Franciscan friar, named Bernardino de Sahagún, who comes to Mexico in 1529, and spends sixty years there. With the help of Mexica scholars, and old men who remembered the world before the conquest, he writes a great encyclopedia of the Mexica world, in twelve books, in Nahuatl and Spanish, with more than two thousand pictures, painted by Mexica artists.

[long-pause]

It is called the General History of the Things of New Spain, or the Florentine Codex, because it is now kept in a library in Florence, in Italy.

[pause]

It describes everything: the gods, and the festivals, and the calendar, and the rulers, and the merchants, and the craftsmen, and the plants, and the animals, and medicine, and the speeches of the old ones, and the story of the conquest, told by the Mexica themselves. Without it, we would know very little about the world of Tenochtitlan.

[pause]

{img:codex-pages-pictures-text-columns-library-florence-light}
Much of what we have told tonight comes, in the end, from the work of Sahagún, and his Mexica helpers, who wanted to save the memory of their world.

[long-pause]

The people of Mexico, Native and Spanish, slowly mix together, over the centuries, and become the Mexican people.

[pause]

The old festivals blend with the Christian ones. The Day of the Dead becomes a festival of All Saints and All Souls, with marigolds and candles and offerings, as in the old days. The Virgin of Guadalupe, who, the story says, appeared to a Native man, Juan Diego, in 1531, on the hill of Tepeyac, where there had once been a temple of a Mexica mother goddess, becomes the most beloved symbol of Mexico.

[pause]

{img:marigold-altar-candles-family-day-of-dead-gentle-glow}
And in 1821, when Mexico wins its independence from Spain, the new country chooses, for its flag, the old symbol of Tenochtitlan: the eagle, sitting on a cactus, on a rock, in a lake, with a snake in its beak. It is still on the flag of Mexico today.

[long-pause]

{img:mexico-city-zocalo-cathedral-great-plaza-blue-sky}
Today, Mexico City is one of the largest cities in the world, with more than twenty million people living in and around it, spreading across the whole valley, where the lakes once were.

[pause]

And in the very heart of the city, the old Tenochtitlan is still there, under the ground.

[long-pause]

In the middle of the city, there is the Zócalo, the great main square, one of the largest public squares in the world. On one side stands the great cathedral, the largest in the Americas, built over three hundred years. On another side stands the National Palace, on the site of the palace of Moctezuma.

[pause]

And just behind the cathedral, there are the ruins of the Great Temple.

[pause]

For four hundred years, nobody knew exactly where the Great Temple had been. It was buried under the houses and the streets of the colonial city. Then, in February 1978, some electricity workers, digging a trench in a street behind the cathedral, found a huge round stone, more than three metres across, carved with the figure of a goddess: Coyolxauhqui, the moon goddess, the sister of Huitzilopochtli.

[long-pause]

After that, the archaeologists dug, and found the Great Temple.

[pause]

It had been rebuilt seven times, by the Mexica, over two hundred years, each time larger than before, with the new temple built over the old one, like the layers of an onion. So the archaeologists found not one temple, but seven, one inside the other, with the oldest, the smallest, in the middle, still with its two shrines on top, and its paintings, and its sculptures.

[pause]

{img:carved-round-moon-goddess-stone-museum-soft-light}
Today, you can walk on wooden walkways, through the ruins of the Great Temple, in the middle of the modern city, and see the stairways, and the serpents' heads, and the shrines, and the offerings. And next to it, there is a museum, full of the treasures found there: sculptures, and masks, and jewellery, and offerings, and the great stone of Coyolxauhqui. The archaeologists are still digging, every year, and still finding new things.

[long-pause]

In the great Museum of Anthropology, in Chapultepec Park, where Moctezuma's gardens once were, you can see the Sun Stone, the great round stone, nearly four metres across, carved with the face of the sun god in the middle, and the symbols of the days and the ages of the world around it. It was found in 1790, buried in the Zócalo.

[pause]

And you can see the great statues of the gods, and the models of Tenochtitlan, as it was in 1519, floating on its lake, and the treasures of the Mexica, and of all the other peoples of ancient Mexico.

[pause]

The hill of Chapultepec, where the springs gave water to Tenochtitlan, is now a great park, with woods, and lakes, and museums, and a castle on the top of the hill. On weekends, families come there to walk, and picnic, and row boats on the lake.

[long-pause]

And in the south of the city, at Xochimilco, the last of the old lakes still survives, with its canals, and its chinampas.

[pause]

Here, the farmers still grow vegetables and flowers on their chinampas, as their ancestors did, five hundred years ago, and take them to market by boat. And on weekends, the people of Mexico City come here, to ride along the canals in long, flat boats, called trajineras, painted in bright colours, with names written on arches of flowers over their bows, while musicians play from other boats, and little canoes come alongside, selling food, and flowers, and drinks.

[pause]

{img:xochimilco-colourful-trajinera-boats-canal-flowers}
In the canals of Xochimilco, the last wild axolotls still live, in the dark water. They are very rare now. Scientists and the local people are working together to protect them, and their canals, and the chinampas.

[long-pause]

And at Tlatelolco, in the north of the city, there is a square called the Plaza of the Three Cultures. On it, you can see the ruins of the temple of Tlatelolco, and a Spanish church, built in the sixteenth century, from the stones of the temple, and modern buildings, of the twentieth century. Three cultures, side by side.

[pause]

A stone there says: on the thirteenth of August, 1521, Tlatelolco fell. It was neither a triumph nor a defeat. It was the painful birth of the mestizo people, which is the Mexico of today.

[pause]

And in the markets of Mexico City, the women still pat tortillas, clap, clap, clap, and sell chili peppers, and avocados, and tomatoes, and flowers, and chocolate, as they did in the market of Tlatelolco, five hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

So the story of the city on the lake comes to an end tonight, with the old stones resting under the new streets, and the boats still drifting along the last canals in the evening light.

[pause]

The eagle on the cactus, the floating gardens, the market of a thousand colours, and the poet king who knew that only flowers and songs remain.
