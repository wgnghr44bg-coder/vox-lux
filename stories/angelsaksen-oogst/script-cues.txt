Tonight we travel back more than thirteen hundred years, to the year 700. We go to the south of England, to a small village by a slow river. It is early August. The fields around the village are golden with ripe wheat and barley. And the people of the village are getting ready for the most important weeks of their whole year. The harvest.

[long-pause]

It is early morning. A soft mist lies over the river meadows. The sun is just coming up over the low hills, and its light turns the mist pink and gold. Swallows are flying low over the water. A cow is lowing somewhere. And from the thatched roofs of the little wooden houses, thin lines of smoke are rising straight up into the still air. The fires are being lit for breakfast.

[pause]

The village is small. There are about ten houses, made of wood, with steep roofs of straw. They stand around a little open space, with a well in the middle. Around the houses, there are gardens with vegetables and herbs, and small fenced yards for animals. Beyond the gardens, the great open fields stretch away on all sides, full of ripe grain, swaying gently in the morning breeze.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:anglo-saxon-family-doorway-morning-light-looking-at-ripe-fields}
The people who live here are Anglo-Saxons. Their ancestors came across the sea to Britain, about two hundred and fifty years ago, from the lands that are now Germany, Denmark and the Netherlands. They speak a language called Old English. It sounds strange to us today, but many of our everyday words come from it. Words like bread, and field, and harvest, and home.

[pause]

The Anglo-Saxons are farmers, above all. Almost everyone lives in small villages like this one, and works the land. Their lives follow the rhythm of the seasons. Ploughing in the spring, weeding in the early summer, harvest in the late summer, and the long, dark winter, when everyone waits for the light to come back. And of all these seasons, the harvest is the one that matters most.

[pause]

{img:father-mother-children-anglo-saxon-walking-to-field-sickles-baskets}
Tonight, we will spend the harvest season with one family in this village. There is a father and a mother, a girl of twelve, a little boy of eight and an old grandmother. We will see their house, their animals and their fields. We will watch them cut the grain with sickles, and bind it into sheaves, and carry it home. We will see them bake the first bread of the new harvest, and take it to the church to be blessed.

[long-pause]

We will also visit the great hall of the local lord, where a poet sings old stories by the fire. We will visit a little church, and a monastery, where monks are writing books by hand. We will see the bees and the geese, the loom and the mill. And at the end of the harvest, we will join the whole village at a great feast, to celebrate.

[pause]

{img:sheaves-of-wheat-standing-in-stubble-field-late-summer-golden-light}
Life in an Anglo-Saxon village was not easy. People worked very hard, and they depended on the weather and the soil. A bad harvest meant a hungry winter. But it was also a life full of small joys. Fresh bread, warm fires, old songs, good neighbours and the beauty of the countryside. So let us begin, on a misty August morning, long ago, in a little village by the river, where the golden fields are waiting.

[pause]

Down by the river, a heron stands perfectly still in the shallow water, waiting for a fish. A woman comes down the path with two wooden buckets, to fetch water. She stops for a moment to look at the fields, shading her eyes against the rising sun. Then she smiles, fills her buckets, and walks back up to the village. It is going to be a fine, dry day. Perfect weather for the harvest.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-britain-ruins-overgrown-villa-columns-grass-sheep-grazing}
Before the Anglo-Saxons came, Britain was part of the Roman Empire. For almost four hundred years, the Romans ruled the island. They built towns with stone houses, straight roads, bath houses and grand country villas. But around the year 410, the Roman soldiers left Britain, to defend other parts of their crumbling empire. And they never came back.

[long-pause]

After the Romans left, Britain changed. The towns slowly emptied. The villas fell into ruin. The people who lived there, the Britons, had to look after themselves. And across the North Sea, new people began to arrive in their boats. They were the Angles, the Saxons and the Jutes, from the coasts of what are now Germany, Denmark and the Netherlands. Some came as soldiers, hired to help defend the land. Others came as farmers, looking for good soil.

[pause]

Over the next two hundred years, more and more of them came. They settled in the east and south of Britain, and slowly spread west. Sometimes there were wars between them and the Britons. Sometimes they lived side by side, and married each other. Over time, the people of the east and south began to speak Old English, and to call themselves English. The land was later named after the Angles. England means the land of the Angles.

[pause]

{img:anglo-saxon-boats-arriving-shore-sea-cliffs-families-early-settlers}
By the year 700, England is not one country. It is divided into several kingdoms, each with its own king. In the north, there is Northumbria. In the middle, there is Mercia. In the east, there is East Anglia. In the southeast, there is Kent. And in the south and west, there is Wessex, the kingdom of the West Saxons. Our village lies in Wessex.

[pause]

The king of Wessex is called Ine. He is a strong and sensible king. He has written down a set of laws for his people. The laws tell people how to settle quarrels, what to pay if they damage someone's crops, and how to keep their fences in good repair. One law says that a farmer who does not fence his field properly cannot complain if his neighbour's cattle eat his grain. They are practical laws, for a land of farmers.

[long-pause]

{img:king-ine-wessex-wooden-hall-advisers-reading-laws-candlelight}
When the Anglo-Saxons first came, they believed in the old gods of the north. Woden, the wise one-eyed god. Thunor, the god of thunder. Frige, the goddess of love and the home. Some of these names still live on in the days of our week. Wednesday is Woden's day. Thursday is Thunor's day. And Friday is Frige's day.

[pause]

But about a hundred years before our story, missionaries came from Rome and from Ireland, to teach the Christian faith. Slowly, the kings and their people became Christians. By the year 700, most people in England are Christians. Churches and monasteries are being built all over the land. But many of the old customs and beliefs still live on, mixed with the new faith, especially in the countryside.

[pause]

{img:small-stone-church-anglo-saxon-village-yew-tree-evening-light}
The Anglo-Saxons were not only farmers. They were also wonderful craftsmen. They made beautiful jewellery of gold and garnets, finely decorated swords and helmets, and delicate glass cups. They were poets and storytellers, who loved long songs about heroes and monsters. And in their monasteries, monks were making some of the most beautiful books the world has ever seen.

[long-pause]

But tonight, we are not going to the courts of kings. We are going to a small village, where ordinary people live ordinary lives. They are the people who grew the food that fed the whole kingdom. And in early August, they are getting ready to bring in the harvest.

[long-pause]

{img:anglo-saxon-farmer-wulfric-beard-tunic-strong-hands-standing-field-edge}
In one of the houses of the village lives a family of farmers. The father is called Wulfric. He is about thirty-five years old, tall and broad, with a fair beard and a calm face. He is a free farmer, what the Anglo-Saxons called a ceorl. He owns his own oxen, and works his own strips of land in the village fields. He also owes some work and some food to the local lord, every year.

[long-pause]

The mother is called Eadgyth. She is a strong, practical woman, with long braids under a linen headscarf. She runs the house. She cooks, bakes and brews. She spins wool and weaves cloth. She looks after the vegetable garden, the chickens and the geese. And at harvest time, she works in the fields beside her husband, from dawn till dusk. She knows how to do almost everything.

[pause]

The oldest child is a girl called Hild. She is twelve years old. She has her mother's braids and her father's calm face. She already helps with everything in the house and the fields. She can spin and weave, milk the cows, bake bread and cut grain with a sickle. She is quick and clever, and very good with animals. The geese follow her around wherever she goes.

[pause]

{img:girl-twelve-anglo-saxon-braids-geese-following-her-farmyard-morning}
The youngest child is a boy called Cuthwine. He is eight years old, with a round face and messy hair that never stays tidy. He is full of energy, and full of questions. Why is the sky blue? Where do the swallows go in winter? Why does the river never stop? His job is to look after the pigs, and to scare the birds away from the grain. He is not always very good at either.

[pause]

And there is the grandmother, Wulfric's mother. Everyone calls her Modor, which means mother. She is very old, perhaps sixty, which is a great age in those days. She cannot work in the fields any more. But she spins wool all day, sitting by the door. She looks after the little children of the village. And she knows all the old stories, the old songs and the old herbs for every illness.

[long-pause]

{img:old-grandmother-spinning-wool-drop-spindle-doorway-sunlight}
The family is not rich, but they are not poor either. They have a pair of oxen for ploughing, two cows, a few pigs, some sheep and a flock of geese and chickens. They have their house, their garden and their strips of land. In a good year, they have enough to eat, and a little left over to sell. In a bad year, they must be careful, and hope that winter is short.

[pause]

The family works together, all year round. Everyone has their tasks, even little Cuthwine. But they also have time for each other. In the evenings, they sit around the fire and talk. Wulfric mends tools. Eadgyth sews. Modor tells stories. Hild plays a game with little stones on the floor with her brother. And the fire crackles, and the smoke drifts up into the dark roof.

[pause]

{img:family-around-central-hearth-evening-firelight-anglo-saxon-house}
Their neighbours are like a big family too. In a village of ten houses, everyone knows everyone. They help each other at harvest time, and when someone is sick, and when a new house must be built. They share the big plough, and the oxen, and the work of the common fields. They quarrel sometimes, as neighbours do. But when there is trouble, they stand together.

[long-pause]

On this August evening, the talk around the fire is all about the harvest. Wulfric has walked through the fields that afternoon. He has rubbed some ears of wheat between his hands, and bitten the grains. They are hard and dry. The wheat is ready. Tomorrow, he says, we will cut the first of it. And Eadgyth will bake the first loaf of the new harvest.

[pause]

The family also has a dog, a big, shaggy, grey dog called Gifre, which means greedy. He got his name as a puppy, when he stole a whole loaf of bread from the table. He helps Wulfric with the sheep and the cows, and guards the house at night. In the evenings, he lies by the fire with his head on Cuthwine's feet, and sighs deeply, as if he too has had a very long day.

[long-pause]

{img:anglo-saxon-timber-house-thatched-roof-wattle-daub-walls-garden-fence}
The house of Wulfric's family is a long, low building of wood. It is about ten metres long and five metres wide. Its walls are made of wooden posts, set in the ground, with the spaces filled with woven branches covered in clay. The clay is mixed with straw and dung, and when it dries, it is hard and keeps out the wind. The roof is very steep, made of thick bundles of straw. It reaches down almost to the ground.

[long-pause]

There is one door, in the middle of one of the long walls. There are no glass windows. Glass is far too expensive for farmers. Instead, there are a few small openings in the walls, which can be closed with wooden shutters. So inside, even in the daytime, it is quite dark. The light comes mostly from the open door, and from the fire.

[pause]

The house has one big room. In the middle of the floor, there is the hearth, a low square of flat stones, where the fire burns day and night. There is no chimney. The smoke rises up into the roof, and finds its way out through the straw. This keeps the roof dry and free of insects. But it also means that the inside of the house always smells a little of smoke, and the roof beams are black.

[pause]

{img:inside-anglo-saxon-house-central-hearth-smoke-rising-beams-benches}
Along the walls, there are wide wooden benches, covered with straw and sheepskins. During the day, the family sits on them. At night, they sleep on them, wrapped in woollen blankets. There are wooden chests, where the family keeps its clothes and its few precious things. There is a loom leaning against the wall. And hanging from the beams, there are herbs, onions, and bags of seeds, drying in the smoke.

[pause]

Near the fire, there are the cooking things. A big iron cauldron, hanging from a chain over the fire. Clay pots for storing food. Wooden bowls and spoons. A quern, which is two round flat stones, one on top of the other, for grinding grain into flour by hand. And a wooden bucket for water, which Hild fills at the well every morning.

[long-pause]

{img:sunken-floored-workshop-hut-anglo-saxon-loom-weights-small-building}
Next to the main house, there are two smaller buildings. They are strange little huts, with their floors dug down into the ground, about half a metre deep. Archaeologists call them sunken huts. One of them is Eadgyth's workshop, where she weaves cloth on a big upright loom. The other is a storehouse, for tools, firewood and food. In winter, the sunken floors keep the huts warmer.

[pause]

Around the buildings, there is a yard, with a fence of woven branches. Here, the chickens scratch in the dust and the geese wander about. There is a pen for the pigs, and a shelter for the oxen and the cows. And behind the house, there is the vegetable garden. Eadgyth grows cabbages, onions, leeks, beans, peas and garlic. There are also herbs. Parsley, mint, sage and dill. And in one corner, under an apple tree, there are three straw beehives.

[pause]

{img:vegetable-garden-anglo-saxon-cabbages-beans-straw-beehives-apple-tree}
The house is simple, but it is warm and cosy. The thick roof keeps out the rain. The fire keeps away the cold. In summer, the door stands open all day, and the light and the warm air come in. Birds fly in and out. The cat sleeps in the sun on the doorstep. And the smell of bread and woodsmoke drifts out into the yard.

[long-pause]

Wulfric built this house himself, with the help of his neighbours, when he married Eadgyth. Before that, he lived in his father's house, at the other end of the village. Building a house takes about a month, if everyone helps. The wood comes from the forest. The straw comes from the fields. The clay comes from the riverbank. Everything that is needed is found close by. And when a house becomes too old, after twenty or thirty years, the family simply builds a new one next to it.

[pause]

At night, the house is very dark. The fire is covered with ashes, so that it glows softly until morning, without going out. The family sleeps close together on the benches, warm under their blankets. The cat curls up on Modor's feet. The dog lies by the door. And sometimes, in the roof above them, a mouse rustles in the straw, or a bird settles down to sleep.

[long-pause]

{img:open-field-strips-anglo-saxon-village-wheat-barley-oats-from-hill}
Around the village, there are the fields. They are not small, fenced fields, like the ones in the countryside today. They are big open fields, without hedges or walls. Each big field is divided into long, narrow strips. And each family in the village has some strips in each field. Wulfric's family has about twenty strips, spread out over the fields. Historians think that this way of farming was only just beginning around the year 700, and became much more common in later centuries. But in some villages, like ours, it had already begun.

[long-pause]

Why are the strips spread out? Because the soil is not the same everywhere. Some parts of the fields are good and rich. Others are stony, or wet, or poor. By giving each family strips in different places, everyone gets a fair share of the good land and the bad land. It is a very old way of farming, and a very fair one.

[pause]

The strips are long and narrow because of the plough. The plough is heavy, made of wood with an iron blade, and it is pulled by oxen. Turning a team of oxen around at the end of the field is slow and difficult. So it is easier to plough long, straight lines, and turn as few times as possible. The length of one furrow, about two hundred metres, became an old English measure, the furlong. It means furrow long.

[pause]

{img:oxen-team-pulling-heavy-plough-farmer-guiding-spring-field-birds}
Ploughing is done together. One family rarely has enough oxen to pull the heavy plough. A full team needs eight oxen. So the families share their oxen, and plough all the strips together, one after another. Wulfric has two oxen. His neighbours have the others. In the spring, the whole village works together, ploughing the fields, from one end to the other.

[pause]

The village has two great fields, and some smaller ones. Every year, one field is planted with wheat or barley, and the other is left empty, to rest. This is called leaving the field fallow. During its year of rest, the cows and sheep graze on it, eating the weeds and leaving their dung, which makes the soil rich again. The next year, the fields swap. So the soil never gets too tired.

[long-pause]

{img:meadow-by-river-cows-grazing-haystacks-early-summer-anglo-saxon}
Besides the fields, there are other kinds of land around the village. Down by the river, there are the meadows. They are too wet for grain, but they grow rich grass. In June, the villagers cut the grass and dry it in the sun, to make hay. The hay is stored for the winter, to feed the animals. Then the cows graze on the meadows for the rest of the summer.

[pause]

On the higher ground, there is the common pasture, where everyone's sheep and cattle can graze. And beyond that, there is the forest. The forest is very important. It gives wood for building, fuel for the fires, nuts and berries, and honey from wild bees. In autumn, the pigs are taken into the forest to eat acorns and beechnuts, which makes them fat for the winter.

[pause]

{img:pigs-in-oak-forest-autumn-acorns-boy-with-stick-watching}
The crops in the fields are wheat, barley, oats and rye. Wheat is the most precious, because it makes the best white bread. But it needs good soil. Barley grows almost everywhere, and is used for bread, porridge and above all for ale. Oats are used for porridge and for feeding horses. Rye is a dark grain, which grows well on poor soil, and makes a heavy, dark bread. There are also beans and peas, grown in strips of their own.

[long-pause]

This year, the crops look good. The spring was mild and the summer has been warm, with just enough rain. The wheat is tall and golden. The barley is ripe. The oats are nodding in the breeze. Wulfric walks along the edges of his strips every evening, looking at the grain and the sky. He knows that the next few weeks will decide whether his family eats well this winter, or goes hungry.

[pause]

Between the strips, there are narrow paths of grass, where people can walk without treading on the crops. In early summer, these paths are full of wild flowers. Red poppies, blue cornflowers, white daisies and yellow buttercups. The children pick them and make little crowns for their hair. The bees buzz from flower to flower. And the larks sing high up in the sky, so high that you can hardly see them.

[long-pause]

{img:anglo-saxon-calendar-feeling-sun-moon-seasons-wheel-of-year-fields}
The Anglo-Saxons had their own names for the months of the year. We know them thanks to a monk called Bede, who lived in a monastery in the north of England, in the time of our story. He was very interested in time and calendars. And he wrote down the old English names of the months, and what they meant.

[long-pause]

The names tell us a lot about the lives of the people. January shared its name with December, Giuli, the time of Yule. Much later, people also called it the wolf month, because in the cold of winter, hungry wolves came close to the villages. February was called Solmonath, the mud month, or perhaps the month of cakes, which people offered to the gods. March was Hrethmonath, named after a goddess. And April was Eosturmonath, named after a goddess of spring called Eostre. Her name lives on in our word Easter.

[pause]

May was called Thrimilchi, the month of three milkings, because the grass was so rich in May that the cows could be milked three times a day. June and July were called Litha, the mild months, the time of long, light evenings. August was called Weodmonath, the weed month, because the weeds grew so fast in the warm weather. And September was Halegmonath, the holy month, when people gave thanks for the harvest.

[pause]

{img:cows-in-lush-may-meadow-woman-milking-wooden-bucket-morning}
October was called Winterfylleth, because winter began with the first full moon of October. November was Blotmonath, the month of sacrifice. In the old days, this was the time when animals were killed, because there was not enough food to feed them all through the winter. Their meat was salted and smoked, to last until spring. And December was called Giuli, the time of Yule, the great midwinter feast.

[pause]

For Bede, the year really had two halves. The summer half, when the days are longer than the nights. And the winter half, when the nights are longer than the days. The Anglo-Saxons felt this very strongly. In a world without electric light, the length of the day decided almost everything. When to get up, when to work, when to sleep. In summer, they worked from four in the morning until nine at night. In winter, the days were short, and the nights were very long.

[long-pause]

{img:monk-bede-writing-at-desk-monastery-window-candle-quill-calendar}
Bede himself is a wonderful person. In the year 700, he is about twenty-seven years old. He lives in a monastery at Jarrow, in Northumbria, far to the north of our village. He has lived there since he was seven years old. He spends his days praying, teaching and writing. He writes about history, about the Bible, about science and about time. He is one of the most learned men in all of Europe.

[pause]

Bede is the first person to use the system of counting years from the birth of Christ, in a big history book. Because of him, this system became popular all over Europe. So, in a way, the very year 700 that we use for our story is a gift from Bede. He also wrote the first history of the English people. Without him, we would know much less about the Anglo-Saxons.

[pause]

{img:village-people-looking-at-sky-evening-weather-signs-clouds-fields}
The people of our village cannot read or write, of course. They do not know Bede. But they know the months and the seasons just as well as he does, from their own lives. They know the signs of the sky and the earth. When the swallows fly low, rain is coming. When the oak leaves come out before the ash leaves, the summer will be dry. When the grain is hard between the teeth, it is time to cut it.

[long-pause]

Now it is Weodmonath, the weed month. The days are still long and warm, but they are getting a little shorter. The evenings come a little earlier. In the mornings, there is dew on the grass, and sometimes mist on the river. The wheat is golden. And everyone in the village knows that the most important time of the year has come.

[long-pause]

{img:woman-grinding-first-wheat-quern-stones-hearth-early-morning}
On the first of August, there is a special day. In later centuries, the Anglo-Saxons called it hlafmæsse. It means loaf mass. Over time, the name became Lammas, and it is still called that in some parts of England today. It is the festival of the first bread of the new harvest.

[long-pause]

A few days before, Wulfric cut a small amount of the new wheat, from the best corner of his best strip. Just enough to fill a basket. He brought it home, and the family beat the grains out of the ears with sticks, on a clean cloth on the floor. Then Hild and her mother ground the grain into flour, with the quern stones. They turned the top stone round and round, and fine white flour came out at the edges.

[pause]

Early on the morning of the first of August, Eadgyth bakes a loaf from this flour. It is the first loaf of the new harvest. She mixes the flour with water, a little salt and some yeast from the ale. She kneads the dough on a wooden board, and shapes it into a round loaf. Then she bakes it on a flat stone in the hot ashes of the fire, covered with an upturned clay pot, so it bakes evenly.

[pause]

{img:round-loaf-baking-under-clay-pot-hot-ashes-hearth-glowing}
The smell of the new bread fills the whole house. Cuthwine wakes up and sniffs the air. Is it ready? Can I have some? His mother laughs and says no. This loaf is not for eating. Not yet. First, it must go to the church, to be blessed. Cuthwine sighs a big sigh. But he knows the custom. Every family in the village does the same.

[pause]

When the loaf is ready, Eadgyth wraps it in a clean white cloth. The whole family puts on their best clothes. Wulfric wears his good woollen tunic, with a leather belt. Eadgyth wears her blue dress, with a brooch at her shoulder. Hild has a new ribbon in her hair. Even Cuthwine has had his face washed, although his hair still sticks up at the back.

[long-pause]

{img:villagers-walking-to-small-church-carrying-loaves-white-cloths-morning}
Then they walk together to the little church. It is about half an hour's walk away, in the next village. Along the way, they meet other families, all carrying their loaves wrapped in white cloths. The children run ahead. The old people walk slowly. Everyone is talking and smiling. It is a happy day. The harvest is ready, and the first bread is baked.

[pause]

The church is small and made of wood, with a little bell hanging in a frame outside. Inside, it is simple and dim, with a wooden altar and a cross. The priest is waiting at the door. He is a kind, middle-aged man, who looks after the people of several villages. He greets everyone by name. Then the people go inside, and lay their loaves on the altar, one by one.

[pause]

{img:priest-blessing-loaves-on-altar-small-wooden-church-candles}
The priest says a prayer over the bread. He thanks God for the sun and the rain, for the soil and the seed, and for the harvest that is ready in the fields. He asks God to protect the harvest, and to keep away the storms, until all the grain is safely home. The people say amen. Then the priest blesses the bread, and gives each family back its loaf.

[long-pause]

Some people break their blessed loaf into four pieces, and put one piece in each corner of their barn. They believe it will protect the grain from mice and damp. It is an old custom, much older than the church. Modor does this every year. Then the rest of the bread is eaten by the family, at home, with fresh butter and honey. Cuthwine finally gets his piece. He says it is the best bread he has ever tasted. He says that every year.

[long-pause]

{img:reapers-with-sickles-cutting-wheat-golden-field-sunrise-anglo-saxon}
On the morning after Lammas, the harvest begins. Everyone is up before the sun. The whole village goes out to the fields together, men and women, young and old. Only the very youngest children stay behind, with Modor and the other grandmothers. The air is cool and fresh. The dew is still on the grass. And the wheat stands tall and golden, waiting.

[long-pause]

The tool for the harvest is the sickle. It is a curved iron blade, like a crescent moon, with a short wooden handle. The reaper holds a bunch of wheat stalks in one hand, and cuts them with the sickle in the other, just below the ears. Swish. Swish. It is a soft, steady sound. Then the reaper lays the cut stalks down in a neat pile, and reaches for the next bunch.

[pause]

The reapers work in a long line across the field, side by side, moving forward slowly together. Wulfric is at one end, with his neighbours. Eadgyth works beside him. Hild is in the line too, with her own small sickle. She has been reaping since she was nine. She is fast and careful. Her mother watches her proudly. She is almost as quick as the grown women now.

[pause]

{img:line-of-reapers-bending-field-women-men-together-morning-light}
Reaping is hard work. You must bend over all day long, cutting and cutting. Your back aches. Your hands get blisters. The sun gets hot, and the dust from the wheat gets into your eyes and your nose. The stalks are sharp, and can scratch your arms. But the people sing as they work, to keep a steady rhythm, and to make the time pass more quickly. Old work songs, which everyone knows.

[pause]

They also take short rests. Every few hours, they stop and sit in the shade at the edge of the field. Someone brings a jug of weak ale, or water from the spring, and everyone drinks. They eat bread and cheese. They wipe the sweat from their faces. Then they stand up, stretch their backs, and go back to work.

[long-pause]

{img:harvest-rest-shade-edge-of-field-people-sharing-bread-jug-of-ale}
Cuthwine has an important job. He walks along the edge of the field with a stick and a little drum, scaring away the birds. The crows and the pigeons love the ripe grain. They circle over the field and try to land among the sheaves. Cuthwine runs at them, shouting and banging his drum. The birds fly up in a great cloud, and land again a little further away. It is a game that never ends. By evening, Cuthwine is hoarse and very tired.

[pause]

Behind the reapers, there are the binders. They gather up the cut stalks into big bundles, called sheaves. They tie each sheaf around the middle with a few twisted stalks of wheat, like a belt. Then they stand the sheaves up in little groups, leaning against each other, with the ears of grain at the top. These groups are called stooks. They let the wind and sun dry the grain.

[pause]

{img:stooks-of-sheaves-standing-rows-field-afternoon-shadows}
By the middle of the afternoon, a whole strip has been cut. The field looks completely different. Instead of tall, waving wheat, there is short stubble, and rows and rows of stooks, standing like little golden huts. Swallows swoop low over the stubble, catching insects. A hare runs out of the last uncut corner of the field, and dashes away toward the woods.

[long-pause]

In the evening, as the sun goes down, the reapers walk home together, their sickles over their shoulders. They are exhausted, dusty and sunburnt. But they are happy. The first day of the harvest is done, and the weather has been kind. At home, Modor has made a big pot of stew, with beans, onions and a little bacon. The family eats in silence, too tired to talk. And then they lie down on their benches, and are asleep before the fire burns low.

[pause]

At midday, the women of the village bring food out to the field in baskets. Bread, cheese, onions, cold bacon and apples, and big jugs of ale. Everyone sits down together in the shade of an old oak tree at the edge of the field. They eat and talk and laugh. The young men tease each other about who is the fastest reaper. The old men say that in their day, the reapers were much faster. They say that every year.

[long-pause]

{img:farmer-looking-at-sky-dark-clouds-gathering-over-golden-field}
During the harvest, everyone watches the sky. The weather is the most important thing of all. The grain must be cut, dried and carried home while it is dry. If it rains too much, the grain in the stooks gets wet. Then it can start to sprout, or rot, or grow mould. A wet harvest can mean a hungry winter. So every morning, the first thing Wulfric does is step outside and look at the sky.

[long-pause]

The Anglo-Saxons knew many signs of the weather. A red sky in the evening meant a fine day tomorrow. A red sky in the morning meant rain. When the cows lay down in the field, rain was coming. When the spiders spun long webs, the weather would be dry. When the swallows flew high, it would be fine. Modor knows all these signs, and many more. She is usually right.

[pause]

For the first week of the harvest, the weather is perfect. Warm, sunny days, with a light breeze. The reapers cut field after field. The stooks stand in long rows, drying in the sun. Everyone works as fast as they can, to use the good weather. Wulfric says that the summer has been kind to them, and they must not waste it.

[pause]

{img:hot-still-afternoon-heavy-air-reapers-hurrying-distant-thunder-clouds}
Then, one afternoon, the air becomes very still and heavy. It is hot, but the sun is hazy. The birds stop singing. The cows stand close together, under the trees. Modor comes out of the house and looks at the sky in the west. Over the hills, big dark clouds are piling up, higher and higher, like grey mountains. In the distance, there is a low rumble of thunder.

[pause]

Wulfric sees it too. He calls out to the whole field. The sheaves in the far field are dry. They must be brought in before the storm. Everyone drops what they are doing and runs. The ox cart is brought out. The men and women load the sheaves onto the cart, as fast as they can, piling them high. Hild climbs up on top, to stack them neatly. Cuthwine leads the oxen. The thunder rumbles again, closer now.

[long-pause]

{img:ox-cart-piled-with-sheaves-racing-home-storm-clouds-wind}
The wind begins to blow. The trees at the edge of the field bend and toss. The first big drops of rain fall, warm and heavy, making dark spots in the dust. The cart rolls slowly toward the village, creaking under its load. Everyone walks beside it, holding the sheaves down against the wind. The barn door is open. The oxen pull the cart inside. And just as the last sheaf is under the roof, the rain comes down in a great roaring curtain.

[pause]

Everyone crowds into the barn, out of breath and laughing. They are wet, but the grain is dry. Outside, the rain pours down. Lightning flashes, and thunder cracks right over the village. The children hide their faces. But inside the barn, everyone is safe and warm. The sweet smell of the dry wheat fills the air. And the rain drums loudly on the thatched roof.

[pause]

{img:inside-barn-people-sheltering-rain-pouring-outside-doorway-sheaves}
The storm lasts for an hour. Then it moves away to the east, grumbling. The rain stops. The clouds break, and the evening sun shines through, low and golden. Everything is wet and shining. The air smells fresh and clean. And over the hills, there is a rainbow, a perfect arch of colours across the sky.

[long-pause]

Not all the stooks were saved. In the fields that were not yet carried in, the sheaves are wet. But Wulfric says it does not matter. If the next days are sunny, they will dry again. The important thing is that most of the grain is safe. Modor smiles and says that she knew the storm was coming, because her knees were aching all morning. Everyone laughs. Her knees are the best weather sign in the village.

[pause]

That evening, Modor sits in the doorway and watches the last of the storm clouds drift away over the hills. The air is cool and sweet after the rain. Drops are still falling from the edge of the thatch, plip, plop, into the puddles. A blackbird begins to sing in the apple tree. And Modor closes her eyes and listens, with a little smile on her face.

[long-pause]

{img:gleaners-women-children-picking-up-fallen-ears-stubble-field-evening}
The harvest goes on for about three weeks. Field after field is cut, bound and carried home. Wheat first, then barley, then oats. And after each field is cleared, the gleaners come. Gleaning means picking up the ears of grain that have fallen on the ground, and were left behind by the reapers.

[long-pause]

Gleaning is usually done by the women and children, and by the poorest people of the village. They walk slowly across the stubble field, bending down to pick up every stray ear of wheat. They put them in their aprons or in little bags. It is slow work. But by the end of the day, a good gleaner may have collected enough grain for a few loaves of bread. For a poor widow, that can make a big difference in winter.

[pause]

There is an old rule about gleaning, from the Bible. It says that farmers should not gather every last grain from their fields. They should leave some for the poor, the widow and the stranger. The priest reminds the villagers of this every year. And the farmers of our village are generous. Wulfric always tells his reapers not to be too careful at the edges of the field.

[pause]

{img:old-widow-gleaning-with-girl-helping-her-sunset-stubble}
Hild goes gleaning with an old widow from the village, called Wynflaed. Her husband died many years ago, and she has no children. She lives alone in a small house at the edge of the village. She is too old to work in the fields. So Hild helps her glean, and carries her bag home for her. Wynflaed is grateful. She tells Hild stories as they work, about the old days when she was young.

[pause]

Then comes the most special moment of the harvest. The cutting of the last sheaf. When the very last strip of the very last field is being cut, the whole village gathers round to watch. There is an old belief that the spirit of the harvest lives in the grain. As the reapers cut, the spirit runs before them, hiding in the grain that is left. And so, at the end, the spirit is hiding in the very last sheaf.

[long-pause]

{img:villagers-gathered-around-last-sheaf-standing-field-sunset-ceremony}
In some villages, the reapers take turns throwing their sickles at the last bunch of standing wheat, from a distance, so that nobody knows who cut it. In other villages, the oldest reaper cuts it. In our village, the honour goes to the youngest reaper. This year, that is Hild. Everyone stands around in a circle and watches her. She takes her sickle, and with one careful stroke, she cuts the last bunch of wheat.

[pause]

Everyone cheers. The last sheaf is lifted up high. The women take it and twist and braid it into a special shape, like a little figure, or a doll, with a body and arms and a head. It is decorated with ribbons and wild flowers. In later times, this figure was called a corn dolly, or a harvest maiden. It is carried home in a procession, on top of the last cart, with everyone singing behind it.

[pause]

{img:corn-dolly-braided-straw-figure-ribbons-flowers-hanging-hearth}
The corn dolly is hung up in the house, above the hearth, or in the barn. It stays there all winter. People believe it keeps the spirit of the harvest safe, through the cold months. And in the spring, when the first field is ploughed, the corn dolly is taken down and ploughed into the soil. So the spirit of the harvest goes back into the earth, to make the new crops grow.

[long-pause]

The priest does not entirely approve of these old customs. They are much older than the Christian faith, and some of them come from the old gods. But he does not forbid them either. He is a wise man, and he knows that the people love them. So, like many priests in those days, he simply adds a prayer to the old custom. And the old and the new live on together, side by side, in the little villages of England.

[long-pause]

{img:anglo-saxon-farmyard-cows-pigs-sheep-geese-chickens-morning-busy}
A farm in Anglo-Saxon England is full of animals. Every family has some, and they are very important. The animals pull the plough, give milk, wool, eggs and meat, and their dung makes the fields fertile. The family knows every animal by name, and looks after them carefully.

[long-pause]

The most valuable animals are the oxen. Wulfric's two oxen are called Brun and Hwita, which means Brown and White. They are big, strong and gentle animals, with long horns. They pull the plough in spring and the cart at harvest time. Without them, Wulfric could not farm his land. He talks to them while they work, and gives them extra hay in winter. Hild loves them, and brushes their coats on Sundays.

[pause]

The two cows give milk. Eadgyth milks them every morning and evening, sitting on a little three-legged stool, with a wooden bucket. From the milk, she makes butter, by shaking the cream in a wooden churn until it turns solid. She makes soft cheese, and hard cheese which keeps for months. The milk that is left over is drunk by the children, or given to the pigs.

[pause]

{img:woman-churning-butter-wooden-churn-doorway-cat-watching}
The sheep are kept mostly for their wool. In early summer, they are washed in the river and then sheared, with big iron shears. The wool is cleaned, combed and spun into thread. Most of the clothes of the family are made from this wool. The sheep also give milk, which makes a strong, tasty cheese. And their dung is good for the fields. Sheep are so important that the wool of England will one day make the country rich.

[pause]

The pigs are the cheapest animals to keep. They eat almost anything. Scraps from the kitchen, roots, worms and the acorns in the forest. In the autumn, Cuthwine takes them into the forest every day, to eat the fallen acorns and beechnuts. They grow fat and round. And in November, the month of sacrifice, some of them will be killed and turned into bacon, ham and sausages for the winter.

[long-pause]

{img:boy-herding-pigs-forest-edge-autumn-oak-trees-acorns}
The geese are Hild's special friends. There are twelve of them, white and grey, with orange beaks. They follow her around the yard, honking. Geese are very useful. They give eggs, meat and soft feathers for pillows. Their big wing feathers are used to make arrows, and also writing quills for the monks. And they are wonderful guards. When a stranger comes near the house, the geese make so much noise that the whole village hears it.

[pause]

The chickens scratch around the yard, looking for seeds and insects. They lay eggs, though not as many as modern chickens. And there is a cat, a big grey cat with green eyes, who catches the mice in the storehouse. Mice are a great danger to the stored grain, so a good cat is very valuable. Cats were so useful that, in Wales a little later, the law even said what you had to pay if you killed someone's cat.

[pause]

{img:bee-skeps-straw-hives-garden-bees-flying-flowers-summer}
And there are the bees. Eadgyth has three beehives, made of coiled straw, shaped like little domes. They stand in a corner of the garden, under the apple tree. Bees are very precious. Honey is the only sweetener the Anglo-Saxons have. They use it in cooking, in medicine, and above all to make mead, a strong, sweet drink made from honey and water. The wax is used to make candles, which are very expensive.

[long-pause]

Modor says that you must always tell the bees the news. If someone in the family is born, or married, or dies, you must go to the hives and tell the bees. Otherwise, they will be offended, and fly away. It is an old, old custom. Some country people in England still do it today. Every evening, Hild goes to the hives and tells the bees about the day. She tells them about the harvest, and the storm, and the last sheaf. The bees hum softly, as if they are listening.

[long-pause]

{img:warp-weighted-loom-clay-weights-woman-weaving-sunken-hut-light}
In the evenings during the harvest, and through the long autumn and winter, Eadgyth weaves. Weaving is one of the most important jobs of an Anglo-Saxon woman. Every piece of cloth that the family wears, sleeps under or uses in the house, has been spun and woven by hand. It takes an enormous amount of time.

[long-pause]

First, the wool must be spun into thread. For this, the women use a drop spindle. It is a simple wooden stick, with a little round weight at the bottom, called a whorl. The spinner pulls a little wool from a bundle, twists it, and lets the spindle hang and spin. The spinning twists the wool into a thin, strong thread. Women spin all day long, whenever their hands are free. While they walk, while they talk, while they watch the children.

[pause]

Modor spins more than anyone. She sits by the door all day, with her spindle turning and turning. Her fingers move without her even looking. She has spun so much thread in her long life that, if you laid it all out in a line, it would probably reach around the whole of England. Hild is learning from her. Her thread is still a bit lumpy. But it is getting better.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-teaching-girl-drop-spindle-wool-thread-doorway}
The loom stands in the sunken hut. It is a big upright frame of wood, taller than a person, leaning against the wall. The long threads of the warp hang down from the top beam. At the bottom, they are held tight by rows of heavy clay rings, called loom weights. The weaver stands in front of the loom, and passes the other thread, the weft, back and forth between the hanging threads. Then she pushes it up tight with a flat wooden sword.

[pause]

It is slow and patient work. A piece of cloth big enough for a tunic can take weeks to weave. Eadgyth weaves plain cloth for everyday clothes. But she also knows how to weave patterns, like diamonds and stripes. For special clothes, she weaves narrow coloured bands, called tablet weaving, to decorate the edges of dresses and tunics. These bands are beautiful, with tiny patterns in red, blue and yellow.

[long-pause]

{img:tablet-weaving-colourful-band-patterns-hands-wooden-tablets}
The colours come from plants. Woad, a plant with yellow flowers, makes blue. Madder, a plant with red roots, makes red. Weld, a tall weed, makes yellow. Onion skins make orange and brown. Eadgyth grows some of these plants in her garden, and gathers others from the fields and hedges. She boils the wool in big pots with the plants, until it takes the colour. Her hands are often stained blue or red for days.

[pause]

The Anglo-Saxons love colour. Their clothes are not dull and brown, as people sometimes think. The rich wear bright reds, blues and greens, with gold thread and embroidery. Even ordinary farmers have some coloured clothes, for feast days. Wulfric has a blue tunic. Eadgyth has a red dress. And Hild is making herself a little band of tablet weaving, red and yellow, to decorate her new dress for the harvest feast.

[pause]

{img:anglo-saxon-woman-red-dress-brooches-beads-feast-day-sunlight}
Women also wear jewellery. Eadgyth has two round bronze brooches, which she wears on her shoulders to hold up her dress. Between them hangs a string of glass and amber beads. These beads came from her own mother. Some of them are very old. Hild will have them one day. Rich women wear brooches of gold and silver, decorated with red garnets. But even simple bronze brooches are made with great skill.

[long-pause]

Anglo-Saxon cloth was famous across Europe. English embroidery, in particular, became so admired that it was later sent to kings and popes in faraway lands. But in our village, the weaving is simply part of everyday life. In the evening light, the soft thump of the loom weights, the click of the wooden sword and the quiet humming of Eadgyth as she works, are among the most familiar sounds of home.

[pause]

Sometimes, the women of the village gather in one house to spin and weave together. They bring their spindles and their work, and sit around the fire, talking and singing. The time passes much faster that way. They share news and stories, give each other advice, and help each other with difficult patterns. The little girls sit with them and learn, and the little boys play on the floor, until they are sent outside.

[long-pause]

{img:anglo-saxon-trading-port-hamwic-ships-river-wooden-warehouses-traders}
After the harvest, Wulfric has a little grain to spare. He also has some cheese, some wool and two young pigs that the family does not need. So one morning, he loads the ox cart, and takes Cuthwine with him to the market. It is a long journey, almost a whole day, to the trading town by the sea. It is called Hamwic. Today, the city of Southampton stands in the same place.

[long-pause]

Hamwic is the biggest town Cuthwine has ever seen. It is a new town, built not long ago, by the kings of Wessex, for trade. About two or three thousand people live there. The streets are laid out in a grid, straight and covered with gravel. There are rows of wooden houses and workshops. And along the river, there is a harbour, with boats from across the sea.

[pause]

The town is full of craftsmen. There are blacksmiths, hammering iron into tools and nails. There are potters, making pots and jugs. There are bone workers, carving combs, needles and pins from the bones of cattle and deer. There are weavers, leather workers and jewellers. The air is full of noise and smoke and smells. Cuthwine stares at everything, holding tightly to his father's hand.

[pause]

{img:busy-anglo-saxon-market-street-stalls-blacksmith-comb-maker-crowd}
At the harbour, there are ships from Frankia, across the sea, which is now France. They bring wine in barrels, millstones, glass, fine pottery and swords. They take back English wool, cloth, hides, tin and lead. Sadly, they sometimes also take people who have been sold as slaves, as happened in many lands in those days. The traders speak many languages. Old English, Frankish, Frisian, and the Latin of the churchmen. Cuthwine hears words he has never heard before.

[pause]

Wulfric sells his grain, his cheese and his wool to a merchant. He sells the pigs to a butcher. In return, he gets some small silver coins. They are called sceattas. They are very small, about the size of a fingernail, with strange little pictures on them. Birds, animals, faces and patterns. Coins are still quite rare in the countryside. Most people in villages trade by exchanging things, not with money. But in Hamwic, coins are used every day.

[long-pause]

{img:tiny-silver-sceat-coins-hand-palm-close-anglo-saxon}
With his coins, Wulfric buys the things the family cannot make itself. A new iron sickle blade, because his old one is worn thin. Some salt, which is very important for preserving meat and butter for the winter. A little bag of iron nails. And a small pot of honey-coloured glass beads, for Hild, for the harvest feast. He hides the beads in his pocket. It is a surprise.

[pause]

Cuthwine is allowed to choose something too. He walks up and down the stalls for a long time. In the end, he chooses a little comb made of deer antler, with a pattern of circles carved on it. It is not for himself. It is for Modor. Her old comb is broken. Wulfric is very proud of his son, but he does not say so. He just puts his hand on the boy's messy hair for a moment.

[pause]

{img:father-son-ox-cart-returning-home-evening-road-countryside}
They spend the night in Hamwic, sleeping in the cart, under the stars. Cuthwine lies awake for a long time, listening to the sounds of the town. Dogs barking, the creak of ships in the harbour, the voices of sailors singing in a strange language. He thinks it is the most exciting place in the world. But he also misses home, and the geese, and his own bench by the fire.

[long-pause]

The next morning, they set off for home, early. The road winds through the fields and forests. Everywhere, people are finishing their harvest. The fields are stubble now, with flocks of birds feeding on the fallen grain. In the late afternoon, they see the smoke of their own village rising above the trees. The geese hear the cart coming, and start honking. And Hild comes running down the path to meet them.

[long-pause]

{img:anglo-saxon-great-hall-timber-carved-doorway-thegn-estate-evening}
A few kilometres from the village, on a low hill, stands the hall of the local lord. He is a thegn, a nobleman who serves the king of Wessex. He owns a lot of land, including the land around our village. The villagers owe him some work and some food every year. In return, he protects them, settles their quarrels, and holds a great feast for them after the harvest.

[long-pause]

The hall is the biggest building for many kilometres. It is long and tall, made of thick oak posts, with a high roof of straw. The doorposts are carved with twisting animals and patterns, painted in red and black. Inside, there is one huge room, with a long hearth down the middle. Around the walls, there are benches and tables. And at the far end, on a raised platform, sits the lord, with his family and his most important guests.

[pause]

The lord is called Aelfric. He is a big man, with a red beard and a loud laugh. He has fought in the king's wars, and he has a sword with a golden handle, which he received as a gift from the king himself. His wife is called Aethelburh. She is tall and dignified, with golden brooches and a dress of fine blue cloth. She is very clever, and helps her husband run the estate. Everyone respects her.

[pause]

{img:lady-of-hall-serving-mead-from-cup-guests-benches-firelight}
In the time after the harvest, Wulfric and the other farmers bring their dues to the hall. Sacks of grain, barrels of ale, cheeses, honey, a few sheep and geese. These are the rent for their land. The lord's steward counts everything and writes it down on a wooden tally stick, with little notches. Then the food goes into the lord's storehouses, to feed his household through the winter.

[pause]

And at the end of the harvest, the lord invites the whole village to a feast in his hall. It is one of the happiest evenings of the year. The hall is filled with people, sitting along the benches. Torches burn along the walls. A great fire burns in the long hearth. And on the tables, there is more food than the villagers have seen all year.

[long-pause]

{img:feast-table-roast-meat-bread-cheese-drinking-horns-anglo-saxon-hall}
There is roast pork and roast beef, turning on spits over the fire. There are geese and chickens, stewed with herbs. There are great round loaves of the new bread, and cheeses, butter and honey. There are apples and plums and blackberries. And there is plenty to drink. Ale, made from barley, and for the important guests, mead, made from honey, and even a little wine from Frankia, across the sea.

[pause]

The lady of the hall has a special role at the feast. She walks around the hall with a beautiful cup, and offers drink to the guests, one by one. First to her husband, then to the most important guests, and then to everyone else. It is a great honour to be served by her. When she comes to Wulfric, he stands up and bows, and his ears turn red.

[pause]

{img:harp-player-scop-singing-hall-firelight-listeners-quiet}
After the meal, the most exciting moment comes. The scop stands up. A scop is a poet and singer. He holds a small wooden lyre, with six strings. He plucks a few notes, and the whole hall goes quiet. Then he begins to sing. He sings old stories, about heroes and kings, about battles and journeys, about dragons and monsters. His voice is deep and strong, and fills the whole hall.

[long-pause]

Cuthwine sits close to the fire, with his mouth open, listening. He has never heard anything so wonderful. Hild sits beside him, holding her knees. The flames flicker. The shadows dance on the walls. Outside, it is dark and the wind is blowing. But inside the hall, there is warmth and light and music. And the scop sings on, late into the night, while the children slowly fall asleep against their parents' shoulders.

[pause]

Late at night, the villagers walk home together along the dark path, carrying burning torches. The children are carried on their fathers' shoulders, fast asleep. The adults are tired and happy, humming the tunes of the scop's songs. Above them, the stars are shining. And far behind them, the windows of the great hall are still glowing in the darkness, like a lantern on the hill.

[long-pause]

{img:scop-with-lyre-telling-story-firelight-faces-listening-shadows}
The stories that the scop sings are very old. Many of them were brought across the sea by the ancestors of the Anglo-Saxons, hundreds of years ago. They were never written down. They were passed on from singer to singer, by memory. A good scop knows thousands of lines by heart. And each time he sings, he changes the story a little, to fit his audience.

[long-pause]

One of the most famous stories of the Anglo-Saxons is about a hero called Beowulf. Nobody knows exactly when it was first made, but many people think it was around the time of our story. It tells about a great hall in Denmark, where a king holds feasts, just like Lord Aelfric. But every night, a terrible monster comes out of the dark marshes and attacks the hall. Then a young hero comes across the sea, to help. His name is Beowulf.

[pause]

We will not tell the battles in the story, for they are rather fierce. But the story is also full of quiet, beautiful moments. The joy of the feast in the hall. The sound of the harp. The gold that the king gives to his friends. The ships sailing over the waves, which the poet calls the whale road. And at the end, the hero, grown old, thinking about his long life, as the evening falls.

[pause]

{img:viking-style-ship-sailing-grey-sea-waves-whale-road-poetry}
The Anglo-Saxons loved a special way of speaking in their poems. Instead of saying the sea, they said the whale road, or the swan road. Instead of saying a ship, they said a sea horse, or a wave floater. The sun was the candle of the sky. A sword was the light of battle. The body was the bone house. These little poetic names are called kennings. People loved to hear new ones, and to guess what they meant.

[pause]

They also loved riddles. In the evenings, around the fire, people asked each other riddles. A scop might say, I am a wonderful thing. I live in the water, and I cannot drown. When I come to the land, I die. Everyone thinks. What is it? The answer is ice. Or, I have no tongue, but I tell many stories. I have no feet, but I travel far. The answer is a book.

[long-pause]

{img:family-by-fire-telling-riddles-children-laughing-evening-house}
At home, in the evenings, Modor tells riddles too. Cuthwine loves them, although he almost never guesses the answer. Hild is better at it. She thinks for a long time, frowning, and then suddenly shouts out the answer. Modor laughs and claps her hands. Then she tells another one, harder this time. The fire crackles. The cat purrs. And outside, the autumn wind blows through the trees.

[pause]

Modor also tells old stories, much older than Beowulf. Stories about the elves who live in the hills and the woods, and who can cause sickness if you anger them. About the dwarfs who make magical things in their workshops under the mountains. About Weland the smith, the greatest craftsman who ever lived, who made wings for himself and flew away from his enemies. The children listen, half afraid and half delighted.

[pause]

{img:misty-hill-at-dusk-old-burial-mound-trees-mysterious-quiet}
Near the village, there is an old burial mound, a low round hill covered with grass and bushes. It is very old, much older than the village. Modor says that a great king of long ago is buried there, with his treasure. And that a dragon guards it. Cuthwine does not believe in dragons, he says. But he does not go near the mound after dark.

[long-pause]

Many of these old stories were later written down by monks, in beautiful books. The story of Beowulf survives in only one old book, which is kept today in the British Library in London. The book was almost destroyed in a fire, about three hundred years ago. Its pages are scorched at the edges. But the story survived. And now, more than a thousand years later, people all over the world still read about Beowulf, and the great hall, and the whale road.

[long-pause]

{img:anglo-saxon-monastery-stone-church-wooden-buildings-river-valley-morning}
About a day's walk from the village, there is a monastery. It lies in a quiet valley by a river, surrounded by fields and orchards. There is a stone church, with small round windows, and a group of wooden buildings around it. About thirty monks live there, and some nuns in a separate house nearby. They pray, work and study, following a strict rule.

[long-pause]

In Anglo-Saxon England, monasteries are very important places. They are places of prayer, of course. But they are also schools, libraries, hospitals and farms. They own a lot of land, and many villages work for them. Rich families give them land and gifts, hoping for prayers for their souls. And kings often send their sons and daughters there, to be educated.

[pause]

Some monasteries are led by women. They are called abbesses. One of the most famous was Hild, the abbess of Whitby, in Northumbria. She died about twenty years before our story. She was so wise that kings and bishops came to ask her advice. Five of her monks later became bishops. Our Hild, the girl in the village, was named after her. Her mother thinks it is a good name, for a wise girl.

[pause]

{img:monks-in-scriptorium-writing-manuscripts-quills-vellum-window-light}
The monks of the monastery spend part of each day in the scriptorium. This is the room where books are written. There is no printing in those days. Every book must be copied by hand, letter by letter, with a quill pen. The pages are made of vellum, which is the skin of calves, cleaned, stretched and dried until it is thin and smooth. A big book needs the skins of hundreds of calves.

[pause]

The ink is made from soot, or from oak galls, which are little round growths on oak trees. The colours for the pictures are made from minerals, plants and even insects. Some colours are very precious. The deep blue is made from a stone called lapis lazuli, which comes all the way from the mountains of Afghanistan. The gold is real gold, beaten into very thin sheets.

[long-pause]

{img:illuminated-manuscript-page-interlace-patterns-birds-colours-gold}
Around the time of our story, at a monastery on a small island called Lindisfarne, in the far north of England, a monk is making one of the most beautiful books ever made. It is a book of the four Gospels. It is called the Lindisfarne Gospels. Its pages are covered with amazing patterns. Twisting lines and knots, birds with long necks, spirals and crosses, all in bright colours. It took the monk, Eadfrith, many years to make.

[pause]

The patterns in these books are similar to the patterns on Anglo-Saxon jewellery and brooches. The monks were using the old art of their people, the twisting animals and knots, to decorate the holy book. Irish monks had brought a similar style. And they added colours and ideas from Rome. So the books are a beautiful mix of all these traditions together.

[pause]

{img:monk-in-orchard-picking-apples-monastery-garden-autumn-baskets}
The monks also farm. They have fields, orchards, fishponds and beehives. They grow herbs for medicine, in a special garden. They brew ale and bake bread. At harvest time, they work in the fields just like the villagers, in their simple brown robes. And they keep the old knowledge of medicine, writing down the uses of hundreds of plants and herbs.

[long-pause]

Once a year, after the harvest, Wulfric goes to the monastery with a cart of grain, as a gift. This year, he takes Hild with him. She has never seen a book. A kind old monk shows her the scriptorium, and lets her look at a page of a book. Hild stares at the patterns and the colours. She cannot read the words. But she thinks it is the most beautiful thing she has ever seen. On the way home, she is very quiet, thinking.

[pause]

Many years later, Hild will remember that page for the rest of her life. The twisting knots of colour, the little birds with long necks, the shining gold. She will tell her own children and grandchildren about it, by the fire, in the long winter evenings. And they will listen, and try to imagine it, a book so beautiful that it looked like it was made by angels.

[long-pause]

{img:barn-threshing-floor-men-flails-beating-sheaves-dust-light-beams}
When all the sheaves are safely home in the barns, the next work begins. The grain must be separated from the straw. This is called threshing. It is done in the barn, on a hard, clean floor, in the weeks after the harvest. And it goes on, on rainy days, all through the autumn and winter.

[long-pause]

The tool for threshing is the flail. It is made of two wooden sticks, joined together by a short leather strap. One stick is long, as a handle. The other is short and thick. The thresher swings the long handle over his head, and the short stick swings round and comes down, thwack, on the sheaves lying on the floor. The blows knock the grains out of the ears.

[pause]

Threshing is hard, dusty work. Usually two or three men thresh together, standing in a circle. They swing their flails in turn, one after another, in a steady rhythm. Thwack, thwack, thwack. The sound can be heard all over the village, on autumn days. It is a good sound. It means there is grain. The men must be careful to keep the rhythm, or the flails will bang into each other.

[pause]

{img:winnowing-woman-tossing-grain-basket-wind-chaff-blowing-barn-door}
After threshing, the floor is covered with a mixture of grain, broken straw and chaff. Chaff is the dry husk around each grain. Now the grain must be cleaned. This is called winnowing. On a windy day, the barn doors are opened on both sides, so the wind blows right through. Eadgyth takes a wide, flat basket, fills it with the mixture, and tosses it up into the air.

[pause]

The heavy grains fall straight back down into the basket. But the light chaff is caught by the wind, and blows away, in little golden clouds. Again and again, she tosses the grain, until it is clean. It is a lovely thing to watch. The golden chaff drifting out of the barn door, glittering in the sunlight, like snow. The chickens rush over to peck at it.

[long-pause]

{img:grain-stored-in-clay-pots-wooden-bins-storehouse-dim-light}
The clean grain is stored carefully. Some of it is kept in big clay pots with lids. Some in wooden chests or bins. It must be kept dry, and safe from mice and rats. That is why the cat is so important. The best grain is set aside as seed, for planting next spring. That grain is never eaten, even in the hungriest winter. Without seed, there would be no harvest next year.

[pause]

The straw is also precious. It is used for many things. For new thatch on the roofs. For bedding for the animals. For stuffing mattresses. For weaving baskets and beehives. For making fires. Nothing is wasted. Even the chaff is gathered up and fed to the animals, or mixed with clay for the walls of houses.

[pause]

{img:watermill-anglo-saxon-wooden-wheel-river-miller-sacks-flour}
To make flour, the grain must be ground. For everyday use, Eadgyth grinds a little every morning with the quern stones at home. It is slow, tiring work. But for larger amounts, there is something new. On the river, a short walk from the village, there is a watermill. It is a small wooden building, with a big wooden wheel turned by the flowing water. Inside, the wheel turns a heavy millstone, which grinds the grain much faster than any person could.

[long-pause]

Watermills were quite new in England in the year 700. They were expensive to build, so usually a lord or a monastery owned them. The farmers brought their grain to the mill, and the miller ground it for them, keeping a small part of the flour as his payment. Cuthwine loves the mill. He loves the splashing of the water, the creaking of the wheel and the deep rumble of the stones. And the miller always gives him a handful of flour to throw into the air, like a cloud.

[pause]

On rainy autumn days, when there is no work in the fields, the whole family helps with the threshing. The barn is warm and dry, and full of the sweet smell of straw. Rain drums on the roof. The flails thump in a steady rhythm. Chaff floats in the light from the open door. And the pile of clean, golden grain slowly grows bigger and bigger, as the days go by.

[long-pause]

{img:clay-oven-outdoor-anglo-saxon-woman-baking-loaves-wooden-peel-morning}
Bread is the most important food of the Anglo-Saxons. They eat it at every meal. In fact, their word for lord, hlaford, means guardian of the bread. And their word for lady, hlæfdige, probably means the one who kneads the bread. So the lord and lady of the house were, in a way, the people who made sure everyone had bread.

[long-pause]

Eadgyth bakes bread every few days. She mixes flour, water, salt and a little yeast in a big wooden trough. The yeast comes from the ale she brews. It makes the bread rise and become soft. She kneads the dough for a long time, pushing and folding it with her strong hands. Then she covers it with a cloth, and leaves it by the warm fire to rise.

[pause]

Some of the bread is baked in the hot ashes of the hearth, under a clay pot. But the village also has a big clay oven, shaped like a beehive, which stands outside. The women take turns using it. They light a fire inside the oven, and let it burn until the clay is very hot. Then they rake out the ashes, put in the loaves on a long wooden paddle, and close the opening. The heat of the clay bakes the bread.

[pause]

{img:fresh-round-loaves-cooling-wooden-table-butter-honey-pot}
There are different kinds of bread. White bread, made from wheat, is the best and the most expensive. It is soft and light. Rich people eat it every day. Ordinary people eat it on feast days. Everyday bread is often made from barley, or from a mixture of grains. It is darker and heavier. And in a poor year, people make bread from beans, peas or even acorns mixed with a little flour. But this year, with a good harvest, Eadgyth bakes wheat bread every week.

[pause]

The second most important thing, after bread, is ale. Everyone drinks ale, even the children. It is made from barley, and it is much weaker than modern beer. In a world where river water is not always clean, ale is safer to drink. And it gives strength for the hard work in the fields. A farmer at harvest time might drink several litres of weak ale a day.

[long-pause]

{img:brewing-ale-large-pot-barley-steam-woman-stirring-wooden-paddle}
Eadgyth brews ale every week. First, she soaks barley grains in water, until they begin to sprout. Then she dries them gently by the fire. This is called malt. She grinds the malt, and mixes it with hot water in a big pot. The water becomes sweet. She lets it cool, and adds a little yeast from the last batch. Then she leaves it for a few days, covered with a cloth, to bubble and ferment.

[pause]

The Anglo-Saxons did not usually add hops to their ale. That came much later. Instead, they flavoured it with herbs, like bog myrtle, yarrow or heather. Each woman had her own recipe, and some were famous for their good ale. Eadgyth's ale is said to be the best in the village. When the neighbours come to visit, they always hope she will offer them a cup.

[pause]

{img:mead-honey-jars-drinking-horn-wooden-cups-table-firelight}
For special occasions, there is mead. Mead is made from honey and water, fermented with yeast. It is sweet and strong, and very precious, because honey is precious. In the great halls, the lords drink mead from drinking horns, decorated with silver. In the stories, the mead hall is the centre of all joy and friendship. To share mead with someone means to be their friend and ally.

[long-pause]

At the end of a long harvest day, there is nothing better than a hunk of fresh bread, with butter and cheese, and a cup of cool ale. The family sits outside the house in the evening light, eating and drinking, and watching the swallows. Wulfric says that a king in his hall eats no better than this. Eadgyth laughs and says that a king would at least wash his hands first. Wulfric looks at his dusty hands, and laughs too.

[long-pause]

{img:last-cart-harvest-home-procession-corn-dolly-villagers-singing-sunset}
When the very last field is cut, and the last cart of sheaves is brought home, the village celebrates. In later centuries, the English called this harvest home. It is one of the happiest moments of the year. The hard work is done. The grain is safe. And everyone can rest, at least for a little while.

[long-pause]

The last cart comes home in a procession. It is decorated with flowers, green branches and ribbons. On top sits the corn dolly, the figure made from the last sheaf. The oxen have flowers around their horns. The children ride on top of the sheaves, laughing and waving. And behind the cart, the whole village walks, singing an old harvest song, all the way from the field to the barn.

[pause]

When the cart arrives in the village, the old people come out of their houses to greet it. They clap and cheer. Modor stands at the door, smiling. Wynflaed, the old widow, waves her stick. And the cart rolls slowly into the barn, where the last sheaves are unloaded and stacked. Wulfric hangs the corn dolly above the barn door, where it will stay until spring.

[pause]

{img:village-green-long-tables-harvest-supper-lanterns-families-together}
Then the village has its own harvest supper. It is not as grand as the feast in the lord's hall. But it is more cosy. Long tables are set up in the open space in the middle of the village, on wooden trestles. Every family brings food. Bread from the new harvest. Stews and soups. Cheese and butter. Apples, plums and blackberries. A roasted pig, turning on a spit over a fire. And barrels of ale.

[pause]

Everyone eats together. Old and young, rich and poor, neighbours and friends. Even the priest comes, and says a prayer of thanks before the meal. He thanks God for the good harvest, for the sun and the rain, and for the strong arms of the reapers. Everyone says amen. And then they eat and drink and talk, until the sun goes down.

[long-pause]

{img:villagers-dancing-in-circle-firelight-music-pipes-drum-evening}
After the meal, there is music. One of the young men plays a pipe, made from a bird bone. Another beats a small drum. And the people dance. Simple dances, in long lines and circles, holding hands, stepping and turning. The children dance too, getting in everyone's way. Wulfric dances with Eadgyth. Hild dances with the other girls. And Cuthwine dances by himself, round and round, until he falls over.

[pause]

There are also games. Wrestling matches between the young men, in the grass. Races for the children. A game where people try to catch a greased pig, which always escapes. Everyone laughs until they cry. And when it gets dark, a big bonfire is lit. The sparks fly up into the night sky, among the stars.

[pause]

{img:girl-receiving-glass-beads-gift-father-bonfire-light-smiling}
By the light of the bonfire, Wulfric takes out the little pot of glass beads he bought in Hamwic. He gives them to Hild. Honey-coloured beads, shining like little drops of sunshine in the firelight. Hild is speechless. She threads them onto a string at once, and wears them around her neck. Then she runs off to show her friends. Eadgyth smiles at her husband. Cuthwine gives Modor her new antler comb. Modor holds it for a long time, and says nothing at all, but her eyes are shining.

[long-pause]

Late at night, the bonfire burns down to glowing embers. People slowly go home to their beds. The stars are very bright. The air is cool, with the first smell of autumn in it. The harvest is home. The barns are full. And for tonight, nobody in the village has a single worry in the world.

[pause]

The next morning, the village is very quiet. Everyone sleeps a little longer than usual. Even the roosters seem to crow more softly. When people finally get up, they smile at each other across the yards, a little sleepy, a little stiff from the dancing. And then, slowly, the ordinary work of the day begins again. But everyone feels lighter. The harvest is home.

[long-pause]

{img:autumn-anglo-saxon-village-orange-trees-smoke-chimneys-misty-morning}
After the harvest, the year begins to turn toward autumn. The days get shorter. The mornings are misty and cool. The leaves of the trees turn yellow, orange and brown, and begin to fall. The swallows gather on the roofs in long rows, and one morning, they are gone. September has come. The Anglo-Saxons call it Halegmonath, the holy month.

[long-pause]

There is still a lot of work to do. The apples and pears must be picked in the orchard, and stored carefully in the storehouse, on beds of straw, so they do not touch each other and rot. The plums are dried in the sun. The blackberries are picked from the hedges, and eaten fresh or cooked with honey. The hazelnuts are gathered in the woods, and the children crack them by the fire in the evenings.

[pause]

The vegetables in the garden are harvested too. Onions, leeks and garlic are tied in bunches and hung from the roof beams. Beans and peas are dried, to be cooked in soups and stews in winter. Cabbages are left in the ground, because they can survive the frost. And Eadgyth gathers the last herbs, and hangs them up to dry, for cooking and medicine.

[pause]

{img:apples-stored-on-straw-shelves-storehouse-woman-checking-autumn}
In October, with the first full moon, winter begins. The Anglo-Saxons call it Winterfylleth. The cows and sheep are brought in from the far pastures. The firewood is cut and stacked high against the walls of the house. The roof is checked for holes and patched with new straw. The cracks in the walls are filled with fresh clay. Everything must be ready before the cold comes.

[pause]

And there is ploughing again. The fallow field, which rested this year, must be ploughed and sown with wheat, which will grow a little before winter and then wait under the snow until spring. So the oxen are brought out again, and the heavy plough is pulled across the field, and the seed from this year's harvest is scattered into the dark soil. The circle of the year begins again.

[long-pause]

{img:farmer-sowing-seed-by-hand-ploughed-field-autumn-crows-following}
Wulfric sows the seed by hand. He carries a basket of grain on his hip, and walks slowly across the ploughed field, throwing handfuls of seed in wide arcs, left and right. Behind him, Cuthwine pulls a branch of thorns across the soil, to cover the seed with earth. Crows follow them, trying to steal the seed. Cuthwine chases them away, shouting. It is the same game as at harvest time, just at the other end of the year.

[pause]

While Wulfric sows, he says a quiet prayer. It is a mixture of old words and new ones. He asks God to bless the seed, and the earth, and the rain. There is an old Anglo-Saxon prayer for the fields, which begins with the words, Hail to you, earth, mother of men. Some of it is very old, older than the Christian faith. The priest has added the name of God, and of Christ. And so, again, the old and the new walk together.

[pause]

{img:family-indoors-autumn-evening-fire-cracking-nuts-spinning-storm-outside}
In the evenings, the family sits by the fire for longer and longer. Outside, the autumn rain falls and the wind blows. Inside, it is warm and smoky. Modor spins. Eadgyth weaves. Wulfric mends the harness for the oxen. Hild sews her new dress. And Cuthwine cracks hazelnuts, one after another, and eats almost all of them.

[long-pause]

On one of these evenings, Hild asks her father whether girls can learn to read. Wulfric is surprised. He thinks for a long time. Then he says that the nuns at the monastery can read. And that perhaps, one day, if the harvests are good, Hild could go and learn from them. Hild says nothing. But she smiles to herself, and holds the honey-coloured beads around her neck. Outside, the first frost is beginning to sparkle on the grass.

[pause]

In the forest, the autumn colours are beautiful. The beech trees turn copper and gold. The oaks turn brown. Mushrooms grow among the fallen leaves, and Modor knows exactly which ones are good to eat and which ones are dangerous. She takes Hild into the woods to teach her. They come home with baskets full of mushrooms, blackberries and hazelnuts, and with red cheeks from the cool air.

[long-pause]

{img:viking-longships-on-horizon-grey-sea-coast-monastery-cliff-later}
The peaceful years did not last forever. In the year 793, almost a hundred years after our story, Viking ships appeared off the coast of Northumbria. They came from Norway and Denmark, across the North Sea, just as the ancestors of the Anglo-Saxons had once done. They attacked the monastery of Lindisfarne, where the beautiful Gospels had been made. It was the beginning of a long and difficult time for England.

[long-pause]

For almost a hundred years, the Vikings raided the coasts of England, and then began to settle there. They conquered the kingdoms of Northumbria, East Anglia and much of Mercia. Only Wessex, the kingdom of our village, held out. And from Wessex came a remarkable king. His name was Alfred. He lived from 849 to 899. And he is the only king of England who is called the Great.

[pause]

Alfred defended his kingdom against the Vikings, and in the end made peace with them. He built fortified towns, called burhs, all over Wessex, where the people could shelter in times of danger. Many of them became important towns, which still exist today. He also loved learning. He was sad that so few people in England could still read, after the Viking raids had destroyed so many monasteries and their books.

[pause]

{img:king-alfred-reading-book-candle-wooden-hall-thoughtful-later}
So Alfred brought scholars to his court. He had important books translated from Latin into Old English, so that ordinary people could understand them. He even translated some books himself. He started the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, a history of England, written year by year, in English. It was kept up in monasteries for more than two hundred and fifty years.

[pause]

Alfred's grandson, Athelstan, became the first king of the whole of England, in the year 927. So the many small kingdoms of the Anglo-Saxons finally became one country. For about a hundred years more, England was ruled by Anglo-Saxon kings, with a short time of Danish kings in between.

[long-pause]

{img:norman-castle-wooden-motte-bailey-english-countryside-later}
Then, in 1066, everything changed again. William, the Duke of Normandy, in France, crossed the sea with an army, and became king of England. The Normans took the land of the Anglo-Saxon lords, and built great stone castles and cathedrals all over the country. For a long time, the rulers of England spoke French, and the important books were written in Latin.

[pause]

But the ordinary people, in the villages, went on speaking English. They farmed the same fields, in the same way, with the same seasons. They still baked bread at Lammas, and brought in the harvest home, and told stories by the fire. Slowly, over hundreds of years, their Old English language changed, mixing with French words, until it became the English we speak today.

[pause]

{img:medieval-village-later-centuries-same-fields-church-tower-harvest}
So, in a way, the Anglo-Saxon villages never really disappeared. Many English villages today stand in the same places as the Anglo-Saxon ones. Their names are often Anglo-Saxon. A name ending in ham means a home or village. A name ending in ton means a farm. A name ending in ley means a clearing in the woods. And many of the old fields, hedges and paths are still there, if you know where to look.

[long-pause]

As for Wulfric and his family, they were ordinary farmers, and their names were never written down. But people like them lived in thousands of villages across England, for hundreds of years. They grew the bread, wove the cloth and kept the old stories alive. Without them, there would have been no kings, no monasteries and no beautiful books. And the land they farmed is still there, still green, still giving its harvest every August.

[long-pause]

{img:sutton-hoo-burial-mounds-heath-grass-sunset-suffolk-today}
Today, the Anglo-Saxons are still with us, in many ways. Their language lives on in the English we speak. Their villages are still on the map. And every year, archaeologists find new traces of their lives, in the soil of England.

[long-pause]

The most famous Anglo-Saxon discovery was made in 1939, at a place called Sutton Hoo, in the east of England. There, on a low hill near a river, there are some old burial mounds. A local landowner, a woman called Edith Pretty, asked an archaeologist to dig into them. And in the biggest mound, he found something extraordinary. The shape of a great ship, almost thirty metres long, buried in the sand.

[pause]

The wood of the ship had rotted away long ago. But its shape was still there in the sand, with rows of iron rivets, like a ghost of a ship. And in the middle of it, there was a burial chamber, full of treasures. A magnificent helmet, decorated with silver and bronze. A golden belt buckle, covered with twisting animals. Shoulder clasps of gold and red garnets. Silver bowls, drinking horns, a lyre and a sword. It was probably the grave of a king of East Anglia, buried about eighty years before our story.

[pause]

{img:sutton-hoo-helmet-museum-glass-case-soft-light-visitors-today}
The treasures of Sutton Hoo are now in the British Museum, in London. The famous helmet has been carefully put back together, from hundreds of little pieces. It has a face mask, with eyebrows, a nose and a moustache, and a little dragon flying across the top. Visitors come from all over the world to see it. It has become a symbol of the Anglo-Saxons.

[pause]

In 2009, a man with a metal detector found another great treasure, in a field in Staffordshire, in the middle of England. More than three thousand five hundred pieces of gold and silver, mostly from swords and helmets. It is called the Staffordshire Hoard. Nobody knows exactly who buried it, or why. It is the largest Anglo-Saxon treasure ever found.

[long-pause]

{img:reconstructed-anglo-saxon-village-west-stow-thatched-huts-visitors-today}
But perhaps the most touching place to visit is West Stow, in Suffolk. There, archaeologists found the remains of an Anglo-Saxon village, with the post holes of houses and the pits of sunken huts. And on the same spot, they have rebuilt the village, using the same materials and methods as the Anglo-Saxons. There are wooden houses with thatched roofs, sunken huts, a hall, and gardens and fields.

[pause]

Visitors can walk into the houses, sit by the hearth, and see how the people lived. They can smell the woodsmoke and see the loom with its clay weights. Sometimes, people in Anglo-Saxon clothes show how to spin wool, bake bread or forge iron. School children come to spend a day there, learning what life was like in an Anglo-Saxon village. It feels very much like the village of our story.

[pause]

{img:lindisfarne-gospels-display-british-library-open-page-colours-today}
The Lindisfarne Gospels are also kept in London, in the British Library. They are so precious that they are only shown for a little while each time, open at one page, in a dark room, to protect the colours from the light. And beside them, in the same library, is the old scorched book with the story of Beowulf. People stand in front of these books, very quietly, looking at the work of hands that have been still for thirteen hundred years.

[long-pause]

And in many English villages, the old harvest customs still live on. Some churches still hold a Lammas service on the first of August, with a loaf of bread from the new harvest on the altar. In the autumn, almost every church holds a harvest festival, decorated with sheaves of wheat, fruit, vegetables and bread. Children bring food, which is given to people in need. And in some villages, people still make corn dollies from the last sheaf, and hang them up in their kitchens, for luck.

[pause]

Even the old Anglo-Saxon names of the days and the seasons are still with us. Every time we say Wednesday, Thursday or Friday, we remember the old gods of the Anglo-Saxons. Every time we say Easter, we remember the old spring goddess Eostre. And every time someone says harvest, or bread, or home, or welcome, they are speaking words that Wulfric and Eadgyth would have understood, more than thirteen hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:anglo-saxon-village-evening-august-sunset-stooks-in-field-smoke-rising}
So we leave our Anglo-Saxon village tonight, in the year 700, as the evening falls. The sun is going down behind the low hills. Its last golden light is shining across the stubble fields, where the stooks stand in long rows, casting long shadows. The swallows are swooping low over the river. And a soft mist is beginning to rise from the meadows.

[long-pause]

In the village, the smoke is rising from the thatched roofs, straight up into the still evening air. The cows have been milked. The geese have been shut in for the night. The oxen are lying down in their shelter, chewing slowly. And in the doorway of the house, the cat is washing its face, in the last warm patch of sunlight.

[pause]

{img:inside-house-night-fire-glowing-family-asleep-on-benches-blankets}
Inside the house, the fire is burning low. Its red glow lights up the dark roof beams, and the bunches of herbs hanging from them. Modor is sitting by the fire, her spindle finally still in her lap.
