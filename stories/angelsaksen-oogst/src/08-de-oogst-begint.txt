On the morning after Lammas, the harvest begins. Everyone is up before the sun. The whole village goes out to the fields together, men and women, young and old. Only the very youngest children stay behind, with Modor and the other grandmothers. The air is cool and fresh. The dew is still on the grass. And the wheat stands tall and golden, waiting.

--

The tool for the harvest is the sickle. It is a curved iron blade, like a crescent moon, with a short wooden handle. The reaper holds a bunch of wheat stalks in one hand, and cuts them with the sickle in the other, just below the ears. Swish. Swish. It is a soft, steady sound. Then the reaper lays the cut stalks down in a neat pile, and reaches for the next bunch.

The reapers work in a long line across the field, side by side, moving forward slowly together. Wulfric is at one end, with his neighbours. Eadgyth works beside him. Hild is in the line too, with her own small sickle. She has been reaping since she was nine. She is fast and careful. Her mother watches her proudly. She is almost as quick as the grown women now.

{img:line-of-reapers-bending-field-women-men-together-morning-light}
Reaping is hard work. You must bend over all day long, cutting and cutting. Your back aches. Your hands get blisters. The sun gets hot, and the dust from the wheat gets into your eyes and your nose. The stalks are sharp, and can scratch your arms. But the people sing as they work, to keep a steady rhythm, and to make the time pass more quickly. Old work songs, which everyone knows.

They also take short rests. Every few hours, they stop and sit in the shade at the edge of the field. Someone brings a jug of weak ale, or water from the spring, and everyone drinks. They eat bread and cheese. They wipe the sweat from their faces. Then they stand up, stretch their backs, and go back to work.

--

{img:harvest-rest-shade-edge-of-field-people-sharing-bread-jug-of-ale}
Cuthwine has an important job. He walks along the edge of the field with a stick and a little drum, scaring away the birds. The crows and the pigeons love the ripe grain. They circle over the field and try to land among the sheaves. Cuthwine runs at them, shouting and banging his drum. The birds fly up in a great cloud, and land again a little further away. It is a game that never ends. By evening, Cuthwine is hoarse and very tired.

Behind the reapers, there are the binders. They gather up the cut stalks into big bundles, called sheaves. They tie each sheaf around the middle with a few twisted stalks of wheat, like a belt. Then they stand the sheaves up in little groups, leaning against each other, with the ears of grain at the top. These groups are called stooks. They let the wind and sun dry the grain.

{img:stooks-of-sheaves-standing-rows-field-afternoon-shadows}
By the middle of the afternoon, a whole strip has been cut. The field looks completely different. Instead of tall, waving wheat, there is short stubble, and rows and rows of stooks, standing like little golden huts. Swallows swoop low over the stubble, catching insects. A hare runs out of the last uncut corner of the field, and dashes away toward the woods.

--

In the evening, as the sun goes down, the reapers walk home together, their sickles over their shoulders. They are exhausted, dusty and sunburnt. But they are happy. The first day of the harvest is done, and the weather has been kind. At home, Modor has made a big pot of stew, with beans, onions and a little bacon. The family eats in silence, too tired to talk. And then they lie down on their benches, and are asleep before the fire burns low.

At midday, the women of the village bring food out to the field in baskets. Bread, cheese, onions, cold bacon and apples, and big jugs of ale. Everyone sits down together in the shade of an old oak tree at the edge of the field. They eat and talk and laugh. The young men tease each other about who is the fastest reaper. The old men say that in their day, the reapers were much faster. They say that every year.
