After the harvest, the year begins to turn toward autumn. The days get shorter. The mornings are misty and cool. The leaves of the trees turn yellow, orange and brown, and begin to fall. The swallows gather on the roofs in long rows, and one morning, they are gone. September has come. The Anglo-Saxons call it Halegmonath, the holy month.

--

There is still a lot of work to do. The apples and pears must be picked in the orchard, and stored carefully in the storehouse, on beds of straw, so they do not touch each other and rot. The plums are dried in the sun. The blackberries are picked from the hedges, and eaten fresh or cooked with honey. The hazelnuts are gathered in the woods, and the children crack them by the fire in the evenings.

The vegetables in the garden are harvested too. Onions, leeks and garlic are tied in bunches and hung from the roof beams. Beans and peas are dried, to be cooked in soups and stews in winter. Cabbages are left in the ground, because they can survive the frost. And Eadgyth gathers the last herbs, and hangs them up to dry, for cooking and medicine.

{img:apples-stored-on-straw-shelves-storehouse-woman-checking-autumn}
In October, with the first full moon, winter begins. The Anglo-Saxons call it Winterfylleth. The cows and sheep are brought in from the far pastures. The firewood is cut and stacked high against the walls of the house. The roof is checked for holes and patched with new straw. The cracks in the walls are filled with fresh clay. Everything must be ready before the cold comes.

And there is ploughing again. The fallow field, which rested this year, must be ploughed and sown with wheat, which will grow a little before winter and then wait under the snow until spring. So the oxen are brought out again, and the heavy plough is pulled across the field, and the seed from this year's harvest is scattered into the dark soil. The circle of the year begins again.

--

{img:farmer-sowing-seed-by-hand-ploughed-field-autumn-crows-following}
Wulfric sows the seed by hand. He carries a basket of grain on his hip, and walks slowly across the ploughed field, throwing handfuls of seed in wide arcs, left and right. Behind him, Cuthwine pulls a branch of thorns across the soil, to cover the seed with earth. Crows follow them, trying to steal the seed. Cuthwine chases them away, shouting. It is the same game as at harvest time, just at the other end of the year.

While Wulfric sows, he says a quiet prayer. It is a mixture of old words and new ones. He asks God to bless the seed, and the earth, and the rain. There is an old Anglo-Saxon prayer for the fields, which begins with the words, Hail to you, earth, mother of men. Some of it is very old, older than the Christian faith. The priest has added the name of God, and of Christ. And so, again, the old and the new walk together.

{img:family-indoors-autumn-evening-fire-cracking-nuts-spinning-storm-outside}
In the evenings, the family sits by the fire for longer and longer. Outside, the autumn rain falls and the wind blows. Inside, it is warm and smoky. Modor spins. Eadgyth weaves. Wulfric mends the harness for the oxen. Hild sews her new dress. And Cuthwine cracks hazelnuts, one after another, and eats almost all of them.

--

On one of these evenings, Hild asks her father whether girls can learn to read. Wulfric is surprised. He thinks for a long time. Then he says that the nuns at the monastery can read. And that perhaps, one day, if the harvests are good, Hild could go and learn from them. Hild says nothing. But she smiles to herself, and holds the honey-coloured beads around her neck. Outside, the first frost is beginning to sparkle on the grass.

In the forest, the autumn colours are beautiful. The beech trees turn copper and gold. The oaks turn brown. Mushrooms grow among the fallen leaves, and Modor knows exactly which ones are good to eat and which ones are dangerous. She takes Hild into the woods to teach her. They come home with baskets full of mushrooms, blackberries and hazelnuts, and with red cheeks from the cool air.
