The harvest goes on for about three weeks. Field after field is cut, bound and carried home. Wheat first, then barley, then oats. And after each field is cleared, the gleaners come. Gleaning means picking up the ears of grain that have fallen on the ground, and were left behind by the reapers.

--

Gleaning is usually done by the women and children, and by the poorest people of the village. They walk slowly across the stubble field, bending down to pick up every stray ear of wheat. They put them in their aprons or in little bags. It is slow work. But by the end of the day, a good gleaner may have collected enough grain for a few loaves of bread. For a poor widow, that can make a big difference in winter.

There is an old rule about gleaning, from the Bible. It says that farmers should not gather every last grain from their fields. They should leave some for the poor, the widow and the stranger. The priest reminds the villagers of this every year. And the farmers of our village are generous. Wulfric always tells his reapers not to be too careful at the edges of the field.

{img:old-widow-gleaning-with-girl-helping-her-sunset-stubble}
Hild goes gleaning with an old widow from the village, called Wynflaed. Her husband died many years ago, and she has no children. She lives alone in a small house at the edge of the village. She is too old to work in the fields. So Hild helps her glean, and carries her bag home for her. Wynflaed is grateful. She tells Hild stories as they work, about the old days when she was young.

Then comes the most special moment of the harvest. The cutting of the last sheaf. When the very last strip of the very last field is being cut, the whole village gathers round to watch. There is an old belief that the spirit of the harvest lives in the grain. As the reapers cut, the spirit runs before them, hiding in the grain that is left. And so, at the end, the spirit is hiding in the very last sheaf.

--

{img:villagers-gathered-around-last-sheaf-standing-field-sunset-ceremony}
In some villages, the reapers take turns throwing their sickles at the last bunch of standing wheat, from a distance, so that nobody knows who cut it. In other villages, the oldest reaper cuts it. In our village, the honour goes to the youngest reaper. This year, that is Hild. Everyone stands around in a circle and watches her. She takes her sickle, and with one careful stroke, she cuts the last bunch of wheat.

Everyone cheers. The last sheaf is lifted up high. The women take it and twist and braid it into a special shape, like a little figure, or a doll, with a body and arms and a head. It is decorated with ribbons and wild flowers. In later times, this figure was called a corn dolly, or a harvest maiden. It is carried home in a procession, on top of the last cart, with everyone singing behind it.

{img:corn-dolly-braided-straw-figure-ribbons-flowers-hanging-hearth}
The corn dolly is hung up in the house, above the hearth, or in the barn. It stays there all winter. People believe it keeps the spirit of the harvest safe, through the cold months. And in the spring, when the first field is ploughed, the corn dolly is taken down and ploughed into the soil. So the spirit of the harvest goes back into the earth, to make the new crops grow.

--

The priest does not entirely approve of these old customs. They are much older than the Christian faith, and some of them come from the old gods. But he does not forbid them either. He is a wise man, and he knows that the people love them. So, like many priests in those days, he simply adds a prayer to the old custom. And the old and the new live on together, side by side, in the little villages of England.
