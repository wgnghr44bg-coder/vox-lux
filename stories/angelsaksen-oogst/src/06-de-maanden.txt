The Anglo-Saxons had their own names for the months of the year. We know them thanks to a monk called Bede, who lived in a monastery in the north of England, in the time of our story. He was very interested in time and calendars. And he wrote down the old English names of the months, and what they meant.

--

The names tell us a lot about the lives of the people. January shared its name with December, Giuli, the time of Yule. Much later, people also called it the wolf month, because in the cold of winter, hungry wolves came close to the villages. February was called Solmonath, the mud month, or perhaps the month of cakes, which people offered to the gods. March was Hrethmonath, named after a goddess. And April was Eosturmonath, named after a goddess of spring called Eostre. Her name lives on in our word Easter.

May was called Thrimilchi, the month of three milkings, because the grass was so rich in May that the cows could be milked three times a day. June and July were called Litha, the mild months, the time of long, light evenings. August was called Weodmonath, the weed month, because the weeds grew so fast in the warm weather. And September was Halegmonath, the holy month, when people gave thanks for the harvest.

{img:cows-in-lush-may-meadow-woman-milking-wooden-bucket-morning}
October was called Winterfylleth, because winter began with the first full moon of October. November was Blotmonath, the month of sacrifice. In the old days, this was the time when animals were killed, because there was not enough food to feed them all through the winter. Their meat was salted and smoked, to last until spring. And December was called Giuli, the time of Yule, the great midwinter feast.

For Bede, the year really had two halves. The summer half, when the days are longer than the nights. And the winter half, when the nights are longer than the days. The Anglo-Saxons felt this very strongly. In a world without electric light, the length of the day decided almost everything. When to get up, when to work, when to sleep. In summer, they worked from four in the morning until nine at night. In winter, the days were short, and the nights were very long.

--

{img:monk-bede-writing-at-desk-monastery-window-candle-quill-calendar}
Bede himself is a wonderful person. In the year 700, he is about twenty-seven years old. He lives in a monastery at Jarrow, in Northumbria, far to the north of our village. He has lived there since he was seven years old. He spends his days praying, teaching and writing. He writes about history, about the Bible, about science and about time. He is one of the most learned men in all of Europe.

Bede is the first person to use the system of counting years from the birth of Christ, in a big history book. Because of him, this system became popular all over Europe. So, in a way, the very year 700 that we use for our story is a gift from Bede. He also wrote the first history of the English people. Without him, we would know much less about the Anglo-Saxons.

{img:village-people-looking-at-sky-evening-weather-signs-clouds-fields}
The people of our village cannot read or write, of course. They do not know Bede. But they know the months and the seasons just as well as he does, from their own lives. They know the signs of the sky and the earth. When the swallows fly low, rain is coming. When the oak leaves come out before the ash leaves, the summer will be dry. When the grain is hard between the teeth, it is time to cut it.

--

Now it is Weodmonath, the weed month. The days are still long and warm, but they are getting a little shorter. The evenings come a little earlier. In the mornings, there is dew on the grass, and sometimes mist on the river. The wheat is golden. And everyone in the village knows that the most important time of the year has come.
