Before the Anglo-Saxons came, Britain was part of the Roman Empire. For almost four hundred years, the Romans ruled the island. They built towns with stone houses, straight roads, bath houses and grand country villas. But around the year 410, the Roman soldiers left Britain, to defend other parts of their crumbling empire. And they never came back.

--

After the Romans left, Britain changed. The towns slowly emptied. The villas fell into ruin. The people who lived there, the Britons, had to look after themselves. And across the North Sea, new people began to arrive in their boats. They were the Angles, the Saxons and the Jutes, from the coasts of what are now Germany, Denmark and the Netherlands. Some came as soldiers, hired to help defend the land. Others came as farmers, looking for good soil.

Over the next two hundred years, more and more of them came. They settled in the east and south of Britain, and slowly spread west. Sometimes there were wars between them and the Britons. Sometimes they lived side by side, and married each other. Over time, the people of the east and south began to speak Old English, and to call themselves English. The land was later named after the Angles. England means the land of the Angles.

{img:anglo-saxon-boats-arriving-shore-sea-cliffs-families-early-settlers}
By the year 700, England is not one country. It is divided into several kingdoms, each with its own king. In the north, there is Northumbria. In the middle, there is Mercia. In the east, there is East Anglia. In the southeast, there is Kent. And in the south and west, there is Wessex, the kingdom of the West Saxons. Our village lies in Wessex.

The king of Wessex is called Ine. He is a strong and sensible king. He has written down a set of laws for his people. The laws tell people how to settle quarrels, what to pay if they damage someone's crops, and how to keep their fences in good repair. One law says that a farmer who does not fence his field properly cannot complain if his neighbour's cattle eat his grain. They are practical laws, for a land of farmers.

--

{img:king-ine-wessex-wooden-hall-advisers-reading-laws-candlelight}
When the Anglo-Saxons first came, they believed in the old gods of the north. Woden, the wise one-eyed god. Thunor, the god of thunder. Frige, the goddess of love and the home. Some of these names still live on in the days of our week. Wednesday is Woden's day. Thursday is Thunor's day. And Friday is Frige's day.

But about a hundred years before our story, missionaries came from Rome and from Ireland, to teach the Christian faith. Slowly, the kings and their people became Christians. By the year 700, most people in England are Christians. Churches and monasteries are being built all over the land. But many of the old customs and beliefs still live on, mixed with the new faith, especially in the countryside.

{img:small-stone-church-anglo-saxon-village-yew-tree-evening-light}
The Anglo-Saxons were not only farmers. They were also wonderful craftsmen. They made beautiful jewellery of gold and garnets, finely decorated swords and helmets, and delicate glass cups. They were poets and storytellers, who loved long songs about heroes and monsters. And in their monasteries, monks were making some of the most beautiful books the world has ever seen.

--

But tonight, we are not going to the courts of kings. We are going to a small village, where ordinary people live ordinary lives. They are the people who grew the food that fed the whole kingdom. And in early August, they are getting ready to bring in the harvest.
