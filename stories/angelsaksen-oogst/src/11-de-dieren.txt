A farm in Anglo-Saxon England is full of animals. Every family has some, and they are very important. The animals pull the plough, give milk, wool, eggs and meat, and their dung makes the fields fertile. The family knows every animal by name, and looks after them carefully.

--

The most valuable animals are the oxen. Wulfric's two oxen are called Brun and Hwita, which means Brown and White. They are big, strong and gentle animals, with long horns. They pull the plough in spring and the cart at harvest time. Without them, Wulfric could not farm his land. He talks to them while they work, and gives them extra hay in winter. Hild loves them, and brushes their coats on Sundays.

The two cows give milk. Eadgyth milks them every morning and evening, sitting on a little three-legged stool, with a wooden bucket. From the milk, she makes butter, by shaking the cream in a wooden churn until it turns solid. She makes soft cheese, and hard cheese which keeps for months. The milk that is left over is drunk by the children, or given to the pigs.

{img:woman-churning-butter-wooden-churn-doorway-cat-watching}
The sheep are kept mostly for their wool. In early summer, they are washed in the river and then sheared, with big iron shears. The wool is cleaned, combed and spun into thread. Most of the clothes of the family are made from this wool. The sheep also give milk, which makes a strong, tasty cheese. And their dung is good for the fields. Sheep are so important that the wool of England will one day make the country rich.

The pigs are the cheapest animals to keep. They eat almost anything. Scraps from the kitchen, roots, worms and the acorns in the forest. In the autumn, Cuthwine takes them into the forest every day, to eat the fallen acorns and beechnuts. They grow fat and round. And in November, the month of sacrifice, some of them will be killed and turned into bacon, ham and sausages for the winter.

--

{img:boy-herding-pigs-forest-edge-autumn-oak-trees-acorns}
The geese are Hild's special friends. There are twelve of them, white and grey, with orange beaks. They follow her around the yard, honking. Geese are very useful. They give eggs, meat and soft feathers for pillows. Their big wing feathers are used to make arrows, and also writing quills for the monks. And they are wonderful guards. When a stranger comes near the house, the geese make so much noise that the whole village hears it.

The chickens scratch around the yard, looking for seeds and insects. They lay eggs, though not as many as modern chickens. And there is a cat, a big grey cat with green eyes, who catches the mice in the storehouse. Mice are a great danger to the stored grain, so a good cat is very valuable. Cats were so useful that, in Wales a little later, the law even said what you had to pay if you killed someone's cat.

{img:bee-skeps-straw-hives-garden-bees-flying-flowers-summer}
And there are the bees. Eadgyth has three beehives, made of coiled straw, shaped like little domes. They stand in a corner of the garden, under the apple tree. Bees are very precious. Honey is the only sweetener the Anglo-Saxons have. They use it in cooking, in medicine, and above all to make mead, a strong, sweet drink made from honey and water. The wax is used to make candles, which are very expensive.

--

Modor says that you must always tell the bees the news. If someone in the family is born, or married, or dies, you must go to the hives and tell the bees. Otherwise, they will be offended, and fly away. It is an old, old custom. Some country people in England still do it today. Every evening, Hild goes to the hives and tells the bees about the day. She tells them about the harvest, and the storm, and the last sheaf. The bees hum softly, as if they are listening.
