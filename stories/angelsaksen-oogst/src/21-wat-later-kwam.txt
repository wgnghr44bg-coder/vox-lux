The peaceful years did not last forever. In the year 793, almost a hundred years after our story, Viking ships appeared off the coast of Northumbria. They came from Norway and Denmark, across the North Sea, just as the ancestors of the Anglo-Saxons had once done. They attacked the monastery of Lindisfarne, where the beautiful Gospels had been made. It was the beginning of a long and difficult time for England.

--

For almost a hundred years, the Vikings raided the coasts of England, and then began to settle there. They conquered the kingdoms of Northumbria, East Anglia and much of Mercia. Only Wessex, the kingdom of our village, held out. And from Wessex came a remarkable king. His name was Alfred. He lived from 849 to 899. And he is the only king of England who is called the Great.

Alfred defended his kingdom against the Vikings, and in the end made peace with them. He built fortified towns, called burhs, all over Wessex, where the people could shelter in times of danger. Many of them became important towns, which still exist today. He also loved learning. He was sad that so few people in England could still read, after the Viking raids had destroyed so many monasteries and their books.

{img:king-alfred-reading-book-candle-wooden-hall-thoughtful-later}
So Alfred brought scholars to his court. He had important books translated from Latin into Old English, so that ordinary people could understand them. He even translated some books himself. He started the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, a history of England, written year by year, in English. It was kept up in monasteries for more than two hundred and fifty years.

Alfred's grandson, Athelstan, became the first king of the whole of England, in the year 927. So the many small kingdoms of the Anglo-Saxons finally became one country. For about a hundred years more, England was ruled by Anglo-Saxon kings, with a short time of Danish kings in between.

--

{img:norman-castle-wooden-motte-bailey-english-countryside-later}
Then, in 1066, everything changed again. William, the Duke of Normandy, in France, crossed the sea with an army, and became king of England. The Normans took the land of the Anglo-Saxon lords, and built great stone castles and cathedrals all over the country. For a long time, the rulers of England spoke French, and the important books were written in Latin.

But the ordinary people, in the villages, went on speaking English. They farmed the same fields, in the same way, with the same seasons. They still baked bread at Lammas, and brought in the harvest home, and told stories by the fire. Slowly, over hundreds of years, their Old English language changed, mixing with French words, until it became the English we speak today.

{img:medieval-village-later-centuries-same-fields-church-tower-harvest}
So, in a way, the Anglo-Saxon villages never really disappeared. Many English villages today stand in the same places as the Anglo-Saxon ones. Their names are often Anglo-Saxon. A name ending in ham means a home or village. A name ending in ton means a farm. A name ending in ley means a clearing in the woods. And many of the old fields, hedges and paths are still there, if you know where to look.

--

As for Wulfric and his family, they were ordinary farmers, and their names were never written down. But people like them lived in thousands of villages across England, for hundreds of years. They grew the bread, wove the cloth and kept the old stories alive. Without them, there would have been no kings, no monasteries and no beautiful books. And the land they farmed is still there, still green, still giving its harvest every August.
