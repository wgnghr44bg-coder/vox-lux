The stories that the scop sings are very old. Many of them were brought across the sea by the ancestors of the Anglo-Saxons, hundreds of years ago. They were never written down. They were passed on from singer to singer, by memory. A good scop knows thousands of lines by heart. And each time he sings, he changes the story a little, to fit his audience.

--

One of the most famous stories of the Anglo-Saxons is about a hero called Beowulf. Nobody knows exactly when it was first made, but many people think it was around the time of our story. It tells about a great hall in Denmark, where a king holds feasts, just like Lord Aelfric. But every night, a terrible monster comes out of the dark marshes and attacks the hall. Then a young hero comes across the sea, to help. His name is Beowulf.

We will not tell the battles in the story, for they are rather fierce. But the story is also full of quiet, beautiful moments. The joy of the feast in the hall. The sound of the harp. The gold that the king gives to his friends. The ships sailing over the waves, which the poet calls the whale road. And at the end, the hero, grown old, thinking about his long life, as the evening falls.

{img:viking-style-ship-sailing-grey-sea-waves-whale-road-poetry}
The Anglo-Saxons loved a special way of speaking in their poems. Instead of saying the sea, they said the whale road, or the swan road. Instead of saying a ship, they said a sea horse, or a wave floater. The sun was the candle of the sky. A sword was the light of battle. The body was the bone house. These little poetic names are called kennings. People loved to hear new ones, and to guess what they meant.

They also loved riddles. In the evenings, around the fire, people asked each other riddles. A scop might say, I am a wonderful thing. I live in the water, and I cannot drown. When I come to the land, I die. Everyone thinks. What is it? The answer is ice. Or, I have no tongue, but I tell many stories. I have no feet, but I travel far. The answer is a book.

--

{img:family-by-fire-telling-riddles-children-laughing-evening-house}
At home, in the evenings, Modor tells riddles too. Cuthwine loves them, although he almost never guesses the answer. Hild is better at it. She thinks for a long time, frowning, and then suddenly shouts out the answer. Modor laughs and claps her hands. Then she tells another one, harder this time. The fire crackles. The cat purrs. And outside, the autumn wind blows through the trees.

Modor also tells old stories, much older than Beowulf. Stories about the elves who live in the hills and the woods, and who can cause sickness if you anger them. About the dwarfs who make magical things in their workshops under the mountains. About Weland the smith, the greatest craftsman who ever lived, who made wings for himself and flew away from his enemies. The children listen, half afraid and half delighted.

{img:misty-hill-at-dusk-old-burial-mound-trees-mysterious-quiet}
Near the village, there is an old burial mound, a low round hill covered with grass and bushes. It is very old, much older than the village. Modor says that a great king of long ago is buried there, with his treasure. And that a dragon guards it. Cuthwine does not believe in dragons, he says. But he does not go near the mound after dark.

--

Many of these old stories were later written down by monks, in beautiful books. The story of Beowulf survives in only one old book, which is kept today in the British Library in London. The book was almost destroyed in a fire, about three hundred years ago. Its pages are scorched at the edges. But the story survived. And now, more than a thousand years later, people all over the world still read about Beowulf, and the great hall, and the whale road.
