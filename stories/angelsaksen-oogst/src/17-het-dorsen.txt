When all the sheaves are safely home in the barns, the next work begins. The grain must be separated from the straw. This is called threshing. It is done in the barn, on a hard, clean floor, in the weeks after the harvest. And it goes on, on rainy days, all through the autumn and winter.

--

The tool for threshing is the flail. It is made of two wooden sticks, joined together by a short leather strap. One stick is long, as a handle. The other is short and thick. The thresher swings the long handle over his head, and the short stick swings round and comes down, thwack, on the sheaves lying on the floor. The blows knock the grains out of the ears.

Threshing is hard, dusty work. Usually two or three men thresh together, standing in a circle. They swing their flails in turn, one after another, in a steady rhythm. Thwack, thwack, thwack. The sound can be heard all over the village, on autumn days. It is a good sound. It means there is grain. The men must be careful to keep the rhythm, or the flails will bang into each other.

{img:winnowing-woman-tossing-grain-basket-wind-chaff-blowing-barn-door}
After threshing, the floor is covered with a mixture of grain, broken straw and chaff. Chaff is the dry husk around each grain. Now the grain must be cleaned. This is called winnowing. On a windy day, the barn doors are opened on both sides, so the wind blows right through. Eadgyth takes a wide, flat basket, fills it with the mixture, and tosses it up into the air.

The heavy grains fall straight back down into the basket. But the light chaff is caught by the wind, and blows away, in little golden clouds. Again and again, she tosses the grain, until it is clean. It is a lovely thing to watch. The golden chaff drifting out of the barn door, glittering in the sunlight, like snow. The chickens rush over to peck at it.

--

{img:grain-stored-in-clay-pots-wooden-bins-storehouse-dim-light}
The clean grain is stored carefully. Some of it is kept in big clay pots with lids. Some in wooden chests or bins. It must be kept dry, and safe from mice and rats. That is why the cat is so important. The best grain is set aside as seed, for planting next spring. That grain is never eaten, even in the hungriest winter. Without seed, there would be no harvest next year.

The straw is also precious. It is used for many things. For new thatch on the roofs. For bedding for the animals. For stuffing mattresses. For weaving baskets and beehives. For making fires. Nothing is wasted. Even the chaff is gathered up and fed to the animals, or mixed with clay for the walls of houses.

{img:watermill-anglo-saxon-wooden-wheel-river-miller-sacks-flour}
To make flour, the grain must be ground. For everyday use, Eadgyth grinds a little every morning with the quern stones at home. It is slow, tiring work. But for larger amounts, there is something new. On the river, a short walk from the village, there is a watermill. It is a small wooden building, with a big wooden wheel turned by the flowing water. Inside, the wheel turns a heavy millstone, which grinds the grain much faster than any person could.

--

Watermills were quite new in England in the year 700. They were expensive to build, so usually a lord or a monastery owned them. The farmers brought their grain to the mill, and the miller ground it for them, keeping a small part of the flour as his payment. Cuthwine loves the mill. He loves the splashing of the water, the creaking of the wheel and the deep rumble of the stones. And the miller always gives him a handful of flour to throw into the air, like a cloud.

On rainy autumn days, when there is no work in the fields, the whole family helps with the threshing. The barn is warm and dry, and full of the sweet smell of straw. Rain drums on the roof. The flails thump in a steady rhythm. Chaff floats in the light from the open door. And the pile of clean, golden grain slowly grows bigger and bigger, as the days go by.
