In one of the houses of the village lives a family of farmers. The father is called Wulfric. He is about thirty-five years old, tall and broad, with a fair beard and a calm face. He is a free farmer, what the Anglo-Saxons called a ceorl. He owns his own oxen, and works his own strips of land in the village fields. He also owes some work and some food to the local lord, every year.

--

The mother is called Eadgyth. She is a strong, practical woman, with long braids under a linen headscarf. She runs the house. She cooks, bakes and brews. She spins wool and weaves cloth. She looks after the vegetable garden, the chickens and the geese. And at harvest time, she works in the fields beside her husband, from dawn till dusk. She knows how to do almost everything.

The oldest child is a girl called Hild. She is twelve years old. She has her mother's braids and her father's calm face. She already helps with everything in the house and the fields. She can spin and weave, milk the cows, bake bread and cut grain with a sickle. She is quick and clever, and very good with animals. The geese follow her around wherever she goes.

{img:girl-twelve-anglo-saxon-braids-geese-following-her-farmyard-morning}
The youngest child is a boy called Cuthwine. He is eight years old, with a round face and messy hair that never stays tidy. He is full of energy, and full of questions. Why is the sky blue? Where do the swallows go in winter? Why does the river never stop? His job is to look after the pigs, and to scare the birds away from the grain. He is not always very good at either.

And there is the grandmother, Wulfric's mother. Everyone calls her Modor, which means mother. She is very old, perhaps sixty, which is a great age in those days. She cannot work in the fields any more. But she spins wool all day, sitting by the door. She looks after the little children of the village. And she knows all the old stories, the old songs and the old herbs for every illness.

--

{img:old-grandmother-spinning-wool-drop-spindle-doorway-sunlight}
The family is not rich, but they are not poor either. They have a pair of oxen for ploughing, two cows, a few pigs, some sheep and a flock of geese and chickens. They have their house, their garden and their strips of land. In a good year, they have enough to eat, and a little left over to sell. In a bad year, they must be careful, and hope that winter is short.

The family works together, all year round. Everyone has their tasks, even little Cuthwine. But they also have time for each other. In the evenings, they sit around the fire and talk. Wulfric mends tools. Eadgyth sews. Modor tells stories. Hild plays a game with little stones on the floor with her brother. And the fire crackles, and the smoke drifts up into the dark roof.

{img:family-around-central-hearth-evening-firelight-anglo-saxon-house}
Their neighbours are like a big family too. In a village of ten houses, everyone knows everyone. They help each other at harvest time, and when someone is sick, and when a new house must be built. They share the big plough, and the oxen, and the work of the common fields. They quarrel sometimes, as neighbours do. But when there is trouble, they stand together.

--

On this August evening, the talk around the fire is all about the harvest. Wulfric has walked through the fields that afternoon. He has rubbed some ears of wheat between his hands, and bitten the grains. They are hard and dry. The wheat is ready. Tomorrow, he says, we will cut the first of it. And Eadgyth will bake the first loaf of the new harvest.

The family also has a dog, a big, shaggy, grey dog called Gifre, which means greedy. He got his name as a puppy, when he stole a whole loaf of bread from the table. He helps Wulfric with the sheep and the cows, and guards the house at night. In the evenings, he lies by the fire with his head on Cuthwine's feet, and sighs deeply, as if he too has had a very long day.
