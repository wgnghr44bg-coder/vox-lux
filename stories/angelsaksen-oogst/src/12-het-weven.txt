In the evenings during the harvest, and through the long autumn and winter, Eadgyth weaves. Weaving is one of the most important jobs of an Anglo-Saxon woman. Every piece of cloth that the family wears, sleeps under or uses in the house, has been spun and woven by hand. It takes an enormous amount of time.

--

First, the wool must be spun into thread. For this, the women use a drop spindle. It is a simple wooden stick, with a little round weight at the bottom, called a whorl. The spinner pulls a little wool from a bundle, twists it, and lets the spindle hang and spin. The spinning twists the wool into a thin, strong thread. Women spin all day long, whenever their hands are free. While they walk, while they talk, while they watch the children.

Modor spins more than anyone. She sits by the door all day, with her spindle turning and turning. Her fingers move without her even looking. She has spun so much thread in her long life that, if you laid it all out in a line, it would probably reach around the whole of England. Hild is learning from her. Her thread is still a bit lumpy. But it is getting better.

{img:grandmother-teaching-girl-drop-spindle-wool-thread-doorway}
The loom stands in the sunken hut. It is a big upright frame of wood, taller than a person, leaning against the wall. The long threads of the warp hang down from the top beam. At the bottom, they are held tight by rows of heavy clay rings, called loom weights. The weaver stands in front of the loom, and passes the other thread, the weft, back and forth between the hanging threads. Then she pushes it up tight with a flat wooden sword.

It is slow and patient work. A piece of cloth big enough for a tunic can take weeks to weave. Eadgyth weaves plain cloth for everyday clothes. But she also knows how to weave patterns, like diamonds and stripes. For special clothes, she weaves narrow coloured bands, called tablet weaving, to decorate the edges of dresses and tunics. These bands are beautiful, with tiny patterns in red, blue and yellow.

--

{img:tablet-weaving-colourful-band-patterns-hands-wooden-tablets}
The colours come from plants. Woad, a plant with yellow flowers, makes blue. Madder, a plant with red roots, makes red. Weld, a tall weed, makes yellow. Onion skins make orange and brown. Eadgyth grows some of these plants in her garden, and gathers others from the fields and hedges. She boils the wool in big pots with the plants, until it takes the colour. Her hands are often stained blue or red for days.

The Anglo-Saxons love colour. Their clothes are not dull and brown, as people sometimes think. The rich wear bright reds, blues and greens, with gold thread and embroidery. Even ordinary farmers have some coloured clothes, for feast days. Wulfric has a blue tunic. Eadgyth has a red dress. And Hild is making herself a little band of tablet weaving, red and yellow, to decorate her new dress for the harvest feast.

{img:anglo-saxon-woman-red-dress-brooches-beads-feast-day-sunlight}
Women also wear jewellery. Eadgyth has two round bronze brooches, which she wears on her shoulders to hold up her dress. Between them hangs a string of glass and amber beads. These beads came from her own mother. Some of them are very old. Hild will have them one day. Rich women wear brooches of gold and silver, decorated with red garnets. But even simple bronze brooches are made with great skill.

--

Anglo-Saxon cloth was famous across Europe. English embroidery, in particular, became so admired that it was later sent to kings and popes in faraway lands. But in our village, the weaving is simply part of everyday life. In the evening light, the soft thump of the loom weights, the click of the wooden sword and the quiet humming of Eadgyth as she works, are among the most familiar sounds of home.

Sometimes, the women of the village gather in one house to spin and weave together. They bring their spindles and their work, and sit around the fire, talking and singing. The time passes much faster that way. They share news and stories, give each other advice, and help each other with difficult patterns. The little girls sit with them and learn, and the little boys play on the floor, until they are sent outside.
