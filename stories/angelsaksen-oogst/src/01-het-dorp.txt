Tonight we travel back more than thirteen hundred years, to the year 700. We go to the south of England, to a small village by a slow river. It is early August. The fields around the village are golden with ripe wheat and barley. And the people of the village are getting ready for the most important weeks of their whole year. The harvest.

--

It is early morning. A soft mist lies over the river meadows. The sun is just coming up over the low hills, and its light turns the mist pink and gold. Swallows are flying low over the water. A cow is lowing somewhere. And from the thatched roofs of the little wooden houses, thin lines of smoke are rising straight up into the still air. The fires are being lit for breakfast.

The village is small. There are about ten houses, made of wood, with steep roofs of straw. They stand around a little open space, with a well in the middle. Around the houses, there are gardens with vegetables and herbs, and small fenced yards for animals. Beyond the gardens, the great open fields stretch away on all sides, full of ripe grain, swaying gently in the morning breeze.

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

--

{img:anglo-saxon-family-doorway-morning-light-looking-at-ripe-fields}
The people who live here are Anglo-Saxons. Their ancestors came across the sea to Britain, about two hundred and fifty years ago, from the lands that are now Germany, Denmark and the Netherlands. They speak a language called Old English. It sounds strange to us today, but many of our everyday words come from it. Words like bread, and field, and harvest, and home.

The Anglo-Saxons are farmers, above all. Almost everyone lives in small villages like this one, and works the land. Their lives follow the rhythm of the seasons. Ploughing in the spring, weeding in the early summer, harvest in the late summer, and the long, dark winter, when everyone waits for the light to come back. And of all these seasons, the harvest is the one that matters most.

{img:father-mother-children-anglo-saxon-walking-to-field-sickles-baskets}
Tonight, we will spend the harvest season with one family in this village. There is a father and a mother, a girl of twelve, a little boy of eight and an old grandmother. We will see their house, their animals and their fields. We will watch them cut the grain with sickles, and bind it into sheaves, and carry it home. We will see them bake the first bread of the new harvest, and take it to the church to be blessed.

--

We will also visit the great hall of the local lord, where a poet sings old stories by the fire. We will visit a little church, and a monastery, where monks are writing books by hand. We will see the bees and the geese, the loom and the mill. And at the end of the harvest, we will join the whole village at a great feast, to celebrate.

{img:sheaves-of-wheat-standing-in-stubble-field-late-summer-golden-light}
Life in an Anglo-Saxon village was not easy. People worked very hard, and they depended on the weather and the soil. A bad harvest meant a hungry winter. But it was also a life full of small joys. Fresh bread, warm fires, old songs, good neighbours and the beauty of the countryside. So let us begin, on a misty August morning, long ago, in a little village by the river, where the golden fields are waiting.

Down by the river, a heron stands perfectly still in the shallow water, waiting for a fish. A woman comes down the path with two wooden buckets, to fetch water. She stops for a moment to look at the fields, shading her eyes against the rising sun. Then she smiles, fills her buckets, and walks back up to the village. It is going to be a fine, dry day. Perfect weather for the harvest.
