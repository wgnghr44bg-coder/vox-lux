Today, the Anglo-Saxons are still with us, in many ways. Their language lives on in the English we speak. Their villages are still on the map. And every year, archaeologists find new traces of their lives, in the soil of England.

--

The most famous Anglo-Saxon discovery was made in 1939, at a place called Sutton Hoo, in the east of England. There, on a low hill near a river, there are some old burial mounds. A local landowner, a woman called Edith Pretty, asked an archaeologist to dig into them. And in the biggest mound, he found something extraordinary. The shape of a great ship, almost thirty metres long, buried in the sand.

The wood of the ship had rotted away long ago. But its shape was still there in the sand, with rows of iron rivets, like a ghost of a ship. And in the middle of it, there was a burial chamber, full of treasures. A magnificent helmet, decorated with silver and bronze. A golden belt buckle, covered with twisting animals. Shoulder clasps of gold and red garnets. Silver bowls, drinking horns, a lyre and a sword. It was probably the grave of a king of East Anglia, buried about eighty years before our story.

{img:sutton-hoo-helmet-museum-glass-case-soft-light-visitors-today}
The treasures of Sutton Hoo are now in the British Museum, in London. The famous helmet has been carefully put back together, from hundreds of little pieces. It has a face mask, with eyebrows, a nose and a moustache, and a little dragon flying across the top. Visitors come from all over the world to see it. It has become a symbol of the Anglo-Saxons.

In 2009, a man with a metal detector found another great treasure, in a field in Staffordshire, in the middle of England. More than three thousand five hundred pieces of gold and silver, mostly from swords and helmets. It is called the Staffordshire Hoard. Nobody knows exactly who buried it, or why. It is the largest Anglo-Saxon treasure ever found.

--

{img:reconstructed-anglo-saxon-village-west-stow-thatched-huts-visitors-today}
But perhaps the most touching place to visit is West Stow, in Suffolk. There, archaeologists found the remains of an Anglo-Saxon village, with the post holes of houses and the pits of sunken huts. And on the same spot, they have rebuilt the village, using the same materials and methods as the Anglo-Saxons. There are wooden houses with thatched roofs, sunken huts, a hall, and gardens and fields.

Visitors can walk into the houses, sit by the hearth, and see how the people lived. They can smell the woodsmoke and see the loom with its clay weights. Sometimes, people in Anglo-Saxon clothes show how to spin wool, bake bread or forge iron. School children come to spend a day there, learning what life was like in an Anglo-Saxon village. It feels very much like the village of our story.

{img:lindisfarne-gospels-display-british-library-open-page-colours-today}
The Lindisfarne Gospels are also kept in London, in the British Library. They are so precious that they are only shown for a little while each time, open at one page, in a dark room, to protect the colours from the light. And beside them, in the same library, is the old scorched book with the story of Beowulf. People stand in front of these books, very quietly, looking at the work of hands that have been still for thirteen hundred years.

--

And in many English villages, the old harvest customs still live on. Some churches still hold a Lammas service on the first of August, with a loaf of bread from the new harvest on the altar. In the autumn, almost every church holds a harvest festival, decorated with sheaves of wheat, fruit, vegetables and bread. Children bring food, which is given to people in need. And in some villages, people still make corn dollies from the last sheaf, and hang them up in their kitchens, for luck.

Even the old Anglo-Saxon names of the days and the seasons are still with us. Every time we say Wednesday, Thursday or Friday, we remember the old gods of the Anglo-Saxons. Every time we say Easter, we remember the old spring goddess Eostre. And every time someone says harvest, or bread, or home, or welcome, they are speaking words that Wulfric and Eadgyth would have understood, more than thirteen hundred years ago.
