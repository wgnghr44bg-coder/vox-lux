A few kilometres from the village, on a low hill, stands the hall of the local lord. He is a thegn, a nobleman who serves the king of Wessex. He owns a lot of land, including the land around our village. The villagers owe him some work and some food every year. In return, he protects them, settles their quarrels, and holds a great feast for them after the harvest.

--

The hall is the biggest building for many kilometres. It is long and tall, made of thick oak posts, with a high roof of straw. The doorposts are carved with twisting animals and patterns, painted in red and black. Inside, there is one huge room, with a long hearth down the middle. Around the walls, there are benches and tables. And at the far end, on a raised platform, sits the lord, with his family and his most important guests.

The lord is called Aelfric. He is a big man, with a red beard and a loud laugh. He has fought in the king's wars, and he has a sword with a golden handle, which he received as a gift from the king himself. His wife is called Aethelburh. She is tall and dignified, with golden brooches and a dress of fine blue cloth. She is very clever, and helps her husband run the estate. Everyone respects her.

{img:lady-of-hall-serving-mead-from-cup-guests-benches-firelight}
In the time after the harvest, Wulfric and the other farmers bring their dues to the hall. Sacks of grain, barrels of ale, cheeses, honey, a few sheep and geese. These are the rent for their land. The lord's steward counts everything and writes it down on a wooden tally stick, with little notches. Then the food goes into the lord's storehouses, to feed his household through the winter.

And at the end of the harvest, the lord invites the whole village to a feast in his hall. It is one of the happiest evenings of the year. The hall is filled with people, sitting along the benches. Torches burn along the walls. A great fire burns in the long hearth. And on the tables, there is more food than the villagers have seen all year.

--

{img:feast-table-roast-meat-bread-cheese-drinking-horns-anglo-saxon-hall}
There is roast pork and roast beef, turning on spits over the fire. There are geese and chickens, stewed with herbs. There are great round loaves of the new bread, and cheeses, butter and honey. There are apples and plums and blackberries. And there is plenty to drink. Ale, made from barley, and for the important guests, mead, made from honey, and even a little wine from Frankia, across the sea.

The lady of the hall has a special role at the feast. She walks around the hall with a beautiful cup, and offers drink to the guests, one by one. First to her husband, then to the most important guests, and then to everyone else. It is a great honour to be served by her. When she comes to Wulfric, he stands up and bows, and his ears turn red.

{img:harp-player-scop-singing-hall-firelight-listeners-quiet}
After the meal, the most exciting moment comes. The scop stands up. A scop is a poet and singer. He holds a small wooden lyre, with six strings. He plucks a few notes, and the whole hall goes quiet. Then he begins to sing. He sings old stories, about heroes and kings, about battles and journeys, about dragons and monsters. His voice is deep and strong, and fills the whole hall.

--

Cuthwine sits close to the fire, with his mouth open, listening. He has never heard anything so wonderful. Hild sits beside him, holding her knees. The flames flicker. The shadows dance on the walls. Outside, it is dark and the wind is blowing. But inside the hall, there is warmth and light and music. And the scop sings on, late into the night, while the children slowly fall asleep against their parents' shoulders.

Late at night, the villagers walk home together along the dark path, carrying burning torches. The children are carried on their fathers' shoulders, fast asleep. The adults are tired and happy, humming the tunes of the scop's songs. Above them, the stars are shining. And far behind them, the windows of the great hall are still glowing in the darkness, like a lantern on the hill.
