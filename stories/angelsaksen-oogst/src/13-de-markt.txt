After the harvest, Wulfric has a little grain to spare. He also has some cheese, some wool and two young pigs that the family does not need. So one morning, he loads the ox cart, and takes Cuthwine with him to the market. It is a long journey, almost a whole day, to the trading town by the sea. It is called Hamwic. Today, the city of Southampton stands in the same place.

--

Hamwic is the biggest town Cuthwine has ever seen. It is a new town, built not long ago, by the kings of Wessex, for trade. About two or three thousand people live there. The streets are laid out in a grid, straight and covered with gravel. There are rows of wooden houses and workshops. And along the river, there is a harbour, with boats from across the sea.

The town is full of craftsmen. There are blacksmiths, hammering iron into tools and nails. There are potters, making pots and jugs. There are bone workers, carving combs, needles and pins from the bones of cattle and deer. There are weavers, leather workers and jewellers. The air is full of noise and smoke and smells. Cuthwine stares at everything, holding tightly to his father's hand.

{img:busy-anglo-saxon-market-street-stalls-blacksmith-comb-maker-crowd}
At the harbour, there are ships from Frankia, across the sea, which is now France. They bring wine in barrels, millstones, glass, fine pottery and swords. They take back English wool, cloth, hides, tin and lead. Sadly, they sometimes also take people who have been sold as slaves, as happened in many lands in those days. The traders speak many languages. Old English, Frankish, Frisian, and the Latin of the churchmen. Cuthwine hears words he has never heard before.

Wulfric sells his grain, his cheese and his wool to a merchant. He sells the pigs to a butcher. In return, he gets some small silver coins. They are called sceattas. They are very small, about the size of a fingernail, with strange little pictures on them. Birds, animals, faces and patterns. Coins are still quite rare in the countryside. Most people in villages trade by exchanging things, not with money. But in Hamwic, coins are used every day.

--

{img:tiny-silver-sceat-coins-hand-palm-close-anglo-saxon}
With his coins, Wulfric buys the things the family cannot make itself. A new iron sickle blade, because his old one is worn thin. Some salt, which is very important for preserving meat and butter for the winter. A little bag of iron nails. And a small pot of honey-coloured glass beads, for Hild, for the harvest feast. He hides the beads in his pocket. It is a surprise.

Cuthwine is allowed to choose something too. He walks up and down the stalls for a long time. In the end, he chooses a little comb made of deer antler, with a pattern of circles carved on it. It is not for himself. It is for Modor. Her old comb is broken. Wulfric is very proud of his son, but he does not say so. He just puts his hand on the boy's messy hair for a moment.

{img:father-son-ox-cart-returning-home-evening-road-countryside}
They spend the night in Hamwic, sleeping in the cart, under the stars. Cuthwine lies awake for a long time, listening to the sounds of the town. Dogs barking, the creak of ships in the harbour, the voices of sailors singing in a strange language. He thinks it is the most exciting place in the world. But he also misses home, and the geese, and his own bench by the fire.

--

The next morning, they set off for home, early. The road winds through the fields and forests. Everywhere, people are finishing their harvest. The fields are stubble now, with flocks of birds feeding on the fallen grain. In the late afternoon, they see the smoke of their own village rising above the trees. The geese hear the cart coming, and start honking. And Hild comes running down the path to meet them.
