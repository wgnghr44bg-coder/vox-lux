Bread is the most important food of the Anglo-Saxons. They eat it at every meal. In fact, their word for lord, hlaford, means guardian of the bread. And their word for lady, hlæfdige, probably means the one who kneads the bread. So the lord and lady of the house were, in a way, the people who made sure everyone had bread.

--

Eadgyth bakes bread every few days. She mixes flour, water, salt and a little yeast in a big wooden trough. The yeast comes from the ale she brews. It makes the bread rise and become soft. She kneads the dough for a long time, pushing and folding it with her strong hands. Then she covers it with a cloth, and leaves it by the warm fire to rise.

Some of the bread is baked in the hot ashes of the hearth, under a clay pot. But the village also has a big clay oven, shaped like a beehive, which stands outside. The women take turns using it. They light a fire inside the oven, and let it burn until the clay is very hot. Then they rake out the ashes, put in the loaves on a long wooden paddle, and close the opening. The heat of the clay bakes the bread.

{img:fresh-round-loaves-cooling-wooden-table-butter-honey-pot}
There are different kinds of bread. White bread, made from wheat, is the best and the most expensive. It is soft and light. Rich people eat it every day. Ordinary people eat it on feast days. Everyday bread is often made from barley, or from a mixture of grains. It is darker and heavier. And in a poor year, people make bread from beans, peas or even acorns mixed with a little flour. But this year, with a good harvest, Eadgyth bakes wheat bread every week.

The second most important thing, after bread, is ale. Everyone drinks ale, even the children. It is made from barley, and it is much weaker than modern beer. In a world where river water is not always clean, ale is safer to drink. And it gives strength for the hard work in the fields. A farmer at harvest time might drink several litres of weak ale a day.

--

{img:brewing-ale-large-pot-barley-steam-woman-stirring-wooden-paddle}
Eadgyth brews ale every week. First, she soaks barley grains in water, until they begin to sprout. Then she dries them gently by the fire. This is called malt. She grinds the malt, and mixes it with hot water in a big pot. The water becomes sweet. She lets it cool, and adds a little yeast from the last batch. Then she leaves it for a few days, covered with a cloth, to bubble and ferment.

The Anglo-Saxons did not usually add hops to their ale. That came much later. Instead, they flavoured it with herbs, like bog myrtle, yarrow or heather. Each woman had her own recipe, and some were famous for their good ale. Eadgyth's ale is said to be the best in the village. When the neighbours come to visit, they always hope she will offer them a cup.

{img:mead-honey-jars-drinking-horn-wooden-cups-table-firelight}
For special occasions, there is mead. Mead is made from honey and water, fermented with yeast. It is sweet and strong, and very precious, because honey is precious. In the great halls, the lords drink mead from drinking horns, decorated with silver. In the stories, the mead hall is the centre of all joy and friendship. To share mead with someone means to be their friend and ally.

--

At the end of a long harvest day, there is nothing better than a hunk of fresh bread, with butter and cheese, and a cup of cool ale. The family sits outside the house in the evening light, eating and drinking, and watching the swallows. Wulfric says that a king in his hall eats no better than this. Eadgyth laughs and says that a king would at least wash his hands first. Wulfric looks at his dusty hands, and laughs too.
