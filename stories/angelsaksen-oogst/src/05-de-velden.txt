Around the village, there are the fields. They are not small, fenced fields, like the ones in the countryside today. They are big open fields, without hedges or walls. Each big field is divided into long, narrow strips. And each family in the village has some strips in each field. Wulfric's family has about twenty strips, spread out over the fields. Historians think that this way of farming was only just beginning around the year 700, and became much more common in later centuries. But in some villages, like ours, it had already begun.

--

Why are the strips spread out? Because the soil is not the same everywhere. Some parts of the fields are good and rich. Others are stony, or wet, or poor. By giving each family strips in different places, everyone gets a fair share of the good land and the bad land. It is a very old way of farming, and a very fair one.

The strips are long and narrow because of the plough. The plough is heavy, made of wood with an iron blade, and it is pulled by oxen. Turning a team of oxen around at the end of the field is slow and difficult. So it is easier to plough long, straight lines, and turn as few times as possible. The length of one furrow, about two hundred metres, became an old English measure, the furlong. It means furrow long.

{img:oxen-team-pulling-heavy-plough-farmer-guiding-spring-field-birds}
Ploughing is done together. One family rarely has enough oxen to pull the heavy plough. A full team needs eight oxen. So the families share their oxen, and plough all the strips together, one after another. Wulfric has two oxen. His neighbours have the others. In the spring, the whole village works together, ploughing the fields, from one end to the other.

The village has two great fields, and some smaller ones. Every year, one field is planted with wheat or barley, and the other is left empty, to rest. This is called leaving the field fallow. During its year of rest, the cows and sheep graze on it, eating the weeds and leaving their dung, which makes the soil rich again. The next year, the fields swap. So the soil never gets too tired.

--

{img:meadow-by-river-cows-grazing-haystacks-early-summer-anglo-saxon}
Besides the fields, there are other kinds of land around the village. Down by the river, there are the meadows. They are too wet for grain, but they grow rich grass. In June, the villagers cut the grass and dry it in the sun, to make hay. The hay is stored for the winter, to feed the animals. Then the cows graze on the meadows for the rest of the summer.

On the higher ground, there is the common pasture, where everyone's sheep and cattle can graze. And beyond that, there is the forest. The forest is very important. It gives wood for building, fuel for the fires, nuts and berries, and honey from wild bees. In autumn, the pigs are taken into the forest to eat acorns and beechnuts, which makes them fat for the winter.

{img:pigs-in-oak-forest-autumn-acorns-boy-with-stick-watching}
The crops in the fields are wheat, barley, oats and rye. Wheat is the most precious, because it makes the best white bread. But it needs good soil. Barley grows almost everywhere, and is used for bread, porridge and above all for ale. Oats are used for porridge and for feeding horses. Rye is a dark grain, which grows well on poor soil, and makes a heavy, dark bread. There are also beans and peas, grown in strips of their own.

--

This year, the crops look good. The spring was mild and the summer has been warm, with just enough rain. The wheat is tall and golden. The barley is ripe. The oats are nodding in the breeze. Wulfric walks along the edges of his strips every evening, looking at the grain and the sky. He knows that the next few weeks will decide whether his family eats well this winter, or goes hungry.

Between the strips, there are narrow paths of grass, where people can walk without treading on the crops. In early summer, these paths are full of wild flowers. Red poppies, blue cornflowers, white daisies and yellow buttercups. The children pick them and make little crowns for their hair. The bees buzz from flower to flower. And the larks sing high up in the sky, so high that you can hardly see them.
