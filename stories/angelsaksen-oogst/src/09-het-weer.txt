During the harvest, everyone watches the sky. The weather is the most important thing of all. The grain must be cut, dried and carried home while it is dry. If it rains too much, the grain in the stooks gets wet. Then it can start to sprout, or rot, or grow mould. A wet harvest can mean a hungry winter. So every morning, the first thing Wulfric does is step outside and look at the sky.

--

The Anglo-Saxons knew many signs of the weather. A red sky in the evening meant a fine day tomorrow. A red sky in the morning meant rain. When the cows lay down in the field, rain was coming. When the spiders spun long webs, the weather would be dry. When the swallows flew high, it would be fine. Modor knows all these signs, and many more. She is usually right.

For the first week of the harvest, the weather is perfect. Warm, sunny days, with a light breeze. The reapers cut field after field. The stooks stand in long rows, drying in the sun. Everyone works as fast as they can, to use the good weather. Wulfric says that the summer has been kind to them, and they must not waste it.

{img:hot-still-afternoon-heavy-air-reapers-hurrying-distant-thunder-clouds}
Then, one afternoon, the air becomes very still and heavy. It is hot, but the sun is hazy. The birds stop singing. The cows stand close together, under the trees. Modor comes out of the house and looks at the sky in the west. Over the hills, big dark clouds are piling up, higher and higher, like grey mountains. In the distance, there is a low rumble of thunder.

Wulfric sees it too. He calls out to the whole field. The sheaves in the far field are dry. They must be brought in before the storm. Everyone drops what they are doing and runs. The ox cart is brought out. The men and women load the sheaves onto the cart, as fast as they can, piling them high. Hild climbs up on top, to stack them neatly. Cuthwine leads the oxen. The thunder rumbles again, closer now.

--

{img:ox-cart-piled-with-sheaves-racing-home-storm-clouds-wind}
The wind begins to blow. The trees at the edge of the field bend and toss. The first big drops of rain fall, warm and heavy, making dark spots in the dust. The cart rolls slowly toward the village, creaking under its load. Everyone walks beside it, holding the sheaves down against the wind. The barn door is open. The oxen pull the cart inside. And just as the last sheaf is under the roof, the rain comes down in a great roaring curtain.

Everyone crowds into the barn, out of breath and laughing. They are wet, but the grain is dry. Outside, the rain pours down. Lightning flashes, and thunder cracks right over the village. The children hide their faces. But inside the barn, everyone is safe and warm. The sweet smell of the dry wheat fills the air. And the rain drums loudly on the thatched roof.

{img:inside-barn-people-sheltering-rain-pouring-outside-doorway-sheaves}
The storm lasts for an hour. Then it moves away to the east, grumbling. The rain stops. The clouds break, and the evening sun shines through, low and golden. Everything is wet and shining. The air smells fresh and clean. And over the hills, there is a rainbow, a perfect arch of colours across the sky.

--

Not all the stooks were saved. In the fields that were not yet carried in, the sheaves are wet. But Wulfric says it does not matter. If the next days are sunny, they will dry again. The important thing is that most of the grain is safe. Modor smiles and says that she knew the storm was coming, because her knees were aching all morning. Everyone laughs. Her knees are the best weather sign in the village.

That evening, Modor sits in the doorway and watches the last of the storm clouds drift away over the hills. The air is cool and sweet after the rain. Drops are still falling from the edge of the thatch, plip, plop, into the puddles. A blackbird begins to sing in the apple tree. And Modor closes her eyes and listens, with a little smile on her face.
