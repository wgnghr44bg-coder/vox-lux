About a day's walk from the village, there is a monastery. It lies in a quiet valley by a river, surrounded by fields and orchards. There is a stone church, with small round windows, and a group of wooden buildings around it. About thirty monks live there, and some nuns in a separate house nearby. They pray, work and study, following a strict rule.

--

In Anglo-Saxon England, monasteries are very important places. They are places of prayer, of course. But they are also schools, libraries, hospitals and farms. They own a lot of land, and many villages work for them. Rich families give them land and gifts, hoping for prayers for their souls. And kings often send their sons and daughters there, to be educated.

Some monasteries are led by women. They are called abbesses. One of the most famous was Hild, the abbess of Whitby, in Northumbria. She died about twenty years before our story. She was so wise that kings and bishops came to ask her advice. Five of her monks later became bishops. Our Hild, the girl in the village, was named after her. Her mother thinks it is a good name, for a wise girl.

{img:monks-in-scriptorium-writing-manuscripts-quills-vellum-window-light}
The monks of the monastery spend part of each day in the scriptorium. This is the room where books are written. There is no printing in those days. Every book must be copied by hand, letter by letter, with a quill pen. The pages are made of vellum, which is the skin of calves, cleaned, stretched and dried until it is thin and smooth. A big book needs the skins of hundreds of calves.

The ink is made from soot, or from oak galls, which are little round growths on oak trees. The colours for the pictures are made from minerals, plants and even insects. Some colours are very precious. The deep blue is made from a stone called lapis lazuli, which comes all the way from the mountains of Afghanistan. The gold is real gold, beaten into very thin sheets.

--

{img:illuminated-manuscript-page-interlace-patterns-birds-colours-gold}
Around the time of our story, at a monastery on a small island called Lindisfarne, in the far north of England, a monk is making one of the most beautiful books ever made. It is a book of the four Gospels. It is called the Lindisfarne Gospels. Its pages are covered with amazing patterns. Twisting lines and knots, birds with long necks, spirals and crosses, all in bright colours. It took the monk, Eadfrith, many years to make.

The patterns in these books are similar to the patterns on Anglo-Saxon jewellery and brooches. The monks were using the old art of their people, the twisting animals and knots, to decorate the holy book. Irish monks had brought a similar style. And they added colours and ideas from Rome. So the books are a beautiful mix of all these traditions together.

{img:monk-in-orchard-picking-apples-monastery-garden-autumn-baskets}
The monks also farm. They have fields, orchards, fishponds and beehives. They grow herbs for medicine, in a special garden. They brew ale and bake bread. At harvest time, they work in the fields just like the villagers, in their simple brown robes. And they keep the old knowledge of medicine, writing down the uses of hundreds of plants and herbs.

--

Once a year, after the harvest, Wulfric goes to the monastery with a cart of grain, as a gift. This year, he takes Hild with him. She has never seen a book. A kind old monk shows her the scriptorium, and lets her look at a page of a book. Hild stares at the patterns and the colours. She cannot read the words. But she thinks it is the most beautiful thing she has ever seen. On the way home, she is very quiet, thinking.

Many years later, Hild will remember that page for the rest of her life. The twisting knots of colour, the little birds with long necks, the shining gold. She will tell her own children and grandchildren about it, by the fire, in the long winter evenings. And they will listen, and try to imagine it, a book so beautiful that it looked like it was made by angels.
