TOPIC: Writing on clay
===
IMG: scribe-pressing-reed-wet-clay | a scribe pressing a sharp reed into a small damp clay tablet held in his left hand
HEAD: Babylonian writing was made of tiny wedges pressed into wet clay with a reed.
BODY: A scribe began with a lump of smooth, damp clay. He shaped it into a tablet small enough to hold in one hand. It was flat on one side and gently rounded on the other. Holding it in his left hand, he pressed the cut end of a reed into the surface with his right. Each press left a small wedge-shaped mark. Signs were made from several wedges, long and short, upright and lying down. Today this script is called cuneiform, which simply means wedge-shaped. Skilled scribes worked quickly, turning the tablet to write on the back and even on the edges. The system was invented by the Sumerians more than three thousand years before Nebuchadnezzar. At first it used simple pictures of sheep, grain and jars of oil. Over the centuries, the pictures became wedges. The signs came to stand for sounds as well as things, and the script could record almost any language.
===
IMG: tablets-drying-in-sun-rows | rows of clay tablets drying on a wooden rack in a sunny courtyard, a scribe turning one over
HEAD: Clay tablets were cheap to make, and baked ones lasted almost forever.
BODY: After writing, a tablet was left in the sun until it was hard. Important tablets were baked in an oven, which made them as tough as brick. Even letters had envelopes, made from a thin layer of clay wrapped around the tablet. The address was written on the outside, and the envelope had to be broken before the letter could be read. Clay was cheap and easy to find. Above all, it lasted. Paper and leather rot, and wood burns. Clay can survive for thousands of years in the ground. When a library burned in this part of the world, the fire often baked its tablets harder and preserved them. That is why hundreds of thousands of tablets have been found. They include letters, laws, lists, prayers, recipes, dictionaries, poems and star charts. A cheap writing material has outlived the empires that used it, and it carries their voices to the present day.
===
IMG: tablet-house-school-boys-benches | a small schoolroom in a courtyard with boys on low brick benches copying wedge signs onto clay while a teacher watches
HEAD: Boys went to the "tablet house" at about seven years old and spent most of the day copying signs.
BODY: The Babylonian school was called the tablet house. It was not a big building. Usually it was the home of a master scribe, who took a few pupils and taught them in a room or courtyard. Most pupils were boys from families of scribes, priests and the well off, though a few girls learned to write as well. They began at about seven or eight and went to school from sunrise to evening for many years. First they learned how to make a tablet, hold the reed and press simple wedges. Then came the signs, one by one, copied again and again from a model. The teacher wrote the model on one side of a tablet, and the pupil copied it on the other. After that came lists of words, trees, animals, stones, gods and cities. Last of all came sayings, letters, hymns and stories.
===
IMG: school-tablet-pupil-copy-closeup | a clay tablet with a neat teacher's model on one side and a clumsy copy on the other, a thumb smoothing the clay
HEAD: Some school tablets show a teacher's neat writing on one side and a pupil's mistakes on the other.
BODY: When a pupil made a mistake, he smoothed the clay with his thumb and started again. Many practice tablets have nevertheless survived. On one side is the teacher's neat model. On the other is the pupil's copy, with wobbling lines, crowded signs and plenty of errors. Pupils also learned mathematics. They practised counting, multiplying and using fractions. They learned to measure a field, to work out how many bricks a wall needed, and to calculate the rations for workers. Teachers were strict, and some school texts describe beatings for poor work. Yet other texts show pride, affection and humour between teachers and pupils. These tablets are among the most human objects to survive from Mesopotamia. They show that the frustrations of learning were familiar thousands of years ago. Children then, as now, struggled, made mistakes and slowly improved with practice and patience.
===
IMG: schoolboy-story-father-dinner-teacher | a father pouring drink for a teacher at a family table while a boy stands nervously nearby, lamps lit
HEAD: A famous school story tells of a boy who was punished all day, until his father invited the teacher to dinner.
BODY: Babylonian pupils copied a funny old story for centuries. A boy goes to school and is punished for being late. Then he is punished for talking too much, for standing up, for writing badly, for just about everything. Sad and tired, he tells his father. The father invites the teacher to dinner. He gives him a fine meal, a new robe and a ring, and pours him drink after drink. And suddenly the teacher begins to praise the boy. He says that he is the best pupil in the school, and that he will become a famous scribe. The story is funny and very human, and it shows that people two and a half thousand years ago were fully capable of laughing at themselves and their teachers. It also shows that a good dinner has always been a useful way to smooth over a difficult situation.
===
IMG: aramaic-ink-leather-scroll-street | a man writing small curved letters with a pen and ink on a leather scroll beside a market stall
HEAD: A new, simple alphabet with only twenty-two letters was spreading across the country.
BODY: Around 600 BC, a new way of writing was becoming common. It was a simple alphabet of twenty-two letters, written in ink on leather or on pieces of pottery. It was much easier to learn than cuneiform, which needed hundreds of signs and many years of study. The language that went with it, Aramaic, was spoken everywhere in the streets and the markets. Traders used it. Ordinary people used it. Soon even officials in the great empires would use it. Yet for the important things, the scribes of Babylon still pressed the old wedges into clay. They used it for temple records, laws, contracts and star charts, as their ancestors had done for thousands of years. Two ways of writing lived side by side, one new and fast, and one old and respected. The old one was written in stone, almost literally, and it outlived the other by many centuries.
===
IMG: clay-library-shelves-archive | long shelves of labelled clay tablets in baskets in a quiet archive room lit by a single lamp
HEAD: Babylon's archives held tablets on everything from medicine to maths, and many are still unread.
BODY: The temples and palaces of Babylon kept their tablets in archives and libraries, on shelves and in baskets labelled with small clay tags. There were lists and dictionaries, prayers and hymns, medical texts, mathematics, omens, laws and stories. Hundreds of thousands of tablets have been found in the ruins of Mesopotamia, and many sit in museum storerooms, waiting for specialists to read them. Every year, a few more are translated, and every year we learn a little more about the people who wrote them. We find their business and their worries, their prayers and sometimes their jokes. It is gentle, patient work. A scholar bends over a small grey tablet under a lamp and reads a line that nobody has read for two and a half thousand years. For a moment, the writer and the reader are in the same room, and the long silence between them is broken.

TOPIC: Counting in sixties and reading the sky
===
IMG: base-sixty-wedge-numbers-tablet | a clay tablet covered in neat columns of wedge-shaped numbers under lamplight
HEAD: The sixty minutes in an hour can be traced back to the mathematics of ancient Mesopotamia.
BODY: The Babylonians did not count in tens. They counted in sixties. Their number system used only two signs, an upright wedge for one and a sideways wedge for ten. With these, any number from one to fifty-nine could be written. At sixty, the count began again in the next column, much as ten is written today with a one in the next place. The reason for choosing sixty is not known for certain. Yet the number has a great advantage for calculation. It divides evenly by two, three, four, five, six, ten, twelve, fifteen, twenty and thirty. That makes fractions much easier. The system was passed on to Greek astronomers and from them to later scholars. As a result, hours still have sixty minutes, minutes have sixty seconds, and circles have three hundred and sixty degrees. A small part of Babylonian mathematics is therefore still used every day.
===
IMG: square-diagonal-tablet-numbers | a small round clay tablet showing a square with two diagonals marked with tiny wedge numbers
HEAD: One small clay tablet shows a very exact value for the diagonal of a square, written about 3,700 years ago.
BODY: Babylonian mathematics was far more advanced than is often assumed. More than a thousand years before Nebuchadnezzar, scribes were already solving difficult problems about areas, volumes and interest on loans. One famous tablet, roughly the size of a hand, shows a square with a line from corner to corner. Along that line, in wedges, is a value for the diagonal. It is remarkably accurate, better than any other known calculation for more than a thousand years. Another famous tablet contains a table of numbers that scholars have discussed for nearly a century. Many believe it shows knowledge of a special rule about right-angled triangles. That was long before the Greek Pythagoras gave his name to it. The Babylonians did not write long formal proofs. They wrote clear practical methods and passed them from teacher to pupil. Their mathematics was built for use, yet it reached a surprising level of precision.
===
IMG: surveyors-measuring-field-rope | surveyors stretching a measuring rope across a field beside a canal while a scribe notes the result
HEAD: For the Babylonians, maths was practical, and they used it to measure fields and divide inheritances.
BODY: Mathematics in Babylon was never just a puzzle. People used it every day. Surveyors measured fields with knotted ropes and calculated their areas. Families divided inheritances between brothers. Officials worked out the wages and rations of workers. Builders calculated how many bricks a wall would need, and how much earth had to be dug from a canal. Merchants worked out the interest on loans. Scribes learned these skills by solving practice problems at school. Many of these problems sound surprisingly familiar. A field of a certain length and width would yield a certain amount of barley. A canal of a certain depth would need a certain number of workers. Such exercises survive on hundreds of school tablets. They show that mathematics grew from the needs of farming, building and trade. In Mesopotamia, numbers helped to organise one of the most complex societies of the ancient world.
===
IMG: lunar-calendar-twelve-months-scribe | a scribe at a table with a clay tablet and a thin crescent moon visible through the doorway at dusk
HEAD: The Babylonian year had twelve months of the moon, with an extra month added every few years.
BODY: The Babylonians kept time by the moon. Each month began when the thin new crescent was first seen in the evening sky, and lasted twenty-nine or thirty days. Twelve such months make a year, but twelve moon months are about eleven days shorter than a year of the sun. If nothing was done, the seasons would slowly slide around the calendar, and the New Year festival would no longer fall in spring. So every few years, the astronomers added an extra month to put everything back in step. They divided the day and the night into twelve parts as well, and the sky into twelve parts. This careful counting, which began with practical needs like farming, planting and festivals, gradually became something more. It became a patient study of the heavens.
===
IMG: astronomers-roof-night-tablets | priests in cloaks on a temple roof at night, looking at the moon and writing on small clay tablets by lamp
HEAD: On temple roofs, star watchers wrote a diary of the sky every night, for six hundred years.
BODY: The night sky over Babylon was extraordinary. The air was dry and clear, and there were no city lights, only small lamps and fires. The stars shone bright and sharp from one edge of the flat land to the other. On top of the great tower and the temple roofs, priests and scribes watched them. Night after night, they recorded where the moon and the planets stood among the stars, when they rose and set, and when the moon went dark. They also noted the weather, the height of the river, the price of barley, dates and wool, and the events of the city. These diaries began around 650 BC and went on for about six hundred years, the longest scientific record kept in the ancient world. Hundreds of fragments still survive in the British Museum, and modern scientists use them to study events in the sky long ago.
===
IMG: night-sky-moon-planets-tower | a dark sky over a flat plain with a bright crescent moon, Venus and Jupiter, a great tower in silhouette
HEAD: With all those notes, the astronomers learned to predict what the sky would do next.
BODY: After generations of observing and recording, Babylonian astronomers found patterns in the sky. They discovered that eclipses of the moon came in cycles. They found that the planets moved in repeating rhythms. Using their arithmetic based on sixty, they calculated when a new moon would appear and where the planets would be in the coming months. Some scholars now believe that they even used a kind of geometry to track the planet Jupiter. That method would not be used again in Europe for well over a thousand years. Babylonian astronomy was not modern science. Its practitioners did not ask what the sky was made of. They wanted to know what it would do, and what it meant. Even so, their records were extraordinarily careful. Greek astronomers later used Babylonian observations and methods, and through them this knowledge shaped the study of the heavens for centuries.
===
IMG: zodiac-star-pictures-bull-lion | a night sky with faint outlines of a bull, a lion, twins and a scorpion drawn among bright stars
HEAD: Many of our star names and the twelve star signs began with the astronomers of Babylon.
BODY: The Babylonians gave names and pictures to groups of stars. They divided the path that the sun, moon and planets seem to follow into twelve equal parts. Each part had its own group of stars, such as the Bull, the Lion, the Scorpion, the Twins and the Scales. Many of these pictures later became the twelve signs of the zodiac. For the Babylonians, the sky was a vast book written by the gods. A dark moon or a planet moving in an unusual way could be read as a message about the king and the country. Astronomers therefore sent regular reports to the palace. Some of these reports still survive. They show kings waiting anxiously for news of eclipses and omens. Yet most of the astronomers' work was patient observation, night after night. The names they gave to the stars have travelled through Greek and Latin and are still used today.

TOPIC: The New Year and the gods
===
IMG: akitu-festival-boats-statues-river | decorated boats carrying gods' statues along the river toward the city at dawn, flags, flowers and musicians
HEAD: The New Year festival lasted twelve days, and statues of the gods came to Babylon by boat.
BODY: Each spring, when the barley was ripening in the fields, Babylon celebrated the Akitu, the New Year. It was the greatest festival of the year and lasted twelve days. In the early days, priests held ceremonies in the temple at dawn, with prayers, hymns and the recital of the great poem about Marduk and the making of the world. Meanwhile, the statues of the gods of other cities were brought to Babylon by boat. They came along rivers and canals, on vessels decorated with gold, flowers and banners, with priests and musicians at their sides. The gods came to visit Marduk, and to take part in the festival, and the city filled with visitors. For a few days, the quays were crowded and the streets were lined with people who had walked from far villages to watch. Babylon at the New Year must have felt like the centre of the world.
===
IMG: king-before-marduk-statue-ceremony | a king kneeling bareheaded before a golden statue while a high priest holds the crown in a dim temple room
HEAD: At the New Year, the most powerful king in the world had to humble himself before the god.
BODY: On the fifth day, a strange and solemn ceremony took place. The king came into the temple and stood before the statue of Marduk. The high priest removed his crown and his royal robes and laid them before the god. Then he struck the king lightly on the cheek and pulled his ears. The king knelt, and said that he had done no wrong. He had not neglected the gods, he had not harmed Babylon, and he had not harmed its people. Then the priest gave him back his crown. Everyone watched the king's face. If tears came to his eyes, it was a good sign. Marduk was pleased, and the year would be a good one. It was a powerful reminder that even the greatest king was a servant of the god, and must rule fairly. Few rulers have ever done anything like it.
===
IMG: procession-through-ishtar-gate-statues | priests carrying a golden god statue through a blue gate between rows of lions, musicians and crowds on both sides
HEAD: On the great day, the statues of the gods paraded through the blue gate while the whole city watched.
BODY: On the eighth and ninth days came the great procession. The king took the hand of Marduk's statue and led him out of the temple. The statues of all the other gods followed, on carts and carried by priests, in a long glittering line. They walked along the holy road between the blue walls and the golden lions, and passed through the Ishtar Gate. There were drums and harps and pipes, singing and clouds of incense. The whole city crowded along the road, on the walls and on the roofs. The procession went out to the river and by boat to a special temple in a garden outside the city. The gods stayed there for a few days with ceremonies and feasts. Then they returned along the same road, in the same splendour. At last the gods gathered to decide the fate of the land for the coming year.
===
IMG: festival-night-lamps-rooftops | rooftops glowing with oil lamps at night, families feasting, musicians and dancers in the street below
HEAD: All through the festival, the city feasted, danced and lit lamps on the rooftops.
BODY: While the priests and the king were busy in the temple, the rest of the city celebrated in its own way. There were feasts and music and dancing in the streets. People put on their best clothes, visited relatives and exchanged gifts. The temples handed out food and drink. At night, lamps burned on the roofs and on the walls, so that the whole city glowed in the dark. Music and singing went on late, long after the children had fallen asleep on their mats. When it was all over, the new year had begun. The world had been made new, the king had been confirmed, and the gods had decided the fate of the land. People went back to their homes and fields and workshops, the barley was harvested, and ordinary life resumed. But for a few days, everyone had felt a part of something far greater than themselves.
===
IMG: gods-of-babylon-shrine-statues | a small shrine with carved figures of a sun god, a moon god and a goddess with a star, lit by oil lamps
HEAD: The world of the Babylonians was full of gods, and one of them even looked after beer.
BODY: The Babylonians believed in a great crowd of gods. There was an old god of the sky, a lord of the wind, and Ea, the clever and kindly god of the fresh waters under the earth, who loved people and helped them. There was the sun god, who saw everything as he crossed the sky and who stood for truth and justice. There was the moon god with his silver curve, and the storm god with his lightning. There was Ishtar, the goddess of love and of the morning and evening star, beautiful and fierce. There were goddesses of healing, writing and beer. And above them all, in Babylon, stood Marduk, with his son Nabu, the god of wisdom. Every city had its own god and its own temple. In Babylon there were dozens of temples and hundreds of little shrines at street corners, where people stopped to leave a few dates or a flower.
===
IMG: household-shrine-evening-prayer | a mother lighting a small lamp before clay figurines in a corner of a room, pouring a little water, children nearby
HEAD: Every family had its own personal god, and prayed to it each evening.
BODY: Besides the great gods, each family had a personal god who watched over them and spoke for them to the greater ones. In a corner of the house, they kept little clay figures of their god and of good spirits. In the evening, a mother or father lit a small lamp, poured a little water and set out a few dates and a piece of bread. Then they said a quiet prayer for the family, the children, the ancestors, the house and the night. The Babylonians believed that the dead lived on in a grey, quiet land beneath the earth, and depended on the living for food and water. So families remembered their ancestors by name and gave them small offerings every month. They wore little charms against evil spirits, and buried small figures of protectors under doorways. In a dangerous world, these small habits made the night feel safer.

TOPIC: Gardens, exiles and craftsmen
===
IMG: hanging-gardens-imagined-terraces | an imagined green terraced garden rising in steps above a city, trees, flowers and falling water, soft evening light
HEAD: The most famous wonder of Babylon is one that may never have existed.
BODY: Greek and Roman writers, centuries afterwards, told a lovely story. Nebuchadnezzar, they said, had a wife who came from the green mountains in the north-east. In flat, brown Babylon, she was homesick. So the king built her a mountain in the middle of his city, a great garden rising in steps like a green hill, planted with trees and flowers from all over the empire. Machines lifted river water to the top, and it flowed down through the terraces in streams. From below, the trees seemed to hang in the air, so people called them the Hanging Gardens, and the Greeks counted them among the Seven Wonders of the World. It is a beautiful story. But there is a mystery. The Babylonians never mention these gardens, in all their clay tablets. Nor does Nebuchadnezzar, who wrote so proudly of everything else he built.
===
IMG: assyrian-garden-hillside-channels | an ancient hillside garden with trees and stone water channels and an arched aqueduct crossing a valley
HEAD: Some scholars think the famous gardens were really in another city, further north.
BODY: The Hanging Gardens of Babylon are famous, but their location remains uncertain. Archaeologists have excavated Babylon for more than a hundred years without finding them for certain. Some scholars think the gardens stood beside the river and were later washed away. Others point to a vaulted building in the palace, with arches and wells, which might have been part of them. A third view suggests that the story really belongs to another city further north. There, an Assyrian king built a famous garden about a century earlier. It was watered by canals and an impressive aqueduct. Later writers may have confused the two cities. Some historians even think the gardens were partly legend. Yet gardens certainly existed in Babylon. The kings and temples owned orchards, pools and palm groves. Whatever the truth, the story shows how much people in a dry land valued shade, water and green growing things.
===
IMG: judean-exiles-canal-willows-dusk | families sitting beside a canal under willow trees at dusk, a harp resting on a branch
HEAD: By the rivers of Babylon, exiles from a distant kingdom sat down and remembered their homes.
BODY: Over the years, many peoples came to Babylon, and some did not come willingly. Far to the west lay the small kingdom of Judah, with its capital Jerusalem. When it rebelled against Nebuchadnezzar, his armies captured the city. Many of its people, including the king and his court, were taken across the desert to Babylonia. The exiles were not kept in prisons. They were settled in towns and villages beside the canals, where they were given land to farm. They could build houses, plant gardens, marry, trade and keep their customs and faith. Clay tablets from the region record Judean names in ordinary contracts and ration lists. Yet many exiles longed for home. One of the most famous psalms in the Bible begins with this memory. By the rivers of Babylon, the exiles sat down and wept as they remembered their homeland. It is one of the oldest songs of exile ever written.
===
IMG: ration-tablet-storeroom-palace | a clay tablet with rows of wedge writing on a table in a palace storeroom beside jars of oil and sacks of barley
HEAD: Clay tablets from the palace stores list the oil and barley given to a king in exile and his five sons.
BODY: Remarkably, we can find these exiles in the clay records. When archaeologists dug in the palace storerooms, they found tablets listing the rations of oil and barley given to foreign guests at court. Among the names is the king of Judah, and his five sons. And in more recent times, other tablets have been read that come from a small town near Babylon, whose name means the town of Judah. The exiles lived there, farmed, paid their taxes and signed contracts. Their Hebrew names appear on the tablets for several generations, fathers and sons and grandsons, living quietly beside the canals. They were also gathering and copying the writings of their people, so that nothing would be lost. Much of what became the Hebrew Bible took shape in these years. Many of the exiles later went home, but many stayed, because Babylon had become their home too.
===
IMG: brickmakers-wooden-moulds-yard | brick makers pressing mud and straw into wooden moulds in a riverside yard, rows of bricks laid to dry in the sun
HEAD: Babylon was built from millions of bricks, made by hand in big yards beside the river.
BODY: In workshops, yards and streets across Babylon, craftsmen were busy. Most important of all were the brick makers. They worked in huge yards outside the city, where the river gave them plenty of clay and water. They mixed clay with water and chopped straw, trod it into thick mud with their feet, and pressed it into square wooden moulds. Then they turned the bricks out in long rows to dry in the sun. In the summer heat, that took a few days. If it rained, the work was ruined. Most bricks were only sun-dried, but the best were baked in enormous ovens until they came out hard and yellow and red. These were used for outer walls, floors and anywhere that had to stand up to water. Around the brick yards, the smoke of the ovens rose into the sky all day long, a sign that the city was still growing.
===
IMG: weavers-dyers-goldsmiths-workshops | a lane of workshops with dyers dipping wool in tubs of red and blue, a weaver at a loom and a goldsmith at a bench
HEAD: Babylonian craftsmen wove, dyed, carved and hammered, and passed their trade down through families.
BODY: Beyond the brick yards, the streets rang with many other trades. There were weavers, men and women, working at looms. There were dyers who dipped wool in tubs of red, blue, purple and yellow, and tailors who sewed patterns into cloth. There were goldsmiths and silversmiths making jewellery and cups, blacksmiths making tools, carpenters, boat builders, potters, leather workers and basket makers. There were perfume makers mixing sweet oils, and carvers who made seals. A seal was a little stone cylinder, about the size of a finger, carved with tiny pictures. Rolled across wet clay, it left a small picture, like a signature, and every important person wore one on a string around the neck. Many craftsmen worked for the temples or the palace, and were paid in barley, wool, oil and silver. Others had their own shops, and often a trade stayed in the same family for generations, from father to son and mother to daughter.

TOPIC: After Nebuchadnezzar, and Babylon today
===
IMG: cyrus-entering-babylon-peaceful-crowd | a king on horseback entering a great gate at dawn, calm crowds holding green branches
HEAD: About sixty years later, a Persian king entered Babylon almost without a fight.
BODY: Nebuchadnezzar ruled for forty-three years and died as an old man. After him, Babylon had several kings in quick succession. The last was Nabonidus, an unusual ruler devoted to the moon god. He spent about ten years in the Arabian desert, leaving his son to govern at home. Meanwhile, a new power was rising in the east. In 539 BC, Cyrus the Great, king of the Persians, arrived with his army. Babylon fell almost without a battle. Cyrus presented himself as a respectful ruler. He honoured Marduk, protected the temples and allowed daily life to continue. On a clay cylinder now in the British Museum, he tells his own version of events. He claims that Marduk chose him to be king of Babylon. He also says he allowed displaced peoples to return home with their gods. Like all royal inscriptions, it was propaganda. Yet it marked the end of Babylon as an independent kingdom.
===
IMG: alexander-babylon-palace-evening | a young general in a plain cloak standing alone in a columned palace hall at evening, the river glowing beyond
HEAD: Alexander the Great planned to make Babylon his capital, but he died there at thirty-two.
BODY: Under the Persians, Babylon stayed rich and important for about two hundred years. Then Alexander the Great arrived and took it from them. He admired the city and planned to make it the capital of his great empire, stretching from Greece to India. But in the old palace of Nebuchadnezzar, he fell ill with a fever, and he died in Babylon at the age of thirty-two. After Alexander, his generals built new cities, and slowly people drifted away from the old one. The temples still stood, and the astronomers still watched the sky and wrote their diaries on clay for another two hundred years. The last cuneiform tablet that we know of was written around seventy-five years after Christ. After that, the ancient writing was forgotten, and for almost two thousand years, nobody could read it at all.
===
IMG: ruined-mounds-desert-palms | low grassy mounds of broken brick in a wide desert plain, a few palm trees and a lone traveller in robes
HEAD: Over the centuries, Babylon faded into a field of mounds, but its name never died.
BODY: Slowly, the river changed its course. The canals filled with mud. The great buildings crumbled, and people took the bricks away to build new villages. The greatest city in the world became a field of mounds and broken brick on a flat desert plain. Now and then, travellers came and looked at it and wondered whether this could really be the Babylon of the Bible and the old stories. Local people dug in the ruins for bricks, and found the stamped ones with the king's name, which they built into houses, walls and dams. Yet Babylon was never forgotten. Its name lived on in the Bible, in Greek and Roman writing, in songs, poems and paintings, for two thousand years. A name of wonder and splendour, waiting patiently under the earth to be found again.
===
IMG: koldewey-excavation-railway-workers | a patient archaeologist with a moustache overseeing workers with baskets and a small railway cart, bricks emerging from a trench
HEAD: In 1899, a German archaeologist arrived in Babylon and spent almost eighteen years digging.
BODY: His name was Robert Koldewey. He was trained as a builder, and he was a careful, patient man with a big moustache and a love of old buildings. He came to Babylon in 1899 and stayed for nearly eighteen years. With hundreds of local workers, he dug layer by layer through the mounds, with a little railway to carry away the earth. He uncovered the great walls, the gates, the palaces and the temples, and mapped and photographed them. One day, in the north of the city, his workers began to find pieces of glazed brick, blue and yellow and white, thousands of them, broken and scattered. They kept digging. Deeper down they found the lower part of a gate still standing, with bulls and dragons in rows along its walls. It had been buried for more than two thousand years. It was the Ishtar Gate.
===
IMG: scholar-reading-tablet-magnifier | a scholar in a museum study bending over a small clay tablet with a magnifying glass and a desk lamp
HEAD: Scholars are still reading Babylon's tablets today, and still learning new things.
BODY: Since Koldewey, archaeologists from many countries, and from Iraq itself, have dug in Babylon and studied what they found. The clay tablets have been read and translated, so that we now know the names of kings and scribes, merchants and star watchers. We know about the ordinary people of Babylon, their business, their prayers and their lives. The sky diaries, the maths tablets, the letters and the stories are still being studied in universities and museums around the world. Every year, new tablets are read, and new things are learned. This is, in a way, the final surprise about Babylon. The great tower is gone, and the king is gone, but the clay remembered. A scribe pressing a reed into a lump of mud, so long ago, has somehow managed to speak to us across more than twenty-five centuries.
===

HEAD: Babylon is now a World Heritage Site, and the river still flows past it slowly.
BODY: Today, Babylon lies in Iraq, about eighty-five kilometres south of Baghdad, beside the Euphrates. It has been recognised as a World Heritage Site, protected as one of the most important places in the history of the world. Visitors can walk through the ruins along old streets between walls of mud brick. Some are original and some have been rebuilt in modern times. They can see the lower part of the Ishtar Gate, where the moulded bulls and dragons are still in place, without their glaze. They can walk along part of the holy road and see a great stone lion carved from black rock, which has stood among the ruins for many centuries. Around the site, there are palm groves, fields, canals and villages. And the river flows past slowly, as it did in the days of Nebuchadnezzar, though over the centuries it has moved a little to the west.

HEAD: Babylon lives on in our hours, our minutes and the names of our stars.
BODY: In museums around the world, Babylon still lives. In Berlin, the Ishtar Gate rises in blue and gold under the roof. In Baghdad, London, Paris and New York, the lions of the holy road are shown and admired, and so are the tablets of the scribes and the bricks of Nebuchadnezzar. But Babylon lives on in less obvious places too. It lives in our sixty minutes and our three hundred and sixty degrees. It lives in the names of the stars and the signs of the zodiac. It lives in the stories of the Bible, in our idea of a city that reaches the sky, and in the very habit of writing things down so that they last. The city was swept away, but it left a great deal behind. It is a quiet thought to carry into sleep, that so much of us began beside a slow brown river.
