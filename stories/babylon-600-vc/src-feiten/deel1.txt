TOPIC: The land between the rivers
===
IMG: mesopotamia-plain-two-rivers | a wide flat plain with two slow rivers, irrigation canals and green fields in morning light
HEAD: The word Mesopotamia simply means "the land between the rivers."
BODY: Babylon stood in a country the Greeks later called Mesopotamia, which means exactly that. The two rivers were the Tigris and the Euphrates. Both began in the mountains far to the north and flowed south for more than a thousand kilometres. Near Babylon they came quite close together before wandering on to the sea. The land between them was flat and brown, with rich soil that the rivers had carried down over thousands of years. There was one great problem. It hardly ever rained. The summers were long, hot and dry, and without the rivers, nothing at all would have grown. So the people who lived here learned to work with the water instead of waiting for the sky. Today most of this land lies in the country of Iraq. But in the time of Nebuchadnezzar, it was the busiest, most crowded and most cultured corner of the known world. Everything that made Babylon possible began with two muddy rivers.
===
IMG: irrigation-canals-palm-groves | a network of straight canals carrying river water to barley fields and palm groves
HEAD: Babylon's farmers turned a desert into a garden by digging channels.
BODY: The secret of the plain was the canal. A canal is simply a channel that leads river water away to fields and gardens, and the people of Mesopotamia had been digging them for thousands of years. Around Babylon, a great web of them spread in every direction. The biggest were as wide as rivers, and boats sailed along them. The smallest were little ditches between fields. Tiny gates of earth and reeds let a farmer open and close them by hand. Wherever the water reached, the land was green, with barley, wheat, vegetables, and forests of date palms. Wherever it did not, there was only dust. Yet canals needed constant care. The rivers carried so much mud that the channels slowly filled up, and every year they had to be dug out again. A neglected canal meant dry fields at the far end, and an empty harvest. Keeping the water flowing was a duty shared by every family, and by the king.
===
IMG: ancient-sumerian-ruins-ziggurat | the weathered stepped ruins of an old brick city beside reed beds in soft light
HEAD: By Nebuchadnezzar's time, the first cities in the world were already ancient history.
BODY: More than two thousand years before our story, in this same southern land, a people called the Sumerians built the first cities anywhere. They raised temples and walls and chose kings. They pressed the first writing into wet clay with the end of a reed. They made wheeled carts, ploughs and sailing boats, and they wrote down the first great poems. By the year 600 BC, the Sumerians themselves were long gone, and many of their cities lay half buried in sand. Yet the Babylonians had not forgotten them. Scribes still copied the old Sumerian texts, and priests still sang the old prayers in the old tongue, a little the way Latin lived on in churches in later times. To a Babylonian child, the past was not a distant thing. It was a deep, respected layer beneath every street, and Babylon felt like the newest page in a very long book.
===
IMG: southern-marsh-reed-houses-canoe | arched reed houses on a green island in a wide marsh, a man poling a slim canoe, buffalo wading
HEAD: In the far south, people lived in houses made of living reeds, much as their ancestors had.
BODY: If you followed the Euphrates all the way down towards the sea, the river would spread out into a great wetland. There, beyond the last villages, lay tall reed marshes full of birds, fish and still, silvery water. People lived in the marshes in houses woven from bundles of giant reeds, bent into graceful arches like the ribs of a ship. They travelled in slim canoes and kept water buffalo, and they caught fish and wildfowl for dinner. Their way of life was already old when the first cities were built, and remarkably, a similar one continued in the same region for thousands of years. For a Babylonian townsman, the marshes were a wild, green, slightly mysterious place at the bottom of the map. But they were also part of the same great country. The reeds fed the city's boats, mats and baskets, and a little of the marsh went home with every trader.
===
IMG: babylon-old-capital-hammurabi-era | an ancient brick city rising beside the river, rebuilt walls and scaffolds among palms
HEAD: Babylon was already more than fifteen hundred years old when Nebuchadnezzar made it great.
BODY: The city was not new. It had stood on the Euphrates for well over a thousand years, and long ago it had been the capital of a famous king called Hammurabi. Since then, Babylon had risen and fallen many times. It had been conquered, burned, rebuilt and conquered again. There had been long years when it was only one town among many, and years when a fierce empire from the north held it in an iron grip. Yet each time, the city came back. People loved it, and the god Marduk was thought to live there. Then, in the lifetime of Nebuchadnezzar's father, the northern empire collapsed, and Babylon was free once more. The new family of kings made an extraordinary decision. They would not simply rule the old city. They would rebuild it, from the walls to the gates to the holy road, and make it grander than it had ever been.
===
IMG: traveller-boat-euphrates-morning | a broad slow river at dawn with a laden boat, mud-brick villages and palm groves along the banks
HEAD: The best way to reach Babylon was by boat, drifting down the river for many days.
BODY: Imagine you are a traveller coming from the northern mountains with a cargo of wine and timber. You would load it onto a boat or a raft and let the Euphrates carry you south. The journey would take days. You would sleep on deck under the stars and watch the land slide by. There would be steep banks, mud-brick villages, groves of palms, and flocks of sheep and goats coming down to drink. Boys fishing from the shore would wave as you passed. The farther south you went, the flatter and greener the land became. Canals branched away on both sides. The villages grew closer and larger, and the river became busier with rafts, barges and small round boats turning slowly in the current. It was a long, calm, drifting approach, and it gave travellers plenty of time to wonder what waited at the end. Nothing they had seen before could quite prepare them.
===
IMG: first-sight-of-tower-haze | a distant stepped tower glowing blue above palm trees on the horizon in morning haze
HEAD: One morning, far across the plain, travellers saw a blue light shining on the horizon.
BODY: Nobody forgot the first sight of Babylon. On a clear morning, the boatman would point ahead, and there, rising from the haze above the palms, you would see a great stepped tower. Its top shone blue in the early sunshine, a tiny gleaming spark in a flat world with no hills. It could be seen from many miles away. As the boat drifted closer, the walls appeared, long and brown, stretching away along the water, with hundreds of towers marching beside them. Smoke rose from the chimneys of the city. Behind the walls you could make out temple roofs and the green tops of gardens. The boat would come to rest at a busy quay outside the gate, among dozens of others, where porters carried sacks up the steps, scribes counted every load on clay, and everyone shouted in a different language. Then you would walk inside, and the noise would swallow you.
===
TOPIC: Nebuchadnezzar and his palace
BRIDGE: The boats are tied up, and the gate stands open. But before we walk the streets, it helps to know who gave the city its shape. A single king stood behind almost everything we are about to see.
===
IMG: king-nebuchadnezzar-portrait-throne | a bearded king in a tall crown and richly embroidered robe seated in a columned hall
HEAD: Nebuchadnezzar's name means "O god Nabu, protect my son."
BODY: In the year 600 BC, the king of Babylon was Nebuchadnezzar, a man in his thirties who had already ruled for several years. His name was really a short prayer, asking the god of writing and wisdom to keep his child safe. His father had freed Babylon from the northern empire that held it, and the young prince had commanded armies even before he was crowned. He would go on to rule for forty-three years, one of the longest reigns in the history of the city. He appears in the Bible as a proud and powerful king, and the Greeks remembered him too. But in his own words, he saw himself as something rather different from a conqueror. He wanted people to remember a king who looked after the gods and made his city beautiful. To see whether he succeeded, we need only look at what he left in the ground.
===
IMG: king-inspecting-building-works-scribes | a king in a long robe inspecting brick workers and surveyors beside a rising wall
HEAD: Nebuchadnezzar rarely wrote about his battles. He wrote about his buildings.
BODY: The king had long inscriptions made on clay cylinders, stone slabs and bricks, and he buried many of them beneath his construction work for future kings to find. What do they say? Mostly, they list buildings. Temples he restored for the gods. Walls he strengthened. Canals he dug. Palaces he raised. He describes laying the foundations, bringing cedar from distant mountains, and covering gates with bronze and doorposts with silver. You rarely read about triumphs or enemies. Instead, you read about a man who wanted to be remembered as a faithful builder, a servant of Marduk and a friend of his city. This may be partly good public relations, as all kings have a taste for that. But the bricks agree with him. During his long reign, he rebuilt almost all of Babylon, and the dust and noise of his construction sites must have been part of daily life for forty years.
===
IMG: stamped-bricks-king-name-closeup | a close view of fired mud bricks, each pressed with a line of wedge-shaped writing, stacked in a yard
HEAD: Millions of bricks were stamped with the king's name before they were even baked.
BODY: To build a city of this size, you need an enormous number of bricks. Brick makers worked in huge yards beside the river, and on many bricks meant for royal buildings, they pressed a stamp into the wet clay. It said, in neat wedge-shaped writing, that the brick belonged to Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon, son of Nabopolassar, king of Babylon. Imagine the work. Every wall, every pavement and every temple was covered in tiny repeating signatures. Today archaeologists have found these stamped bricks by the thousand in the ruins, and they now sit in museums around the world. Local villagers also dug them out of the mounds for centuries and used them to build their own houses, so the name of the king can still be found in walls far from Babylon. He hoped that his name would last forever. In a small and slightly humbling way, it did, one brick at a time.
===
IMG: southern-palace-courtyards-babylon | a vast palace of many courtyards with tall glazed gateways and palm trees in warm light
HEAD: The royal palace was like a small city inside the city, with five great courtyards in a row.
BODY: Near the river and the northern gate stood the Southern Palace, the king's main home and office. Visitors walked through one huge courtyard after another, each more impressive than the last. Around the courtyards were halls, offices, kitchens and storerooms, where officials and servants kept the machinery of an empire turning. There were rooms for the royal family, for the guards, for the treasury and for the great archives. Here the king's scribes wrote his letters and orders, and kept the accounts of the whole kingdom on baskets of clay tablets. Barley, oil and dates were counted into the stores here. Gold and silver were weighed here. The palace was not simply a house with a crown on top. It was the working heart of an empire that stretched from the sea in the south to the mountains in the north, and west almost to the border of Egypt. It never completely slept.
===
IMG: throne-room-blue-facade-lions | a long throne hall whose courtyard wall is covered in blue glazed bricks with slender golden columns and a row of lions
HEAD: The throne room had a front wall of shining blue bricks and a parade of golden lions.
BODY: At the heart of the palace lay the throne room, a long hall more than fifty metres from end to end, with three wide doorways opening onto the courtyard. Its outer wall glowed with colour. On a deep blue background, tall slender columns rose like palm trees, topped with bands of flowers, while beneath them a row of lions walked in white and gold. Visitors who entered the courtyard on a sunny morning must have been dazzled. Inside, opposite the main doorway, there was a deep niche in the wall, and in that niche stood the royal throne. From there the king could receive officials, priests and guests from across his empire, framed by the shadows of the room and seen from the far end of the hall. Much of this shining facade survived in pieces. Archaeologists found fragments of it in the ruins, and a careful reconstruction still shows how bright it must have been.
===
IMG: ambassadors-bowing-tribute-gifts | foreign envoys in different robes bowing before a throne with gifts and scribes taking notes
HEAD: Ambassadors had to walk through five courtyards before they could even see the king.
BODY: Imagine arriving at the palace as an envoy from Egypt or from one of the coastal cities, carrying gifts and a letter. You would be led through the courtyards one by one, with every gateway taller and every wall more splendid than the last. By the time you reached the final hall, you would feel very small. The king would be sitting at the far end, in the shadows, wearing rich robes and a tall crown, with his officials and guards standing around him. Everything was designed to impress, and it worked. Messengers bowed low, spoke carefully and chose their words with great attention, because a single careless sentence could make a king angry. Diplomacy in those days was done in person, with gifts, flattery and enormous patience. And all of it was written down by scribes on clay, so the king could remember exactly what had been promised, by whom and when.
===
IMG: palace-collection-old-statues-hall | a quiet palace hall with shelves of ancient statues, carved stones and clay tablets in lamplight
HEAD: The kings of Babylon collected antiques, and one palace held what has been called an early museum.
BODY: Nebuchadnezzar loved old things. In one of his palaces, archaeologists found a collection of statues, carved stones and inscribed objects that were already ancient in his day. Some came from other lands, taken as spoils of war or received as gifts. Some were pieces of Babylon's own earlier past. A statue from a far-off country, a stone with writing in a forgotten style, a carved piece that had probably belonged to an earlier king. There is no way to be sure that the objects were arranged for visitors to admire, but many scholars believe this was a kind of royal collection, with the past on display. It fits the character of the time. The kings of Babylon repaired old temples, copied old writings and searched for the foundation records of their ancestors. They respected the people who came before them, and they wanted to understand them. A king who collects history is also a king who knows he will one day be part of it.
===
IMG: palace-roof-walk-evening-city | a king walking alone on a high palace wall at sunset above the river, tower and temples glowing
HEAD: When the day's business ended, the great palace fell silent, and lamps were lit one by one.
BODY: In the evening, the visitors went to their rooms, the scribes walked home through the cooling streets, and the courtyards emptied. Small clay lamps burning sesame oil were lit along the corridors. The king might have taken a quiet walk along the high walls above the river, in the breeze. From there he could see the tower and the temples dark against the sunset, the river turning gold and red, and the lamps coming on in thousands of houses. Beyond the walls, the date palms stretched out towards the edge of the world. It is nice to imagine him pausing there for a moment. A man who had armies, tribute and a palace the size of a town, but who still stopped to look at the evening light on the water. Like everyone in Babylon, he lived under the same sky, and the same cool breeze reached him as well.
===
TOPIC: Walls, gates and the blue road
BRIDGE: The palace is quiet now. Let us step outside, because there is something enormous that all the city's visitors passed through first, and none of them ever forgot it.
===
IMG: double-walls-towers-moat | a double wall of mud brick with regular towers, a water-filled moat in front, glowing in low sun
HEAD: Babylon was protected by two enormous walls, with a moat of river water in front.
BODY: The inner city was almost square, and the Euphrates ran straight through the middle. Around it ran a double wall of mud brick, with a deep ditch full of river water in front of it. The inner wall was about six metres thick, and the outer one nearly four. Between them was a wide space, and every twenty metres or so along the whole length, a tower stood up above the line. There were hundreds of them. Beyond all this, an even larger outer wall formed a great triangle many kilometres around, enclosing gardens, fields and palm groves as well as the city. A besieging army would have to cross the moat, get through two walls under fire from hundreds of towers, and then face the streets. For centuries it was one of the strongest defences in the ancient world. People inside could sleep soundly. Or at least they hoped so.
===
IMG: walls-chariot-top-herodotus | a broad wall top with a team of horses and a small cart turning on it, the plain stretching behind
HEAD: One Greek visitor claimed that a four-horse chariot could turn around on top of the walls.
BODY: Later in history, a Greek traveller named Herodotus wrote about Babylon, and he described the walls with real astonishment. According to him, they were so wide that a four-horse chariot could drive along the top and even turn around between the little guard houses. He also gave exact figures for their height, and these are almost certainly too large. Herodotus was a wonderful storyteller, but he heard many of his facts from guides and passing talkers, and he sometimes repeated what they said without checking. Still, a kernel of truth lies behind his amazement. The walls really were remarkably broad, and wide enough for guards to patrol and for soldiers to move around quickly. The stories grew taller each time they were told. Most of us do the same, when we describe a place that impressed us. Babylon was not an exaggeration, but it did invite one.
===
IMG: eight-city-gates-god-names | one of the great city gates with towers and heavy bronze-covered doors, travellers and donkeys passing through
HEAD: Each of Babylon's eight great gates was named after a god.
BODY: Eight gates opened through the city walls, and none of them was just a hole in a wall. Each was a tall double gateway with towers, a long passage and heavy doors of cedar covered in bronze. Each had a name, and each name was a god. There was a gate of the sun god, a gate of the storm god, a gate of the moon god and a gate of the king of the gods himself. Passing through one was a small religious act, even if you were only a farmer bringing onions to the market. Guards stood on the towers all day and all night. At sunset the doors were closed and barred, and the gates were sealed against the dark. Inside the gate, the long passage was cool and dim, and people paused to let their eyes adjust. Then they emerged into the full noise of the city.
===
IMG: ishtar-gate-front-blue-towers | a vast gateway covered in deep blue glazed bricks, with rows of golden bulls and dragons in morning sun
HEAD: The most beautiful gate in the ancient world was covered from top to bottom in shining blue.
BODY: In the north of the city stood the Ishtar Gate, named for the goddess of love and the morning star. Nebuchadnezzar had it rebuilt more than once, each time higher and grander than before. Its two great towers framed a passage tall enough for the statues of the gods to roll through on their carts at festival time. And every surface was covered in glazed bricks of a deep, brilliant blue, as rich as lapis lazuli, the precious stone from the far mountains. On the blue, in long rows, walked animals made of moulded bricks that stood out from the wall. They shone in yellow, white and gold. Along the top ran battlements and bands of flowers. In the morning light, the whole gate glowed like a jewel. People who came to Babylon for the first time stopped in the road, just to stare. Some of them stood there for a long time.
===
IMG: dragon-and-bull-glazed-relief | a close view of a glazed brick relief of a horned dragon with a scaled body and a bull, blue background
HEAD: The animals on the gate were dragons, bulls and, above all, a mixture of several creatures.
BODY: The Ishtar Gate carried hundreds of animals, arranged in rows. Some were bulls with long blue horns, which belonged to the storm god. The others were dragons that the Babylonians called by a name meaning something like "the fierce snake." These creatures had the head of a serpent, a lion's front legs, an eagle's back feet, and a long tail ending in a scorpion's sting. They belonged to Marduk, the great god of the city. They were shown walking in solemn lines, heads held high, as though marching towards the city to guard it. Their scales and horns were picked out in yellow and white against the blue. It may sound fierce. But to the Babylonians, they were protectors. Strangers entering Babylon passed beneath hundreds of them, and the message was clear. This city was under watchful, powerful care.
===
IMG: glazed-bricks-moulds-craftsmen | craftsmen fitting numbered moulded bricks into a wall relief, a glaze pot nearby
HEAD: Every animal on the gate was assembled like a jigsaw puzzle from numbered bricks.
BODY: The animals were not painted onto the wall. Each brick was pressed into a special mould, so that one part of the creature stood out from it. A single brick might carry a hoof, a piece of mane, or the curve of a tail. Craftsmen glazed the bricks in blue, yellow, white and brown, with thin dark lines to keep the colours from running together. The glaze was a type of coloured glass, made from sand, plant ash and minerals. Then everything was baked again, until the surface became hard and shiny. Each brick was marked on its side with a small sign, so the builders knew exactly where it belonged. When all of them were laid in the right order, the whole animal appeared on the wall. One brick in the wrong place, and the lion would have a tail on its head. It was clever work, and a good reminder of how much patience went into every wonder.
===
IMG: processional-way-lions-blue | a long straight paved road between high blue walls lined with striding lions in white and gold
HEAD: The holy road was lined with about a hundred and twenty striding lions.
BODY: Beyond the Ishtar Gate, a broad straight street ran north and south through the city. It was about twenty metres wide, paved with big slabs of white and reddish stone, and walled on both sides with high, blue brick walls. On those walls, one after another, walked lions. There were about a hundred and twenty of them, sixty on each side, with white and golden bodies, open mouths and raised tails. The lion belonged to Ishtar, the goddess of the gate. They strode slowly and proudly towards the gate, and above and below them ran bands of flowers. Walking along the Processional Way must have felt like walking through a long, shining blue valley, with the lions keeping you company at every step. On ordinary days it was simply a busy street, full of donkeys, carts and traders. But a few days each year, it became something much grander.
===
IMG: gate-today-berlin-museum-hall | a great blue gateway rebuilt inside a large museum hall with visitors looking up
HEAD: You can still walk through a rebuilt part of the Ishtar Gate today, in a museum in Berlin.
BODY: After the gate was buried by time, it was found again at the beginning of the twentieth century. Thousands of broken, glazed bricks were carefully packed into crates and sent by ship to Berlin. There, museum workers spent years cleaning, sorting and fitting them together, like a giant puzzle with hundreds of thousands of pieces. The result was the front of the Ishtar Gate, along with part of the Processional Way and its lions, reconstructed inside a huge hall. It opened to the public in 1930. Since then, millions of people have walked through it. Some pause quietly, as visitors must have done in the time of Nebuchadnezzar. The blue is a little different in the electric light than in the Mesopotamian sun, but it is the same blue, glazed in the same city. Other lions from the holy road are spread across museums in Paris, London and New York. They still stride on their blue background.

TOPIC: The great tower and the house of Marduk
BRIDGE: Beyond the gate, the street opens out, and the buildings grow taller. At the heart of the city stands the thing that all the travellers saw first from the river.
===
IMG: etemenanki-ziggurat-seven-stages | a huge square stepped tower of brick rising in stages, a blue-tiled shrine on top, in a walled courtyard
HEAD: The great tower of Babylon was probably as tall as a building of thirty floors.
BODY: In the middle of the old city stood a ziggurat, a tower built in steps. Its name was Etemenanki, which means something like "the house that is the foundation of heaven and earth." It rose from a square base about ninety metres on each side, in stage after stage, each one smaller than the one below, like a staircase for giants. According to a clay tablet that has survived, it may have been about as tall as it was wide. For its time, it was the tallest building in the world. A wide triple stairway ran up the front, and at the very top stood a small temple for Marduk, covered in shining blue bricks, which glittered in the sun. People could see it across the flat plain from a long way off. Nothing else in all of Mesopotamia looked quite like it. Everyone who saw it felt a little smaller, and a little closer to the sky.
===
IMG: tower-of-babel-languages-market | a crowded market street with traders speaking and gesturing, the great tower rising behind the rooftops
HEAD: Many people believe this tower inspired the story of the Tower of Babel.
BODY: In the Bible, there is a story about people who tried to build a tower to reach heaven. God confused their languages, so they could no longer understand each other. The builders scattered across the earth. "Babel" is simply the Hebrew form of the name Babylon. Many scholars think the story was inspired by this very tower, which the exiles from Judah saw rising above the city. And you really could hear many languages in the streets of Babylon. There was Babylonian, Aramaic and Hebrew, Egyptian and Greek and Persian, all mixed together in the markets and quays. A traveller might understand every third word of a conversation. So the idea of a tower and a confusion of tongues fits the city well. A story can begin with a real building, and grow from there into something that belongs to everyone.
===
IMG: priests-climbing-ziggurat-stars | a small group of priests climbing the broad stairway of the tower at dusk, stars appearing
HEAD: Only priests were allowed to climb to the top, and there they watched the stars.
BODY: Why did the Babylonians build such a tower? They believed the gods lived in the heavens. The tower was a kind of bridge or stairway between the sky and the earth, by which the gods could come down to visit their city. The small temple at the top was a resting place for the god on his way. Ordinary people were never allowed up the stairs. Only priests climbed to the summit, where they made offerings and prayers high above the roofs. And at night, they did something else up there. They watched the sky, and wrote down what they saw. For a Babylonian priest, the top of the tower was both a place of worship and a good observatory, with a clear view across a flat plain in every direction. Few places in the ancient world had a finer seat beneath the stars.
===
IMG: ziggurat-base-today-reeds-water | the low square outline of an ancient brick base, partly filled with still water and reeds, at sunset
HEAD: Almost nothing remains of the great tower, but we know its measurements from a clay tablet.
BODY: Today, the tower is gone. Over the centuries, its bricks were taken away to build other things, and all that remains is the base, a square outline in the ground near the ruins, often filled with water and reeds. Yet we still know a good deal about how it looked. A Babylonian scribe wrote down its measurements on a clay tablet, and the tablet survived. Another account, by a Greek traveller, described its many levels, its winding stairway and the temple at the top. Together, they let us picture it quite clearly. The building does not stand, but its memory does. This is a pattern we will meet again and again in Babylon. The tall things fell, and the little clay things survived. A cheap tablet, forgotten in a pile of rubble, can outlast a tower that once touched the clouds.
===
IMG: esagila-temple-courtyard-priests | a large temple courtyard with thick walls, tall gateways, priests walking and incense rising
HEAD: Beside the tower stood the temple of Marduk, whose name means "the house whose top is high."
BODY: Right next to the ziggurat lay Esagila, the greatest temple in the city. It was not one building but a group of them, arranged around courtyards, with thick walls, tall gateways and many rooms for priests, treasures and gifts. In the holiest room of all lived the god himself. Marduk was a statue of precious wood covered in gold, dressed in rich robes sewn with gold and jewels, and seated on a golden throne before a golden table. A Greek writer later claimed that all this gold added up to an enormous weight, and the number is probably exaggerated. But the Babylonians themselves wrote that the sanctuary shone like the sun. People stood in the courtyard and looked towards the dim, golden doorway, with their hands raised to their faces in prayer. Through the day, the smell of incense drifted over the walls and into the streets.
===
IMG: priests-feeding-god-golden-table | priests laying out bread, fruit and cakes on a golden table before a gold-covered statue by lamplight
HEAD: Each day, priests woke the god, washed him, dressed him and served him two meals.
BODY: The god was looked after like a great king living in his palace. In the morning, priests woke him with songs and prayers. They washed the statue and dressed it in fresh clothes. Twice a day, they served him a meal. There were loaves and cakes, meat and fish, fruit and dates, with beer and wine, all set out on the golden table with music and incense. When the god had finished, the food was shared among the priests and temple workers, so nothing went to waste. In the evening, the priests put the god to bed, closed the doors of his room and left a few lamps burning. The temple grew quiet, and the guards kept watch. It might sound strange to us. But to the Babylonians, it was a simple act of care. If the god was happy and well fed in his house, the city beyond would be safe.
===
IMG: creation-poem-recited-priests | priests reciting from clay tablets in a lamplit temple hall, a mural of waves and stars behind them
HEAD: Every spring, the priests recited a poem about how Marduk made the world out of chaos.
BODY: The Babylonians had a great poem about the beginning of everything. At first, it said, there was nothing but water and darkness. A mighty goddess of the salt sea ruled over this chaos. Then the young god Marduk rose up and defeated her. From her body, he made the sky and the earth. He set the stars and the moon in their places, to mark the months and the seasons. Then the other gods built Babylon for him, as his home. Marduk had once been only a small local god of a single city. As Babylon grew great, so did he, until he became the king of all the gods. The poem was recited every year at the New Year festival, and it explained why the city was so important. In the Babylonian way of thinking, Babylon was not just a place on a map. It was where the world had started.
===
IMG: temple-workshops-scribes-bakers | a busy temple yard with bakers, weavers, scribes with clay tablets, and herdsmen bringing sheep
HEAD: The great temple was also a bank, a farm, a bakery and a school, with hundreds of workers.
BODY: A visitor expecting only prayer would have been surprised. Esagila employed hundreds of people. There were priests and singers, musicians and star watchers, but also cooks, bakers, brewers, weavers, goldsmiths, gardeners and herdsmen. The temple owned fields, palm groves and flocks all around the city, and collected a share of their produce. It lent out grain and silver. Its scribes kept careful records of every sack and every sheep on baked clay tablets. In a society without banks or government offices as we know them, the temple filled many roles at once. It was a religious centre, a storehouse, an employer and a source of help in hard times. People brought gifts to the god and asked for healing, for children, for justice and for good luck. And then, on the way out, they might stop at the temple workshop and buy a loaf of bread for the walk home.
