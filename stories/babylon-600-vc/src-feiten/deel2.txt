TOPIC: The river Euphrates
BRIDGE: The incense drifts out over the walls and down towards the water. Follow it, and you will come to the river that gave Babylon its life.
===
IMG: stone-bridge-boat-shaped-piers | a long bridge on boat-shaped brick piers crossing a broad river between two halves of a city
HEAD: A great bridge on boat-shaped pillars joined the two halves of the city.
BODY: The Euphrates flowed straight through Babylon and divided it in two. On the east bank lay the old city, with the great temples, the palace and the tower. On the west bank was the newer part, with its own streets, houses and gardens. To join them, the kings built one of the first great bridges in the ancient world. It was around a hundred and twenty metres long and rested on thick pillars of brick and stone. The pillars were shaped like boats, pointed at the front, so the current would slide around them without wearing them down. The road across was made of wooden beams, and at night the beams could be taken away. In that way, nobody could cross in the dark, and the two halves of the city slept behind their own walls. In the morning, the planks went back, and the whole city began to flow across again.
===
IMG: round-reed-boats-donkeys-cargo | round boats like giant baskets covered in dark leather on the river, loaded with jars and a donkey
HEAD: Some river boats were round, like enormous baskets coated in tar.
BODY: There were boats of every kind on the Euphrates. Some were long and narrow, with sails and oars. Some were rafts of logs floating on inflated goatskins, bringing stone, timber and wine down from the northern mountains. And some were round like a shield, made from woven reeds, covered in leather, and coated with a sticky black tar that seeped from the ground nearby. Herodotus, the Greek traveller, was fascinated by them. He wrote that a boat might carry a heavy load of wine jars and a donkey or two. When it reached Babylon, it was unloaded and taken apart. The leather was strapped on the donkeys' backs, and the donkeys walked home up the river by land, because the current was too strong to row against. Then, at home, someone built another round boat. It was a simple, thrifty and rather charming way to move cargo.
===
IMG: fishermen-dawn-nets-herons | fishermen casting nets from small boats at dawn among reeds with herons and ducks
HEAD: At dawn, the river was full of fishermen, herons and kingfishers.
BODY: Long before the markets opened, small boats slipped out onto the Euphrates. Fishermen stood in the bows, casting their nets in wide circles that spread across the water like open hands. They brought back fish for the morning stalls, to be grilled, dried or salted. The banks were full of other life too. Herons stood very still in the shallows. Ducks, egrets and pelicans floated among the reeds, and kingfishers flashed blue and orange between the stems. In the middle of a huge city, the river still felt like countryside, especially in the first grey light, when the mist lay on the water and the only sound was a paddle dipping. Many a Babylonian began the day by walking down to the quay and breathing the river air. It was not a bad way to wake up, even in the biggest city in the world.
===
IMG: spring-flood-platforms-houses | houses standing on raised brick platforms with spring floodwater creeping across the fields behind them
HEAD: Every spring, melting mountain snow made the river rise, and the city prepared for a flood.
BODY: The Euphrates was a generous river, but not always a gentle one. Far to the north, in spring, the snow melted in the mountains and the river swelled. Sometimes it spilled over its banks, across the fields and towards the city. Everyone watched the water creep higher, day by day. The Babylonians were ready for it. They built their houses on raised platforms. They set their temples on great terraces. They kept the dams and canals in good repair, and they prayed to the gods to hold the floods back. In the end, a flood was a normal part of life. It brought trouble, but it also brought fresh silt, which made the fields rich again. The Babylonian farmer lived somewhere between gratitude and worry, as farmers by rivers have always done. He knew that the thing which fed him could, one wet spring, come to his door.
===
IMG: quay-evening-families-water | families sitting on brick steps at the river's edge at sunset, children paddling, boats coming home
HEAD: In the evening, half of Babylon came down to the water to cool off.
BODY: When the sun sank behind the palms on the west bank, the river turned red and gold. The fishing boats came home, and the traders tied up their barges for the night. People left their hot houses and walked down to the brick steps that led to the water. They filled their jars, washed their faces and sat with their feet in the cool current. Children splashed in the shallows. Old men talked about the price of barley, and women compared the day's news. A little breeze came off the river, carrying the smell of reeds and wet clay. For a few minutes before dark, the whole city seemed to breathe out. The great tower, the temples and the walls were all quiet silhouettes, and the water slid past them, unhurried. It had been doing so for thousands of years, and it showed no sign of stopping.
===
TOPIC: A house in Babylon
BRIDGE: The sun has set, and lamps are being lit in the narrow streets. Let us follow one family home through a low wooden door, and see how an ordinary Babylonian lived.
===
IMG: plain-street-low-wooden-door | a narrow dusty street between tall windowless mud-brick walls with a single low wooden door
HEAD: From the street, a Babylonian house looked like a plain wall with a door.
BODY: The streets of Babylon were narrow, winding and unpaved, dusty in summer and muddy after winter rain. The houses along them showed almost nothing to the passer-by. There was a high wall of mud brick, with no windows, and a single low wooden door. It looked plain, even unfriendly. But that was the whole idea. The thick blank walls kept out the heat, the dust and the noise, and gave each family some privacy. Behind the door, everything was quite different. Passing through, you stepped into a small entrance room, and then out into an open courtyard, bright with sunlight and full of life. Many cities of the warm Middle East still have houses built on exactly this plan, with a quiet heart hidden from the street. In Babylon, the wall was plain and the home inside was lively, and everybody understood that.
===
IMG: courtyard-house-family-morning | a brick-paved courtyard open to the sky with rooms around it, a woman at a loom, children playing in shade
HEAD: The heart of the house was an open courtyard where the family cooked, worked and played.
BODY: The courtyard was paved with bricks and open to the sky. All the rooms of the house stood around it, and the door of each one opened onto it. Here the family cooked in the morning, wove in the shade at midday, and talked at night. Children played here, and so did the dog. On the south side, there was often a long room that faced north, so it stayed cool even in the worst heat of the day. Guests were welcomed there. There were bedrooms, storerooms for grain, oil and dates, and a kitchen with a clay oven. Perhaps there was a small washing room with a drain. The walls were thick, and the rooms were cool and dark, lit only by the light from the doorways. Floors of beaten earth or brick were covered in reed mats and woollen rugs. It was a simple house, but it was cleverly made for its climate.
===
IMG: house-furniture-lamps-chests | a dim room with a low wooden bed, cushions, a chest and clay jars, one small oil lamp burning
HEAD: There was little furniture, but every house had lamps, chests and plenty of baskets.
BODY: A Babylonian home was not crowded with furniture. People sat on mats, stools and cushions, and ate from low tables. Beds were low wooden frames with mattresses and blankets. Clothes and valuables were kept in wooden chests with tidy lids. Clay jars held water, oil, grain and beer, and baskets stood in every corner, filled with dates, wool, bread and tools. At night, the light came from small clay lamps that burned sesame oil, giving off a soft, flickering glow that threw gentle shadows on the walls. There was no glass, no chimney, and no table lamp as we think of one. But a single little flame was enough for a quiet meal and a story. Even now, in a dark room, a candle makes people speak more softly. Perhaps something in us still remembers that warm, close, golden light.
===
IMG: flat-roof-family-sleeping-stars | a family lying on reed mats on a flat mud roof under a starry summer sky, other roofs around them
HEAD: In summer, the whole family climbed a ladder and slept on the roof under the stars.
BODY: The flat roof was another room of the house. It was made of palm trunks and reed mats, covered with thick, smooth mud, and reached by a wooden ladder. During the day, people dried dates, grain and washing there. In the cool of the evening, they sat on the roof and talked with the neighbours on the next roofs. And in the hot summer, when the rooms were stifling, the whole family slept there, lying on mats under the open sky in the breeze. All over Babylon on summer nights, the rooftops were full of sleeping families. They looked up at the stars, talked quietly, and slowly drifted off to the sounds of the city. Dogs barked in the lanes. Frogs croaked in the canals. Far off, the watchmen called the hours from the walls. If you ever wonder how to fall asleep, start with this picture.
===
IMG: baking-flatbread-clay-oven-dawn | a woman slapping flat round dough onto the inside wall of a glowing clay oven in a courtyard at dawn
HEAD: Breakfast began with flat loaves slapped onto the hot wall of a clay oven.
BODY: In a typical Babylonian family, the day began at dawn, when light slid into the courtyard and the birds woke in the palm tree next door. A servant girl lit the fire in the clay oven in the corner. Then the mother made bread. She shaped flat round loaves and slapped them against the hot inside wall of the oven, where they baked in a few minutes. She peeled them off, soft and steaming and smelling wonderful. The family ate breakfast sitting on mats in the courtyard. There was bread and dates, a little cheese, some onions, and a drink of thick, sweet barley beer. Everyone had some, even the children. It was a simple meal, but it set the day in motion. The same method of baking bread is still used in many parts of the region today, and the smell is just as good.
===
IMG: scribe-father-walking-to-temple | a man with a basket of clay tablets and a reed pen walking through a busy lane with donkeys and water carriers
HEAD: The father of our imaginary family was a scribe who wrote contracts for the temple.
BODY: Let us borrow a typical household. The father worked as a scribe at the temple of Marduk. He wrote contracts and accounts on clay tablets, and every morning he walked to work through narrow lanes that were already busy. Donkeys plodded past with sacks. Water carriers hurried by with jars. Sellers called out their wares. He carried a sharp reed pen for writing and a little basket of clay tablets. His eldest son, who was twelve, went off to a teacher's house to learn the same trade. The mother ran the home and wove cloth, and she also had a little side business, lending small amounts of silver to her neighbours. There were four children, a grandmother, a servant girl and a small dog. This was not a rich family, but it was comfortable, respected, and well supplied with news about the city.
===
IMG: women-spinning-wool-afternoon-shade | women and girls spinning wool and weaving in the shade of a courtyard in the afternoon, a child with a clay toy
HEAD: In the heat of the afternoon, everyone rested, while the women spun and talked in the shade.
BODY: By midday, the sun beat down on the courtyard like a hammer, and the streets emptied. Work slowed or stopped. People retreated into the cool dark rooms to doze. In the shaded corner of the courtyard, the mother sat at her loom or spun wool on a spindle, as she had done for most of her life. Her sister might come to visit from the next street, and the two would talk for hours while their hands kept moving. Children played quietly with clay toys, little wheeled carts on strings, spinning tops and balls. The only sounds were the creak of the loom, the hum of flies, and a distant donkey. It was a deeply peaceful hour, and it was repeated on almost every afternoon of the year. When the heat finally broke, life began again, a little slower than before.
===
IMG: women-signing-contracts-witnesses | a woman pressing a cylinder seal into a clay tablet in front of a scribe and two witnesses
HEAD: Babylonian women could own property, sign contracts and win cases in court.
BODY: Most women in Babylon spent their lives as wives and mothers, running the house and raising children. But they had more rights than women in many other ancient lands. A woman could own houses, fields and silver. She could buy and sell, lend and borrow, make contracts and act as a witness. If she had a quarrel, she could go to court. When she married, her family gave her a dowry of furniture, clothes, jewellery and sometimes land, and it stayed hers all her life. It was written carefully in the marriage contract on clay. If her husband sent her away without good reason, he had to give it all back. Some contracts even promised that if the husband took a second wife, he would pay the first one a large sum of silver. Of course, not everything was equal. But these women had a real voice, and clay tablets still preserve it.
===
IMG: businesswomen-weavers-taverns | women running a busy weaving workshop with looms and wool, and another woman serving beer from large jars
HEAD: Some women ran taverns, weaving workshops and even family businesses.
BODY: Women worked outside the home as well. Many wove in the large workshops of the temples and the palace. Others brewed beer and ran taverns, sold goods in the streets, or worked as nurses and midwives. Some became writers, singers and musicians, and some served as priestesses. In the records of Babylon, we meet women who bought and sold houses, lent silver and grain, rented out fields, and managed the family property when their husbands were away. In the papers of one wealthy family, the wives and mothers appear again and again, signing and witnessing. The Babylonians also respected women as keepers of the house and of family memory. They had a saying that a house without a wife is like a field without a plough. At the end of the day, mothers, daughters and grandmothers sat together on the rooftops, combing each other's hair and watching the sun go down.
===
IMG: grandmother-telling-stories-gilgamesh | a grandmother telling stories to children on a rooftop at dusk, a city of lamps behind them
HEAD: At night, grandmothers told the children about Gilgamesh and the great flood.
BODY: On the roof, after supper, the grandmother told stories. There were tales of gods and heroes, and above all of Gilgamesh, the great king of an ancient city, and his wild friend Enkidu. She told about their adventures, their journey and their sorrow. Then she told about the flood. Long ago, the gods sent water over the whole earth. But one wise man was warned in time. He built a great boat and saved his family and the animals. Would the boat survive the storm? The children always held their breath at that point, even though they knew how it ended. Stories like these had been told for more than a thousand years, and were written down on clay long before our time. Yet they still worked best when spoken aloud, by lamplight, with the stars overhead, to a child just beginning to feel sleepy.
===
TOPIC: Dates, barley and beer
BRIDGE: The children are asleep now, one by one, under the stars. While they dream, let us think about the food and drink that kept Babylon going, which began in the fields and groves around it.
===
IMG: date-palm-grove-harvest | tall date palms in a dense grove with a climber on a rope harvesting heavy bunches of dates
HEAD: To the Babylonians, the date palm was the tree of life.
BODY: Around the city, along every river and canal, stood groves of date palms. They were tall and slender, with crowns of long feathery leaves that whispered in the wind. In late summer, heavy bunches of dates hung beneath them, turning orange, red and brown. The Babylonians used nearly every part of the tree. The fruit was sweet and filling, and it could be eaten fresh, dried for months, or pressed into a syrup like honey. The leaves were woven into baskets, mats and roofs. The fibres became rope. The trunks became beams and bridges, and even the stones were burned for fuel or fed to animals. Nothing went to waste. Underneath the palms, in their shade, farmers grew pomegranates, figs, grapes, onions and lettuce, so the groves were like cool green gardens in the burning summer. They were one of the loveliest parts of the country.
===
IMG: palm-pollination-climber-flowers | a farmer high on a palm trunk tying a bunch of flowers among the fronds, tied to the tree by a rope
HEAD: Date palms needed help from farmers, who climbed the trees to pollinate them by hand.
BODY: A date palm is either male or female, and only the female trees bear fruit. In nature, the wind carries pollen from one tree to another, but it is not always reliable. So every spring, the farmers climbed the tall trunks, wrapped in a rope that circled both the man and the tree. It was a long way up, and one slip would be dangerous. At the top, a farmer tied a small bunch of flowers from a male tree among the flowers of a female tree. With that done, the dates would grow. Babylonians understood this perfectly, and they treated the work with great care. Farmers in Iraq still pollinate their palms by hand, almost exactly as their ancestors did. It is the sort of knowledge that passes quietly from grandfather to grandson, through thousands of springs, without ever needing to be written down at all.
===
IMG: shaduf-lifting-water-field | a farmer lifting a bucket on a long balanced pole beside a canal to water a green barley field
HEAD: A balanced pole with a bucket on one end and a lump of clay on the other lifted water into the fields.
BODY: Getting water from the canal up into the field was hard work, so farmers invented a clever tool. It was a long pole balanced on an upright post. A bucket hung from one end and a heavy lump of clay from the other. The farmer pulled the bucket down into the canal, and the weight of the clay helped him lift it again. Then he swung the pole around and emptied the bucket into a channel that carried the water to his plants. With a tool like this, a single man could lift a surprising amount of water in a day. It was cheap, simple and strong, and it needed no animals. Versions of it are still seen along rivers in the region today. Sometimes the best inventions are the ones nobody ever needs to improve, and this was one of them.
===
IMG: seed-plough-oxen-barley-field | a farmer behind a pair of oxen with a wooden plough that has a funnel on top, sowing barley in a brown field
HEAD: Mesopotamian farmers invented a plough with a built-in funnel that planted seeds as it moved.
BODY: Barley was the most important crop in Babylon. It gave the people bread and it gave them beer, and it was also used to pay wages. To plant it, farmers used a wooden plough pulled by oxen. On top of the plough was a funnel. The farmer dropped seeds into it, and they fell straight into the furrow as the plough moved forward. This saved a great deal of time, and wasted far fewer seeds than scattering them by hand. It was a clever invention of this land, and it was used for centuries. In spring, the barley ripened, and the harvest came in April and May. Farmers cut it with curved blades, donkeys carried it to threshing floors, and oxen walked round and round on it to shake the grain loose. Then the wind blew away the husks, and the golden grain went into sacks.
===
IMG: beer-drinkers-straws-large-jar | friends seated around a large clay jar drinking through long reed straws, a lamp glowing
HEAD: Everybody in Babylon drank beer, and they drank it through straws.
BODY: Babylonian beer was not much like the beer we know. It was made from barley, and it was thick, cloudy and sweet, and not very strong. It was more like a liquid food than a drink. Men, women and children had it every day with their meals. To make it, brewers soaked barley until it sprouted, dried it, ground it and baked it into loaves. They crumbled the loaves into water in big jars, added dates for sweetness, and left the mixture to bubble for a few days. The result had bits of grain floating on top, so people drank through long reed straws, and some straws had little filters at the bottom. Old pictures from the region show friends sitting around one big jar, all sipping through their straws and chatting. It was a friendly and rather sociable way to have a drink.
===
IMG: tavern-keeper-woman-serving | a woman tavern keeper pouring from a big jar for a few guests in a small lamplit room
HEAD: Taverns were often run by women, and the old laws had rules for them.
BODY: In the evening, people liked to gather in a tavern to drink, talk and listen to music. Many were run by women who brewed their own beer. More than a thousand years before our story, the laws of Hammurabi already had rules for tavern keepers. They had to sell fair amounts at fair prices, and if they cheated customers, they could be punished. There was also wine in Babylon, though it was expensive. It came down the river on rafts from the northern mountains, and it was drunk by the wealthy and offered to the gods. A goddess even watched over beer, which tells you how seriously the Babylonians took it. A good tavern was a place where friends met, news was swapped and a long day ended well. It was not very different from the corner pubs we know. The jars were bigger, the straws were longer, and the lamps were smaller.
===
IMG: clay-moulds-cakes-dates-honey | a kitchen table with small clay moulds, honey cakes shaped like fish and stars, dates and sesame
HEAD: The oldest cookbook in the world was written on clay in this land.
BODY: The Babylonians loved their food, and they had dozens of kinds of bread and cake. They made flat breads baked on oven walls, round loaves, and sweet cakes of honey, dates, sesame and butter. Some were baked in clay moulds shaped like fish, animals or stars. Remarkably, the oldest known collection of recipes in the world comes from this land. A handful of clay tablets, written more than a thousand years before Nebuchadnezzar, describe stews of lamb, pigeon and duck, cooked with onions, leeks, garlic and herbs. In our own time, some cooks have followed them to see how the food tasted. In the evening, the smell of baking bread, onions and stew drifted over the rooftops, together with wood smoke. Families gathered in courtyards and on roofs to eat, talk about the day, and enjoy a meal that was, in many ways, not so far from our own.
===
TOPIC: Trade, money and justice
BRIDGE: The smell of supper is fading, and the lamps are going out one by one. But there is a side of Babylon that never quite slept, the busy world of buying, selling and settling arguments.
===
IMG: market-street-merchants-donkeys | a crowded street of small shops and stalls with pottery, textiles and spices, merchants and laden donkeys
HEAD: The land had barley and dates and clay, but almost no stone, no metal and no good wood.
BODY: Babylon was rich, but it lacked many things. The country had plenty of barley, dates, wool, reeds and clay. But it had almost no stone, almost no useful timber except palm, and no metal at all. So nearly everything else had to come from far away, and the traders of Babylon traded with the whole known world. Cedar for the temples came down the rivers on rafts from the western mountains. Copper, tin, iron, silver and gold arrived from the northern and eastern hills. A deep blue stone flecked with gold, the Babylonians' favourite, came from lands far to the east. Incense and spices came from Egypt and Arabia, and ivory, cotton and gems from India by sea. In return, Babylon sent out barley, dates, sesame oil and, above all, beautiful dyed woollen cloth, which was famous everywhere.
===
IMG: camel-caravan-arriving-at-gate | a line of camels and donkeys loaded with bundles arriving at a city gate at sunset
HEAD: Caravans crossed the desert from one water hole to the next, and missing one could be fatal.
BODY: Goods travelled in long lines of donkeys, and more and more on camels, which were becoming common. The caravans crossed the dry lands from one water hole to the next. The journey was long and hot, and the traders knew every well on the way. If they missed one, they could be in real danger. Other merchants moved their goods by boat along the rivers, down to the sea and out into the wider ocean. Babylon did not have a single great market square. Trade happened everywhere, at the city gates, on the quays where boats unloaded, in the streets, in little shops, in the temples and in the courtyards of rich merchant families. When a caravan came in at the end of the day, the whole street seemed to wake up. Camels groaned, bells rang and everyone asked the same question. What have you brought?
===
IMG: silver-scales-weights-merchant | a merchant weighing small pieces of silver on a balance scale with stone weights, a clay tablet beside him
HEAD: There were no coins in Babylon. People paid by weighing out silver.
BODY: Coins had only just been invented, far to the west, and they had not yet reached Babylon. So people paid in silver, weighed on scales, in little pieces, bars and rings. The usual unit was the shekel, which was simply a weight. Barley served as money too, and wages were often paid in grain or oil. Everything had its price in shekels and barley, from a house to an ox to a jar of sesame oil. Traders carried small scales and sets of stone weights, often shaped like ducks or little domes. To a modern eye, it would look very slow. Yet it worked, and it worked for centuries. And since silver could be cut, melted and weighed, people had to trust one another, or at least trust a scribe. Which brings us to the thing that held the whole system together.
===
IMG: contracts-clay-seals-witnesses | a scribe pressing a clay tablet while two merchants roll cylinder seals beside their names, witnesses watching
HEAD: Every sale, loan, marriage and adoption was written on a clay tablet and sealed.
BODY: In Babylon, nothing important happened without a scribe. When people bought a house, lent silver, rented a boat, married or adopted a child, they went to a writer. He wrote a contract on a clay tablet with the names of those involved, the witnesses and the date. Then everyone pressed their seal into the clay. Some, who had no seal, simply pressed a fingernail. Thousands of these contracts survive. They tell us the price of a house, an ox or a field. They tell us the rent of a boat and the wages of a worker. One famous family of bankers and traders appears in more than a thousand tablets across five generations. We can follow their business, their marriages, even their quarrels, for over a century. It is remarkable to know so much about ordinary people who lived two and a half thousand years ago.
===
IMG: hammurabi-stele-black-stone | a tall black stone pillar carved with a king before a seated sun god at the top and close lines of wedge writing below
HEAD: A black stone pillar carved with nearly three hundred laws stood in Babylon's temple.
BODY: More than a thousand years before our story, the great king Hammurabi had his laws carved on a tall black stone, more than two metres high. At the top, the king stood before the sun god, the god of justice, who handed him the law. Below, in neat close lines, were nearly three hundred rules. They dealt with property, trade and wages, debts, marriage and divorce, fields, canals, boats and animals, and with doctors, builders and tavern keepers. Some punishments were harsh, in the old way. But many laws were about fairness and protecting the weak. Hammurabi explained that he wanted the strong not to crush the weak, and widows and orphans to receive justice. By 600 BC, the stone was very old, but scribes still copied the laws as a classic text. Centuries later, the stone was carried away to another city as a prize of war, and finally found by French archaeologists in 1901.
===
IMG: judges-at-temple-gate-oath | judges seated at a temple gate listening to two neighbours and their witnesses, scribes holding tablets
HEAD: When people quarrelled, the judges sat at the temple gates and asked them to swear an oath.
BODY: If two neighbours could not settle a dispute, they went to the judges. These sat at temple gates or in courts, listened to both sides and the witnesses, and examined the contracts on their tablets. Then they decided. Sometimes they asked a person to swear an oath before the gods. That was a very serious thing. People's hands shook when they did it, because nobody wanted to lie before a god. And everything was written down on clay, the case, the witnesses, the decision and the date. Thanks to that, we can still read about a quarrel over the edge of a field, or the return of a wedding gift, or the payment of a debt, and learn how a judge in Babylon decided. Most arguments never got that far. The elders of a street would sit down together in a courtyard in the evening, over a jar of beer, and talk until everyone agreed.
