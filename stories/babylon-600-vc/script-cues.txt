{img:ancient-babylon-600bc-city-walls-ziggurat-euphrates-dawn-palms}
Tonight we go more than two and a half thousand years back. We go to the great city of Babylon, around the year 600 before Christ.

[long-pause]

It is early morning on a wide flat plain. The sun rises out of the brown land in the east. Its first light falls on a great river, the Euphrates. It is wide and slow and brown. It flows between banks of reeds and groves of date palms. And beside the river, out of the morning haze, rises a city.

[pause]

It is the biggest city in the world. People say its walls are so thick that a chariot with four horses can drive along the top and turn round. Behind them are temples and palaces and houses and gardens. And above them all rises a great stepped tower. Level after level, it climbs toward the sky, with a temple at the very top.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

This is Babylon.

[pause]

{img:ishtar-gate-blue-glazed-bricks-golden-lions-dragons-morning-light}
Its name is still famous today. It is in the Bible and in old stories and songs. The Tower of Babel. The Hanging Gardens. The rivers of Babylon. And yet, for a very long time, Babylon was only a name. A mound of earth and broken bricks in the desert.

[pause]

In the year 600 before Christ, it is at the height of its glory. It is the capital of a great empire. It reaches from the sea in the south to the mountains in the north. It reaches west all the way to the Mediterranean Sea and the border of Egypt. And it is ruled by one of the most famous kings of the ancient world. His name is Nebuchadnezzar. But even a great empire can have enemies watching from the edges.

[long-pause]

Tonight we will walk through Babylon. We will pass through the great blue gate with its golden lions and dragons. We will walk along the holy road. We will climb toward the great tower and visit the temple of the god Marduk. We will spend a day with a family in their mud brick house and sleep on the flat roof under the stars. We will sit in a school where boys learn to write on clay. We will watch the star watchers on their towers at night. And we will join the great festival of the New Year.

[pause]

{img:babylon-street-mudbrick-houses-people-donkeys-morning-market}
So let us walk together along the river bank, in the cool of the early morning. Among the palm trees and the reeds and the fishing boats. Toward the great walls and the gates of Babylon.

[long-pause]

{img:mesopotamia-plain-two-rivers-irrigation-canals-palms-fields-aerial}
Babylon lies in a land called Mesopotamia. In Greek, that means the land between the rivers.

[pause]

The two rivers are the Tigris and the Euphrates. They start in the mountains far to the north. They flow south for more than a thousand kilometres across the plains. Near Babylon they come close together. Then they flow on to the sea. Today most of this land is in the country of Iraq.

[long-pause]

It is a flat land of rich brown soil. The rivers brought it down over thousands of years. But it hardly ever rains here. The summers are long and very hot and dry. Without the rivers, nothing would grow.

[pause]

But with the rivers, everything grows. For thousands of years, people here have dug canals. These are channels that carry river water out to the fields and gardens and palm groves. So along the rivers and the canals, the land is green and rich. There are fields of barley and wheat, gardens of vegetables, and great forests of date palms. But the canals must be kept clean. If they fill with mud, the fields will dry and die.

[pause]

{img:ancient-sumerian-city-ruins-ziggurat-ur-reed-marsh-boats}
This is one of the places where cities began.

[long-pause]

More than five thousand years ago, here in the south, a people called the Sumerians built the first cities in the world. They had temples and walls and kings. They invented writing, the first writing in the world, pressed into clay with a reed. They made the wheel and the plough and the sailboat. They wrote the first laws and the first great stories.

[pause]

By the year 600 before Christ, all this is already ancient history. The Sumerians are long gone. Their cities are old, and some are ruins. But the Babylonians remember them and honour them. They copy their writings and prayers. And they still use their old language in the temples, a bit like Latin is used in some churches.

[pause]

{img:reed-marshes-southern-mesopotamia-reed-houses-canoe-water-buffalo}
In the far south, near the sea, there are great marshes of reeds and water. People live there in houses made of reeds. They travel in canoes and fish and keep water buffalo, as they have for thousands of years.

[long-pause]

And in the middle of this ancient land, on the river Euphrates, lies Babylon. It has been here for more than fifteen hundred years. Long ago it was the capital of a great king called Hammurabi. He wrote down one of the first big sets of laws in the world. Since then, Babylon has been taken and destroyed and rebuilt, again and again.

[pause]

And now a new family of kings has made it great once more. Greater than it has ever been.

[long-pause]

{img:traveller-boat-down-euphrates-palms-villages-towers-distance-morning}
Let us imagine we are travellers coming to Babylon for the first time.

[pause]

We have come from the north, down the river, on a boat carrying wine and wood from the mountains. It has taken many days. We have slept on the deck under the stars. We have watched the land go by. Steep banks and mud brick villages. Fields and palm groves. Sheep and goats coming down to drink. And boys fishing from the banks, who wave as we pass.

[long-pause]

As we go further south, the land gets flatter and greener. Canals branch off from the river in every direction. The villages get closer together and bigger. The river gets busier, with boats and rafts and round baskets that spin slowly in the current.

[pause]

And one morning, early, the boatman points ahead down the river. There, on the horizon, rising out of the haze above the palm trees, we see it. A great stepped tower standing against the sky. Its top shines blue in the first sunlight. The tower of Babylon.

[pause]

{img:approaching-babylon-river-view-walls-towers-ziggurat-haze-boats}
As we come closer, we see the walls. They are long and high and brown. They stretch away on both sides of the river. There are towers all along them, hundreds of them, like the teeth of a great comb. Behind them, smoke rises from the city. We see the tops of the temples and the palms in the gardens.

[long-pause]

Our boat comes in to the quay outside the walls. Dozens of other boats are tied up there and unloading. Porters carry jars and sacks up the steps on their backs. Writers count everything and write it down on clay tablets. Officials collect the fees. And everyone is shouting in a dozen languages.

[pause]

We climb up onto the quay with our bags. We are a little unsteady after so many days on the water. We walk with the crowds toward the nearest gate in the great wall. The guards look at us. Will they let us in? They glance at our bags. Then they wave us through. We walk through the long dark cool passage under the gate. And out into the city.

[pause]

{img:traveller-first-view-babylon-street-crowds-temples-amazed-sunlight}
And there is Babylon, all around us. The noise and the crowds. The smells of bread and smoke and spices and donkeys. The narrow streets between the high blank walls of the houses. Temples rise above the roofs. And at the end of a long street stands the great tower. It is enormous against the blue sky. Much bigger, now that we are close, than we had ever imagined.

[long-pause]

We stand still for a moment in the middle of the street. People push past us. And we stare, like every traveller who has ever come to Babylon. Then we go on to find a place to stay. And we begin to explore the greatest city in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:king-nebuchadnezzar-babylonian-robes-tall-hat-beard-palace-throne}
The king of Babylon in the year 600 before Christ is Nebuchadnezzar.

[pause]

In his own language, his name means, O god Nabu, protect my son. Nabu is the god of writing and wisdom. He is the son of Marduk, the great god of Babylon.

[long-pause]

Nebuchadnezzar became king a few years ago, when his father passed away. His father had made Babylon free and strong again. Before that, for a long time, Babylon had been ruled by a fierce empire from the north. That empire had just fallen. As a young prince, Nebuchadnezzar led his father's armies. And he won great battles.

[pause]

He will rule for forty-three years. He will become the most famous king of Babylon. He appears in the Bible too, as a great and proud and powerful king.

[pause]

{img:babylonian-king-inspecting-building-works-bricks-workers-scribes}
But in his own writings, Nebuchadnezzar does not talk much about his wars. He had his words written on clay and stone and bricks. He buried them under his buildings. And in them, he talks about what he built. The temples he repaired for the gods. The walls he built for the city. The canals he dug and the palaces he made.

[long-pause]

He wants to be remembered as a builder. A king who loved the gods and made Babylon beautiful.

[pause]

And he did. During his long reign he rebuilt almost all of Babylon. New walls and gates and palaces and temples. A new bridge over the river. New streets and canals. He finished the great tower. And he covered the great gate and the holy road with shining blue bricks.

[pause]

{img:mud-bricks-stamped-cuneiform-inscription-king-name-close-detail}
Millions of bricks were made for his buildings. On many of them, the brick makers pressed a stamp with the king's name. Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon, son of Nabopolassar, king of Babylon.

[long-pause]

Archaeologists have found these bricks by the thousand in the ruins. They are now in museums all over the world. The king hoped his name would last forever. And it still speaks from his bricks, more than two and a half thousand years later.

[pause]

In the year 600 before Christ, the king is still young, perhaps in his thirties. Much of his building is just beginning. All around the city there is noise and dust. Brick ovens smoke. Workers carry baskets of earth and bricks. Writers count and builders measure. And the great walls and gates and temples rise slowly, brick by brick, under the hot sun.

[long-pause]

{img:southern-palace-babylon-throne-room-facade-glazed-palms-lions-courtyard}
Near the great blue gate, by the river, stands the king's palace.

[pause]

It is enormous, like a whole city inside the city. It has five great courtyards, one after another. Around each one are rooms and halls and offices and store rooms. There are rooms for the king and his family, his officials and his servants and his guards.

[long-pause]

In the middle is the throne room. It is a huge hall, more than fifty metres long, with three great doorways onto the courtyard. The wall facing the courtyard is decorated with shining coloured bricks. On a deep blue background there are tall columns like palm trees, with bands of flowers. Below them walks a row of golden lions.

[pause]

Inside, at the back of the hall, opposite the main door, there is a space in the wall. The king's throne stands there. The king sits there to receive visitors from all over his empire and beyond.

[pause]

{img:ambassadors-bowing-throne-room-babylon-gifts-tribute-scribes}
Visitors come from Egypt and from the lands of the north and from the cities on the coast. They come with gifts and messages and servants. They are led through the great courtyards, one after another. Each one is grander than the last. At last they reach the throne room. At the far end of the long hall, in the shadows, sits the king. He wears rich robes and a tall crown. His officials and guards stand around him. The visitors bow low. One wrong word here could start a war.

[long-pause]

The palace is also the centre of government. Here the king's writers write his letters and orders. They keep the accounts of the whole empire on clay tablets, stored in great archives. Here are the treasuries, full of gold and silver and precious things. And here are store rooms full of barley and oil and dates.

[pause]

In another palace nearby, the kings keep a collection of old statues and old writings from earlier times and other lands. They love the past and want to understand it. Some people call it one of the first museums in the world.

[pause]

{img:palace-garden-courtyard-babylon-evening-pool-palms-lamps-quiet}
In the evenings, when the day's business is done, the palace grows quiet. The visitors go to their rooms. The writers go home. Lamps are lit along the corridors and courtyards. The king perhaps walks on the walls above the river, in the cool breeze. He looks out over his city and the great tower and the temples. The river glows in the sunset. And beyond the walls, the palm groves stretch away to the edge of the world.

[long-pause]

{img:babylon-double-walls-towers-moat-mudbrick-sunset-river}
Babylon is surrounded by walls.

[pause]

There is an outer wall, a huge triangle many kilometres long. It encloses the whole city and its gardens and fields and palm groves. And inside there is the inner city. It is almost square. The river runs right through the middle of it. A double wall of mud brick surrounds it. In front of the wall is a moat, a deep ditch full of water from the river.

[long-pause]

The inner wall is about six metres thick. The outer one is nearly four. There is a space between them. Every twenty metres or so a tower stands up above the walls, all the way round. There are hundreds of them. It is one of the strongest city walls of the ancient world. Any army that comes here will have to think twice.

[pause]

Eight great gates lead through the walls. Each one is named after a god. There is a gate of the sun god and a gate of the storm god and a gate of the moon god. And the most beautiful of all is in the north. It is the Ishtar Gate.

[pause]

{img:ishtar-gate-front-view-blue-towers-aurochs-dragons-processional-way}
The Ishtar Gate is named after Ishtar, the goddess of love and of the morning star. Nebuchadnezzar rebuilds it more than once, each time higher and grander. It is a double gate with tall towers on each side. The passage between them is high enough for the great statues of the gods to pass through on their carts, at festival time.

[long-pause]

And the gate is blue.

[pause]

It is covered all over with shining bricks in a deep brilliant blue. The blue is like a precious blue stone from the far mountains. And on the blue, in rows, there are animals. They are made of bricks shaped so they stand out from the wall. They shine in yellow and gold and white and brown. There are bulls with blue horns. And there are dragons. They have the head of a snake, the body of a lion, the feet of an eagle and the tail of a scorpion.

[pause]

{img:mushhushshu-dragon-glazed-brick-relief-blue-wall-golden}
The bulls belong to the storm god. The dragons belong to Marduk, the great god of Babylon. There are hundreds of them in rows all over the gate. They seem to march toward the city.

[long-pause]

Along the top of the gate there are battlements and flowers in white and yellow. Inside the passage, there is a great written message. In it, Nebuchadnezzar says he built the gate and covered it with blue bricks and bulls and dragons. So that all people might look at it in wonder.

[pause]

The great doors are made of cedar wood covered with bronze. At night they are closed and barred. Guards stand watch on the towers. And the city sleeps behind its walls.

[pause]

{img:ishtar-gate-detail-aurochs-bull-glazed-bricks-yellow-blue-closeup}
When the morning sun shines on the Ishtar Gate, it glows like a jewel. Blue and gold and white against the brown walls and the pale sky. People who come to Babylon for the first time stop and stare. They are amazed.

[long-pause]

Today you can still walk through the front of the Ishtar Gate. It has been rebuilt from the original bricks, in a museum in Berlin. And the people who see it, more than two and a half thousand years later, still stop and stare. And they are still amazed.

[long-pause]

{img:processional-way-babylon-walls-blue-glazed-lions-procession-wide-road}
Through the Ishtar Gate and out to the north runs the holy road of Babylon. It is called the Processional Way.

[pause]

It is wide and straight, about twenty metres wide. It is paved with big slabs of white and reddish stone. High walls line it on both sides.

[long-pause]

And on these walls there are lions.

[pause]

On a background of shining blue bricks, lions walk one after another, in a long line on each side of the road. They stand out from the wall in white and yellow and gold. Their mouths are open and their tails are raised. They stride slowly and proudly toward the gate. There are about a hundred and twenty of them, sixty on each side. The lion is the animal of Ishtar, the goddess of the gate.

[pause]

{img:babylonian-lion-glazed-brick-relief-striding-blue-background-detail}
Above and below them there are bands of flowers. The walls are so high that walking along the road is like walking through a great blue shining valley. And the lions walk beside you all the way.

[long-pause]

On most days the road is a busy street. People come and go through the gate with donkeys and carts and baskets. Soldiers and priests and traders and farmers bring their goods to the city.

[pause]

But at the New Year, in spring, it becomes the holy road. The statues of the gods are carried along it in great splendour. They go from the temple of Marduk, through the Ishtar Gate, to a special temple outside the city. And then back again. There is music and singing and dancing. Great crowds watch from both sides.

[pause]

{img:procession-priests-carrying-gold-statue-marduk-processional-way-crowds}
Even the stones of the road have writing on them. They carry Nebuchadnezzar's name and a little prayer to Marduk, asking for a long life.

[long-pause]

Today the lions of the road are spread around the world. Some are still in Babylon. Many are in Berlin, where part of the road has been rebuilt beside the gate. Others are in museums in Paris and London and New York and many other cities. Lions from Babylon, striding on their blue background through museum halls all over the world. Just as they once strode toward the Ishtar Gate.

[pause]

When you stand in front of one and look closely, you can see the marks of the brick makers' hands. You can see little bubbles in the glaze. And you can imagine the hot sun of Babylon shining on it, the crowds walking past, and the river flowing nearby.

[long-pause]

{img:etemenanki-ziggurat-babylon-seven-levels-temple-top-blue-sky}
In the heart of Babylon stands the great tower. It is a ziggurat, a tower built in steps.

[pause]

Its name means the temple of the foundation of heaven and earth. It is built of mud brick and baked brick. It rises in levels, one on top of another. Each level is smaller than the one below. It is like a great staircase to the sky.

[long-pause]

It stands in its own great courtyard beside the temple of Marduk, near the river. Its base is square, about ninety metres on each side. It may be just as tall. That is as tall as a building of thirty floors. In its time, it is the tallest building in the whole world.

[pause]

On the front, a great triple stairway leads up from the courtyard. And on the very top stands a small temple for the god Marduk. It is covered with blue shining bricks. It glows in the sun high above the city. You can see it from many kilometres away across the flat plain.

[pause]

The tower is very old. There has been a tower here for more than a thousand years. It has been rebuilt and repaired again and again. The king's father began to rebuild it. Now Nebuchadnezzar is finishing it. He writes that he raised its top to rival the heavens.

[long-pause]

Many people believe this tower is the Tower of Babel in the Bible. In that story, people try to build a tower up to heaven. Then God mixes up their languages. Suddenly they cannot understand each other anymore. They stop building and scatter over the earth. Babel is the Hebrew name for Babylon.

[pause]

And in the streets of Babylon you can indeed hear many languages. Babylonian and Aramaic and Hebrew. Egyptian and Greek and Persian. All mixed together in the markets and the streets.

[pause]

{img:babylon-skyline-ziggurat-rising-above-houses-palms-evening-glow}
Why did the Babylonians build the tower?

[long-pause]

They believed the gods lived in the heavens. The tower was a bridge or a stairway between heaven and earth. The gods could come down it to visit the city and the people. The little temple on top was a resting place for the god on his way down.

[pause]

Only the priests could climb to the top. There, high above the city under the stars, they made their offerings and prayers. And they watched the sky.

[pause]

{img:ziggurat-ruins-babylon-today-low-mound-water-filled-base-reeds}
Today almost nothing is left of the great tower. Over the centuries its bricks were taken away to build other buildings. Only its base remains. It is a square outline in the ground near the ruins, sometimes filled with water and reeds.

[long-pause]

But we know what it looked like. A Babylonian writer wrote down its measurements on a clay tablet, and it has survived. And a Greek traveller described it more than a hundred years later. He wrote about its many levels, its stairway winding around the outside, and its temple at the top.

[long-pause]

{img:esagila-temple-marduk-courtyard-gold-doors-priests-incense}
Beside the great tower stands the greatest temple of Babylon. It is the temple of Marduk.

[pause]

Its name means the house whose top is high. It is a great group of buildings around courtyards. It has thick walls and tall gateways. There are many rooms for the gods and the priests and the treasures and the gifts.

[long-pause]

In the heart of it, in the holiest room, lives the god himself. Marduk, the lord of Babylon, the king of the gods. He is a great statue made of precious wood covered with gold. He wears rich robes sewn with gold and jewels. He sits on a golden throne with a golden table before him.

[pause]

A Greek writer later said the statue and the throne and the table together held a huge amount of gold. That may not be quite true. But the Babylonians themselves wrote that the room was covered with gold, like the sun.

[pause]

{img:priests-feeding-god-statue-golden-table-food-offerings-shrine}
Every day the priests look after Marduk as if he were a great king. In the morning they wake him with songs and prayers. They wash him and dress him in fresh clothes. Twice a day they bring him meals. Great feasts of bread and cakes and meat and fish and fruit and dates. Beer and wine. They set it out on the golden table with music and incense. When the god has eaten, the food is shared among the priests and the temple workers.

[long-pause]

At night the priests put the god to bed. They close the doors of his room. The temple is quiet. Only the lamps burn, and the guards watch.

[pause]

Long ago Marduk was only a small god of this one city. But as Babylon grew great, so did he. Now he is the king of all the gods. Every year the priests recite a great poem about him. It tells how at the beginning of time there was only water and chaos. A great goddess of the salt sea ruled it all. And the young god Marduk beat her. He made the heavens and the earth. He set the stars and the moon in their places. And the gods built Babylon for him, as his home.

[pause]

{img:temple-workers-esagila-scribes-musicians-singers-courtyard-day}
The temple is not only a temple. It is like a small city. Hundreds of people work there. Priests and singers and musicians. Writers and star watchers. Cooks and bakers and brewers. Weavers and goldsmiths and gardeners and herdsmen. The temple owns fields and palm groves and flocks all around Babylon. It lends money and grain. And it keeps careful accounts of everything on clay tablets.

[long-pause]

The people of Babylon come to the temple to pray and bring gifts. They ask the god for help. For healing and for children. For good luck and for justice. They stand in the courtyard and look toward the room where the god sits in the golden dimness. And they pray with their hands raised to their faces.

[pause]

The sweet smell of incense drifts out over the courtyards and the walls. It drifts into the streets of the city, all day long.

[long-pause]

{img:euphrates-river-babylon-stone-bridge-piers-boats-palms-afternoon}
Through the middle of Babylon flows the river Euphrates.

[pause]

It divides the city in two. On the east bank is the old city, with the great temples and palaces and the tower. On the west bank is the new city, with its own temples and houses and gardens. To join them, the king has built a great bridge.

[long-pause]

It is one of the first great bridges of the ancient world. It is about a hundred and twenty metres long. It rests on thick pillars of brick and stone. The pillars are shaped like boats, pointed at the front, so the water flows past them smoothly. The road across the bridge is made of wooden beams. At night the beams can be taken away. That way nobody can cross in the dark.

[pause]

Along the river inside the city there are brick quays with steps down to the water. Boats tie up there. People come down to fetch water and wash and bathe.

[pause]

{img:round-coracle-quffa-boats-river-euphrates-traders-cargo-reeds}
And on the river there are boats of every kind.

[long-pause]

There are long narrow wooden boats with sails and oars. They carry goods up and down the river. There are rafts made of logs floating on blown-up goatskins. They bring stone and wood and wine down from the mountains. And there are round boats. They are big round baskets of woven reeds covered with leather. They are coated with a black sticky tar that seeps up from the ground here. That keeps the water out.

[pause]

A Greek traveller later wrote about these round boats with wonder. They are round like a shield, he wrote. They carry big loads of wine. And they carry a live donkey or two. When the boat arrives and is unloaded, it is taken apart. The leather is loaded on the donkeys. And it is carried back up the river by land, because the current is too strong to row against.

[pause]

{img:fishermen-casting-nets-euphrates-dawn-reeds-herons-quiet}
There are fish in the river. Fishermen go out at dawn in little boats. They cast their nets and come back with fish for the market. And there are birds. Herons and egrets and ducks and pelicans. Kingfishers in the reeds along the banks.

[long-pause]

But the river can also be dangerous. Every spring the snow melts in the mountains far to the north. The river rises. Sometimes it floods over its banks, across the land, and into the city. People watch the water creep higher day by day. The Babylonians build their houses on platforms. They build their temples on great raised terraces. They keep their canals and dams in good repair. And they pray to the gods to keep the floods away.

[pause]

In the evening, as the sun sets behind the palms on the west bank, the river glows red and gold. The boats come home. People sit on the quays and on the roofs in the cool breeze from the water. And they watch the light fade over the great slow river.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonian-house-courtyard-mudbrick-rooms-doors-family-morning-light}
Let us visit a family in their house in Babylon.

[pause]

They live in the east part of the old city, not far from the great temple. Their street is narrow and winding between the houses. It is not paved. It is dusty in summer and muddy after the winter rains.

[long-pause]

From the street, the house looks very plain. It is just a high wall of mud brick, with no windows. There is a single low wooden door. But inside it is quite different.

[pause]

Through the door is a little entrance room. Then comes the courtyard. It is an open space in the middle of the house, paved with bricks and open to the sky. The rooms of the house stand all around it. The courtyard is the heart of the house. The family lives and works and cooks and talks there. And the children play there, in the shade and the light.

[pause]

{img:courtyard-family-weaving-grinding-grain-children-playing-babylon}
Around the courtyard are the rooms. There is a long room on the south side that faces north, so it stays cool. The family welcomes guests there and sits there in the heat of the day. There are bedrooms and store rooms for grain and oil and dates. There is a kitchen with a clay oven. And perhaps a little room for washing, with a drain.

[long-pause]

The walls are thick, to keep out the heat and the cold. The rooms are dark and cool. The only light comes from the doors onto the courtyard. The floors are beaten earth or brick, covered with reed mats and wool rugs.

[pause]

There is not much furniture. Low wooden beds with mattresses and cushions. Stools and a little table. Wooden chests for clothes. Clay jars for water and oil and grain. And baskets everywhere. At night, little clay lamps burn with sesame oil.

[pause]

{img:flat-roof-babylon-house-ladder-family-sleeping-summer-night-stars}
On top of the house is a flat roof. It is made of palm trunks and reed mats covered with thick smooth mud. The family climbs up by a ladder. They use the roof as another room. They dry dates and grain and washing in the sun. And in the cool of the evening they sit and talk with the neighbours on the next roofs.

[long-pause]

In summer, when it is very hot, the whole family sleeps on the roof at night. They lie on mats under the open sky, in the cool breeze, under the stars. All over Babylon on summer nights, families lie on their roofs. They look up at the stars and talk quietly. And they fall asleep to the sound of dogs barking in the streets, frogs in the canals, and the watchmen calling the hours from the walls.

[pause]

The family buries its dead under the floor of the house. That way the ancestors are still with them in the house. Every month the family gives them a little food and water. And they remember them.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonian-woman-baking-flatbread-clay-oven-courtyard-dawn}
Let us spend a day with this family.

[pause]

The father is a writer who works for the temple of Marduk. He writes contracts and accounts on clay tablets. The mother runs the house and weaves. She also has a little business of her own. She lends small amounts of silver to her neighbours. There are four children. There is the father's old mother, a servant girl, and a little dog.

[long-pause]

They wake at dawn when the light comes into the courtyard and birds sing in the palm tree next door. The servant girl lights the fire in the clay oven in the corner. The mother makes bread. She slaps flat round loaves onto the hot inside wall of the oven. They bake in a few minutes. She peels them off, hot and soft and smelling wonderful.

[pause]

They eat breakfast on mats in the courtyard. Bread and dates, a little cheese and some onions. And a drink of thick sweet barley beer. Everyone drinks it, even the children.

[pause]

{img:scribe-father-walking-street-tablets-basket-temple-morning-crowd}
Then the father goes to work at the temple. He walks through the narrow streets, which are already busy. There are people and donkeys and carts and water carriers. Sellers call out their goods. He carries a sharp reed for writing and a little basket of clay tablets.

[long-pause]

The eldest son, who is twelve, goes to school at a teacher's house. He is learning to be a writer like his father. The younger children stay home and play in the courtyard and the street. They play with clay toys and balls and spinning tops. And with little carts on wheels, pulled on a string.

[pause]

The mother spins and weaves at her loom in the shade of the courtyard. She makes wool cloth for the family's clothes. She grinds barley with the servant girl. She goes to the market. And she visits her sister in the next street.

[pause]

{img:babylonian-women-chatting-courtyard-spinning-wool-afternoon-shade}
In the heat of the afternoon, everyone rests in the cool dark rooms. The sun beats down on the courtyard. The streets are almost empty and quiet.

[long-pause]

In the late afternoon the father comes home and washes. The family eats the main meal of the day. Bread, and a stew of lentils and chickpeas and onions and garlic. Sometimes a little lamb or fish from the river. Sesame oil and herbs and spices. And then dates and figs and pomegranates.

[pause]

In the evening, as the air cools, they climb up onto the roof. They bring a jug of beer and some dates. They sit together on their mats and talk. They watch the sky turn red and gold and purple over the city and the great tower and the river. Swallows swoop. And the bats come out.

[pause]

{img:babylon-rooftops-evening-families-sitting-ziggurat-silhouette-sunset}
The grandmother tells the children old stories. Stories of the gods and heroes. Of Gilgamesh, the great king, and his wild friend Enkidu, and their adventures. And of the great flood long ago. The gods sent water over the whole earth. But one wise man built a great boat and saved his family and the animals. Will the boat survive the storm? The children always hold their breath at that part, even though they know the ending.

[long-pause]

And the children fall asleep on the roof one by one, under the stars. The grown-ups talk softly. And the city grows quiet around them.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonian-woman-signing-contract-scribe-witnesses-seal-courtyard}
The women of Babylon have more rights and more freedom than women in many other places in the ancient world.

[pause]

Most women spend their lives as wives and mothers. They run the house and raise the children. They spin and weave and cook and brew. But a Babylonian woman can own things of her own. Houses and fields and silver. She can buy and sell and lend and borrow. She can make contracts and be a witness. She can go to court and win her case.

[long-pause]

When she marries, her family gives her a gift to take with her. Money and furniture and clothes and jewellery. Sometimes even a house or a field. It stays hers all her life. It is written down carefully on a clay tablet in the marriage contract. If her husband sends her away without a good reason, he must give it all back. And when she dies, it goes to her children.

[pause]

Many marriage contracts from Babylon have been found. They are quite simple. The husband asks the bride's father for his daughter. The father agrees. The gift is listed. And sometimes there is a promise. If the husband ever takes a second wife, he must pay the first one a lot of silver. And she may go home to her family.

[pause]

{img:women-weaving-workshop-temple-babylon-looms-wool-supervisor}
Some women work outside the home. They weave in the big workshops of the temples and the palace. They run taverns and brew beer. They are midwives and nurses. They sell things in the streets and markets. And some are writers and singers and musicians and priestesses in the temples.

[long-pause]

There are businesswomen too. In the old records of Babylon there are women who buy and sell houses. They lend silver and grain. They rent out fields. They manage the family's property when their husbands are away or have passed away. In the papers of one rich family, the wives and mothers appear again and again. They buy and sell and sign and witness.

[pause]

Women are also respected as mothers and as keepers of the house and the family memory. The Babylonians had a saying. A house without a wife is like a field without a plough.

[pause]

{img:mother-daughters-babylon-roof-evening-combing-hair-talking-sunset}
In the evening, on the rooftops of Babylon, mothers and daughters and grandmothers sit together. They comb each other's hair. They talk and laugh and share the news of the neighbourhood. They plan the marriages of the young people. And they watch the sun go down over the city.

[long-pause]

{img:date-palm-grove-babylon-climber-harvesting-dates-ropes-baskets}
Around Babylon, along all the rivers and canals, grow the date palms.

[pause]

They grow in great groves, tall and slender. Their crowns of long green feathery leaves rustle in the wind. Big bunches of dates hang below. In late summer they ripen, orange and red and brown.

[long-pause]

For the Babylonians, the date palm is the tree of life. Its fruit is sweet and filling. It can be eaten fresh or dried and kept for months. It can be pressed into a sweet syrup like honey. The leaves are woven into baskets and mats and roofs. The fibres become ropes. The trunks become beams and bridges. The stones are burned as fuel or fed to the animals. Nothing is wasted.

[pause]

Under the palms, in their shade, farmers grow other plants. Pomegranates and figs and grapes. Onions and garlic and lettuce and cucumbers. The palm groves are like great gardens, cool and green and shady in the burning summer heat.

[pause]

The date palms need help. There are male trees and female trees. Only the female trees give fruit. So in spring, the farmers climb up the tall trunks with a rope around themselves and the tree. It is a long way up. One slip could be dangerous. At the top, they tie a little bunch of flowers from a male tree among the flowers of the female tree. Then the dates will grow. Farmers in Iraq still do this today, in the same way.

[long-pause]

And everything depends on the canals.

[pause]

All around Babylon there is a great web of canals, big and small. They carry the river water out to the fields and the palm groves. The big canals are as wide as rivers, and boats sail on them. The small ones are little ditches between the fields. Farmers open and close little gates of earth and reeds. That lets the water into their fields when they need it.

[pause]

{img:irrigation-canal-shaduf-farmer-lifting-water-bucket-fields-barley}
To lift water from the canal into the field, they use a clever tool. It is a long pole balanced on a post. A bucket hangs on one end. A heavy lump of clay sits on the other end. The farmer dips the bucket into the canal. The weight helps him lift it easily. Then he swings it round and empties it into the field.

[long-pause]

The canals must be cleaned and repaired every year. The river carries a lot of mud. It settles in the canals and fills them up. So they must be dug out again and again. It is hard work. If a canal is left too long, the fields at the end of it get no water. And the crops dry up.

[pause]

In the fields, the farmers grow barley above all. It is the main food and drink of Babylon, for bread and beer. They also grow wheat and sesame for oil. They plough with oxen and a wooden plough. On top of the plough sits a funnel. The farmer drops seed into it. The seed falls straight into the ground as the plough moves along. It is a clever invention of this land.

[pause]

{img:barley-harvest-mesopotamia-sickles-sheaves-donkeys-threshing-floor}
At harvest, in April and May, the barley is cut with curved knives. Donkeys carry it to the threshing floors. There oxen walk round and round on it to shake out the grain. The wind blows away the husks. The grain is measured and put in big sacks. And it is taken to the store rooms of the temples and the families and the king.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonians-drinking-beer-straws-large-jar-tavern-friends}
In Babylon, everyone drinks beer.

[pause]

It is made from barley. It is quite different from beer today. It is thick and cloudy and sweet, and not very strong. It is more like a kind of liquid food than a drink. Everyone drinks it every day with every meal. Men and women and children. The king and even the gods.

[long-pause]

To make it, brewers soak barley in water until it sprouts. Then they dry it and grind it and bake it into loaves. They crumble the loaves into water in big jars. They add dates for sweetness. And they leave it for a few days to bubble.

[pause]

The beer is thick, with bits of grain floating on top. So people drink it through long reed straws. Some straws have a little filter at the bottom to keep out the bits. In old pictures from this land, people sit around one big jar. They all drink through their long straws together and talk.

[pause]

{img:babylonian-tavern-keeper-woman-serving-beer-jars-evening-lamp}
There are taverns in Babylon where people go in the evening to drink and talk and hear music. Many are run by women who brew the beer themselves. The old laws of Hammurabi, more than a thousand years before, had rules for them. They had to sell honest amounts at honest prices.

[long-pause]

There is wine too. It comes down the river on rafts from the mountains. It is costly. The rich and the king drink it, and it is offered to the gods.

[pause]

And bread is everywhere. The Babylonians have dozens of kinds of bread and cake. Flat breads baked on the oven walls. Round loaves. Sweet cakes made with honey and dates and sesame and butter. Some are baked in clay moulds shaped like animals and fish and stars.

[pause]

{img:clay-baking-moulds-patterned-cakes-dates-honey-kitchen-table}
There is also a very old set of recipes from this land, written on clay tablets. It is more than a thousand years older than our story. It is the oldest cookbook in the world. It has recipes for stews of lamb and pigeon and duck. They are cooked with onions and leeks and garlic and herbs. In our own time, some cooks have made these recipes again, to taste what the food was like.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the smell of baking bread and onions and garlic and stew drifts over the rooftops. Wood smoke drifts too. Families gather around their meals in their courtyards and on their roofs. They drink their beer through their straws. And they talk about the day.

[long-pause]

{img:babylon-market-street-stalls-pottery-textiles-spices-merchants-donkeys}
Babylon is a city of trade.

[pause]

This land has plenty of barley and dates and wool and clay and reeds. But it has almost no stone. It has no good wood except palm. And it has no metal. So everything else must come from far away. The traders of Babylon trade with the whole known world.

[long-pause]

From the mountains in the west comes cedar wood for the roofs and doors of temples and palaces. It floats down the rivers on great rafts. From the mountains in the north and east come copper and tin, iron and silver and gold. From far to the east comes a deep blue stone flecked with gold. The Babylonians love it more than any other stone. From Egypt and Arabia come incense and spices. And from India, by sea, come ivory and cotton and precious stones.

[pause]

In return, Babylon sends out its own goods. Barley and dates and sesame oil. And above all, cloth. Fine wool cloth, dyed red and blue and purple, and sewn with patterns. It is famous everywhere.

[pause]

{img:camel-donkey-caravan-arriving-babylon-gate-traders-bundles}
The traders travel in long lines of donkeys, and more and more on camels. They cross the deserts from one water hole to the next. The journey is long and hot. If they miss a water hole, they are in great danger. Others travel by boat along the rivers to the sea.

[long-pause]

There is no single big market square in Babylon. Trade happens everywhere. At the city gates and on the quays where the boats unload. In the streets and in little shops. In the temples and in the houses of rich trading families.

[pause]

There are no coins yet. Coins have just been invented far away in the west. But they have not reached Babylon. Instead people pay in silver, weighed on scales, in little pieces and rings. Or they pay in barley. A small weight of silver called a shekel is the usual measure. Everything has its price in shekels and in barley.

[pause]

{img:merchant-weighing-silver-scales-stone-weights-clay-tablet-contract}
And everything is written down. When people buy or sell or lend or rent, they go to a writer. When they marry or adopt a child, they go to a writer. He writes a contract on a clay tablet. It has the names of the people and the witnesses and the date. Then they press their seals into the clay to sign it. Some just press their fingernail.

[long-pause]

Thousands of these contracts have been found in the ruins. They tell us about the business of ordinary people two and a half thousand years ago. The price of a house and an ox and a field. The price of a jar of oil. The rent of a boat. The wages of a worker.

[pause]

One great family of Babylon were traders and bankers for five generations. More than a thousand of their tablets have been found. They bought and sold houses and fields and dates and barley. They lent money and rented out land. And their tablets tell the story of their family for more than a hundred years. Their business, their marriages and their quarrels.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonian-judges-court-temple-gate-plaintiffs-scribes-tablets}
In Babylon there are judges and courts and laws.

[pause]

More than a thousand years before our story, a great king of Babylon called Hammurabi had his laws carved on a tall black stone. It is more than two metres high. He set it up in a temple so everyone could see it. At the top there is a picture of the king. He stands before the sun god, the god of justice, who gives him the laws. Below, in neat close lines, are the laws themselves. There are nearly three hundred.

[long-pause]

The laws deal with everything. Property and trade and wages. Debts and marriage and divorce. Fields and canals and animals and boats. Doctors and builders and tavern keepers. Some punishments are very harsh, in the old way. But many laws are about fairness and protecting the weak.

[pause]

Hammurabi wrote why he made the laws. So that the strong would not crush the weak, he said. So that orphans and widows would get justice. Anyone with a quarrel, he said, should come and stand before his stone. They should read the laws and understand their case. And then their heart would be at peace.

[pause]

{img:basalt-stele-laws-hammurabi-shamash-relief-top-columns-cuneiform}
By the year 600 before Christ these laws are very old. But writers still copy them in their schools, as a classic text. And the idea that the king must give justice and protect the weak is still at the heart of being king in Babylon.

[long-pause]

Long after, Hammurabi's stone was carried away to a city in Iran. It was a prize of war. About a hundred and twenty years ago, French archaeologists found it there. Today it stands in a museum in Paris.

[pause]

In Nebuchadnezzar's Babylon, when there is a quarrel, people go to the judges. The judges sit at the temple gates or in the courts. They listen to both sides and to the witnesses. They look at the contracts on their tablets. And they decide. Sometimes they ask people to swear an oath before the gods that they are telling the truth. That is a very serious thing. People's hands shake when they do it. Nobody wants to lie before the gods.

[pause]

{img:oath-before-divine-symbols-temple-courtyard-witnesses-solemn-light}
And everything is written down on clay. The case and the witnesses and the decision and the date. So two and a half thousand years later we can still read about a quarrel over the edge of a field. Or the return of a wedding gift. Or the payment of a debt. And we can read how the judges of Babylon decided it.

[long-pause]

Most quarrels never come to court though. Families and neighbours settle them quietly. The elders of the street sit together in a courtyard in the evening, over a jar of beer. They talk until everyone agrees. And then everyone goes home to sleep.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonian-scribe-pressing-reed-stylus-wet-clay-tablet-cuneiform}
In Babylon, people write on clay.

[pause]

The writing is made of little wedge-shaped marks. A writer presses them into soft wet clay with the sharp end of a reed. Each sign is made of several wedges. Long and short, standing up and lying down. Today we call this writing cuneiform. It means wedge-shaped.

[long-pause]

It is the oldest writing in the world. The Sumerians invented it more than five thousand years ago. At first it was little pictures drawn on clay. Pictures of the things they were counting, like sheep and barley and jars of oil. Over the centuries the pictures became simpler and turned into wedges. The signs came to stand for sounds as well as words. Then people could write down anything at all.

[pause]

By the year 600 before Christ, this writing has been used for more than two and a half thousand years. It is used for many languages. There are hundreds of different signs. It takes many years to learn them all.

[pause]

{img:clay-tablets-drying-sun-rows-courtyard-scribes-workshop}
The writer takes a lump of smooth damp clay. He shapes it in his hands into a tablet, small enough to hold in one hand. It is flat on one side and a little rounded on the other, like a small cushion. He holds it in his left hand and the reed in his right. He presses the signs quickly, line after line. He turns it over to write on the back, and even on the edges.

[long-pause]

Then the tablet dries in the sun and gets hard. If it is important, it is baked in an oven. Then it becomes as hard as a brick and will last almost forever. Letters are put in envelopes made of clay too. The address is written on the outside. You have to break the envelope to read the letter.

[pause]

Clay tablets are cheap and easy to make. And they last. Paper and leather rot and burn. But clay survives for thousands of years in the ground. When a library burns, the fire bakes the tablets hard. And that keeps them safe.

[pause]

{img:library-shelves-clay-tablets-baskets-labels-temple-archive-lamp}
So hundreds of thousands of clay tablets have been found in the ruins of this land. Letters and contracts and laws. Lists and dictionaries and prayers. Stories and poems. Medicine and maths and star charts. School work and recipes.

[long-pause]

Many are still unread. They wait in museum store rooms around the world for scholars to study them. Every year more are read and translated. And we learn new things about the people who wrote them. Their business and their worries. Their prayers and their jokes and their lives.

[pause]

In the year 600 before Christ, a new kind of writing is also spreading. It is a simple alphabet with only twenty-two letters. It is written with ink on leather. It is much easier to learn. Many people now speak this new language in the streets and write it with pen and ink. But for important things, the writers still use the old wedges on clay. For temples and laws and contracts and the stars. Just as their ancestors have done for thousands of years.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonian-school-boys-sitting-benches-copying-tablets-teacher}
The eldest son of our family goes to school. He is learning to be a writer.

[pause]

The Babylonians call the school the tablet house. It is not a big building. Usually it is the house of a master writer. He takes a few pupils and teaches them in a room or in his courtyard. Most pupils are boys, the sons of writers and priests and rich families. A few girls learn to write too.

[long-pause]

The boys start young, at about seven or eight. They go to school every day from sunrise until evening, for many years. And they work hard.

[pause]

First they learn to make tablets and hold the reed and press simple wedges. Then they learn the signs one by one. They copy them again and again from a model. The teacher writes the model on one side of a tablet. The pupil copies it on the other side. Then they learn lists of words. Trees and animals and stones and gods and cities. Then sayings and letters and hymns and stories.

[pause]

{img:school-tablet-teacher-copy-pupil-copy-clumsy-wedges-close}
When they make a mistake, they smooth out the clay with their thumb and start again. Many school tablets have been found. On one side is the teacher's neat model. On the other is the pupil's clumsy copy, full of mistakes.

[long-pause]

They also learn maths. Counting and times tables and fractions. How to measure a field. How many bricks a wall needs. How much earth must be dug for a canal. How much barley the workers should be paid.

[pause]

The teachers are strict. There are old stories, copied by pupils for centuries, about life at school. A boy is late, and he gets punished. He talks too much, and he gets punished. He writes badly, and he gets punished. Then his father invites the teacher home. He gives him a good dinner and a new robe and a ring. And suddenly the teacher says the boy is the best pupil in the school.

[pause]

{img:babylonian-boy-walking-home-school-tablet-satchel-street-evening}
These stories are funny and very human. They show that schoolboys and teachers and parents have not changed very much in four thousand years.

[long-pause]

After many years of school, the young writers can work for the temples or the palace. They can work for traders or for the courts. They can become teachers or doctors or star watchers. They are respected and well paid and proud of their skill.

[pause]

And they have a goddess of their own. She is the goddess of writing and of grain. At the end of their tablets, many writers wrote a little line. Praise be to the goddess of writing.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonian-mathematics-tablet-numbers-wedges-scribe-calculating-lamp}
Every time we look at a clock, we use something from Babylon.

[pause]

The Babylonians do not count in tens like we do. They count in sixties. Their number system has only two signs. An upright wedge for one, and a sideways wedge for ten. With these they can write any number from one to fifty-nine. At sixty they start again, with a single wedge in the next column. It is just like how we write ten, with a one in the next column.

[long-pause]

Why sixty? Nobody knows for certain. But sixty is a wonderful number for sums. You can split it evenly in so many ways. By two and three and four and five and six. By ten and twelve and fifteen and twenty and thirty. So fractions are easy. Half of sixty, a third, a quarter, a fifth, a sixth. They are all whole numbers.

[pause]

And from the Babylonians and their sixties, we got our hours of sixty minutes. Our minutes of sixty seconds. And our circles of three hundred and sixty degrees.

[pause]

{img:old-babylonian-tablet-square-diagonal-numbers-plimpton-style-close}
Babylonian maths is very advanced. More than a thousand years before our story, writers were already solving hard problems. They worked out areas and volumes and interest on loans.

[long-pause]

There is a famous little tablet from that time, about the size of a hand. It shows a square with a line across it from corner to corner. On the line, in wedges, is a number that is very hard to work out. And the Babylonians got it right, more exactly than anyone else would for more than a thousand years.

[pause]

There is another famous tablet with a table of numbers. Scholars have argued about it for nearly a hundred years. It seems to show that the Babylonians knew a special rule about triangles. Much later, a Greek called Pythagoras became famous for that same rule. But the Babylonians knew it more than a thousand years before him.

[pause]

{img:babylonian-surveyors-measuring-field-rope-stakes-canal-scribe-notes}
For the Babylonians, maths is practical. They use it every day. To measure fields and split up what someone leaves behind. To work out wages and food for workers. To count bricks for a wall and earth for a canal. And to work out the interest on a loan.

[long-pause]

And they use it to measure time.

[pause]

They divide the day and the night into twelve parts. They divide the sky into twelve parts. They divide the year into twelve months of the moon. Each month has twenty-nine or thirty days. Every few years they add an extra month. That keeps the calendar in step with the sun and the seasons.

[pause]

So every time we see sixty minutes in an hour, or turn a corner of ninety degrees, or count the twelve months of the year, we are remembering the writers of Babylon. Without knowing it. Pressing their wedges into clay.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonian-astronomers-roof-night-observing-stars-writing-tablets-lamp}
At night, on the roofs of the temples and on top of the great tower, the star watchers watch the sky.

[pause]

The night sky over Babylon is wonderful. The air is dry and clear. There are no city lights, only little lamps in the houses and fires on the walls. So the stars shine bright and sharp in their thousands, from one side of the flat land to the other.

[long-pause]

The star watchers are priests and writers of the temple of Marduk. They have trained for many years in the old knowledge of the sky. And every night, for hundreds of years, they have watched and written down what they see.

[pause]

They keep diaries of the sky on clay tablets. Night after night, they write down where the moon and the planets are among the stars. When they rise and set. When the moon goes dark. They also write down the weather and the level of the river. They write the prices of barley and dates and wool in the market. And the important events in the city and the world.

[pause]

{img:night-sky-babylon-moon-venus-jupiter-ziggurat-silhouette-desert}
These diaries began around the year 650 before Christ. They went on for six hundred years. It is the longest science record kept by anyone in the ancient world. Hundreds of them still exist in a museum in London. Scientists still use them today, to study things that happened in the sky long ago.

[long-pause]

With all these notes, the star watchers learned to predict the sky. When the new moon would appear. When the moon would go dark. Where the planets would move. They used clever maths with their numbers in sixties.

[pause]

Some scholars now think the Babylonians even used a kind of geometry to work out the path of the planet Jupiter. Nobody in Europe would use that method again for more than a thousand years.

[pause]

{img:zodiac-constellations-painted-sky-lion-bull-scorpion-babylonian-style}
The Babylonians also named the stars and the star pictures. Many names we use today come from them. They divided the path of the sun and moon and planets into twelve parts. Each part has its own star picture. The Bull and the Lion and the Scorpion. The Twins and the Scales. Later these became the twelve star signs.

[long-pause]

For the Babylonians, the sky is a great book written by the gods. In it they can read the future. If the moon goes dark, or a planet moves strangely, it is a message. A sign of what is coming for the king and the country. So the star watchers send reports to the king. And sometimes the king waits nervously for their news.

[pause]

But mostly they simply watch and write and calculate, night after night, with great patience and care and love for the sky. On a warm night in Babylon you can see them on the temple roof. They are wrapped in their cloaks. They look up at the moon rising over the palm trees and the river and the great dark plain. And they press their reed softly into the clay.

[long-pause]

{img:akitu-new-year-festival-babylon-procession-statues-gods-boats-river}
In spring, when the barley is ripening in the fields, Babylon celebrates the New Year.

[pause]

It is the greatest festival of the year. It lasts for twelve days.

[long-pause]

In the first days, the priests hold ceremonies in the temple at dawn. There are prayers and songs. They recite the great poem of how Marduk beat the chaos and made the world and the stars and the people.

[pause]

On the fifth day there is a strange and solemn ceremony. The king comes to the temple. He stands before the statue of Marduk. The high priest takes away his crown and his royal robes. He lays them before the god. Then the priest taps the king on the cheek and pulls his ears. The king kneels before the god. He says he has done no wrong. He has not neglected his duties. He has not harmed Babylon or its people.

[pause]

{img:king-kneeling-before-marduk-statue-high-priest-temple-ceremony}
Then the priest gives him back his crown. Everyone watches the king's face. If the king has tears in his eyes, it is a good sign. Marduk is pleased. And the year will be good.

[long-pause]

So every year, the most powerful king in the world must make himself humble before the god. And he must promise to rule well and fairly.

[pause]

Then the gods arrive. From all the cities of the land, the statues of the gods are brought by boat to Babylon. They come along the rivers and canals to visit Marduk for the New Year. Their boats are decorated with gold and flowers and banners. Priests and musicians come with them.

[pause]

{img:procession-through-ishtar-gate-golden-statues-chariots-music-crowds}
On the eighth and ninth days there is the great procession. The king takes the hand of Marduk's statue and leads him out of his temple. The statues of all the gods follow, on carts and carried by priests. They go along the holy road between the blue walls and the golden lions. They pass through the Ishtar Gate. There are drums and harps and pipes and singing and incense. The whole city crowds along the road and on the walls and the roofs to watch.

[long-pause]

They go out to the river and by boat to a special temple in a garden outside the city. The gods stay there for a few days. There are ceremonies and feasts.

[pause]

Then the gods come back into the city in another procession. Through the Ishtar Gate and along the holy road to the temple. The gods meet together to decide the fate of the land for the coming year. Then they go home to their own cities in their boats.

[pause]

{img:babylon-festival-night-lamps-rooftops-music-families-celebrating}
All through the festival, the city celebrates. There are feasts and music and dancing in the streets. People wear their best clothes. They visit their families and give each other gifts. The temples give out food and drink. At night lamps burn on the roofs and the walls. The city glows. And the music and singing go on late into the night.

[long-pause]

When it is all over, the new year has begun. The world has been made new. The king has been confirmed. The gods have decided the fate of the land. People go back to their homes and their fields and their work. The barley is harvested. And life goes on.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonian-household-shrine-clay-figurines-lamp-offering-dates-evening}
The world of the Babylonians is full of gods.

[pause]

There are the great gods of the sky and the earth and the waters. There is the old god of the sky. There is the lord of the wind. And there is Ea, the clever kind god of the sweet waters under the earth. He loves people and helps them. There is the sun god, who sees everything as he crosses the sky each day. He is the god of justice and truth. There is the moon god with his silver curve. And there is the storm god with his lightning.

[long-pause]

There is Ishtar, goddess of love and of the morning and evening star. She is beautiful and fierce. There are many other goddesses too. A goddess of healing with her dog. A goddess of writing. Even a goddess of beer.

[pause]

And above them all, in Babylon, is Marduk, king of the gods. And his son Nabu, the god of writing and wisdom.

[pause]

{img:temple-of-ishtar-babylon-courtyard-worshippers-raising-hands-prayer}
Every city has its own god and its own temple. In Babylon there are dozens of temples all over the city. And there are hundreds of little shrines at street corners and in walls. People stop there and leave a small gift. A little flour, a few dates, a little lamp. And they say a prayer.

[long-pause]

Every family has its own god too. It is a personal god who watches over them and speaks for them to the great gods. In a corner of the house, they keep little clay figures of their god and of good spirits. They give them small gifts every day and pray.

[pause]

The Babylonians believe the gods send messages everywhere. In the stars and the clouds. In the flight of birds. In patterns of oil poured on water. In the smoke of incense. In dreams. Special priests know how to read these signs. They tell people what the gods want and what the future will bring. People wait anxiously for their answer.

[pause]

{img:diviner-priest-reading-oil-on-water-bowl-client-temple-room}
They also believe in spirits all around them, good and bad. In the desert and the wind and the dark. So they wear little charms around their necks. They bury little figures of good spirits under the floors and doorways. Great stone guardians stand at the palace gates. They have the bodies of bulls or lions, with wings and human heads. They are there to keep bad things out.

[long-pause]

When people are ill, they go to two kinds of healer. One is like a doctor. He uses medicines from plants and minerals, and bandages and creams. The other is like a priest. He uses prayers and spells to chase away whatever caused the illness. Often people go to both.

[pause]

The Babylonians do not believe in a happy heaven after death. They believe the dead go down to a grey quiet land under the earth. There they live a shadowy life. They depend on their families to remember them and give them food and water. So families remember their ancestors. They speak their names. And every month they give them a little food and water, so they will be at peace.

[pause]

{img:babylonian-family-offering-ancestors-water-bread-floor-lamp-courtyard}
In the evening, in many houses of Babylon, a mother or father lights a little lamp before the family's god in the corner of the room. They pour a little water and leave a few dates and some bread. And they say a quiet prayer. For the family and the children and the ancestors. For the house. And for the night.

[long-pause]

{img:hanging-gardens-babylon-terraces-trees-flowers-waterfalls-palace-imagined}
Of all the wonders of Babylon, the most famous is one that may never have existed. The Hanging Gardens.

[pause]

Greek and Roman writers, centuries later, told a beautiful story. They said King Nebuchadnezzar had a wife who was a princess from the mountains to the north-east. Her land was green, with mountains and forests and streams.

[long-pause]

When she came to Babylon to marry the king, she was homesick. The land was flat and hot and brown. She missed the green hills and the trees of home. So the king built her a mountain in the middle of his city. It was a great garden rising up in steps, one above another, like a green hill. It was planted with trees and flowers from all over the empire. Machines lifted water from the river to the top. From there it flowed down through the steps in streams and waterfalls.

[pause]

From below, the trees seemed to hang in the air above the city. So people called them the Hanging Gardens of Babylon. The Greeks counted them among the Seven Wonders of the World. Together with the Pyramids of Egypt and other great works.

[pause]

{img:terraced-garden-palace-babylon-cedar-trees-pomegranates-queen-walking}
It is a lovely story. But there is a mystery.

[long-pause]

The Babylonians wrote down so much on their clay tablets. But they never mention the Hanging Gardens. Nebuchadnezzar wrote so proudly about all his buildings. But he never says a word about them. And archaeologists have dug in Babylon for more than a hundred years. They have never found them for certain.

[pause]

So where are they? Some scholars think the gardens were real but were close to the river, and have been washed away. Some think a strange building found in the palace, with arches and wells, might have been part of them. Others think the story is really about another city further north. There, about a hundred years earlier, a king built a famous garden on a hill. It was watered by great canals and machines. Maybe later writers mixed up the two cities.

[pause]

{img:assyrian-garden-relief-style-trees-aqueduct-hillside-water-channels}
And others think the gardens were never really there at all. Perhaps they were only a dream. A legend of a beautiful green impossible garden in the desert.

[long-pause]

But there certainly were gardens in Babylon. The kings and the nobles and the temples had gardens and orchards. They had palms and fruit trees and flowers and pools. And perhaps somewhere in the palace by the river there was a garden on a high terrace. A homesick queen could walk there in the evening in the shade of the trees. She could smell the flowers and look out over the walls toward the north-east, where her mountains were.

[pause]

So we will leave the Hanging Gardens as a beautiful mystery. Green and cool, floating above the city in the dreams of the world, as they have for more than two thousand years.

[long-pause]

{img:judean-exiles-sitting-riverbank-willows-harps-babylon-canal-evening}
In these years, many peoples come to Babylon. But some of them do not come because they want to.

[pause]

Far to the west, beyond the desert, lies a small kingdom called Judah. Its capital is Jerusalem. It refused to obey Nebuchadnezzar. Twice, his armies went there. The city was taken and its temple was destroyed. The king of Judah and many of his people were brought a long way across the desert to Babylon. Nobles and priests and craftsmen and their families. They were made to live far from home. They are called the exiles.

[long-pause]

We will leave the sad days of the war and the long journey there, gently, in the past. And we will think of the people and their new lives in a strange land.

[pause]

The exiles are not kept in prisons. They are settled in villages and towns around Babylon, beside the canals. They are given land to farm and houses to live in. Some live in the city itself. They can work and farm and trade and marry. They can build houses and plant gardens. And they can keep their own customs and their own faith.

[pause]

{img:clay-ration-tablet-cuneiform-king-jehoiachin-palace-storeroom}
The king of Judah lives at the court of Babylon with his family and servants. Archaeologists found clay tablets in the palace store rooms. They list the oil and barley given to the foreign guests at court. And there is his name, written in wedges. The king of the land of Judah, and his five sons.

[long-pause]

Other tablets were found more recently. They come from a small town near Babylon. Its name means the town of Judah. The exiles lived there and farmed and paid their taxes and made contracts. Their Hebrew names appear in the tablets for several generations. Fathers and sons and grandsons, living and working and raising their families beside the canals of Babylon.

[pause]

But many of the exiles are homesick for their own land and hills and city and temple. And they remember it and sing about it. One of the most famous songs in the Bible begins like this.

[pause]

{img:willow-trees-babylon-canal-quiet-dusk-lyres-hanging-branches}
By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, and we wept, when we remembered home. We hung our harps upon the willows.

[long-pause]

In Babylon, during these years, the exiles and their teachers did something important. They gathered and wrote down and copied the old writings of their people. Their laws and stories and the words of their prophets. That way nothing would be forgotten, far from home. Much of what became the Hebrew Bible took shape in these years, beside the rivers of Babylon.

[pause]

The Bible also tells stories of young men from Judah at the court of Nebuchadnezzar. Daniel and his three friends. They learned the language and writing of the Babylonians and served the king wisely. But they stayed faithful to their own God, even when it was dangerous.

[pause]

{img:exile-family-courtyard-babylon-lamp-reading-scroll-children-listening}
About sixty years later, the Persians will take Babylon. The Persian king will allow the exiles to go home to Jerusalem and rebuild their temple. Many will go. But many will stay in Babylon. They were born there and grew up there. They have made their homes and their lives there.

[long-pause]

For more than two and a half thousand years, until our own times, there was a great and learned Jewish community in this land. It became one of the most important centres of Jewish learning in the world. Many centuries later, it wrote a great book of law and teaching that is still studied today.

[long-pause]

{img:brickmakers-babylon-wooden-moulds-mud-straw-rows-drying-sun}
All through Babylon, in workshops and yards and streets, the craftsmen are at work.

[pause]

In these years, the most important of all are the brick makers. Babylon is built of bricks. Millions of them. For the walls and temples and palaces. For the houses and streets and quays.

[long-pause]

Outside the city by the river there is plenty of clay and water. The brick makers work there in big yards. They mix clay with water and chopped straw. They tread it with their feet into thick mud. They press it into square wooden moulds. Then they turn the bricks out in long rows on the ground to dry in the sun. In the summer heat they dry in a few days. But if it rains, all that work is ruined.

[pause]

Most bricks are simply dried in the sun. But the best ones are baked in big ovens. They come out hard and yellow and red. They are used for outer walls and floors and places that must stand up to water. For the most important buildings, each brick is stamped with the king's name before it dries.

[pause]

Then there are the glaze makers. They make the wonderful blue bricks of the Ishtar Gate and the holy road.

[long-pause]

It is very skilled and very difficult work. Each brick is made in a special mould. It has one part of an animal standing out from it. A leg, or a mane, or a tail. When all the bricks are laid in the right order, the whole animal appears. The craftsmen mark each brick with a little sign. That way they know exactly where it goes. One brick in the wrong place, and the lion would have a tail on its head.

[pause]

Then the glaze is painted on. It is a kind of liquid glass, made from sand and plant ash and minerals. It is coloured blue and yellow and white and brown. Thin black lines keep the colours from running together. Then the bricks are baked again. The glaze melts and becomes hard and shining like glass.

[pause]

{img:weaver-babylon-loom-dyed-wool-red-blue-patterns-workshop-light}
There are weavers, men and women, at their looms in the temples and houses. There are dyers who dip wool in great tubs of red and blue and purple and yellow. There are tailors and people who sew patterns into cloth.

[long-pause]

There are goldsmiths and silversmiths making jewellery and cups and statues. There are blacksmiths making tools and pots. There are carpenters and boat builders. There are reed workers weaving baskets and mats and boats. There are potters at their wheels and leather workers. There are perfume makers mixing sweet oils. And there are stone carvers who make seals.

[pause]

A seal is a little stone cylinder about the size of a finger. It is carved with tiny pictures of gods and animals and people. When you roll it across wet clay, it leaves a little picture. It is like a signature. Every important person in Babylon has a seal. They wear it on a string around the neck or the wrist.

[pause]

{img:cylinder-seal-rolled-on-clay-impression-tiny-figures-close-detail}
Many craftsmen work for the temples and the palace. They are paid in barley and dates and wool and oil and silver. Others have their own workshops and sell what they make in the streets and at the gates. Often the same craft is passed down in the same family for generations. From father to son and mother to daughter, in the same streets of Babylon.

[long-pause]

{img:persian-army-cyrus-entering-babylon-gate-peaceful-crowds-539bc}
Nebuchadnezzar rules for forty-three years. He passes away as an old man.

[pause]

After him, Babylon has several kings in a short time. The last king of Babylon is an unusual man who loves old things and the moon god. He spends ten years far away in the deserts of Arabia. He leaves his son to rule in Babylon. And far to the east, a new power is growing.

[long-pause]

About sixty years after our story, Cyrus the Great, king of the Persians, comes with his army from the east. Babylon is taken quickly, almost without a fight. It is said the people welcome Cyrus with green branches. He enters the city in peace. He honours Marduk and the temples. And he lets the people live as before.

[pause]

On a clay cylinder, now in a museum in London, Cyrus tells his story. He says Marduk chose him to be king of Babylon. And he says he let the peoples who had been brought to Babylon go home, with their gods.

[pause]

{img:alexander-the-great-babylon-palace-323bc-quiet-evening-columns}
Babylon becomes one of the capitals of the Persian Empire. It stays rich and great for two hundred years. Then Alexander the Great comes and takes it from the Persians. He plans to make it the capital of his own great empire, from Greece to India. But he falls ill with a fever in Babylon, in the old palace of Nebuchadnezzar. And he passes away there, at only thirty-two.

[long-pause]

After Alexander, his generals build new cities. Slowly people move away from Babylon. The old temples are still kept. The star watchers still watch the sky and write their diaries on clay, for another two hundred years. The last clay tablet in the old writing that we know of was written about seventy-five years after Christ. After that, the old writing was forgotten.

[pause]

Slowly, over the centuries, Babylon fades. The river changes its course. The canals fill with mud. The great buildings crumble. People take the bricks away to build new houses in new villages. And the great city becomes a field of ruins and mounds and broken bricks in the desert, beside the river.

[pause]

{img:ruined-mounds-babylon-desert-broken-bricks-palms-medieval-traveller}
Now and then over the centuries, travellers came and looked at the mounds. Could this really be the great Babylon of the Bible and the old stories? Local people dug in the ruins for bricks. They found the stamped bricks of Nebuchadnezzar. They used them in their houses and walls and dams. So the king's name was built into houses all around the region.

[long-pause]

But Babylon was not forgotten. Its name lived on in the Bible. It lived on in the writings of the Greeks and Romans. In poems and stories and paintings and songs all over the world, for two thousand years. A name of wonder and splendour and mystery. Waiting under the earth to be found again.

[long-pause]

{img:koldewey-excavation-babylon-1900-workers-baskets-railway-ishtar-gate-emerging}
In the year 1899, a German archaeologist called Robert Koldewey came to Babylon.

[pause]

He was a builder by training. He was a careful patient man with a big moustache and a love of old buildings. He was sent to dig in the ruins of Babylon. He stayed for nearly eighteen years.

[long-pause]

With hundreds of local workers, he dug carefully, layer by layer, through the mounds of earth and broken bricks. A little railway with small wagons carried the earth away. He uncovered the great walls and gates and palaces and temples. The streets and the houses of Nebuchadnezzar's city. He mapped them and drew them and photographed them.

[pause]

One day, in the north of the city, his workers began to find pieces of glazed brick. Blue and yellow and white. Thousands of them, broken and scattered in the earth. What could they be? They kept digging. And deeper down they found the bottom of a great gate, still standing. The bulls and dragons were still in place, in rows on the walls. They had been buried for more than two thousand years. It was the Ishtar Gate.

[pause]

{img:museum-workers-reassembling-glazed-brick-fragments-tables-berlin-1920s}
The thousands of broken pieces were packed carefully into crates. They were sent by ship to Berlin. There, over many years, museum workers sorted them. They cleaned them and fitted them together like a huge jigsaw puzzle with hundreds of thousands of pieces. And slowly they rebuilt the front of the Ishtar Gate and part of the holy road with its lions, in a museum in Berlin.

[long-pause]

It opened to the public almost a hundred years ago. Since then millions of people have walked through it. They have stood beneath the great blue gate with its golden animals. And for a moment they have felt as if they were in Babylon.

[pause]

Since then, archaeologists from many countries, and from Iraq itself, have dug in Babylon and studied it. The clay tablets found there have been read and translated. So we know the names of the kings and the writers and the traders and the star watchers. We know about the ordinary people of Babylon. Their business and their prayers and their lives.

[pause]

{img:scholar-museum-reading-cuneiform-tablet-magnifying-glass-lamp}
And the sky diaries and the maths tablets and the letters and the stories are still being read today. In universities and museums all over the world. Every year new tablets are read. And new things are learned.

[long-pause]

{img:babylon-ruins-today-iraq-reconstructed-walls-palms-euphrates-sunset}
Today Babylon lies in Iraq, about eighty-five kilometres south of Baghdad, beside the river Euphrates.

[pause]

A few years ago it became a World Heritage Site. It is protected as one of the most important places in the history of the world.

[long-pause]

Visitors can walk through the ruins along the old streets between the mud brick walls. Some walls are still standing. Some have been rebuilt in modern times. Visitors can see the bottom of the Ishtar Gate. The moulded bulls and dragons are still in place there. They have no glaze, because they were below the blue bricks, deep in the ground. Visitors can walk along part of the holy road. And they can see the great stone lion of Babylon. It is carved of black stone and is very old and mysterious. It has stood in the ruins for many centuries.

[pause]

At the entrance there is a smaller modern copy of the Ishtar Gate, painted blue. Visitors walk through it and take photos.

[pause]

Around the ruins there are palm groves and fields and canals and villages. And the river flows past slowly, as it did in the time of Nebuchadnezzar. Over the centuries it has moved a little to the west.

[long-pause]

In museums around the world, Babylon lives on. In Berlin, the Ishtar Gate rises blue and gold under the museum roof. In Baghdad, and in London and Paris and New York, the lions of the holy road are shown and admired. And so are the tablets of the writers and the bricks of Nebuchadnezzar.

[pause]

And Babylon lives on in our hours and minutes and degrees. In the names of the stars and the star signs. In the stories of the Bible. In our language and our imagination. A name of wonder and splendour and mystery.

[pause]

{img:euphrates-palm-groves-evening-today-iraq-river-calm-reflections}
So we leave Babylon tonight, in the year 600 before Christ, as the evening falls.

[long-pause]

The sun is setting behind the palm groves on the west bank of the river. The great tower glows red and gold in the last light. The blue bricks of the Ishtar Gate turn deep dark blue. The golden lions on the walls of the holy road fade into the dusk. On the roofs of the houses, families spread their mats and light their little lamps. The children lie down to sleep under the open sky. On the temple roof, an old star watcher settles down with his tablet and his reed. He watches the moon rise over the river. And the stars come out one by one, over the great city and the palm trees and the quiet flowing water.
