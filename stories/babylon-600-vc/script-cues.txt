Around the year 600 BC, one city stood above all others in the ancient world. Its name was Babylon. It rose from a flat plain beside the Euphrates, with blue gates, a great stepped tower and streets full of traders from distant lands. Yet behind its famous walls lived ordinary families, scribes, brewers and star watchers. In this story, we will look at how the city worked, and why it was remembered for so long.

[long-pause]

The word Mesopotamia simply means "the land between the rivers." Babylon stood in a country the Greeks later called Mesopotamia, which means exactly that. The two rivers were the Tigris and the Euphrates. Both began in the mountains far to the north and flowed south for more than a thousand kilometres. Near Babylon they came quite close together before wandering on to the sea. The land between them was flat and brown, with rich soil that the rivers had carried down over thousands of years. There was one great problem. It hardly ever rained. The summers were long, hot and dry, and without the rivers, nothing at all would have grown. So the people who lived here learned to work with the water instead of waiting for the sky. Today most of this land lies in the country of Iraq. But in the time of Nebuchadnezzar, it was the busiest, most crowded and most cultured corner of the known world. Everything that made Babylon possible began with two muddy rivers.

[long-pause]

{img:02-irrigation-canals-palm-groves}

Babylon's farmers turned a desert into a garden by digging channels. The secret of the plain was the canal. A canal is simply a channel that leads river water away to fields and gardens, and the people of Mesopotamia had been digging them for thousands of years. Around Babylon, a great web of them spread in every direction. The biggest were as wide as rivers, and boats sailed along them. The smallest were little ditches between fields, with tiny gates of earth and reeds that a farmer could open and close by hand. Wherever the water reached, the land was green, with barley, wheat, vegetables, and forests of date palms. Wherever it did not, there was only dust. Yet canals needed constant care. The rivers carried so much mud that the channels slowly filled up, and every year they had to be dug out again. A neglected canal meant dry fields at the far end, and an empty harvest. Keeping the water flowing was a duty shared by every family, and by the king.

[long-pause]

{img:03-ancient-sumerian-ruins-ziggurat}

By Nebuchadnezzar's time, the first cities in the world were already ancient history. More than two thousand years before Nebuchadnezzar, in this same southern land, a people called the Sumerians built some of the first cities anywhere. They raised temples and walls and chose kings. They pressed the first writing into wet clay with the end of a reed. They made wheeled carts, ploughs and sailing boats, and they wrote down the earliest great poems. By the year 600 BC, the Sumerians themselves were long gone, and many of their cities lay half buried in sand. Yet the Babylonians had not forgotten them. Scribes still copied the old Sumerian texts. Priests still sang the old prayers in the old tongue, much as Latin later lived on in churches. To an educated Babylonian, the past was not a distant thing. It was a deep and respected layer beneath every street. Babylon was simply the newest page in a very long book.

[long-pause]

{img:04-southern-marsh-reed-houses-canoe}

In the far south, people lived in houses made of living reeds, much as their ancestors had. Far to the south, where the Euphrates approached the sea, the river spread out into a great wetland. Beyond the last villages lay tall reed marshes full of birds, fish and still, silvery water. People there lived in houses woven from bundles of giant reeds. The reeds were bent into graceful arches, like the ribs of a ship. Families travelled in slim canoes, kept water buffalo and caught fish and wildfowl. This way of life was already old when the first cities were built. Remarkably, a similar one continued in the same region for thousands of years. To a townsman in Babylon, the marshes were a wild and slightly mysterious place at the bottom of the map. Yet they were part of the same country. Reeds from the marshes became the city's mats, baskets and boats. Even the greatest city depended on the quiet wetlands far downstream.

[long-pause]

{img:05-babylon-old-capital-hammurabi-era}

Babylon was already more than fifteen hundred years old when Nebuchadnezzar made it great. The city was not new. It had stood on the Euphrates for well over a thousand years, and long ago it had been the capital of a famous king called Hammurabi. Since then, Babylon had risen and fallen many times. It had been conquered, burned, rebuilt and conquered again. There had been long years when it was only one town among many, and years when a fierce empire from the north held it in an iron grip. Yet each time, the city came back. People loved it, and the god Marduk was thought to live there. Then, in the lifetime of Nebuchadnezzar's father, the northern empire collapsed, and Babylon was free once more. The new family of kings made an extraordinary decision. They would not simply rule the old city. They would rebuild it, from the walls to the gates to the holy road, and make it grander than it had ever been.

[long-pause]

{img:06-traveller-boat-euphrates-morning}

Many travellers reached Babylon by boat, drifting down the Euphrates for many days. Traders from the northern mountains often came to Babylon by river. They loaded wine and timber onto boats or rafts and let the current carry them south. The journey could take many days. Boatmen slept on deck under the stars as the land slid slowly past. They passed steep banks, mud-brick villages, groves of palms and flocks coming down to drink. The farther south they went, the flatter and greener the land became. Canals branched away on both sides. Villages grew larger and closer together. The river filled with rafts, barges and small round boats turning in the current. Travel by water was slow, but it was easier than crossing the dry plain on foot. It also suited heavy cargo, which no donkey could carry far. In a land without hills or forests, the river was the great road. Almost everything that reached Babylon from the north arrived this way.

[long-pause]

{img:07-first-sight-of-tower-haze}

From many miles away, travellers could see the top of Babylon's great tower shining blue. On the flat Mesopotamian plain, there were no hills to hide the view. So the first sign of Babylon appeared long before the city itself. Above the palms, a great stepped tower rose out of the morning haze. The shrine on its summit was covered in blue glazed bricks, and it caught the early sunlight. As boats came closer, the long brown walls appeared, with hundreds of towers along them. Smoke rose from the houses, and temple roofs showed above the walls. Outside the gates lay busy quays, crowded with boats. Porters carried sacks up the steps. Scribes counted every load and recorded it on clay. Many languages could be heard at once. Ancient writers who described Babylon often began with its size and splendour. That was the impression the city was built to give. Its first message to every visitor was simple. This was a place of power, wealth and the gods.

[long-pause]

{img:08-king-nebuchadnezzar-portrait-throne}

Nebuchadnezzar's name means "O god Nabu, protect my son." In the year 600 BC, the king of Babylon was Nebuchadnezzar, a man in his thirties who had already ruled for several years. His name was really a short prayer to Nabu, the god of writing and wisdom. His father had freed Babylon from the Assyrian empire that once held it. The young prince commanded armies even before he was crowned. He went on to rule for forty-three years, one of the longest reigns in the city's history. He appears in the Bible as a proud and powerful king, and Greek writers remembered him too. Yet his own inscriptions present a different picture. In them, he describes himself mainly as a builder and a servant of the gods. He wanted to be remembered for temples, walls and canals rather than for conquest. The ruins of Babylon suggest that this was not only boasting. A great deal of what survives from the city was built in his reign.

[long-pause]

{img:09-king-inspecting-building-works-scribes}

Nebuchadnezzar rarely wrote about his battles. He wrote about his buildings. Nebuchadnezzar had long inscriptions written on clay cylinders, stone slabs and bricks. Many were buried in the foundations of his buildings, for future kings to find. Most of these texts are lists of building work. They describe temples he restored for the gods, walls he strengthened and canals he dug. They mention cedar brought from distant mountains, gates covered in bronze and doorposts plated with silver. Battles and enemies appear far less often. Instead, the king presents himself as a faithful builder, a servant of Marduk and a friend of his city. This was partly royal publicity, as all such inscriptions were. Even so, the archaeological evidence supports him. During his long reign, almost every major structure in Babylon was rebuilt or enlarged. For forty years, the dust and noise of construction must have been part of ordinary life. The city that later travellers admired was largely his work.

[long-pause]

{img:10-stamped-bricks-king-name-closeup}

Millions of bricks were stamped with the king's name before they were even baked. Building a city on this scale required an enormous number of bricks. Brick makers worked in large yards near the river. On many bricks meant for royal buildings, they pressed a stamp into the wet clay before it was fired. The stamp carried a short line of wedge-shaped writing. It named Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon, son of Nabopolassar, king of Babylon. Walls, pavements and temples were therefore filled with tiny repeated signatures. Archaeologists have found these stamped bricks by the thousand. Many now rest in museums around the world. For centuries, local villagers also dug bricks out of the ruins and reused them in their own houses. As a result, the king's name can still be found in walls far from the ancient city. Nebuchadnezzar hoped his name would last forever. In a small and rather humbling way, it did, preserved one brick at a time.

[long-pause]

{img:11-southern-palace-courtyards-babylon}

The royal palace was like a small city inside the city, with five great courtyards in a row. Near the river and the northern gate stood the Southern Palace, the king's main home and office. Visitors walked through one huge courtyard after another, each more impressive than the last. Around the courtyards were halls, offices, kitchens and storerooms, where officials and servants kept the machinery of an empire turning. There were rooms for the royal family, for the guards, for the treasury and for the great archives. Here the king's scribes wrote his letters and orders, and kept the accounts of the whole kingdom on baskets of clay tablets. Barley, oil and dates were counted into the stores here. Gold and silver were weighed here. The palace was not simply a house with a crown on top. It was the working heart of an empire that stretched from the sea in the south to the mountains in the north, and west almost to the border of Egypt. It never completely slept.

[long-pause]

{img:12-throne-room-blue-facade-lions}

The throne room had a front wall of shining blue bricks and a parade of golden lions. At the heart of the palace lay the throne room, a long hall more than fifty metres from end to end, with three wide doorways opening onto the courtyard. Its outer wall glowed with colour. On a deep blue background, tall slender columns rose like palm trees, topped with bands of flowers, while beneath them a row of lions walked in white and gold. Visitors who entered the courtyard on a sunny morning must have been dazzled. Inside, opposite the main doorway, there was a deep niche in the wall, and in that niche stood the royal throne. From there the king could receive officials, priests and guests from across his empire, framed by the shadows of the room and seen from the far end of the hall. Much of this shining facade survived in pieces. Archaeologists found fragments of it in the ruins, and a careful reconstruction still shows how bright it must have been.

[long-pause]

{img:13-ambassadors-bowing-tribute-gifts}

Ambassadors had to walk through five courtyards before they could even see the king. Envoys from Egypt, the coastal cities and the mountain kingdoms all came to Babylon's palace. They brought gifts and letters, and they were led slowly through one courtyard after another. Each gateway was taller and each wall more splendid than the last. The design was deliberate. By the time visitors reached the throne room, they were meant to feel small. The king sat at the far end in rich robes and a tall crown, surrounded by officials and guards. Messengers bowed low and chose their words with great care. A careless sentence could anger a powerful ruler. Diplomacy in the ancient Near East depended on personal meetings, gifts, flattery and patience. Every promise was also written down by scribes on clay. Letters from this wider world of kings have survived in several archives. They show that ancient rulers watched each other closely, and that ceremony was itself a kind of language.

[long-pause]

{img:14-palace-collection-old-statues-hall}

The kings of Babylon collected antiques, and one palace held what has been called an early museum. Nebuchadnezzar loved old things. In one of his palaces, archaeologists found a collection of statues, carved stones and inscribed objects that were already ancient in his day. Some came from other lands, taken as spoils of war or received as gifts. Some were pieces of Babylon's own earlier past. A statue from a far-off country, a stone with writing in a forgotten style, a carved piece that had probably belonged to an earlier king. There is no way to be sure that the objects were arranged for visitors to admire, but many scholars believe this was a kind of royal collection, with the past on display. It fits the character of the time. The kings of Babylon repaired old temples, copied old writings and searched for the foundation records of their ancestors. They respected the people who came before them, and they wanted to understand them. A king who collects history is also a king who knows he will one day be part of it.

[long-pause]

{img:15-palace-roof-walk-evening-city}

When the day's business ended, the great palace fell silent, and lamps were lit one by one. When the day's business ended, the palace slowly emptied. Visitors went to their rooms, and scribes walked home through the cooling streets. Servants lit small clay lamps that burned sesame oil, and their light spread along the corridors. From the high walls above the river, the whole city could be seen in the evening. The great tower and the temples stood dark against the sunset. The Euphrates turned gold and then red. Lamps appeared in thousands of houses, and the date palms stretched away towards the horizon. Palace life was not only about ceremony and power. It was also a place where people ate, rested and slept like everyone else. The king who received ambassadors in the morning still lived under the same sky as the city's farmers and potters. In the end, the evening breeze off the river reached the palace and the smallest house alike.

[long-pause]

{img:16-double-walls-towers-moat}

Babylon was protected by two enormous walls, with a moat of river water in front. The inner city was almost square, and the Euphrates ran straight through the middle. Around it ran a double wall of mud brick, with a deep ditch full of river water in front of it. The inner wall was about six metres thick, and the outer one nearly four. Between them was a wide space, and every twenty metres or so along the whole length, a tower stood up above the line. There were hundreds of them. Beyond all this, an even larger outer wall formed a great triangle many kilometres around, enclosing gardens, fields and palm groves as well as the city. A besieging army would have to cross the moat, get through two walls under fire from hundreds of towers, and then face the streets. For centuries it was one of the strongest defences in the ancient world. People inside could sleep soundly. Or at least they hoped so.

[long-pause]

{img:17-walls-chariot-top-herodotus}

One Greek visitor claimed that a four-horse chariot could turn around on top of the walls. Herodotus, a Greek writer of the fifth century BC, described Babylon with real astonishment. According to him, the walls were so wide that a four-horse chariot could drive along the top and even turn around. He also gave exact figures for their height, and these are almost certainly too large. Herodotus was a gifted storyteller, but he relied on guides and travellers for much of his information. He sometimes repeated what he heard without checking it. Even so, a kernel of truth lies behind his amazement. Excavations show that the walls really were remarkably broad. They were wide enough for guards to patrol and for soldiers to move quickly from one section to another. Stories about famous places often grow with every telling. Babylon was impressive enough without exaggeration. Yet its size invited exaggeration, and ancient writers were happy to provide it.

[long-pause]

{img:18-eight-city-gates-god-names}

Each of Babylon's eight great gates was named after a god. Eight gates opened through the city walls, and none of them was just a hole in a wall. Each was a tall double gateway with towers, a long passage and heavy doors of cedar covered in bronze. Each had a name, and each name was a god. There was a gate of the sun god, a gate of the storm god, a gate of the moon god and a gate of the king of the gods himself. Passing through one was a small religious act, even if you were only a farmer bringing onions to the market. Guards stood on the towers all day and all night. At sunset the doors were closed and barred, and the gates were sealed against the dark. Inside the gate, the long passage was cool and dim, and people paused to let their eyes adjust. Then they emerged into the full noise of the city.

[long-pause]

{img:19-ishtar-gate-front-blue-towers}

The most beautiful gate in the ancient world was covered from top to bottom in shining blue. In the north of the city stood the Ishtar Gate, named for the goddess of love and the morning star. Nebuchadnezzar had it rebuilt more than once, each time higher and grander than before. Its two great towers framed a passage tall enough for the statues of the gods to roll through on their carts at festival time. And every surface was covered in glazed bricks of a deep, brilliant blue, as rich as lapis lazuli, the precious stone from the far mountains. On the blue, in long rows, walked animals made of moulded bricks that stood out from the wall. They shone in yellow, white and gold. Along the top ran battlements and bands of flowers. In the morning light, the whole gate glowed like a jewel. People who came to Babylon for the first time stopped in the road, just to stare. Some of them stood there for a long time.

[long-pause]

{img:20-dragon-and-bull-glazed-relief}

The animals on the gate were dragons, bulls and, above all, a mixture of several creatures. The Ishtar Gate carried hundreds of animals, arranged in rows. Some were bulls with long blue horns, which belonged to the storm god. The others were dragons that the Babylonians called by a name meaning something like "the fierce snake." These creatures had the head of a serpent, a lion's front legs, an eagle's back feet, and a long tail ending in a scorpion's sting. They belonged to Marduk, the great god of the city. They were shown walking in solemn lines, heads held high, as though marching towards the city to guard it. Their scales and horns were picked out in yellow and white against the blue. It may sound a little fierce, but in the eyes of the Babylonians they were protectors. Strangers entering Babylon passed beneath hundreds of them, and the message was clear. This city was under watchful, powerful care.

[long-pause]

{img:21-glazed-bricks-moulds-craftsmen}

Every animal on the gate was assembled like a jigsaw puzzle from numbered bricks. The animals were not painted onto the wall. Each brick was pressed into a special mould, so that one part of the creature stood out from it. A single brick might carry a hoof, a piece of mane, or the curve of a tail. Craftsmen glazed the bricks in blue, yellow, white and brown, with thin dark lines to keep the colours from running together. The glaze was a type of coloured glass, made from sand, plant ash and minerals. Then everything was baked again, until the surface became hard and shiny. Each brick was marked on its side with a small sign, so the builders knew exactly where it belonged. When all of them were laid in the right order, the whole animal appeared on the wall. One brick in the wrong place, and the lion would have a tail on its head. It was clever work, and a good reminder of how much patience went into every wonder.

[long-pause]

{img:22-processional-way-lions-blue}

The holy road was lined with about a hundred and twenty striding lions. Beyond the Ishtar Gate, a broad straight street ran through the city. It is now called the Processional Way. It was about twenty metres wide and paved with large slabs of white and reddish stone. High walls of blue glazed brick lined both sides. On those walls, one after another, walked lions. There were about a hundred and twenty of them, with white and golden bodies, open mouths and raised tails. The lion was the animal of Ishtar, the goddess of the gate. Bands of flowers ran above and below them. On ordinary days, this was a busy street, full of donkeys, carts and traders. During religious festivals, it became the route for the statues of the gods and their great processions. The road therefore served both daily life and sacred ceremony. Few streets in the ancient world combined beauty, religion and practical use so completely.

[long-pause]

{img:23-gate-today-berlin-museum-hall}

A reconstructed part of the Ishtar Gate still stands today, inside a museum in Berlin. Over the centuries, the Ishtar Gate was buried by time and rubble. German archaeologists excavated it at the beginning of the twentieth century. Thousands of broken glazed bricks were packed into crates and shipped to Berlin. There, museum workers spent years cleaning, sorting and fitting the pieces together. It was like a giant puzzle with hundreds of thousands of fragments. The result was a reconstruction of the gate's front, with part of the Processional Way and its lions. It opened to the public in 1930 inside a vast museum hall. Since then, millions of people have walked through it. Other lions from the holy road are now in museums in Paris, London and New York. The colours look slightly different under museum lighting than under the Mesopotamian sun. Yet the glaze is the same glaze, made in Babylon more than two and a half thousand years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:24-etemenanki-ziggurat-seven-stages}

The great tower of Babylon was probably as tall as a building of thirty floors. In the middle of the old city stood a ziggurat, a tower built in steps. Its name was Etemenanki, which means something like "the house that is the foundation of heaven and earth." It rose from a square base about ninety metres on each side, in stage after stage, each one smaller than the one below, like a staircase for giants. According to a clay tablet that has survived, it may have been about as tall as it was wide. For its time, it was the tallest building in the world. A wide triple stairway ran up the front, and at the very top stood a small temple for Marduk, covered in shining blue bricks, which glittered in the sun. People could see it across the flat plain from a long way off. Nothing else in all of Mesopotamia looked quite like it. Everyone who saw it felt a little smaller, and a little closer to the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:25-tower-of-babel-languages-market}

Many people believe this tower inspired the story of the Tower of Babel. In the Bible, there is a story about people who tried to build a tower that would reach heaven. God confused their languages, so that they could no longer understand each other, and the builders scattered across the earth. Babel is the Hebrew form of the name Babylon. Many scholars think the story was inspired by this very tower, which exiles from Judah saw rising above the city. Babylon really was a city of many languages. Babylonian and Aramaic were spoken in its streets. Hebrew, Egyptian, Greek and Persian could also be heard in its markets and on its quays. Traders and scribes often moved between several tongues. The idea of a great tower and a confusion of languages therefore fits the city remarkably well. Stories of this kind often begin with something real. Over time, they grow into something larger, and they come to belong to people far beyond the place that inspired them.

[long-pause]

{img:26-priests-climbing-ziggurat-stars}

Only priests were allowed to climb to the top, and there they watched the stars. The Babylonians believed the gods lived in the heavens. The ziggurat served as a kind of stairway between sky and earth, by which the gods could come down to their city. The small temple at its summit was a resting place for the god on that journey. Ordinary people were not allowed to climb the stairs. Only priests went to the top, where they made offerings and prayers high above the rooftops. The summit also had a practical use. From there, priests could watch the night sky across a flat plain with clear views in every direction. Babylonian astronomers kept detailed records of the moon, the planets and the stars. Some of their observations were probably made from temple roofs and towers like this one. For the Babylonians, studying the sky and serving the gods were closely connected. A building made for worship could therefore also serve as an observatory.

[long-pause]

{img:27-ziggurat-base-today-reeds-water}

Almost nothing remains of the great tower, but we know its measurements from a clay tablet. Today, the great tower has disappeared. Over the centuries, its bricks were taken away and reused in other buildings. All that remains is the base, a square outline in the ground near the ruins. It is often filled with water and reeds. Yet a good deal is known about how the tower once looked. A Babylonian scribe recorded its measurements on a clay tablet, and that tablet survived. A Greek writer also described its many levels and the temple at the top. Together, these sources allow scholars to reconstruct its general shape. The building itself is gone, but its record remains. This is a common pattern in Mesopotamian history. Great monuments of brick crumbled and were reused, while small clay tablets survived in the ground. A modest record, forgotten in the rubble, has outlasted a tower that once dominated the skyline of the greatest city on earth.

[long-pause]

{img:28-esagila-temple-courtyard-priests}

Beside the tower stood the temple of Marduk, whose name means "the house whose top is high." Right next to the ziggurat lay Esagila, the greatest temple in the city. It was not one building but a group of them, arranged around courtyards, with thick walls, tall gateways and many rooms for priests, treasures and gifts. In the holiest room of all lived the god himself. Marduk was a statue of precious wood covered in gold, dressed in rich robes sewn with gold and jewels, and seated on a golden throne before a golden table. A Greek writer later claimed that all this gold added up to an enormous weight, and the number is probably exaggerated. But the Babylonians themselves wrote that the sanctuary shone like the sun. People stood in the courtyard and looked towards the dim, golden doorway, with their hands raised to their faces in prayer. Through the day, the smell of incense drifted over the walls and into the streets.

[long-pause]

{img:29-priests-feeding-god-golden-table}

Each day, priests woke the god, washed him, dressed him and served him two meals. The god was looked after like a great king living in his palace. In the morning, priests woke him with songs and prayers. They washed the statue and dressed it in fresh clothes. Twice a day, they served him a meal. There were loaves and cakes, meat and fish, fruit and dates, with beer and wine, all set out on the golden table with music and incense. When the god had finished, the food was shared among the priests and temple workers, so nothing went to waste. In the evening, the priests put the god to bed, closed the doors of his room and left a few lamps burning. The temple grew quiet, and the guards kept watch. It might sound strange to us. But to the Babylonians, it was a simple act of care. If the god was happy and well fed in his house, the city beyond would be safe.

[long-pause]

{img:30-creation-poem-recited-priests}

Every spring, the priests recited a poem about how Marduk made the world out of chaos. The Babylonians had a great poem about the beginning of everything. At first, it said, there was nothing but water and darkness. A mighty goddess of the salt sea ruled over this chaos. Then the young god Marduk rose up and defeated her. From her body, he made the sky and the earth. He set the stars and the moon in their places, to mark the months and the seasons. Then the other gods built Babylon for him, as his home. Marduk had once been only a small local god of a single city. As Babylon grew great, so did he, until he became the king of all the gods. The poem was recited every year at the New Year festival, and it explained why the city was so important. In the Babylonian way of thinking, Babylon was not just a place on a map. It was where the world had started.

[long-pause]

{img:31-temple-workshops-scribes-bakers}

The great temple was also a bank, a farm, a bakery and a school, with hundreds of workers. A visitor expecting only prayer would have been surprised. Esagila employed hundreds of people. There were priests and singers, musicians and star watchers, but also cooks, bakers, brewers, weavers, goldsmiths, gardeners and herdsmen. The temple owned fields, palm groves and flocks all around the city, and collected a share of their produce. It lent out grain and silver. Its scribes kept careful records of every sack and every sheep on baked clay tablets. In a society without banks or government offices as we know them, the temple filled many roles at once. It was a religious centre, a storehouse, an employer and a source of help in hard times. People brought gifts to the god and asked for healing, for children, for justice and for good luck. And then, on the way out, they might stop at the temple workshop and buy a loaf of bread for the walk home.

[long-pause]

{img:32-stone-bridge-boat-shaped-piers}

A great bridge on boat-shaped pillars joined the two halves of the city. The Euphrates flowed straight through Babylon and divided it in two. On the east bank lay the old city, with the great temples, the palace and the tower. On the west bank was the newer part, with its own streets, houses and gardens. To join them, the kings built one of the first great bridges in the ancient world. It was around a hundred and twenty metres long and rested on thick pillars of brick and stone. The pillars were shaped like boats, pointed at the front, so the current would slide around them without wearing them down. The road across was made of wooden beams, and at night the beams could be taken away. In that way, nobody could cross in the dark, and the two halves of the city slept behind their own walls. In the morning, the planks went back, and the whole city began to flow across again.

[long-pause]

{img:33-round-reed-boats-donkeys-cargo}

Some river boats were round, like enormous baskets coated in tar. There were boats of every kind on the Euphrates. Some were long and narrow, with sails and oars. Some were rafts of logs floating on inflated goatskins, bringing stone, timber and wine down from the northern mountains. And some were round like a shield, made from woven reeds, covered in leather, and coated with a sticky black tar that seeped from the ground nearby. Herodotus, the Greek traveller, was fascinated by them. He wrote that a boat might carry a heavy load of wine jars and a donkey or two. When it reached Babylon, it was unloaded and taken apart. The leather was strapped on the donkeys' backs, and the donkeys walked home up the river by land, because the current was too strong to row against. Then, at home, someone built another round boat. It was a simple, thrifty and rather charming way to move cargo.

[long-pause]

{img:34-fishermen-dawn-nets-herons}

At dawn, the river was full of fishermen, herons and kingfishers. Long before the markets opened, small boats slipped out onto the Euphrates. Fishermen stood in the bows, casting their nets in wide circles that spread across the water like open hands. They brought back fish for the morning stalls, to be grilled, dried or salted. The banks were full of other life too. Herons stood very still in the shallows. Ducks, egrets and pelicans floated among the reeds, and kingfishers flashed blue and orange between the stems. In the middle of a huge city, the river still felt like countryside, especially in the first grey light, when the mist lay on the water and the only sound was a paddle dipping. Many a Babylonian began the day by walking down to the quay and breathing the river air. It was not a bad way to wake up, even in the biggest city in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:35-spring-flood-platforms-houses}

Every spring, melting mountain snow made the river rise, and the city prepared for a flood. The Euphrates was a generous river, but not always a gentle one. Far to the north, in spring, the snow melted in the mountains and the river swelled. Sometimes it spilled over its banks, across the fields and towards the city. Everyone watched the water creep higher, day by day. The Babylonians were ready for it. They built their houses on raised platforms. They set their temples on great terraces. They kept the dams and canals in good repair, and they prayed to the gods to hold the floods back. In the end, a flood was a normal part of life. It brought trouble, but it also brought fresh silt, which made the fields rich again. The Babylonian farmer lived somewhere between gratitude and worry, as farmers by rivers have always done. He knew that the thing which fed him could, one wet spring, come to his door.

[long-pause]

{img:36-quay-evening-families-water}

In the evening, half of Babylon came down to the water to cool off. When the sun sank behind the palms on the west bank, the river turned red and gold. The fishing boats came home, and the traders tied up their barges for the night. People left their hot houses and walked down to the brick steps that led to the water. They filled their jars, washed their faces and sat with their feet in the cool current. Children splashed in the shallows. Old men talked about the price of barley, and women compared the day's news. A little breeze came off the river, carrying the smell of reeds and wet clay. For a few minutes before dark, the whole city seemed to breathe out. The great tower, the temples and the walls were all quiet silhouettes, and the water slid past them, unhurried. It had been doing so for thousands of years, and it showed no sign of stopping.

[long-pause]

{img:37-plain-street-low-wooden-door}

From the street, a Babylonian house looked like a plain wall with a door. The streets of Babylon were narrow, winding and unpaved. They were dusty in summer and muddy after winter rain. The houses along them showed almost nothing to passers-by. There was a high wall of mud brick, with no windows, and a single low wooden door. It looked plain, even unfriendly. Yet that was the whole idea. Thick blank walls kept out the heat, the dust and the noise of the street. They also gave each family privacy. Behind the door, the house was quite different. A small entrance room led to an open courtyard, bright with sunlight and full of life. Excavations of Babylonian houses show this plan again and again. Many towns of the warm Middle East still use the same idea today, with a quiet heart hidden from the street. In Babylon, the outer wall was plain, while the life of the family went on inside.

[long-pause]

{img:38-courtyard-house-family-morning}

The heart of the house was an open courtyard where the family cooked, worked and played. The courtyard was paved with bricks and open to the sky. All the rooms of the house stood around it, and the door of each one opened onto it. Here the family cooked in the morning, wove in the shade at midday, and talked at night. Children played here, and so did the dog. On the south side, there was often a long room that faced north, so it stayed cool even in the worst heat of the day. Guests were welcomed there. There were bedrooms, storerooms for grain, oil and dates, and a kitchen with a clay oven. Perhaps there was a small washing room with a drain. The walls were thick, and the rooms were cool and dark, lit only by the light from the doorways. Floors of beaten earth or brick were covered in reed mats and woollen rugs. It was a simple house, but it was cleverly made for its climate.

[long-pause]

{img:39-house-furniture-lamps-chests}

There was little furniture, but every house had lamps, chests and plenty of baskets. A Babylonian home contained little furniture. People sat on mats, stools and cushions, and ate from low tables. Beds were low wooden frames with mattresses and blankets. Clothes and valuables were kept in wooden chests. Clay jars held water, oil, grain and beer. Baskets stood in every corner, filled with dates, wool, bread and tools. At night, light came from small clay lamps that burned sesame oil. Their flames gave a soft, flickering glow and threw gentle shadows on the walls. There was no glass in the windows and no chimney. Yet these simple objects were enough for a comfortable household. Many of them have been found in excavations, from lamps to storage jars. They show that ordinary life in Babylon depended on a few practical things, used every day for many years. A single small flame was enough for a quiet meal and an evening of conversation.

[long-pause]

{img:40-flat-roof-family-sleeping-stars}

In summer, the whole family climbed a ladder and slept on the roof under the stars. The flat roof served as another room of the house. It was made of palm trunks and reed mats, covered with thick, smooth mud, and reached by a wooden ladder. During the day, people dried dates, grain and washing there. In the cool of the evening, they sat on the roof and talked with neighbours on nearby roofs. In the hot summer, when the rooms below were stifling, whole families slept there on mats under the open sky. On summer nights, the rooftops of Babylon were full of sleeping families. Dogs barked in the lanes. Frogs croaked in the canals. Watchmen called the hours from the walls. The custom of sleeping on the roof continued in the region for thousands of years, because it made good sense in a hot climate. A breeze, a mat and the open sky were often more comfortable than any room.

[long-pause]

{img:41-baking-flatbread-clay-oven-dawn}

Breakfast began with flat loaves slapped onto the hot wall of a clay oven. In a typical Babylonian family, the day began at dawn, when light slid into the courtyard and the birds woke in the palm tree next door. A servant girl lit the fire in the clay oven in the corner. Then the mother made bread. She shaped flat round loaves and slapped them against the hot inside wall of the oven, where they baked in a few minutes. She peeled them off, soft and steaming and smelling wonderful. The family ate breakfast sitting on mats in the courtyard. There was bread and dates, a little cheese, some onions, and a drink of thick, sweet barley beer. Everyone had some, even the children. It was a simple meal, but it set the day in motion. The same method of baking bread is still used in many parts of the region today, and the smell is just as good.

[long-pause]

{img:42-scribe-father-walking-to-temple}

Many Babylonian families earned their living as scribes, writing contracts and accounts for the temples. A typical comfortable household in Babylon might be headed by a scribe. Such a man wrote contracts and accounts on clay tablets for a temple or a wealthy family. Each morning he walked to work through narrow lanes that were already busy. Donkeys plodded past with sacks. Water carriers hurried by with jars, and sellers called out their wares. He carried a sharp reed pen and a small basket of tablets. Sons often learned the same trade from their fathers or from a teacher. Wives ran the household and wove cloth, and some managed small businesses of their own. Surviving contracts show women lending silver, buying property and acting as witnesses. A household might also include grandparents, children and a servant. Such families were not rich, but they were respected and well informed. Their records are among the richest sources for daily life in the city.

[long-pause]

{img:43-women-spinning-wool-afternoon-shade}

In the heat of the afternoon, everyone rested, while the women spun and talked in the shade. By midday, the sun beat down on the courtyard like a hammer, and the streets emptied. Work slowed or stopped. People retreated into the cool dark rooms to doze. In the shaded corner of the courtyard, the mother sat at her loom or spun wool on a spindle, as she had done for most of her life. Her sister might come to visit from the next street, and the two would talk for hours while their hands kept moving. Children played quietly with clay toys, little wheeled carts on strings, spinning tops and balls. The only sounds were the creak of the loom, the hum of flies, and a distant donkey. It was a deeply peaceful hour, and it was repeated on almost every afternoon of the year. When the heat finally broke, life began again, a little slower than before.

[long-pause]

{img:44-women-signing-contracts-witnesses}

Babylonian women could own property, sign contracts and win cases in court. Most women in Babylon spent their lives as wives and mothers, running the house and raising children. But they had more rights than women in many other ancient lands. A woman could own houses, fields and silver. She could buy and sell, lend and borrow, make contracts and act as a witness. If she had a quarrel, she could go to court. When she married, her family gave her a dowry of furniture, clothes, jewellery and sometimes land, and it stayed hers all her life. It was written carefully in the marriage contract on clay. If her husband sent her away without good reason, he had to give it all back. Some contracts even promised that if the husband took a second wife, he would pay the first one a large sum of silver. Of course, not everything was equal. But these women had a real voice, and clay tablets still preserve it.

[long-pause]

{img:45-businesswomen-weavers-taverns}

Some women ran taverns, weaving workshops and even family businesses. Women worked outside the home as well. Many wove in the large workshops of the temples and the palace. Others brewed beer and ran taverns, sold goods in the streets, or worked as nurses and midwives. Some became writers, singers and musicians, and some served as priestesses. In the records of Babylon, we meet women who bought and sold houses, lent silver and grain, rented out fields, and managed the family property when their husbands were away. In the papers of one wealthy family, the wives and mothers appear again and again, signing and witnessing. The Babylonians also respected women as keepers of the house and of family memory. They had a saying that a house without a wife is like a field without a plough. At the end of the day, mothers, daughters and grandmothers sat together on the rooftops, combing each other's hair and watching the sun go down.

[long-pause]

{img:46-grandmother-telling-stories-gilgamesh}

Babylonian children grew up with stories about Gilgamesh and the great flood. The Epic of Gilgamesh was already very old in Nebuchadnezzar's time. It told of a great king of an ancient city and his wild friend Enkidu. It described their adventures, their long journey and their grief. One part of the poem tells of a great flood. The gods sent water over the whole earth, but one wise man was warned in time. He built a great boat and saved his family and the animals. Scholars have long noticed how closely this story resembles the later biblical flood. Copies of the epic were written on clay tablets and studied by scribes. Yet stories like these lived above all in spoken form. They were told and retold in homes, often in the evening. Such stories had been passed down for more than a thousand years. They show how deeply the people of Mesopotamia valued memory, storytelling and the lessons of the distant past.

[long-pause]

{img:47-date-palm-grove-harvest}

To the Babylonians, the date palm was the tree of life. Around the city, along every river and canal, stood groves of date palms. They were tall and slender, with crowns of long feathery leaves that whispered in the wind. In late summer, heavy bunches of dates hung beneath them, turning orange, red and brown. The Babylonians used nearly every part of the tree. The fruit was sweet and filling, and it could be eaten fresh, dried for months, or pressed into a syrup like honey. The leaves were woven into baskets, mats and roofs. The fibres became rope. The trunks became beams and bridges, and even the stones were burned for fuel or fed to animals. Nothing went to waste. Underneath the palms, in their shade, farmers grew pomegranates, figs, grapes, onions and lettuce, so the groves were like cool green gardens in the burning summer. They were one of the loveliest parts of the country.

[long-pause]

{img:48-palm-pollination-climber-flowers}

Date palms needed help from farmers, who climbed the trees to pollinate them by hand. A date palm is either male or female, and only the female trees bear fruit. In nature, the wind carries pollen from one tree to another, but it is not always reliable. So every spring, the farmers climbed the tall trunks, wrapped in a rope that circled both the man and the tree. It was a long way up, and one slip would be dangerous. At the top, a farmer tied a small bunch of flowers from a male tree among the flowers of a female tree. With that done, the dates would grow. Babylonians understood this perfectly, and they treated the work with great care. Farmers in Iraq still pollinate their palms by hand, almost exactly as their ancestors did. It is the sort of knowledge that passes quietly from grandfather to grandson, through thousands of springs, without ever needing to be written down at all.

[long-pause]

{img:49-shaduf-lifting-water-field}

A balanced pole with a bucket on one end and a lump of clay on the other lifted water into the fields. Getting water from the canal up into the field was hard work, so farmers invented a clever tool. It was a long pole balanced on an upright post. A bucket hung from one end and a heavy lump of clay from the other. The farmer pulled the bucket down into the canal, and the weight of the clay helped him lift it again. Then he swung the pole around and emptied the bucket into a channel that carried the water to his plants. With a tool like this, a single man could lift a surprising amount of water in a day. It was cheap, simple and strong, and it needed no animals. Versions of it are still seen along rivers in the region today. Sometimes the best inventions are the ones nobody ever needs to improve, and this was one of them.

[long-pause]

{img:50-seed-plough-oxen-barley-field}

Mesopotamian farmers invented a plough with a built-in funnel that planted seeds as it moved. Barley was the most important crop in Babylon. It gave the people bread and it gave them beer, and it was also used to pay wages. To plant it, farmers used a wooden plough pulled by oxen. On top of the plough was a funnel. The farmer dropped seeds into it, and they fell straight into the furrow as the plough moved forward. This saved a great deal of time, and wasted far fewer seeds than scattering them by hand. It was a clever invention of this land, and it was used for centuries. In spring, the barley ripened, and the harvest came in April and May. Farmers cut it with curved blades, donkeys carried it to threshing floors, and oxen walked round and round on it to shake the grain loose. Then the wind blew away the husks, and the golden grain went into sacks.

[long-pause]

{img:51-beer-drinkers-straws-large-jar}

Everybody in Babylon drank beer, and they drank it through straws. Babylonian beer was not much like the beer we know. It was made from barley, and it was thick, cloudy and sweet, and not very strong. It was more like a liquid food than a drink. Men, women and children had it every day with their meals. To make it, brewers soaked barley until it sprouted, dried it, ground it and baked it into loaves. They crumbled the loaves into water in big jars, added dates for sweetness, and left the mixture to bubble for a few days. The result had bits of grain floating on top, so people drank through long reed straws, and some straws had little filters at the bottom. Old pictures from the region show friends sitting around one big jar, all sipping through their straws and chatting. It was a friendly and rather sociable way to have a drink.

[long-pause]

{img:52-tavern-keeper-woman-serving}

Taverns were often run by women, and the old laws had rules for them. In the evening, people gathered in taverns to drink, talk and listen to music. Many taverns were run by women who brewed their own beer. More than a thousand years before Nebuchadnezzar, the laws of Hammurabi already contained rules for tavern keepers. They had to sell fair amounts at fair prices. If they cheated customers, they could be punished. There was also wine in Babylon, though it was expensive. It came down the river on rafts from the northern mountains. It was drunk by the wealthy and offered to the gods. Beer was far more common, and a goddess even watched over it. That alone shows how seriously the Babylonians took it. A good tavern was a place where friends met and news was exchanged. In many ways, it served the same purpose as the local inn of later centuries. The jars were simply larger, and the lamps were smaller.

[long-pause]

{img:53-clay-moulds-cakes-dates-honey}

The oldest cookbook in the world was written on clay in this land. The Babylonians loved their food, and they had dozens of kinds of bread and cake. They made flat breads baked on oven walls, round loaves, and sweet cakes of honey, dates, sesame and butter. Some were baked in clay moulds shaped like fish, animals or stars. Remarkably, the oldest known collection of recipes in the world comes from this land. A handful of clay tablets, written more than a thousand years before Nebuchadnezzar, describe stews of lamb, pigeon and duck, cooked with onions, leeks, garlic and herbs. In our own time, some cooks have followed them to see how the food tasted. In the evening, the smell of baking bread, onions and stew drifted over the rooftops, together with wood smoke. Families gathered in courtyards and on roofs to eat, talk about the day, and enjoy a meal that was, in many ways, not so far from our own.

[long-pause]

{img:54-market-street-merchants-donkeys}

The land had barley and dates and clay, but almost no stone, no metal and no good wood. Babylon was rich, but it lacked many things. The country had plenty of barley, dates, wool, reeds and clay. But it had almost no stone, almost no useful timber except palm, and no metal at all. So nearly everything else had to come from far away, and the traders of Babylon traded with the whole known world. Cedar for the temples came down the rivers on rafts from the western mountains. Copper, tin, iron, silver and gold arrived from the northern and eastern hills. A deep blue stone flecked with gold, the Babylonians' favourite, came from lands far to the east. Incense and spices came from Egypt and Arabia, and ivory, cotton and gems from India by sea. In return, Babylon sent out barley, dates, sesame oil and, above all, beautiful dyed woollen cloth, which was famous everywhere.

[long-pause]

{img:55-camel-caravan-arriving-at-gate}

Caravans crossed the desert from one water hole to the next, and missing one could be fatal. Goods travelled overland in long lines of donkeys, and increasingly on camels, which were becoming more common. Caravans crossed the dry lands from one water hole to the next. Traders knew every well along the route. Missing one could put the whole caravan in serious danger. Other merchants moved goods by boat along the rivers, down to the sea and into the wider ocean. Babylon did not have a single great market square. Trade happened everywhere. It took place at the city gates and on the quays where boats unloaded. It happened in the streets, in small shops and in the courtyards of merchant families. Temples were also major traders. When a caravan arrived, it brought more than goods. It brought news, rumours and strangers from distant lands. Long-distance trade linked Babylon to Egypt, Arabia, Persia and beyond. It made the city a meeting point for much of the ancient world.

[long-pause]

{img:56-silver-scales-weights-merchant}

There were no coins in Babylon. People paid by weighing out silver. Coins had only just been invented, far to the west, and they had not yet reached Babylon. So people paid in silver, weighed on scales, in little pieces, bars and rings. The usual unit was the shekel, which was simply a weight. Barley served as money too, and wages were often paid in grain or oil. Everything had its price in shekels and barley, from a house to an ox to a jar of sesame oil. Traders carried small scales and sets of stone weights, often shaped like ducks or little domes. To a modern eye, it would look very slow. Yet it worked, and it worked for centuries. And since silver could be cut, melted and weighed, people had to trust one another, or at least trust a scribe. Which brings us to the thing that held the whole system together.

[long-pause]

{img:57-contracts-clay-seals-witnesses}

Every sale, loan, marriage and adoption was written on a clay tablet and sealed. In Babylon, important agreements required a scribe. When people bought a house, lent silver, rented a boat, married or adopted a child, the agreement was written down. The scribe recorded the names of those involved, the witnesses and the date. Then everyone pressed their seal into the clay. Those without a seal sometimes pressed a fingernail instead. Thousands of these contracts survive. They record the price of a house, an ox or a field. They show the rent of a boat and the wages of a worker. One famous family of bankers and traders, known as the Egibi family, appears in more than a thousand tablets across five generations. Their business deals, marriages and disputes can be traced for over a century. Very few ancient societies left such detailed records of ordinary life. In Babylon, clay preserved not only the deeds of kings, but also the daily affairs of ordinary families.

[long-pause]

{img:58-hammurabi-stele-black-stone}

A black stone pillar carved with nearly three hundred laws stood in Babylon's temple. More than a thousand years before Nebuchadnezzar, King Hammurabi had his laws carved on a tall black stone, more than two metres high. At the top, the king stood before the sun god, the god of justice. Below, in neat close lines, were nearly three hundred rules. They dealt with property, trade and wages, debts, marriage and divorce. Others concerned fields, canals, boats and animals, and the work of doctors, builders and tavern keepers. Some punishments were harsh. Yet many laws aimed at fairness and the protection of the weak. Hammurabi wrote that he wanted the strong not to oppress the weak, and widows and orphans to receive justice. By 600 BC, the laws were ancient, but scribes still copied them as a classic text. The stone itself was later carried away to another city as a prize of war. French archaeologists found it there in 1901.

[long-pause]

{img:59-judges-at-temple-gate-oath}

When people quarrelled, the judges sat at the temple gates and asked them to swear an oath. If two neighbours could not settle a dispute, they went to the judges. These sat at temple gates or in courts, listened to both sides and the witnesses, and examined the contracts on their tablets. Then they decided. Sometimes they asked a person to swear an oath before the gods. That was a very serious thing. People's hands shook when they did it, because nobody wanted to lie before a god. And everything was written down on clay, the case, the witnesses, the decision and the date. Thanks to that, we can still read about a quarrel over the edge of a field, or the return of a wedding gift, or the payment of a debt, and learn how a judge in Babylon decided. Most arguments never got that far. The elders of a street would sit down together in a courtyard in the evening, over a jar of beer, and talk until everyone agreed.

[long-pause]

{img:60-scribe-pressing-reed-wet-clay}

Babylonian writing was made of tiny wedges pressed into wet clay with a reed. A scribe began with a lump of smooth, damp clay. He shaped it into a tablet small enough to hold in one hand. It was flat on one side and gently rounded on the other. Holding it in his left hand, he pressed the cut end of a reed into the surface with his right. Each press left a small wedge-shaped mark. Signs were made from several wedges, long and short, upright and lying down. Today this script is called cuneiform, which simply means wedge-shaped. Skilled scribes worked quickly, turning the tablet to write on the back and even on the edges. The system was invented by the Sumerians more than three thousand years before Nebuchadnezzar. At first it used simple pictures of sheep, grain and jars of oil. Over the centuries, the pictures became wedges. The signs came to stand for sounds as well as things, and the script could record almost any language.

[long-pause]

{img:61-tablets-drying-in-sun-rows}

Clay tablets were cheap to make, and baked ones lasted almost forever. After writing, a tablet was left in the sun until it was hard. Important tablets were baked in an oven, which made them as tough as brick. Even letters had envelopes, made from a thin layer of clay wrapped around the tablet. The address was written on the outside, and the envelope had to be broken before the letter could be read. Clay was cheap and easy to find. Above all, it lasted. Paper and leather rot, and wood burns. Clay can survive for thousands of years in the ground. When a library burned in this part of the world, the fire often baked its tablets harder and preserved them. That is why hundreds of thousands of tablets have been found. They include letters, laws, lists, prayers, recipes, dictionaries, poems and star charts. A cheap writing material has outlived the empires that used it, and it carries their voices to the present day.

[long-pause]

{img:62-tablet-house-school-boys-benches}

Boys went to the "tablet house" at about seven years old and spent most of the day copying signs. The Babylonian school was called the tablet house. It was not a big building. Usually it was the home of a master scribe, who took a few pupils and taught them in a room or courtyard. Most pupils were boys from families of scribes, priests and the well off, though a few girls learned to write as well. They began at about seven or eight and went to school from sunrise to evening for many years. First they learned how to make a tablet, hold the reed and press simple wedges. Then came the signs, one by one, copied again and again from a model. The teacher wrote the model on one side of a tablet, and the pupil copied it on the other. After that came lists of words, trees, animals, stones, gods and cities. Last of all came sayings, letters, hymns and stories.

[long-pause]

{img:63-school-tablet-pupil-copy-closeup}

Some school tablets show a teacher's neat writing on one side and a pupil's mistakes on the other. When a pupil made a mistake, he smoothed the clay with his thumb and started again. Many practice tablets have nevertheless survived. On one side is the teacher's neat model. On the other is the pupil's copy, with wobbling lines, crowded signs and plenty of errors. Pupils also learned mathematics. They practised counting, multiplying and using fractions. They learned to measure a field, to work out how many bricks a wall needed, and to calculate the rations for workers. Teachers were strict, and some school texts describe beatings for poor work. Yet other texts show pride, affection and humour between teachers and pupils. These tablets are among the most human objects to survive from Mesopotamia. They show that the frustrations of learning were familiar thousands of years ago. Children then, as now, struggled, made mistakes and slowly improved with practice and patience.

[long-pause]

{img:64-schoolboy-story-father-dinner-teacher}

A famous school story tells of a boy who was punished all day, until his father invited the teacher to dinner. Babylonian pupils copied a funny old story for centuries. A boy goes to school and is punished for being late. Then he is punished for talking too much, for standing up, for writing badly, for just about everything. Sad and tired, he tells his father. The father invites the teacher to dinner. He gives him a fine meal, a new robe and a ring, and pours him drink after drink. And suddenly the teacher begins to praise the boy. He says that he is the best pupil in the school, and that he will become a famous scribe. The story is funny and very human, and it shows that people two and a half thousand years ago were fully capable of laughing at themselves and their teachers. It also shows that a good dinner has always been a useful way to smooth over a difficult situation.

[long-pause]

{img:65-aramaic-ink-leather-scroll-street}

A new, simple alphabet with only twenty-two letters was spreading across the country. Around 600 BC, a new way of writing was becoming common. It was a simple alphabet of twenty-two letters, written in ink on leather or on pieces of pottery. It was much easier to learn than cuneiform, which needed hundreds of signs and many years of study. The language that went with it, Aramaic, was spoken everywhere in the streets and the markets. Traders used it. Ordinary people used it. Soon even officials in the great empires would use it. Yet for the important things, the scribes of Babylon still pressed the old wedges into clay. They used it for temple records, laws, contracts and star charts, as their ancestors had done for thousands of years. Two ways of writing lived side by side, one new and fast, and one old and respected. The old one was written in stone, almost literally, and it outlived the other by many centuries.

[long-pause]

{img:66-clay-library-shelves-archive}

Babylon's archives held tablets on everything from medicine to maths, and many are still unread. The temples and palaces of Babylon kept their tablets in archives and libraries, on shelves and in baskets labelled with small clay tags. There were lists and dictionaries, prayers and hymns, medical texts, mathematics, omens, laws and stories. Hundreds of thousands of tablets have been found in the ruins of Mesopotamia, and many sit in museum storerooms, waiting for specialists to read them. Every year, a few more are translated, and every year we learn a little more about the people who wrote them. We find their business and their worries, their prayers and sometimes their jokes. It is gentle, patient work. A scholar bends over a small grey tablet under a lamp and reads a line that nobody has read for two and a half thousand years. For a moment, the writer and the reader are in the same room, and the long silence between them is broken.

[long-pause]

{img:67-base-sixty-wedge-numbers-tablet}

The sixty minutes in an hour can be traced back to the mathematics of ancient Mesopotamia. The Babylonians did not count in tens. They counted in sixties. Their number system used only two signs, an upright wedge for one and a sideways wedge for ten. With these, any number from one to fifty-nine could be written. At sixty, the count began again in the next column, much as ten is written today with a one in the next place. The reason for choosing sixty is not known for certain. Yet the number has a great advantage for calculation. It divides evenly by two, three, four, five, six, ten, twelve, fifteen, twenty and thirty. That makes fractions much easier. The system was passed on to Greek astronomers and from them to later scholars. As a result, hours still have sixty minutes, minutes have sixty seconds, and circles have three hundred and sixty degrees. A small part of Babylonian mathematics is therefore still used every day.

[long-pause]

{img:68-square-diagonal-tablet-numbers}

One small clay tablet shows a very exact value for the diagonal of a square, written about 3,700 years ago. Babylonian mathematics was far more advanced than is often assumed. More than a thousand years before Nebuchadnezzar, scribes were already solving difficult problems about areas, volumes and interest on loans. One famous tablet, roughly the size of a hand, shows a square with a line from corner to corner. Along that line, in wedges, is a value for the diagonal. It is remarkably accurate, better than any other known calculation for more than a thousand years. Another famous tablet contains a table of numbers that scholars have discussed for nearly a century. Many believe it shows knowledge of a special rule about right-angled triangles. That was long before the Greek Pythagoras gave his name to it. The Babylonians did not write long formal proofs. They wrote clear practical methods and passed them from teacher to pupil. Their mathematics was built for use, yet it reached a surprising level of precision.

[long-pause]

{img:69-surveyors-measuring-field-rope}

For the Babylonians, maths was practical, and they used it to measure fields and divide inheritances. Mathematics in Babylon was never just a puzzle. People used it every day. Surveyors measured fields with knotted ropes and calculated their areas. Families divided inheritances between brothers. Officials worked out the wages and rations of workers. Builders calculated how many bricks a wall would need, and how much earth had to be dug from a canal. Merchants worked out the interest on loans. Scribes learned these skills by solving practice problems at school. Many of these problems sound surprisingly familiar. A field of a certain length and width would yield a certain amount of barley. A canal of a certain depth would need a certain number of workers. Such exercises survive on hundreds of school tablets. They show that mathematics grew from the needs of farming, building and trade. In Mesopotamia, numbers helped to organise one of the most complex societies of the ancient world.

[long-pause]

{img:70-lunar-calendar-twelve-months-scribe}

The Babylonian year had twelve months of the moon, with an extra month added every few years. The Babylonians kept time by the moon. Each month began when the thin new crescent was first seen in the evening sky, and lasted twenty-nine or thirty days. Twelve such months make a year, but twelve moon months are about eleven days shorter than a year of the sun. If nothing was done, the seasons would slowly slide around the calendar, and the New Year festival would no longer fall in spring. So every few years, the astronomers added an extra month to put everything back in step. They divided the day and the night into twelve parts as well, and the sky into twelve parts. This careful counting, which began with practical needs like farming, planting and festivals, gradually became something more. It became a patient study of the heavens.

[long-pause]

{img:71-astronomers-roof-night-tablets}

On temple roofs, star watchers wrote a diary of the sky every night, for six hundred years. The night sky over Babylon was extraordinary. The air was dry and clear, and there were no city lights, only small lamps and fires. The stars shone bright and sharp from one edge of the flat land to the other. On top of the great tower and the temple roofs, priests and scribes watched them. Night after night, they recorded where the moon and the planets stood among the stars, when they rose and set, and when the moon went dark. They also noted the weather, the height of the river, the price of barley, dates and wool, and the events of the city. These diaries began around 650 BC and went on for about six hundred years, the longest scientific record kept in the ancient world. Hundreds of fragments still survive in the British Museum, and modern scientists use them to study events in the sky long ago.

[long-pause]

{img:72-night-sky-moon-planets-tower}

With all those notes, the astronomers learned to predict what the sky would do next. After generations of observing and recording, Babylonian astronomers found patterns in the sky. They discovered that eclipses of the moon came in cycles. They found that the planets moved in repeating rhythms. Using their arithmetic based on sixty, they calculated when a new moon would appear and where the planets would be in the coming months. Some scholars now believe that they even used a kind of geometry to track the planet Jupiter. That method would not be used again in Europe for well over a thousand years. Babylonian astronomy was not modern science. Its practitioners did not ask what the sky was made of. They wanted to know what it would do, and what it meant. Even so, their records were extraordinarily careful. Greek astronomers later used Babylonian observations and methods, and through them this knowledge shaped the study of the heavens for centuries.

[long-pause]

{img:73-zodiac-star-pictures-bull-lion}

Many of our star names and the twelve star signs began with the astronomers of Babylon. The Babylonians gave names and pictures to groups of stars. They divided the path that the sun, moon and planets seem to follow into twelve equal parts. Each part had its own group of stars, such as the Bull, the Lion, the Scorpion, the Twins and the Scales. Many of these pictures later became the twelve signs of the zodiac. For the Babylonians, the sky was a vast book written by the gods. A dark moon or a planet moving in an unusual way could be read as a message about the king and the country. Astronomers therefore sent regular reports to the palace. Some of these reports still survive. They show kings waiting anxiously for news of eclipses and omens. Yet most of the astronomers' work was patient observation, night after night. The names they gave to the stars have travelled through Greek and Latin and are still used today.

[long-pause]

{img:74-akitu-festival-boats-statues-river}

The New Year festival lasted twelve days, and statues of the gods came to Babylon by boat. Each spring, when the barley was ripening in the fields, Babylon celebrated the Akitu, the New Year. It was the greatest festival of the year and lasted twelve days. In the early days, priests held ceremonies in the temple at dawn, with prayers, hymns and the recital of the great poem about Marduk and the making of the world. Meanwhile, the statues of the gods of other cities were brought to Babylon by boat. They came along rivers and canals, on vessels decorated with gold, flowers and banners, with priests and musicians at their sides. The gods came to visit Marduk, and to take part in the festival, and the city filled with visitors. For a few days, the quays were crowded and the streets were lined with people who had walked from far villages to watch. Babylon at the New Year must have felt like the centre of the world.

[long-pause]

{img:75-king-before-marduk-statue-ceremony}

At the New Year, the most powerful king in the world had to humble himself before the god. On the fifth day, a strange and solemn ceremony took place. The king came into the temple and stood before the statue of Marduk. The high priest removed his crown and his royal robes and laid them before the god. Then he struck the king lightly on the cheek and pulled his ears. The king knelt, and said that he had done no wrong. He had not neglected the gods, he had not harmed Babylon, and he had not harmed its people. Then the priest gave him back his crown. Everyone watched the king's face. If tears came to his eyes, it was a good sign. Marduk was pleased, and the year would be a good one. It was a powerful reminder that even the greatest king was a servant of the god, and must rule fairly. Few rulers have ever done anything like it.

[long-pause]

{img:76-procession-through-ishtar-gate-statues}

On the great day, the statues of the gods paraded through the blue gate while the whole city watched. On the eighth and ninth days came the great procession. The king took the hand of Marduk's statue and led him out of the temple. The statues of all the other gods followed, on carts and carried by priests, in a long glittering line. They walked along the holy road between the blue walls and the golden lions, and passed through the Ishtar Gate. There were drums and harps and pipes, singing and clouds of incense. The whole city crowded along the road, on the walls and on the roofs. The procession went out to the river and by boat to a special temple in a garden outside the city. The gods stayed there for a few days with ceremonies and feasts. Then they returned along the same road, in the same splendour. At last the gods gathered to decide the fate of the land for the coming year.

[long-pause]

{img:77-festival-night-lamps-rooftops}

All through the festival, the city feasted, danced and lit lamps on the rooftops. While the priests and the king were busy in the temple, the rest of the city celebrated in its own way. There were feasts and music and dancing in the streets. People put on their best clothes, visited relatives and exchanged gifts. The temples handed out food and drink. At night, lamps burned on the roofs and on the walls, so that the whole city glowed in the dark. Music and singing went on late, long after the children had fallen asleep on their mats. When it was all over, the new year had begun. The world had been made new, the king had been confirmed, and the gods had decided the fate of the land. People went back to their homes and fields and workshops, the barley was harvested, and ordinary life resumed. But for a few days, everyone had felt a part of something far greater than themselves.

[long-pause]

{img:78-gods-of-babylon-shrine-statues}

The world of the Babylonians was full of gods, and one of them even looked after beer. The Babylonians believed in a great crowd of gods. There was an old god of the sky, a lord of the wind, and Ea, the clever and kindly god of the fresh waters under the earth, who loved people and helped them. There was the sun god, who saw everything as he crossed the sky and who stood for truth and justice. There was the moon god with his silver curve, and the storm god with his lightning. There was Ishtar, the goddess of love and of the morning and evening star, beautiful and fierce. There were goddesses of healing, writing and beer. And above them all, in Babylon, stood Marduk, with his son Nabu, the god of wisdom. Every city had its own god and its own temple. In Babylon there were dozens of temples and hundreds of little shrines at street corners, where people stopped to leave a few dates or a flower.

[long-pause]

{img:79-household-shrine-evening-prayer}

Every family had its own personal god, and prayed to it each evening. Besides the great gods, each family had a personal god who watched over them and spoke for them to the greater ones. In a corner of the house, they kept little clay figures of their god and of good spirits. In the evening, a mother or father lit a small lamp, poured a little water and set out a few dates and a piece of bread. Then they said a quiet prayer for the family, the children, the ancestors, the house and the night. The Babylonians believed that the dead lived on in a grey, quiet land beneath the earth, and depended on the living for food and water. So families remembered their ancestors by name and gave them small offerings every month. They wore little charms against evil spirits, and buried small figures of protectors under doorways. In a dangerous world, these small habits made the night feel safer.

[long-pause]

{img:80-hanging-gardens-imagined-terraces}

The most famous wonder of Babylon is one that may never have existed. Greek and Roman writers, centuries afterwards, told a lovely story. Nebuchadnezzar, they said, had a wife who came from the green mountains in the north-east. In flat, brown Babylon, she was homesick. So the king built her a mountain in the middle of his city, a great garden rising in steps like a green hill, planted with trees and flowers from all over the empire. Machines lifted river water to the top, and it flowed down through the terraces in streams. From below, the trees seemed to hang in the air, so people called them the Hanging Gardens, and the Greeks counted them among the Seven Wonders of the World. It is a beautiful story. But there is a mystery. The Babylonians never mention these gardens, in all their clay tablets. Nor does Nebuchadnezzar, who wrote so proudly of everything else he built.

[long-pause]

{img:81-assyrian-garden-hillside-channels}

Some scholars think the famous gardens were really in another city, further north. The Hanging Gardens of Babylon are famous, but their location remains uncertain. Archaeologists have excavated Babylon for more than a hundred years without finding them for certain. Some scholars think the gardens stood beside the river and were later washed away. Others point to a vaulted building in the palace, with arches and wells, which might have been part of them. A third view suggests that the story really belongs to another city further north. There, an Assyrian king built a famous garden about a century earlier. It was watered by canals and an impressive aqueduct. Later writers may have confused the two cities. Some historians even think the gardens were partly legend. Yet gardens certainly existed in Babylon. The kings and temples owned orchards, pools and palm groves. Whatever the truth, the story shows how much people in a dry land valued shade, water and green growing things.

[long-pause]

{img:82-judean-exiles-canal-willows-dusk}

By the rivers of Babylon, exiles from a distant kingdom sat down and remembered their homes. Over the years, many peoples came to Babylon, and some did not come willingly. Far to the west lay the small kingdom of Judah, with its capital Jerusalem. When it rebelled against Nebuchadnezzar, his armies captured the city. Many of its people, including the king and his court, were taken across the desert to Babylonia. The exiles were not kept in prisons. They were settled in towns and villages beside the canals, where they were given land to farm. They could build houses, plant gardens, marry, trade and keep their customs and faith. Clay tablets from the region record Judean names in ordinary contracts and ration lists. Yet many exiles longed for home. One of the most famous psalms in the Bible begins with this memory. By the rivers of Babylon, the exiles sat down and wept as they remembered their homeland. It is one of the oldest songs of exile ever written.

[long-pause]

{img:83-ration-tablet-storeroom-palace}

Clay tablets from the palace stores list the oil and barley given to a king in exile and his five sons. Remarkably, we can find these exiles in the clay records. When archaeologists dug in the palace storerooms, they found tablets listing the rations of oil and barley given to foreign guests at court. Among the names is the king of Judah, and his five sons. And in more recent times, other tablets have been read that come from a small town near Babylon, whose name means the town of Judah. The exiles lived there, farmed, paid their taxes and signed contracts. Their Hebrew names appear on the tablets for several generations, fathers and sons and grandsons, living quietly beside the canals. They were also gathering and copying the writings of their people, so that nothing would be lost. Much of what became the Hebrew Bible took shape in these years. Many of the exiles later went home, but many stayed, because Babylon had become their home too.

[long-pause]

{img:84-brickmakers-wooden-moulds-yard}

Babylon was built from millions of bricks, made by hand in big yards beside the river. In workshops, yards and streets across Babylon, craftsmen were busy. Most important of all were the brick makers. They worked in huge yards outside the city, where the river gave them plenty of clay and water. They mixed clay with water and chopped straw, trod it into thick mud with their feet, and pressed it into square wooden moulds. Then they turned the bricks out in long rows to dry in the sun. In the summer heat, that took a few days. If it rained, the work was ruined. Most bricks were only sun-dried, but the best were baked in enormous ovens until they came out hard and yellow and red. These were used for outer walls, floors and anywhere that had to stand up to water. Around the brick yards, the smoke of the ovens rose into the sky all day long, a sign that the city was still growing.

[long-pause]

{img:85-weavers-dyers-goldsmiths-workshops}

Babylonian craftsmen wove, dyed, carved and hammered, and passed their trade down through families. Beyond the brick yards, the streets rang with many other trades. There were weavers, men and women, working at looms. There were dyers who dipped wool in tubs of red, blue, purple and yellow, and tailors who sewed patterns into cloth. There were goldsmiths and silversmiths making jewellery and cups, blacksmiths making tools, carpenters, boat builders, potters, leather workers and basket makers. There were perfume makers mixing sweet oils, and carvers who made seals. A seal was a little stone cylinder, about the size of a finger, carved with tiny pictures. Rolled across wet clay, it left a small picture, like a signature, and every important person wore one on a string around the neck. Many craftsmen worked for the temples or the palace, and were paid in barley, wool, oil and silver. Others had their own shops, and often a trade stayed in the same family for generations, from father to son and mother to daughter.

[long-pause]

{img:86-cyrus-entering-babylon-peaceful-crowd}

About sixty years later, a Persian king entered Babylon almost without a fight. Nebuchadnezzar ruled for forty-three years and died as an old man. After him, Babylon had several kings in quick succession. The last was Nabonidus, an unusual ruler devoted to the moon god. He spent about ten years in the Arabian desert, leaving his son to govern at home. Meanwhile, a new power was rising in the east. In 539 BC, Cyrus the Great, king of the Persians, arrived with his army. Babylon fell almost without a battle. Cyrus presented himself as a respectful ruler. He honoured Marduk, protected the temples and allowed daily life to continue. On a clay cylinder now in the British Museum, he tells his own version of events. He claims that Marduk chose him to be king of Babylon. He also says he allowed displaced peoples to return home with their gods. Like all royal inscriptions, it was propaganda. Yet it marked the end of Babylon as an independent kingdom.

[long-pause]

{img:87-alexander-babylon-palace-evening}

Alexander the Great planned to make Babylon his capital, but he died there at thirty-two. Under the Persians, Babylon stayed rich and important for about two hundred years. Then Alexander the Great arrived and took it from them. He admired the city and planned to make it the capital of his great empire, stretching from Greece to India. But in the old palace of Nebuchadnezzar, he fell ill with a fever, and he died in Babylon at the age of thirty-two. After Alexander, his generals built new cities, and slowly people drifted away from the old one. The temples still stood, and the astronomers still watched the sky and wrote their diaries on clay for another two hundred years. The last cuneiform tablet that we know of was written around seventy-five years after Christ. After that, the ancient writing was forgotten, and for almost two thousand years, nobody could read it at all.

[long-pause]

{img:88-ruined-mounds-desert-palms}

Over the centuries, Babylon faded into a field of mounds, but its name never died. Slowly, the river changed its course. The canals filled with mud. The great buildings crumbled, and people took the bricks away to build new villages. The greatest city in the world became a field of mounds and broken brick on a flat desert plain. Now and then, travellers came and looked at it and wondered whether this could really be the Babylon of the Bible and the old stories. Local people dug in the ruins for bricks, and found the stamped ones with the king's name, which they built into houses, walls and dams. Yet Babylon was never forgotten. Its name lived on in the Bible, in Greek and Roman writing, in songs, poems and paintings, for two thousand years. A name of wonder and splendour, waiting patiently under the earth to be found again.

[long-pause]

{img:89-koldewey-excavation-railway-workers}

In 1899, a German archaeologist arrived in Babylon and spent almost eighteen years digging. His name was Robert Koldewey. He was trained as a builder, and he was a careful, patient man with a big moustache and a love of old buildings. He came to Babylon in 1899 and stayed for nearly eighteen years. With hundreds of local workers, he dug layer by layer through the mounds, with a little railway to carry away the earth. He uncovered the great walls, the gates, the palaces and the temples, and mapped and photographed them. One day, in the north of the city, his workers began to find pieces of glazed brick, blue and yellow and white, thousands of them, broken and scattered. They kept digging. Deeper down they found the lower part of a gate still standing, with bulls and dragons in rows along its walls. It had been buried for more than two thousand years. It was the Ishtar Gate.

[long-pause]

{img:90-scholar-reading-tablet-magnifier}

Scholars are still reading Babylon's tablets today, and still learning new things. Since Koldewey, archaeologists from many countries, and from Iraq itself, have dug in Babylon and studied what they found. The clay tablets have been read and translated, so that we now know the names of kings and scribes, merchants and star watchers. We know about the ordinary people of Babylon, their business, their prayers and their lives. The sky diaries, the maths tablets, the letters and the stories are still being studied in universities and museums around the world. Every year, new tablets are read, and new things are learned. This is, in a way, the final surprise about Babylon. The great tower is gone, and the king is gone, but the clay remembered. A scribe pressing a reed into a lump of mud, so long ago, has somehow managed to speak to us across more than twenty-five centuries.

[long-pause]

{img:91-babylon-ruins-today-palms-euphrates}

Babylon is now a World Heritage Site, and the river still flows past it slowly. Today, Babylon lies in Iraq, about eighty-five kilometres south of Baghdad, beside the Euphrates. It has been recognised as a World Heritage Site, protected as one of the most important places in the history of the world. Visitors can walk through the ruins along old streets between walls of mud brick. Some are original and some have been rebuilt in modern times. They can see the lower part of the Ishtar Gate, where the moulded bulls and dragons are still in place, without their glaze. They can walk along part of the holy road and see a great stone lion carved from black rock, which has stood among the ruins for many centuries. Around the site, there are palm groves, fields, canals and villages. And the river flows past slowly, as it did in the days of Nebuchadnezzar, though over the centuries it has moved a little to the west.

[long-pause]

{img:92-babylon-legacy-clock-stars-museum}

Babylon lives on in our hours, our minutes and the names of our stars. In museums around the world, Babylon still lives. In Berlin, the Ishtar Gate rises in blue and gold under the roof. In Baghdad, London, Paris and New York, the lions of the holy road are shown and admired, and so are the tablets of the scribes and the bricks of Nebuchadnezzar. But Babylon lives on in less obvious places too. It lives in our sixty minutes and our three hundred and sixty degrees. It lives in the names of the stars and the signs of the zodiac. It lives in the stories of the Bible, in our idea of a city that reaches the sky, and in the very habit of writing things down so that they last. The city was swept away, but it left a great deal behind. It is a quiet thought to carry into sleep, that so much of us began beside a slow brown river.

We have walked through the gates of Babylon, past its blue lions and its great tower, into its houses, schools and temples. We have seen how a city of mud brick became the wonder of its age.
