Tonight we are going more than two and a half thousand years back, to the great city of Babylon, around the year 600 BC.

{img:ishtar-gate-blue-glazed-bricks-golden-lions-dragons-morning-light}

The waters of Babylon. And yet, for a very long time, it was only a name, and a mound of earth, and broken bricks, in the desert.

In the year 600 BC, it is at the height of its glory. {img:mesopotamia-plain-two-rivers-irrigation-canals-palms-fields-aerial}
Babylon lies in Mesopotamia, which means, in Greek, the land between the rivers.

[pause]

The two rivers are the Tigris, and the Euphrates. They rise in the mountains of Turkey, far to the north, and flow south, for more than a thousand kilometres, across the plains, coming close together, near Babylon, and then flowing on, to the Persian Gulf. Today, most of this land is in the country of Iraq.

[long-pause]

It is a flat land, of rich, brown soil, brought down by the rivers, over thousands of years, and laid down, in their floods. But it hardly ever rains. The summers are long, and very hot, and dry. And so, without the rivers, nothing would grow.

[pause]

But with the rivers, everything grows. For thousands of years, the people of Mesopotamia have dug canals, to carry the river water out, across the land, to the fields, and the gardens, and the palm groves. And so, along the rivers, and the canals, the land is green, and rich, with fields of barley, and wheat, and sesame, and gardens of vegetables, and great forests of date palms.

[pause]

{img:ancient-sumerian-city-ruins-ziggurat-ur-reed-marsh-boats}
This is one of the places where civilisation began.

[long-pause]

More than five thousand years ago, here, in the south of Mesopotamia, the Sumerians built the first cities in the world: Ur, and Uruk, and Eridu, and Lagash, with their temples, and their walls, and their kings. Here, they invented writing, the first writing in the world, pressed into clay, with a reed. Here, they invented the wheel, and the plough, and the sailboat, and mathematics, and law, and the first written stories, and poems, like the great Epic of Gilgamesh.

[pause]

By the year 600 BC, all of this is already ancient history. The Sumerians are long gone. Their cities are old, and some are ruined. But the Babylonians remember them, and honour them, and copy their writings, and their prayers, and still use their ancient language, in the temples, as we might use Latin.

[pause]

{img:reed-marshes-southern-mesopotamia-reed-houses-canoe-water-buffalo}
In the south, near the sea, there are great marshes, of reeds, and water, where the people live in houses made of reeds, and travel in canoes, and fish, and keep water buffalo, as they have for thousands of years.

[long-pause]

And in the middle of this ancient land, on the river Euphrates, there is Babylon: a city that has been here for more than fifteen hundred years. More than a thousand years before, it was the capital of the great king Hammurabi, who made one of the first great collections of laws, in the world. Since then, it has been conquered, and destroyed, and rebuilt, again, and again.

[pause]

And now, in the year 600 BC, a new dynasty of kings has made it great once more, greater than it has ever been.

[long-pause]

{img:traveller-boat-down-euphrates-palms-villages-towers-distance-morning}
Let us imagine that we are travellers, coming to Babylon, for the first time, in the year 600 BC.

[pause]

We have come from the north, down the river Euphrates, on a boat, carrying wine, and timber, from the mountains, for many days. We have slept on the deck, under the stars, and watched the land go by: the steep banks, and the villages, of mud brick, and the fields, and the palm groves, and the herds of sheep, and goats, coming down to drink, at the river's edge, and the boys, fishing, from the banks, who wave, as we pass.

[long-pause]

As we come further south, the land becomes flatter, and greener, and the canals more numerous, branching off from the river, in every direction, and the villages, closer together, and larger, and the river busier, with boats, and rafts, and the round quffas, spinning slowly, in the current.

[pause]

And one morning, early, the boatman points ahead, down the river. And there, on the horizon, rising out of the morning haze, above the palm trees, we see it: a great stepped tower, standing up, against the sky, its top shining, blue, in the first sunlight. The ziggurat of Babylon.

[pause]

{img:approaching-babylon-river-view-walls-towers-ziggurat-haze-boats}
As we come closer, we see the walls: long, and high, and brown, stretching away, on both sides of the river, with towers, all along them, hundreds of them, like the teeth of a great comb, and the smoke rising, from the city, behind them, and the tops of the temples, and the palms, in the gardens.

[long-pause]

The boat comes in, to the quay, outside the walls, where dozens of other boats are tied up, and unloading, and the porters are carrying the jars, and the bales, and the sacks, on their backs, up the steps, and the scribes are counting, and writing, on their tablets, and the officials are collecting the tolls, and everyone is shouting, in a dozen languages.

[pause]

We climb up, onto the quay, with our bags, a little unsteady, after so many days on the water, and walk, with the crowds, toward the nearest gate, in the great wall. We pass the guards, at the gate, who glance at us, and wave us through. And we walk, through the long, dark, cool passage, under the gate, and out, into the city.

[pause]

{img:traveller-first-view-babylon-street-crowds-temples-amazed-sunlight}
And there is Babylon, all around us: the noise, and the crowds, and the smells, of bread, and smoke, and spices, and donkeys, and the narrow streets, between the high, blank walls, of the houses, and the temples, rising, above the rooftops, and, at the end of a long street, the great tower, standing, enormous, against the blue sky, so much bigger, now that we are close, than we had ever imagined.

[long-pause]

We stand still, for a moment, in the middle of the street, with the people pushing past us, and stare, like every traveller who has ever come to Babylon, and then, we go on, to find a place to stay, and to begin to explore the greatest city in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:king-nebuchadnezzar-babylonian-robes-tall-hat-beard-palace-throne}
The king of Babylon, in the year 600 BC, is Nebuchadnezzar, the second of that name.

[pause]

His name, in Babylonian, is Nabu-kudurri-usur, which means: O Nabu, protect my heir. Nabu is the god of writing, and wisdom, the son of Marduk, the great god of Babylon.

[long-pause]

He became king in the year 605 BC, when his father, Nabopolassar, died. His father had made Babylon free, and strong, again, after a long time under the rule of the Assyrians, from the north, whose great empire had fallen, a few years before. And Nebuchadnezzar, as a young prince, had led his father's armies, and won great victories.

[pause]

He will rule for forty-three years, until 562 BC, and he will become the most famous king of Babylon. In the Bible, he appears in the books of Kings, and Jeremiah, and Daniel, as a great, and proud, and powerful king.

[pause]

{img:babylonian-king-inspecting-building-works-bricks-workers-scribes}
But in his own inscriptions, which he had written, on clay cylinders, and stone tablets, and bricks, and buried in the foundations of his buildings, Nebuchadnezzar does not talk much about his wars. He talks about his buildings. He talks about the temples he has restored, for the gods, and the walls he has built, for the city, and the canals he has dug, and the palaces he has made.

[long-pause]

He wants to be remembered as a builder, and a pious king, who loved the gods, and made Babylon beautiful.

[pause]

And he did. During his long reign, he rebuilt almost all of Babylon. He built new walls, and new gates, and new palaces, and new temples, and a new bridge, over the Euphrates, and new streets, and canals. He finished the great ziggurat, and covered the great gate, and the Processional Way, with brilliant blue glazed bricks.

[pause]

{img:mud-bricks-stamped-cuneiform-inscription-king-name-close-detail}
Millions of bricks were made for his buildings. And on many of them, the brick makers pressed a stamp, with the king's name, and titles, written in cuneiform: Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon, who provides for the temples Esagila and Ezida, eldest son of Nabopolassar, king of Babylon.

[long-pause]

Archaeologists have found these bricks, by the thousand, in the ruins of Babylon, and they are in museums, all over the world, so that the king's name, which he hoped would last forever, still speaks, from his bricks, more than two and a half thousand years later.

[pause]

In the year 600 BC, the king is still young, perhaps in his thirties, and much of his building work is just beginning. All around the city, there is the noise, and the dust, of building: the brick kilns, smoking, and the workers, carrying baskets of earth, and bricks, and the scribes, counting, and the architects, measuring, and the great walls, and gates, and temples, rising, slowly, brick by brick, under the hot sun.

[long-pause]

{img:southern-palace-babylon-throne-room-facade-glazed-palms-lions-courtyard}
Near the Ishtar Gate, inside the inner wall, by the river, there is the king's palace: the Southern Palace.

[pause]

It is enormous, a whole city, inside the city, with five great courtyards, one after another, from east to west, each one surrounded by rooms, and halls, and offices, and storerooms, and apartments, for the king, and his family, and his officials, and his servants, and his guards.

[long-pause]

In the middle of it, on the south side of the great central courtyard, there is the throne room: a huge hall, more than fifty metres long, and seventeen metres wide, with three great doorways, onto the courtyard. And on the outside wall, facing the courtyard, there is a wonderful decoration, of glazed bricks: on a deep blue background, tall columns, like stylised palm trees, with yellow, and white, capitals, and bands of flowers, and below, a row of lions, walking, in yellow, and gold.

[pause]

Inside, at the back of the hall, opposite the main doorway, there is a niche, in the wall, where the king's throne stands, and there, the king sits, to receive ambassadors, and officials, and visitors, from all over his empire, and beyond.

[pause]

{img:ambassadors-bowing-throne-room-babylon-gifts-tribute-scribes}
The ambassadors come, from Egypt, and from Lydia, and from the Medes, and from the cities of Phoenicia, on the coast, and from Arabia, with their gifts, and their messages, and their servants, and they are led through the great courtyards, one after another, each one grander than the last, until they come to the throne room, and see the king, sitting, in the shadows, at the end of the long hall, in his robes, and his tall crown, surrounded by his officials, and his guards.

[long-pause]

The palace is also the centre of government. Here, the king's scribes write his letters, and his orders, and his decrees, and keep the accounts, of the whole empire, on clay tablets, stored, in great archives. Here are the treasuries, full of gold, and silver, and precious things, brought, as tribute, and as gifts. And here are the storerooms, full of barley, and oil, and dates, for the king's household, and the many people who depend on him.

[pause]

In another palace, nearby, the kings of Babylon keep a collection of old statues, and inscriptions, and treasures, from earlier times, and other lands, because they love the past, and want to understand it. Some people call it one of the first museums in the world.

[pause]

{img:palace-garden-courtyard-babylon-evening-pool-palms-lamps-quiet}
In the evenings, when the business of the day is done, the palace grows quiet. The ambassadors go to their lodgings. The scribes go home. The lamps are lit, along the corridors, and in the courtyards, and the king walks, perhaps, on the walls, above the river, in the cool breeze, and looks out over his city, and the great tower, and the temples, and the river, glowing, in the sunset, and the palm groves, beyond the walls, stretching away, to the edge of the world.

[long-pause]

{img:babylon-double-walls-towers-moat-mudbrick-sunset-river}
Babylon is surrounded by walls.

[pause]

There is an outer wall, a great triangle of walls, many kilometres long, enclosing the whole city, and the gardens, and the fields, and the palm groves, around it, and the king's summer palace, in the north. And inside, there is the inner city, almost square, with the river Euphrates running through the middle of it, surrounded by a double wall, of mud brick, with a moat, full of water, from the river, in front of it.

[long-pause]

The inner wall is about six metres thick, and the outer one, nearly four metres, with a space between them, and towers, every twenty metres, or so, all the way round, hundreds of them, standing up above the walls, with battlements, along the tops. It is one of the greatest fortifications of the ancient world.

[pause]

And through the walls, there are eight great gates, each one named after a god: the Gate of Marduk, and the Gate of Shamash, the sun god, and the Gate of Enlil, and the Gate of Adad, the storm god, and the Gate of Zababa, and the Gate of Urash, and the Gate of the god Sin, of the moon. And the most beautiful of all, in the north, the Ishtar Gate.

[pause]

{img:ishtar-gate-front-view-blue-towers-aurochs-dragons-processional-way}
The Ishtar Gate is named after Ishtar, the goddess of love, and war, and the morning star. Nebuchadnezzar has it rebuilt, more than once, during his long reign, each time higher, and more splendid. It is a double gate, with a smaller gate, in the outer wall, and a great one, behind it, in the inner wall, with tall towers, on each side, and a passage, between them, high enough for the great statues of the gods to pass through, on their carts, in the festival processions.

[long-pause]

And the gate is blue.

[pause]

It is covered, all over, with glazed bricks, shining, in a deep, brilliant blue, like lapis lazuli, the precious blue stone, from the mountains of Afghanistan. And on the blue, in rows, one above another, there are animals, made of bricks, moulded, so that they stand out from the wall, and glazed, in yellow, and gold, and white, and brown: bulls, with blue horns, and dragons, with the heads of snakes, and the bodies of lions, and the feet of eagles, and the tails of scorpions.

[pause]

{img:mushhushshu-dragon-glazed-brick-relief-blue-wall-golden}
The bulls are the animals of Adad, the god of the storm. The dragons are the mushhushshu, the animals of Marduk, the great god of Babylon. There are hundreds of them, in rows, all over the gate, marching, as if they were walking, toward the city.

[long-pause]

Above the gate, along the top, there are battlements, and flowers, rosettes, in white, and yellow, and on the walls, inside the passage, there is a great inscription, in cuneiform, in which Nebuchadnezzar tells how he built the gate, and covered it, with blue bricks, and wild bulls, and dragons, so that all mankind might gaze on it in wonder.

[pause]

At the doors, there are great cedar wood doors, covered with bronze, and at night, they are closed, and barred, and the guards stand watch, on the towers, and the city sleeps, behind its walls.

[pause]

{img:ishtar-gate-detail-aurochs-bull-glazed-bricks-yellow-blue-closeup}
When the sun shines on the Ishtar Gate, in the morning, it glows, like a jewel, blue, and gold, and white, against the brown of the mud brick walls, and the pale sky, and the people who come to Babylon, for the first time, from the villages, and the faraway countries, stop, and stare, and are amazed.

[long-pause]

Today, you can still walk through the Ishtar Gate, or at least the front of it, rebuilt, from the original bricks, in a museum, in Berlin. And the people who see it, more than two and a half thousand years later, still stop, and stare, and are amazed.

[long-pause]

{img:processional-way-babylon-walls-blue-glazed-lions-procession-wide-road}
Through the Ishtar Gate, and out, to the north, runs the Processional Way: the great sacred road of Babylon.

[pause]

It is a wide, straight road, about twenty metres wide, paved with great slabs of white limestone, and red breccia, a kind of reddish stone, and lined, on each side, with high walls.

[long-pause]

And on these walls, there are lions.

[pause]

On a background of shining blue glazed bricks, there walk lions, one after another, in a long line, on each side of the road, moulded in relief, and glazed, in white, and yellow, and gold, with red, or golden, manes, with their mouths open, and their tails raised, striding, slowly, majestically, toward the gate. About a hundred and twenty of them, sixty on each side. The lion is the animal of Ishtar, the goddess of the gate.

[pause]

{img:babylonian-lion-glazed-brick-relief-striding-blue-background-detail}
Above, and below them, there are bands of flowers, rosettes, in white, and yellow, and red. And the walls are so high, that walking along the road, between them, is like walking through a great, blue, shining canyon, with the lions, walking beside you, all the way.

[long-pause]

On most days, the Processional Way is a busy street, with people coming, and going, through the gate, with their donkeys, and their carts, and their baskets, and soldiers, and priests, and merchants, and farmers, bringing their goods to the city.

[pause]

But at the New Year, in the spring, it becomes the sacred road, for the great procession, when the statues of the gods are carried along it, in splendour, from the temple of Marduk, through the Ishtar Gate, and out, to a special temple, outside the city, and back again, with music, and singing, and dancing, and great crowds, watching, from both sides.

[pause]

{img:procession-priests-carrying-gold-statue-marduk-processional-way-crowds}
The stones of the road are inscribed, too, with Nebuchadnezzar's name, and a little prayer, to Marduk, asking for a long life.

[long-pause]

Today, the lions of the Processional Way are scattered, around the world. Some are still in Babylon, in Iraq, and many are in Berlin, in the museum, where part of the road has been rebuilt, beside the Ishtar Gate. And others are in museums in Istanbul, and Paris, and London, and New York, and Chicago, and Copenhagen, and Munich, and Detroit, and elsewhere: lions from Babylon, striding, on their blue background, through the halls of museums, all over the world, as they once strode, toward the Ishtar Gate.

[pause]

And when you stand in front of one, and look at it, closely, you can see the marks of the brick makers' hands, and the little bubbles in the glaze, and imagine the hot sun of Babylon, shining on it, and the crowds, walking past, and the river, flowing, nearby.

[long-pause]

{img:etemenanki-ziggurat-babylon-seven-levels-temple-top-blue-sky}
In the heart of Babylon, there is the great tower: the ziggurat.

[pause]

Its name is Etemenanki, which means, in the ancient Sumerian language, the temple of the foundation of heaven and earth. It is a great stepped tower, built of mud brick, and baked brick, rising up, in levels, one on top of another, each one smaller than the one below, like a great staircase, to the sky.

[long-pause]

It stands in its own great courtyard, surrounded by walls, beside the temple of Marduk, near the river. Its base is square, about ninety metres on each side, and it is perhaps as tall as it is wide, about ninety metres high, the height of a building of thirty storeys, the tallest building in the whole world, in its time.

[pause]

On the front, there is a great triple stairway, leading up, from the courtyard, to the first levels. And on the very top, there is a small temple, for the god Marduk, covered with blue glazed bricks, shining, in the sun, high above the city, which can be seen from far away, across the flat plain, for many kilometres.

[pause]

The ziggurat is very old. There has been a tower here, for more than a thousand years, rebuilt, and repaired, again, and again. The king's father, Nabopolassar, began to rebuild it, and Nebuchadnezzar is finishing it. In his inscriptions, he says that he raised its top, to rival the heavens.

[long-pause]

Many people believe that the ziggurat of Babylon is the Tower of Babel, in the Bible: the great tower, which the people of the earth began to build, in the land of Shinar, with its top in the heavens, until God confused their languages, so that they could no longer understand each other, and they stopped building, and were scattered, over the face of the earth. Babel is the Hebrew name for Babylon.

[pause]

And in the streets of Babylon, in the year 600 BC, you can indeed hear many languages: Babylonian, and Aramaic, and Hebrew, and Egyptian, and Greek, and Persian, and Elamite, and the languages of Arabia, and the mountains, all mixed together, in the markets, and the streets.

[pause]

{img:babylon-skyline-ziggurat-rising-above-houses-palms-evening-glow}
Why did the Babylonians build the ziggurat?

[long-pause]

They believed that the gods lived in the heavens, and that the ziggurat was a bridge, or a stairway, between heaven, and earth, where the gods could come down, to visit the city, and the people. The little temple on the top was a place for the god to rest, on his way down.

[pause]

Only the priests could climb to the top. And there, high above the city, under the stars, they made their offerings, and their prayers, and watched the sky.

[pause]

{img:ziggurat-ruins-babylon-today-low-mound-water-filled-base-reeds}
Today, almost nothing is left of the great ziggurat of Babylon. Its bricks were taken away, over the centuries, to build other buildings. Only its base remains, a square outline, in the ground, sometimes filled with water, and reeds, near the ruins of the city.

[long-pause]

But we know what it looked like, because a Babylonian scribe wrote down its measurements, on a clay tablet, which has survived, and because a Greek writer, Herodotus, described it, more than a hundred years later, with its eight levels, and its winding stairway, around the outside, and its temple, at the top.

[long-pause]

{img:esagila-temple-marduk-courtyard-gold-doors-priests-incense}
Beside the ziggurat, there is the greatest temple of Babylon: Esagila, the temple of Marduk.

[pause]

Its name means: the house whose top is high. It is a great complex of buildings, around courtyards, with thick walls, and tall gateways, and many shrines, and rooms, for the gods, and the priests, and the treasures, and the offerings.

[long-pause]

In the heart of it, in the most holy room, there lives the god himself: Marduk, the lord of Babylon, the king of the gods, in the form of a great statue, made of precious wood, covered with gold, dressed in rich robes, embroidered with gold, and jewels, sitting on a golden throne, with a golden table, before him.

[pause]

The Greek writer Herodotus, who wrote about Babylon later, said that the statue, and the throne, and the table, together, were made of so much gold, that it weighed more than twenty tons. That may be an exaggeration. But the Babylonians themselves wrote that the shrine was covered with gold, like the sun.

[pause]

{img:priests-feeding-god-statue-golden-table-food-offerings-shrine}
Every day, the priests of Esagila take care of Marduk, as if he were a great king. In the morning, they wake him, with songs, and prayers, and wash him, and dress him, in fresh clothes. Twice a day, they bring him meals: great feasts, of bread, and cakes, and meat, and fish, and fruit, and dates, and beer, and wine, set out on the golden table, before him, with music, and incense, and then, after the god has eaten, the food is shared out, among the priests, and the temple workers, and sent to the king's table.

[long-pause]

At night, the priests put the god to bed, and close the doors of his shrine, and the temple is quiet, with only the lamps, burning, and the guards, watching.

[pause]

Marduk is the great god of Babylon. He was once only a small god, of this one city. But as Babylon grew great, so did he, and now he is the king of all the gods. In the great poem of creation, the Enuma Elish, which the priests recite, every year, at the New Year festival, it tells how, at the beginning of time, there was only water, and chaos, ruled by Tiamat, the goddess of the salt sea, and how the young god Marduk defeated her, and made the heavens, and the earth, from her body, and set the stars, and the moon, in their places, and made people, to serve the gods, and how the gods built Babylon for him, as his home.

[pause]

{img:temple-workers-esagila-scribes-musicians-singers-courtyard-day}
Esagila is not only a temple. It is like a small city. Hundreds of people work there: priests, and singers, and musicians, and scribes, and astronomers, and cooks, and bakers, and brewers, and weavers, and goldsmiths, and carpenters, and gardeners, and herdsmen. The temple owns land, and fields, and palm groves, and flocks, and herds, all around Babylon, and lends money, and grain, and keeps careful accounts, of everything, on clay tablets.

[long-pause]

And the people of Babylon come to Esagila, to pray, and to make offerings, and to ask the god for help: for healing, and for children, and for good fortune, and for justice. They bring little gifts, and stand in the courtyard, and look toward the shrine, where the god sits, in the golden dimness, and pray, with their hands raised to their faces.

[pause]

And the smell of incense, of cedar, and cypress, and myrrh, drifts out, over the courtyards, and the walls, into the streets of the city, all day long.

[long-pause]

{img:euphrates-river-babylon-stone-bridge-piers-boats-palms-afternoon}
Through the middle of Babylon flows the river Euphrates.

[pause]

It divides the city in two: the old city, on the east bank, with the great temples, and the palaces, and the ziggurat, and the new city, on the west bank, with its own temples, and houses, and gardens. And to join them, Nebuchadnezzar's father, and Nebuchadnezzar, have built a great bridge.

[long-pause]

It is one of the first great bridges of the ancient world: about a hundred and twenty metres long, resting on piers, of baked brick, and stone, shaped like boats, pointed at the front, so that the water flows past them, smoothly, without damaging them. The road across the bridge is made of wooden beams, of cedar, and palm, laid across the piers, which can be taken away, at night, so that nobody can cross, in the dark.

[pause]

Along the river, inside the city, there are quays, of baked brick, with steps, leading down, to the water, where the boats tie up, and the people come, to fetch water, and wash, and bathe.

[pause]

{img:round-coracle-quffa-boats-river-euphrates-traders-cargo-reeds}
And on the river, there are boats of every kind.

[long-pause]

There are long, narrow boats, of wood, with sails, and oars, carrying goods, up and down the river, from the sea, and from the north. There are rafts, made of logs, floated on inflated goatskins, carrying stone, and timber, and wine, from the mountains, down the river. And there are the round boats, called quffas: big round baskets, woven of reeds, or willow, and covered with leather, or coated with bitumen, the black, sticky tar, which seeps up from the ground, in Mesopotamia, to make them waterproof.

[pause]

The Greek writer Herodotus describes them, with wonder: round, like a shield, and carrying great loads, wine, in palm-wood casks, and a live donkey, or two, which are taken along, so that, when the boat arrives, and has been unloaded, it can be taken apart, and the leather loaded on the donkeys, and carried back, up the river, by land, because the current is too strong to row against.

[pause]

{img:fishermen-casting-nets-euphrates-dawn-reeds-herons-quiet}
In the river, there are fish, and the fishermen go out, at dawn, in their little boats, and cast their nets, and come back, with carp, and catfish, and barbel, for the market. And there are birds: herons, and egrets, and ducks, and pelicans, and kingfishers, in the reeds, along the banks.

[long-pause]

But the river can also be dangerous. Every spring, when the snow melts, in the mountains, far to the north, the river rises, and sometimes floods, over its banks, across the land, and into the city. The Babylonians build their houses on platforms, and raise their temples on great terraces, and keep their canals, and dykes, in good repair, and pray to the gods, to keep the floods away.

[pause]

And in the evening, as the sun sets, behind the palms, on the west bank, the river glows, red, and gold, and the boats come home, and the people sit on the quays, and on the roofs, in the cool breeze, from the water, and watch the light fade, over the great, slow river.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonian-house-courtyard-mudbrick-rooms-doors-family-morning-light}
Let us visit a family, in their house, in Babylon.

[pause]

They live in the eastern part of the old city, in a neighbourhood called Merkes, not far from the great temple, on a narrow street, which winds between the houses, unpaved, dusty in summer, and muddy, after the winter rains.

[long-pause]

From the street, the house looks very plain: just a high wall, of mud brick, plastered with mud, without windows, with a single, low wooden door. But inside, it is quite different.

[pause]

Through the door, there is a little entrance room, and then, the courtyard: an open space, in the middle of the house, paved with baked bricks, open to the sky, with the rooms of the house, all around it. The courtyard is the heart of the house, where the family lives, and works, and cooks, and talks, and the children play, in the shade, and the light.

[pause]

{img:courtyard-family-weaving-grinding-grain-children-playing-babylon}
Around the courtyard, there are the rooms: a long reception room, on the south side, facing north, so that it stays cool, where the family receives guests, and sits, in the heat of the day. And there are bedrooms, and storerooms, for grain, and oil, and dates, and a kitchen, with a clay oven, and perhaps a little room, for washing, with a drain.

[long-pause]

The walls are thick, of mud brick, to keep out the heat, and the cold. The rooms are dark, and cool, lit only by the light, from the doors, onto the courtyard. The floors are of beaten earth, or brick, covered with reed mats, and carpets, of wool.

[pause]

There is not much furniture: low wooden beds, with mattresses, and cushions, and stools, and a little table, and wooden chests, for clothes, and clay jars, for water, and oil, and grain, and baskets, everywhere. At night, the rooms are lit by little clay lamps, burning sesame oil.

[pause]

{img:flat-roof-babylon-house-ladder-family-sleeping-summer-night-stars}
And on top of the house, there is the flat roof, made of palm trunks, and reed mats, covered with a thick layer of mud, rolled smooth. The family climbs up to the roof, by a ladder, or a little stairway, and uses it as another room: to dry dates, and grain, and washing, in the sun, and to sit, in the cool of the evening, and talk with the neighbours, on the next roofs.

[long-pause]

And in the summer, when it is very hot, the whole family sleeps on the roof, at night, on mats, under the open sky, in the cool breeze, under the stars. All over Babylon, on summer nights, the families lie on their roofs, and look up at the stars, and talk, quietly, and fall asleep, to the sound of the dogs barking, in the streets, and the frogs, in the canals, and the watchmen, calling the hours, from the walls.

[pause]

Under the floor of the house, the family buries its dead, in clay coffins, or jars, so that the ancestors are still with them, in the house, and the family gives them offerings, of food, and water, every month, and remembers them.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonian-woman-baking-flatbread-clay-oven-courtyard-dawn}
Let us spend a day with this family of Babylon.

[pause]

The father is a scribe, who works for the temple of Marduk, writing contracts, and accounts, on clay tablets. The mother runs the house, and weaves, and has a little business of her own, lending small amounts of silver, to her neighbours. There are four children, and the father's old mother, and a servant girl, and a little dog.

[long-pause]

They wake at dawn, when the light comes into the courtyard, and the birds begin to sing, in the palm tree, next door. The servant girl lights the fire, in the clay oven, in the corner of the courtyard, and the mother makes bread: flat, round loaves, of barley flour, slapped onto the hot inside wall of the oven, where they bake, in a few minutes, and are peeled off, hot, and soft, and smelling wonderful.

[pause]

They eat breakfast, sitting on mats, in the courtyard: bread, and dates, and a little cheese, and some onions, and a drink of beer, thick, and sweet, made from barley, which everyone drinks, even the children.

[pause]

{img:scribe-father-walking-street-tablets-basket-temple-morning-crowd}
Then the father goes to work, at the temple, walking through the narrow streets, which are already busy, with people, and donkeys, and carts, and water carriers, and sellers, calling their wares. He carries his stylus, a sharp reed, for writing, and a little basket, of clay tablets.

[long-pause]

The eldest son, who is twelve, goes to school, at the house of a teacher, where he is learning to be a scribe, like his father. The younger children stay at home, and play, in the courtyard, and in the street, with the neighbours' children, with clay toys, and balls, and spinning tops, and little carts, on wheels, pulled by a string.

[pause]

The mother spins, and weaves, at her loom, in the courtyard, in the shade, making cloth, of wool, for the family's clothes. And she grinds the barley, on a grinding stone, with the servant girl, and goes to the market, to buy what she needs, and to visit her friends, and her sister, who lives in the next street.

[pause]

{img:babylonian-women-chatting-courtyard-spinning-wool-afternoon-shade}
In the heat of the afternoon, everyone rests, in the cool, dark rooms, while the sun beats down, on the courtyard, and the streets are almost empty, and quiet.

[long-pause]

In the late afternoon, the father comes home, and washes, and the family eats the main meal of the day: bread, and lentils, and chickpeas, and onions, and garlic, and leeks, cooked together, in a stew, with a little mutton, or fish, from the river, with sesame oil, and herbs, and cumin, and coriander, and dates, and figs, and pomegranates, for afterwards.

[pause]

And in the evening, as the sun goes down, and the air cools, they climb up onto the roof, with a jug of beer, and some dates, and sit, together, on their mats, and talk, and watch the sky turn red, and gold, and purple, over the city, and the great tower, and the river, and the palm trees, and the swallows, swooping, and the bats, coming out.

[pause]

{img:babylon-rooftops-evening-families-sitting-ziggurat-silhouette-sunset}
The grandmother tells the children stories, old, old stories, of the gods, and the heroes: of Gilgamesh, the great king of Uruk, and his friend, Enkidu, the wild man, and their adventures, and of the great flood, long ago, when the gods sent the waters over the whole earth, and one wise man, Utnapishtim, built a great boat, and saved his family, and the animals, and of the clever god, Ea, who lives in the sweet waters, under the earth.

[long-pause]

And the children fall asleep, on the roof, one by one, under the stars, while the grown-ups talk, softly, and the city grows quiet, around them.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonian-woman-signing-contract-scribe-witnesses-seal-courtyard}
The women of Babylon have more rights, and more freedom, than women in many other places, in the ancient world.

[pause]

Most women, of course, spend their lives, as wives, and mothers, running their households, and bringing up their children, and spinning, and weaving, and cooking, and brewing. But a Babylonian woman can own property, of her own: houses, and fields, and slaves, and silver. She can buy, and sell, and lend, and borrow. She can make contracts, and be a witness, and go to court, and win her case.

[long-pause]

When she marries, her family gives her a dowry: money, and furniture, and clothes, and jewellery, and sometimes, a house, or a field, or a servant. And the dowry remains hers, all her life, and is written down, carefully, on a clay tablet, in the marriage contract. If her husband divorces her, without good reason, he must give it back, and sometimes pay her more besides. And when she dies, it goes to her children.

[pause]

Many marriage contracts, from Babylon, in these years, have been found. They are quite simple. The husband asks the father of the bride, for his daughter, in marriage. The father agrees. The dowry is listed. And sometimes, there is a promise: that if the husband takes a second wife, he must pay the first one a large sum of silver, and let her go home, to her family.

[pause]

{img:women-weaving-workshop-temple-babylon-looms-wool-supervisor}
Some women work outside their homes. They are weavers, and spinners, in the great workshops of the temples, and the palace. They run taverns, and brew beer. They are midwives, and nurses, and wet-nurses. They sell things, in the streets, and the markets. And some of them are scribes, and singers, and musicians, and priestesses, in the temples.

[long-pause]

There are businesswomen too. In the archives of Babylon, there are women who buy, and sell, houses, and lend silver, and grain, at interest, and rent out fields, and manage their family's property, when their husbands are away, or have died. In the Egibi family archive, there are wives, and mothers, who appear, again and again, in the business of the family, buying, and selling, and signing, and witnessing.

[pause]

The women of Babylon are also respected, as mothers, and as the keepers of the house, and the family's gods, and the ancestors' memory. The Babylonians had a saying, that a house without a wife is like a field without a plough.

[pause]

{img:mother-daughters-babylon-roof-evening-combing-hair-talking-sunset}
And in the evening, on the rooftops of Babylon, the mothers, and the daughters, and the grandmothers, sit together, and comb each other's hair, and talk, and laugh, and tell each other the news, and the gossip, of the neighbourhood, and plan the marriages, of the young people, and watch the sun go down, over the city.

[long-pause]

{img:date-palm-grove-babylon-climber-harvesting-dates-ropes-baskets}
Around Babylon, and along all the rivers, and canals, of Mesopotamia, there are the date palms.

[pause]

They grow in great groves, tall, and slender, with their crowns of long, green, feathery leaves, rustling in the wind, and their great bunches of dates, hanging below, orange, and red, and brown, as they ripen, in the late summer.

[long-pause]

The date palm is the tree of life, for the Babylonians. Its fruit is sweet, and nourishing, and can be eaten fresh, or dried, and kept, for months, or pressed, into a sweet syrup, like honey, or made into a kind of wine. Its leaves are woven into baskets, and mats, and roofs. Its fibres are made into ropes. Its trunk is used for beams, and roofs, and bridges. Its stones are burned as fuel, or fed to the animals. Nothing is wasted.

[pause]

Under the palms, in their shade, the farmers grow other trees, and plants: pomegranates, and figs, and apples, and grapes, and vegetables, onions, and garlic, and leeks, and lettuce, and cucumbers, and herbs. The palm groves are like great gardens, cool, and green, and shady, in the burning heat of the summer.

[pause]

The date palms need help. There are male trees, and female trees, and only the female trees bear fruit. So, in the spring, the farmers climb up the tall trunks of the female trees, with a rope, around themselves, and the trunk, and tie a little bunch of the flowers of the male tree, among the flowers of the female tree, so that the dates will grow. They have been doing this for thousands of years, and in Iraq, the farmers still do it, today, in the same way.

[long-pause]

And everything depends on the canals.

[pause]

All around Babylon, there is a great network of canals, large, and small, carrying the water of the Euphrates, out across the land, to the fields, and the palm groves. The great canals are as wide as rivers, and boats sail along them. The small ones are little ditches, running between the fields. The farmers open, and close, little gates, of earth, and reeds, to let the water flow into their fields, when they need it.

[pause]

{img:irrigation-canal-shaduf-farmer-lifting-water-bucket-fields-barley}
To lift the water, from the canals, into the fields, they use the shaduf: a long pole, balanced on a post, with a bucket, on one end, and a heavy weight, of clay, on the other, so that the farmer can dip the bucket in the canal, and lift it, easily, and swing it round, and empty it, into the field.

[long-pause]

The canals must be kept clean, and repaired, every year. The river carries a great deal of mud, and it settles in the canals, and fills them up, so that they must be dug out, again, and again. This is hard work, done by the farmers, and by workers, sent by the king, and the temples.

[pause]

In the fields, the farmers grow barley, above all, which is the main food, and drink, of Babylon, for bread, and beer, and wheat, and emmer, and sesame, for oil, and flax, for linen. They plough with oxen, and a wooden plough, with a funnel, on the top, through which the seed is dropped, into the furrow, as the plough moves along, a clever invention, of the Mesopotamians.

[pause]

{img:barley-harvest-mesopotamia-sickles-sheaves-donkeys-threshing-floor}
And at the harvest, in the spring, in April, and May, the barley is cut, with sickles, and carried, on donkeys, to the threshing floors, where it is threshed, by oxen, walking round, and round, on it, and winnowed, in the wind, and measured, and taken, in great sacks, to the storehouses, of the temples, and the families, and the king.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonians-drinking-beer-straws-large-jar-tavern-friends}
In Babylon, everyone drinks beer.

[pause]

It is made from barley, and it is quite different from the beer of today: thick, and cloudy, and sweet, and not very strong, more like a kind of liquid food, than a drink, and it is drunk, every day, by everyone, with every meal, men, and women, and children, and the king, and the gods.

[long-pause]

To make it, the brewers soak the barley in water, until it sprouts, and then dry it, and grind it, and bake it, into loaves, called beer bread. Then they crumble the loaves into water, in great jars, with dates, or date syrup, for sweetness, and leave it, for a few days, to ferment, and bubble.

[pause]

The beer is thick, with bits of grain, and husks, floating on the top. So people drink it through long straws, of reed, sometimes decorated with metal, or with a little filter, at the bottom, to keep out the bits. In pictures, and carvings, from Mesopotamia, we see people sitting, around a great jar, all drinking through their long straws, together, and talking.

[pause]

{img:babylonian-tavern-keeper-woman-serving-beer-jars-evening-lamp}
There are taverns, in Babylon, where people go, in the evening, to drink, and talk, and listen to music. Many of them are run by women, tavern-keepers, who brew the beer themselves. The old laws of Hammurabi, more than a thousand years before, had rules about them, to make sure they sold honest measures, at honest prices.

[long-pause]

And there is wine, too, from the mountains, in the north, brought down the river, on rafts, which is expensive, and drunk by the rich, and the king, and offered to the gods.

[pause]

And bread is everywhere. The Babylonians have dozens of kinds of bread, and cakes: flat breads, baked on the walls of the oven, and round loaves, and sweet cakes, made with honey, and dates, and sesame, and butter, and spices, baked in moulds, of clay, in the shapes of animals, and fish, and stars.

[pause]

{img:clay-baking-moulds-patterned-cakes-dates-honey-kitchen-table}
There is also a very old collection of recipes, from Mesopotamia, written on clay tablets, more than a thousand years before our story, the oldest cookbook in the world. The recipes are for stews, and broths, of lamb, and mutton, and pigeon, and duck, cooked with water, and fat, and onions, and leeks, and garlic, and herbs, and salt, and crushed grain, and some of them have been cooked, again, by scholars, and cooks, in modern times, to taste what the food of Mesopotamia was like.

[long-pause]

In the evening, in the houses of Babylon, the smell of baking bread, and onions, and garlic, and stew, and wood smoke, drifts out, over the rooftops, and the streets, and the families gather, around their meals, in their courtyards, and on their roofs, and drink their beer, through their straws, and talk, about the day.

[long-pause]

{img:babylon-market-street-stalls-pottery-textiles-spices-merchants-donkeys}
Babylon is a city of trade.

[pause]

Mesopotamia is a land rich in barley, and dates, and wool, and clay, and reeds, and bitumen. But it has almost no stone, no timber, except palm wood, and no metals. So everything else must be brought from far away, and the merchants of Babylon trade with the whole known world.

[long-pause]

From the mountains of Lebanon, in the west, comes cedar wood, for the roofs, and the doors, of the temples, and the palaces, floated down the rivers, in great rafts. From the mountains of Iran, and Anatolia, come copper, and tin, to make bronze, and iron, and silver, and gold. From Afghanistan, far to the east, comes lapis lazuli, the deep blue stone, flecked with gold, which the Babylonians love above all others. From Egypt, and Arabia, come incense, and myrrh, and spices. From India, by sea, and up the Persian Gulf, come ivory, and cotton, and precious stones.

[pause]

And in return, Babylon sends out its own goods: barley, and dates, and sesame oil, and above all, cloth: fine woollen cloth, woven, and dyed, in brilliant colours, red, and blue, and purple, and embroidered, which is famous, everywhere.

[pause]

{img:camel-donkey-caravan-arriving-babylon-gate-traders-bundles}
The merchants travel in caravans, with long lines of donkeys, and, more and more, camels, across the deserts, from oasis to oasis, and along the rivers, in boats, to the sea.

[long-pause]

In Babylon, there is no single great market place, like a Greek, or Roman forum. Instead, trade happens everywhere: at the city gates, and on the quays, by the river, where the boats unload, and in the streets, and in little shops, opening onto the streets, and in the temples, and the houses of the great merchant families.

[pause]

There is no coined money, yet. Coins have just been invented, in Lydia, in Turkey, but they have not yet come to Babylon. Instead, people pay in silver, weighed out, on scales, in little pieces, and rings, and coils, and in barley. A shekel of silver, about eight grams, is the common measure of value, and everything has its price, in shekels, and in barley.

[pause]

{img:merchant-weighing-silver-scales-stone-weights-clay-tablet-contract}
And everything is written down. When the people of Babylon buy, or sell, or lend, or rent, or hire, or marry, or adopt a child, or divide an inheritance, they go to a scribe, and he writes a contract, on a clay tablet, with the names of the people, and the witnesses, and the date, and the agreement, and they press their seals, or their fingernails, into the clay, to sign it.

[long-pause]

Thousands of these contracts have been found, in the ruins of Babylon, and the other cities, and they tell us about the business of ordinary people, two and a half thousand years ago: the price of a house, and a slave, and an ox, and a field, and a jar of oil, and the rent of a boat, and the interest on a loan, and the wages of a worker.

[pause]

One great family of Babylon, the Egibi family, were businessmen, and bankers, for five generations, during these years, and their archive, of more than a thousand tablets, has been found. They bought, and sold, houses, and fields, and dates, and barley, and lent money, and rented out land, and managed property, for other people, and their tablets tell the story of their business, and their family, their marriages, and their quarrels, for more than a hundred years.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonian-judges-court-temple-gate-plaintiffs-scribes-tablets}
In Babylon, there are judges, and courts, and laws.

[pause]

More than a thousand years before our story, the great king Hammurabi, of Babylon, had his laws carved, on a tall stone, of black basalt, more than two metres high, and set up, in a temple, so that everyone could see them. At the top, there is a carving, of the king, standing, before the sun god, Shamash, the god of justice, who gives him the laws. And below, in close, neat columns, there are the laws themselves: nearly three hundred of them.

[long-pause]

The laws deal with everything: with property, and trade, and wages, and debts, and loans, and marriage, and divorce, and inheritance, and adoption, and the care of fields, and canals, and animals, and boats, and houses, and doctors, and builders, and tavern-keepers. Some of the punishments are very harsh, in the old way, an eye for an eye. But many of the laws are about fairness, and protecting the weak.

[pause]

In his words, at the beginning, and the end, Hammurabi says that he wrote the laws so that the strong should not oppress the weak, and so that the orphan, and the widow, should have justice. And anyone who has a quarrel, he says, should come, and stand before his stone, and read the laws, and understand his case, and his heart will be at peace.

[pause]

{img:basalt-stele-laws-hammurabi-shamash-relief-top-columns-cuneiform}
By the year 600 BC, the laws of Hammurabi are very old, but they are still copied, by the scribes, in their schools, as a classic text, like the laws of the ancestors. And the idea that the king must give justice, and protect the weak, is still at the heart of kingship, in Babylon.

[long-pause]

The stone of Hammurabi was carried away, long after, to the city of Susa, in Iran, as a trophy of war, and was found there, by French archaeologists, in 1901. Today, it stands in the Louvre Museum, in Paris.

[pause]

In Nebuchadnezzar's Babylon, when there is a dispute, people go to the judges, who sit at the gates of the temples, or in the courts, and listen, to both sides, and to the witnesses, and look at the contracts, on their tablets, and decide. Sometimes, they ask people to swear an oath, before the gods, and the symbols of the gods, that they are telling the truth, which is a very serious, and frightening thing to do.

[pause]

{img:oath-before-divine-symbols-temple-courtyard-witnesses-solemn-light}
And everything is written down, on clay: the case, and the witnesses, and the judgement, and the names of the judges, and the date. So, two and a half thousand years later, we can still read about a quarrel, over the boundary of a field, or the price of a slave, or the return of a dowry, or the payment of a debt, and how the judges of Babylon decided it.

[long-pause]

Most quarrels, though, never come to court. They are settled, quietly, by the families, and the neighbours, and the elders, of the street, sitting together, in a courtyard, in the evening, over a jar of beer, and talking, until everyone agrees, and goes home, to sleep.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonian-scribe-pressing-reed-stylus-wet-clay-tablet-cuneiform}
In Babylon, people write on clay.

[pause]

The writing is called cuneiform, from the Latin word for a wedge, because it is made of little wedge-shaped marks, pressed into soft, wet clay, with the end of a reed, cut to a sharp point, like a little triangle. Each sign is made of several wedges, long, and short, upright, and lying down, and slanting, pressed in, in a pattern.

[long-pause]

It is the oldest writing system in the world. It was invented, by the Sumerians, more than five thousand years ago, at first as little pictures, drawn on clay, of the things they were counting: sheep, and barley, and jars of oil. Then, over the centuries, the pictures became simpler, and turned into wedges, and the signs came to stand for sounds, and syllables, as well as words, so that they could write down anything, in any language.

[pause]

By 600 BC, cuneiform has been used for more than two and a half thousand years, for many different languages: Sumerian, and Akkadian, and Babylonian, and Assyrian, and Hittite, and Elamite, and Persian. There are hundreds of different signs, and it takes many years to learn them all.

[pause]

{img:clay-tablets-drying-sun-rows-courtyard-scribes-workshop}
The scribe takes a lump of clay, smooth, and damp, and shapes it, in his hands, into a tablet, small enough to hold in one hand, flat on one side, and slightly rounded on the other, like a little cushion. He holds it in his left hand, and the reed in his right, and presses the signs into it, quickly, line after line, turning the tablet over, to write on the back, and even on the edges.

[long-pause]

Then the tablet is left to dry, in the sun, and becomes hard. If it is important, it is baked, in an oven, and becomes as hard as a brick, and will last, almost forever. Letters are put into envelopes, also made of clay, with the address written on the outside, and the envelope must be broken, to read the letter.

[pause]

Clay tablets are cheap, and easy to make, and they last. Paper, and parchment, and papyrus, rot, and burn. But clay survives, for thousands of years, in the ground, and when a library burns, the fire bakes the tablets, and preserves them.

[pause]

{img:library-shelves-clay-tablets-baskets-labels-temple-archive-lamp}
And so, hundreds of thousands of clay tablets have been found, in the ruins of the cities of Mesopotamia: letters, and contracts, and laws, and accounts, and lists, and dictionaries, and prayers, and hymns, and stories, and poems, and medical texts, and mathematics, and astronomy, and school exercises, and recipes, and spells.

[long-pause]

Many of them are still unread, waiting, in the storerooms of museums, around the world, for scholars to study them. Every year, more are read, and translated, and published, and we learn new things about the people who wrote them: their business, and their worries, and their prayers, and their jokes, and their lives.

[pause]

In Babylon, in the year 600 BC, a new script is also spreading: the alphabet of the Aramaeans, with only twenty-two letters, written in ink, on parchment, and leather, and potsherds, which is much easier to learn. Many people now speak Aramaic, in the streets, and write it, with pen, and ink. But for important things, for temples, and laws, and contracts, and astronomy, the scribes still use the ancient cuneiform, on clay, as their ancestors have done, for thousands of years.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonian-school-boys-sitting-benches-copying-tablets-teacher}
The eldest son of our family goes to school, to learn to be a scribe.

[pause]

The Babylonians call the school the tablet house. It is not a big building, but usually the house of a teacher, a master scribe, who takes a few pupils, and teaches them, in a room, or in the courtyard of his house. Most of the pupils are boys, the sons of scribes, and priests, and rich families, though a few girls learn to write, too.

[long-pause]

The boys start young, at about seven, or eight, and they go to school, every day, from sunrise, until evening, for many years. And they work hard.

[pause]

First, they learn to make tablets, and to hold the reed, and to press the simple wedges. Then, they learn the signs, one by one, copying them, again, and again, from a model tablet, written by the teacher, on one side, and their own copy, on the other. Then, they learn lists of words: lists of trees, and animals, and stones, and gods, and professions, and cities, in Sumerian, and Babylonian. Then, proverbs, and sentences, and contracts, and letters, and hymns, and stories.

[pause]

{img:school-tablet-teacher-copy-pupil-copy-clumsy-wedges-close}
When they make a mistake, they smooth out the clay, with their thumb, and start again. Many of these school tablets have been found, with the teacher's neat model, on one side, and the pupil's clumsy copy, on the other, full of mistakes.

[long-pause]

And they learn mathematics: counting, and multiplication tables, and division, and fractions, and how to measure fields, and calculate the amount of bricks, for a wall, or the amount of earth, for a canal, or the amount of barley, for the workers' wages.

[pause]

The teachers are strict. There are old Sumerian stories, written down, and copied, by the pupils, for centuries, about life at school: about a boy who is late, and is beaten, and who talks too much, and is beaten, and who writes badly, and is beaten. And about how his father invites the teacher home, and gives him a good dinner, and a new robe, and a ring, and then the teacher says that the boy is the best pupil in the school.

[pause]

{img:babylonian-boy-walking-home-school-tablet-satchel-street-evening}
These stories are funny, and very human, and show that schoolboys, and teachers, and parents, have not changed very much, in four thousand years.

[long-pause]

When they have finished school, after many years, the young scribes can work for the temples, or the palace, or the merchants, or the courts, or as teachers, or doctors, or astronomers, or diviners. They are respected, and well paid, and proud of their skill.

[pause]

And they have a goddess of their own, Nisaba, the goddess of writing, and grain, and a god, Nabu, the god of writing, and wisdom, whose symbol is a stylus, standing on a tablet. At the end of their tablets, many scribes wrote a little line: Praise be to Nisaba.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonian-mathematics-tablet-numbers-wedges-scribe-calculating-lamp}
Every time we look at a clock, we are using something invented in Mesopotamia.

[pause]

The Babylonians count, not in tens, as we do, but in sixties. Their number system has only two signs: an upright wedge, for one, and a sideways wedge, for ten. And with these, they can write any number, from one to fifty-nine. And then, for sixty, they start again, with a single wedge, in the next column, just as we write ten, with a one, in the next column.

[long-pause]

Why sixty? Nobody knows for certain. But sixty is a wonderful number, for calculating, because it can be divided, evenly, by so many other numbers: by two, and three, and four, and five, and six, and ten, and twelve, and fifteen, and twenty, and thirty. So it is very easy to work with fractions: a half, a third, a quarter, a fifth, a sixth, of sixty, are all whole numbers.

[pause]

And from the Babylonians, and their sixties, we have inherited our hours, of sixty minutes, and our minutes, of sixty seconds, and our circles, of three hundred and sixty degrees.

[pause]

{img:old-babylonian-tablet-square-diagonal-numbers-plimpton-style-close}
Babylonian mathematics is very advanced. More than a thousand years before our story, the scribes of Babylon were already solving difficult problems: calculating areas, and volumes, and interest on loans, and the square roots of numbers.

[long-pause]

There is a famous little tablet, from that time, about the size of a hand, which shows a square, with its two diagonals, and on the diagonal, written in cuneiform, the square root of two, calculated correctly, to about six decimal places, more accurately than anyone else would manage, for more than a thousand years.

[pause]

And there is another famous tablet, called Plimpton 322, after the man who collected it, with a table of numbers, which scholars have studied, and argued about, for nearly a hundred years, which seems to show that the Babylonians knew about the special relationship between the sides of a right-angled triangle, more than a thousand years before the Greek, Pythagoras, after whom it is named.

[pause]

{img:babylonian-surveyors-measuring-field-rope-stakes-canal-scribe-notes}
For the Babylonians, mathematics is practical. They use it, every day, to measure fields, and divide inheritances, and calculate rations, and wages, and the bricks for a wall, and the earth for a canal, and the barley for a temple, and the interest on a loan.

[long-pause]

And they use it to measure time.

[pause]

The Babylonians divide the day, and the night, into twelve double hours, and the circle of the sky into twelve parts, and the year into twelve months, of the moon, twenty-nine, or thirty days each, adding an extra month, every few years, to keep the calendar in step with the sun, and the seasons.

[pause]

So, every time we look at a clock, and see sixty minutes, in an hour, or turn a corner, of ninety degrees, or count the twelve months of the year, we are remembering, without knowing it, the scribes of Babylon, pressing their wedges into clay.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonian-astronomers-roof-night-observing-stars-writing-tablets-lamp}
At night, on the roofs of the temples of Babylon, and on the top of the great ziggurat, the astronomers watch the sky.

[pause]

The sky over Babylon is wonderful, at night. The air is dry, and clear, and there are no city lights, only the little lamps, in the houses, and the fires, on the walls, so that the stars shine, bright, and sharp, in their thousands, from horizon to horizon, over the flat land.

[long-pause]

The astronomers are priests, and scribes, of the temple of Marduk, trained, for many years, in the old knowledge of the sky. And every night, for hundreds of years, they have watched, and written down, what they see.

[pause]

They write it in astronomical diaries: clay tablets, on which, night after night, month after month, they record the positions of the moon, and the planets, among the stars, and when they rise, and set, and the eclipses, and the comets, and the shooting stars, and the weather, the clouds, and the rain, and the wind, and the level of the river, and the prices of barley, and dates, and wool, and oil, in the market, and the important events, in the city, and the world.

[pause]

{img:night-sky-babylon-moon-venus-jupiter-ziggurat-silhouette-desert}
These diaries began, as far as we know, around 650 BC, and they continued, for six hundred years, until the time of the Romans. It is the longest scientific record, kept by any civilisation, in the ancient world. Hundreds of them survive, in the British Museum, in London, and scholars still use them, today, to study ancient eclipses, and the history of the earth's rotation.

[long-pause]

With all these observations, the Babylonian astronomers learned to predict the movements of the sky: when the new moon would appear, and when the eclipses of the moon would come, and the movements of the planets, Venus, and Jupiter, and Mars, and Saturn, and Mercury, using clever mathematical methods, with their numbers, in sixties.

[pause]

Some scholars think, recently, that the Babylonians even used a kind of geometry, to calculate the path of Jupiter, across the sky, a method that was not used again, in Europe, until more than a thousand years later.

[pause]

{img:zodiac-constellations-painted-sky-lion-bull-scorpion-babylonian-style}
The Babylonians also named the stars, and the constellations, and many of the names we use today, come from them. They divided the path of the sun, and the moon, and the planets, across the sky, into twelve parts, each with its own constellation: the Bull, and the Lion, and the Scorpion, and the Twins, and the Scales, and the others, which, later, became the twelve signs of the zodiac.

[long-pause]

For the Babylonians, the sky is a great book, written by the gods, in which they can read the future. An eclipse, or a strange movement of a planet, is a message, a sign, of what is to come, for the king, and the country. And so, the astronomers send reports to the king, of what they have seen, and what it may mean.

[pause]

But they also simply watch, and record, and calculate, night after night, with great patience, and care, and love, for the sky. And on a warm night, in Babylon, you can see them, on the roof of the temple, wrapped in their cloaks, looking up, at the moon, rising, over the palm trees, and the river, and the great dark plain, and pressing their reed, softly, into the clay.

[long-pause]

{img:akitu-new-year-festival-babylon-procession-statues-gods-boats-river}
In the spring, at the beginning of the month of Nisan, in March, or April, when the barley is ripening, in the fields, Babylon celebrates the New Year: the festival of Akitu.

[pause]

It is the greatest festival of the year, and it lasts for twelve days.

[long-pause]

During the first days, the priests of Esagila perform their ceremonies, in the temple, at dawn: prayers, and hymns, and purifications, and the reciting of the great poem of creation, the Enuma Elish, telling how Marduk defeated the chaos, and made the world, and the stars, and the people.

[pause]

On the fifth day, there is a strange, and solemn, ceremony. The king comes to the temple, and stands before the statue of Marduk. And the high priest takes away his crown, and his sceptre, and his royal robes, and lays them before the god. And then, the priest strikes the king, on the cheek, and pulls his ears, and the king kneels, before the god, and says that he has not sinned, and has not neglected his duties, and has not harmed Babylon, or its people.

[pause]

{img:king-kneeling-before-marduk-statue-high-priest-temple-ceremony}
And then the priest gives him back his crown, and his sceptre, and strikes him again, on the cheek. And it is said that if the king has tears in his eyes, it is a good sign: Marduk is pleased, and the year will be good.

[long-pause]

So every year, the most powerful king in the world must humble himself, before the god, and promise to rule well, and justly.

[pause]

Then, the gods arrive. From all the cities of Babylonia, the statues of the gods are brought, by boat, along the rivers, and the canals, to Babylon, to visit Marduk, for the New Year: Nabu, his son, from the city of Borsippa, nearby, and many others, in their boats, decorated with gold, and flowers, and banners, with their priests, and their musicians.

[pause]

{img:procession-through-ishtar-gate-golden-statues-chariots-music-crowds}
And on the eighth day, and the ninth, there is the great procession. The king takes the hand of the statue of Marduk, and leads him out, from his temple, and the statues of all the gods, on their carts, and their litters, carried by priests, go out, along the Processional Way, between the walls, with the blue bricks, and the golden lions, and through the Ishtar Gate, with music, of drums, and harps, and lyres, and pipes, and singing, and incense, and the whole population of Babylon, crowding along the road, and on the walls, and the roofs, to watch.

[long-pause]

They go out, to the river, and by boat, to a special temple, outside the city, in a garden, the house of the Akitu festival, where the gods stay, for a few days, and there are ceremonies, and feasts.

[pause]

And then, the gods come back, into the city, in procession, again, through the Ishtar Gate, and along the Processional Way, to Esagila. And the gods meet, together, to decide the fate of the land, for the coming year. And then, they go home, to their own cities, in their boats.

[pause]

{img:babylon-festival-night-lamps-rooftops-music-families-celebrating}
All through the festival, the city celebrates. There are feasts, and music, and dancing, in the streets, and the people wear their best clothes, and visit their families, and give each other gifts. The temples give out food, and drink, to the people. And at night, the lamps burn, on the roofs, and the walls, and the city glows, and the music, and the singing, go on, until late into the night.

[long-pause]

And when it is all over, the new year has begun, the world has been renewed, the king has been confirmed, and the gods have decided the fate of the land. And the people go back to their homes, and their fields, and their work, and the barley is harvested, and life goes on.

[long-pause]

{img:babylonian-household-shrine-clay-figurines-lamp-offering-dates-evening}
The world of the Babylonians is full of gods.

[pause]

There are the great gods, of the heavens, and the earth, and the waters: Anu, the old god of the sky, and Enlil, the lord of the wind, and Ea, the clever, kind god of the sweet waters, under the earth, and of wisdom, and magic, who loves people, and helps them. There is Shamash, the sun god, who sees everything, as he crosses the sky, every day, and is the god of justice, and truth. There is Sin, the moon god, with his silver crescent, and Adad, the god of storms, and rain, with his lightning.

[long-pause]

There is Ishtar, the goddess of love, and war, the morning, and evening, star, beautiful, and fierce, and many other goddesses: Gula, the goddess of healing, with her dog, and Ninlil, and Nisaba, and Ninkasi, the goddess of beer.

[pause]

And above them all, in Babylon, there is Marduk, the king of the gods, and his son, Nabu, the god of writing, and wisdom.

[pause]

{img:temple-of-ishtar-babylon-courtyard-worshippers-raising-hands-prayer}
Every city has its own god, and its own temple. And in Babylon, there are dozens of temples, large, and small, all over the city, for all the gods, and hundreds of little shrines, at the street corners, and in the walls, where people stop, and leave a little offering, of flour, or dates, or a little lamp, and say a prayer.

[long-pause]

And every family has its own god, too: a personal god, or goddess, who watches over them, and protects them, and speaks for them, to the great gods. In their houses, in a corner, or a niche, in the wall, they keep little figures, of clay, of their god, and of protective spirits, and they make offerings, every day, and pray.

[pause]

The Babylonians believe that the gods send messages, and signs, everywhere: in the stars, and the clouds, and the flight of birds, and the behaviour of animals, and the patterns of oil, poured on water, and the smoke of incense, and dreams. And there are special priests, diviners, who know how to read these signs, and tell what the gods want, and what the future will bring.

[pause]

{img:diviner-priest-reading-oil-on-water-bowl-client-temple-room}
They also believe in spirits, good, and bad, all around them, in the desert, and the wind, and the dark. And so they wear little amulets, around their necks, and bury little figures, of protective spirits, under the floors, and the doorways, of their houses, to keep the bad spirits away. Great winged bulls, and lions, with human heads, the lamassu, stand at the gates of the palaces, to protect them.

[long-pause]

When they are ill, they go to two kinds of healer: the asu, who is like a doctor, and uses medicines, made from plants, and minerals, and bandages, and ointments, and the ashipu, who is like a priest, or a magician, and uses prayers, and spells, and rituals, to drive away the spirit, or the anger of the god, that has caused the illness. Often, they go to both.

[pause]

The Babylonians do not believe in a happy heaven, after death. They believe that the dead go down, to a grey, quiet land, under the earth, where they live, a shadowy life, and depend on their families, to remember them, and give them food, and water. So the family remembers its ancestors, and speaks their names, and gives them offerings, every month, so that they will be at peace.

[pause]

{img:babylonian-family-offering-ancestors-water-bread-floor-lamp-courtyard}
And in the evening, in many houses of Babylon, the mother, or the father, lights a little lamp, before the family's god, in the corner of the room, and pours a little water, and leaves a few dates, and a little bread, and says a quiet prayer, for the family, and the children, and the ancestors, and the house, and the night.

[long-pause]

{img:hanging-gardens-babylon-terraces-trees-flowers-waterfalls-palace-imagined}
Of all the wonders of Babylon, the most famous is one that may never have existed: the Hanging Gardens.

[pause]

The Greek, and Roman, writers, centuries later, told a beautiful story. They said that King Nebuchadnezzar had a wife, a princess, called Amytis, from the land of the Medes, in the mountains of Iran, to the north-east, a green land, of mountains, and forests, and streams.

[long-pause]

When she came to Babylon, to marry the king, she was homesick, in the flat, hot, brown land, and missed the green hills, and the trees, of her homeland. And so, the king built her a mountain, in the middle of his city: a great garden, rising up, in terraces, one above another, like a green hill, planted with trees, and flowers, and plants, from all over the empire, and watered by machines, which lifted the water, from the river, up to the top, so that it could flow down, through the terraces, in streams, and waterfalls.

[pause]

From below, the trees seemed to hang in the air, above the city. And so they were called the Hanging Gardens of Babylon, and the Greeks counted them, among the Seven Wonders of the World, with the Pyramids of Egypt, and the great statue of Zeus, at Olympia, and the others.

[pause]

{img:terraced-garden-palace-babylon-cedar-trees-pomegranates-queen-walking}
It is a lovely story. But there is a mystery.

[long-pause]

The Babylonians themselves, who wrote down so much, on their clay tablets, and in their inscriptions, never mention the Hanging Gardens. Nebuchadnezzar, who wrote so proudly about all his buildings, never says a word about them. And the archaeologists, who have dug in Babylon, for more than a hundred years, have never found them, for certain.

[pause]

Some scholars think that the gardens were real, but have simply not been found, or were near the river, where everything has been washed away. Some think they were in Nebuchadnezzar's palace, where archaeologists found a strange building, with vaults, and wells, which might have been part of them. Others think that the story is about another city, Nineveh, in the north, the capital of the Assyrians, where King Sennacherib, about a hundred years before, built a famous garden, on a hill, watered by great canals, and aqueducts, and machines, and that later writers mixed up the two cities.

[pause]

{img:assyrian-garden-relief-style-trees-aqueduct-hillside-water-channels}
And others think that the gardens were never really there at all, but were a dream, a legend, of a beautiful, green, impossible garden, in the desert.

[long-pause]

But there were certainly gardens in Babylon. The kings, and the nobles, and the temples, had gardens, and orchards, and parks, with palms, and fruit trees, and flowers, and pools, and the Babylonians loved them. And perhaps, somewhere, in the palace, beside the river, there was a garden, on a terrace, high above the city, where a homesick queen could walk, in the evening, in the shade of the trees, and smell the flowers, and look out, over the walls, toward the north-east, where her mountains were.

[pause]

So we will leave the Hanging Gardens, as a beautiful mystery, floating, green, and cool, above the city, in the imagination of the world, as they have for more than two thousand years.

[long-pause]

{img:judean-exiles-sitting-riverbank-willows-harps-babylon-canal-evening}
In these years, among the many peoples who come to Babylon, there are some who do not come of their own free will.

[pause]

In the year 597 BC, and again, ten years later, in 587 BC, Nebuchadnezzar's armies went to the little kingdom of Judah, far to the west, in the hills, beyond the desert, with its capital, Jerusalem, which had refused to obey him. The city was taken, and the temple of Jerusalem was destroyed, and the king of Judah, and many of its people, the nobles, and the priests, and the craftsmen, and their families, were brought, a long way, across the desert, to Babylon, to live in exile.

[long-pause]

We will leave the sad days of the war, and the long journey, there, gently, in the past, and think of the people, and their new lives, in a strange land.

[pause]

The exiles are not kept in prisons. They are settled, in villages, and towns, in the countryside, around Babylon, beside the canals, and given land, to farm, and houses, to live in. Some of them live in the city itself. They can work, and farm, and trade, and marry, and build houses, and plant gardens, and keep their own customs, and their own faith.

[pause]

{img:clay-ration-tablet-cuneiform-king-jehoiachin-palace-storeroom}
The king of Judah, Jehoiachin, lives at the court of Babylon, with his family, and his servants. Archaeologists have found clay tablets, in the storerooms of Nebuchadnezzar's palace, which list the rations of oil, and barley, given to him, and his five sons, and the other foreigners, at the court. His name is written there, in cuneiform: Ya'ukin, king of the land of Yahud.

[long-pause]

And there are other tablets, found more recently, from a little town, in the countryside, near Babylon, called Al-Yahudu, which means the town of Judah, where the exiles lived, and farmed, and paid their taxes, and made their contracts, and their names, Hebrew names, appear in the tablets, for several generations: fathers, and sons, and grandsons, living, and working, and raising their families, beside the canals of Babylon.

[pause]

But many of the exiles are homesick, for their own land, and their hills, and their city, and their temple. And they remember it, and sing about it. One of the most famous songs, of the Bible, Psalm 137, begins:

[pause]

{img:willow-trees-babylon-canal-quiet-dusk-lyres-hanging-branches}
By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion. We hanged our harps upon the willows in the midst thereof.

[long-pause]

And it was in Babylon, during the exile, that many of the exiles, and their teachers, and priests, gathered together, and wrote down, and collected, and copied, and studied, the old writings, and the laws, and the stories, and the prophecies, of their people, so that they would not be forgotten, far from home. And much of what became the Hebrew Bible was shaped, during these years, beside the rivers of Babylon.

[pause]

The book of Daniel tells stories of young men of Judah, at the court of Nebuchadnezzar: Daniel, and his three friends, Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, who were taught the language, and the writing, of the Babylonians, and served the king, wisely, but stayed faithful to their own God.

[pause]

{img:exile-family-courtyard-babylon-lamp-reading-scroll-children-listening}
Fifty years later, when the Persians come, and take Babylon, the Persian king, Cyrus, will allow the exiles to go home, to Jerusalem, and rebuild their temple. Many of them go. But many stay, in Babylon, where they have been born, and grown up, and made their homes, and their lives.

[long-pause]

And for more than two and a half thousand years, until our own times, there was a great, and learned, Jewish community, in Babylon, and Mesopotamia, which became one of the most important centres of Jewish learning, in the whole world, and wrote, many centuries later, the great book of law, and teaching, called the Babylonian Talmud.

[long-pause]

{img:brickmakers-babylon-wooden-moulds-mud-straw-rows-drying-sun}
All through Babylon, in the workshops, and the yards, and the streets, the craftsmen are at work.

[pause]

The most important of all, in these years, are the brick makers. Babylon is built of bricks: millions of them, for the walls, and the temples, and the palaces, and the houses, and the streets, and the quays.

[long-pause]

Outside the city, by the river, and the canals, where there is plenty of clay, and water, the brick makers work, in great yards. They mix the clay, with water, and chopped straw, with their feet, into a thick mud, and press it into wooden moulds, square, about thirty centimetres across, and turn them out, in long rows, on the ground, to dry, in the sun. In the summer heat, they dry in a few days.

[pause]

Most bricks are simply dried in the sun. But the best ones, for the outer walls, and the floors, and the places that must resist water, are baked, in great kilns, burning reeds, and palm wood, and they come out hard, and yellow, and red. And for the most important buildings, they are stamped, before they dry, with the name of the king.

[pause]

And then there are the glazers: the craftsmen who make the wonderful blue bricks, of the Ishtar Gate, and the Processional Way.

[long-pause]

It is very skilled, and very difficult work. First, the bricks are made in special moulds, so that each brick has a part of the animal, a leg, or a mane, or a tail, standing out from the surface, and when all the bricks are laid together, in the right order, the whole animal appears. The craftsmen mark each brick, with a little sign, so that they know where it goes.

[pause]

Then, the glaze is painted on: a kind of liquid glass, made from sand, and plant ash, and minerals, coloured blue, with cobalt, and copper, and yellow, with antimony, and lead, and white, and black, and brown. The colours are separated by thin lines, of a black, glassy paste, so that they do not run together. And then the bricks are fired, in the kiln, again, and the glaze melts, and becomes hard, and shining, like glass.

[pause]

{img:weaver-babylon-loom-dyed-wool-red-blue-patterns-workshop-light}
There are weavers, men, and women, working at their looms, in the temples, and the houses, weaving fine wool, and linen, and dyers, dipping the wool, in great vats, of red, and blue, and purple, and yellow, and embroiderers, and tailors.

[long-pause]

There are metalworkers: goldsmiths, and silversmiths, making jewellery, and cups, and statues, for the temples, and the court, and coppersmiths, and blacksmiths, making tools, and weapons, and pots. There are carpenters, and boat builders, and reed workers, weaving baskets, and mats, and boats. There are potters, at their wheels, and leather workers, and perfume makers, mixing scented oils, of cedar, and myrrh, and stone carvers, making seals.

[pause]

The seals are little cylinders, of stone, about the size of a finger, carved with tiny pictures, of gods, and animals, and people, and inscriptions, and when they are rolled across the wet clay, of a tablet, or a jar stopper, they leave a little picture, in the clay, like a signature. Everyone of importance in Babylon has a seal, and wears it, on a string, around the neck, or the wrist.

[pause]

{img:cylinder-seal-rolled-on-clay-impression-tiny-figures-close-detail}
Many of the craftsmen work for the temples, and the palace, and are paid in barley, and dates, and wool, and oil, and silver. Others have their own workshops, and sell what they make, in the streets, and at the gates. And the same crafts are often passed down, from father to son, and mother to daughter, for generations, in the same families, in the same streets, of Babylon.

[long-pause]

{img:persian-army-cyrus-entering-babylon-gate-peaceful-crowds-539bc}
Nebuchadnezzar rules for forty-three years, and dies, an old man, in 562 BC.

[pause]

After him, Babylon has several kings, in a short time, and then, the last king of Babylon, Nabonidus, an unusual, scholarly man, who loves the moon god, Sin, more than Marduk, and spends ten years, far away, in the deserts of Arabia, at the oasis of Tayma, leaving his son, Belshazzar, to rule, in Babylon.

[long-pause]

And in the year 539 BC, sixty-one years after our story, Cyrus the Great, the king of the Persians, comes, with his army, from the east. And Babylon is taken, quickly, and almost without a fight. The Persian soldiers enter the city, and the people of Babylon, it is said, welcome Cyrus, with green branches, and he enters the city, in peace, and honours Marduk, and the temples, and lets the people live, as before.

[pause]

On a clay cylinder, now in the British Museum, the Cyrus Cylinder, Cyrus tells how Marduk chose him, to be king of Babylon, and how he let the people, who had been brought to Babylon, from other lands, go home, with their gods.

[pause]

{img:alexander-the-great-babylon-palace-323bc-quiet-evening-columns}
Babylon becomes one of the capitals of the Persian Empire, rich, and great, for two hundred years. Then, in 331 BC, Alexander the Great comes, and takes it, from the Persians, and plans to make it the capital of his own great empire, stretching from Greece to India. But in 323 BC, he dies, in Babylon, in the palace of Nebuchadnezzar, of a fever, at the age of only thirty-two.

[long-pause]

After Alexander, his generals found new cities, and slowly, the people move away from Babylon. The old temples are still kept, and the astronomers still watch the sky, and write their diaries, on clay, for another two hundred years. The last cuneiform tablet that we know of, an astronomical almanac, was written in Babylonia, in about 75 AD, and after that, the old writing was forgotten.

[pause]

And slowly, over the centuries, Babylon fades. The river changes its course. The canals silt up. The great buildings crumble. The people take the bricks away, to build new houses, in new villages, and towns. And the great city becomes a field of ruins, and mounds, and broken bricks, in the desert, beside the river.

[pause]

{img:ruined-mounds-babylon-desert-broken-bricks-palms-medieval-traveller}
Travellers came, now and then, over the centuries, and looked at the mounds, and wondered if this could really be the great Babylon, of the Bible, and the old stories. And the local people dug in the ruins, for bricks, and found the stamped bricks, of Nebuchadnezzar, and used them, in their houses, and their walls, and their canals, so that the king's name was built into houses, and dams, all around the region.

[long-pause]

But Babylon was not forgotten. Its name lived on, in the Bible, and in the writings of the Greeks, and the Romans, and in poems, and stories, and paintings, and songs, all over the world, for two thousand years: a name of wonder, and splendour, and mystery, waiting, under the earth, to be found again.

[long-pause]

{img:koldewey-excavation-babylon-1900-workers-baskets-railway-ishtar-gate-emerging}
In the year 1899, a German archaeologist, called Robert Koldewey, came to Babylon.

[pause]

He was an architect, and a careful, patient man, with a big moustache, and a love of old buildings, and he had been sent, by the German Oriental Society, to dig in the ruins of Babylon. He stayed for nearly eighteen years, until 1917.

[long-pause]

With hundreds of local workers, he dug, carefully, layer by layer, through the mounds of earth, and broken bricks, using a little railway, with small wagons, to carry the earth away. He uncovered the great walls, and the gates, and the palaces, and the temples, and the streets, and the houses, of the city of Nebuchadnezzar, and mapped them, and drew them, and photographed them.

[pause]

And one day, in the north of the city, his workers began to find pieces of glazed brick, blue, and yellow, and white, thousands of them, broken, and scattered, in the earth. And then, deeper down, still standing, the foundations of a great gate, with the moulded animals, the bulls, and the dragons, still in place, in rows, on the walls, buried, for more than two thousand years. It was the Ishtar Gate.

[pause]

{img:museum-workers-reassembling-glazed-brick-fragments-tables-berlin-1920s}
The thousands of broken pieces of glazed brick were packed, carefully, into crates, and sent, by ship, to Berlin. And there, over many years, in the 1920s, and 1930s, the museum workers sorted them, and cleaned them, and fitted them together, like a great jigsaw puzzle, with hundreds of thousands of pieces, and rebuilt the front of the Ishtar Gate, and part of the Processional Way, with its lions, in the Pergamon Museum, in Berlin.

[long-pause]

It was opened to the public, in 1930, and since then, millions of people have walked through it, and stood, beneath the great blue gate, with its golden animals, and felt, for a moment, as if they were in Babylon.

[pause]

Other archaeologists, and scholars, from many countries, and from Iraq itself, have dug in Babylon, and studied it, since then. And the clay tablets found there, and in the other cities of Mesopotamia, have been read, and translated, so that we know the names of the kings, and the scribes, and the merchants, and the astronomers, and the ordinary people of Babylon, and their business, and their prayers, and their lives.

[pause]

{img:scholar-museum-reading-cuneiform-tablet-magnifying-glass-lamp}
And the Babylonian astronomical diaries, and the mathematical tablets, and the letters, and the stories, are still being read, and studied, today, in universities, and museums, all over the world, and every year, new tablets are read, and new things are learned.

[long-pause]

{img:babylon-ruins-today-iraq-reconstructed-walls-palms-euphrates-sunset}
Today, Babylon lies in Iraq, about eighty-five kilometres south of Baghdad, near the town of Hillah, beside the river Euphrates.

[pause]

In 2019, it became a World Heritage Site, protected, as one of the most important places in the history of the world.

[long-pause]

Visitors can walk through the ruins, along the old streets, between the walls, of mud brick, some of them still standing, and some rebuilt, in modern times. They can see the foundations of the Ishtar Gate, with the moulded bulls, and dragons, still in place, in the walls, unglazed, below the level where the blue bricks were, deep in the ground, where Koldewey found them. They can walk along part of the Processional Way, and see the great stone lion of Babylon, carved of black basalt, standing over a fallen man, very old, and mysterious, which has stood, in the ruins, for many centuries.

[pause]

Beside the site, there is a smaller copy of the Ishtar Gate, painted blue, made in modern times, at the entrance, which visitors pass through, and photograph.

[pause]

Around the ruins, there are palm groves, and fields, and canals, and villages, and the river Euphrates flows past, slowly, as it did in the time of Nebuchadnezzar, though it has moved a little, over the centuries, to the west.

[long-pause]

And in the museums of the world, Babylon lives on: in Berlin, where the Ishtar Gate rises, blue, and gold, under the museum roof, and in Baghdad, in the Iraq Museum, and in London, and Paris, and New York, and Chicago, and Istanbul, where the lions of the Processional Way, and the tablets of the scribes, and the bricks of Nebuchadnezzar, are shown, and studied, and admired.

[pause]

And Babylon lives on, in our hours, and minutes, and degrees, and in the names of the stars, and the zodiac, and in the stories of the Bible, and in our language, and our imagination, as a name of wonder, and splendour, and mystery.

[pause]

{img:euphrates-palm-groves-evening-today-iraq-river-calm-reflections}
So we leave Babylon, tonight, in the year 600 BC, as the evening falls.

[long-pause]

The sun is setting, behind the palm groves, on the west bank of the river, and the great tower glows, red, and gold, in the last light. The blue bricks of the Ishtar Gate are turning deep, dark blue, and the golden lions, on the walls of the Processional Way, are fading into the dusk. On the roofs of the houses, the families are spreading their mats, and lighting their little lamps, and the children are lying down, to sleep, under the open sky. On the roof of the temple, an old astronomer is settling down, with his tablet, and his reed, to watch the moon rise, over the river. And the stars are coming out, one by one, over the great city, and the palm trees, and the quiet, flowing water.
