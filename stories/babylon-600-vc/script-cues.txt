{img:01-mesopotamia-plain-two-rivers}

Most of us carry a picture of Babylon in our heads. A wicked city of towering walls and gardens hanging in the sky, where everything was enormous, golden and slightly dangerous. It is a wonderful picture. But it was painted mostly by storytellers who lived long after the city's great days, and who never saw it with their own eyes. The real Babylon was quieter than that, and in many ways more surprising. A city of mud brick and date palms, of schoolboys and brewers and star watchers, where families slept on their rooftops through the hot summer nights.

[pause]

So why was this one city, on a flat brown plain beside a slow river, the greatest city on earth in the days of King Nebuchadnezzar? Why did the whole ancient world know its name, and why do we still say it today? Come with me, and we will walk in through the gate and find out, one small wonder at a time.

[pause]

Just before we pass under the gate, one small thing. If slow evenings like this are a comfort to you, you are welcome to subscribe and keep them close at hand. And if you would rather simply listen, that is a perfectly good way to spend the night.

[pause]

I often wonder where you are listening from. Which country, which town, and how far into the night has it gone where you are? A few words in the comments would make me smile, because I like picturing all those small lit windows.

[pause]

Now let your eyes grow heavy. Loosen your jaw, and let your hands lie open on the blanket. Breathe in slowly, and out more slowly still. The morning mist is lifting from the river, and the road to the city lies ahead.

[long-pause]

The word Mesopotamia simply means "the land between the rivers."

[pause]

Babylon stood in a country the Greeks later called Mesopotamia, which means exactly that. The two rivers were the Tigris and the Euphrates. Both began in the mountains far to the north and flowed south for more than a thousand kilometres. Near Babylon they came quite close together before wandering on to the sea. The land between them was flat and brown, with rich soil that the rivers had carried down over thousands of years. There was one great problem. It hardly ever rained. The summers were long, hot and dry, and without the rivers, nothing at all would have grown. So the people who lived here learned to work with the water instead of waiting for the sky. Today most of this land lies in the country of Iraq. But in the time of Nebuchadnezzar, it was the busiest, most crowded and most cultured corner of the known world. Everything that made Babylon possible began with two muddy rivers.

[long-pause]

{img:02-irrigation-canals-palm-groves}

Babylon's farmers turned a desert into a garden by digging channels.

[pause]

The secret of the plain was the canal. A canal is simply a channel that leads river water away to fields and gardens, and the people of Mesopotamia had been digging them for thousands of years. Around Babylon, a great web of them spread in every direction. The biggest were as wide as rivers, and boats sailed along them. The smallest were little ditches between fields, with tiny gates of earth and reeds that a farmer could open and close by hand. Wherever the water reached, the land was green, with barley, wheat, vegetables, and forests of date palms. Wherever it did not, there was only dust. Yet canals needed constant care. The rivers carried so much mud that the channels slowly filled up, and every year they had to be dug out again. A neglected canal meant dry fields at the far end, and an empty harvest. Keeping the water flowing was a duty shared by every family, and by the king.

[long-pause]

{img:03-ancient-sumerian-ruins-ziggurat}

By Nebuchadnezzar's time, the first cities in the world were already ancient history.

[pause]

More than two thousand years before our story, in this same southern land, a people called the Sumerians built the first cities anywhere. They raised temples and walls and chose kings. They pressed the first writing into wet clay with the end of a reed. They made wheeled carts, ploughs and sailing boats, and they wrote down the first great poems. By the year 600 BC, the Sumerians themselves were long gone, and many of their cities lay half buried in sand. Yet the Babylonians had not forgotten them. Scribes still copied the old Sumerian texts, and priests still sang the old prayers in the old tongue, a little the way Latin lived on in churches in later times. To a Babylonian child, the past was not a distant thing. It was a deep, respected layer beneath every street, and Babylon felt like the newest page in a very long book.

[long-pause]

{img:04-southern-marsh-reed-houses-canoe}

In the far south, people lived in houses made of living reeds, much as their ancestors had.

[pause]

If you followed the Euphrates all the way down towards the sea, the river would spread out into a great wetland. There, beyond the last villages, lay tall reed marshes full of birds, fish and still, silvery water. People lived in the marshes in houses woven from bundles of giant reeds, bent into graceful arches like the ribs of a ship. They travelled in slim canoes and kept water buffalo, and they caught fish and wildfowl for dinner. Their way of life was already old when the first cities were built, and remarkably, a similar one continued in the same region for thousands of years. For a Babylonian townsman, the marshes were a wild, green, slightly mysterious place at the bottom of the map. But they were also part of the same great country. The reeds fed the city's boats, mats and baskets, and a little of the marsh went home with every trader.

[long-pause]

{img:05-babylon-old-capital-hammurabi-era}

Babylon was already more than fifteen hundred years old when Nebuchadnezzar made it great.

[pause]

The city was not new. It had stood on the Euphrates for well over a thousand years, and long ago it had been the capital of a famous king called Hammurabi. Since then, Babylon had risen and fallen many times. It had been conquered, burned, rebuilt and conquered again. There had been long years when it was only one town among many, and years when a fierce empire from the north held it in an iron grip. Yet each time, the city came back. People loved it, and the god Marduk was thought to live there. Then, in the lifetime of Nebuchadnezzar's father, the northern empire collapsed, and Babylon was free once more. The new family of kings made an extraordinary decision. They would not simply rule the old city. They would rebuild it, from the walls to the gates to the holy road, and make it grander than it had ever been.

[long-pause]

{img:06-traveller-boat-euphrates-morning}

The best way to reach Babylon was by boat, drifting down the river for many days.

[pause]

Imagine you are a traveller coming from the northern mountains with a cargo of wine and timber. You would load it onto a boat or a raft and let the Euphrates carry you south. The journey would take days. You would sleep on deck under the stars and watch the land slide by. There would be steep banks, mud-brick villages, groves of palms, and flocks of sheep and goats coming down to drink. Boys fishing from the shore would wave as you passed. The farther south you went, the flatter and greener the land became. Canals branched away on both sides. The villages grew closer and larger, and the river became busier with rafts, barges and small round boats turning slowly in the current. It was a long, calm, drifting approach, and it gave travellers plenty of time to wonder what waited at the end. Nothing they had seen before could quite prepare them.

[long-pause]

{img:07-first-sight-of-tower-haze}

One morning, far across the plain, travellers saw a blue light shining on the horizon.

[pause]

Nobody forgot the first sight of Babylon. On a clear morning, the boatman would point ahead, and there, rising from the haze above the palms, you would see a great stepped tower. Its top shone blue in the early sunshine, a tiny gleaming spark in a flat world with no hills. It could be seen from many miles away. As the boat drifted closer, the walls appeared, long and brown, stretching away along the water, with hundreds of towers marching beside them. Smoke rose from the chimneys of the city. Behind the walls you could make out temple roofs and the green tops of gardens. The boat would come to rest at a busy quay outside the gate, among dozens of others, where porters carried sacks up the steps, scribes counted every load on clay, and everyone shouted in a different language. Then you would walk inside, and the noise would swallow you.

[long-pause]

{img:08-king-nebuchadnezzar-portrait-throne}

The boats are tied up, and the gate stands open. But before we walk the streets, it helps to know who gave the city its shape. A single king stood behind almost everything we are about to see.

[pause]

Nebuchadnezzar's name means "O god Nabu, protect my son."

[pause]

In the year 600 BC, the king of Babylon was Nebuchadnezzar, a man in his thirties who had already ruled for several years. His name was really a short prayer, asking the god of writing and wisdom to keep his child safe. His father had freed Babylon from the northern empire that held it, and the young prince had commanded armies even before he was crowned. He would go on to rule for forty-three years, one of the longest reigns in the history of the city. He appears in the Bible as a proud and powerful king, and the Greeks remembered him too. But in his own words, he saw himself as something rather different from a conqueror. He wanted people to remember a king who looked after the gods and made his city beautiful. To see whether he succeeded, we need only look at what he left in the ground.

[long-pause]

{img:09-king-inspecting-building-works-scribes}

Nebuchadnezzar rarely wrote about his battles. He wrote about his buildings.

[pause]

The king had long inscriptions made on clay cylinders, stone slabs and bricks, and he buried many of them beneath his construction work for future kings to find. What do they say? Mostly, they list buildings. Temples he restored for the gods. Walls he strengthened. Canals he dug. Palaces he raised. He describes laying the foundations, bringing cedar from distant mountains, and covering gates with bronze and doorposts with silver. You rarely read about triumphs or enemies. Instead, you read about a man who wanted to be remembered as a faithful builder, a servant of Marduk and a friend of his city. This may be partly good public relations, as all kings have a taste for that. But the bricks agree with him. During his long reign, he rebuilt almost all of Babylon, and the dust and noise of his construction sites must have been part of daily life for forty years.

[long-pause]

{img:10-stamped-bricks-king-name-closeup}

Millions of bricks were stamped with the king's name before they were even baked.

[pause]

To build a city of this size, you need an enormous number of bricks. Brick makers worked in huge yards beside the river, and on many bricks meant for royal buildings, they pressed a stamp into the wet clay. It said, in neat wedge-shaped writing, that the brick belonged to Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon, son of Nabopolassar, king of Babylon. Imagine the work. Every wall, every pavement and every temple was covered in tiny repeating signatures. Today archaeologists have found these stamped bricks by the thousand in the ruins, and they now sit in museums around the world. Local villagers also dug them out of the mounds for centuries and used them to build their own houses, so the name of the king can still be found in walls far from Babylon. He hoped that his name would last forever. In a small and slightly humbling way, it did, one brick at a time.

[long-pause]

{img:11-southern-palace-courtyards-babylon}

The royal palace was like a small city inside the city, with five great courtyards in a row.

[pause]

Near the river and the northern gate stood the Southern Palace, the king's main home and office. Visitors walked through one huge courtyard after another, each more impressive than the last. Around the courtyards were halls, offices, kitchens and storerooms, where officials and servants kept the machinery of an empire turning. There were rooms for the royal family, for the guards, for the treasury and for the great archives. Here the king's scribes wrote his letters and orders, and kept the accounts of the whole kingdom on baskets of clay tablets. Barley, oil and dates were counted into the stores here. Gold and silver were weighed here. The palace was not simply a house with a crown on top. It was the working heart of an empire that stretched from the sea in the south to the mountains in the north, and west almost to the border of Egypt. It never completely slept.

[long-pause]

{img:12-throne-room-blue-facade-lions}

The throne room had a front wall of shining blue bricks and a parade of golden lions.

[pause]

At the heart of the palace lay the throne room, a long hall more than fifty metres from end to end, with three wide doorways opening onto the courtyard. Its outer wall glowed with colour. On a deep blue background, tall slender columns rose like palm trees, topped with bands of flowers, while beneath them a row of lions walked in white and gold. Visitors who entered the courtyard on a sunny morning must have been dazzled. Inside, opposite the main doorway, there was a deep niche in the wall, and in that niche stood the royal throne. From there the king could receive officials, priests and guests from across his empire, framed by the shadows of the room and seen from the far end of the hall. Much of this shining facade survived in pieces. Archaeologists found fragments of it in the ruins, and a careful reconstruction still shows how bright it must have been.

[long-pause]

{img:13-ambassadors-bowing-tribute-gifts}

Ambassadors had to walk through five courtyards before they could even see the king.

[pause]

Imagine arriving at the palace as an envoy from Egypt or from one of the coastal cities, carrying gifts and a letter. You would be led through the courtyards one by one, with every gateway taller and every wall more splendid than the last. By the time you reached the final hall, you would feel very small. The king would be sitting at the far end, in the shadows, wearing rich robes and a tall crown, with his officials and guards standing around him. Everything was designed to impress, and it worked. Messengers bowed low, spoke carefully and chose their words with great attention, because a single careless sentence could make a king angry. Diplomacy in those days was done in person, with gifts, flattery and enormous patience. And all of it was written down by scribes on clay, so the king could remember exactly what had been promised, by whom and when.

[long-pause]

{img:14-palace-collection-old-statues-hall}

The kings of Babylon collected antiques, and one palace held what has been called an early museum.

[pause]

Nebuchadnezzar loved old things. In one of his palaces, archaeologists found a collection of statues, carved stones and inscribed objects that were already ancient in his day. Some came from other lands, taken as spoils of war or received as gifts. Some were pieces of Babylon's own earlier past. A statue from a far-off country, a stone with writing in a forgotten style, a carved piece that had probably belonged to an earlier king. There is no way to be sure that the objects were arranged for visitors to admire, but many scholars believe this was a kind of royal collection, with the past on display. It fits the character of the time. The kings of Babylon repaired old temples, copied old writings and searched for the foundation records of their ancestors. They respected the people who came before them, and they wanted to understand them. A king who collects history is also a king who knows he will one day be part of it.

[long-pause]

{img:15-palace-roof-walk-evening-city}

When the day's business ended, the great palace fell silent, and lamps were lit one by one.

[pause]

In the evening, the visitors went to their rooms, the scribes walked home through the cooling streets, and the courtyards emptied. Small clay lamps burning sesame oil were lit along the corridors. The king might have taken a quiet walk along the high walls above the river, in the breeze. From there he could see the tower and the temples dark against the sunset, the river turning gold and red, and the lamps coming on in thousands of houses. Beyond the walls, the date palms stretched out towards the edge of the world. It is nice to imagine him pausing there for a moment. A man who had armies, tribute and a palace the size of a town, but who still stopped to look at the evening light on the water. Like everyone in Babylon, he lived under the same sky, and the same cool breeze reached him as well.

[long-pause]

{img:16-double-walls-towers-moat}

The palace is quiet now. Let us step outside, because there is something enormous that all the city's visitors passed through first, and none of them ever forgot it.

[pause]

Babylon was protected by two enormous walls, with a moat of river water in front.

[pause]

The inner city was almost square, and the Euphrates ran straight through the middle. Around it ran a double wall of mud brick, with a deep ditch full of river water in front of it. The inner wall was about six metres thick, and the outer one nearly four. Between them was a wide space, and every twenty metres or so along the whole length, a tower stood up above the line. There were hundreds of them. Beyond all this, an even larger outer wall formed a great triangle many kilometres around, enclosing gardens, fields and palm groves as well as the city. A besieging army would have to cross the moat, get through two walls under fire from hundreds of towers, and then face the streets. For centuries it was one of the strongest defences in the ancient world. People inside could sleep soundly. Or at least they hoped so.

[long-pause]

{img:17-walls-chariot-top-herodotus}

One Greek visitor claimed that a four-horse chariot could turn around on top of the walls.

[pause]

Later in history, a Greek traveller named Herodotus wrote about Babylon, and he described the walls with real astonishment. According to him, they were so wide that a four-horse chariot could drive along the top and even turn around between the little guard houses. He also gave exact figures for their height, and these are almost certainly too large. Herodotus was a wonderful storyteller, but he heard many of his facts from guides and passing talkers, and he sometimes repeated what they said without checking. Still, a kernel of truth lies behind his amazement. The walls really were remarkably broad, and wide enough for guards to patrol and for soldiers to move around quickly. The stories grew taller each time they were told. Most of us do the same, when we describe a place that impressed us. Babylon was not an exaggeration, but it did invite one.

[long-pause]

{img:18-eight-city-gates-god-names}

Each of Babylon's eight great gates was named after a god.

[pause]

Eight gates opened through the city walls, and none of them was just a hole in a wall. Each was a tall double gateway with towers, a long passage and heavy doors of cedar covered in bronze. Each had a name, and each name was a god. There was a gate of the sun god, a gate of the storm god, a gate of the moon god and a gate of the king of the gods himself. Passing through one was a small religious act, even if you were only a farmer bringing onions to the market. Guards stood on the towers all day and all night. At sunset the doors were closed and barred, and the gates were sealed against the dark. Inside the gate, the long passage was cool and dim, and people paused to let their eyes adjust. Then they emerged into the full noise of the city.

[long-pause]

{img:19-ishtar-gate-front-blue-towers}

The most beautiful gate in the ancient world was covered from top to bottom in shining blue.

[pause]

In the north of the city stood the Ishtar Gate, named for the goddess of love and the morning star. Nebuchadnezzar had it rebuilt more than once, each time higher and grander than before. Its two great towers framed a passage tall enough for the statues of the gods to roll through on their carts at festival time. And every surface was covered in glazed bricks of a deep, brilliant blue, as rich as lapis lazuli, the precious stone from the far mountains. On the blue, in long rows, walked animals made of moulded bricks that stood out from the wall. They shone in yellow, white and gold. Along the top ran battlements and bands of flowers. In the morning light, the whole gate glowed like a jewel. People who came to Babylon for the first time stopped in the road, just to stare. Some of them stood there for a long time.

[long-pause]

{img:20-dragon-and-bull-glazed-relief}

The animals on the gate were dragons, bulls and, above all, a mixture of several creatures.

[pause]

The Ishtar Gate carried hundreds of animals, arranged in rows. Some were bulls with long blue horns, which belonged to the storm god. The others were dragons that the Babylonians called by a name meaning something like "the fierce snake." These creatures had the head of a serpent, a lion's front legs, an eagle's back feet, and a long tail ending in a scorpion's sting. They belonged to Marduk, the great god of the city. They were shown walking in solemn lines, heads held high, as though marching towards the city to guard it. Their scales and horns were picked out in yellow and white against the blue. It may sound a little fierce, but in the eyes of the Babylonians they were protectors. Strangers entering Babylon passed beneath hundreds of them, and the message was clear. This city was under watchful, powerful care.

[long-pause]

{img:21-glazed-bricks-moulds-craftsmen}

Every animal on the gate was assembled like a jigsaw puzzle from numbered bricks.

[pause]

The animals were not painted onto the wall. Each brick was pressed into a special mould, so that one part of the creature stood out from it. A single brick might carry a hoof, a piece of mane, or the curve of a tail. Craftsmen glazed the bricks in blue, yellow, white and brown, with thin dark lines to keep the colours from running together. The glaze was a type of coloured glass, made from sand, plant ash and minerals. Then everything was baked again, until the surface became hard and shiny. Each brick was marked on its side with a small sign, so the builders knew exactly where it belonged. When all of them were laid in the right order, the whole animal appeared on the wall. One brick in the wrong place, and the lion would have a tail on its head. It was clever work, and a good reminder of how much patience went into every wonder.

[long-pause]

{img:22-processional-way-lions-blue}

The holy road was lined with about a hundred and twenty striding lions.

[pause]

Beyond the Ishtar Gate, a broad straight street ran north and south through the city. It was about twenty metres wide, paved with big slabs of white and reddish stone, and walled on both sides with high, blue brick walls. On those walls, one after another, walked lions. There were about a hundred and twenty of them, sixty on each side, with white and golden bodies, open mouths and raised tails. The lion belonged to Ishtar, the goddess of the gate. They strode slowly and proudly towards the gate, and above and below them ran bands of flowers. Walking along the Processional Way must have felt like walking through a long, shining blue valley, with the lions keeping you company at every step. On ordinary days it was simply a busy street, full of donkeys, carts and traders. But a few days each year, it became something much grander.

[long-pause]

{img:23-gate-today-berlin-museum-hall}

You can still walk through a rebuilt part of the Ishtar Gate today, in a museum in Berlin.

[pause]

After the gate was buried by time, it was found again at the beginning of the twentieth century. Thousands of broken, glazed bricks were carefully packed into crates and sent by ship to Berlin. There, museum workers spent years cleaning, sorting and fitting them together, like a giant puzzle with hundreds of thousands of pieces. The result was the front of the Ishtar Gate, along with part of the Processional Way and its lions, reconstructed inside a huge hall. It opened to the public in 1930. Since then, millions of people have walked through it. Some pause quietly, as visitors must have done in the time of Nebuchadnezzar. The blue is a little different in the electric light than in the Mesopotamian sun, but it is the same blue, glazed in the same city. Other lions from the holy road are spread across museums in Paris, London and New York, still striding on their blue background.

[long-pause]

{img:24-etemenanki-ziggurat-seven-stages}

Beyond the gate, the street opens out, and the buildings grow taller. At the heart of the city stands the thing that all the travellers saw first from the river.

[pause]

The great tower of Babylon was probably as tall as a building of thirty floors.

[pause]

In the middle of the old city stood a ziggurat, a tower built in steps. Its name was Etemenanki, which means something like "the house that is the foundation of heaven and earth." It rose from a square base about ninety metres on each side, in stage after stage, each one smaller than the one below, like a staircase for giants. According to a clay tablet that has survived, it may have been about as tall as it was wide. For its time, it was the tallest building in the world. A wide triple stairway ran up the front, and at the very top stood a small temple for Marduk, covered in shining blue bricks, which glittered in the sun. People could see it across the flat plain from a long way off. Nothing else in all of Mesopotamia looked quite like it. Everyone who saw it felt a little smaller, and a little closer to the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:25-tower-of-babel-languages-market}

Many people believe this tower inspired the story of the Tower of Babel.

[pause]

In the Bible, there is a story about people who tried to build a tower to reach heaven. God confused their languages, so that they could no longer understand each other, and the builders scattered across the earth. "Babel" is simply the Hebrew form of the name Babylon. Many scholars think the story was inspired by this very tower, which the exiles from Judah saw rising above the city. And you really could hear many languages in the streets of Babylon. There was Babylonian, Aramaic and Hebrew, Egyptian and Greek and Persian, all mixed together in the markets and quays. A traveller might understand every third word of a conversation. So the idea of a tower and a confusion of tongues fits the city well. A story can begin with a real building, and grow from there into something that belongs to everyone.

[long-pause]

{img:26-priests-climbing-ziggurat-stars}

Only priests were allowed to climb to the top, and there they watched the stars.

[pause]

Why did the Babylonians build such a tower? They believed the gods lived in the heavens. The tower was a kind of bridge or stairway between the sky and the earth, by which the gods could come down to visit their city. The small temple at the top was a resting place for the god on his way. Ordinary people were never allowed up the stairs. Only priests climbed to the summit, where they made offerings and prayers high above the roofs. And at night, they did something else up there. They watched the sky, and wrote down what they saw. For a Babylonian priest, the top of the tower was both a place of worship and a good observatory, with a clear view across a flat plain in every direction. Few places in the ancient world had a finer seat beneath the stars.

[long-pause]

{img:27-ziggurat-base-today-reeds-water}

Almost nothing remains of the great tower, but we know its measurements from a clay tablet.

[pause]

Today, the tower is gone. Over the centuries, its bricks were taken away to build other things, and all that remains is the base, a square outline in the ground near the ruins, often filled with water and reeds. Yet we still know a good deal about how it looked. A Babylonian scribe wrote down its measurements on a clay tablet, and the tablet survived. Another account, by a Greek traveller, described its many levels, its winding stairway and the temple at the top. Together, they let us picture it quite clearly. The building does not stand, but its memory does. This is a pattern we will meet again and again in Babylon. The tall things fell, and the little clay things survived. A cheap tablet, forgotten in a pile of rubble, can outlast a tower that once touched the clouds.

[long-pause]

{img:28-esagila-temple-courtyard-priests}

Beside the tower stood the temple of Marduk, whose name means "the house whose top is high."

[pause]

Right next to the ziggurat lay Esagila, the greatest temple in the city. It was not one building but a group of them, arranged around courtyards, with thick walls, tall gateways and many rooms for priests, treasures and gifts. In the holiest room of all lived the god himself. Marduk was a statue of precious wood covered in gold, dressed in rich robes sewn with gold and jewels, and seated on a golden throne before a golden table. A Greek writer later claimed that all this gold added up to an enormous weight, and the number is probably exaggerated. But the Babylonians themselves wrote that the sanctuary shone like the sun. People stood in the courtyard and looked towards the dim, golden doorway, with their hands raised to their faces in prayer. Through the day, the smell of incense drifted over the walls and into the streets.

[long-pause]

{img:29-priests-feeding-god-golden-table}

Each day, priests woke the god, washed him, dressed him and served him two meals.

[pause]

The god was looked after like a great king living in his palace. In the morning, priests woke him with songs and prayers. They washed the statue and dressed it in fresh clothes. Twice a day, they served him a meal. There were loaves and cakes, meat and fish, fruit and dates, with beer and wine, all set out on the golden table with music and incense. When the god had finished, the food was shared among the priests and temple workers, so nothing went to waste. In the evening, the priests put the god to bed, closed the doors of his room and left a few lamps burning. The temple grew quiet, and the guards kept watch. It might sound strange to us. But to the Babylonians, it was a simple act of care. If the god was happy and well fed in his house, the city beyond would be safe.

[long-pause]

{img:30-creation-poem-recited-priests}

Every spring, the priests recited a poem about how Marduk made the world out of chaos.

[pause]

The Babylonians had a great poem about the beginning of everything. At first, it said, there was nothing but water and darkness. A mighty goddess of the salt sea ruled over this chaos. Then the young god Marduk rose up and defeated her. From her body, he made the sky and the earth. He set the stars and the moon in their places, to mark the months and the seasons. Then the other gods built Babylon for him, as his home. Marduk had once been only a small local god of a single city. As Babylon grew great, so did he, until he became the king of all the gods. The poem was recited every year at the New Year festival, and it explained why the city was so important. In the Babylonian way of thinking, Babylon was not just a place on a map. It was where the world had started.

[long-pause]

{img:31-temple-workshops-scribes-bakers}

The great temple was also a bank, a farm, a bakery and a school, with hundreds of workers.

[pause]

A visitor expecting only prayer would have been surprised. Esagila employed hundreds of people. There were priests and singers, musicians and star watchers, but also cooks, bakers, brewers, weavers, goldsmiths, gardeners and herdsmen. The temple owned fields, palm groves and flocks all around the city, and collected a share of their produce. It lent out grain and silver. Its scribes kept careful records of every sack and every sheep on baked clay tablets. In a society without banks or government offices as we know them, the temple filled many roles at once. It was a religious centre, a storehouse, an employer and a source of help in hard times. People brought gifts to the god and asked for healing, for children, for justice and for good luck. And then, on the way out, they might stop at the temple workshop and buy a loaf of bread for the walk home.

[long-pause]

{img:32-stone-bridge-boat-shaped-piers}

The incense drifts out over the walls and down towards the water. Follow it, and you will come to the river that gave Babylon its life.

[pause]

A great bridge on boat-shaped pillars joined the two halves of the city.

[pause]

The Euphrates flowed straight through Babylon and divided it in two. On the east bank lay the old city, with the great temples, the palace and the tower. On the west bank was the newer part, with its own streets, houses and gardens. To join them, the kings built one of the first great bridges in the ancient world. It was around a hundred and twenty metres long and rested on thick pillars of brick and stone. The pillars were shaped like boats, pointed at the front, so the current would slide around them without wearing them down. The road across was made of wooden beams, and at night the beams could be taken away. In that way, nobody could cross in the dark, and the two halves of the city slept behind their own walls. In the morning, the planks went back, and the whole city began to flow across again.

[long-pause]

{img:33-round-reed-boats-donkeys-cargo}

Some river boats were round, like enormous baskets coated in tar.

[pause]

There were boats of every kind on the Euphrates. Some were long and narrow, with sails and oars. Some were rafts of logs floating on inflated goatskins, bringing stone, timber and wine down from the northern mountains. And some were round like a shield, made from woven reeds, covered in leather, and coated with a sticky black tar that seeped from the ground nearby. Herodotus, the Greek traveller, was fascinated by them. He wrote that a boat might carry a heavy load of wine jars and a donkey or two. When it reached Babylon, it was unloaded and taken apart. The leather was strapped on the donkeys' backs, and the donkeys walked home up the river by land, because the current was too strong to row against. Then, at home, someone built another round boat. It was a simple, thrifty and rather charming way to move cargo.

[long-pause]

{img:34-fishermen-dawn-nets-herons}

At dawn, the river was full of fishermen, herons and kingfishers.

[pause]

Long before the markets opened, small boats slipped out onto the Euphrates. Fishermen stood in the bows, casting their nets in wide circles that spread across the water like open hands. They brought back fish for the morning stalls, to be grilled, dried or salted. The banks were full of other life too. Herons stood very still in the shallows. Ducks, egrets and pelicans floated among the reeds, and kingfishers flashed blue and orange between the stems. In the middle of a huge city, the river still felt like countryside, especially in the first grey light, when the mist lay on the water and the only sound was a paddle dipping. Many a Babylonian began the day by walking down to the quay and breathing the river air. It was not a bad way to wake up, even in the biggest city in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:35-spring-flood-platforms-houses}

Every spring, melting mountain snow made the river rise, and the city prepared for a flood.

[pause]

The Euphrates was a generous river, but not always a gentle one. Far to the north, in spring, the snow melted in the mountains and the river swelled. Sometimes it spilled over its banks, across the fields and towards the city. Everyone watched the water creep higher, day by day. The Babylonians were ready for it. They built their houses on raised platforms. They set their temples on great terraces. They kept the dams and canals in good repair, and they prayed to the gods to hold the floods back. In the end, a flood was a normal part of life. It brought trouble, but it also brought fresh silt, which made the fields rich again. The Babylonian farmer lived somewhere between gratitude and worry, as farmers by rivers have always done. He knew that the thing which fed him could, one wet spring, come to his door.

[long-pause]

{img:36-quay-evening-families-water}

In the evening, half of Babylon came down to the water to cool off.

[pause]

When the sun sank behind the palms on the west bank, the river turned red and gold. The fishing boats came home, and the traders tied up their barges for the night. People left their hot houses and walked down to the brick steps that led to the water. They filled their jars, washed their faces and sat with their feet in the cool current. Children splashed in the shallows. Old men talked about the price of barley, and women compared the day's news. A little breeze came off the river, carrying the smell of reeds and wet clay. For a few minutes before dark, the whole city seemed to breathe out. The great tower, the temples and the walls were all quiet silhouettes, and the water slid past them, unhurried. It had been doing so for thousands of years, and it showed no sign of stopping.

[long-pause]

{img:37-plain-street-low-wooden-door}

The sun has set, and lamps are being lit in the narrow streets. Let us follow one family home through a low wooden door, and see how an ordinary Babylonian lived.

[pause]

From the street, a Babylonian house looked like a plain wall with a door.

[pause]

The streets of Babylon were narrow, winding and unpaved, dusty in summer and muddy after winter rain. The houses along them showed almost nothing to the passer-by. There was a high wall of mud brick, with no windows, and a single low wooden door. It looked plain, even unfriendly. But that was the whole idea. The thick blank walls kept out the heat, the dust and the noise, and gave each family some privacy. Behind the door, everything was quite different. Passing through, you stepped into a small entrance room, and then out into an open courtyard, bright with sunlight and full of life. Many cities of the warm Middle East still have houses built on exactly this plan, with a quiet heart hidden from the street. In Babylon, the wall was plain and the home inside was lively, and everybody understood that.

[long-pause]

{img:38-courtyard-house-family-morning}

The heart of the house was an open courtyard where the family cooked, worked and played.

[pause]

The courtyard was paved with bricks and open to the sky. All the rooms of the house stood around it, and the door of each one opened onto it. Here the family cooked in the morning, wove in the shade at midday, and talked at night. Children played here, and so did the dog. On the south side, there was often a long room that faced north, so it stayed cool even in the worst heat of the day. Guests were welcomed there. There were bedrooms, storerooms for grain, oil and dates, and a kitchen with a clay oven. Perhaps there was a small washing room with a drain. The walls were thick, and the rooms were cool and dark, lit only by the light from the doorways. Floors of beaten earth or brick were covered in reed mats and woollen rugs. It was a simple house, but it was cleverly made for its climate.

[long-pause]

{img:39-house-furniture-lamps-chests}

There was little furniture, but every house had lamps, chests and plenty of baskets.

[pause]

A Babylonian home was not crowded with furniture. People sat on mats, stools and cushions, and ate from low tables. Beds were low wooden frames with mattresses and blankets. Clothes and valuables were kept in wooden chests with tidy lids. Clay jars held water, oil, grain and beer, and baskets stood in every corner, filled with dates, wool, bread and tools. At night, the light came from small clay lamps that burned sesame oil, giving off a soft, flickering glow that threw gentle shadows on the walls. There was no glass, no chimney, and no table lamp as we think of one. But a single little flame was enough for a quiet meal and a story. Even now, in a dark room, a candle makes people speak more softly. Perhaps something in us still remembers that warm, close, golden light.

[long-pause]

{img:40-flat-roof-family-sleeping-stars}

In summer, the whole family climbed a ladder and slept on the roof under the stars.

[pause]

The flat roof was another room of the house. It was made of palm trunks and reed mats, covered with thick, smooth mud, and reached by a wooden ladder. During the day, people dried dates, grain and washing there. In the cool of the evening, they sat on the roof and talked with the neighbours on the next roofs. And in the hot summer, when the rooms were stifling, the whole family slept there, lying on mats under the open sky in the breeze. All over Babylon on summer nights, the rooftops were full of sleeping families. They looked up at the stars, talked quietly, and slowly drifted off to the sounds of the city. Dogs barked in the lanes. Frogs croaked in the canals. Far off, the watchmen called the hours from the walls. If you ever wonder how to fall asleep, start with this picture.

[long-pause]

{img:41-baking-flatbread-clay-oven-dawn}

Breakfast began with flat loaves slapped onto the hot wall of a clay oven.

[pause]

In a typical Babylonian family, the day began at dawn, when light slid into the courtyard and the birds woke in the palm tree next door. A servant girl lit the fire in the clay oven in the corner. Then the mother made bread. She shaped flat round loaves and slapped them against the hot inside wall of the oven, where they baked in a few minutes. She peeled them off, soft and steaming and smelling wonderful. The family ate breakfast sitting on mats in the courtyard. There was bread and dates, a little cheese, some onions, and a drink of thick, sweet barley beer. Everyone had some, even the children. It was a simple meal, but it set the day in motion. The same method of baking bread is still used in many parts of the region today, and the smell is just as good.

[long-pause]

{img:42-scribe-father-walking-to-temple}

The father of our imaginary family was a scribe who wrote contracts for the temple.

[pause]

Let us borrow a typical household. The father worked as a scribe at the temple of Marduk. He wrote contracts and accounts on clay tablets, and every morning he walked to work through narrow lanes that were already busy. Donkeys plodded past with sacks. Water carriers hurried by with jars. Sellers called out their wares. He carried a sharp reed pen for writing and a little basket of clay tablets. His eldest son, who was twelve, went off to a teacher's house to learn the same trade. The mother ran the home and wove cloth, and she also had a little side business, lending small amounts of silver to her neighbours. There were four children, a grandmother, a servant girl and a small dog. This was not a rich family, but it was comfortable, respected, and well supplied with news about the city.

[long-pause]

{img:43-women-spinning-wool-afternoon-shade}

In the heat of the afternoon, everyone rested, while the women spun and talked in the shade.

[pause]

By midday, the sun beat down on the courtyard like a hammer, and the streets emptied. Work slowed or stopped. People retreated into the cool dark rooms to doze. In the shaded corner of the courtyard, the mother sat at her loom or spun wool on a spindle, as she had done for most of her life. Her sister might come to visit from the next street, and the two would talk for hours while their hands kept moving. Children played quietly with clay toys, little wheeled carts on strings, spinning tops and balls. The only sounds were the creak of the loom, the hum of flies, and a distant donkey. It was a deeply peaceful hour, and it was repeated on almost every afternoon of the year. When the heat finally broke, life began again, a little slower than before.

[long-pause]

{img:44-women-signing-contracts-witnesses}

Babylonian women could own property, sign contracts and win cases in court.

[pause]

Most women in Babylon spent their lives as wives and mothers, running the house and raising children. But they had more rights than women in many other ancient lands. A woman could own houses, fields and silver. She could buy and sell, lend and borrow, make contracts and act as a witness. If she had a quarrel, she could go to court. When she married, her family gave her a dowry of furniture, clothes, jewellery and sometimes land, and it stayed hers all her life. It was written carefully in the marriage contract on clay. If her husband sent her away without good reason, he had to give it all back. Some contracts even promised that if the husband took a second wife, he would pay the first one a large sum of silver. Of course, not everything was equal. But these women had a real voice, and clay tablets still preserve it.

[long-pause]

{img:45-businesswomen-weavers-taverns}

Some women ran taverns, weaving workshops and even family businesses.

[pause]

Women worked outside the home as well. Many wove in the large workshops of the temples and the palace. Others brewed beer and ran taverns, sold goods in the streets, or worked as nurses and midwives. Some became writers, singers and musicians, and some served as priestesses. In the records of Babylon, we meet women who bought and sold houses, lent silver and grain, rented out fields, and managed the family property when their husbands were away. In the papers of one wealthy family, the wives and mothers appear again and again, signing and witnessing. The Babylonians also respected women as keepers of the house and of family memory. They had a saying that a house without a wife is like a field without a plough. At the end of the day, mothers, daughters and grandmothers sat together on the rooftops, combing each other's hair and watching the sun go down.

[long-pause]

{img:46-grandmother-telling-stories-gilgamesh}

At night, grandmothers told the children about Gilgamesh and the great flood.

[pause]

On the roof, after supper, the grandmother told stories. There were tales of gods and heroes, and above all of Gilgamesh, the great king of an ancient city, and his wild friend Enkidu. She told about their adventures, their journey and their sorrow. Then she told about the flood. Long ago, the gods sent water over the whole earth. But one wise man was warned in time. He built a great boat and saved his family and the animals. Would the boat survive the storm? The children always held their breath at that point, even though they knew how it ended. Stories like these had been told for more than a thousand years, and were written down on clay long before our time. Yet they still worked best when spoken aloud, by lamplight, with the stars overhead, to a child just beginning to feel sleepy.

[long-pause]

{img:47-date-palm-grove-harvest}

The children are asleep now, one by one, under the stars. While they dream, let us think about the food and drink that kept Babylon going, which began in the fields and groves around it.

[pause]

To the Babylonians, the date palm was the tree of life.

[pause]

Around the city, along every river and canal, stood groves of date palms. They were tall and slender, with crowns of long feathery leaves that whispered in the wind. In late summer, heavy bunches of dates hung beneath them, turning orange, red and brown. The Babylonians used nearly every part of the tree. The fruit was sweet and filling, and it could be eaten fresh, dried for months, or pressed into a syrup like honey. The leaves were woven into baskets, mats and roofs. The fibres became rope. The trunks became beams and bridges, and even the stones were burned for fuel or fed to animals. Nothing went to waste. Underneath the palms, in their shade, farmers grew pomegranates, figs, grapes, onions and lettuce, so the groves were like cool green gardens in the burning summer. They were one of the loveliest parts of the country.

[long-pause]

{img:48-palm-pollination-climber-flowers}

Date palms needed help from farmers, who climbed the trees to pollinate them by hand.

[pause]

A date palm is either male or female, and only the female trees bear fruit. In nature, the wind carries pollen from one tree to another, but it is not always reliable. So every spring, the farmers climbed the tall trunks, wrapped in a rope that circled both the man and the tree. It was a long way up, and one slip would be dangerous. At the top, a farmer tied a small bunch of flowers from a male tree among the flowers of a female tree. With that done, the dates would grow. Babylonians understood this perfectly, and they treated the work with great care. Farmers in Iraq still pollinate their palms by hand, almost exactly as their ancestors did. It is the sort of knowledge that passes quietly from grandfather to grandson, through thousands of springs, without ever needing to be written down at all.

[long-pause]

{img:49-shaduf-lifting-water-field}

A balanced pole with a bucket on one end and a lump of clay on the other lifted water into the fields.

[pause]

Getting water from the canal up into the field was hard work, so farmers invented a clever tool. It was a long pole balanced on an upright post. A bucket hung from one end and a heavy lump of clay from the other. The farmer pulled the bucket down into the canal, and the weight of the clay helped him lift it again. Then he swung the pole around and emptied the bucket into a channel that carried the water to his plants. With a tool like this, a single man could lift a surprising amount of water in a day. It was cheap, simple and strong, and it needed no animals. Versions of it are still seen along rivers in the region today. Sometimes the best inventions are the ones nobody ever needs to improve, and this was one of them.

[long-pause]

{img:50-seed-plough-oxen-barley-field}

Mesopotamian farmers invented a plough with a built-in funnel that planted seeds as it moved.

[pause]

Barley was the most important crop in Babylon. It gave the people bread and it gave them beer, and it was also used to pay wages. To plant it, farmers used a wooden plough pulled by oxen. On top of the plough was a funnel. The farmer dropped seeds into it, and they fell straight into the furrow as the plough moved forward. This saved a great deal of time, and wasted far fewer seeds than scattering them by hand. It was a clever invention of this land, and it was used for centuries. In spring, the barley ripened, and the harvest came in April and May. Farmers cut it with curved blades, donkeys carried it to threshing floors, and oxen walked round and round on it to shake the grain loose. Then the wind blew away the husks, and the golden grain went into sacks.

[long-pause]

{img:51-beer-drinkers-straws-large-jar}

Everybody in Babylon drank beer, and they drank it through straws.

[pause]

Babylonian beer was not much like the beer we know. It was made from barley, and it was thick, cloudy and sweet, and not very strong. It was more like a liquid food than a drink. Men, women and children had it every day with their meals. To make it, brewers soaked barley until it sprouted, dried it, ground it and baked it into loaves. They crumbled the loaves into water in big jars, added dates for sweetness, and left the mixture to bubble for a few days. The result had bits of grain floating on top, so people drank through long reed straws, and some straws had little filters at the bottom. Old pictures from the region show friends sitting around one big jar, all sipping through their straws and chatting. It was a friendly and rather sociable way to have a drink.

[long-pause]

{img:52-tavern-keeper-woman-serving}

Taverns were often run by women, and the old laws had rules for them.

[pause]

In the evening, people liked to gather in a tavern to drink, talk and listen to music. Many were run by women who brewed their own beer. More than a thousand years before our story, the laws of Hammurabi already had rules for tavern keepers. They had to sell fair amounts at fair prices, and if they cheated customers, they could be punished. There was also wine in Babylon, though it was expensive. It came down the river on rafts from the northern mountains, and it was drunk by the wealthy and offered to the gods. A goddess even watched over beer, which tells you how seriously the Babylonians took it. A good tavern was a place where friends met, news was swapped and a long day ended well. It was not very different from the corner pubs we know. The jars were bigger, the straws were longer, and the lamps were smaller.

[long-pause]

{img:53-clay-moulds-cakes-dates-honey}

The oldest cookbook in the world was written on clay in this land.

[pause]

The Babylonians loved their food, and they had dozens of kinds of bread and cake. They made flat breads baked on oven walls, round loaves, and sweet cakes of honey, dates, sesame and butter. Some were baked in clay moulds shaped like fish, animals or stars. Remarkably, the oldest known collection of recipes in the world comes from this land. A handful of clay tablets, written more than a thousand years before Nebuchadnezzar, describe stews of lamb, pigeon and duck, cooked with onions, leeks, garlic and herbs. In our own time, some cooks have followed them to see how the food tasted. In the evening, the smell of baking bread, onions and stew drifted over the rooftops, together with wood smoke. Families gathered in courtyards and on roofs to eat, talk about the day, and enjoy a meal that was, in many ways, not so far from our own.

[long-pause]

{img:54-market-street-merchants-donkeys}

The smell of supper is fading, and the lamps are going out one by one. But there is a side of Babylon that never quite slept, the busy world of buying, selling and settling arguments.

[pause]

The land had barley and dates and clay, but almost no stone, no metal and no good wood.

[pause]

Babylon was rich, but it lacked many things. The country had plenty of barley, dates, wool, reeds and clay. But it had almost no stone, almost no useful timber except palm, and no metal at all. So nearly everything else had to come from far away, and the traders of Babylon traded with the whole known world. Cedar for the temples came down the rivers on rafts from the western mountains. Copper, tin, iron, silver and gold arrived from the northern and eastern hills. A deep blue stone flecked with gold, the Babylonians' favourite, came from lands far to the east. Incense and spices came from Egypt and Arabia, and ivory, cotton and gems from India by sea. In return, Babylon sent out barley, dates, sesame oil and, above all, beautiful dyed woollen cloth, which was famous everywhere.

[long-pause]

{img:55-camel-caravan-arriving-at-gate}

Caravans crossed the desert from one water hole to the next, and missing one could be fatal.

[pause]

Goods travelled in long lines of donkeys, and more and more on camels, which were becoming common. The caravans crossed the dry lands from one water hole to the next. The journey was long and hot, and the traders knew every well on the way. If they missed one, they could be in real danger. Other merchants moved their goods by boat along the rivers, down to the sea and out into the wider ocean. Babylon did not have a single great market square. Trade happened everywhere, at the city gates, on the quays where boats unloaded, in the streets, in little shops, in the temples and in the courtyards of rich merchant families. When a caravan came in at the end of the day, the whole street seemed to wake up. Camels groaned, bells rang and everyone asked the same question. What have you brought?

[long-pause]

{img:56-silver-scales-weights-merchant}

There were no coins in Babylon. People paid by weighing out silver.

[pause]

Coins had only just been invented, far to the west, and they had not yet reached Babylon. So people paid in silver, weighed on scales, in little pieces, bars and rings. The usual unit was the shekel, which was simply a weight. Barley served as money too, and wages were often paid in grain or oil. Everything had its price in shekels and barley, from a house to an ox to a jar of sesame oil. Traders carried small scales and sets of stone weights, often shaped like ducks or little domes. To a modern eye, it would look very slow. Yet it worked, and it worked for centuries. And since silver could be cut, melted and weighed, people had to trust one another, or at least trust a scribe. Which brings us to the thing that held the whole system together.

[long-pause]

{img:57-contracts-clay-seals-witnesses}

Every sale, loan, marriage and adoption was written on a clay tablet and sealed.

[pause]

In Babylon, nothing important happened without a scribe. When people bought a house, lent silver, rented a boat, married or adopted a child, they went to a writer. He wrote a contract on a clay tablet with the names of those involved, the witnesses and the date. Then everyone pressed their seal into the clay. Some, who had no seal, simply pressed a fingernail. Thousands of these contracts survive. They tell us the price of a house, an ox or a field. They tell us the rent of a boat and the wages of a worker. One famous family of bankers and traders appears in more than a thousand tablets across five generations. We can follow their business, their marriages, even their quarrels, for over a century. It is remarkable to know so much about ordinary people who lived two and a half thousand years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:58-hammurabi-stele-black-stone}

A black stone pillar carved with nearly three hundred laws stood in Babylon's temple.

[pause]

More than a thousand years before our story, the great king Hammurabi had his laws carved on a tall black stone, more than two metres high. At the top, the king stood before the sun god, the god of justice, who handed him the law. Below, in neat close lines, were nearly three hundred rules. They dealt with property, trade and wages, debts, marriage and divorce, fields, canals, boats and animals, and with doctors, builders and tavern keepers. Some punishments were harsh, in the old way. But many laws were about fairness and protecting the weak. Hammurabi explained that he wanted the strong not to crush the weak, and widows and orphans to receive justice. By 600 BC, the stone was very old, but scribes still copied the laws as a classic text. Centuries later, the stone was carried away to another city as a prize of war, and finally found by French archaeologists in 1901.

[long-pause]

{img:59-judges-at-temple-gate-oath}

When people quarrelled, the judges sat at the temple gates and asked them to swear an oath.

[pause]

If two neighbours could not settle a dispute, they went to the judges. These sat at temple gates or in courts, listened to both sides and the witnesses, and examined the contracts on their tablets. Then they decided. Sometimes they asked a person to swear an oath before the gods. That was a very serious thing. People's hands shook when they did it, because nobody wanted to lie before a god. And everything was written down on clay, the case, the witnesses, the decision and the date. Thanks to that, we can still read about a quarrel over the edge of a field, or the return of a wedding gift, or the payment of a debt, and learn how a judge in Babylon decided. Most arguments never got that far. The elders of a street would sit down together in a courtyard in the evening, over a jar of beer, and talk until everyone agreed.

[long-pause]

{img:60-scribe-pressing-reed-wet-clay}

The arguments are settled, the contracts are sealed, and the tablets are stacked to dry. Everything in Babylon was written on clay, so it is worth looking at how that was done.

[pause]

Babylonian writing was made of tiny wedges pressed into wet clay with a reed.

[pause]

The writer took a lump of smooth, damp clay and shaped it in his hand into a tablet, small enough to hold in one palm. It was flat on one side and gently rounded on the other, like a little cushion. Holding it in his left hand, he pressed the sharp end of a cut reed into the surface with his right. Each press left a small wedge-shaped mark. Signs were made from several wedges, long and short, upright and lying down. Today, we call this style cuneiform, which simply means wedge-shaped. A scribe worked fast, line after line, turning the tablet to write on the back and even on the edges. The Sumerians invented it more than three thousand years before our story, and at first it was simple pictures of sheep, grain and jars of oil. Over the centuries, the pictures turned into wedges, and the signs began to stand for sounds as well as things.

[long-pause]

{img:61-tablets-drying-in-sun-rows}

Clay tablets were cheap to make, and baked ones lasted almost forever.

[pause]

After writing, the tablet was left to dry in the sun until it was hard. If it was important, it was baked in an oven, and then it became as tough as a brick. Even letters had envelopes, made of a thin layer of clay wrapped around the tablet. The address was written on the outside, and you had to break the envelope to read the letter. Clay was cheap and easy to find. Best of all, it lasted. Paper and leather rot, and wood burns. But clay survives for thousands of years in the ground. When a library burned down in this part of the world, the fire often baked the tablets harder, which preserved them. That is why hundreds of thousands of tablets have been found. We have letters, laws, lists, prayers, recipes, dictionaries, poems and star charts. It is the strangest kind of luck that a cheap writing material has outlived the empires it recorded.

[long-pause]

{img:62-tablet-house-school-boys-benches}

Boys went to the "tablet house" at about seven years old and spent most of the day copying signs.

[pause]

The Babylonian school was called the tablet house. It was not a big building. Usually it was the home of a master scribe, who took a few pupils and taught them in a room or courtyard. Most pupils were boys from families of scribes, priests and the well off, though a few girls learned to write as well. They began at about seven or eight and went to school from sunrise to evening for many years. First they learned how to make a tablet, hold the reed and press simple wedges. Then came the signs, one by one, copied again and again from a model. The teacher wrote the model on one side of a tablet, and the pupil copied it on the other. After that came lists of words, trees, animals, stones, gods and cities. Last of all came sayings, letters, hymns and stories.

[long-pause]

{img:63-school-tablet-pupil-copy-closeup}

Some school tablets show a teacher's neat writing on one side and a pupil's mistakes on the other.

[pause]

When a pupil made a mistake, he smoothed the clay with his thumb and started again. But many of the practice tablets survived, and they are charming. On one side is the teacher's neat, confident model. On the other is the pupil's clumsy copy, with wobbling lines, crowded signs and plenty of errors. You can almost see the boy's tongue between his teeth. Pupils also learned mathematics, how to count, multiply and use fractions, how to measure a field, how many bricks a wall needed, or how much barley the workers should be paid. The teachers were strict. Nevertheless, you can feel the warmth behind some of the old school writings. They show that schoolboys, parents and teachers have not changed very much in four thousand years. Somewhere in a museum, a long-ago child's untidy homework is still waiting to be marked.

[long-pause]

{img:64-schoolboy-story-father-dinner-teacher}

A famous school story tells of a boy who was punished all day, until his father invited the teacher to dinner.

[pause]

Babylonian pupils copied a funny old story for centuries. A boy goes to school and is punished for being late. Then he is punished for talking too much, for standing up, for writing badly, for just about everything. Sad and tired, he tells his father. The father invites the teacher to dinner. He gives him a fine meal, a new robe and a ring, and pours him drink after drink. And suddenly the teacher begins to praise the boy. He says that he is the best pupil in the school, and that he will become a famous scribe. The story is funny and very human, and it shows that people two and a half thousand years ago were fully capable of laughing at themselves and their teachers. It also shows that a good dinner has always been a useful way to smooth over a difficult situation.

[long-pause]

{img:65-aramaic-ink-leather-scroll-street}

A new, simple alphabet with only twenty-two letters was spreading across the country.

[pause]

Around 600 BC, a new way of writing was becoming common. It was a simple alphabet of twenty-two letters, written in ink on leather or on pieces of pottery. It was much easier to learn than cuneiform, which needed hundreds of signs and many years of study. The language that went with it, Aramaic, was spoken everywhere in the streets and the markets. Traders used it. Ordinary people used it. Soon even officials in the great empires would use it. Yet for the important things, the scribes of Babylon still pressed the old wedges into clay. They used it for temple records, laws, contracts and star charts, as their ancestors had done for thousands of years. Two ways of writing lived side by side, one new and fast, and one old and respected. The old one was written in stone, almost literally, and it outlived the other by many centuries.

[long-pause]

{img:66-clay-library-shelves-archive}

Babylon's archives held tablets on everything from medicine to maths, and many are still unread.

[pause]

The temples and palaces of Babylon kept their tablets in archives and libraries, on shelves and in baskets labelled with small clay tags. There were lists and dictionaries, prayers and hymns, medical texts, mathematics, omens, laws and stories. Hundreds of thousands of tablets have been found in the ruins of Mesopotamia, and many sit in museum storerooms, waiting for specialists to read them. Every year, a few more are translated, and every year we learn a little more about the people who wrote them. We find their business and their worries, their prayers and sometimes their jokes. It is gentle, patient work. A scholar bends over a small grey tablet under a lamp and reads a line that nobody has read for two and a half thousand years. For a moment, the writer and the reader are in the same room, and the long silence between them is broken.

[long-pause]

{img:67-base-sixty-wedge-numbers-tablet}

A pupil at the tablet house also learned to count, and the way the Babylonians counted has followed us all the way into the present.

[pause]

Every time you check the hour, you are using something from Babylon.

[pause]

The Babylonians did not count in tens as we do. They counted in sixties. Their number system used only two signs, an upright wedge for one and a sideways wedge for ten. With those, they could write any number from one to fifty-nine. At sixty, they started again, with a single wedge in the next column, much as we do when we write ten with a one in the next place. Why sixty? Nobody knows for certain. But sixty has a marvellous property for calculation. It divides evenly by two, three, four, five, six, ten, twelve, fifteen, twenty and thirty. Fractions become easy. And that is why, thousands of years later, we still have hours of sixty minutes, minutes of sixty seconds, and circles of three hundred and sixty degrees. Without knowing it, we all carry a little piece of Babylon in our days.

[long-pause]

{img:68-square-diagonal-tablet-numbers}

One small clay tablet shows a very exact value for the diagonal of a square, written about 3,700 years ago.

[pause]

Babylonian mathematics was far more advanced than you might expect. More than a thousand years before our story, scribes were already solving hard problems about areas, volumes and interest on loans. One famous tablet, roughly the size of a hand, shows a square with a line running from corner to corner. On the line, in wedges, is a number that is very hard to work out. The scribes had calculated it to a remarkable accuracy, better than anyone else for more than a thousand years. Another famous tablet holds a table of numbers that scholars have discussed for nearly a century. Many believe it shows that the Babylonians knew a special rule about triangles, long before the Greek Pythagoras gave his name to it. They did not write long proofs. They wrote methods, practical and clear, and handed them down from teacher to pupil.

[long-pause]

{img:69-surveyors-measuring-field-rope}

For the Babylonians, maths was practical, and they used it to measure fields and divide inheritances.

[pause]

Mathematics in Babylon was never just a puzzle. People used it every day. Surveyors measured fields with knotted ropes and worked out their areas. Families divided an inheritance between brothers. Officials worked out the wages and rations of workers. Builders calculated how many bricks a wall would need, and how much earth must be dug out of a canal. Merchants worked out the interest on a loan. Scribes learned these skills by solving practice problems in school, many of which sound surprisingly familiar. A field is this long and this wide, so how much barley will it yield? A canal is this deep. How many workmen will it take? It is oddly comforting to imagine a long row of boys frowning at their tablets, doing homework that is not so different from ours. Some things never change, even when the numbers are written in wedges.

[long-pause]

{img:70-lunar-calendar-twelve-months-scribe}

The Babylonian year had twelve months of the moon, with an extra month added every few years.

[pause]

The Babylonians kept time by the moon. Each month began when the thin new crescent was first seen in the evening sky, and lasted twenty-nine or thirty days. Twelve such months make a year, but twelve moon months are about eleven days shorter than a year of the sun. If nothing was done, the seasons would slowly slide around the calendar, and the New Year festival would no longer fall in spring. So every few years, the astronomers added an extra month to put everything back in step. They divided the day and the night into twelve parts as well, and the sky into twelve parts. This careful counting, which began with practical needs like farming, planting and festivals, gradually became something more. It became a patient study of the heavens.

[long-pause]

{img:71-astronomers-roof-night-tablets}

On temple roofs, star watchers wrote a diary of the sky every night, for six hundred years.

[pause]

The night sky over Babylon was extraordinary. The air was dry and clear, and there were no city lights, only small lamps and fires. The stars shone bright and sharp from one edge of the flat land to the other. On top of the great tower and the temple roofs, priests and scribes watched them. Night after night, they recorded where the moon and the planets stood among the stars, when they rose and set, and when the moon went dark. They also noted the weather, the height of the river, the price of barley, dates and wool, and the events of the city. These diaries began around 650 BC and went on for about six hundred years, the longest scientific record kept in the ancient world. Hundreds of fragments still survive in the British Museum, and modern scientists use them to study events in the sky long ago.

[long-pause]

{img:72-night-sky-moon-planets-tower}

With all those notes, the astronomers learned to predict what the sky would do next.

[pause]

After generations of watching and writing, the Babylonian astronomers found patterns. They discovered that eclipses of the moon came in cycles, and that planets moved in repeating rhythms. Using their clever arithmetic with sixties, they calculated when a new moon would appear and where the planets would be next month. Some scholars now think they even used a kind of geometry, with shapes drawn on a graph of time and speed, to follow the path of the planet Jupiter. Nobody in Europe would use that method again for well over a thousand years. This was not what we call modern science, since the Babylonians did not ask what the sky was made of. They wanted to know what it would do, and what it meant. But their patience and their numbers laid the foundations for later astronomers all around the Mediterranean.

[long-pause]

{img:73-zodiac-star-pictures-bull-lion}

Many of our star names and the twelve star signs began with the astronomers of Babylon.

[pause]

The Babylonians gave names and pictures to the stars. They divided the road that the sun, moon and planets seem to follow into twelve equal parts, and each part had its own group of stars. The Bull, the Lion and the Scorpion. The Twins and the Scales. Many of those pictures later became the twelve signs of the zodiac, known to us today. For the Babylonians, the sky was a vast book written by the gods. A dark moon or a planet that moved in a strange way could be a message about the king and the country. So the astronomers sent regular reports to the palace, and sometimes the king waited nervously for their news. But mostly they simply watched and wrote, with great care and love of the sky. On a warm night, you could see them on the temple roof, wrapped in cloaks, pressing their reeds softly into the clay.

[long-pause]

{img:74-akitu-festival-boats-statues-river}

The astronomers watch the moon rise. In spring, when that moon is just right and the barley is turning gold, the whole city puts down its tools for the greatest festival of the year.

[pause]

The New Year festival lasted twelve days, and statues of the gods came to Babylon by boat.

[pause]

Each spring, when the barley was ripening in the fields, Babylon celebrated the Akitu, the New Year. It was the greatest festival of the year and lasted twelve days. In the early days, priests held ceremonies in the temple at dawn, with prayers, hymns and the recital of the great poem about Marduk and the making of the world. Meanwhile, the statues of the gods of other cities were brought to Babylon by boat. They came along rivers and canals, on vessels decorated with gold, flowers and banners, with priests and musicians at their sides. The gods came to visit Marduk, and to take part in the festival, and the city filled with visitors. For a few days, the quays were crowded and the streets were lined with people who had walked from far villages to watch. Babylon at the New Year must have felt like the centre of the world.

[long-pause]

{img:75-king-before-marduk-statue-ceremony}

At the New Year, the most powerful king in the world had to humble himself before the god.

[pause]

On the fifth day, a strange and solemn ceremony took place. The king came into the temple and stood before the statue of Marduk. The high priest removed his crown and his royal robes and laid them before the god. Then he struck the king lightly on the cheek and pulled his ears. The king knelt, and said that he had done no wrong. He had not neglected the gods, he had not harmed Babylon, and he had not harmed its people. Then the priest gave him back his crown. Everyone watched the king's face. If tears came to his eyes, it was a good sign. Marduk was pleased, and the year would be a good one. It was a powerful reminder that even the greatest king was a servant of the god, and must rule fairly. Few rulers have ever done anything like it.

[long-pause]

{img:76-procession-through-ishtar-gate-statues}

On the great day, the statues of the gods paraded through the blue gate while the whole city watched.

[pause]

On the eighth and ninth days came the great procession. The king took the hand of Marduk's statue and led him out of the temple. The statues of all the other gods followed, on carts and carried by priests, in a long glittering line. They walked along the holy road between the blue walls and the golden lions, and passed through the Ishtar Gate. There were drums and harps and pipes, singing and clouds of incense. The whole city crowded along the road, on the walls and on the roofs. The procession went out to the river and by boat to a special temple in a garden outside the city. The gods stayed there for a few days with ceremonies and feasts. Then they returned along the same road, in the same splendour. At last the gods gathered to decide the fate of the land for the coming year.

[long-pause]

{img:77-festival-night-lamps-rooftops}

All through the festival, the city feasted, danced and lit lamps on the rooftops.

[pause]

While the priests and the king were busy in the temple, the rest of the city celebrated in its own way. There were feasts and music and dancing in the streets. People put on their best clothes, visited relatives and exchanged gifts. The temples handed out food and drink. At night, lamps burned on the roofs and on the walls, so that the whole city glowed in the dark. Music and singing went on late, long after the children had fallen asleep on their mats. When it was all over, the new year had begun. The world had been made new, the king had been confirmed, and the gods had decided the fate of the land. People went back to their homes and fields and workshops, the barley was harvested, and ordinary life resumed. But for a few days, everyone had felt a part of something far greater than themselves.

[long-pause]

{img:78-gods-of-babylon-shrine-statues}

The world of the Babylonians was full of gods, and one of them even looked after beer.

[pause]

The Babylonians believed in a great crowd of gods. There was an old god of the sky, a lord of the wind, and Ea, the clever and kindly god of the fresh waters under the earth, who loved people and helped them. There was the sun god, who saw everything as he crossed the sky and who stood for truth and justice. There was the moon god with his silver curve, and the storm god with his lightning. There was Ishtar, the goddess of love and of the morning and evening star, beautiful and fierce. There were goddesses of healing, writing and beer. And above them all, in Babylon, stood Marduk, with his son Nabu, the god of wisdom. Every city had its own god and its own temple. In Babylon there were dozens of temples and hundreds of little shrines at street corners, where people stopped to leave a few dates or a flower.

[long-pause]

{img:79-household-shrine-evening-prayer}

Every family had its own personal god, and prayed to it each evening.

[pause]

Besides the great gods, each family had a personal god who watched over them and spoke for them to the greater ones. In a corner of the house, they kept little clay figures of their god and of good spirits. In the evening, a mother or father lit a small lamp, poured a little water and set out a few dates and a piece of bread. Then they said a quiet prayer for the family, the children, the ancestors, the house and the night. The Babylonians believed that the dead lived on in a grey, quiet land beneath the earth, and depended on the living for food and water. So families remembered their ancestors by name and gave them small offerings every month. They wore little charms against evil spirits, and buried small figures of protectors under doorways. In a dangerous world, these small habits made the night feel safer.

[long-pause]

{img:80-hanging-gardens-imagined-terraces}

The lamps are burning low. In a half-dream, we can think about the most famous thing that Babylon is supposed to have held, which might not have been there at all.

[pause]

The most famous wonder of Babylon is one that may never have existed.

[pause]

Greek and Roman writers, centuries afterwards, told a lovely story. Nebuchadnezzar, they said, had a wife who came from the green mountains in the north-east. In flat, brown Babylon, she was homesick. So the king built her a mountain in the middle of his city, a great garden rising in steps like a green hill, planted with trees and flowers from all over the empire. Machines lifted river water to the top, and it flowed down through the terraces in streams. From below, the trees seemed to hang in the air, so people called them the Hanging Gardens, and the Greeks counted them among the Seven Wonders of the World. It is a beautiful story. But there is a mystery. The Babylonians never mention these gardens, in all their clay tablets. Nor does Nebuchadnezzar, who wrote so proudly of everything else he built.

[long-pause]

{img:81-assyrian-garden-hillside-channels}

Some scholars think the famous gardens were really in another city, further north.

[pause]

Where, then, were the Hanging Gardens? Archaeologists have dug in Babylon for more than a hundred years, and they have never found them for certain. Some scholars think the gardens did exist but stood beside the river and were washed away. Some point to a strange vaulted building in the palace, with arches and wells, which might have been part of them. Others suggest that the story is really about another city, further north, where a king built a famous garden about a century earlier. That garden was watered by great canals and an impressive aqueduct, and later writers may have mixed the two cities up. And some people think the gardens were only ever a dream, a legend of a beautiful, green and impossible garden in the desert. But whichever it is, a garden of some sort certainly existed. The kings and temples of Babylon had orchards, pools and palm groves, and the shade of a terrace by the river is easy to imagine.

[long-pause]

{img:82-judean-exiles-canal-willows-dusk}

By the rivers of Babylon, exiles from a distant kingdom sat down and remembered their homes.

[pause]

Over the years, many peoples came to Babylon, and some did not come willingly. Far to the west lay the small kingdom of Judah, with its capital Jerusalem. When it refused to obey Nebuchadnezzar, his armies went there, and many of its people, including the king and his court, were brought across the desert to Babylon. We will leave the sad days of the war and the long journey gently in the past. The exiles were not kept in prisons. They were settled in towns and villages beside the canals, where they were given land to farm. They could build houses, plant gardens, marry, trade and keep their customs and their faith. But many missed their homes. One of the most famous songs in the Bible begins like this. By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, and wept, when we remembered home. We hung our harps upon the willows.

[long-pause]

{img:83-ration-tablet-storeroom-palace}

Clay tablets from the palace stores list the oil and barley given to a king in exile and his five sons.

[pause]

Remarkably, we can find these exiles in the clay records. When archaeologists dug in the palace storerooms, they found tablets listing the rations of oil and barley given to foreign guests at court. Among the names is the king of Judah, and his five sons. And in more recent times, other tablets have been read that come from a small town near Babylon, whose name means the town of Judah. The exiles lived there, farmed, paid their taxes and signed contracts. Their Hebrew names appear on the tablets for several generations, fathers and sons and grandsons, living quietly beside the canals. They were also gathering and copying the writings of their people, so that nothing would be lost. Much of what became the Hebrew Bible took shape in these years. Many of the exiles later went home, but many stayed, because Babylon had become their home too.

[long-pause]

{img:84-brickmakers-wooden-moulds-yard}

Babylon was built from millions of bricks, made by hand in big yards beside the river.

[pause]

In workshops, yards and streets across Babylon, craftsmen were busy. Most important of all were the brick makers. They worked in huge yards outside the city, where the river gave them plenty of clay and water. They mixed clay with water and chopped straw, trod it into thick mud with their feet, and pressed it into square wooden moulds. Then they turned the bricks out in long rows to dry in the sun. In the summer heat, that took a few days. If it rained, the work was ruined. Most bricks were only sun-dried, but the best were baked in enormous ovens until they came out hard and yellow and red. These were used for outer walls, floors and anywhere that had to stand up to water. Around the brick yards, the smoke of the ovens rose into the sky all day long, a sign that the city was still growing.

[long-pause]

{img:85-weavers-dyers-goldsmiths-workshops}

Babylonian craftsmen wove, dyed, carved and hammered, and passed their trade down through families.

[pause]

Beyond the brick yards, the streets rang with many other trades. There were weavers, men and women, working at looms. There were dyers who dipped wool in tubs of red, blue, purple and yellow, and tailors who sewed patterns into cloth. There were goldsmiths and silversmiths making jewellery and cups, blacksmiths making tools, carpenters, boat builders, potters, leather workers and basket makers. There were perfume makers mixing sweet oils, and carvers who made seals. A seal was a little stone cylinder, about the size of a finger, carved with tiny pictures. Rolled across wet clay, it left a small picture, like a signature, and every important person wore one on a string around the neck. Many craftsmen worked for the temples or the palace, and were paid in barley, wool, oil and silver. Others had their own shops, and often a trade stayed in the same family for generations, from father to son and mother to daughter.

[long-pause]

{img:86-cyrus-entering-babylon-peaceful-crowd}

The craftsmen put down their tools, and the lamps in the workshops go out. Now we may look a little further, past the end of this night, to see what the future held for the city.

[pause]

About sixty years later, a Persian king entered Babylon almost without a fight.

[pause]

Nebuchadnezzar ruled for forty-three years and died as an old man. After him, Babylon had several kings in a short time. The last was an unusual man who loved old things and the moon god, and who spent ten years away in the Arabian deserts, leaving his son to rule at home. Meanwhile, far to the east, a new power was growing. About sixty years after our story, Cyrus the Great, king of the Persians, arrived with his army. Babylon was taken almost without a battle. It is said that people welcomed him with green branches. He honoured Marduk, respected the temples and let people live as before. On a clay cylinder now in a museum in London, Cyrus tells his own story. He says Marduk chose him to be king of Babylon, and that he allowed the peoples who had been brought there to go home, with their gods.

[long-pause]

{img:87-alexander-babylon-palace-evening}

Alexander the Great planned to make Babylon his capital, but he died there at thirty-two.

[pause]

Under the Persians, Babylon stayed rich and important for about two hundred years. Then Alexander the Great arrived and took it from them. He admired the city and planned to make it the capital of his great empire, stretching from Greece to India. But in the old palace of Nebuchadnezzar, he fell ill with a fever, and he died in Babylon at the age of thirty-two. After Alexander, his generals built new cities, and slowly people drifted away from the old one. The temples still stood, and the astronomers still watched the sky and wrote their diaries on clay for another two hundred years. The last cuneiform tablet that we know of was written around seventy-five years after Christ. After that, the ancient writing was forgotten, and for almost two thousand years, nobody could read it at all.

[long-pause]

{img:88-ruined-mounds-desert-palms}

Over the centuries, Babylon faded into a field of mounds, but its name never died.

[pause]

Slowly, the river changed its course. The canals filled with mud. The great buildings crumbled, and people took the bricks away to build new villages. The greatest city in the world became a field of mounds and broken brick on a flat desert plain. Now and then, travellers came and looked at it and wondered whether this could really be the Babylon of the Bible and the old stories. Local people dug in the ruins for bricks, and found the stamped ones with the king's name, which they built into houses, walls and dams. Yet Babylon was never forgotten. Its name lived on in the Bible, in Greek and Roman writing, in songs, poems and paintings, for two thousand years. A name of wonder and splendour, waiting patiently under the earth to be found again.

[long-pause]

{img:89-koldewey-excavation-railway-workers}

In 1899, a German archaeologist arrived in Babylon and spent almost eighteen years digging.

[pause]

His name was Robert Koldewey. He was trained as a builder, and he was a careful, patient man with a big moustache and a love of old buildings. He came to Babylon in 1899 and stayed for nearly eighteen years. With hundreds of local workers, he dug layer by layer through the mounds, with a little railway to carry away the earth. He uncovered the great walls, the gates, the palaces and the temples, and mapped and photographed them. One day, in the north of the city, his workers began to find pieces of glazed brick, blue and yellow and white, thousands of them, broken and scattered. They kept digging. Deeper down they found the lower part of a gate still standing, with bulls and dragons in rows along its walls. It had been buried for more than two thousand years. It was the Ishtar Gate.

[long-pause]

{img:90-scholar-reading-tablet-magnifier}

Scholars are still reading Babylon's tablets today, and still learning new things.

[pause]

Since Koldewey, archaeologists from many countries, and from Iraq itself, have dug in Babylon and studied what they found. The clay tablets have been read and translated, so that we now know the names of kings and scribes, merchants and star watchers. We know about the ordinary people of Babylon, their business, their prayers and their lives. The sky diaries, the maths tablets, the letters and the stories are still being studied in universities and museums around the world. Every year, new tablets are read, and new things are learned. This is, in a way, the final surprise about Babylon. The great tower is gone, and the king is gone, but the clay remembered. A scribe pressing a reed into a lump of mud, so long ago, has somehow managed to speak to us across more than twenty-five centuries.

[long-pause]

{img:91-babylon-ruins-today-palms-euphrates}

Babylon is now a World Heritage Site, and the river still flows past it slowly.

[pause]

Today, Babylon lies in Iraq, about eighty-five kilometres south of Baghdad, beside the Euphrates. It has been recognised as a World Heritage Site, protected as one of the most important places in the history of the world. Visitors can walk through the ruins along old streets between walls of mud brick. Some are original and some have been rebuilt in modern times. They can see the lower part of the Ishtar Gate, where the moulded bulls and dragons are still in place, without their glaze. They can walk along part of the holy road and see a great stone lion carved from black rock, which has stood among the ruins for many centuries. Around the site, there are palm groves, fields, canals and villages. And the river flows past slowly, as it did in the days of Nebuchadnezzar, though over the centuries it has moved a little to the west.

[long-pause]

{img:92-babylon-legacy-clock-stars-museum}

Babylon lives on in our hours, our minutes and the names of our stars.

[pause]

In museums around the world, Babylon still lives. In Berlin, the Ishtar Gate rises in blue and gold under the roof. In Baghdad, London, Paris and New York, the lions of the holy road are shown and admired, and so are the tablets of the scribes and the bricks of Nebuchadnezzar. But Babylon lives on in less obvious places too. It lives in our sixty minutes and our three hundred and sixty degrees. It lives in the names of the stars and the signs of the zodiac. It lives in the stories of the Bible, in our idea of a city that reaches the sky, and in the very habit of writing things down so that they last. The city was swept away, but it left a great deal behind. It is a quiet thought to carry into sleep, that so much of us began beside a slow brown river.

[long-pause]

And so the day ends over Babylon, as it did on thousands of ordinary evenings long ago.

[pause]

The sun slips behind the palm groves on the far bank. The blue of the great gate deepens to the colour of the night sky. On the flat roofs, mats are unrolled, and a child asks one last question about the stars. A small clay lamp is blown out. Far above the sleeping streets, the tower stands dark against the first stars, and the river carries on towards the sea, slow and silent.

[pause]

If sleep has not quite come yet, another gentle story from long ago is ready on the screen, whenever you want it. And if this one has been good company, a subscription helps you find the next one easily.

[pause]

Let the roofs of Babylon grow dim. The stars are doing all the watching now, and you may rest.

[pause]

Sleep well, and good night.
