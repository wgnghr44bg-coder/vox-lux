At night, on the roofs of the temples of Babylon, and on the top of the great ziggurat, the astronomers watch the sky.

The sky over Babylon is wonderful, at night. The air is dry, and clear, and there are no city lights, only the little lamps, in the houses, and the fires, on the walls, so that the stars shine, bright, and sharp, in their thousands, from horizon to horizon, over the flat land.

--

The astronomers are priests, and scribes, of the temple of Marduk, trained, for many years, in the old knowledge of the sky. And every night, for hundreds of years, they have watched, and written down, what they see.

They write it in astronomical diaries: clay tablets, on which, night after night, month after month, they record the positions of the moon, and the planets, among the stars, and when they rise, and set, and the eclipses, and the comets, and the shooting stars, and the weather, the clouds, and the rain, and the wind, and the level of the river, and the prices of barley, and dates, and wool, and oil, in the market, and the important events, in the city, and the world.

{img:night-sky-babylon-moon-venus-jupiter-ziggurat-silhouette-desert}
These diaries began, as far as we know, around 650 BC, and they continued, for six hundred years, until the time of the Romans. It is the longest scientific record, kept by any civilisation, in the ancient world. Hundreds of them survive, in the British Museum, in London, and scholars still use them, today, to study ancient eclipses, and the history of the earth's rotation.

--

With all these observations, the Babylonian astronomers learned to predict the movements of the sky: when the new moon would appear, and when the eclipses of the moon would come, and the movements of the planets, Venus, and Jupiter, and Mars, and Saturn, and Mercury, using clever mathematical methods, with their numbers, in sixties.

Some scholars think, recently, that the Babylonians even used a kind of geometry, to calculate the path of Jupiter, across the sky, a method that was not used again, in Europe, until more than a thousand years later.

{img:zodiac-constellations-painted-sky-lion-bull-scorpion-babylonian-style}
The Babylonians also named the stars, and the constellations, and many of the names we use today, come from them. They divided the path of the sun, and the moon, and the planets, across the sky, into twelve parts, each with its own constellation: the Bull, and the Lion, and the Scorpion, and the Twins, and the Scales, and the others, which, later, became the twelve signs of the zodiac.

--

For the Babylonians, the sky is a great book, written by the gods, in which they can read the future. An eclipse, or a strange movement of a planet, is a message, a sign, of what is to come, for the king, and the country. And so, the astronomers send reports to the king, of what they have seen, and what it may mean.

But they also simply watch, and record, and calculate, night after night, with great patience, and care, and love, for the sky. And on a warm night, in Babylon, you can see them, on the roof of the temple, wrapped in their cloaks, looking up, at the moon, rising, over the palm trees, and the river, and the great dark plain, and pressing their reed, softly, into the clay.
