Nebuchadnezzar rules for forty-three years, and dies, an old man, in 562 BC.

After him, Babylon has several kings, in a short time, and then, the last king of Babylon, Nabonidus, an unusual, scholarly man, who loves the moon god, Sin, more than Marduk, and spends ten years, far away, in the deserts of Arabia, at the oasis of Tayma, leaving his son, Belshazzar, to rule, in Babylon.

--

And in the year 539 BC, sixty-one years after our story, Cyrus the Great, the king of the Persians, comes, with his army, from the east. And Babylon is taken, quickly, and almost without a fight. The Persian soldiers enter the city, and the people of Babylon, it is said, welcome Cyrus, with green branches, and he enters the city, in peace, and honours Marduk, and the temples, and lets the people live, as before.

On a clay cylinder, now in the British Museum, the Cyrus Cylinder, Cyrus tells how Marduk chose him, to be king of Babylon, and how he let the people, who had been brought to Babylon, from other lands, go home, with their gods.

{img:alexander-the-great-babylon-palace-323bc-quiet-evening-columns}
Babylon becomes one of the capitals of the Persian Empire, rich, and great, for two hundred years. Then, in 331 BC, Alexander the Great comes, and takes it, from the Persians, and plans to make it the capital of his own great empire, stretching from Greece to India. But in 323 BC, he dies, in Babylon, in the palace of Nebuchadnezzar, of a fever, at the age of only thirty-two.

--

After Alexander, his generals found new cities, and slowly, the people move away from Babylon. The old temples are still kept, and the astronomers still watch the sky, and write their diaries, on clay, for another two hundred years. The last cuneiform tablet that we know of, an astronomical almanac, was written in Babylonia, in about 75 AD, and after that, the old writing was forgotten.

And slowly, over the centuries, Babylon fades. The river changes its course. The canals silt up. The great buildings crumble. The people take the bricks away, to build new houses, in new villages, and towns. And the great city becomes a field of ruins, and mounds, and broken bricks, in the desert, beside the river.

{img:ruined-mounds-babylon-desert-broken-bricks-palms-medieval-traveller}
Travellers came, now and then, over the centuries, and looked at the mounds, and wondered if this could really be the great Babylon, of the Bible, and the old stories. And the local people dug in the ruins, for bricks, and found the stamped bricks, of Nebuchadnezzar, and used them, in their houses, and their walls, and their canals, so that the king's name was built into houses, and dams, all around the region.

--

But Babylon was not forgotten. Its name lived on, in the Bible, and in the writings of the Greeks, and the Romans, and in poems, and stories, and paintings, and songs, all over the world, for two thousand years: a name of wonder, and splendour, and mystery, waiting, under the earth, to be found again.
