Let us spend a day with this family of Babylon.

The father is a scribe, who works for the temple of Marduk, writing contracts, and accounts, on clay tablets. The mother runs the house, and weaves, and has a little business of her own, lending small amounts of silver, to her neighbours. There are four children, and the father's old mother, and a servant girl, and a little dog.

--

They wake at dawn, when the light comes into the courtyard, and the birds begin to sing, in the palm tree, next door. The servant girl lights the fire, in the clay oven, in the corner of the courtyard, and the mother makes bread: flat, round loaves, of barley flour, slapped onto the hot inside wall of the oven, where they bake, in a few minutes, and are peeled off, hot, and soft, and smelling wonderful.

They eat breakfast, sitting on mats, in the courtyard: bread, and dates, and a little cheese, and some onions, and a drink of beer, thick, and sweet, made from barley, which everyone drinks, even the children.

{img:scribe-father-walking-street-tablets-basket-temple-morning-crowd}
Then the father goes to work, at the temple, walking through the narrow streets, which are already busy, with people, and donkeys, and carts, and water carriers, and sellers, calling their wares. He carries his stylus, a sharp reed, for writing, and a little basket, of clay tablets.

--

The eldest son, who is twelve, goes to school, at the house of a teacher, where he is learning to be a scribe, like his father. The younger children stay at home, and play, in the courtyard, and in the street, with the neighbours' children, with clay toys, and balls, and spinning tops, and little carts, on wheels, pulled by a string.

The mother spins, and weaves, at her loom, in the courtyard, in the shade, making cloth, of wool, for the family's clothes. And she grinds the barley, on a grinding stone, with the servant girl, and goes to the market, to buy what she needs, and to visit her friends, and her sister, who lives in the next street.

{img:babylonian-women-chatting-courtyard-spinning-wool-afternoon-shade}
In the heat of the afternoon, everyone rests, in the cool, dark rooms, while the sun beats down, on the courtyard, and the streets are almost empty, and quiet.

--

In the late afternoon, the father comes home, and washes, and the family eats the main meal of the day: bread, and lentils, and chickpeas, and onions, and garlic, and leeks, cooked together, in a stew, with a little mutton, or fish, from the river, with sesame oil, and herbs, and cumin, and coriander, and dates, and figs, and pomegranates, for afterwards.

And in the evening, as the sun goes down, and the air cools, they climb up onto the roof, with a jug of beer, and some dates, and sit, together, on their mats, and talk, and watch the sky turn red, and gold, and purple, over the city, and the great tower, and the river, and the palm trees, and the swallows, swooping, and the bats, coming out.

{img:babylon-rooftops-evening-families-sitting-ziggurat-silhouette-sunset}
The grandmother tells the children stories, old, old stories, of the gods, and the heroes: of Gilgamesh, the great king of Uruk, and his friend, Enkidu, the wild man, and their adventures, and of the great flood, long ago, when the gods sent the waters over the whole earth, and one wise man, Utnapishtim, built a great boat, and saved his family, and the animals, and of the clever god, Ea, who lives in the sweet waters, under the earth.

--

And the children fall asleep, on the roof, one by one, under the stars, while the grown-ups talk, softly, and the city grows quiet, around them.
