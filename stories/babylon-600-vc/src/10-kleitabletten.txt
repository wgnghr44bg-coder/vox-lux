In Babylon, people write on clay.

The writing is made of little wedge-shaped marks. A writer presses them into soft wet clay with the sharp end of a reed. Each sign is made of several wedges. Long and short, standing up and lying down. Today we call this writing cuneiform. It means wedge-shaped.

--

It is the oldest writing in the world. The Sumerians invented it more than five thousand years ago. At first it was little pictures drawn on clay. Pictures of the things they were counting, like sheep and barley and jars of oil. Over the centuries the pictures became simpler and turned into wedges. The signs came to stand for sounds as well as words. Then people could write down anything at all.

By the year 600 before Christ, this writing has been used for more than two and a half thousand years. It is used for many languages. There are hundreds of different signs. It takes many years to learn them all.

{img:clay-tablets-drying-sun-rows-courtyard-scribes-workshop}
The writer takes a lump of smooth damp clay. He shapes it in his hands into a tablet, small enough to hold in one hand. It is flat on one side and a little rounded on the other, like a small cushion. He holds it in his left hand and the reed in his right. He presses the signs quickly, line after line. He turns it over to write on the back, and even on the edges.

--

Then the tablet dries in the sun and gets hard. If it is important, it is baked in an oven. Then it becomes as hard as a brick and will last almost forever. Letters are put in envelopes made of clay too. The address is written on the outside. You have to break the envelope to read the letter.

Clay tablets are cheap and easy to make. And they last. Paper and leather rot and burn. But clay survives for thousands of years in the ground. When a library burns, the fire bakes the tablets hard. And that keeps them safe.

{img:library-shelves-clay-tablets-baskets-labels-temple-archive-lamp}
So hundreds of thousands of clay tablets have been found in the ruins of this land. Letters and contracts and laws. Lists and dictionaries and prayers. Stories and poems. Medicine and maths and star charts. School work and recipes.

--

Many are still unread. They wait in museum store rooms around the world for scholars to study them. Every year more are read and translated. And we learn new things about the people who wrote them. Their business and their worries. Their prayers and their jokes and their lives.

In the year 600 before Christ, a new kind of writing is also spreading. It is a simple alphabet with only twenty-two letters. It is written with ink on leather. It is much easier to learn. Many people now speak this new language in the streets and write it with pen and ink. But for important things, the writers still use the old wedges on clay. For temples and laws and contracts and the stars. Just as their ancestors have done for thousands of years.
