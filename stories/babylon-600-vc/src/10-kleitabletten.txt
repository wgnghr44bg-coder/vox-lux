In Babylon, people write on clay.

The writing is called cuneiform, from the Latin word for a wedge, because it is made of little wedge-shaped marks, pressed into soft, wet clay, with the end of a reed, cut to a sharp point, like a little triangle. Each sign is made of several wedges, long, and short, upright, and lying down, and slanting, pressed in, in a pattern.

--

It is the oldest writing system in the world. It was invented, by the Sumerians, more than five thousand years ago, at first as little pictures, drawn on clay, of the things they were counting: sheep, and barley, and jars of oil. Then, over the centuries, the pictures became simpler, and turned into wedges, and the signs came to stand for sounds, and syllables, as well as words, so that they could write down anything, in any language.

By 600 BC, cuneiform has been used for more than two and a half thousand years, for many different languages: Sumerian, and Akkadian, and Babylonian, and Assyrian, and Hittite, and Elamite, and Persian. There are hundreds of different signs, and it takes many years to learn them all.

{img:clay-tablets-drying-sun-rows-courtyard-scribes-workshop}
The scribe takes a lump of clay, smooth, and damp, and shapes it, in his hands, into a tablet, small enough to hold in one hand, flat on one side, and slightly rounded on the other, like a little cushion. He holds it in his left hand, and the reed in his right, and presses the signs into it, quickly, line after line, turning the tablet over, to write on the back, and even on the edges.

--

Then the tablet is left to dry, in the sun, and becomes hard. If it is important, it is baked, in an oven, and becomes as hard as a brick, and will last, almost forever. Letters are put into envelopes, also made of clay, with the address written on the outside, and the envelope must be broken, to read the letter.

Clay tablets are cheap, and easy to make, and they last. Paper, and parchment, and papyrus, rot, and burn. But clay survives, for thousands of years, in the ground, and when a library burns, the fire bakes the tablets, and preserves them.

{img:library-shelves-clay-tablets-baskets-labels-temple-archive-lamp}
And so, hundreds of thousands of clay tablets have been found, in the ruins of the cities of Mesopotamia: letters, and contracts, and laws, and accounts, and lists, and dictionaries, and prayers, and hymns, and stories, and poems, and medical texts, and mathematics, and astronomy, and school exercises, and recipes, and spells.

--

Many of them are still unread, waiting, in the storerooms of museums, around the world, for scholars to study them. Every year, more are read, and translated, and published, and we learn new things about the people who wrote them: their business, and their worries, and their prayers, and their jokes, and their lives.

In Babylon, in the year 600 BC, a new script is also spreading: the alphabet of the Aramaeans, with only twenty-two letters, written in ink, on parchment, and leather, and potsherds, which is much easier to learn. Many people now speak Aramaic, in the streets, and write it, with pen, and ink. But for important things, for temples, and laws, and contracts, and astronomy, the scribes still use the ancient cuneiform, on clay, as their ancestors have done, for thousands of years.
