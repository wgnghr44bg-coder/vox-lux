Babylon is a city of trade.

This land has plenty of barley and dates and wool and clay and reeds. But it has almost no stone. It has no good wood except palm. And it has no metal. So everything else must come from far away. The traders of Babylon trade with the whole known world.

--

From the mountains in the west comes cedar wood for the roofs and doors of temples and palaces. It floats down the rivers on great rafts. From the mountains in the north and east come copper and tin, iron and silver and gold. From far to the east comes a deep blue stone flecked with gold. The Babylonians love it more than any other stone. From Egypt and Arabia come incense and spices. And from India, by sea, come ivory and cotton and precious stones.

In return, Babylon sends out its own goods. Barley and dates and sesame oil. And above all, cloth. Fine wool cloth, dyed red and blue and purple, and sewn with patterns. It is famous everywhere.

{img:camel-donkey-caravan-arriving-babylon-gate-traders-bundles}
The traders travel in long lines of donkeys, and more and more on camels. They cross the deserts from one water hole to the next. The journey is long and hot. If they miss a water hole, they are in great danger. Others travel by boat along the rivers to the sea.

--

There is no single big market square in Babylon. Trade happens everywhere. At the city gates and on the quays where the boats unload. In the streets and in little shops. In the temples and in the houses of rich trading families.

There are no coins yet. Coins have just been invented far away in the west. But they have not reached Babylon. Instead people pay in silver, weighed on scales, in little pieces and rings. Or they pay in barley. A small weight of silver called a shekel is the usual measure. Everything has its price in shekels and in barley.

{img:merchant-weighing-silver-scales-stone-weights-clay-tablet-contract}
And everything is written down. When people buy or sell or lend or rent, they go to a writer. When they marry or adopt a child, they go to a writer. He writes a contract on a clay tablet. It has the names of the people and the witnesses and the date. Then they press their seals into the clay to sign it. Some just press their fingernail.

--

Thousands of these contracts have been found in the ruins. They tell us about the business of ordinary people two and a half thousand years ago. The price of a house and an ox and a field. The price of a jar of oil. The rent of a boat. The wages of a worker.

One great family of Babylon were traders and bankers for five generations. More than a thousand of their tablets have been found. They bought and sold houses and fields and dates and barley. They lent money and rented out land. And their tablets tell the story of their family for more than a hundred years. Their business, their marriages and their quarrels.
