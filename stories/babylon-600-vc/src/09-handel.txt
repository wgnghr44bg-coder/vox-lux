Babylon is a city of trade.

Mesopotamia is a land rich in barley, and dates, and wool, and clay, and reeds, and bitumen. But it has almost no stone, no timber, except palm wood, and no metals. So everything else must be brought from far away, and the merchants of Babylon trade with the whole known world.

--

From the mountains of Lebanon, in the west, comes cedar wood, for the roofs, and the doors, of the temples, and the palaces, floated down the rivers, in great rafts. From the mountains of Iran, and Anatolia, come copper, and tin, to make bronze, and iron, and silver, and gold. From Afghanistan, far to the east, comes lapis lazuli, the deep blue stone, flecked with gold, which the Babylonians love above all others. From Egypt, and Arabia, come incense, and myrrh, and spices. From India, by sea, and up the Persian Gulf, come ivory, and cotton, and precious stones.

And in return, Babylon sends out its own goods: barley, and dates, and sesame oil, and above all, cloth: fine woollen cloth, woven, and dyed, in brilliant colours, red, and blue, and purple, and embroidered, which is famous, everywhere.

{img:camel-donkey-caravan-arriving-babylon-gate-traders-bundles}
The merchants travel in caravans, with long lines of donkeys, and, more and more, camels, across the deserts, from oasis to oasis, and along the rivers, in boats, to the sea.

--

In Babylon, there is no single great market place, like a Greek, or Roman forum. Instead, trade happens everywhere: at the city gates, and on the quays, by the river, where the boats unload, and in the streets, and in little shops, opening onto the streets, and in the temples, and the houses of the great merchant families.

There is no coined money, yet. Coins have just been invented, in Lydia, in Turkey, but they have not yet come to Babylon. Instead, people pay in silver, weighed out, on scales, in little pieces, and rings, and coils, and in barley. A shekel of silver, about eight grams, is the common measure of value, and everything has its price, in shekels, and in barley.

{img:merchant-weighing-silver-scales-stone-weights-clay-tablet-contract}
And everything is written down. When the people of Babylon buy, or sell, or lend, or rent, or hire, or marry, or adopt a child, or divide an inheritance, they go to a scribe, and he writes a contract, on a clay tablet, with the names of the people, and the witnesses, and the date, and the agreement, and they press their seals, or their fingernails, into the clay, to sign it.

--

Thousands of these contracts have been found, in the ruins of Babylon, and the other cities, and they tell us about the business of ordinary people, two and a half thousand years ago: the price of a house, and a slave, and an ox, and a field, and a jar of oil, and the rent of a boat, and the interest on a loan, and the wages of a worker.

One great family of Babylon, the Egibi family, were businessmen, and bankers, for five generations, during these years, and their archive, of more than a thousand tablets, has been found. They bought, and sold, houses, and fields, and dates, and barley, and lent money, and rented out land, and managed property, for other people, and their tablets tell the story of their business, and their family, their marriages, and their quarrels, for more than a hundred years.
