In these years, among the many peoples who come to Babylon, there are some who do not come of their own free will.

In the year 597 BC, and again, ten years later, in 587 BC, Nebuchadnezzar's armies went to the little kingdom of Judah, far to the west, in the hills, beyond the desert, with its capital, Jerusalem, which had refused to obey him. The city was taken, and the temple of Jerusalem was destroyed, and the king of Judah, and many of its people, the nobles, and the priests, and the craftsmen, and their families, were brought, a long way, across the desert, to Babylon, to live in exile.

--

We will leave the sad days of the war, and the long journey, there, gently, in the past, and think of the people, and their new lives, in a strange land.

The exiles are not kept in prisons. They are settled, in villages, and towns, in the countryside, around Babylon, beside the canals, and given land, to farm, and houses, to live in. Some of them live in the city itself. They can work, and farm, and trade, and marry, and build houses, and plant gardens, and keep their own customs, and their own faith.

{img:clay-ration-tablet-cuneiform-king-jehoiachin-palace-storeroom}
The king of Judah, Jehoiachin, lives at the court of Babylon, with his family, and his servants. Archaeologists have found clay tablets, in the storerooms of Nebuchadnezzar's palace, which list the rations of oil, and barley, given to him, and his five sons, and the other foreigners, at the court. His name is written there, in cuneiform: Ya'ukin, king of the land of Yahud.

--

And there are other tablets, found more recently, from a little town, in the countryside, near Babylon, called Al-Yahudu, which means the town of Judah, where the exiles lived, and farmed, and paid their taxes, and made their contracts, and their names, Hebrew names, appear in the tablets, for several generations: fathers, and sons, and grandsons, living, and working, and raising their families, beside the canals of Babylon.

But many of the exiles are homesick, for their own land, and their hills, and their city, and their temple. And they remember it, and sing about it. One of the most famous songs, of the Bible, Psalm 137, begins:

{img:willow-trees-babylon-canal-quiet-dusk-lyres-hanging-branches}
By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion. We hanged our harps upon the willows in the midst thereof.

--

And it was in Babylon, during the exile, that many of the exiles, and their teachers, and priests, gathered together, and wrote down, and collected, and copied, and studied, the old writings, and the laws, and the stories, and the prophecies, of their people, so that they would not be forgotten, far from home. And much of what became the Hebrew Bible was shaped, during these years, beside the rivers of Babylon.

The book of Daniel tells stories of young men of Judah, at the court of Nebuchadnezzar: Daniel, and his three friends, Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, who were taught the language, and the writing, of the Babylonians, and served the king, wisely, but stayed faithful to their own God.

{img:exile-family-courtyard-babylon-lamp-reading-scroll-children-listening}
Fifty years later, when the Persians come, and take Babylon, the Persian king, Cyrus, will allow the exiles to go home, to Jerusalem, and rebuild their temple. Many of them go. But many stay, in Babylon, where they have been born, and grown up, and made their homes, and their lives.

--

And for more than two and a half thousand years, until our own times, there was a great, and learned, Jewish community, in Babylon, and Mesopotamia, which became one of the most important centres of Jewish learning, in the whole world, and wrote, many centuries later, the great book of law, and teaching, called the Babylonian Talmud.
