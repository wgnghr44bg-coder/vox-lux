In these years, many peoples come to Babylon. But some of them do not come because they want to.

Far to the west, beyond the desert, lies a small kingdom called Judah. Its capital is Jerusalem. It refused to obey Nebuchadnezzar. Twice, his armies went there. The city was taken and its temple was destroyed. The king of Judah and many of his people were brought a long way across the desert to Babylon. Nobles and priests and craftsmen and their families. They were made to live far from home. They are called the exiles.

--

We will leave the sad days of the war and the long journey there, gently, in the past. And we will think of the people and their new lives in a strange land.

The exiles are not kept in prisons. They are settled in villages and towns around Babylon, beside the canals. They are given land to farm and houses to live in. Some live in the city itself. They can work and farm and trade and marry. They can build houses and plant gardens. And they can keep their own customs and their own faith.

{img:clay-ration-tablet-cuneiform-king-jehoiachin-palace-storeroom}
The king of Judah lives at the court of Babylon with his family and servants. Archaeologists found clay tablets in the palace store rooms. They list the oil and barley given to the foreign guests at court. And there is his name, written in wedges. The king of the land of Judah, and his five sons.

--

Other tablets were found more recently. They come from a small town near Babylon. Its name means the town of Judah. The exiles lived there and farmed and paid their taxes and made contracts. Their Hebrew names appear in the tablets for several generations. Fathers and sons and grandsons, living and working and raising their families beside the canals of Babylon.

But many of the exiles are homesick for their own land and hills and city and temple. And they remember it and sing about it. One of the most famous songs in the Bible begins like this.

{img:willow-trees-babylon-canal-quiet-dusk-lyres-hanging-branches}
By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, and we wept, when we remembered home. We hung our harps upon the willows.

--

In Babylon, during these years, the exiles and their teachers did something important. They gathered and wrote down and copied the old writings of their people. Their laws and stories and the words of their prophets. That way nothing would be forgotten, far from home. Much of what became the Hebrew Bible took shape in these years, beside the rivers of Babylon.

The Bible also tells stories of young men from Judah at the court of Nebuchadnezzar. Daniel and his three friends. They learned the language and writing of the Babylonians and served the king wisely. But they stayed faithful to their own God, even when it was dangerous.

{img:exile-family-courtyard-babylon-lamp-reading-scroll-children-listening}
About sixty years later, the Persians will take Babylon. The Persian king will allow the exiles to go home to Jerusalem and rebuild their temple. Many will go. But many will stay in Babylon. They were born there and grew up there. They have made their homes and their lives there.

--

For more than two and a half thousand years, until our own times, there was a great and learned Jewish community in this land. It became one of the most important centres of Jewish learning in the world. Many centuries later, it wrote a great book of law and teaching that is still studied today.
