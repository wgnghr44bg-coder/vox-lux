In Babylon, there are judges, and courts, and laws.

More than a thousand years before our story, the great king Hammurabi, of Babylon, had his laws carved, on a tall stone, of black basalt, more than two metres high, and set up, in a temple, so that everyone could see them. At the top, there is a carving, of the king, standing, before the sun god, Shamash, the god of justice, who gives him the laws. And below, in close, neat columns, there are the laws themselves: nearly three hundred of them.

--

The laws deal with everything: with property, and trade, and wages, and debts, and loans, and marriage, and divorce, and inheritance, and adoption, and the care of fields, and canals, and animals, and boats, and houses, and doctors, and builders, and tavern-keepers. Some of the punishments are very harsh, in the old way, an eye for an eye. But many of the laws are about fairness, and protecting the weak.

In his words, at the beginning, and the end, Hammurabi says that he wrote the laws so that the strong should not oppress the weak, and so that the orphan, and the widow, should have justice. And anyone who has a quarrel, he says, should come, and stand before his stone, and read the laws, and understand his case, and his heart will be at peace.

{img:basalt-stele-laws-hammurabi-shamash-relief-top-columns-cuneiform}
By the year 600 BC, the laws of Hammurabi are very old, but they are still copied, by the scribes, in their schools, as a classic text, like the laws of the ancestors. And the idea that the king must give justice, and protect the weak, is still at the heart of kingship, in Babylon.

--

The stone of Hammurabi was carried away, long after, to the city of Susa, in Iran, as a trophy of war, and was found there, by French archaeologists, in 1901. Today, it stands in the Louvre Museum, in Paris.

In Nebuchadnezzar's Babylon, when there is a dispute, people go to the judges, who sit at the gates of the temples, or in the courts, and listen, to both sides, and to the witnesses, and look at the contracts, on their tablets, and decide. Sometimes, they ask people to swear an oath, before the gods, and the symbols of the gods, that they are telling the truth, which is a very serious, and frightening thing to do.

{img:oath-before-divine-symbols-temple-courtyard-witnesses-solemn-light}
And everything is written down, on clay: the case, and the witnesses, and the judgement, and the names of the judges, and the date. So, two and a half thousand years later, we can still read about a quarrel, over the boundary of a field, or the price of a slave, or the return of a dowry, or the payment of a debt, and how the judges of Babylon decided it.

--

Most quarrels, though, never come to court. They are settled, quietly, by the families, and the neighbours, and the elders, of the street, sitting together, in a courtyard, in the evening, over a jar of beer, and talking, until everyone agrees, and goes home, to sleep.
