In Babylon there are judges and courts and laws.

More than a thousand years before our story, a great king of Babylon called Hammurabi had his laws carved on a tall black stone. It is more than two metres high. He set it up in a temple so everyone could see it. At the top there is a picture of the king. He stands before the sun god, the god of justice, who gives him the laws. Below, in neat close lines, are the laws themselves. There are nearly three hundred.

--

The laws deal with everything. Property and trade and wages. Debts and marriage and divorce. Fields and canals and animals and boats. Doctors and builders and tavern keepers. Some punishments are very harsh, in the old way. But many laws are about fairness and protecting the weak.

Hammurabi wrote why he made the laws. So that the strong would not crush the weak, he said. So that orphans and widows would get justice. Anyone with a quarrel, he said, should come and stand before his stone. They should read the laws and understand their case. And then their heart would be at peace.

{img:basalt-stele-laws-hammurabi-shamash-relief-top-columns-cuneiform}
By the year 600 before Christ these laws are very old. But writers still copy them in their schools, as a classic text. And the idea that the king must give justice and protect the weak is still at the heart of being king in Babylon.

--

Long after, Hammurabi's stone was carried away to a city in Iran. It was a prize of war. About a hundred and twenty years ago, French archaeologists found it there. Today it stands in a museum in Paris.

In Nebuchadnezzar's Babylon, when there is a quarrel, people go to the judges. The judges sit at the temple gates or in the courts. They listen to both sides and to the witnesses. They look at the contracts on their tablets. And they decide. Sometimes they ask people to swear an oath before the gods that they are telling the truth. That is a very serious thing. People's hands shake when they do it. Nobody wants to lie before the gods.

{img:oath-before-divine-symbols-temple-courtyard-witnesses-solemn-light}
And everything is written down on clay. The case and the witnesses and the decision and the date. So two and a half thousand years later we can still read about a quarrel over the edge of a field. Or the return of a wedding gift. Or the payment of a debt. And we can read how the judges of Babylon decided it.

--

Most quarrels never come to court though. Families and neighbours settle them quietly. The elders of the street sit together in a courtyard in the evening, over a jar of beer. They talk until everyone agrees. And then everyone goes home to sleep.
