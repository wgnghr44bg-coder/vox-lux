Beside the ziggurat, there is the greatest temple of Babylon: Esagila, the temple of Marduk.

Its name means: the house whose top is high. It is a great complex of buildings, around courtyards, with thick walls, and tall gateways, and many shrines, and rooms, for the gods, and the priests, and the treasures, and the offerings.

--

In the heart of it, in the most holy room, there lives the god himself: Marduk, the lord of Babylon, the king of the gods, in the form of a great statue, made of precious wood, covered with gold, dressed in rich robes, embroidered with gold, and jewels, sitting on a golden throne, with a golden table, before him.

The Greek writer Herodotus, who wrote about Babylon later, said that the statue, and the throne, and the table, together, were made of so much gold, that it weighed more than twenty tons. That may be an exaggeration. But the Babylonians themselves wrote that the shrine was covered with gold, like the sun.

{img:priests-feeding-god-statue-golden-table-food-offerings-shrine}
Every day, the priests of Esagila take care of Marduk, as if he were a great king. In the morning, they wake him, with songs, and prayers, and wash him, and dress him, in fresh clothes. Twice a day, they bring him meals: great feasts, of bread, and cakes, and meat, and fish, and fruit, and dates, and beer, and wine, set out on the golden table, before him, with music, and incense, and then, after the god has eaten, the food is shared out, among the priests, and the temple workers, and sent to the king's table.

--

At night, the priests put the god to bed, and close the doors of his shrine, and the temple is quiet, with only the lamps, burning, and the guards, watching.

Marduk is the great god of Babylon. He was once only a small god, of this one city. But as Babylon grew great, so did he, and now he is the king of all the gods. In the great poem of creation, the Enuma Elish, which the priests recite, every year, at the New Year festival, it tells how, at the beginning of time, there was only water, and chaos, ruled by Tiamat, the goddess of the salt sea, and how the young god Marduk defeated her, and made the heavens, and the earth, from her body, and set the stars, and the moon, in their places, and made people, to serve the gods, and how the gods built Babylon for him, as his home.

{img:temple-workers-esagila-scribes-musicians-singers-courtyard-day}
Esagila is not only a temple. It is like a small city. Hundreds of people work there: priests, and singers, and musicians, and scribes, and astronomers, and cooks, and bakers, and brewers, and weavers, and goldsmiths, and carpenters, and gardeners, and herdsmen. The temple owns land, and fields, and palm groves, and flocks, and herds, all around Babylon, and lends money, and grain, and keeps careful accounts, of everything, on clay tablets.

--

And the people of Babylon come to Esagila, to pray, and to make offerings, and to ask the god for help: for healing, and for children, and for good fortune, and for justice. They bring little gifts, and stand in the courtyard, and look toward the shrine, where the god sits, in the golden dimness, and pray, with their hands raised to their faces.

And the smell of incense, of cedar, and cypress, and myrrh, drifts out, over the courtyards, and the walls, into the streets of the city, all day long.
