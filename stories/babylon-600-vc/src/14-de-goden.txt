The world of the Babylonians is full of gods.

There are the great gods, of the heavens, and the earth, and the waters: Anu, the old god of the sky, and Enlil, the lord of the wind, and Ea, the clever, kind god of the sweet waters, under the earth, and of wisdom, and magic, who loves people, and helps them. There is Shamash, the sun god, who sees everything, as he crosses the sky, every day, and is the god of justice, and truth. There is Sin, the moon god, with his silver crescent, and Adad, the god of storms, and rain, with his lightning.

--

There is Ishtar, the goddess of love, and war, the morning, and evening, star, beautiful, and fierce, and many other goddesses: Gula, the goddess of healing, with her dog, and Ninlil, and Nisaba, and Ninkasi, the goddess of beer.

And above them all, in Babylon, there is Marduk, the king of the gods, and his son, Nabu, the god of writing, and wisdom.

{img:temple-of-ishtar-babylon-courtyard-worshippers-raising-hands-prayer}
Every city has its own god, and its own temple. And in Babylon, there are dozens of temples, large, and small, all over the city, for all the gods, and hundreds of little shrines, at the street corners, and in the walls, where people stop, and leave a little offering, of flour, or dates, or a little lamp, and say a prayer.

--

And every family has its own god, too: a personal god, or goddess, who watches over them, and protects them, and speaks for them, to the great gods. In their houses, in a corner, or a niche, in the wall, they keep little figures, of clay, of their god, and of protective spirits, and they make offerings, every day, and pray.

The Babylonians believe that the gods send messages, and signs, everywhere: in the stars, and the clouds, and the flight of birds, and the behaviour of animals, and the patterns of oil, poured on water, and the smoke of incense, and dreams. And there are special priests, diviners, who know how to read these signs, and tell what the gods want, and what the future will bring.

{img:diviner-priest-reading-oil-on-water-bowl-client-temple-room}
They also believe in spirits, good, and bad, all around them, in the desert, and the wind, and the dark. And so they wear little amulets, around their necks, and bury little figures, of protective spirits, under the floors, and the doorways, of their houses, to keep the bad spirits away. Great winged bulls, and lions, with human heads, the lamassu, stand at the gates of the palaces, to protect them.

--

When they are ill, they go to two kinds of healer: the asu, who is like a doctor, and uses medicines, made from plants, and minerals, and bandages, and ointments, and the ashipu, who is like a priest, or a magician, and uses prayers, and spells, and rituals, to drive away the spirit, or the anger of the god, that has caused the illness. Often, they go to both.

The Babylonians do not believe in a happy heaven, after death. They believe that the dead go down, to a grey, quiet land, under the earth, where they live, a shadowy life, and depend on their families, to remember them, and give them food, and water. So the family remembers its ancestors, and speaks their names, and gives them offerings, every month, so that they will be at peace.

{img:babylonian-family-offering-ancestors-water-bread-floor-lamp-courtyard}
And in the evening, in many houses of Babylon, the mother, or the father, lights a little lamp, before the family's god, in the corner of the room, and pours a little water, and leaves a few dates, and a little bread, and says a quiet prayer, for the family, and the children, and the ancestors, and the house, and the night.
