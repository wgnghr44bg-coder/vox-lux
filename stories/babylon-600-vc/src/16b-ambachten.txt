All through Babylon, in the workshops, and the yards, and the streets, the craftsmen are at work.

The most important of all, in these years, are the brick makers. Babylon is built of bricks: millions of them, for the walls, and the temples, and the palaces, and the houses, and the streets, and the quays.

--

Outside the city, by the river, and the canals, where there is plenty of clay, and water, the brick makers work, in great yards. They mix the clay, with water, and chopped straw, with their feet, into a thick mud, and press it into wooden moulds, square, about thirty centimetres across, and turn them out, in long rows, on the ground, to dry, in the sun. In the summer heat, they dry in a few days.

Most bricks are simply dried in the sun. But the best ones, for the outer walls, and the floors, and the places that must resist water, are baked, in great kilns, burning reeds, and palm wood, and they come out hard, and yellow, and red. And for the most important buildings, they are stamped, before they dry, with the name of the king.

And then there are the glazers: the craftsmen who make the wonderful blue bricks, of the Ishtar Gate, and the Processional Way.

--

It is very skilled, and very difficult work. First, the bricks are made in special moulds, so that each brick has a part of the animal, a leg, or a mane, or a tail, standing out from the surface, and when all the bricks are laid together, in the right order, the whole animal appears. The craftsmen mark each brick, with a little sign, so that they know where it goes.

Then, the glaze is painted on: a kind of liquid glass, made from sand, and plant ash, and minerals, coloured blue, with cobalt, and copper, and yellow, with antimony, and lead, and white, and black, and brown. The colours are separated by thin lines, of a black, glassy paste, so that they do not run together. And then the bricks are fired, in the kiln, again, and the glaze melts, and becomes hard, and shining, like glass.

There are weavers, men, and women, working at their looms, in the temples, and the houses, weaving fine wool, and linen, and dyers, dipping the wool, in great vats, of red, and blue, and purple, and yellow, and embroiderers, and tailors.

--

There are metalworkers: goldsmiths, and silversmiths, making jewellery, and cups, and statues, for the temples, and the court, and coppersmiths, and blacksmiths, making tools, and weapons, and pots. There are carpenters, and boat builders, and reed workers, weaving baskets, and mats, and boats. There are potters, at their wheels, and leather workers, and perfume makers, mixing scented oils, of cedar, and myrrh, and stone carvers, making seals.

The seals are little cylinders, of stone, about the size of a finger, carved with tiny pictures, of gods, and animals, and people, and inscriptions, and when they are rolled across the wet clay, of a tablet, or a jar stopper, they leave a little picture, in the clay, like a signature. Everyone of importance in Babylon has a seal, and wears it, on a string, around the neck, or the wrist.

{img:cylinder-seal-rolled-on-clay-impression-tiny-figures-close-detail}
Many of the craftsmen work for the temples, and the palace, and are paid in barley, and dates, and wool, and oil, and silver. Others have their own workshops, and sell what they make, in the streets, and at the gates. And the same crafts are often passed down, from father to son, and mother to daughter, for generations, in the same families, in the same streets, of Babylon.
