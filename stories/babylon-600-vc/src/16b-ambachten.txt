All through Babylon, in workshops and yards and streets, the craftsmen are at work.

In these years, the most important of all are the brick makers. Babylon is built of bricks. Millions of them. For the walls and temples and palaces. For the houses and streets and quays.

--

Outside the city by the river there is plenty of clay and water. The brick makers work there in big yards. They mix clay with water and chopped straw. They tread it with their feet into thick mud. They press it into square wooden moulds. Then they turn the bricks out in long rows on the ground to dry in the sun. In the summer heat they dry in a few days. But if it rains, all that work is ruined.

Most bricks are simply dried in the sun. But the best ones are baked in big ovens. They come out hard and yellow and red. They are used for outer walls and floors and places that must stand up to water. For the most important buildings, each brick is stamped with the king's name before it dries.

Then there are the glaze makers. They make the wonderful blue bricks of the Ishtar Gate and the holy road.

--

It is very skilled and very difficult work. Each brick is made in a special mould. It has one part of an animal standing out from it. A leg, or a mane, or a tail. When all the bricks are laid in the right order, the whole animal appears. The craftsmen mark each brick with a little sign. That way they know exactly where it goes. One brick in the wrong place, and the lion would have a tail on its head.

Then the glaze is painted on. It is a kind of liquid glass, made from sand and plant ash and minerals. It is coloured blue and yellow and white and brown. Thin black lines keep the colours from running together. Then the bricks are baked again. The glaze melts and becomes hard and shining like glass.

{img:weaver-babylon-loom-dyed-wool-red-blue-patterns-workshop-light}
There are weavers, men and women, at their looms in the temples and houses. There are dyers who dip wool in great tubs of red and blue and purple and yellow. There are tailors and people who sew patterns into cloth.

--

There are goldsmiths and silversmiths making jewellery and cups and statues. There are blacksmiths making tools and pots. There are carpenters and boat builders. There are reed workers weaving baskets and mats and boats. There are potters at their wheels and leather workers. There are perfume makers mixing sweet oils. And there are stone carvers who make seals.

A seal is a little stone cylinder about the size of a finger. It is carved with tiny pictures of gods and animals and people. When you roll it across wet clay, it leaves a little picture. It is like a signature. Every important person in Babylon has a seal. They wear it on a string around the neck or the wrist.

{img:cylinder-seal-rolled-on-clay-impression-tiny-figures-close-detail}
Many craftsmen work for the temples and the palace. They are paid in barley and dates and wool and oil and silver. Others have their own workshops and sell what they make in the streets and at the gates. Often the same craft is passed down in the same family for generations. From father to son and mother to daughter, in the same streets of Babylon.
