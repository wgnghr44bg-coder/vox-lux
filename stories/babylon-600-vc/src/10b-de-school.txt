The eldest son of our family goes to school. He is learning to be a writer.

The Babylonians call the school the tablet house. It is not a big building. Usually it is the house of a master writer. He takes a few pupils and teaches them in a room or in his courtyard. Most pupils are boys, the sons of writers and priests and rich families. A few girls learn to write too.

--

The boys start young, at about seven or eight. They go to school every day from sunrise until evening, for many years. And they work hard.

First they learn to make tablets and hold the reed and press simple wedges. Then they learn the signs one by one. They copy them again and again from a model. The teacher writes the model on one side of a tablet. The pupil copies it on the other side. Then they learn lists of words. Trees and animals and stones and gods and cities. Then sayings and letters and hymns and stories.

{img:school-tablet-teacher-copy-pupil-copy-clumsy-wedges-close}
When they make a mistake, they smooth out the clay with their thumb and start again. Many school tablets have been found. On one side is the teacher's neat model. On the other is the pupil's clumsy copy, full of mistakes.

--

They also learn maths. Counting and times tables and fractions. How to measure a field. How many bricks a wall needs. How much earth must be dug for a canal. How much barley the workers should be paid.

The teachers are strict. There are old stories, copied by pupils for centuries, about life at school. A boy is late, and he gets punished. He talks too much, and he gets punished. He writes badly, and he gets punished. Then his father invites the teacher home. He gives him a good dinner and a new robe and a ring. And suddenly the teacher says the boy is the best pupil in the school.

{img:babylonian-boy-walking-home-school-tablet-satchel-street-evening}
These stories are funny and very human. They show that schoolboys and teachers and parents have not changed very much in four thousand years.

--

After many years of school, the young writers can work for the temples or the palace. They can work for traders or for the courts. They can become teachers or doctors or star watchers. They are respected and well paid and proud of their skill.

And they have a goddess of their own. She is the goddess of writing and of grain. At the end of their tablets, many writers wrote a little line. Praise be to the goddess of writing.
