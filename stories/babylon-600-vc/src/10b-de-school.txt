The eldest son of our family goes to school, to learn to be a scribe.

The Babylonians call the school the tablet house. It is not a big building, but usually the house of a teacher, a master scribe, who takes a few pupils, and teaches them, in a room, or in the courtyard of his house. Most of the pupils are boys, the sons of scribes, and priests, and rich families, though a few girls learn to write, too.

--

The boys start young, at about seven, or eight, and they go to school, every day, from sunrise, until evening, for many years. And they work hard.

First, they learn to make tablets, and to hold the reed, and to press the simple wedges. Then, they learn the signs, one by one, copying them, again, and again, from a model tablet, written by the teacher, on one side, and their own copy, on the other. Then, they learn lists of words: lists of trees, and animals, and stones, and gods, and professions, and cities, in Sumerian, and Babylonian. Then, proverbs, and sentences, and contracts, and letters, and hymns, and stories.

{img:school-tablet-teacher-copy-pupil-copy-clumsy-wedges-close}
When they make a mistake, they smooth out the clay, with their thumb, and start again. Many of these school tablets have been found, with the teacher's neat model, on one side, and the pupil's clumsy copy, on the other, full of mistakes.

--

And they learn mathematics: counting, and multiplication tables, and division, and fractions, and how to measure fields, and calculate the amount of bricks, for a wall, or the amount of earth, for a canal, or the amount of barley, for the workers' wages.

The teachers are strict. There are old Sumerian stories, written down, and copied, by the pupils, for centuries, about life at school: about a boy who is late, and is beaten, and who talks too much, and is beaten, and who writes badly, and is beaten. And about how his father invites the teacher home, and gives him a good dinner, and a new robe, and a ring, and then the teacher says that the boy is the best pupil in the school.

{img:babylonian-boy-walking-home-school-tablet-satchel-street-evening}
These stories are funny, and very human, and show that schoolboys, and teachers, and parents, have not changed very much, in four thousand years.

--

When they have finished school, after many years, the young scribes can work for the temples, or the palace, or the merchants, or the courts, or as teachers, or doctors, or astronomers, or diviners. They are respected, and well paid, and proud of their skill.

And they have a goddess of their own, Nisaba, the goddess of writing, and grain, and a god, Nabu, the god of writing, and wisdom, whose symbol is a stylus, standing on a tablet. At the end of their tablets, many scribes wrote a little line: Praise be to Nisaba.
