Around Babylon, and along all the rivers, and canals, of Mesopotamia, there are the date palms.

They grow in great groves, tall, and slender, with their crowns of long, green, feathery leaves, rustling in the wind, and their great bunches of dates, hanging below, orange, and red, and brown, as they ripen, in the late summer.

--

The date palm is the tree of life, for the Babylonians. Its fruit is sweet, and nourishing, and can be eaten fresh, or dried, and kept, for months, or pressed, into a sweet syrup, like honey, or made into a kind of wine. Its leaves are woven into baskets, and mats, and roofs. Its fibres are made into ropes. Its trunk is used for beams, and roofs, and bridges. Its stones are burned as fuel, or fed to the animals. Nothing is wasted.

Under the palms, in their shade, the farmers grow other trees, and plants: pomegranates, and figs, and apples, and grapes, and vegetables, onions, and garlic, and leeks, and lettuce, and cucumbers, and herbs. The palm groves are like great gardens, cool, and green, and shady, in the burning heat of the summer.

The date palms need help. There are male trees, and female trees, and only the female trees bear fruit. So, in the spring, the farmers climb up the tall trunks of the female trees, with a rope, around themselves, and the trunk, and tie a little bunch of the flowers of the male tree, among the flowers of the female tree, so that the dates will grow. They have been doing this for thousands of years, and in Iraq, the farmers still do it, today, in the same way.

--

And everything depends on the canals.

All around Babylon, there is a great network of canals, large, and small, carrying the water of the Euphrates, out across the land, to the fields, and the palm groves. The great canals are as wide as rivers, and boats sail along them. The small ones are little ditches, running between the fields. The farmers open, and close, little gates, of earth, and reeds, to let the water flow into their fields, when they need it.

{img:irrigation-canal-shaduf-farmer-lifting-water-bucket-fields-barley}
To lift the water, from the canals, into the fields, they use the shaduf: a long pole, balanced on a post, with a bucket, on one end, and a heavy weight, of clay, on the other, so that the farmer can dip the bucket in the canal, and lift it, easily, and swing it round, and empty it, into the field.

--

The canals must be kept clean, and repaired, every year. The river carries a great deal of mud, and it settles in the canals, and fills them up, so that they must be dug out, again, and again. This is hard work, done by the farmers, and by workers, sent by the king, and the temples.

In the fields, the farmers grow barley, above all, which is the main food, and drink, of Babylon, for bread, and beer, and wheat, and emmer, and sesame, for oil, and flax, for linen. They plough with oxen, and a wooden plough, with a funnel, on the top, through which the seed is dropped, into the furrow, as the plough moves along, a clever invention, of the Mesopotamians.

{img:barley-harvest-mesopotamia-sickles-sheaves-donkeys-threshing-floor}
And at the harvest, in the spring, in April, and May, the barley is cut, with sickles, and carried, on donkeys, to the threshing floors, where it is threshed, by oxen, walking round, and round, on it, and winnowed, in the wind, and measured, and taken, in great sacks, to the storehouses, of the temples, and the families, and the king.
