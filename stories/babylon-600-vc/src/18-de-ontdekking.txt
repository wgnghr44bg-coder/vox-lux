In the year 1899, a German archaeologist, called Robert Koldewey, came to Babylon.

He was an architect, and a careful, patient man, with a big moustache, and a love of old buildings, and he had been sent, by the German Oriental Society, to dig in the ruins of Babylon. He stayed for nearly eighteen years, until 1917.

--

With hundreds of local workers, he dug, carefully, layer by layer, through the mounds of earth, and broken bricks, using a little railway, with small wagons, to carry the earth away. He uncovered the great walls, and the gates, and the palaces, and the temples, and the streets, and the houses, of the city of Nebuchadnezzar, and mapped them, and drew them, and photographed them.

And one day, in the north of the city, his workers began to find pieces of glazed brick, blue, and yellow, and white, thousands of them, broken, and scattered, in the earth. And then, deeper down, still standing, the foundations of a great gate, with the moulded animals, the bulls, and the dragons, still in place, in rows, on the walls, buried, for more than two thousand years. It was the Ishtar Gate.

{img:museum-workers-reassembling-glazed-brick-fragments-tables-berlin-1920s}
The thousands of broken pieces of glazed brick were packed, carefully, into crates, and sent, by ship, to Berlin. And there, over many years, in the 1920s, and 1930s, the museum workers sorted them, and cleaned them, and fitted them together, like a great jigsaw puzzle, with hundreds of thousands of pieces, and rebuilt the front of the Ishtar Gate, and part of the Processional Way, with its lions, in the Pergamon Museum, in Berlin.

--

It was opened to the public, in 1930, and since then, millions of people have walked through it, and stood, beneath the great blue gate, with its golden animals, and felt, for a moment, as if they were in Babylon.

Other archaeologists, and scholars, from many countries, and from Iraq itself, have dug in Babylon, and studied it, since then. And the clay tablets found there, and in the other cities of Mesopotamia, have been read, and translated, so that we know the names of the kings, and the scribes, and the merchants, and the astronomers, and the ordinary people of Babylon, and their business, and their prayers, and their lives.

{img:scholar-museum-reading-cuneiform-tablet-magnifying-glass-lamp}
And the Babylonian astronomical diaries, and the mathematical tablets, and the letters, and the stories, are still being read, and studied, today, in universities, and museums, all over the world, and every year, new tablets are read, and new things are learned.
