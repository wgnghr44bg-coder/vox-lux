Tonight we travel about two thousand two hundred years back in time. We go to the north coast of Africa, where the land reaches out into the blue Mediterranean Sea. The year is around 180 before Christ. And here, on a green hill above the sea, stands one of the richest cities in the world. It is called Carthage.

[long-pause]

It is early morning, and the sea is calm and silver. The sky over the mountains to the east is turning pink and gold. Fishing boats are coming in to the shore, with their sails folded. Seagulls cry and circle above the water. On the hill above the harbour, the white walls of houses and temples catch the first light of the sun. And somewhere in the city, a cock is crowing, and a door is opening, and a new day is beginning.

[pause]

The air smells of salt and fresh bread, of olive oil and wood smoke. In the narrow streets, the first people are coming out of their houses. A baker is pulling flat loaves from a clay oven. A woman is carrying a jar of water on her head. A boy is leading a donkey loaded with baskets of figs. And down at the harbour, the sailors are already at work.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:carthage-circular-military-harbour-cothon-ship-sheds-island-tower}
Carthage is a city of the sea. Its people are famous sailors and traders. Their ships sail all over the Mediterranean, and even beyond it, into the great ocean. They bring back silver and tin, gold and ivory, wine and oil. They sell purple cloth and fine pottery, glass beads and carved ivory. Carthage has two great harbours, one for trade and one for warships. The warship harbour is round, with an island in the middle. There is nothing like it anywhere else in the world.

[pause]

Most people today know Carthage only because of its wars with Rome, and its famous general Hannibal, who crossed the mountains with elephants. But tonight, we will not follow the wars. They were long and sad. Tonight we will visit Carthage in a time of peace, between the wars, when the city was rich and busy and full of life again.

[pause]

{img:carthaginian-family-house-courtyard-mother-children-morning-light-180bc}
We will meet a family who lives near the harbour. The father builds ships in the great round harbour. The mother weaves cloth and sells it in the market. Their son wants to be a sailor, and their daughter loves to listen to old stories. And their grandmother remembers the city of Tyre, far away in the east, where their people first came from.

[long-pause]

We will learn about the old Phoenicians, who invented the alphabet. We will hear the story of Queen Elissa, who founded the city with a clever trick and an oxhide. We will see how the people of Carthage made the most precious colour in the world, from little sea snails. We will hear about a sailor who travelled far down the coast of Africa. And we will wait with the family, for a ship that has sailed beyond the edge of the known world.

[pause]

{img:boy-sitting-harbour-wall-watching-ships-sails-carthage-morning-sun}
So let us begin beside the harbour of Carthage, on a summer morning long ago. A boy of twelve is sitting on the stone wall of the quay, swinging his legs. He is watching the ships come in, one after another, with their painted eyes and their square sails. He knows the name of every ship. And one day, he is sure, he will sail on one of them, all the way to the end of the world.

[pause]

Along the shore, the fishermen are unloading their catch. There are silver sardines and red mullet, tuna and octopus. Women come down with baskets to buy fish for the day. Children run between the boats, looking for crabs among the rocks. And an old fisherman sits on an upturned boat, mending his net with a wooden needle, humming an old song about the sea.

[pause]

On the hill of Byrsa, the priests of the temple of Eshmun are climbing the long stairs in the early light. They carry little bowls of incense and jars of oil. At the top, they open the great doors of the temple and light the lamps inside. Sweet smoke rises into the morning air. From up there, they can see the whole city waking below them, and the sea stretching away to the horizon.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-tyre-island-city-phoenician-ships-walls-harbour-lebanon-coast}
The people of Carthage are Phoenicians. Their ancestors came from a land far to the east, on the coast of what is now Lebanon.

[pause]

The Phoenicians lived in a row of cities along the eastern shore of the Mediterranean Sea. The most famous of these cities were Tyre and Sidon, and Byblos. Behind them were high mountains, covered with great forests of cedar trees. In front of them was the sea. There was not much land for farming. So the Phoenicians turned to the sea. They became the greatest sailors and traders of the ancient world.

[long-pause]

The Phoenicians built strong wooden ships from the cedars of their mountains. They sailed across the whole Mediterranean, from one end to the other. They traded with Egypt and Greece, with Cyprus and Sicily, with Spain and Africa. Everywhere they went, they built little trading posts and harbours. Some of these grew into towns and cities. Carthage was the greatest of them all.

[pause]

{img:phoenician-merchant-ship-cedar-wood-square-sail-horse-head-prow-sea}
The Phoenicians gave the world a wonderful gift: the alphabet. Before them, people wrote with hundreds of little pictures or signs, which were very hard to learn. The Phoenicians made a simple set of just twenty-two letters. Each letter stood for one sound. With these few letters, you could write any word. It was much easier to learn. The Greeks learned it from the Phoenicians, and then the Romans learned it from the Greeks. The letters you are reading in books today come from that old Phoenician alphabet.

[long-pause]

The first letter of the Phoenician alphabet was called aleph, which means ox. It looked a little like the head of an ox, with two horns. The second letter was called beth, which means house. From aleph and beth, the Greeks made alpha and beta. And from alpha and beta comes our word alphabet. So every time we say alphabet, we are speaking a little bit of Phoenician.

[pause]

{img:phoenician-letters-carved-stone-tablet-aleph-beth-close-sunlight}
The Phoenicians were also famous craftsmen. They made beautiful things of glass, ivory, silver and bronze. They made little glass beads in bright colours, with faces and eyes on them. They carved ivory into delicate panels, with pictures of animals and plants. They made bowls of silver, decorated with scenes of hunting and feasting. People all over the ancient world wanted to buy them.

[long-pause]

The people of Carthage still speak the old Phoenician language, though it has changed a little over the centuries. Today we call it Punic. They still worship the old gods of Tyre. And they still feel connected to their mother city, far away in the east. Every year, Carthage sends a ship to Tyre, with gifts for the temple of the god Melqart. It is a way of saying: we have not forgotten where we came from.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-telling-children-about-tyre-evening-lamp-courtyard-carthage}
Grandmother Batbaal, in our story, loves to tell the children about Tyre. She has never been there herself. But her own grandmother told her about it, when she was a little girl. Tyre is a city on an island, she says, with high walls rising straight out of the sea. Its harbours are full of ships, and its streets are full of purple cloth and shining glass. And one day, she says, perhaps one of you will sail there, and see it for us all.

[pause]

The Phoenicians were also famous for their ships' figureheads. The front of a Phoenician ship often had a carved horse's head, painted and decorated. Sailors believed it brought good luck and helped the ship to find its way. The Greeks called these ships hippoi, which means horses. And people who saw them coming over the sea knew at once: the Phoenicians are here.

[pause]

In the evenings, the old sailors of Carthage like to sit together by the harbour and talk about the past. They talk about the old voyages of their ancestors, to Egypt and Greece and the islands. They talk about the cedar forests of Lebanon and the great city of Tyre. They have never seen them. But they talk about them as if they were home.

[pause]

The Phoenicians also brought new plants and animals to the lands where they settled. They brought olive trees and grape vines, pomegranates and figs. They brought chickens and new kinds of sheep. They taught local peoples how to make glass and purple dye, how to build ships and how to write. Wherever they went, they left something new behind.

[long-pause]

{img:queen-elissa-dido-ships-arriving-african-coast-legend-dawn-followers}
The people of Carthage tell a wonderful story about how their city began. It is the story of a queen called Elissa.

[pause]

Long ago, the story says, Elissa was a princess of Tyre. Her brother became king of Tyre. He was greedy and unkind. He wanted Elissa's wealth for himself. So Elissa decided to leave Tyre. One night, she secretly loaded her treasure onto ships. With a group of loyal friends and followers, she sailed away, west across the sea.

[long-pause]

After a long voyage, with a stop on the island of Cyprus, Elissa and her people came to the coast of Africa. They found a beautiful place, with a sheltered bay and a green hill rising above it. It was a perfect place for a harbour and a city. But the land belonged to a local king, called Iarbas. Elissa went to him and asked if she could buy some land to settle on.

[pause]

{img:local-african-king-meeting-elissa-tent-oxhide-on-ground-bargain}
The king did not want to give her much land. So he said, with a smile, that she could have as much land as could be covered by the hide of one ox. An oxhide is not very big. It would cover only a small patch of ground, about the size of a room. The king thought that was a good joke. But Elissa was clever. She agreed at once.

[long-pause]

Then she took the oxhide and cut it into very thin strips, thinner than a thread. She cut and cut, until she had one enormous long strip of leather. Then she laid the strip on the ground, in a great circle, around the whole hill above the bay. The king was amazed. But he had made a promise, and he kept it. The whole hill belonged to Elissa.

[pause]

{img:people-laying-thin-leather-strip-circle-around-hill-legend-sunset}
That hill was called Byrsa, which some people say means oxhide in Greek. On it, Elissa and her people built their new city. They called it Qart-hadasht, which means New City in Phoenician. The Greeks and Romans later called it Carthage. And the hill of Byrsa became the heart of the city, with its temples and its strong walls.

[long-pause]

The Romans later told the story differently. They called the queen Dido. In a famous Roman poem, Dido falls in love with a Trojan hero called Aeneas, who comes to Carthage after a long journey. But Aeneas must leave her, to found a city in Italy. It is a sad and beautiful story, and it was very famous for many centuries. But the people of Carthage told their own story, about their wise and brave queen, Elissa.

[pause]

{img:byrsa-hill-carthage-temples-walls-top-view-sea-bay-afternoon}
The old writers say that Carthage was founded in the year 814 before Christ. That is more than six hundred years before our story. Archaeologists think it was a little later, perhaps fifty or a hundred years. Either way, by the time of our story, Carthage is very old and very rich. Its people are proud of their long history. And every child of Carthage knows the story of Elissa and the oxhide.

[pause]

The story also says that when the first people of Carthage dug the foundations of the city, they found the head of an ox. That was a sign, the priests said, that the city would be rich, but would have to work hard. So they dug in another place, and found the head of a horse. That was a sign that the city would be strong and brave. And the horse became one of the symbols of Carthage, found on many of its coins.

[pause]

Elissa, the girl in our story, is very proud of her name. She knows that she is named after the great queen. When she plays with her friends, she always wants to be Queen Elissa, cutting the oxhide into strips. She uses a piece of string instead. And she lays it out in a circle around the little garden on the roof, and says: this is my city now.

[pause]

The old stories say that Queen Elissa ruled Carthage wisely for many years. She built walls and temples and harbours. She made laws, and settled quarrels between her people. The people loved her. After she was gone, they honoured her as a goddess. And for hundreds of years, they told her story to their children, as Grandmother Batbaal tells it now.

[long-pause]

{img:carthage-city-180bc-streets-grid-white-houses-byrsa-hill-harbours-overview}
Let us walk through the city of Carthage, around the year 180 before Christ.

[pause]

Carthage is a big city, perhaps with two or three hundred thousand people. It lies on a wide point of land, with the sea on several sides. Great walls protect it on the land side, tall and thick, with towers along them. Inside the walls, there are houses and temples, markets and workshops, gardens and fields. And in the middle rises the hill of Byrsa, with its temples on top.

[long-pause]

From the harbour, a wide street leads up toward the hill. It is busy all day long, with people and donkeys and carts. On both sides, there are shops and workshops. Near the bottom of the hill, the streets are laid out in a neat grid, straight and at right angles. The houses are tall, sometimes four or five storeys high. They are made of stone and mud brick, covered with white plaster. In the sunshine, the whole city gleams white.

[pause]

{img:carthage-street-tall-white-houses-shops-donkeys-people-busy-morning}
On the slopes of the hill of Byrsa, there are fine houses for rich families. They have courtyards with columns and gardens, mosaic floors and painted walls. Some of them have water tanks under the floor, to collect the rain. Water is precious in Carthage, because it does not rain much in summer. Almost every house has its own cistern, where rainwater is stored.

[long-pause]

On top of the hill, there is the great temple of Eshmun, the god of healing. It is the biggest temple in the city. A long flight of sixty steps leads up to it. From the top, you can see the whole city spread out below, and the harbours, and the bay, and the mountains across the water. It is the most beautiful view in Carthage.

[pause]

{img:temple-eshmun-byrsa-hill-long-stairs-columns-sea-view-below}
Near the sea, there are the two great harbours. The first is the trade harbour, a long rectangle, full of merchant ships from all over the Mediterranean. The second, behind it, is the round harbour for warships. You can only reach it by sailing through the trade harbour. It is surrounded by high walls, so nobody can see inside. It is one of the wonders of Carthage.

[long-pause]

Outside the old centre, there is a great area of gardens and fields, called Megara. It is inside the city walls, but green and peaceful, almost like the countryside. Rich families have country houses there, with orchards and vineyards, and channels of water running between them. There are fig trees and pomegranate trees, olive trees and palm trees. People go there in the hot summer, to rest in the shade.

[pause]

{img:megara-gardens-carthage-orchards-water-channels-villas-palm-trees}
The people of Carthage are proud of their city. Its government is run by its citizens. Each year, they choose two leaders, called suffetes, which means judges. There is also a council of elders, and an assembly of the people. A few years before our story, the famous general Hannibal was chosen as one of the suffetes. He made the government fairer, and helped to make the city rich again after the war. People in Carthage still talk about him with great respect.

[pause]

The streets of Carthage are full of the sounds of work. You can hear the hammers of the metalworkers and the clatter of the potters' wheels. You can hear the creak of carts and the braying of donkeys. You can hear people calling out from their windows, and children laughing in the streets. And over everything, you can hear the sound of the sea, never very far away.

[pause]

At night, the city is quieter. The shops close their wooden shutters. Little lamps glow in the windows. Watchmen walk along the city walls, with torches. In the harbours, the ships rock gently on the dark water. And on the hill of Byrsa, a fire burns in front of the temple of Eshmun, all through the night.

[pause]

Water for the fountains of the city comes partly from wells, and partly from the rain stored in the cisterns. In the time of the Romans, much later, a great aqueduct will be built, bringing fresh water from the mountains, more than a hundred kilometres away. But in the time of our story, every drop of rain is precious. People are very careful not to waste water.

[long-pause]

{img:carthaginian-shipwright-father-strong-hands-adze-wood-harbour-workshop}
Let us meet a family who lives in a narrow street near the harbours of Carthage.

[pause]

The father is called Himilco. He is a shipwright, a builder of ships. He works in the great round harbour, where the warships are built and repaired. He is about forty years old, with a short black beard and strong arms. He knows every kind of wood and every part of a ship. He is a calm and quiet man, careful in everything he does. He is proud of his work, and of the ships he helps to build.

[pause]

The mother is called Arishat. She is a weaver. She weaves fine linen and wool on a big loom in the courtyard of their house. She sells her cloth in the market, near the trade harbour. She is lively and cheerful, with quick hands and a quick laugh. She knows everybody in the street, and everybody knows her.

[long-pause]

They have two children. The son is called Hanno, and he is twelve. He is named after a famous sailor of Carthage, from long ago. He loves the sea and the ships more than anything. He spends every free moment at the harbour, watching the ships and talking to the sailors. He wants to be a sailor himself, like his uncle. His father wants him to be a shipwright. They talk about it often, without agreeing.

[pause]

{img:boy-twelve-talking-with-sailors-quay-ropes-ships-carthage-afternoon}
The daughter is called Elissa, after the founding queen. She is nine years old, with long dark hair and big serious eyes. She helps her mother with the spinning and the weaving. She loves stories more than anything, and she listens to every story she can. She asks her grandmother for a new story every evening. And she remembers every one.

[long-pause]

The grandmother is called Batbaal. She is Arishat's mother, about seventy years old. She was born in Carthage, like her parents and grandparents. She remembers the long, hard war with Rome, which ended about twenty years ago. She does not like to talk about it much. She prefers to tell the old stories of Tyre and of Queen Elissa, and the tales of the gods. She sits in the courtyard all day, spinning wool, and watching over the house.

[pause]

{img:old-carthaginian-grandmother-spinning-wool-courtyard-shade-smiling}
There is one more member of the family, who is not at home. He is Arishat's younger brother, Uncle Bomilcar. He is a sailor on a big merchant ship. His ship sails west, past Spain, through the narrow strait that the Greeks call the Pillars of Heracles, and out into the great ocean. It sails to the city of Gades, and sometimes even further north, to buy tin. It is a long and adventurous voyage. Hanno thinks his uncle is the bravest man in the world.

[long-pause]

The family is not rich, but they live comfortably. Himilco earns a good wage in the harbour. Arishat earns a little more from her weaving. They have a small house, a little garden on the roof, and enough to eat. They have a cat, a sleek grey cat called Shadow, who hunts the mice in the storeroom. And they have a little dog, who barks at everyone who comes to the door.

[pause]

{img:carthaginian-family-supper-courtyard-lamps-bread-olives-fish-evening}
In the evenings, the family eats together in the courtyard. There is bread and olives, cheese and fish, figs and grapes. They talk about the day. Himilco tells them about the new ship he is building. Arishat tells them the news from the market. Hanno tells them about the ships in the harbour. Elissa asks for a story. And Grandmother Batbaal smiles, and begins.

[pause]

On their days off, the family likes to walk along the beach outside the city walls. They look for shells and pretty stones. The children paddle in the shallow water and chase the little waves. Himilco and Arishat walk slowly behind, talking. Grandmother Batbaal sits on a rock in the sun and watches them. It is the most peaceful time of the week.

[pause]

Grandmother Batbaal has a little box of treasures that she keeps under her bed. Inside, there are a few glass beads, a silver ring that belonged to her mother, and a little carved ivory comb. There is also a small stone with the sign of Tanit scratched on it, which her husband gave her when they were young. Sometimes she takes them out and shows them to Elissa, and tells her the story of each one.

[long-pause]

{img:punic-house-courtyard-red-floor-white-stones-well-rooms-around-sunlight}
The family lives in a house on the slope of the hill, a short walk from the harbours. Like most houses in Carthage, it is built around an open courtyard.

[pause]

From the street, you step through a narrow corridor into the courtyard. The courtyard is the heart of the house. It is open to the sky, so the light and air come in. The floor is made of a special kind of red cement, with little white stones scattered in it, like stars in a red sky. It is hard and smooth and easy to clean. In the middle, there is the opening of the cistern, where the rainwater is kept.

[long-pause]

Around the courtyard, there are the rooms of the house. There is a kitchen, with a clay oven and a hearth. There is a storeroom, with big clay jars of olive oil, wine, grain and dried fish. There is a room where the family eats and sits in winter. There are bedrooms, small and simple, with beds of wood and woven cord. And there is the loom room, where Arishat weaves.

[pause]

{img:small-stone-bathtub-punic-house-bathroom-water-jar-sunlight}
And there is something very unusual for the time: a little bathroom. It has a small sitting bathtub, made of stone and covered with the red cement. You sit in it with your knees up, and pour water over yourself from a jar. Many houses in Carthage have bathrooms like this, even quite ordinary houses. The people of Carthage like to be clean. In this, they are ahead of many other peoples of their time.

[long-pause]

A wooden staircase leads up to the upper floor and the flat roof. On the roof, Arishat has a little garden in pots and old jars. She grows herbs, like mint and coriander. She grows a few flowers. And she dries figs and grapes in the sun, on mats. From the roof, you can see the harbours and the sea. On hot summer nights, the family sometimes sleeps up there, where the breeze is cool.

[pause]

{img:flat-roof-carthage-herbs-pots-drying-figs-mats-harbour-view-evening}
In one corner of the courtyard, there is a little niche in the wall, with a small figure of a goddess. It is Tanit, the great goddess of Carthage, the protector of the city. Her sign is very simple: a triangle, with a line across the top, and a circle above it. It looks a little like a person with raised arms. The same sign is painted on the floor near the door, made of little white stones. It is meant to bring good luck to the house.

[long-pause]

Above the door, there is also a little carved hand, with the palm open. It is a sign of protection, to keep bad luck away. Many houses in Carthage have them. And near the door, hanging on a nail, there are a few little glass beads, shaped like faces, with big eyes. These are for luck too. Elissa likes to look at them. She thinks the little faces are smiling at her, every time she comes home.

[pause]

{img:tanit-symbol-floor-mosaic-white-stones-red-cement-doorway-close}
In the evening, when the lamps are lit, the courtyard is very peaceful. The little clay lamps burn with a soft yellow flame, fed with olive oil. Their light flickers on the white walls and the red floor. The cat sleeps on the edge of the cistern. Grandmother Batbaal sits in her corner, spinning. And the sounds of the city come softly over the walls: voices, footsteps, a donkey braying, and far away, the sea.

[pause]

Water from the cistern is drawn up with a bucket on a rope. It is cool and clean, even on the hottest days. Elissa's job is to fill the big water jar in the kitchen every morning. She lowers the bucket into the dark cistern, and listens to the splash far below. Then she pulls it up, hand over hand, and pours the water into the jar. She has to do it many times before the jar is full.

[pause]

In winter, the house can be cold and damp, because the walls are made of stone and the rain comes in through the open courtyard. The family gathers around a little brazier of hot charcoal in the evenings. They wrap themselves in woollen blankets. Grandmother Batbaal sits closest to the warmth. And the cat curls up on her lap, purring, as if the brazier had been lit just for her.

[long-pause]

{img:carthage-dawn-mother-baking-bread-clay-oven-courtyard-family-waking}
Let us spend an ordinary day with the family, in the early summer.

[pause]

The day begins before sunrise. Arishat gets up first and lights the clay oven in the corner of the courtyard. She mixes flour and water, and shapes flat round loaves. She slaps them onto the hot inside wall of the oven, where they bake quickly. Soon the smell of fresh bread fills the house. Grandmother Batbaal sits beside her, grinding grain on a flat stone, as she has done every morning of her life.

[long-pause]

Breakfast is warm bread with olive oil and a little salt, some olives and a handful of dates. Himilco eats quickly and sets off for the round harbour. He must be there by sunrise, when the work begins. Hanno goes with him today. His father has promised to show him the new warship they are building. Hanno is so excited that he can hardly finish his bread.

[pause]

{img:father-son-walking-to-harbour-dawn-street-tools-carthage-morning}
Arishat and Elissa stay at home for the morning. Arishat sits at her loom and weaves. The loom is tall and upright, with the threads hanging down, held tight by little clay weights. She passes the thread back and forth, back and forth, with a little wooden shuttle. Slowly, the cloth grows, line by line. Elissa sits beside her and spins wool, twisting it into thread with a little spindle.

[long-pause]

In the middle of the morning, they go to the market to sell some cloth and buy food. The market is near the trade harbour, in a big square full of stalls. Arishat has her own little stall, under a cloth shade. She spreads out her linen and wool, in white and brown and soft blue. Customers come and feel the cloth between their fingers. They bargain politely. Arishat smiles and bargains back.

[pause]

{img:market-stall-woman-selling-woven-cloth-customers-feeling-fabric-shade}
While her mother sells, Elissa looks around the market. There is so much to see. There are stalls with fish and fruit, vegetables and spices. There are potters with their red and black pots, and glassmakers with their bright beads. There are traders from far away, with strange clothes and strange languages. There is a man with a monkey on his shoulder, and a woman selling little birds in cages.

[long-pause]

At midday, it is hot. Arishat closes her stall, and they go home through the shady streets. They eat a simple lunch: bread, cheese, cucumbers and cold lentils. Then everyone rests in the coolest part of the house, during the hottest hours. Grandmother Batbaal sleeps in her corner. The cat sleeps on the cool stone floor. Even the dog is too hot to bark.

[pause]

{img:family-resting-midday-heat-shady-room-cat-sleeping-cool-floor}
In the late afternoon, when it is cooler, Himilco and Hanno come home, dusty and tired. Hanno cannot stop talking about the warship. It is huge, he says, with five rows of oars, and a bronze ram at the front. He tells everyone about every plank and every nail. His father smiles quietly. In the evening, the family eats supper together in the courtyard. And when the stars come out, Grandmother Batbaal tells Elissa her story, as she does every night.

[pause]

Before bed, Arishat checks that everything in the house is in order. She closes the door to the street and bars it with a wooden beam. She covers the jars in the storeroom, so the mice cannot get in. She puts out the fire in the oven. Then she looks in on the children, already asleep in their beds. She smiles, and blows out the last lamp.

[pause]

In the evening, the street outside the house is lively and friendly. Neighbours sit on their doorsteps and talk. Children play games in the last light. A man sells roasted chickpeas from a little tray. A woman calls her children in for supper. The smell of cooking drifts from every courtyard. And the swallows swoop low over the rooftops, catching insects in the evening air.

[pause]

Sometimes, after supper, the neighbours come into the courtyard to visit. They bring a jug of wine or a plate of sweet cakes. The grown-ups sit and talk about the news of the city: the price of grain, the ships in the harbour, the decisions of the council. The children play in the corner with the cat. And the evening passes slowly and happily, in the soft light of the lamps.

[long-pause]

{img:carthage-cothon-circular-harbour-island-admiral-tower-ship-sheds-columns}
The round harbour of Carthage is one of the most amazing places in the ancient world. It is called the cothon. And it is where Himilco works every day.

[pause]

To reach it, you must first sail into the trade harbour, a long rectangular basin, full of merchant ships. At the far end, there is a narrow opening, closed by gates. Through the gates, you pass into the round harbour. It is a great circle of water, about three hundred metres across. In the middle of it, there is a round island. And all around the edge of the harbour, and around the island too, there are ship sheds.

[long-pause]

The ship sheds are long, sloping buildings, side by side, like the slices of a cake. Each one is just big enough for one warship. The ships are pulled up out of the water, on wooden slipways, and kept inside the sheds. There, they are safe from the sun and the rain, which can rot their wood. When they are needed, they slide back down into the water. There are sheds for about a hundred and seventy ships.

[pause]

{img:warship-sheds-sloping-slipways-ships-pulled-up-columns-front-shade}
At the front of each shed, there are two tall columns. So when you look at the harbour, you see a great ring of columns, all the way round, like a temple. It is very beautiful. Above the sheds, there are storerooms for oars and sails, ropes and other gear. Everything is neat and in its place.

[long-pause]

On the round island in the middle, there is a tall tower. It is the headquarters of the admiral, the commander of the fleet. From the top of the tower, the admiral can see the whole harbour, and out over the walls to the open sea. He can give signals with trumpets and flags. A little bridge connects the island to the edge of the harbour.

[pause]

{img:admiral-tower-island-harbour-trumpeter-top-view-sea-beyond-walls}
The round harbour is surrounded by high walls. Nobody outside can see what is happening inside. The people of Carthage keep their warships secret. Only the shipwrights and sailors who work there are allowed inside. Himilco is very proud that he works there. When he comes home, he does not talk too much about the warships. But he tells his family about the harbour, and how beautiful it is in the morning light.

[long-pause]

In the years after the war with Rome, Carthage is not allowed to have a big navy. The peace treaty says so. So in the time of our story, the round harbour is quiet. There are only a few warships inside. Most of the work is repairing and looking after them, and building merchant ships for trade. But the harbour is still a wonder, and the people of Carthage are still proud of it.

[pause]

{img:shipwrights-repairing-hull-planks-shed-quiet-morning-light-harbour}
Today, Hanno walks through the round harbour with his father. He looks up at the great ring of columns, glowing in the morning sun. He looks at the ships in their sheds, with their long oars and their painted eyes. He looks at the tower on the island, with a flag fluttering on top. He thinks it is the most wonderful place in the world. And he decides, at least for today, that perhaps being a shipwright is not so bad after all.

[pause]

Early in the morning, before the work begins, the round harbour is very quiet and beautiful. The water is still and smooth, like a mirror. The columns are reflected in it, upside down. Seabirds float on the water and dive for little fish. The first workers arrive, talking softly. And then the sound of hammers begins, and the day's work starts.

[pause]

The sailors who work on the warships live in barracks near the harbour. They train every day, rowing and practising with the sails. Even in peacetime, they must be ready. In the evenings, they sit outside their barracks, mending their clothes and playing games with little stones. Hanno likes to listen to them talk about the sea. They tease him and call him the little captain.

[long-pause]

{img:shipwrights-building-hull-ribs-planks-carthage-yard-adzes-sawdust-sun}
The shipwrights of Carthage are famous for their skill. They build ships faster than anyone else in the ancient world. How do they do it?

[pause]

First, they need wood. The best wood for ships is pine and cedar, fir and oak. Carthage does not have many big forests nearby. So the wood comes from the mountains of North Africa, and from Spain and Sardinia. It arrives in big logs, on ships and carts. In the harbour, workers saw the logs into long planks, with big saws worked by two men.

[long-pause]

The ships of Carthage are built shell first. That means the shipwrights first build the outer shell of planks, and then add the ribs inside, to make it strong. The planks are joined edge to edge, very tightly. They are held together by little wooden tongues, fitted into slots cut in the edges of the planks. Then they are fixed with wooden pegs. It is very careful work. When it is done, the hull is smooth and strong.

[pause]

{img:close-planks-mortise-tenon-joints-wooden-pegs-hull-craftsmanship}
The shipwrights of Carthage have a clever trick. They build the ships from parts that are all made in the same way, so they fit together perfectly. Each part is marked with a little Phoenician letter, painted on the wood. The letters show where each part goes. So the workers can put the ship together quickly, like a puzzle, following the letters.

[long-pause]

Many hundreds of years later, divers found a Carthaginian warship at the bottom of the sea, near Sicily. On its timbers, they could still see the little painted letters, showing where each part should go. It was like finding the instructions for a ship, written more than two thousand years ago. It shows how clever and organised the shipwrights of Carthage were.

[pause]

{img:shipwreck-timbers-seabed-painted-phoenician-letters-divers-light-later}
Himilco works with a team of shipwrights. Each one has his own special job. Some cut the planks. Some fit the joints. Some carve the curved pieces at the front and back of the ship. Some fit the long oars and the steering paddles. Himilco is a master at fitting the planks together. He works with an adze, a tool like a small axe with the blade turned sideways. He shapes each plank with small, careful strokes.

[long-pause]

When the hull is finished, it is covered with hot black pitch, made from pine resin, to make it waterproof. Then the ship is painted. Carthaginian ships often have a big eye painted on each side of the front. The eyes help the ship to see its way across the sea, people say. Then the mast and the sail are fitted, and the oars. And at last, the ship slides down into the water, while everyone cheers.

[pause]

{img:ship-launch-sliding-into-harbour-painted-eye-bow-crowd-cheering-sunlight}
Hanno helps his father in the shipyard sometimes, on days when he is allowed. He carries planks and sweeps up the wood shavings. He holds the pegs while his father hammers them in. He learns the names of all the parts of the ship. And when a ship is launched, he stands at the edge of the water with the other workers, and cheers as loudly as anyone. He is beginning to understand why his father loves this work.

[pause]

At midday, the shipwrights stop work and eat together, in the shade of a ship shed. They eat bread and cheese, olives and onions, and drink water mixed with a little wine. They talk about their families and the news of the city. Some of them doze for a while in the shade. Then the foreman calls, and they go back to work.

[pause]

The shipwrights of Carthage also build smaller boats: fishing boats and little ferries, and boats for carrying goods along the coast. These boats are simpler and quicker to make. Himilco sometimes builds one of these in the evenings, for a fisherman or a farmer. He enjoys it. A little boat, he says, is like a little song. A big ship is like a long poem.

[long-pause]

{img:murex-sea-snail-shells-piles-beach-dye-workshop-vats-purple-cloth}
The most precious thing that the Phoenicians made was a colour. It was a deep, rich purple, the colour of kings and emperors. And it came from little sea snails.

[pause]

The snails are called murex. They live in the sea along the coasts of the Mediterranean. They have spiky shells, about as long as a finger. Inside each snail, there is a tiny gland, which makes a little drop of clear liquid. When this liquid is exposed to the air and the sun, it changes colour. First it turns yellow, then green, then blue, and at last a deep, glowing purple.

[long-pause]

To make the dye, fishermen catch thousands and thousands of murex snails, in baskets lowered into the sea. The dyers crack open the shells and take out the little glands. They put them in big vats with salt water. They heat the vats slowly for many days. The liquid becomes a dark purple dye. Then they dip wool or silk into the vats, again and again, until it is the right colour.

[pause]

{img:dyers-workshop-vats-steaming-purple-wool-hanging-drying-workers}
It takes about ten thousand snails to make just a little bit of dye, enough for the edge of a cloak. So purple cloth is very, very expensive. Only the richest people can buy it: kings and queens, nobles and priests. In Rome, later, only the emperor will be allowed to wear a robe that is purple all over. People call it royal purple, or Tyrian purple, after the city of Tyre, where it was first made.

[long-pause]

The name Phoenician may even come from this colour. The Greeks called the Phoenicians Phoinikes, which may mean the purple people. Perhaps because they sold purple cloth. Or perhaps because their hands were stained purple from making it. The dyers' hands are always stained, and the colour never quite washes off.

[pause]

{img:dyers-stained-purple-hands-holding-cloth-sunlight-shore-carthage}
Making purple dye has one big problem. It smells terrible. The rotting snails and the heated vats make a very strong, very unpleasant smell. So the dye workshops are always outside the city, near the shore, where the wind can blow the smell away. Even so, people say you can smell a dye workshop from far away. The dyers do not mind. They say it is the smell of money.

[long-pause]

Near the dye workshops, there are great heaps of broken murex shells, left over from the work. Some of these heaps are as big as small hills. Archaeologists have found them along the coasts where the Phoenicians lived. Children of Carthage like to climb on them and look for the prettiest shells.

[pause]

{img:children-climbing-shell-mound-collecting-shells-shore-sunset}
Arishat cannot afford to weave with purple wool. It is far too expensive. But sometimes, a rich customer asks her to weave a cloth with a thin purple stripe along the edge. Then the customer brings the purple thread himself. Arishat weaves it in very carefully, so that not a single thread is wasted. Elissa watches with wide eyes. She has never seen such a beautiful colour. It seems to glow, deep and rich, like the evening sky.

[pause]

Purple dye is not the only colour the people of Carthage make. They also make red dye from the roots of a plant called madder. They make yellow from saffron, and blue from a plant called woad. The dyers of Carthage are famous for their bright, strong colours. On market days, the cloth stalls are like rainbows, full of red and yellow, blue and green.

[pause]

The people of Carthage also make beautiful glass. Glassmakers heat sand and plant ashes in very hot furnaces until they melt. They wind the hot glass around a little core of clay and sand, to make little bottles for perfume and oil. They decorate them with zigzag lines of yellow and white and blue. These little glass bottles are sold all over the Mediterranean, and archaeologists have found them everywhere.

[long-pause]

{img:carthage-market-square-stalls-pottery-fruit-spices-crowd-awnings-morning}
The great market of Carthage is near the trade harbour. It is one of the busiest places in the city, and one of the most colourful.

[pause]

Here you can buy almost anything. There are farmers from the countryside, selling fruit and vegetables, cheese and eggs. There are fishermen with baskets of fresh fish, still wet from the sea. There are bakers and butchers, sellers of oil and wine. There are potters with their jugs and bowls and lamps, painted in red and black. There are craftsmen selling tools, glass beads, jewellery and little carved figures.

[long-pause]

The fruit of Carthage is famous. There are figs and pomegranates, grapes and dates, almonds and olives. The figs of Carthage are especially sweet and large. There is an old story, from a little later, about a Roman senator called Cato. He came back from a visit to Carthage, and held up a beautiful fresh fig in front of the Roman senate. He said that it had been picked in Carthage just three days before. He wanted to show the Romans how rich and how close Carthage was.

[pause]

{img:fruit-stall-figs-pomegranates-grapes-dates-baskets-woman-girl-buying}
The pomegranate is a special fruit in Carthage. It is round and red, with a little crown on top, and inside it is full of shining red seeds. The Romans even called it the Punic apple, the apple of Carthage. People eat the seeds, and make juice from them. The pomegranate is also a sign of good luck and plenty. You can see it carved on stones and painted on pots all over the city.

[long-pause]

There are also many traders from far away in the market. There are Greeks with their wine and painted vases. There are traders from Egypt, with linen and papyrus. There are Numidians from the lands to the west, with horses and cattle and grain. There are men from inner Africa, with ivory and ostrich eggs and gold dust. Everyone bargains in a mix of languages: Punic and Greek, Libyan and Latin.

[pause]

{img:traders-many-lands-market-carthage-ivory-ostrich-eggs-amphorae-bargaining}
Ostrich eggs are very popular in Carthage. They are huge and white, much bigger than a hen's egg. The people of Carthage cut them in half and decorate them with paintings of faces, flowers and patterns. They use them as bowls and cups, and as lucky charms. Some of them are painted with red faces, with big eyes, to keep away bad luck.

[long-pause]

Elissa loves to walk through the market with her mother. She looks at the painted ostrich eggs and the glass beads. She smells the spices and the fresh bread. She listens to the traders calling out in many languages. Sometimes her mother buys her a little treat: a handful of dates, or a honey cake, or a slice of melon. Today, Arishat buys her a little glass bead, blue and yellow, with a tiny face on it.

[pause]

{img:girl-holding-small-glass-face-bead-blue-yellow-market-delighted}
Elissa holds the bead up to the light and looks at the tiny face. It has big round eyes and a little beard, and it seems to be smiling. She threads it onto a string and wears it around her neck. Grandmother Batbaal says it will keep her safe from bad dreams. Elissa wears it every day after that, and at night, she holds it in her hand as she falls asleep.

[pause]

There are also many food stalls in the market, where people can buy something to eat. There are little fried fish and fresh flat bread. There are bowls of chickpeas cooked with cumin. There are sweet cakes made with honey and sesame seeds, and dates stuffed with almonds. The smells of cooking drift over the whole market, and make everyone hungry.

[pause]

In one corner of the market, there are scribes who sit at little tables. People who cannot read or write come to them for help. The scribes write letters to relatives in faraway towns. They write contracts for traders, and lists of goods. They read out letters that people have received. They write in neat Punic letters, on papyrus or on little wax tablets. And they charge a small coin for each letter.

[pause]

In the late afternoon, the market slowly empties. The traders pack up their goods. The farmers load their donkeys and set off home to the countryside. The fishermen wash their baskets in the sea. Cats come out to look for scraps. And the square becomes quiet, with only a few people walking across it in the golden evening light.

[long-pause]

{img:carthaginian-merchant-ships-trade-harbour-loading-amphorae-sails-busy}
Carthage became rich through trade. Its merchant ships sailed all over the Mediterranean, and beyond.

[pause]

To the west, the ships sailed to Spain, where there were rich mines of silver. They brought back silver and lead, olive oil and salted fish. To the north, they sailed to Sardinia and Sicily, for grain and wine. To the east, they sailed to Greece and Egypt and Tyre, for fine goods of every kind. And to the south, along the coast of Africa, they traded for gold and ivory.

[long-pause]

The merchant ships of Carthage are big and round, with a wide hull and one big square sail. They are slower than warships, but they can carry a lot of cargo. Most of the cargo is carried in tall clay jars, called amphorae, with two handles and a pointed bottom. The jars are packed tightly in the hold, in rows, leaning against each other. They are full of wine and oil, fish sauce and grain.

[pause]

{img:ship-hold-amphorae-packed-rows-straw-sailors-loading-lantern}
There is an old story about how the Carthaginians traded with peoples in Africa, beyond the desert, who did not speak their language. The Greek writer Herodotus told it. The Carthaginians sailed to a certain beach. They unloaded their goods and laid them out in a row on the sand. Then they went back to their ships and lit a fire, so that the smoke could be seen.

[long-pause]

When the local people saw the smoke, they came down to the beach. They looked at the goods. Then they put some gold beside the goods, and went away again. The Carthaginians came back and looked at the gold. If they thought it was enough, they took it and sailed away. If not, they went back to their ships and waited. The local people then came and added more gold. Nobody cheated, Herodotus says. The Carthaginians never took the gold until it was enough. And the local people never took the goods until the gold was accepted. This is called silent trade.

[pause]

{img:silent-trade-beach-goods-laid-out-gold-dust-smoke-ships-offshore}
The most adventurous voyages go through the Pillars of Heracles, the narrow strait between Spain and Africa, out into the great ocean. Beyond the Pillars, there is the old Phoenician city of Gades, which is now Cádiz in Spain. And beyond Gades, the ships sail north, along the coasts, to faraway islands in the cold, misty sea. There, they buy tin, which is needed to make bronze. Those islands are probably Britain.

[long-pause]

The Carthaginians keep their sea routes secret. They do not want other peoples, like the Greeks and the Romans, to find the way to the tin and the silver. There is a story about a Carthaginian captain who saw that a Roman ship was following him, to find the way. So he steered his own ship onto a sandbank on purpose, and the Roman ship followed him and was wrecked too. The captain escaped, and when he got home, the city paid him for the ship he had lost.

[pause]

{img:sailor-uncle-telling-stories-children-courtyard-lamp-sea-voyage}
Uncle Bomilcar sails on these long voyages. He has been to Gades many times, and once even to the misty tin islands in the north. When he comes home, he tells Hanno and Elissa about the great ocean, with its huge waves and its tides. In the Mediterranean, there are almost no tides. But in the ocean, he says, the sea goes out and comes back twice a day, as far as you can see. Hanno and Elissa can hardly believe it.

[pause]

The traders of Carthage keep careful records of everything they buy and sell. They write on wax tablets and on papyrus, in Punic letters. They count with little stones and with their fingers. They know the prices of everything, in every port. And they make friends with traders all over the world, because trust is the most important thing in trade.

[pause]

Trade also brings new ideas and new stories to Carthage. Sailors come back from faraway places with tales of strange animals and strange customs. Traders bring books and new kinds of tools. Craftsmen learn new techniques from foreign workers. And the people of Carthage, in turn, share their own skills and stories with the peoples they meet. So the whole Mediterranean is connected, through the ships that sail across it.

[long-pause]

{img:hanno-navigator-fleet-sixty-ships-sailing-west-african-coast-ancient}
Long before our story, about three hundred years earlier, there was a famous sailor from Carthage. His name was Hanno. And the boy in our story is named after him.

[pause]

Hanno was sent by the city of Carthage on a great voyage. He sailed with sixty ships, full of men and women, food and supplies. They sailed west, through the Pillars of Heracles, and then south, along the coast of Africa. Their plan was to build new towns and trading posts along the coast. Nobody from the Mediterranean had ever sailed so far south.

[long-pause]

When Hanno came home, he wrote an account of his voyage. It was carved on a tablet and placed in a temple in Carthage. Later, it was translated into Greek, and the Greek version still exists today. It is one of the oldest stories of exploration in the world. Grandmother Batbaal tells it to the children, the way she heard it from her grandmother.

[pause]

{img:african-coast-river-mouth-forest-hills-ships-anchored-explorers-landing}
Hanno sailed past mountains and deserts and rivers. He founded several little towns along the coast, and left people to live in them. He met friendly peoples, who helped him and gave him guides. He saw a river full of crocodiles and hippopotamuses. He saw great forests, and mountains covered with trees. He saw elephants grazing near the shore.

[long-pause]

Further south, the land became stranger. At night, Hanno and his sailors saw fires burning along the coast, and heard the sound of drums and pipes, far away in the dark. They saw a great mountain, which seemed to be on fire, with flames reaching up to the sky. They called it the Chariot of the Gods. Perhaps it was a volcano. Or perhaps it was the people of the land, burning the dry grass at the end of the season.

[pause]

{img:night-coast-fires-burning-hills-ships-sailors-watching-wonder}
On an island near the end of the voyage, Hanno's men met some strange, hairy creatures, who climbed the rocks and threw stones. The local guides called them gorillai. Many years later, when Europeans saw great apes in the forests of Africa, they remembered Hanno's story. And they gave them the name gorilla. So the word gorilla comes from the voyage of Hanno of Carthage.

[long-pause]

At last, Hanno's supplies ran out, and he turned back for home. Nobody knows exactly how far he sailed. Some people think he reached the land that is now Sierra Leone, or even Cameroon, far down the coast of Africa. It was an amazing journey, more than two thousand years ago.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-telling-hanno-story-children-wide-eyes-lamp-courtyard-night}
Hanno, the boy, loves this story more than any other. He makes his grandmother tell it again and again. He wants to know everything: how big the ships were, what the crocodiles looked like, how loud the drums were. He draws maps in the dust of the courtyard, with the coast of Africa and the little ships. One day, he says, I will sail even further than Hanno the Navigator. Grandmother Batbaal laughs. First, she says, you must learn to swim properly. Hanno blushes. He is still not a very good swimmer.

[pause]

There was another famous Carthaginian explorer, called Himilco, at about the same time as Hanno. He sailed north, along the coasts of Spain and France, toward the tin islands. He wrote about the great ocean, with its thick seaweed, its fogs and its strange sea creatures. The father in our story is named after him. He likes to tease Hanno that his name is just as famous.

[pause]

Some old writers say that Hanno brought back the skins of the hairy creatures he met, and hung them in the temple of Tanit in Carthage. People came to look at them for many years. Nobody knows if this is true. But it is a wonderful story. And Hanno, the boy, likes to imagine walking into the temple and seeing them, hanging high on the wall, from the great voyage long ago.

[long-pause]

{img:carthaginian-countryside-farm-olive-groves-vineyards-wheat-fields-hills}
Outside the walls of Carthage, there is a rich and beautiful countryside. The people of Carthage are not only sailors and traders. They are also wonderful farmers.

[pause]

The land around Carthage is green and fertile. There are wide fields of wheat and barley. There are vineyards on the hillsides, and orchards of figs and pomegranates, almonds and pears. And above all, there are olive trees, thousands and thousands of them, in long neat rows across the hills. The olive oil of Carthage is famous all over the Mediterranean.

[long-pause]

Many rich families of Carthage own farms in the countryside. They have country houses there, with gardens and courtyards. Workers live on the farms and look after the fields and the animals. There are cattle and sheep, goats and pigs, horses and donkeys. There are beehives for honey. There are pigeons and chickens, and fish ponds.

[pause]

{img:olive-harvest-workers-ladders-nets-baskets-olive-trees-autumn-sun}
A famous Carthaginian called Mago wrote a great book about farming. It was in twenty-eight volumes, and it covered everything. How to plant vines and olive trees. How to look after cattle and bees. How to choose a good farm. How to treat the workers well. Mago's book was so good that, much later, the Romans translated it into Latin. They thought it was the best book about farming in the world. Sadly, the book itself is lost. We only know parts of it, from quotations by other writers.

[long-pause]

One of Mago's pieces of advice is still remembered. He said that a man who buys a farm should sell his house in the town. That way, he will live on his farm, and look after it properly. He believed that the best farmers are those who love their land and know it well.

[pause]

{img:farm-courtyard-press-olive-oil-millstone-donkey-workers-sunny}
In autumn, the olives are harvested. The workers spread big cloths under the trees. They beat the branches with long sticks, and the olives fall onto the cloths. Then the olives are carried to the press. There, a big round millstone, turned by a donkey, crushes them into a paste. The paste is put into baskets and pressed, and the golden oil runs out into jars. It is stored in big clay jars, and sold all over the world.

[long-pause]

Once a year, at the end of the summer, the family goes to visit Arishat's cousin, who works on a farm in the countryside. They walk out through the city gate early in the morning, with a donkey carrying their bags. They walk along dusty roads, past fields and orchards. The children run ahead, picking wild flowers and chasing butterflies.

[pause]

{img:family-visiting-farm-children-eating-grapes-vineyard-cousins-summer}
On the farm, the children have a wonderful time. They help to pick the grapes, and eat as many as they want. They feed the chickens and the goats. They play with their cousins in the orchards. They swim in a little stream. In the evening, everyone eats together under a big tree, with bread and cheese and fresh fruit. And at night, the children sleep on the roof of the farmhouse, under more stars than they have ever seen in the city.

[pause]

The farms of Carthage also grow many vegetables. There are onions and garlic, leeks and cabbages, beans and lentils, cucumbers and melons. There are herbs like coriander and mint. The farmers water their gardens from wells and cisterns, with little channels running between the rows of plants. In the dry summer, the gardens are green islands in the golden countryside.

[pause]

The farm workers sing as they work in the fields. They sing old songs about the harvest and the rain, the sun and the soil. In the evenings, they gather around a fire and eat together. They tell stories and jokes. Some of them play little flutes made of reed. And the sound of their music drifts across the quiet fields, as the stars come out over the hills.

[long-pause]

{img:temple-carthage-incense-priests-offering-altar-columns-morning-light}
The people of Carthage worship many gods. Their gods came with them from Tyre, long ago, and some new ones were added in Africa.

[pause]

The most important god is Baal Hammon. He is an old, wise god, with a long beard, who sits on a throne. He is the lord of the sky and the harvest. The most important goddess is Tanit. She is the great mother goddess of Carthage, the protector of the city and its people. Her sign, the triangle with the circle, is seen everywhere in Carthage, on houses and stones and coins.

[long-pause]

There is also Melqart, the god of the city of Tyre. He is a god of the sea and of sailors, and of new beginnings. The Greeks thought he was like their hero Heracles. There is Eshmun, the god of healing and good health. His great temple stands on top of the hill of Byrsa. People go there when they are ill, to pray for help. And there are many other gods and goddesses, of the sea and the fields, of the home and the family.

[pause]

{img:sailors-praying-small-shrine-harbour-offering-wine-melqart-before-voyage}
The people of Carthage pray to their gods every day. They have little shrines in their homes, with figures of the gods. They pour a little wine or oil as an offering. They burn incense, which fills the air with sweet smoke. Before a long voyage, sailors go to the temple of Melqart and pray for a safe journey. When they come home safely, they give thanks.

[long-pause]

The priests of Carthage are important people. They wear long linen robes, and they shave their heads. They look after the temples and lead the ceremonies. They know the old prayers and the old songs. They play music on flutes and drums and lyres at the festivals. Some of them also study the stars, and know the calendar of the seasons.

[pause]

{img:priests-linen-robes-shaved-heads-procession-temple-steps-flutes-drums}
The Greeks and Romans later told many unkind stories about the religion of Carthage. Some of these stories may have had some truth in them, and some were probably exaggerated by enemies. Historians still discuss them today. For our story, we will remember the ordinary people of Carthage, praying in their homes and temples, for good health, safe journeys and happy families, just as people do everywhere.

[long-pause]

Every evening, in the family's house, Grandmother Batbaal lights a little lamp in front of the figure of Tanit, in the niche of the courtyard. She says a short prayer, very softly. She asks the goddess to look after the family: Himilco in the harbour, Arishat and the children, and Uncle Bomilcar, far away on the sea. Elissa often stands beside her, and says the prayer too. She likes the little flame, and the quiet words, and the feeling of being safe.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-girl-lighting-lamp-tanit-niche-courtyard-evening-prayer}
On feast days, the whole family goes to the temples. They wear their best clothes. They bring little gifts: cakes and flowers, wine and incense. They join the processions through the streets, with music and singing. They see the priests in their white robes, and the great statues of the gods. And afterwards, they have a special meal at home, with meat and sweets and wine. It is a time of joy and thanks, for the whole city.

[pause]

One of the most important festivals in Carthage is in spring, when the year begins again. People clean their houses and put on new clothes. They decorate the temples with flowers and green branches. There is music in the streets, and dancing, and feasting. And people pray for a good year, with plenty of rain and a rich harvest.

[pause]

The people of Carthage also believe that the spirits of their ancestors are still near them. They visit the graves of their families on special days. They bring little gifts of food and flowers, and oil for the lamps. They talk to their ancestors, and tell them the news of the family. In this way, the people of Carthage feel that their families are never really separated, even by death.

[long-pause]

{img:carthaginian-children-playing-street-knucklebones-ball-hoops-afternoon}
What is life like for children in Carthage, long ago?

[pause]

Children help their families from a young age. Boys often learn their father's trade, and girls learn from their mothers. They help to fetch water from the cistern, and to carry bread from the oven. They run errands to the market. They look after their younger brothers and sisters. They help in the workshops and the gardens.

[long-pause]

Some children also go to school, especially the sons of merchants and craftsmen. They learn to read and write the Punic alphabet, and to count. Merchants need to keep accounts and write letters, so reading and writing are important. Some children also learn Greek, because many traders speak Greek. A few clever children even learn to read the old books of their people.

[pause]

{img:boys-learning-punic-letters-teacher-wax-tablets-courtyard-shade}
Hanno goes to a little school in the mornings, run by an old scribe. He learns his letters and his numbers. He is good at counting, and he likes learning about other lands. But he finds it hard to sit still. His mind is always wandering off to the harbour and the ships. The old scribe sighs and tells him to pay attention. Then he smiles, because he was once a boy who loved ships too.

[long-pause]

The children of Carthage love to play. They play with balls made of leather, stuffed with wool. They play with hoops and spinning tops. They play knucklebones, with little bones from sheep, throwing and catching them. They play hide and seek in the narrow streets. Girls play with dolls of clay and wood. Archaeologists have found many little toys from Carthage: tiny clay animals, little whistles, and rattles shaped like birds.

[pause]

{img:clay-toys-bird-rattle-small-animals-dolls-museum-case-carthage-later}
In summer, the children swim in the sea. There are little beaches along the coast, with soft sand and clear water. They splash and dive and look for shells. They catch little crabs in the rock pools. Hanno is learning to swim better, because he wants to be a sailor. Uncle Bomilcar taught him the basics. Now he practises every day, until he can swim far out from the shore and back again.

[long-pause]

Elissa has a best friend called Sophonisba, who lives across the street. Her father is a potter. The two girls play together almost every day. They make little pots and figures from leftover clay in the potter's workshop. Sophonisba's father lets them fire them in his kiln. Elissa made a little clay dog, which looks a bit like their own dog. She is very proud of it.

[pause]

{img:two-girls-making-clay-figures-potters-workshop-wheel-kiln-afternoon}
In the evenings, the children of the street gather on the doorsteps and tell each other stories. They tell stories of Queen Elissa and the oxhide, of Hanno the Navigator and the gorillas. They tell stories of ghosts and sea monsters and brave heroes. Elissa tells the best stories of all, because she has learned so many from her grandmother. The other children sit in a circle around her, listening, until their mothers call them in for bed.

[pause]

Children in Carthage also love animals. Many families keep cats and dogs. Some keep birds in little cages. Some children have pet tortoises, which wander slowly around the courtyard. Elissa's friend Sophonisba has a tortoise called Old Slow, which is older than her grandmother, people say. The children feed it lettuce leaves and watch it eat, very slowly.

[pause]

When children in Carthage are born, their families have a little celebration. They give the baby a name, often the name of a god or a grandparent. They give the baby little gifts: a necklace of beads, a little amulet for protection. Friends and neighbours come to visit, bringing food and good wishes. And the grandmother holds the new baby, and sings it an old lullaby from Tyre.

[pause]

On feast days, children in Carthage get little presents. Sometimes it is a new toy, or a little cake shaped like an animal. Sometimes it is a new tunic, or a ribbon for the hair. Hanno once got a little bronze ship, no bigger than his hand. He keeps it on a shelf beside his bed, next to the carved ship from his uncle. He says one day he will have a real one.

[long-pause]

{img:merchant-ship-sailing-through-strait-pillars-heracles-rocks-ocean-beyond}
In the spring, Uncle Bomilcar sets off on a long voyage. His ship will sail to Gades, beyond the Pillars of Heracles. Then it will sail north, along the ocean coast, to buy tin. It will be away for many months.

[pause]

Before he leaves, he comes to say goodbye to the family. He brings presents: a little carved ship for Hanno, and a shell necklace for Elissa. He tells them he will be back before the autumn storms. He hugs his sister and his mother. Then he walks down to the harbour, with his bag on his shoulder. The family goes with him, to wave as the ship sails away.

[long-pause]

The ship is big and round, with a tall mast and a great square sail. Its name is the Star of Tanit. It has a crew of about twenty sailors, and a captain who has made this voyage many times. The ship is loaded with goods to sell: purple cloth, pottery, glass beads and wine. It will come back with tin and silver, if all goes well.

[pause]

{img:family-waving-from-quay-merchant-ship-leaving-harbour-spring-morning}
The family watches the ship sail slowly out of the harbour, with its sail filling in the wind. It grows smaller and smaller, until it is just a little white speck on the blue sea. Then it is gone. Hanno stands on the harbour wall for a long time, still looking. His mother puts her hand on his shoulder. He will come back, she says. He always does.

[long-pause]

Spring turns into summer. Life goes on in Carthage. Himilco works in the harbour. Arishat weaves and sells her cloth. The children go to school and play in the streets. But every day, Hanno goes down to the harbour to watch for the Star of Tanit. And every evening, Grandmother Batbaal lights the lamp for Tanit, and prays for her son on the sea.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-praying-lamp-niche-evening-worried-hopeful-courtyard}
In late summer, news arrives from Gades, with another ship. The Star of Tanit reached Gades safely, the news says. It sailed north, toward the tin islands, at the beginning of the summer. Everyone is relieved. But they know that the ocean beyond Gades is wild and dangerous, with great waves, strong tides and thick fogs. And the autumn storms will come soon.

[long-pause]

Autumn comes. The days get shorter. The wind changes, and the sea becomes grey and rough. One by one, the trading ships come home to Carthage, before the winter. But the Star of Tanit does not come. Every day, Hanno watches. Every day, the family waits. Arishat is quiet. Grandmother Batbaal prays longer in the evenings.

[pause]

{img:boy-watching-grey-sea-harbour-wall-autumn-wind-waiting-alone}
Then, one stormy evening, a ship comes into the harbour. It is not the Star of Tanit. But it brings news. Its captain met the Star of Tanit at Gades, two weeks ago, he says. The Star of Tanit was damaged by a great storm in the ocean, and had to stop for repairs. But all the crew were safe. It would set off for Carthage as soon as it could. The family hugs each other with relief. And they wait, a little more patiently now.

[pause]

On the ship, the sailors have a hard but simple life. They sleep on the deck, wrapped in blankets. They eat bread and dried fish, olives and cheese. They drink water from big jars, and a little wine. They work the sails and the steering oars, and pump out the water that leaks in. And in the evenings, they sing old sailors' songs and tell stories under the stars.

[pause]

While her brother is away, Arishat weaves a special cloth for him. It is a thick cloak of wool, dyed blue like the sea, with a border of little ships. She works on it every evening, after her other work is done. Elissa helps her wind the wool. It takes the whole summer. She wants it to be ready when he comes home, to keep him warm on his next voyage.

[long-pause]

{img:merchant-ship-patched-sail-entering-harbour-carthage-late-autumn-sunrise}
Ten days later, early in the morning, Hanno is at the harbour, as always. The sky is clear after the storms, and the sea is calm and blue. And there, coming slowly into the trade harbour, is a big round ship, with a patched sail and a painted eye on its side. It is the Star of Tanit.

[pause]

Hanno runs all the way home, faster than he has ever run in his life. He bursts into the courtyard, shouting. The ship is here! Uncle Bomilcar is home! The whole family drops what they are doing and runs down to the harbour. Even Grandmother Batbaal comes, as fast as her old legs can carry her, holding Elissa's hand.

[long-pause]

On the quay, the sailors are tying up the ship. And there is Uncle Bomilcar, with a big smile on his face. He is thin and brown from the sun and the wind, and his beard has grown long. He jumps down onto the quay and hugs everyone at once. Grandmother Batbaal cries with happiness. Elissa hugs his leg and will not let go. Hanno just grins and grins.

[pause]

{img:sailor-uncle-reunion-quay-family-hugging-grandmother-crying-happiness}
That evening, there is a feast in the courtyard of the family's house. Arishat cooks a big pot of fish stew, with onions and herbs. There is fresh bread, olives, cheese and figs. Himilco brings a jar of good wine. Neighbours come to welcome Bomilcar home. The courtyard is full of people, laughing and talking. Someone plays a flute, and the children dance.

[long-pause]

After supper, Uncle Bomilcar tells the story of his voyage. He tells them about Gades, the old city on an island at the edge of the ocean. He tells them about the great tides, and the waves as high as houses. He tells them about the fog, so thick that you could not see the front of the ship. He tells them about the storm that tore the sail and broke the mast. And he tells them about the green misty islands in the north, where the people live in round houses and dig tin from the earth.

[pause]

{img:courtyard-feast-night-lamps-uncle-telling-voyage-children-listening-neighbours}
Then he opens his bag and takes out his presents. For his mother, a soft warm cloak of wool from the north. For Arishat, a little silver bracelet from Spain. For Himilco, a fine knife with a handle of bone. For Elissa, a little piece of amber, golden and glowing, with a tiny insect trapped inside. And for Hanno, a real sailor's knife, in a leather case. Hanno holds it as if it were made of gold.

[long-pause]

{img:boy-and-uncle-flat-roof-night-stars-pointing-north-star-harbour-below}
Late that night, when the neighbours have gone home, Hanno sits with his uncle on the roof of the house. They look up at the stars. Uncle Bomilcar shows him the stars that sailors use to find their way at night. That one points to the north, he says. Follow it, and you will never be lost. Hanno looks up at the star for a long time. Then he asks if he can sail with his uncle, one day. Uncle Bomilcar smiles. When you are older, he says. And when you can swim properly. Hanno laughs. And this time, he does not blush at all.

[pause]

The next day, the whole family goes to the temple of Melqart to give thanks for Bomilcar's safe return. They bring a little gift of incense and a cake of honey. Bomilcar kneels before the altar and says a prayer of thanks. Grandmother Batbaal says a prayer too, with tears in her eyes. Then they walk home together through the sunny streets, all of them smiling.

[pause]

Arishat gives her brother the blue cloak with the little ships. Bomilcar puts it on at once and turns around, to show everyone. It is the most beautiful cloak he has ever had, he says. He will wear it on every voyage, and think of his sister and her family every time. Arishat blushes with pleasure. And Elissa says proudly that she helped to wind the wool.

[long-pause]

{img:north-african-forest-elephants-grazing-hills-trees-ancient-wild}
In the hills and forests of North Africa, in the time of our story, there are elephants. They are smaller than the elephants of India, or the great elephants of the African plains. They are called North African forest elephants. Today, they are no longer there. But long ago, they lived in the mountains and valleys not far from Carthage.

[long-pause]

The people of Carthage learned to catch and tame these elephants. They kept them in great stables, near the city walls. Some old writers say there were stables for three hundred elephants inside the walls of Carthage. The elephants were trained by special keepers, often from India or from the peoples of Africa, who knew how to look after them. They were used in the army, a little like big strong horses.

[pause]

{img:elephant-stables-city-walls-keepers-feeding-elephants-hay-calm}
The most famous elephants of Carthage belonged to the general Hannibal. About forty years before our story, during the long war with Rome, Hannibal did something nobody thought was possible. He led his army from Spain, across the high mountains of the Alps, into Italy. And he took with him thirty-seven elephants.

[long-pause]

The crossing of the Alps was very hard. It was autumn, and the snow was beginning to fall. The paths were narrow and steep, along the edges of cliffs. The elephants had to walk carefully, step by step, on the icy rocks. The soldiers and the animals were cold and hungry. But they kept going. And at last, they came down out of the mountains, into the green valleys of Italy. People talked about it for thousands of years.

[pause]

{img:hannibal-elephants-crossing-alps-snow-mountain-path-autumn-distant-calm}
We will not follow the war. It was long and hard, and in the end Carthage lost it. After the war, the peace treaty said that Carthage was not allowed to keep war elephants anymore. So in the time of our story, the great elephant stables are empty and quiet. But the people of Carthage still remember the elephants, and the general who crossed the mountains.

[long-pause]

Grandmother Batbaal saw the elephants of Carthage when she was a girl. She remembers them walking through the streets, on their way to the stables. They were huge and grey, with big flapping ears and long swaying trunks. The keepers sat on their backs, with little sticks. The children of the street followed them, laughing and shouting. Sometimes an elephant would reach out its trunk and take a fig from a child's hand.

[pause]

{img:elephant-taking-fig-from-child-hand-street-keeper-smiling-crowd}
Elissa loves this story. She asks her grandmother to tell it again and again. What did the elephant's trunk feel like? Was it rough or soft? Did it say thank you? Grandmother Batbaal laughs. It was warm and a little rough, she says, like an old leather bag. And yes, she says, it did say thank you. It flapped its ears, and made a deep, happy rumble. Elissa closes her eyes and tries to imagine it. And that night, she dreams of elephants.

[pause]

The elephants of Carthage were also used for work. They could carry heavy loads and pull great logs out of the forests. They could move big stones for building. The keepers loved their elephants and gave them names. They washed them in the sea and fed them hay and fruit. And the elephants, people said, loved their keepers too.

[pause]

Elephants were also used in Egypt and Syria in those times, by the Greek kings who ruled there. They came from Africa and India. There was even a kind of rivalry between the kings, about who had the most and the best elephants. People came from far away to see them. Elephants were seen as a sign of power and wealth, like a great palace or a fine fleet.

[pause]

Hanno asks his grandmother if there are still elephants in the forests, far away in the mountains. Grandmother Batbaal says that there are, she thinks, though fewer than before. Perhaps one day, she says, you will see one in the wild, on a journey into the hills. Hanno decides that he will go and look for them, one day, when he is older. Right after he has sailed to the tin islands, of course.

[long-pause]

{img:carthage-150bc-city-walls-harbours-sea-quiet-before-storm-evening-later}
The peace of our story did not last forever. About thirty years later, there was a third and last war between Carthage and Rome.

[pause]

Carthage had become rich again after the second war. The Romans were worried. A Roman senator called Cato ended every speech he made, on any subject, with the same words: Carthage must be destroyed. In the year 149 before Christ, Rome went to war against Carthage again. The people of Carthage defended their city bravely, for three years. But in 146 before Christ, the city fell.

[long-pause]

We will not dwell on what happened then. It was a very sad time. Much of the city was destroyed, and many of its people suffered. The great harbours were left empty. The temples on the hill of Byrsa were burned. We remember the people of Carthage with sorrow and respect. And we remember that their city had stood for more than six hundred years, one of the greatest cities of the ancient world.

[pause]

{img:roman-carthage-rebuilt-forum-baths-columns-sea-later-centuries}
But that was not the end of the story. About a hundred years later, the Romans built a new city on the same place. Julius Caesar planned it, and the emperor Augustus built it. Roman Carthage became one of the biggest and richest cities of the Roman Empire. It had a great forum, theatres, temples and enormous public baths by the sea. It was the capital of the Roman province of Africa.

[long-pause]

And the old Punic culture did not disappear completely. In the countryside of North Africa, people went on speaking Punic for hundreds of years. They went on worshipping some of the old gods, under new names. They went on farming olives and grapes, as Mago had taught. A famous Christian writer from North Africa, called Augustine, wrote that the farmers near his town still called themselves Canaanites, the old name of the Phoenicians, in his time, six hundred years after the fall of Carthage.

[pause]

{img:great-roman-baths-antoninus-carthage-ruins-columns-sea-later}
Later, Carthage became part of other empires: the Vandals, the Byzantines, and then the Arab caliphate. Slowly, the city faded. A new city, Tunis, grew up nearby, and became the main city of the region. The old stones of Carthage were taken to build new houses and mosques. But the name of Carthage was never forgotten.

[long-pause]

{img:old-woman-elissa-telling-stories-grandchildren-courtyard-lamp-evening}
What about the family in our story? We can only imagine. Perhaps Hanno became a sailor after all, and sailed with his uncle through the Pillars of Heracles, to the misty tin islands in the north. Perhaps he became a captain, with his own ship. Perhaps Elissa became a weaver like her mother, and a teller of stories like her grandmother. And perhaps, when she was old, she sat in a courtyard with her grandchildren. And she told them about Queen Elissa and the oxhide, Hanno the Navigator and the gorillas, and the elephant who took a fig and said thank you.

[pause]

In Roman times, the people of Carthage went on making beautiful mosaics, floors made of little coloured stones. They showed fishermen and farmers, animals and plants, the sea and the seasons. Many of these mosaics have survived. They show the everyday life of the people of North Africa, with their fields and their boats, their feasts and their hunts.

[pause]

The story of Carthage was told again and again, by writers and poets, for two thousand years. Painters painted pictures of Queen Elissa and of Hannibal crossing the Alps. Composers wrote operas about Dido. Children all over the world learned about Hannibal and his elephants. And so, in a way, Carthage never really disappeared. It lived on in the stories that people told about it.

[long-pause]

{img:archaeologists-excavating-punic-houses-byrsa-hill-trenches-walls-sunny-today}
For a long time, people thought there was almost nothing left of the old Punic city of Carthage. The Romans had built their own city on top of it. But in the last hundred years, archaeologists have found that a great deal is still there, under the ground.

[pause]

On the hill of Byrsa, archaeologists dug down under the Roman ruins. And there, they found a whole neighbourhood of Punic houses. The houses stood along narrow streets, just as in our story. They had courtyards and cisterns, and floors of red cement with little white stones. Some of them even had little bathrooms with sitting bathtubs. It is called the Hannibal Quarter, because it is from about the time of Hannibal.

[long-pause]

Down by the sea, the two great harbours of Carthage can still be seen. Today they are two quiet lagoons, one long and one round. In the middle of the round one, there is still a little round island. Archaeologists have dug there too. They found the remains of the ship sheds, the slipways and the stone foundations of the columns. They found that the harbour was built just as the old Greek writers described it.

[pause]

{img:circular-lagoon-carthage-today-island-middle-quiet-water-houses-around-today}
Archaeologists have also found many small objects from everyday life. There are clay lamps and cooking pots, perfume bottles and glass beads. There are little masks made of clay, with funny or frightening faces. There are painted ostrich eggs. There are coins, with the head of Tanit on one side and a horse or a palm tree on the other. There are little figures of gods and goddesses, and children's toys.

[long-pause]

There are also many stone tablets with writing in Punic. They are carved with the old Phoenician letters. Many of them are dedications to the gods, with the names of the people who made them. So we know the names of thousands of ordinary people of Carthage. Names like Hanno and Himilco, Arishat and Batbaal, Bomilcar and Sophonisba. Some tablets also tell us the jobs of the people: weavers and shipbuilders, scribes and doctors, cooks and goldsmiths.

[pause]

{img:punic-stone-tablets-inscriptions-tanit-symbol-museum-garden-today}
Near the city of Kerkouane, on the coast not far from Carthage, there is a whole small Punic town, which was never rebuilt by the Romans. It was left empty more than two thousand years ago, and slowly covered by sand. Today, you can walk through its streets and into its houses. You can see the courtyards, the red floors with the sign of Tanit, the little bathrooms, the wells. It is the best place in the world to see how the people of Carthage really lived.

[long-pause]

Divers have explored the sea too, near Sicily and Sardinia. They have found Punic shipwrecks, with their cargoes still on board. One of them, near the town of Marsala in Sicily, was a warship. On its timbers, the little Phoenician letters, painted by the shipbuilders, could still be read. It is now in a museum in Marsala, carefully preserved. Visitors can see the long curved planks, and the little letters that helped the shipwrights of Carthage put it together.

[pause]

{img:punic-ship-remains-museum-marsala-timbers-display-light-visitors-today}
Little by little, the archaeologists are bringing the people of Carthage back into the light. For a long time, we only knew them through the writings of their enemies, the Greeks and the Romans. Now we can see their own houses, their own pots and their own names. We can see how they cooked and washed, played and prayed. And we can see that they were people very much like us, living their lives, by the edge of the sea.

[pause]

Archaeologists have also studied the old food of Carthage. They have found seeds and bones in the old houses and rubbish heaps. They found olive stones and grape pips, fig seeds and pomegranate seeds. They found the bones of fish and sheep, goats and chickens. So we know quite a lot about what the people of Carthage ate, more than two thousand years ago.

[pause]

Some of the most beautiful finds from Carthage are little masks made of clay. Some show laughing faces, with big grins. Some show old men with wrinkled foreheads. Some show fierce faces, with wide eyes and open mouths. People probably hung them in their houses or put them in graves, to protect against bad luck. Today they are in museums, and they still look at visitors with their strange expressions.

[long-pause]

{img:carthage-today-tunisia-white-houses-blue-doors-sea-view-ruins-hill-today}
Today, Carthage is a quiet and beautiful town, by the sea, in the country of Tunisia. It is a suburb of the busy capital city, Tunis.

[pause]

The town is green and peaceful, with white houses and gardens full of flowers. Bougainvillea climbs over the walls, in pink and purple. Palm trees and olive trees grow along the streets. And everywhere, among the houses and the gardens, there are ruins: old stones and columns, mosaics and foundations, from Punic and Roman times. The whole town is a World Heritage Site.

[long-pause]

On the hill of Byrsa, there is now a museum, full of things from old Carthage. There are statues and mosaics, pottery and jewellery, glass beads and painted ostrich eggs. There are little clay masks and coins with the face of Tanit. From the top of the hill, you can look out over the bay, just as the people of Carthage did long ago. You can see the two old harbours, the blue sea, and the mountains on the other side of the bay.

[pause]

{img:byrsa-hill-museum-view-bay-mountains-harbours-lagoons-sunset-today}
Down by the sea, there are the great Roman baths, called the Antonine Baths. They are some of the biggest Roman baths ever built. Their huge stone columns still stand by the shore, against the blue sea. Children play among the ruins, and families walk in the gardens around them. The waves wash gently onto the rocks below.

[long-pause]

Not far from Carthage, on a hill above the sea, there is a beautiful little village called Sidi Bou Said. All its houses are painted white, with bright blue doors and windows and shutters. There are little cafés with terraces, looking out over the sea. People sit there in the evenings, drinking mint tea with pine nuts, and watching the sun go down over the bay of Carthage.

[pause]

{img:sidi-bou-said-white-blue-houses-cafe-terrace-mint-tea-sunset-sea-today}
The people of Tunisia are proud of Carthage. They see it as an important part of their history. On their money, there are pictures of Carthage and of Hannibal. Schoolchildren learn about Queen Elissa and the oxhide. And every summer, there is a great music festival in the old Roman theatre of Carthage, with concerts under the stars.

[long-pause]

In the markets of Tunis, there are still many things that the people of old Carthage would recognise. There are piles of olives and dates, figs and pomegranates. There are jars of golden olive oil. There are woven cloths and carpets, in bright colours. There are potters making pots, and craftsmen working with copper and glass. And there are little hands of Fatima, a sign of protection, hanging over the doors. They look very much like the little carved hands of old Carthage.

[pause]

{img:tunis-medina-market-today-olives-dates-carpets-copper-colourful-busy-today}
The fishermen of Tunisia still sail out into the bay in the early morning, as the fishermen of Carthage did. They still bring back their catch to the little harbours. And in the evening, when the sun goes down over the sea, the old stones of Carthage glow golden in the last light. The two old harbours lie quiet and still. And the waves wash softly onto the shore, where Queen Elissa landed, long, long ago.

[pause]

Tunisian food still has many of the old flavours. There are olives and olive oil, bread and couscous, fish from the sea. There are dates and figs and pomegranates. There is harissa, a hot red pepper paste, and many spices. Families still sit together in their courtyards in the evenings, eating and talking, just as the families of Carthage did long ago.

[pause]

Every year, many visitors come to Carthage from all over the world. They walk among the ruins and visit the museum. They stand by the round harbour and imagine the ships. They look out over the bay from the hill of Byrsa. And many of them say that Carthage has a special feeling, quiet and peaceful, as if the old city were still there, sleeping under the gardens and the white houses.

[long-pause]

{img:carthage-evening-180bc-sunset-harbours-byrsa-temple-golden-sea-calm}
So we leave Carthage tonight, around the year 180 before Christ, as the evening falls.

[pause]

The sun is setting over the sea, to the west, toward the Pillars of Heracles and the great ocean beyond. The sky turns gold, then pink, then a deep soft blue. The white houses of the city glow warm in the last light. On the hill of Byrsa, the temple of Eshmun shines like gold. And in the harbours, the ships rock gently at anchor, their sails folded for the night.

In the round harbour, the shipwrights have gone home. The ship sheds are quiet. The columns stand in their great circle, dark against the evening sky. On the little island in the middle, a light is burning in the admiral's tower. And the water of the harbour is still and smooth, reflecting the first stars.

In the family's house, the lamps are lit in the courtyard. Himilco is sitting on a bench, resting his tired arms. Arishat is putting away the last of the cloth from the loom. Uncle Bomilcar is home, sitting in the warm courtyard with a cup of wine, telling one more story about the ocean. Hanno sits close beside him, with his new sailor's knife in his lap, listening to every word.

Grandmother Batbaal is sitting in her corner, wrapped in the warm woollen cloak from the north. She lights the little lamp in front of the figure of Tanit, and says her evening prayer. Tonight, she adds a special thank you, because her son has come home safely from the sea. The little flame flickers softly in the evening breeze.

Elissa is sitting beside her grandmother, holding the little piece of amber up to the lamp. The light shines through it, golden and warm. She can see the tiny insect inside, trapped there for thousands of years. She wonders where it came from, and how long it has been there. Then she leans her head on her grandmother's shoulder. And Grandmother Batbaal begins to tell her a story, softly, about a queen who came from the sea, long ago.

Up on the roof, the stars are coming out over Carthage, one by one, and then by the thousands. The north star shines bright and steady, the star that guides the sailors home. Down in the street, a dog barks once, and then is quiet. A door closes. A mother sings softly to her baby. And the sea whispers against the harbour walls, as it has every night, since Queen Elissa first saw this shore.

Slowly, the lamps go out in the houses, one after another. The family goes to bed. Hanno falls asleep dreaming of ships and faraway islands. Elissa falls asleep with the amber in her hand. And the great city of the sea sleeps peacefully under the stars, on the edge of Africa, beside the quiet, endless sea.
