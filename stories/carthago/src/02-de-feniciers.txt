The people of Carthage are Phoenicians. Their ancestors came from a land far to the east, on the coast of what is now Lebanon.

The Phoenicians lived in a row of cities along the eastern shore of the Mediterranean Sea. The most famous of these cities were Tyre and Sidon, and Byblos. Behind them were high mountains, covered with great forests of cedar trees. In front of them was the sea. There was not much land for farming. So the Phoenicians turned to the sea. They became the greatest sailors and traders of the ancient world.

--

The Phoenicians built strong wooden ships from the cedars of their mountains. They sailed across the whole Mediterranean, from one end to the other. They traded with Egypt and Greece, with Cyprus and Sicily, with Spain and Africa. Everywhere they went, they built little trading posts and harbours. Some of these grew into towns and cities. Carthage was the greatest of them all.

{img:phoenician-merchant-ship-cedar-wood-square-sail-horse-head-prow-sea}
The Phoenicians gave the world a wonderful gift: the alphabet. Before them, people wrote with hundreds of little pictures or signs, which were very hard to learn. The Phoenicians made a simple set of just twenty-two letters. Each letter stood for one sound. With these few letters, you could write any word. It was much easier to learn. The Greeks learned it from the Phoenicians, and then the Romans learned it from the Greeks. The letters you are reading in books today come from that old Phoenician alphabet.

--

The first letter of the Phoenician alphabet was called aleph, which means ox. It looked a little like the head of an ox, with two horns. The second letter was called beth, which means house. From aleph and beth, the Greeks made alpha and beta. And from alpha and beta comes our word alphabet. So every time we say alphabet, we are speaking a little bit of Phoenician.

{img:phoenician-letters-carved-stone-tablet-aleph-beth-close-sunlight}
The Phoenicians were also famous craftsmen. They made beautiful things of glass, ivory, silver and bronze. They made little glass beads in bright colours, with faces and eyes on them. They carved ivory into delicate panels, with pictures of animals and plants. They made bowls of silver, decorated with scenes of hunting and feasting. People all over the ancient world wanted to buy them.

--

The people of Carthage still speak the old Phoenician language, though it has changed a little over the centuries. Today we call it Punic. They still worship the old gods of Tyre. And they still feel connected to their mother city, far away in the east. Every year, Carthage sends a ship to Tyre, with gifts for the temple of the god Melqart. It is a way of saying: we have not forgotten where we came from.

{img:grandmother-telling-children-about-tyre-evening-lamp-courtyard-carthage}
Grandmother Batbaal, in our story, loves to tell the children about Tyre. She has never been there herself. But her own grandmother told her about it, when she was a little girl. Tyre is a city on an island, she says, with high walls rising straight out of the sea. Its harbours are full of ships, and its streets are full of purple cloth and shining glass. And one day, she says, perhaps one of you will sail there, and see it for us all.

The Phoenicians were also famous for their ships' figureheads. The front of a Phoenician ship often had a carved horse's head, painted and decorated. Sailors believed it brought good luck and helped the ship to find its way. The Greeks called these ships hippoi, which means horses. And people who saw them coming over the sea knew at once: the Phoenicians are here.

In the evenings, the old sailors of Carthage like to sit together by the harbour and talk about the past. They talk about the old voyages of their ancestors, to Egypt and Greece and the islands. They talk about the cedar forests of Lebanon and the great city of Tyre. They have never seen them. But they talk about them as if they were home.

The Phoenicians also brought new plants and animals to the lands where they settled. They brought olive trees and grape vines, pomegranates and figs. They brought chickens and new kinds of sheep. They taught local peoples how to make glass and purple dye, how to build ships and how to write. Wherever they went, they left something new behind.
