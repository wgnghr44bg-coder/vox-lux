Tonight we are going nearly two hundred years back, to the great grey city of London, in the weeks before Christmas, in the year 1843.

[long-pause]

It is a cold December afternoon, and the light is already fading.

[pause]

A thick, yellowish fog is rolling up from the river Thames, and creeping through the streets, so that the gas lamps, just being lit, glow like little blurred moons in the gloom. Above the rooftops, the great dome of Saint Paul's Cathedral looms out of the mist, grey and enormous, and then disappears again.

[pause]

If you enjoy quiet journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel. Now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

London, in 1843, is the largest city in the world.

[pause]

Nearly two million people live here, more than in any city that has ever existed. Every year, more of them arrive: from the countryside of England, where there is not enough work on the farms; from Ireland, where the people are poor and hungry; from Scotland and Wales, and from every country in Europe.

[pause]

The city stretches for miles along both banks of the Thames, an endless maze of streets and squares, alleys and courtyards, churches and chapels, markets and warehouses, factories and docks, grand mansions and crowded little houses, all built of brick and stone, and all blackened by the smoke of a million coal fires.

[long-pause]

The river is the heart of the city.

[pause]

It is crowded with ships, so many that, in the Pool of London, below London Bridge, their masts stand together like a leafless forest. There are tall sailing ships from India and China and America, with cargoes of tea and silk and cotton and spices. There are little steamboats, puffing up and down the river, carrying passengers from one landing stage to another for a penny or two. There are barges, with rust-red sails, loaded with coal and bricks and hay.

[pause]

{img:pool-of-london-ships-masts-steamboats-winter}
On the banks, there are wharves and warehouses, cranes and docks, where thousands of men work, loading and unloading the ships, all day long, in all weathers.

[long-pause]

This year, there are two new wonders in London, which everyone has been to see.

[pause]

In March, the Thames Tunnel opened: the first tunnel ever built under a river, a long, gaslit brick passage beneath the water, from Wapping to Rotherhithe, designed by the great engineer Marc Brunel, and his son, Isambard. On the first day, fifty thousand people walked through it, marvelling at the thought of the ships sailing over their heads.

[pause]

{img:thames-tunnel-gaslit-brick-arches-visitors}
And in Trafalgar Square, just a few weeks ago, in November, the workmen finished putting up a tall column, with a statue of Admiral Nelson on the top, high above the square, looking out over the city toward the sea.

[long-pause]

But in December, Londoners are thinking about other things.

[pause]

They are thinking about Christmas.

[pause]

In the shops along the great streets, the windows are being filled with good things: geese and turkeys, puddings and pies, oranges and figs, toys and books and gifts. In the markets, the stalls are piled high with holly and mistletoe. In the kitchens of the houses, the women are stirring the Christmas puddings, and the children are being sent out to fetch the ingredients.

[pause]

And in a large, comfortable house near Regent's Park, a young writer of thirty-one is working, late at night, by the light of a candle, on a little book.

[long-pause]

His name is Charles Dickens.

[pause]

He is already famous. His stories, The Pickwick Papers, Oliver Twist, The Old Curiosity Shop, have been read and loved by millions of people, all over Britain and America. But this little book will become, in the end, the most famous of all his works, and one of the most famous stories ever written.

[pause]

{img:young-writer-desk-candle-quill-night-1843}
He calls it A Christmas Carol.

[pause]

In the next few hours, we are going to spend the weeks before Christmas, in the year 1843, in the London that he knew and loved, and walked through, night after night, and wrote about. We will meet the people of the city, rich and poor, and see how they lived, and how they celebrated Christmas. And we will hear, once more, the story that he told.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-street-morning-carts-crossing-sweeper}
The streets of London in 1843 are full of life from before dawn until long after dark.

[pause]

In the early morning, while it is still dark, the carts begin to rumble in from the countryside, loaded with vegetables and milk and hay and meat, toward the great markets. The milkmaids walk through the streets with their yokes and their pails, calling out at the doors. The chimney sweeps, little boys with brushes over their shoulders, go from house to house, to clean the chimneys before the fires are lit.

[long-pause]

As the light grows, the streets fill up.

[pause]

There are carts and wagons, carriages and cabs, horses and donkeys, and crowds of people on foot, all pushing and jostling along the narrow pavements. Clerks in black coats and tall hats hurry to their offices in the City. Shop boys take down the shutters from the windows. Maids in white caps scrub the front steps of the houses. Street sellers set up their stalls on the corners.

[pause]

There are omnibuses, too: big, boxy carriages pulled by two or three horses, with passengers crammed inside and perched on the roof, and a conductor standing on a little step at the back, shouting out the stops. A ride across London costs sixpence. And there are the cabs, light two-wheeled carriages, with the driver sitting high up behind, which can be hailed in the street.

[long-pause]

The noise is tremendous.

[pause]

Iron-shod wheels and horses' hooves clatter on the cobblestones. Drivers shout and crack their whips. Street sellers cry their wares: hot chestnuts, baked potatoes, oranges, muffins, watercress, matches, old clothes. Organ grinders play on the corners. Church bells ring the hours, one after another, from a hundred towers.

[pause]

{img:street-sellers-hot-chestnuts-baked-potatoes-cart}
Visitors from the country are amazed and bewildered by it all. Londoners hardly notice.

[long-pause]

At every crossing, there is a crossing sweeper.

[pause]

They are poor boys and girls, and sometimes old men and women, with a broom, who sweep a clean path across the muddy street, so that ladies and gentlemen can cross without getting mud on their shoes and their long skirts. In return, they hope for a penny, or a halfpenny.

[pause]

The streets are very muddy, especially in winter. They are covered with a thick layer of black mud, made of earth and soot and horse manure and rain, which splashes up onto everyone who passes.

[long-pause]

{img:gas-lamplighter-ladder-foggy-street-dusk}
In the late afternoon, in December, as the light fades early, the lamplighters come out.

[pause]

They walk through the streets with their long poles and their little ladders, from lamp post to lamp post, turning on the gas and lighting the flames, one after another. Gas lighting is still quite new and wonderful. London has had gas lamps in its streets for about thirty years, and they are much brighter than the old oil lamps. In the main streets, the shops are lit by gas too, and their windows glow with warm, golden light.

[long-pause]

On a winter evening, when the fog comes down, the streets of London become strange and mysterious.

[pause]

The fog is thick and yellow-brown, made of the smoke of a million coal fires, mixed with the damp air from the river. It smells of soot and sulphur. It is so thick, sometimes, that people cannot see the other side of the street, or even their own hands in front of their faces. The gas lamps glow faintly through it, like little smudges of light. Carriages creep along at a walking pace, with boys walking in front of them carrying torches. People lose their way, a few streets from home.

[pause]

{img:link-boy-torch-guiding-carriage-thick-fog}
Charles Dickens loves the London fog. He will describe it, many times, in his books.

[long-pause]

The great streets of London are lined with fine shops.

[pause]

Regent Street, near Dickens's house, is the most elegant street in London, with a long, curving row of white stucco buildings, and shops selling silks and lace, hats and gloves, jewellery and perfume, to the ladies of fashion, who arrive in their carriages. The Strand and Oxford Street and Cheapside are crowded with shops of every kind.

[pause]

But just behind the grand streets, there are narrow alleys and courts, where the poor live, crowded together in old, damp, falling-down houses. London is a city of the very rich and the very poor, living side by side, a few streets apart.

[long-pause]

At night, the streets grow quieter, but they never become completely silent.

[pause]

There are always cabs rattling home, and late travellers, and policemen walking their beats, in their blue coats and tall hats, with their lanterns. The policemen are still quite new, too. They were introduced only fourteen years ago, by Sir Robert Peel, and people call them bobbies, or peelers, after him.

[pause]

{img:victorian-policeman-lantern-quiet-street-night}
And there is a young man, walking through the streets, mile after mile, late into the night, thinking: Charles Dickens, who often cannot sleep, and who walks the streets of London in the dark, ten or fifteen or twenty miles, to think about his stories.

[long-pause]

{img:devonshire-terrace-house-regents-park-winter}
Charles Dickens lives in a large, handsome house at Number One, Devonshire Terrace, near Regent's Park, in the north of London.

[pause]

It is a big, comfortable family house, with a garden behind it, and a high wall, and tall windows looking out onto the street. Dickens has filled it with good furniture, and books, and pictures, and mirrors, which he loves, and he keeps it bright and clean and tidy, because he hates untidiness.

[long-pause]

He is thirty-one years old in 1843.

[pause]

He is slim and lively and handsome, with long, wavy brown hair, bright, quick, sparkling eyes, and a restless energy that never seems to stop. He dresses rather flamboyantly, in bright waistcoats and colourful neckties, which some people think a little vulgar. He talks fast, and laughs a great deal, and he is wonderful company.

[pause]

He loves parties and games and theatre and conjuring tricks. He loves long walks, very fast, for many miles. He loves his friends, and his children, and his work.

[long-pause]

He is married to Catherine, a gentle, plump, kindly woman, the daughter of a Scottish journalist. They have been married for seven years, and they have four children: Charley, who is six; Mamie, who is five; Katey, who is four; and Walter, who is two. And Catherine is expecting another baby, in the new year.

[pause]

{img:victorian-family-parlour-four-children-fire}
The house is full of children's voices, and toys, and nursemaids, and pets: a talking raven, like the raven called Grip that he once had and put into one of his novels, and a dog.

[long-pause]

Dickens has worked very hard to get where he is.

[pause]

He was born in 1812, in Portsmouth, the son of a clerk in the navy pay office, a cheerful, generous, hopeless man named John Dickens, who was always in debt. When Charles was ten, the family moved to London, to a small, poor house in Camden Town.

[pause]

When he was twelve, his father was arrested for debt, and sent to the Marshalsea prison, in Southwark, and his mother and the younger children went to live there with him, as families sometimes did in those days. And young Charles was sent out to work, in a factory beside the river, pasting labels onto pots of boot blacking, for six shillings a week, ten hours a day.

[long-pause]

He never forgot those months.

[pause]

He felt that his childhood had been taken from him, and that he had been abandoned, and he was deeply ashamed, all his life, of what had happened. He told almost nobody about it, not even his wife. But it made him understand, as few writers of his time did, what it was like to be a poor child in London: hungry, lonely, frightened, working long hours in a dark, dirty place, with no one to care for you.

[pause]

{img:boy-working-blacking-factory-window-thames-light}
After a few months, his father inherited a little money, and was released from prison, and Charles was sent back to school. Then, at fifteen, he went to work as a clerk in a lawyer's office, and taught himself shorthand, and became a reporter, writing down the speeches in Parliament, and then a journalist, and then, at twenty-four, a writer.

[long-pause]

His first great success was The Pickwick Papers, published in monthly parts in 1836 and 1837.

[pause]

It was a funny, warm-hearted book about a kindly old gentleman, Mr. Pickwick, and his friends, and his servant, Sam Weller, travelling around England, having adventures. At first, it did not sell very well. But then, when Sam Weller appeared, it became a sensation. Everyone in Britain was reading it, from the Queen to the butcher's boy.

[pause]

{img:people-reading-monthly-parts-bookshop-crowd-1830s}
Then came Oliver Twist, and Nicholas Nickleby, and The Old Curiosity Shop, and Barnaby Rudge. Each one was more popular than the last. When the little girl in The Old Curiosity Shop, Little Nell, died, people all over Britain and America wept.

[long-pause]

But in 1843, things are not going so well.

[pause]

His newest novel, Martin Chuzzlewit, which is coming out in monthly parts, is not selling as well as his earlier books. His publishers are worried, and have told him that they might have to reduce his payments. He has a big house, and a large family, and many expenses, and his father and his brothers are always asking him for money.

[pause]

Dickens is worried too. He needs money. And he wants to write something that will touch people's hearts again.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, after dinner, the family gathers in the drawing room.

[pause]

Dickens plays with the children, and tells them stories, and makes them laugh, with funny voices and faces. He loves to do magic tricks for them. He reads aloud to Catherine and her sister, Georgina, who lives with them. Friends come to dinner, and stay late, talking and laughing.

[pause]

{img:victorian-drawing-room-father-magic-trick-children}
And then, when the house is quiet, and everyone has gone to bed, he often goes out alone, into the dark streets, and walks, and thinks.

[long-pause]

{img:ragged-school-dim-room-children-teacher-candles}
In September 1843, Charles Dickens visited a school in one of the poorest parts of London.

[pause]

It was called the Field Lane Ragged School, near Saffron Hill, not far from Holborn. It was a school for the poorest children in the city, children who were too poor, and too ragged, to go to any other school: children who lived in the streets, and slept in doorways and under arches, and earned a few pennies by sweeping crossings or selling matches, or did not earn anything at all.

[long-pause]

The school was held in the evenings, in a couple of dark, crowded rooms, lit by candles.

[pause]

A few kind, patient volunteers, mostly young men and women from churches, came to teach the children to read and write, and to tell them about the Bible, and to give them a little warmth and kindness. The children came in from the streets, cold and hungry, in rags, many of them without shoes.

[pause]

Dickens was deeply moved by what he saw there. He wrote to a friend that he had seldom seen, in all the strange and dreadful things he had seen in London, anything so sad as the faces of those children. And he gave money to help the school.

[long-pause]

At about the same time, he read a report by a government commission on the employment of children in mines and factories.

[pause]

It described children of five and six years old working in coal mines, deep underground, in the dark, for twelve hours a day, and children working in factories and workshops for long hours, for very little pay. Dickens was angry. He decided that he would write something to make people care about these children.

[pause]

{img:writer-reading-government-report-study-lamp}
At first, he planned to write a pamphlet, a little political essay, called An Appeal to the People of England on behalf of the Poor Man's Child. But then he had a better idea.

[long-pause]

At the beginning of October, he travelled to Manchester, in the north of England, to make a speech.

[pause]

Manchester was the great factory city of England, the city of cotton mills, with tall chimneys pouring black smoke into the sky, and thousands of people, men and women and children, working long hours at the looms and the spinning machines. Dickens spoke at a great hall, called the Athenaeum, which had been built to give working people a place to read and learn in the evenings.

[pause]

He spoke about the importance of education for the poor, and about the children he had seen in London, and about how ignorance and want were the enemies of everyone, rich and poor alike.

[long-pause]

And it seems that it was there, in Manchester, perhaps walking through the dark streets after the speech, that the idea came to him.

[pause]

He would not write a pamphlet. He would write a story. A Christmas story. A story about a hard, cold, mean old man, who cared only about money, and who learned, through one long Christmas night, to care about other people. A story that would make people laugh and cry, and that would remind them, at Christmas, of the poor and the lonely, and of the joy of kindness.

[pause]

{img:walking-alone-dark-manchester-street-thinking}
It would be short, so that it could be finished in time for Christmas, and cheap enough for people to buy as a gift. He would call it A Christmas Carol, in Prose, Being a Ghost Story of Christmas.

[long-pause]

He came home to London, and began to write.

[pause]

He wrote very fast, in about six weeks, from the middle of October to the end of November, while he was also writing the monthly parts of Martin Chuzzlewit. He wrote in his study at Devonshire Terrace, at a desk by the window, with a quill pen, in his small, quick, slanting handwriting, crossing out and rewriting as he went.

[pause]

{img:study-desk-manuscript-crossings-out-window-autumn}
He was completely caught up in the story. He wrote later that he wept over it, and laughed, and wept again, and that he walked about the black streets of London, fifteen and twenty miles, many a night, when all sober people had gone to bed, thinking about it.

[long-pause]

On those night walks, he passed through every part of the city.

[pause]

He walked through the quiet squares of the rich, where the gas lamps glowed on the white houses. He walked through the City, where the counting houses and the banks stood dark and empty, and the old churches loomed over the narrow lanes. He walked along the river, past the dark wharves, and over the bridges. He walked through the poor districts, where the lights burned late in the little rooms, and people huddled around tiny fires.

[pause]

He looked in at the windows, and saw families eating their suppers, and children playing, and old people sitting alone. He saw clerks working late in cold offices, by a single candle. He saw it all, and it all went into the story.

[long-pause]

At the beginning of December, the story was finished.

[pause]

Dickens wrote to a friend that he had never been so moved by anything he had written. And he wanted the book to be beautiful: bound in red cloth, with gold lettering on the cover, gold edges to the pages, and coloured pictures, drawn by his friend John Leech, a young artist who drew for the comic magazine Punch.

[pause]

{img:red-cloth-book-gold-lettering-illustrations-table}
It would be expensive to make. But Dickens did not care. He wanted it to be a Christmas present that people would treasure.

[long-pause]

{img:narrow-court-poor-houses-washing-lines-children}
Not far from the grand streets of London, there are the poor districts.

[pause]

They are called rookeries, because the people are crowded together in them like rooks in a tree. There is one at Saint Giles, near Covent Garden. There are others in Southwark, across the river, and in the East End, near the docks, and at Saffron Hill and Field Lane, where the ragged school is.

[long-pause]

In the rookeries, the old houses are packed together along narrow, winding alleys and courts, so close that the sunlight hardly reaches the ground.

[pause]

Many of the houses are very old, and falling down. Whole families live in a single room, sometimes two or three families together, sharing one fireplace, and one bed, or a pile of straw on the floor. Water comes from a single pump in the court, shared by dozens of families. In winter, the rooms are cold and damp, and the wind blows through the broken windows, which are stuffed with rags and paper.

[pause]

It is a hard life. But even here, people help each other, and share what little they have, and laugh, and sing, and look after their children as best they can.

[long-pause]

The poor of London work at whatever they can find.

[pause]

The men are labourers and porters, dockworkers and carters, bricklayers and street sweepers. The women take in washing, or sew shirts for a few pennies each, working late into the night by the light of a single candle. Many people sell things in the streets: fruit and vegetables, flowers and matches, shoelaces and ribbons, hot food and drink.

[pause]

{img:flower-seller-girl-covent-garden-basket-winter}
Children work too, from a very young age. They sweep crossings and run errands, hold horses for gentlemen, sell matches and newspapers, and help their parents. Some of them have no parents at all, and live in the streets, and sleep under the arches of the bridges, or in doorways.

[long-pause]

For those who cannot support themselves at all, there is the workhouse.

[pause]

The workhouse is a large, plain, forbidding building, where the poorest people can go to get food and shelter, in return for hard work, like breaking stones or picking apart old ropes. But the rules are strict, and families are separated: husbands from wives, parents from children. People are very afraid of the workhouse, and will do almost anything to avoid going there.

[pause]

Dickens hates the workhouses. He wrote about one in Oliver Twist, where the hungry boy Oliver dares to ask for more food: Please, sir, I want some more.

[long-pause]

In the winter, life in the poor districts is hardest of all.

[pause]

Coal is expensive, and many families cannot afford enough to keep their rooms warm. Food is expensive too. There is often not enough to eat. Many people fall ill, in the cold and the damp, with coughs and fevers.

[pause]

{img:charity-soup-kitchen-queue-winter-1843-snow}
But there is help, too. There are churches and charities that give out soup and bread and blankets and coal to the poor in winter. There are kind people, rich and poor, who give what they can. And at Christmas, especially, there are many acts of kindness.

[long-pause]

At Christmas, even the poorest families try to have something special.

[pause]

Many of them belong to goose clubs. They pay a few pennies every week into a club at a local pub or shop, all through the year, and at Christmas, they get a goose, or a joint of meat, and a bottle of gin or a few oranges.

[pause]

{img:family-carrying-goose-home-christmas-eve-street}
Most poor families do not have an oven at home. So, on Christmas morning, they take their goose, or their joint of meat, and their potatoes, in a dish, to the local baker's shop, where the baker cooks them in his big oven for a penny or two, while the families go to church. Then, after church, they collect their dinners, steaming hot, and carry them home through the streets.

[long-pause]

Dickens knows all of this, from his own childhood.

[pause]

He remembers the years in Camden Town, when his family was poor, and the months in the blacking factory, and the visits to his father in the Marshalsea prison. He remembers walking through the streets of London as a hungry boy, looking in at the windows of the cookshops, at the puddings and the pies, with no money to buy them.

[pause]

And in A Christmas Carol, he will write about a poor family, the family of a clerk named Bob Cratchit, who lives in a small house in Camden Town, just like the house that Dickens lived in as a boy, and about their Christmas dinner, and about their little son, Tiny Tim.

[long-pause]

On winter evenings, in the poor districts, the windows glow with the light of little fires and candles.

[pause]

{img:poor-street-windows-glowing-candles-fog-evening}
Inside, families are sitting together, around the fire, as close as they can get, telling stories, singing songs, mending clothes, and talking about Christmas: about the goose, if they have one, and the pudding, and the oranges, and the little presents for the children. And for a while, in the warmth of the fire, they forget the cold, and the fog, and the hard times, and are happy.

[long-pause]

{img:costermonger-donkey-cart-vegetables-street-morning}
On every street in London, there are people selling things.

[pause]

They are called costermongers, and street sellers, and hawkers, and there are tens of thousands of them. A few years later, a journalist named Henry Mayhew walked all over London, talking to them, and writing down their stories, in a great book called London Labour and the London Poor. And because of him, we know a great deal about their lives.

[long-pause]

The costermongers sell fruit and vegetables and fish from barrows and donkey carts.

[pause]

They get up at four in the morning, and go to Covent Garden or Billingsgate to buy their goods, and then push their barrows through the streets all day, calling out their wares, in a special sing-song cry that everyone in the neighbourhood knows. They are proud and independent people, who look after each other, and have their own customs, and their own slang, and love their donkeys, and dress up in bright neckerchiefs and velvet waistcoats on Sundays.

[pause]

In winter, there are the sellers of hot food, which is very welcome in the cold. There is the hot chestnut man, with his little iron brazier full of glowing coals, and the chestnuts roasting on top, which he sells in twists of paper for a penny. There is the baked potato man, with his shiny tin oven on a barrow, with a little chimney, and steam coming out, who sells hot potatoes with butter and salt. There are sellers of hot pies, and muffins and crumpets, and hot pea soup, and hot eels.

[long-pause]

There are girls who sell watercress, getting up before dawn to buy it at the market, and then walking the streets with their baskets, crying: Fresh watercress! Four bunches a penny!

[pause]

Mayhew talked to one of them, a little girl of eight years old, who had been selling watercress since she was a very small child. She told him, quite seriously, about her work, and the prices, and the cold in the winter mornings, which hurt her fingers. She had never played in a park, or had a doll. But she said she liked going to the market in the morning, and she was proud of being able to help her mother.

[pause]

{img:little-watercress-girl-basket-dawn-cold-street}
There are sellers of matches and bootlaces and needles and pins, and old women selling oranges and apples from little trays, and men selling newspapers and song sheets, ballads printed on long strips of paper, for a halfpenny, which they sing in the street to attract customers.

[long-pause]

There are street performers, too.

[pause]

There are organ grinders, with little barrel organs on wheels, and sometimes a monkey in a red coat, collecting pennies in a little cap. There are German bands, small groups of musicians with brass instruments, who play on the street corners. There are Punch and Judy shows, in little striped booths, where the puppets squeak and fight and make the children shriek with laughter.

[pause]

{img:punch-and-judy-booth-children-crowd-street}
There are acrobats and jugglers, and men with dancing dogs, and fire eaters, and stilt walkers. There are singers and fiddlers, who play outside the pubs in the evening. There are people who draw pictures on the pavement in coloured chalk, and wait for passers-by to throw them a coin.

[long-pause]

At Christmas, the street sellers do their best business of the year.

[pause]

They sell holly and mistletoe, and little Christmas trees, and oranges and nuts and chestnuts, and toys: little tin toys, and wooden toys, and paper toys, cheap and colourful, which they make themselves, or buy from the toy makers in the East End. There are sellers of Christmas cards, and almanacs for the new year, and song sheets with Christmas carols.

[pause]

The streets ring with their cries, in the cold, dark December evenings, under the gas lamps: Holly, holly, a penny a bunch! Mistletoe, fine mistletoe! Christmas oranges, sweet and juicy! Toys for the little ones, toys for Christmas!

[long-pause]

And at night, when the shops have closed, the street sellers push their barrows home, through the dark streets, to their homes in the poor districts, tired and cold, with a few shillings in their pockets.

[pause]

{img:costermonger-pushing-barrow-home-night-gaslight}
They stop at the pub on the corner for a pint of beer and a warm by the fire, and talk with their friends about the day's business. And then they go home to their families, and their little rooms, and their beds, to sleep a few hours, before getting up again in the dark, to go back to the market, and begin again.

[long-pause]

{img:small-terraced-house-camden-town-winter-evening}
Let us visit a family that we might call the Browns, who live in a small house in Camden Town, in the north of London.

[pause]

Mr. Brown is a clerk. He works in an office in the City, for a merchant, copying letters and keeping accounts, at a tall desk, with a quill pen, from nine in the morning until seven at night, six days a week. He earns about fifteen shillings a week, which is not very much, but it is a steady wage, and he is grateful for it.

[long-pause]

The Browns live in a narrow, two-storey brick house, in a long row of houses that all look exactly the same.

[pause]

On the ground floor, there is a front parlour, which is kept for best, and only used on Sundays and special occasions, and a back room, which is the kitchen, where the family spends most of its time. Upstairs, there are two small bedrooms. In the yard at the back, there is a privy, and a coal shed, and a little patch of garden, where Mrs. Brown grows a few herbs and some flowers.

[pause]

The kitchen is the warmest room in the house, because of the fire. There is an iron range, set into the fireplace, with a coal fire burning in the middle, a little oven on one side, and a boiler for hot water on the other. A kettle sings on the hob. A clock ticks on the mantelpiece.

[long-pause]

Mrs. Brown runs the household.

[pause]

She gets up before dawn, to clean out the ashes and light the fire, and boil the water, and make the breakfast: bread and butter, and tea, and perhaps a little porridge for the children. Then she sends Mr. Brown off to work, with a bundle of bread and cheese for his dinner, and sends the older children off to school.

[pause]

{img:mother-blacking-range-kitchen-morning-candle}
Then she begins her day's work: cleaning the house, scrubbing the floors, blacking the range, washing the clothes, mending, sewing, shopping, cooking. It never stops.

[long-pause]

They have five children.

[pause]

The eldest, a girl of fourteen, has just started work as an apprentice to a dressmaker, and comes home at night tired, with sore fingers. The eldest boy, who is twelve, is a messenger boy in an office in the City, and runs through the streets all day with letters and parcels. The three youngest, who are eight and six and three, are at home with their mother, or at the little school at the end of the street, run by an old lady, where they learn their letters and their sums.

[pause]

{img:children-slates-dame-school-small-room}
The Browns are not poor, like the families in the rookeries. But they must be careful with every penny. They eat bread and potatoes and porridge, with a little meat on Sundays. The children wear clothes that have been handed down, and mended many times. In the winter, they can only afford to keep one fire going, in the kitchen, and the bedrooms are very cold.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, the family gathers in the kitchen, around the fire.

[pause]

Mr. Brown comes home in the dark, cold and tired, and takes off his wet boots, and sits by the fire in his chair, and the little ones climb onto his lap. They have supper together: bread and dripping, perhaps a herring, a cup of tea. Then the mother sews, and the father reads aloud from the newspaper, or from a book borrowed from a friend, and the children play on the rag rug in front of the fire.

[pause]

{img:father-reading-aloud-kitchen-fire-children-rug}
Sometimes, they sing together, the old songs and hymns. Sometimes, the father tells them stories about when he was a boy. And then the children are sent up to bed, with a candle, and a hot brick wrapped in flannel to warm their beds.

[long-pause]

In December, the children are excited about Christmas.

[pause]

They have been saving their pennies for weeks, to buy little presents for each other and for their parents: a ribbon, a handkerchief, a twist of sweets, a little tin toy. Mrs. Brown has been putting a few pennies a week into the goose club at the shop on the corner. And this year, Mr. Brown has been promised a little extra money at Christmas, from his employer, who is a kind man.

[pause]

In the evenings, the children sit by the fire, and talk about what they will have for Christmas dinner, and whether it will snow.

[long-pause]

On Saturday evenings, the whole family goes out together, to do the shopping for the week.

[pause]

The shops stay open late on Saturdays, until ten or eleven at night, and the streets are crowded with families, doing their shopping, by the light of the gas lamps and the flaring naphtha lamps of the street stalls. There is music, and laughter, and the cries of the sellers. It is a treat for the children, to be out so late, in the bright, busy streets.

[pause]

{img:saturday-night-market-naphtha-flares-families-shopping}
And in December, the shop windows are full of Christmas things, and the children press their noses against the glass, and look at the toys and the sweets and the puddings and the geese, and dream.

[long-pause]

{img:clerks-walking-london-bridge-morning-fog-top-hats}
Every morning, as the church clocks strike eight, an enormous crowd of men walks into the City of London.

[pause]

They come across London Bridge from the south, in a great black stream of coats and hats, thousands and thousands of them. They come down from Islington and Camden Town in the north, and along the Strand from the west. They walk, mostly, because the omnibus costs too much. They are the clerks.

[long-pause]

The City of London is the old heart of London, the square mile around Saint Paul's and the Bank of England, where the merchants and the bankers and the insurance companies and the shipping companies have their offices.

[pause]

In 1843, almost all the work of the City is done by hand, with a quill pen and ink, by clerks. They copy letters, and keep accounts, and write out bills and receipts and contracts, all day long, in neat, careful handwriting, in great leather-bound books.

[pause]

There are tens of thousands of clerks in the City. Some of them are young men, starting out, hoping one day to become merchants themselves. Some of them are old men, who have worked at the same desk for forty years. Most of them earn very little.

[long-pause]

A clerk's office, or counting house, is usually a dim, narrow room, on the ground floor or the first floor of an old building, in a narrow lane.

[pause]

The clerks sit on high stools, at tall, sloping desks, along the walls, near the windows, to catch the light. There is a small fire in the grate, and the master of the office sits in a separate room, with a bigger fire. In winter, the offices are cold and dark, and the clerks work by the light of candles, from early afternoon, wearing their coats and their scarves, and blowing on their fingers to keep them warm.

[pause]

{img:clerk-copying-letter-quill-candle-cold-office}
The work is quiet and repetitive and dull. The only sounds are the scratching of the pens, and the ticking of the clock, and the shuffling of papers, and now and then, a cough.

[long-pause]

At midday, the clerks go out for their dinner.

[pause]

The richer ones go to the chop houses, where they can sit at a wooden table in a cosy, wood-panelled room, and eat a mutton chop or a beefsteak, cooked over the fire, with bread and potatoes and a pint of porter. The poorer ones buy a pie or a baked potato from a street seller, or bring bread and cheese from home, wrapped in a handkerchief, and eat it at their desks, or standing in a doorway.

[pause]

{img:city-chop-house-wood-panelled-booths-clerks-lunch}
In the old taverns and coffee houses of the City, the merchants and the brokers meet, to buy and sell and make deals, and read the newspapers, and talk about the price of tea and cotton and the arrival of the ships.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, the work goes on, until six or seven o'clock in the evening, or even later, if there is a lot to do.

[pause]

Then the clerks put on their hats and their coats, and go out into the dark, foggy streets, and walk home, in another great stream, back across London Bridge, and up toward Islington and Camden Town, through the gaslit streets, to their families and their suppers.

[pause]

Bob Cratchit, in A Christmas Carol, is one of these clerks. He works for Scrooge in a cold office, for fifteen shillings a week, and walks home every evening to Camden Town, through the streets, to his wife and his children. Dickens knew many clerks like him. He had been one himself, as a young man, in a lawyer's office.

[long-pause]

At Christmas, a kind employer would give his clerks a little present: a goose, or a turkey, or a little extra money, and the day off on Christmas Day.

[pause]

But not every employer was kind. In 1843, Christmas Day was a holiday for most people, but Boxing Day was not yet a holiday by law, and many clerks had to go back to work on the twenty-sixth of December.

[pause]

{img:kind-merchant-giving-goose-to-clerk-office-door}
In some offices, on Christmas Eve, the master would bring in a bowl of punch, and some mince pies, and the clerks would drink a glass together, and wish each other a merry Christmas, before going home, early, for once, through the foggy streets, to their families, and their Christmas.

[long-pause]

{img:covent-garden-market-dawn-carts-vegetables-1843}
In the weeks before Christmas, the great markets of London are busier than at any other time of the year.

[pause]

The most famous of them all is Covent Garden, in the middle of the city, where the fruit and the vegetables and the flowers are sold. Long before dawn, the carts and wagons come rumbling in from the market gardens around London, from Chelsea and Fulham and Battersea, and from the countryside beyond, piled high with cabbages and carrots, turnips and potatoes, onions and celery, apples and pears.

[long-pause]

The market is in a great open square, with a handsome new market building in the middle, with colonnades and glass roofs, built about fifteen years ago.

[pause]

By five o'clock in the morning, the square is crowded and noisy and full of life. Porters carry great baskets on their heads, piled six or seven high, balancing them perfectly as they walk through the crowds. Costermongers, the street sellers of London, come with their donkey carts and their barrows to buy fruit and vegetables, which they will sell later in the day, in the streets, all over the city.

[pause]

In December, there are piles of holly and ivy and mistletoe, brought in from the woods and the hedges of the countryside, in great green bundles, with bright red berries. There are oranges and lemons, from Spain and Portugal, in wooden crates, and figs and dates and raisins, from the Mediterranean, and nuts of every kind: chestnuts and walnuts and hazelnuts and almonds.

[long-pause]

The flower sellers sit along the edges of the market, mostly women and girls, with baskets of flowers, even in winter: violets and snowdrops and hothouse roses, and little bunches of holly and mistletoe, which they will sell in the streets, for a penny a bunch.

[pause]

{img:flower-girls-violets-holly-bunches-market-edge}
There are coffee stalls, too, where the porters and the market people can buy a mug of hot coffee and a slice of bread and butter for a penny, in the cold early morning, and stand around the stall, warming their hands, and talking.

[long-pause]

In the east of the City, there is Leadenhall Market, where the poultry and the game are sold.

[pause]

In December, Leadenhall is a wonderful sight. The shops and stalls are hung with hundreds and hundreds of birds: geese and turkeys, ducks and chickens, pheasants and partridges, hares and rabbits, hanging in long rows from hooks, from the ceiling to the floor. Some of the geese and turkeys have been walked all the way to London from Norfolk and Suffolk, in great flocks, over many weeks, with their feet dipped in tar to protect them on the roads.

[pause]

The poulterers stand in front of their shops, in their white aprons and straw hats, calling out to the passers-by, and weighing the birds, and wrapping them up.

[long-pause]

Not far away, there is Smithfield, the great meat market, where cattle and sheep and pigs are brought in from all over the country, and sold.

[pause]

In the week before Christmas, there is a great Christmas show at Smithfield, where the farmers bring their finest, fattest animals, and the butchers buy them, and decorate them with ribbons and rosettes, and hang the meat in their shop windows for everyone to admire.

[pause]

{img:butcher-shop-window-ribbons-rosettes-christmas-meat}
On the last Saturday before Christmas, the butchers' shops all over London are decorated with holly and evergreens and paper flowers, and lit with gas, and the butchers stand at the doors, calling out: Buy, buy, buy!

[long-pause]

Then there is Billingsgate, by the river, where the fish is sold.

[pause]

Billingsgate is famous for the rough language of its fish sellers, which is so colourful that people say a person who swears a lot talks Billingsgate. In December, there are oysters, in great heaps, which are cheap and popular, and eaten by everyone, rich and poor, and salmon and cod and herring, and eels, which are stewed or jellied or baked in pies.

[pause]

{img:billingsgate-fish-market-oysters-river-morning}
In the streets near the market, there are oyster stalls, where people stand and eat a dozen oysters with bread and butter and a glass of porter, for a few pennies.

[long-pause]

In the poorer parts of London, there are street markets, which are busiest on Saturday nights.

[pause]

The stalls stretch along the pavements, lit by flaring naphtha lamps, which burn with a bright, smoky, flickering flame. They sell everything: meat and fish, vegetables and fruit, bread and cheese, crockery and saucepans, old clothes and boots, toys and sweets.

[pause]

The crowds push and jostle along the pavements, bargaining and laughing. Children run between the stalls. Organ grinders play. Sellers shout: Here's your fine Christmas oranges, a penny apiece! Here's your holly, a penny a bunch!

[long-pause]

All this food, flowing into London from all over the country and all over the world, is one of the wonders of the city.

[pause]

Every day, the markets feed nearly two million people. And at Christmas, they feed them better than at any other time of the year.

[pause]

{img:carts-leaving-market-dawn-fog-loaded-holly}
When Dickens describes Christmas in A Christmas Carol, he describes the shops and the markets of London, piled high with good things to eat: the great round, pot-bellied baskets of chestnuts, the ruddy, brown-faced, broad-girthed Spanish onions, the pears and apples clustered high in blooming pyramids, the bunches of grapes, the piles of filberts, the Norfolk biffins, squat and swarthy, the gold and silver fish, the raisins and almonds and cinnamon, and the candied fruits, so caked and spotted with molten sugar as to make the coldest lookers-on feel faint.

[long-pause]

{img:first-christmas-card-family-toast-illustration-desk}
In December 1843, in London, something new appeared in the shops: the first Christmas card.

[pause]

It was the idea of a busy, energetic civil servant named Henry Cole, who worked hard to improve all kinds of things, from the post office to museums and schools. Every Christmas, he had a great many friends and acquaintances to write to, and not enough time to write to them all. So he had an idea.

[long-pause]

He asked an artist friend, John Callcott Horsley, to design a card, which could be printed, and sent to everyone, with a simple message.

[pause]

The card that Horsley designed showed a happy family, around a table, raising their glasses of wine in a toast, with the words A Merry Christmas and A Happy New Year to You. On either side, there were little pictures of people doing good deeds: giving food and clothes to the poor.

[pause]

About a thousand copies were printed, and coloured by hand, and sold for a shilling each, which was rather expensive. Some people disapproved of the picture, because even the little children in the family were drinking wine. But the idea was a good one. Over the next few years, more and more people began to send Christmas cards, and soon, it became one of the great customs of Christmas.

[long-pause]

Sending cards had become easier, because of another new idea.

[pause]

Just three years earlier, in 1840, the Penny Post had been introduced. Before that, letters were expensive to send, and the person who received the letter had to pay. Now, anyone could send a letter anywhere in Britain for just one penny, paid in advance, with a little sticky stamp, with the head of young Queen Victoria on it, called the Penny Black.

[pause]

{img:postman-red-coat-delivering-letters-snowy-door}
The postmen, in their red coats, now delivered letters all over London several times a day. And at Christmas, they were busier than ever.

[long-pause]

In 1843, Christmas in England was beginning to change.

[pause]

For a long time, it had been a rather quiet feast, in many places. The old Christmas customs of the countryside, the twelve days of feasting, the Yule log, the mummers and the wassailers, had faded away in many towns, as people moved into the cities to work in the factories, and had only one day's holiday, or none at all.

[pause]

But now, Christmas was coming back. And one of the people who helped to bring it back was the young Queen Victoria, and her German husband, Prince Albert.

[long-pause]

In Germany, families had decorated Christmas trees for many years: little fir trees, brought into the house, and decorated with candles and sweets and little gifts.

[pause]

Prince Albert had grown up with Christmas trees, in his childhood home in Coburg, and he loved them. So, every Christmas, at Windsor Castle, he set up Christmas trees for the royal family, decorated with candles and sweets and gingerbread and little presents, for the Queen and their young children.

[pause]

{img:royal-children-candlelit-tree-windsor-drawing-room}
In a few years, in 1848, a picture of the royal family gathered around their Christmas tree at Windsor would be printed in a newspaper, and seen by people all over the country. And soon, families everywhere would want a Christmas tree of their own.

[long-pause]

In 1843, the Christmas tree was still quite rare in English homes.

[pause]

Instead, people decorated their houses with greenery: holly and ivy and evergreen branches, along the mantelpieces and over the pictures and the doors. And everyone hung up a bunch of mistletoe, or a kissing bough, a round ball of evergreens and ribbons and apples and candles, hanging from the ceiling, under which people were allowed to kiss.

[pause]

{img:kissing-bough-mistletoe-hanging-parlour-candles}
There were Christmas carols, too. In the weeks before Christmas, groups of singers, called waits, went from house to house in the evenings, carrying lanterns, singing the old carols at the doors, and were given money, or a glass of something warm, or a mince pie.

[long-pause]

There were Christmas games, played in the parlour, by the fire, after dinner.

[pause]

There was blind man's buff, where one person was blindfolded and tried to catch the others. There was hunt the slipper, and forfeits, and charades, and a game called snapdragon, where raisins were put in a bowl of brandy, and the brandy was set alight, and everyone tried to snatch the raisins out of the blue flames, and pop them in their mouths, without burning their fingers.

[pause]

And there were stories, especially ghost stories, told by the fire on Christmas Eve, in the darkness, with only the light of the flames. It was an old custom, to tell ghost stories at Christmas. And that is why Dickens called his little book a Ghost Story of Christmas.

[long-pause]

After Christmas, there was Boxing Day, on the twenty-sixth of December, when people gave Christmas boxes, little gifts of money, to the tradesmen and the servants and the postmen who had served them during the year.

[pause]

And on Twelfth Night, on the fifth of January, there was a Twelfth Night party, with a great Twelfth cake, iced and decorated with sugar figures, and games, and dancing, and characters drawn from a hat, so that everyone pretended to be someone else for the evening.

[pause]

{img:twelfth-cake-sugar-figures-confectioner-window}
In the windows of the confectioners' shops, in the first days of January, there were great Twelfth cakes on display, decorated with sugar castles and crowns and figures, and the children of London pressed their noses against the glass to look at them.

[long-pause]

{img:family-stirring-pudding-bowl-kitchen-stir-up-sunday}
In many English homes, the preparations for Christmas begin on a Sunday at the end of November, five weeks before Christmas.

[pause]

It is called Stir-up Sunday, because the prayer that is read in church on that day begins with the words: Stir up, we beseech thee, O Lord, the wills of thy faithful people. And the families take it as a reminder that it is time to stir up the Christmas pudding.

[long-pause]

The Christmas pudding takes a long time to make, and must be made well in advance, so that it can mature.

[pause]

The ingredients are laid out on the kitchen table: suet, which is the hard fat from around the kidneys of a cow, chopped very fine; breadcrumbs and flour; dark sugar and treacle; eggs; raisins and currants and sultanas; candied peel, of orange and lemon; chopped almonds; grated nutmeg and cinnamon and cloves and allspice; a little brandy, or beer, or both.

[pause]

Everything is mixed together in a great earthenware bowl, into a thick, dark, sticky mixture, which smells wonderful.

[long-pause]

Then, every member of the family takes a turn to stir it.

[pause]

Each person must stir it three times, from east to west, in honour of the three wise men, who came from the East, and while they stir, they close their eyes, and make a wish. The father stirs, and the mother, and the grandmother, and all the children, even the baby, with someone holding its little hand around the wooden spoon.

[pause]

{img:small-child-stirring-pudding-mother-helping-wish}
In some families, little charms are stirred into the mixture: a silver threepenny bit, for wealth; a ring, for a wedding; a thimble, for a happy single life; a button, for a bachelor. Whoever finds them in their slice of pudding on Christmas Day will have that fortune in the coming year.

[long-pause]

Then the mixture is wrapped in a floured pudding cloth, tied tightly at the top with string, so that it makes a round ball, and boiled in a great copper pot of water, for many hours, six or eight or ten.

[pause]

The whole house fills with the warm, rich, spicy smell of the pudding. Then it is taken out, and hung up in the cool larder, still in its cloth, until Christmas Day, when it will be boiled again, for a few more hours, and turned out onto a plate, dark and shining and round, and carried to the table, with brandy poured over it, and set alight, so that it blazes with a blue flame.

[pause]

Mrs. Cratchit, in A Christmas Carol, boils her pudding in the copper, the great boiler that is used for washing the clothes. That is why, Dickens says, there is a smell like a washing day, on Christmas Day at the Cratchits'.

[long-pause]

There are mince pies to be made, too.

[pause]

The mincemeat is made weeks in advance, from chopped apples, and raisins and currants, and suet, and candied peel, and spices, and sugar, and brandy, and in some families, a little minced meat as well, as in the old days. It is packed into jars, and left to mature.

[pause]

{img:mince-pies-baking-tin-tray-oven-warm-kitchen}
Then, in the days before Christmas, the pastry is made, and rolled out, and cut into little rounds, and the mince pies are filled and baked, by the dozen, and stored in tins, to be eaten all through the Christmas season. There is an old saying that you should eat a mince pie on each of the twelve days of Christmas, in a different house each time, for twelve happy months in the coming year.

[long-pause]

And there is the Christmas cake, rich and dark and full of fruit, and the gingerbread, and the sweets, and the punch, a great bowl of hot wine or rum, with sugar and lemons and spices, which is served to visitors.

[pause]

In the poorer homes, the Christmas food is much simpler. But even there, the mothers try to make a little pudding, however small, for the children, and a few mince pies, and to have an orange for each child on Christmas morning.

[long-pause]

By Christmas Eve, the larder is full.

[pause]

The pudding hangs in its cloth from a hook in the ceiling. The mince pies are in their tins. The goose or the turkey is waiting in the coldest corner, or at the poulterer's, to be collected. The vegetables and the fruit are in their baskets. The holly is over the pictures, and the mistletoe is hanging in the hall.

[pause]

{img:larder-pudding-hanging-cloth-goose-holly-evening}
And the children lie in their beds, on Christmas Eve, smelling the warm, spicy smell of the kitchen drifting up the stairs, and listening to the bells ringing across the city, and trying very hard to go to sleep.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-bookshop-strand-red-books-window-gaslight}
On the nineteenth of December, 1843, A Christmas Carol was published, by the firm of Chapman and Hall, in the Strand.

[pause]

It was a small book, of about a hundred and sixty pages, bound in red cloth, with gold lettering on the cover and the spine, and gold edges to the pages. Inside, there were eight pictures by John Leech: four of them small black and white engravings, and four of them full-page etchings, coloured by hand, in bright, cheerful colours.

[long-pause]

It cost five shillings.

[pause]

That was quite a lot of money for a little book, about as much as a labourer earned in two days. Dickens had wanted it to be cheaper, so that more people could buy it. But the red cloth and the gold edges and the coloured pictures were expensive, and he would not do without them.

[pause]

And so the book went on sale, in the bookshops of London, a week before Christmas, and Dickens waited anxiously, to see what would happen.

[long-pause]

What happened was wonderful.

[pause]

By Christmas Eve, five days later, the first printing of six thousand copies had been sold out. People bought it as a Christmas present, for their families and their friends. People read it aloud, by the fire, on Christmas Eve, to their families, and laughed and cried. The bookshops ordered more, and more, and the printers worked day and night to print them.

[pause]

{img:family-reading-red-book-aloud-fireside-christmas-eve}
Within a few months, it had been reprinted many times, and was being read all over Britain, and soon, all over America too.

[long-pause]

The critics loved it.

[pause]

The writer William Makepeace Thackeray, who was not always kind about Dickens, wrote that it seemed to him a national benefit, and to every man or woman who reads it, a personal kindness. A Scottish writer wrote that it had made people kinder, and that people who read it went out and gave money to the poor, and were good to their servants and their workers.

[pause]

There was even a story, told many years later, of an American factory owner who read A Christmas Carol on Christmas Eve, and was so moved by it that he closed his factory on Christmas Day, and gave every worker a turkey.

[long-pause]

Dickens himself was delighted.

[pause]

He wrote to a friend that the book was a most prodigious success, the greatest, he thought, that he had ever achieved. He received letters from strangers, all over the country, telling him how much the book had meant to them, and how it had made them think about the poor, and about kindness, and about Christmas.

[pause]

{img:writer-reading-letters-study-delighted-fireside}
But there was a disappointment, too. Because the book had been so expensive to make, with its red cloth and gold edges and coloured pictures, Dickens made very little money from it, at first. He had hoped for a thousand pounds. He got about two hundred and thirty. He was furious with his publishers, and soon left them.

[long-pause]

And there was another trouble. A few weeks after the book came out, a cheap magazine published a pirated copy of the story, without permission, rewritten a little, and sold it for twopence.

[pause]

Dickens went to court to stop them. He won the case. But the pirates went bankrupt, and he never got the money that they owed him, and had to pay the costs of the court case himself. It cost him more than he had earned from the book.

[pause]

Even so, A Christmas Carol was never out of print again. It has been printed in hundreds of editions, in dozens of languages, and read by hundreds of millions of people, all over the world. It has been turned into plays, and films, and radio programmes, and musicals, and cartoons, again and again.

[long-pause]

So what is the story that Charles Dickens told, in the winter of 1843?

[pause]

{img:open-old-book-candlelight-armchair-fireside-night}
Let us settle down by the fire, as the families of London did on that Christmas Eve, and hear it once again, gently, in a few short chapters. Dickens called his chapters staves, like the verses of a song, because the book was a carol.

[long-pause]

{img:cold-counting-house-clerk-tiny-fire-candle-1843}
The story begins on Christmas Eve, in a cold, dark counting house, in the City of London.

[pause]

It is a bitter, foggy afternoon. Outside, the fog is so thick that the houses opposite are mere phantoms, and the candles in the windows of the offices glow like ruddy smears in the gloom. Inside, the office is cold, and dim, and silent.

[long-pause]

At a desk, sits an old man named Ebenezer Scrooge.

[pause]

He is a merchant, and a moneylender, and he is very rich. And he is also, Dickens tells us, a squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous old sinner. Hard and sharp as flint. Solitary as an oyster. The cold within him frosts his old features, nips his pointed nose, and makes his eyes red and his thin lips blue.

[pause]

Nobody ever stops him in the street to say, my dear Scrooge, how are you? Even the dogs, Dickens says, seem to know him, and pull their owners into doorways when they see him coming.

[long-pause]

In a dismal little room beside him, his clerk, Bob Cratchit, is copying letters.

[pause]

Bob has a very small fire, so small that it looks like one coal. He cannot put on any more coal, because Scrooge keeps the coal box in his own room. So Bob sits at his desk, wrapped in his white comforter, trying to warm himself at his candle, and failing.

[pause]

{img:poor-clerk-white-scarf-warming-hands-candle}
Scrooge's cheerful young nephew, Fred, comes in to wish him a merry Christmas, and to invite him to Christmas dinner. Bah, says Scrooge. Humbug.

[long-pause]

Two kind gentlemen come in next, to ask Scrooge for a donation to help the poor at Christmas.

[pause]

Are there no prisons? asks Scrooge. And the workhouses, are they still in operation? I help to support them. Those who are badly off must go there. And he sends the gentlemen away, with nothing.

[pause]

At last, it is time to close. Scrooge grumbles that Bob will want the whole of Christmas Day off, and that it is a poor excuse for picking a man's pocket every twenty-fifth of December. But he lets him go. And Bob runs home, through the foggy streets, sliding on the ice with a group of boys, twenty times, in honour of its being Christmas Eve.

[long-pause]

Scrooge eats his lonely supper in a gloomy tavern, and goes home to his dark, cold rooms, in an old building in a courtyard.

[pause]

As he puts his key in the door, something strange happens. The old knocker on the door seems, for a moment, to change into the face of his old business partner, Jacob Marley, who died seven years ago, on Christmas Eve. Scrooge blinks, and looks again, and it is only a knocker. Humbug, he says.

[pause]

{img:old-door-brass-knocker-foggy-courtyard-lamp}
He goes upstairs, and locks his door, and sits down by his small fire, in his dressing gown and nightcap, with a little saucepan of gruel.

[long-pause]

And then, Dickens tells us, the ghost of Jacob Marley comes into the room.

[pause]

He comes through the locked door, wearing his old waistcoat and boots, pale and transparent, and wrapped in a long chain, made of cash boxes, and keys, and padlocks, and ledgers, and heavy purses, which he forged, he says, in life, link by link, by caring only for money, and never for other people.

[pause]

Marley has come to warn Scrooge. He tells him that he has one last chance to change his ways. He will be visited, Marley says, by three spirits. Expect the first tomorrow, when the bell tolls one.

[pause]

Then the ghost floats out through the window, into the foggy night, and Scrooge, very tired, goes to bed without undressing, and falls asleep at once.

[long-pause]

It is not a frightening story, really.

[pause]

Dickens tells it with a twinkle in his eye, and with jokes and funny phrases on every page. The ghost of Marley is sad, more than frightening, and kind, in his way, because he has come to help his old friend.

[pause]

{img:old-man-asleep-curtained-bed-dim-room-quiet}
And when the readers of 1843 sat by their fires on Christmas Eve, reading this part of the story aloud, they shivered a little, pleasantly, and drew a little closer to the fire, and smiled, and turned the page, to see what would happen next.

[long-pause]

{img:gentle-spirit-candle-light-bedside-old-man-waking}
When Scrooge wakes, it is dark, and the church bells are chiming the hour. One o'clock.

[pause]

The curtains of his bed are drawn aside, and there stands the first of the three spirits: the Ghost of Christmas Past. It is a strange figure, like a child, and also like an old man, with long white hair, and a bright, clear jet of light shining from the top of its head, and a sprig of fresh green holly in its hand.

[long-pause]

The spirit takes Scrooge by the hand, and they pass through the wall, and suddenly, they are standing on an open country road, on a cold, clear winter's day, with fields on either side, and snow on the ground.

[pause]

Scrooge knows this place. It is the place where he grew up, as a boy. He sees the little market town, with its bridge and its church and its winding river, and boys on ponies, riding home for the Christmas holidays, shouting merry Christmas to each other. And Scrooge, the hard old man, finds that he has tears in his eyes.

[pause]

They come to an old school, and there, in a bare, dreary classroom, a lonely boy is sitting alone, reading, by a feeble fire, left behind at school by his family over Christmas. It is Scrooge himself, as a child. And the old man sits down beside the boy he used to be, and weeps.

[long-pause]

Then the spirit shows him another Christmas, when he was a young man, an apprentice, in the warehouse of a kind, jolly old merchant named Mr. Fezziwig.

[pause]

On Christmas Eve, when the work is done, old Fezziwig claps his hands, and calls out: No more work tonight! Christmas Eve! Clear away! And the apprentices clear away the desks and the stools, and sweep the floor, and light the lamps, and heap coal on the fire, until the warehouse is as snug and warm and dry and bright a ballroom as you would wish to see upon a winter's night.

[pause]

{img:fezziwig-warehouse-ball-fiddler-dancing-lamps}
In comes a fiddler, and tunes up his fiddle. In comes Mrs. Fezziwig, one vast substantial smile. In come the three Miss Fezziwigs, beaming and lovable, and all the young men and women who work in the business, and the housemaid, and the baker, and the cook, and the milkman. And they dance, and dance, and eat cake and roast and mince pies, and drink negus, until eleven o'clock, when the ball breaks up, and old Fezziwig and his wife stand at the door, shaking hands with everyone, and wishing them a merry Christmas.

[long-pause]

Scrooge watches it all, and remembers how happy he was that night, and how much a little kindness from his old master meant to him and the others.

[pause]

And then the spirit shows him a sadder scene: a young woman, whom he loved, and was going to marry, telling him, gently, that she must let him go, because he has come to love money more than he loves her.

[pause]

Scrooge cannot bear any more. And suddenly, he is back in his own bed, in the dark, and falls into a deep sleep.

[long-pause]

When he wakes again, the bell is striking one once more. And there is a strange, bright light coming from the next room.

[pause]

He goes in, and finds that his dark, gloomy sitting room has been transformed. The walls and the ceiling are hung with living green, holly and mistletoe and ivy, glistening with berries. A mighty blaze is roaring up the chimney. And heaped upon the floor, to form a kind of throne, are turkeys, geese, game, poultry, great joints of meat, sucking pigs, long wreaths of sausages, mince pies, plum puddings, barrels of oysters, red-hot chestnuts, cherry-cheeked apples, juicy oranges, luscious pears, immense Twelfth cakes, and seething bowls of punch.

[pause]

{img:jolly-giant-spirit-green-robe-feast-room-holly}
And on the throne sits a jolly giant, in a long green robe trimmed with white fur, with a holly wreath on his head, and a glowing torch in his hand. It is the Ghost of Christmas Present.

[long-pause]

The spirit takes Scrooge out into the streets of London, on Christmas morning.

[pause]

It is snowing, and the people are shovelling the snow from the pavements and the roofs, and throwing snowballs at each other, and laughing. The shops are open, and full of good things. The people are hurrying to church, in their best clothes, and carrying their dinners to the bakers' shops, to be cooked. And wherever the spirit goes, he sprinkles a little incense from his torch on the people's dinners, and on any quarrels, and makes them kind and happy again.

[pause]

{img:snowy-london-street-christmas-morning-people-snowballs}
Then the spirit takes him to a small house in Camden Town. It is the home of Bob Cratchit, his clerk.

[long-pause]

The Cratchits are poor. Bob earns fifteen shillings a week from Scrooge. Mrs. Cratchit is dressed out but poorly, in a twice-turned gown, but brave in ribbons, which are cheap. The children are busy getting the dinner ready, and Bob comes home from church, carrying his youngest son, Tiny Tim, on his shoulder.

[pause]

Tiny Tim is small and thin and lame, and walks with a little crutch, and has his legs supported by an iron frame. But he is the gentlest, most cheerful little boy in the world, and everyone loves him.

[long-pause]

Then comes the goose, which Mrs. Cratchit has roasted, and the apple sauce, and the mashed potatoes, and the whole family gathers round the little table, and Bob says grace.

[pause]

The goose is not very big, but there is enough for everyone, with the apple sauce and the potatoes, and everyone says that there never was such a goose. And then Mrs. Cratchit goes out to fetch the pudding, nervously, in case it has broken in the copper. But it has not. In she comes, smiling proudly, with the pudding, like a speckled cannonball, so hard and firm, blazing in half of half a quartern of ignited brandy, and bedight with Christmas holly stuck into the top.

[pause]

{img:mother-carrying-flaming-christmas-pudding-holly}
Oh, a wonderful pudding! Everybody says so. And afterwards, the family gathers round the fire, with apples and oranges and roasted chestnuts, and Bob raises his glass, and says: A merry Christmas to us all, my dears. God bless us!

[pause]

And Tiny Tim, the last of all, says: God bless us every one!

[long-pause]

{img:lighthouse-keepers-singing-christmas-stormy-sea}
The Ghost of Christmas Present takes Scrooge on a long journey that Christmas Day, far beyond London.

[pause]

They visit a lonely moor, where a family of miners is singing a Christmas song together in a little hut. They visit a lighthouse, far out on a rock in the stormy sea, where two lighthouse keepers are wishing each other a merry Christmas, with their hands joined across a rough table. They visit a ship, far out on the dark ocean, where every sailor, on watch or below deck, is humming a Christmas tune, or thinking of home.

[long-pause]

Then they visit the house of Scrooge's nephew, Fred, where there is a merry Christmas party going on.

[pause]

There is music, and laughter, and games. They play blind man's buff, and forfeits, and a game called Yes and No, where Fred thinks of something, and everyone else must guess what it is, by asking questions, to which he can only answer yes or no. The answer turns out to be: Uncle Scrooge.

[pause]

Scrooge, invisible, enjoys the games so much that he joins in with the guessing, and begs the spirit to let him stay a little longer. And Fred raises a glass to his uncle, and wishes him a merry Christmas and a happy new year, wherever he is.

[long-pause]

As the day goes on, the spirit grows older. His hair turns grey.

[pause]

And then, from under his green robe, he shows Scrooge two thin, ragged children, a boy and a girl, clinging to him. Their names, he says, are Ignorance and Want. They are the children of all humankind, and Scrooge must beware of them both, and especially of the boy.

[pause]

{img:old-spirit-green-robe-two-ragged-children-gentle}
It is the message that Dickens wanted to give, from the beginning: that a society that leaves its children poor, and hungry, and uneducated, will suffer for it. And Scrooge, remembering his own hard words to the kind gentlemen the day before, is ashamed.

[long-pause]

Then the clock strikes twelve, and the second spirit is gone, and in its place, there is a third.

[pause]

It is the Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come, a tall, silent figure, wrapped in a long dark robe, which never speaks, but only points, with one hand.

[pause]

It shows Scrooge some scenes from the future, in the cold grey light of a winter's morning. He sees a few businessmen in the street, talking carelessly about a man who has died, a man whom nobody seemed to care about. He sees the Cratchits' little house, very quiet and sad, with the little crutch standing in the corner, by the fire, carefully kept, and no Tiny Tim. And at last, he sees a neglected grave, in a lonely churchyard, and on the stone, his own name.

[long-pause]

Scrooge falls to his knees, and clutches at the spirit's robe, and begs.

[pause]

Spirit, he says, hear me. I am not the man I was. I will honour Christmas in my heart, and try to keep it all the year. I will live in the Past, the Present, and the Future. The spirits of all three shall strive within me. Tell me that I may yet change these shadows that you have shown me.

[pause]

{img:old-man-kneeling-praying-bedpost-dawn-light}
And as he holds up his hands in prayer, the spirit's robe shrinks, and collapses, and dwindles down into a bedpost. His own bedpost.

[long-pause]

He is in his own bed, in his own room, and it is morning, and he is alive.

[pause]

And he is so happy, that he laughs, and cries, and dances around the room, in his nightcap, and does not know what to do with himself. I don't know what day of the month it is, he says. I don't know how long I've been among the spirits. I don't know anything. I'm quite a baby. Never mind. I don't care. I'd rather be a baby.

[pause]

He runs to the window, and opens it, and puts out his head. No fog, no mist. Clear, bright, jovial, stirring, cold. Golden sunlight. Heavenly sky. Sweet fresh air. Merry bells.

[long-pause]

Down in the street below, there is a boy, in his Sunday clothes.

[pause]

What's today, my fine fellow? Scrooge calls down. Today? says the boy, amazed. Why, Christmas Day. It's Christmas Day! says Scrooge to himself. I haven't missed it. The spirits have done it all in one night.

[pause]

And he asks the boy whether he knows the poulterer's shop on the next street, and whether they have sold the prize turkey that was hanging up there, the big one. And he sends the boy to buy it, and has it sent, secretly, to Bob Cratchit's house in Camden Town. It is twice the size of Tiny Tim.

[pause]

{img:boy-carrying-giant-turkey-cab-snowy-street-laughing}
Then Scrooge dresses himself in his best, and goes out into the streets, smiling at everyone, and wishing everyone a merry Christmas. He meets one of the kind gentlemen who came to ask for money for the poor, and whispers in his ear a sum so large that the gentleman's breath is taken away. And he goes to church, and walks about the streets, and pats children on the head, and finds that everything gives him pleasure.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, he goes to his nephew Fred's house, and knocks on the door, and asks, nervously: Will you let me in, Fred?

[pause]

Let him in! Dickens says. It's a mercy he didn't shake his arm off. And Scrooge spends the afternoon at the party, playing games, and is happier than he has been in all his life.

[pause]

The next morning, he is at the office early, waiting for Bob Cratchit to arrive late. And when Bob creeps in, eighteen minutes late, Scrooge pretends to be angry, and then tells him that he is going to raise his salary, and help his struggling family. Make up the fires, Bob, he says, and buy another coal scuttle before you dot another i.

[long-pause]

And Scrooge was better than his word, Dickens tells us.

[pause]

He did it all, and infinitely more. And to Tiny Tim, who did not die, he was a second father. He became as good a friend, as good a master, and as good a man, as the good old city knew. And it was always said of him, that he knew how to keep Christmas well, if any man alive possessed the knowledge.

[pause]

{img:old-man-young-boy-crutch-walking-snowy-park-smiling}
May that be truly said of us, and all of us. And so, as Tiny Tim observed, God bless us, every one.

[long-pause]

{img:christmas-eve-london-street-snow-gaslight-crowds-1843}
On Christmas Eve, 1843, the streets of London are crowded until late at night.

[pause]

The shops stay open, lit by gas, with their windows full of good things, decorated with holly and evergreens. The poulterers and the grocers and the butchers are busy until midnight, selling the last geese and turkeys and puddings to the people who have just been paid their wages, and have hurried to the shops to buy their Christmas dinners.

[long-pause]

The people of London are out in their thousands, carrying parcels and baskets, and geese under their arms, and bunches of holly, and Christmas trees, a few of them, for those who have heard about the new German custom.

[pause]

Children run between the legs of the crowds, shouting with excitement. Street sellers cry their wares: hot chestnuts, oranges, holly, mistletoe, a penny a bunch. The waits are singing carols on the corners, with their lanterns. A brass band is playing in a square.

[pause]

The cold is sharp, and the breath of the people rises in clouds in the gaslight. But nobody seems to mind.

[long-pause]

At Number One, Devonshire Terrace, the Dickens family is preparing for Christmas.

[pause]

The house is decorated with holly and ivy and evergreens. There is a great fire burning in the drawing room. The children are wild with excitement. Their father has promised them a wonderful Christmas, and their father always keeps his promises about Christmas.

[pause]

{img:victorian-drawing-room-holly-fire-children-excited}
Dickens loves Christmas more than almost anyone. He loves the games and the food and the decorations, the parties and the dancing, and above all, the children's excitement. He is like a big child himself, at Christmas, his friends say.

[long-pause]

On Christmas Eve, he plays with the children in the nursery, and tells them stories, and does his magic tricks.

[pause]

He is a very good conjuror. He has bought a set of conjuring apparatus, and practised for hours, and he can make coins appear and disappear, and turn a handkerchief into a bunch of flowers, and cook a plum pudding in a gentleman's hat, and produce it, hot and steaming, to the astonishment of everyone.

[pause]

The children scream with delight. Their mother laughs. And then they are sent up to bed, with a candle, and hang up their stockings at the end of their beds, and try very hard to go to sleep.

[long-pause]

Later, Dickens sits by the fire, with Catherine and Georgina, and a few friends, and talks about the book, which has been in the shops for five days, and has already sold out.

[pause]

He is very happy. All over London, that very night, he knows, families are sitting by their fires, reading his little red book aloud: the story of Scrooge, and the ghosts, and Fezziwig's ball, and the Cratchits' Christmas dinner, and Tiny Tim.

[pause]

And all over London, people are laughing, and crying, and thinking about the poor, and about kindness, and about Christmas.

[long-pause]

Late in the evening, the church bells begin to ring, all over the city, for the midnight services.

[pause]

People walk through the frosty streets to church, wrapped in their coats and shawls and mufflers, carrying lanterns. In the great churches, Saint Paul's Cathedral and Westminster Abbey and the old churches of the City, the candles are lit, and the choirs sing the old carols, and the organs play.

[pause]

{img:old-city-church-candles-choir-midnight-christmas}
In the small churches and chapels, too, all over London, the people gather, in the candlelight, and sing, and pray, and wish each other a happy Christmas.

[long-pause]

In the poor districts, too, there is Christmas.

[pause]

In the little rooms, by the light of a single candle, families are getting ready for the next day. The goose from the goose club is hanging in the corner, or has been taken to the baker's, ready for the morning. There are a few oranges, and some nuts, and a little bunch of holly over the fireplace. The children's stockings are hanging by the little fire, with perhaps an orange and a penny and a sugar mouse in each.

[pause]

At the ragged school in Field Lane, the volunteers are giving the children a Christmas supper, of bread and meat and pudding, and singing carols with them.

[long-pause]

And at last, late in the night, the streets grow quiet.

[pause]

The shops close their shutters. The last carriages rattle home. The waits put out their lanterns. The bells stop ringing. The great city settles down to sleep, under a sky that, for once, is clear of fog, and full of cold, bright stars.

[pause]

{img:sleeping-london-rooftops-chimneys-stars-christmas-night}
In a million houses, rich and poor, families are asleep, waiting for Christmas morning. And in a house near Regent's Park, a young writer lies awake for a while, listening to the silence, and smiling.

[long-pause]

{img:christmas-morning-children-stockings-bed-1843}
On Christmas morning, 1843, the children of London wake up early.

[pause]

In thousands of houses, they creep out of bed, in the cold and the dark, to see what has been put in their stockings: an orange, some nuts, a few sweets, a sugar mouse, a shiny new penny, perhaps a little toy, a wooden soldier, a doll, a ball, a spinning top. In the richer houses, there are books and dolls' houses and rocking horses and toy theatres. In the poorer houses, there may be only an orange and a penny. But every child, almost, has something.

[long-pause]

Then the families have breakfast, and dress in their best clothes, and go to church.

[pause]

The bells ring out across the city, from every church tower. The streets are full of families walking to church, the men in their best coats and hats, the women in their best dresses and bonnets and shawls, the children scrubbed and brushed and polished, holding hands.

[pause]

On their way, many of the poorer families stop at the baker's shop, to leave their Christmas dinner to be cooked: the goose, or the joint of meat, in a big dish, with potatoes around it, each dish with a little number on it, so that it can be collected later. The bakers' ovens will be full all morning.

[long-pause]

In church, the people sing the Christmas hymns: Hark the Herald Angels Sing, and O Come All Ye Faithful, and While Shepherds Watched Their Flocks by Night.

[pause]

The churches are decorated with holly and evergreens, along the pews and the pillars and the pulpit. The vicar reads the Christmas story, from the Gospel of Luke: the journey to Bethlehem, and the baby in the manger, and the shepherds in the fields. And then the people go home, through the frosty streets, shaking hands and wishing each other a merry Christmas.

[pause]

{img:church-interior-holly-evergreens-congregation-singing}
In the poor districts, the people collect their dinners from the bakers, steaming hot, and carry them home through the streets, the smell of roast goose and sage and onions drifting behind them.

[long-pause]

Then comes Christmas dinner, the great event of the day.

[pause]

In most houses, it is eaten in the middle of the afternoon. In the middle-class houses, there is roast goose, or roast beef, or perhaps a turkey, which is still quite a luxury, with stuffing and apple sauce and gravy, and roast potatoes and vegetables. In the richer houses, there might be soup and fish first, and then several kinds of meat, and game.

[pause]

{img:victorian-family-christmas-dinner-goose-candles-table}
And then, of course, the Christmas pudding: a great round pudding, dark and rich, full of raisins and currants and suet and spices, boiled for hours in a cloth, and brought to the table blazing with brandy, with a sprig of holly stuck in the top. Followed by mince pies, and nuts, and oranges, and figs, and a glass of port wine or punch.

[long-pause]

After dinner, the families gather in the parlour, by the fire.

[pause]

The grownups doze a little, after the big meal. The children play with their new toys. And then, as the afternoon darkens into evening, the games begin: blind man's buff, and hunt the slipper, and charades, and forfeits, and snapdragon, with the blue flames flickering on the laughing faces in the darkened room.

[pause]

{img:victorian-parlour-charades-family-firelight-evening}
Somebody plays the piano, and everyone sings. Somebody tells a ghost story, and everyone shivers. The children are allowed to stay up late, until they fall asleep on the sofa, or on the rug in front of the fire.

[long-pause]

At Devonshire Terrace, the Dickens family has a wonderful Christmas.

[pause]

There is a great dinner, with friends and relatives, and games and dancing afterwards, and Dickens leading everything, full of energy and fun. He dances with the children, and with the ladies, and with his friends, until he is exhausted. A few days later, at a children's party at the house of their friend, the actor William Macready, he and his friend John Forster do conjuring tricks together, for an hour, and the children are amazed.

[pause]

Dickens writes to a friend in America that such dinings, such dancings, such conjurings, such blind man's buffings, such theatre-goings, such kissings-out of old years and kissings-in of new ones, never took place in these parts before.

[long-pause]

In the streets, as night falls, the people of London are walking home from visiting their families and friends.

[pause]

The gas lamps glow. The windows of the houses glow with firelight and candlelight. From the houses come the sounds of music and singing and laughter. The night is cold and clear, and the stars shine over the rooftops.

[pause]

{img:christmas-night-street-glowing-windows-music-frost}
And in many of those houses, by the fire, someone is reading aloud from a little red book, about an old man named Scrooge, and a little boy named Tiny Tim, while the family listens, and the fire crackles, and the candles burn low.

[long-pause]

{img:boxing-day-postman-tradesmen-christmas-box-doorway}
The day after Christmas is Boxing Day.

[pause]

In the morning, the tradesmen and the delivery boys and the postmen and the lamplighters and the crossing sweepers come to the doors of the houses where they have worked all year, and knock, and wish the family a merry Christmas, and hope for a Christmas box: a little gift of money, a shilling or a half crown, to thank them for their service. The servants of the house are given their Christmas boxes too, and often a day off, to visit their own families.

[long-pause]

For many Londoners, Boxing Day is the day for the theatre.

[pause]

On Boxing Day night, all the great theatres of London open their Christmas pantomimes. The pantomime is a great, noisy, colourful entertainment, for children and grownups alike, with music and dancing, and jokes, and magic, and spectacular scenery, which changes in front of the audience's eyes.

[pause]

It begins with a fairy tale, Cinderella, or Aladdin, or Jack and the Beanstalk, or Puss in Boots. And then, at the end, there is a magical transformation scene, where the good fairy waves her wand, and the scenery changes into an enchanted palace, glittering with lights, and the characters of the story are transformed into the characters of the harlequinade: Harlequin, the magical hero, in his diamond-patterned costume; Columbine, his sweetheart; Pantaloon, the foolish old man; and the Clown, with his white face and his red cheeks, who plays tricks on everyone, and steals sausages, and falls over, and makes the children scream with laughter.

[long-pause]

Dickens loves the pantomime.

[pause]

As a boy, he loved the famous clown Joseph Grimaldi, the greatest clown of them all, and later, as a young writer, he edited a book of Grimaldi's memoirs. Every Christmas, he takes his children to the pantomime, at Drury Lane, or Covent Garden, and laughs as loudly as they do.

[pause]

{img:family-theatre-box-children-laughing-pantomime}
The theatres are huge, and lit by gas, with tier upon tier of boxes and galleries, rising up to the ceiling, all full of families. The poorer people sit in the gallery, high up near the roof, where the seats are cheap, and shout and cheer and throw orange peel. The richer families sit in the boxes. Everyone has a wonderful time.

[long-pause]

In the days between Christmas and New Year, London is quieter than usual.

[pause]

Many of the offices are closed, or half empty. The shops are open, but not so busy. The families visit each other, and go for walks in the parks, and skate on the frozen ponds, if it is cold enough.

[pause]

In Regent's Park, and in Hyde Park and Saint James's Park, when the lakes freeze over, thousands of people come to skate, and slide, and watch, and the ice is crowded with skaters, gliding and wobbling and falling over, and laughing, while the sellers of hot chestnuts and gingerbread set up their stalls along the banks.

[long-pause]

On New Year's Eve, there are parties again, and at midnight, the church bells ring out the old year and ring in the new.

[pause]

People open their front doors, to let the old year out and the new year in. In some houses, there is an old custom called first footing: the first person to cross the threshold after midnight should be a tall, dark man, carrying a piece of coal, a piece of bread, and a little salt, for good luck in the coming year.

[pause]

{img:new-years-eve-bells-church-tower-crowd-lanterns}
In London, crowds gather outside Saint Paul's Cathedral to hear the great bells ring in the new year, and cheer, and shake hands, and wish each other a happy new year.

[long-pause]

And then there is Twelfth Night, on the fifth of January, the end of the Christmas season.

[pause]

In the days before Twelfth Night, the windows of the confectioners' shops are filled with great Twelfth cakes, iced in white sugar, and decorated with sugar figures of kings and queens, castles and knights, and stars and crowns, and crowds of people stand in front of the windows to admire them.

[pause]

{img:crowd-admiring-twelfth-cakes-shop-window-gaslight}
In the houses, there are Twelfth Night parties. A great cake is cut, with a bean and a pea hidden inside it, and whoever finds the bean is the king for the night, and whoever finds the pea is the queen. There are games and dancing and characters, drawn from a hat, so that everyone pretends to be someone else for the evening.

[long-pause]

At Devonshire Terrace, Twelfth Night is a very special day.

[pause]

It is the birthday of Dickens's eldest son, Charley. So every year, Dickens gives a great Twelfth Night party for Charley and his friends, with a huge Twelfth cake, and games, and dancing, and a magic show, with Dickens himself as the conjuror, in a long robe and a tall hat, calling himself The Unparalleled Necromancer, Rhia Rhama Rhoos.

[pause]

The children's parties at Devonshire Terrace are famous among the families of Dickens's friends. Everyone wants to be invited.

[long-pause]

And then, after Twelfth Night, the holly and the ivy are taken down, and burned, and the Christmas season is over.

[pause]

The children go back to school. The fathers go back to their offices and their workshops. The shops take down their Christmas decorations. The pantomimes go on for a few more weeks, and then close. And London settles down into the long, grey, foggy months of January and February, waiting for the spring.

[pause]

{img:january-london-street-fog-ordinary-day-clerks}
But that winter, many people kept a little red book by their fireside, and read it again, now and then, in the long evenings, and remembered.

[long-pause]

{img:dickens-reading-desk-stage-gaslight-audience-1850s}
Charles Dickens wrote a Christmas book every year for the next few years, after A Christmas Carol.

[pause]

There was The Chimes, in 1844, about the bells of an old London church, and The Cricket on the Hearth, in 1845, about a happy family and a cricket chirping by the fire, and three more after that. They were all popular. But none of them was ever loved as much as the first.

[long-pause]

He went on to write many more novels: Dombey and Son, David Copperfield, which was his favourite, and partly the story of his own childhood, Bleak House, Little Dorrit, A Tale of Two Cities, Great Expectations, Our Mutual Friend.

[pause]

He became the most famous writer in the world. He travelled to America, and to Italy, and to Switzerland. He started his own magazines, and wrote articles about the poor, and the schools, and the prisons, and the hospitals, and helped to make many of them better.

[pause]

He worked very hard, all his life, and helped many people: poor families, struggling writers, homeless women, sick children. He helped to found a hospital for children, in Great Ormond Street, in London, which is still there today, one of the most famous children's hospitals in the world.

[long-pause]

In 1853, ten years after he wrote it, he read A Christmas Carol aloud, in public, for the first time.

[pause]

It was in Birmingham, in a great hall, just after Christmas, to raise money for a new institute for working people. Two thousand people came to hear him, mostly working men and women, who had paid very little for their tickets, because Dickens had asked that the tickets should be cheap, so that working people could come.

[pause]

{img:great-hall-birmingham-working-people-audience-1853}
He read for three hours, standing at a little desk, on the stage, in the gaslight. He did all the voices: Scrooge's cold, cracked voice, and Bob Cratchit's timid one, and Fezziwig's jolly one, and Tiny Tim's little piping voice. The audience laughed and cried and cheered. Dickens said afterwards that it was one of the happiest evenings of his life.

[long-pause]

After that, he gave many more readings, all over Britain and Ireland, and in America.

[pause]

He had a special reading desk made, of red wood, with a little shelf for his book, and a rail for his gloves, and a little block of wood to lean his elbow on. He stood behind it, on a stage, with a dark red screen behind him, and gas lights shining on him, and read from his books, acting out all the parts.

[pause]

A Christmas Carol was always the favourite. He read it more than a hundred and twenty times, in great halls, to audiences of thousands. People queued for hours to get in. When he read it in America, in 1867 and 1868, people stood in line all night in the snow to buy tickets.

[long-pause]

The readings made Dickens very rich. But they also wore him out.

[pause]

He was getting older, and his health was not good. The travelling and the excitement and the long readings exhausted him. His doctors told him to stop. But he loved the audiences, and the applause, and he would not stop.

[pause]

{img:tired-writer-train-carriage-window-countryside-evening}
In 1857, he bought a house in Kent, called Gad's Hill Place, a red-brick house on a hill, near the old road from London to Canterbury, which he had admired as a little boy, when he walked past it with his father, who told him that if he worked very hard, one day he might live in a house like that. And he did.

[long-pause]

In March 1870, he gave his farewell reading in London. He read A Christmas Carol, for the last time.

[pause]

At the end, he stood in the gaslight, before the great audience, with tears in his eyes, and said: From these garish lights I vanish now for evermore, with a heartfelt, grateful, respectful, affectionate farewell.

[pause]

{img:gads-hill-place-red-brick-house-kent-summer-garden}
Three months later, in June 1870, at Gad's Hill, he died suddenly, at the age of fifty-eight.

[long-pause]

He had asked to be buried quietly, in a small country churchyard. But the nation would not allow it. He was buried in Westminster Abbey, in Poets' Corner, among the greatest writers of England.

[pause]

For days afterwards, thousands of people came to the grave, and left flowers, until it was covered with them: rich and poor, old and young, from every part of the country. A little girl, it is said, when she heard that he had died, asked: Mr. Dickens dead? Then will Father Christmas die too?

[pause]

He did not. Father Christmas, and the Christmas that Dickens loved, and wrote about, and helped to bring back, lived on.

[long-pause]

Many people say that Charles Dickens invented the modern Christmas.

[pause]

That is not quite true. Christmas was celebrated long before him, and many of the customs that he wrote about were already old. But he helped, more than almost anyone, to make Christmas what it is today: a feast of family and home, of good food and warm fires, of games and stories and laughter, of generosity and kindness, especially to the poor and the lonely.

[pause]

{img:victorian-family-christmas-later-years-tree-candles}
And above all, he gave the world a story, which has been told again and again, every Christmas, for nearly two hundred years: the story of an old man who learned, in one long night, to open his heart.

[long-pause]

{img:doughty-street-dickens-house-museum-georgian-door}
London is still there today, much bigger, and much changed.

[pause]

But in many places, the London of Charles Dickens can still be found, if you know where to look.

[long-pause]

On Doughty Street, in Bloomsbury, there is a tall, narrow Georgian house of brown brick, with a black front door and a fanlight above it.

[pause]

Dickens lived there as a young man, from 1837 to 1839, with Catherine and their first children, when he was writing Oliver Twist and Nicholas Nickleby. It is the only one of his London homes that still stands. Today, it is the Charles Dickens Museum.

[pause]

Inside, the rooms are furnished as they were in his day. There is his desk, and his chair, and the reading desk he used for his public readings. There are his books, and his letters, and portraits of him and his family. And every Christmas, the house is decorated with holly and candles, as it would have been in his time, and visitors can listen to readings of A Christmas Carol, in the old drawing room, by the fire.

[long-pause]

Devonshire Terrace, where Dickens wrote A Christmas Carol, is gone. The house was pulled down long ago. But on the wall of the building that stands there now, near Regent's Park, there is a stone panel, with pictures of some of the characters from his books, and a little plaque, which says that he lived there.

[pause]

{img:covent-garden-today-market-christmas-lights-evening}
Covent Garden is still there, too, though the fruit and vegetable market moved away many years ago. The old market buildings are full of shops and cafés now, and at Christmas, there is a giant Christmas tree in the square, and lights hanging from the glass roofs, and singers and musicians performing in the streets, just as there were in Dickens's day.

[long-pause]

Leadenhall Market is still there, in the City, with its beautiful painted roof of iron and glass, built later in the Victorian age.

[pause]

At Christmas, it is decorated with lights and greenery, and the old shops, though they no longer sell geese and turkeys, are still busy and bright. Smithfield is still a meat market, in its great Victorian buildings, and on Christmas Eve, the butchers there still hold a famous auction, selling turkeys and meat to the crowds.

[pause]

{img:leadenhall-market-painted-roof-christmas-lights}
And the old churches of the City are still there, many of them, with their bells, which still ring out across the streets at Christmas.

[long-pause]

Great Ormond Street Hospital, which Dickens helped to support, is still caring for sick children, more than a hundred and seventy years later.

[pause]

And the ragged schools, which Dickens supported, were the beginning of a movement that led, in time, to free schools for every child in Britain.

[pause]

Every Christmas, all over the world, A Christmas Carol is read aloud, and performed on stage, and shown on television. Children learn about Scrooge and Tiny Tim at school. And people say, God bless us, every one, and Bah, humbug, without always knowing where the words came from.

[long-pause]

On a winter evening in London, when the fog comes down over the river, it is not hard to imagine the city that Dickens knew.

[pause]

The lamps glow in the mist along the Embankment. The great dome of Saint Paul's looms out of the gloom, and disappears again. The bells of the old City churches ring out the hours. In the narrow lanes, the windows of the old pubs glow warm and golden, with their fires burning inside.

[pause]

{img:foggy-london-evening-lamps-river-st-pauls-dome}
And at Christmas, the streets are full of people, carrying parcels, and Christmas trees, and the shop windows are full of lights and good things, and somewhere, on a corner, carol singers are singing, with their breath rising in the cold air, under the street lamps.

[long-pause]

So we leave London for tonight, the great old city by the river, with its lamps glowing softly in the winter fog, and its bells ringing out across the rooftops, and its people going home to their warm firesides, on a cold December night, long ago, and today.
