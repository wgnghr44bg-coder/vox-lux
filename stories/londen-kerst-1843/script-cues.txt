In December 1843, London was preparing for Christmas, and the shop windows glowed with gaslight. In December 1843, the streets of London were busy with people preparing for Christmas. Historians note that the city was the largest in the world, with about two million inhabitants. It was the capital of a growing empire and the centre of trade, industry and finance. Even so, Christmas in 1843 was not quite the same as Christmas today. Some customs, such as Christmas trees and Christmas cards, were only just appearing. Others, such as Christmas dinner, carol singing and decorating homes with holly, were old traditions. For the rich, Christmas was a time of feasts and parties. For ordinary families, Christmas was a day of rest, a special dinner and time together. For most working families in London in 1843, Christmas Day was one of the few holidays of the year. Historians note that many people worked six days a week, often for long hours, and had little free time. On Christmas Day, most shops, offices and workshops closed. Families went to church, shared a special dinner and spent time together. Even so, not everyone had the day off. Servants, cooks, cab drivers and some shop workers still had to work. For many families, the Christmas dinner was the best meal of the year. They saved money for weeks to buy a goose, a joint of beef or a pudding. Some joined goose clubs, paying a small amount each week to receive a goose at Christmas. Historians note that Christmas was becoming more important in Victorian times. It was increasingly seen as a family celebration, centred on the home, children, food and kindness to others.

On the nineteenth of December 1843, Charles Dickens published A Christmas Carol. On the nineteenth of December 1843, a small book with a red cloth cover and gold edges appeared in the bookshops of London. It was called A Christmas Carol, and it was written by Charles Dickens, the most popular author in Britain. Historians note that the book told the story of Ebenezer Scrooge, a mean old businessman who is visited by ghosts on Christmas Eve and learns to be kind and generous. It cost five shillings, which was quite expensive at the time. Even so, it sold very quickly. The first printing of six thousand copies was sold out by Christmas Eve. Readers loved its warmth, humour and message of kindness. The book was illustrated with four coloured pictures by the artist John Leech. Historians note that A Christmas Carol has never been out of print since. It helped to shape the idea of Christmas as a time of family, generosity and goodwill, which is still important today.

London in 1843 was a city of great wealth and great poverty, side by side. In 1843, London was a city of contrasts. Historians note that it was home to some of the richest people in the world, who lived in grand houses in the West End, with servants, carriages and fine furniture. It was also home to hundreds of thousands of poor people, who lived in crowded, dirty and unhealthy conditions. Often, the rich and poor lived only a few streets apart. Even so, the city was full of energy and change. New railways, bridges, docks and buildings were being built. Factories and workshops produced goods of every kind. Ships from around the world brought tea, sugar, cotton, spices and other goods to the London docks. Historians note that this mix of wealth and poverty, progress and suffering, deeply affected Charles Dickens. In A Christmas Carol, he showed both sides of London. He wanted his readers to remember the poor at Christmas, and to share their good fortune with those in need.

In 1843, Britain was ruled by the young Queen Victoria, who had married Prince Albert three years before. In 1843, Queen Victoria was twenty-four years old. She had become queen in 1837, at the age of eighteen, and had married her cousin, Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha, in 1840. Historians note that the royal couple were very happy together, and they had three young children by the end of 1843. Prince Albert brought some German Christmas customs to the British court, including the Christmas tree. Even so, these customs were not yet widely known in Britain in 1843. They became popular a few years later. In 1848, a newspaper called the Illustrated London News printed a picture of the royal family gathered around a decorated Christmas tree at Windsor Castle. Families across Britain began to copy them. Historians note that the royal family's example, together with Dickens's book, helped create the image of the Victorian family Christmas. The queen and her husband spent Christmas 1843 at Windsor Castle with their children.

As darkness fell each December afternoon, lamplighters walked the streets lighting the gas lamps. In December, darkness fell early in London, at about four o'clock in the afternoon. Historians note that the streets were lit by gas lamps, which had been introduced in the early nineteenth century. Each evening, lamplighters walked through the streets with long poles or ladders, turning on the gas and lighting each lamp with a small flame. In the morning, they returned to put them out. Even so, gas lighting was not as bright as electric light today. The lamps gave a soft yellow glow, which created pools of light along the streets, with darkness in between. On foggy nights, the light was even dimmer. London's fogs were famous. They were caused by smoke from millions of coal fires mixing with damp air from the river. The fog could be thick, yellow and choking. In A Christmas Carol, Dickens described the fog creeping into Scrooge's office through every crack and keyhole.

{img:07-victorian-family-fireside-christmas-evening}

For many families, the best part of Christmas was gathering around the fire together. In the cold, dark days of December, the fireplace was the heart of every home in Victorian London. Historians note that most houses were heated by coal fires, which burned in grates in each room. In poorer homes, there might be only one fire, in the main room, where the family cooked, ate, worked and gathered in the evenings. Even so, families made the most of their time together. They told stories, sang songs, played games and read aloud. Reading aloud was a popular pastime, especially in families where not everyone could read. Many families gathered around the fire to listen to the latest novel by Charles Dickens, read by a parent or an older child. Historians note that Dickens wrote A Christmas Carol to be read aloud in this way. He called it a ghost story of Christmas, and he hoped that families would enjoy it together by the fireside, as the winter nights drew in.

In 1843, London was the largest city in the world and the busiest port on Earth. By 1843, London had grown into the largest city in the world, with about two million inhabitants. Historians note that it had doubled in size in the previous forty years, as people moved from the countryside and from other countries to find work. It was the centre of the British Empire, which stretched across the globe. The river Thames was crowded with ships, and the London docks were the busiest in the world. Even so, the city's rapid growth brought problems. Housing could not keep up with the number of people. Streets were overcrowded, and many people lived in poor conditions. The river was polluted with sewage and waste. Diseases such as cholera, typhus and tuberculosis spread easily. Historians note that London was both a place of opportunity and a place of danger. People came hoping for a better life, but many found only hard work, low wages and crowded rooms.

{img:09-railway-station-steam-train-1843-crowd}

New railways connected London with other cities, and stations were full of travellers going home for Christmas. In the 1830s and 1840s, Britain was gripped by railway fever. Historians note that new railway lines were being built across the country, connecting London with Birmingham, Manchester, Bristol, Brighton and other towns. The first railway station in London, London Bridge, opened in 1836, followed by Euston in 1837 and Paddington in 1838. Even so, rail travel was still new and exciting in 1843. Trains were pulled by small steam locomotives. Passengers travelled in first, second or third class carriages. Third class carriages were often open to the weather, with wooden benches and no roof. Yet trains were much faster than stagecoaches. A journey that had taken days could now be done in hours. At Christmas, railways made it easier for people to travel home to their families. Historians note that this helped make Christmas a family celebration, bringing relatives together from distant parts of the country.

Horse-drawn omnibuses and cabs carried passengers through the crowded streets. Getting around London in 1843 was not easy. Historians note that most people walked, sometimes for miles each day. Those who could afford it travelled by horse-drawn omnibus, a large carriage that carried passengers along fixed routes. The first London omnibus had started in 1829. Passengers sat inside or on the roof, which was cheaper but cold and wet in winter. Hansom cabs, small two-wheeled carriages pulled by a single horse, carried one or two passengers. Even so, the streets were often blocked by traffic. Omnibuses, cabs, carts, wagons, carriages and horses competed for space. The noise of wheels and hooves on the cobblestones was constant. The streets were also dirty, covered with mud and horse manure. Crossing sweepers, often poor children, cleared paths across the streets for pedestrians, in return for a few coins. Historians note that the traffic of London was one of the wonders, and one of the trials, of the Victorian city.

{img:11-thames-tunnel-opening-1843-crowds-arches}

In March 1843, the Thames Tunnel opened, the first tunnel ever built under a river in a city. On the twenty-fifth of March 1843, the Thames Tunnel opened between Rotherhithe and Wapping, in east London. Historians note that it was the first tunnel ever built beneath a navigable river. It was designed by the engineer Marc Brunel and his son Isambard Kingdom Brunel, and it took eighteen years to build. The work was dangerous, and the tunnel flooded several times. Even so, it was finally completed. It had been intended for horse-drawn carriages, but the money ran out before ramps could be built. Instead, it was opened to people on foot, who paid a penny to walk through. In its first fifteen weeks, about a million people visited it. Stalls sold souvenirs inside, and fairs were held there. Historians note that it was one of the great sights of London in 1843. In the 1860s, it was sold to a railway company, and trains still run through it today.

In November 1843, the statue of Admiral Nelson was raised to the top of his column in Trafalgar Square. Trafalgar Square was laid out in the 1830s and 1840s, in memory of Admiral Horatio Nelson's great naval victory at the Battle of Trafalgar in 1805. Historians note that a tall column was built in the middle of the square, about fifty-two metres high, with a statue of Nelson on top. In November 1843, the statue was raised to the top of the column. Before it was lifted, fourteen workmen climbed up and had a dinner on top of the column, high above the square. Even so, the monument was not yet finished. The famous bronze lions at the base were added later, in 1867. The National Gallery, at the north side of the square, had opened in 1838. Historians note that Trafalgar Square was becoming a symbol of London and of British national pride. In December 1843, Londoners walking through the square could admire the newly raised statue, looking out over the city towards the sea.

Since 1840, a letter could be sent anywhere in Britain for a single penny, using a stamp. In 1840, Britain introduced a new postal system called the Penny Post. Historians note that it was invented by Rowland Hill, who argued that letters should be paid for by the sender, using a stamp, and should cost the same to send anywhere in the country. The first stamp, the Penny Black, showed the head of Queen Victoria. Before 1840, sending letters was expensive, and the receiver usually paid. Even so, the Penny Post was a great success. The number of letters sent in Britain doubled in the first year. People could now write to family and friends cheaply and easily. At Christmas, this made it possible to send greetings to distant relatives. Historians note that in 1843, a civil servant named Henry Cole, who had helped Rowland Hill, commissioned the first commercial Christmas card. It was designed by the artist John Callcott Horsley and showed a family raising their glasses in a toast.

The first Christmas card, made in 1843, showed a family raising glasses in a Christmas toast. In 1843, Henry Cole was a busy man with many friends. Historians note that he did not have time to write personal Christmas letters to all of them. So he asked his friend, the artist John Callcott Horsley, to design a card with a Christmas message. The card showed a family gathered around a table, raising glasses of wine in a toast. On either side were scenes of people giving food and clothing to the poor. The message read: A Merry Christmas and a Happy New Year to you. Even so, the card was not universally admired. Some people disapproved of it, because it showed children drinking wine. About one thousand copies were printed, and they were sold for a shilling each, which was expensive. Historians note that Christmas cards did not become popular until later, when printing became cheaper. Today, only a few of the original 1843 cards survive. They are valuable collectors' items.

London's famous fogs were caused by the smoke of millions of coal fires. In the winter, London was often covered by thick fog. Historians note that it was caused by the smoke from coal fires in homes, factories and workshops, mixing with damp air from the river. The fog could be yellow, brown or even green, and it had a sharp, sooty smell. It could be so thick that people could not see across the street. Even so, Londoners carried on with their daily lives. They lit lamps during the day and walked carefully through the gloom. Link boys, boys carrying torches, guided people through the streets for a few coins. Historians note that the fogs were also harmful to health. They caused coughing, breathing problems and illness, especially among children and the elderly. In later decades, they became even worse. Dickens described the London fog in many of his books. In A Christmas Carol, the fog comes pouring in at every chink and keyhole of Scrooge's office.

{img:16-gas-lamps-glowing-narrow-court-evening}

Gas lamps had transformed London at night, making the streets brighter and safer. Before the nineteenth century, London streets were lit by oil lamps and candles, which gave only a dim light. Historians note that in 1807, the first gas street lamps were installed on Pall Mall, in the West End. Within a few decades, gas lighting had spread across most of the city. Gas was produced at gasworks by heating coal and was carried through pipes beneath the streets. Even so, gas lighting was not available everywhere. Poorer areas often had fewer lamps, and some narrow courts and alleys remained dark. Many homes still used candles and oil lamps. Historians note that gas lighting changed London life. It made the streets safer at night and allowed shops to stay open later. Shop windows glowed with light, attracting customers in the evening. At Christmas, the brightly lit shops were a magical sight, especially for children. Dickens described them in A Christmas Carol, glowing with warmth and colour.

{img:17-police-constable-top-hat-street-1843}

The Metropolitan Police, founded in 1829, patrolled the streets in tall hats and dark blue coats. In 1829, the Home Secretary, Sir Robert Peel, founded the Metropolitan Police, the first modern police force in London. Historians note that before that, the city had been protected by watchmen and parish constables, who were often old, poorly paid and ineffective. The new police officers wore dark blue tailcoats and tall top hats, to look different from soldiers. They were nicknamed Bobbies or Peelers, after Robert Peel. Even so, the police were not popular with everyone at first. Some people feared that they would be used to spy on citizens or to suppress protests. Over time, they won more public trust. By 1843, police constables walked their beats day and night, watching for crime and helping people in need. Historians note that in 1842, a detective department had been created. Dickens was fascinated by the new detectives. He later wrote articles about them and created one of the first detectives in English fiction, Inspector Bucket.

Poor children earned a few coins by sweeping paths across the muddy streets. The streets of Victorian London were muddy, dirty and covered with horse manure. Historians note that crossing sweepers, often poor children, swept paths across the streets so that people could cross without dirtying their shoes and clothes. In return, they hoped to receive a small coin. Some crossing sweepers worked the same corner for years and became familiar figures. Even so, it was a hard and uncertain way to earn a living. The sweepers worked outside in all weathers, often barefoot or in worn shoes. They depended on the kindness of passers-by. Many earned only a few pence a day. Historians note that the journalist Henry Mayhew later interviewed many crossing sweepers for his famous study of the London poor. He found boys and girls, old men and women, some disabled, all struggling to survive. Dickens wrote about a crossing sweeper named Jo in his novel Bleak House, a boy who had nothing in the world.

{img:19-warm-shop-window-toys-children-looking}

At Christmas, children pressed their noses against shop windows full of toys and sweets. In the weeks before Christmas, the shops of London were filled with goods for the holiday. Historians note that toy shops displayed dolls, wooden animals, toy soldiers, spinning tops, rocking horses and picture books. Sweet shops sold sugar mice, gingerbread, toffee and candied fruit. Grocers' shops were filled with raisins, currants, oranges, lemons, nuts and spices for Christmas puddings and mince pies. Even so, many children could only look. Poor children gathered outside the brightly lit windows, admiring the treasures they could never afford. For them, the shop windows were a free show, a glimpse of a world of plenty. Historians note that many Victorian writers described these scenes. Dickens remembered his own childhood, when his family was poor, and he felt deeply for children who went without. In A Christmas Carol, he described the poulterers' and grocers' shops in loving detail, as a feast for the eyes.

{img:20-church-bells-snowy-steeple-night}

Across London, church bells rang to mark the hours and call people to worship. In 1843, London had hundreds of churches, each with its own bells. Historians note that their sound filled the city, marking the hours of the day and calling people to services. On Christmas Eve and Christmas morning, the bells rang out joyfully. Many people attended church on Christmas Day, before going home for their Christmas dinner. Even so, not everyone went to church. In the poorer parts of the city, many people had no time or money for church, or did not feel welcome there. Some churches had pews that had to be rented by families, leaving little space for the poor. Historians note that this worried many Victorian reformers. New churches were built in poor areas, and missions and ragged schools were set up to help the poor. In A Christmas Carol, the bells play a special role. They ring out as the ghosts come and go, and on Christmas morning, Scrooge hears them pealing across the city.

{img:21-charles-dickens-portrait-1843-study}

In 1843, Charles Dickens was thirty-one years old and already the most famous writer in Britain. Charles Dickens was born in 1812 in Portsmouth, on the south coast of England. Historians note that by 1843, he was already famous. His first novel, The Pickwick Papers, published in monthly parts in 1836 and 1837, had made him a household name. It was followed by Oliver Twist, Nicholas Nickleby, The Old Curiosity Shop and Barnaby Rudge. Readers across Britain and America eagerly waited for each new instalment. Even so, 1843 was a difficult year for him. His latest novel, Martin Chuzzlewit, was not selling as well as his earlier books. He had a growing family and large expenses, and he was worried about money. He had also been disappointed by a visit to America in 1842. Historians note that Dickens was energetic, ambitious and sensitive. He loved theatre, long walks and parties. He was also deeply concerned about poverty and injustice, which he had experienced himself as a child.

[long-pause]

{img:22-devonshire-terrace-house-regents-park}

Dickens lived with his family in a large house near Regent's Park. In 1843, Charles Dickens lived at 1 Devonshire Terrace, a large house near Regent's Park, in northwest London. Historians note that he had moved there in 1839 with his wife, Catherine, and their growing family. By the end of 1843, they had four children, and a fifth was born in January 1844. The house had a large garden and was comfortably furnished. Dickens wrote in a study on the ground floor, overlooking the garden. Even so, running such a household was expensive. There were servants to pay, children to feed and clothe, and many guests to entertain. Dickens loved to give dinners and parties, especially at Christmas. His family later remembered the joyful Christmas celebrations at Devonshire Terrace, with games, music, conjuring tricks and dancing. Dickens himself often led the fun. Historians note that the house was demolished in the twentieth century. A stone carving on the building that replaced it shows Dickens and some of his characters.

{img:23-boy-working-blacking-factory-1824}

As a boy of twelve, Dickens had been sent to work in a factory while his father was in a debtors' prison. When Charles Dickens was twelve years old, his family fell into debt. Historians note that in 1824, his father, John Dickens, was sent to the Marshalsea, a prison for people who could not pay their debts. His mother and younger siblings joined his father in the prison, as was common at the time. Charles was sent to work at Warren's Blacking Factory, a warehouse near the Thames that made shoe polish. He worked long hours, sticking labels on pots, for six shillings a week. Even so, the experience lasted only a few months. When his father inherited some money, the family left the prison, and Charles went back to school. Yet he never forgot the shame and loneliness of that time. He told almost no one about it. Historians note that his childhood suffering gave him deep sympathy for poor children, and it influenced many of his books, including A Christmas Carol.

[long-pause]

{img:24-dickens-walking-london-streets-night}

Dickens loved to walk through the streets of London at night, sometimes for fifteen or twenty miles. Charles Dickens was a tireless walker. Historians note that he often walked through the streets of London at night, covering fifteen or twenty miles. He walked when he was thinking about his stories, when he could not sleep and when he needed to calm his mind. He explored every part of the city, from the grand streets of the West End to the poorest slums of the East End. Even so, his walks were not only exercise. They were also research. He observed the people, the buildings, the shops and the street life of London. He noticed details that other people missed, such as the faces of the poor, the sounds of the streets and the smells of the markets. Historians note that he later said that while writing A Christmas Carol, he walked about the black streets of London many a night, after all sober people had gone to bed. The city itself was one of his greatest inspirations.

[long-pause]

{img:25-field-lane-ragged-school-children-1843}

In September 1843, Dickens visited a school for the poorest children of London, and he was deeply shocked. In September 1843, Charles Dickens visited the Field Lane Ragged School, near Saffron Hill, in one of the poorest parts of London. Historians note that ragged schools were free schools for very poor children, run by volunteers. They taught reading, writing and religion to children who could not afford ordinary schools. Many of the children were dirty, hungry and dressed in rags, which gave the schools their name. Even so, the schools did important work. They gave children some education and hope. Dickens was deeply moved and shocked by what he saw. He wrote to a friend that he had seldom seen such misery. He wanted to do something to help. Historians note that this visit was one of the inspirations for A Christmas Carol. In the book, the Ghost of Christmas Present shows Scrooge two ragged children, a boy and a girl, named Ignorance and Want. They represent the poor children Dickens had seen.

[long-pause]

{img:26-government-report-children-mines-1843}

A government report about child labour in 1843 made Dickens angry and determined to act. In 1842 and 1843, a government commission published reports on the employment of children in mines and factories. Historians note that the reports described terrible conditions. Children as young as five or six worked in coal mines, pulling carts through narrow tunnels for twelve hours a day. Others worked in factories and workshops, in dangerous and unhealthy conditions. The reports were illustrated with engravings that shocked the public. Even so, many employers defended child labour, arguing that poor families needed the children's wages. Dickens read the second report in early 1843 and was horrified. At first, he planned to write a pamphlet called An Appeal to the People of England on behalf of the Poor Man's Child. Historians note that he later changed his mind. He decided that a story would reach more people than a pamphlet. He said that his book would come down with twenty thousand times the force of a pamphlet.

[long-pause]

{img:27-manchester-athenaeum-speech-hall-1843}

In October 1843, Dickens gave a speech in Manchester about the importance of education for the poor. In October 1843, Charles Dickens travelled to Manchester to speak at a fundraising event for the Manchester Athenaeum, an institution that offered education to working people. Historians note that Manchester was one of the great industrial cities of Britain, full of cotton mills and factories. It was also a place of great poverty. In his speech, Dickens spoke about the importance of education in fighting poverty and crime. He argued that ignorance was the most dangerous enemy of society. Even so, the visit gave him more than a chance to speak. During his stay, he walked through the poor districts of the city and saw the conditions in which factory workers lived. Historians believe that it was during this visit that he first had the idea for A Christmas Carol. Soon after returning to London, he began to write. In just six weeks, he finished the book that would make him famous all over again.

[long-pause]

{img:28-slum-court-washing-lines-children-1843}

Hundreds of thousands of Londoners lived in crowded and unhealthy slums. In 1843, many of London's poor lived in slums, areas of crowded, old and decaying housing. Historians note that some of the worst were in Saint Giles, near Covent Garden, in Saffron Hill and in the East End. Families often lived in a single room, sometimes sharing it with other families. There was little light, no running water and no proper drainage. Toilets were shared by many people, and waste often overflowed into the streets. Even so, people in the slums tried to make the best of their lives. They worked hard, cared for their children and helped their neighbours. Many worked as labourers, street sellers, washerwomen and servants. Historians note that disease spread quickly in the slums. Cholera, typhus and tuberculosis killed thousands of people each year. Children were especially at risk. In some areas, almost half of all children died before the age of five. Reformers began to campaign for better housing and sanitation.

[long-pause]

{img:29-workhouse-gates-grey-building-1843}

For the very poorest, the only help was the workhouse, a place many feared more than hunger. In 1834, the British Parliament passed the New Poor Law. Historians note that it changed the way the poor were helped. Instead of giving money or food to the poor in their own homes, the law said that people in need should go into a workhouse. Workhouses were large institutions where the poor lived and worked in return for food and shelter. Even so, the workhouses were deliberately harsh. The idea was that only the truly desperate would choose to enter them. Families were separated, with men, women and children living in different parts of the building. The food was plain, the work was hard and the rules were strict. Many poor people feared the workhouse and would do almost anything to avoid it. Historians note that Dickens strongly criticised the workhouses. In Oliver Twist, he described a workhouse where the boys were starved. In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge asks whether there are no prisons and no workhouses for the poor.

[long-pause]

{img:30-mother-sewing-candle-poor-room-night}

Many poor women earned a living by sewing, working late into the night for very low pay. In Victorian London, thousands of poor women earned their living by sewing. Historians note that they made shirts, dresses, trousers and other clothes for shops and merchants, working at home or in small workshops. They were paid by the piece, which meant they earned money for each item they finished, rather than for the hours they worked. The pay was very low. Even so, sewing was one of the few kinds of work that women could do at home while caring for their children. Many seamstresses worked twelve to sixteen hours a day, often late into the night, by candlelight. Their eyes and health suffered. In December 1843, the magazine Punch published a poem by Thomas Hood called The Song of the Shirt. It described the suffering of a poor seamstress and became very popular. Historians note that it helped raise awareness of the conditions of working women, in the same month that A Christmas Carol was published.

[long-pause]

{img:31-soup-kitchen-queue-snow-volunteers}

In winter, charities opened soup kitchens to feed the hungry poor. Winter was the hardest time of year for the poor of London. Historians note that cold weather meant higher costs for coal and warm clothing, while many types of work, such as building and dock labour, became scarce. Families struggled to buy food and fuel. Even so, many people tried to help. Churches, charities and wealthy individuals opened soup kitchens, where the hungry could receive free bowls of soup and bread. Some gave out coal, blankets and clothing. At Christmas, many charities made special efforts to provide dinners for the poor. Historians note that Victorian charity was often well intentioned, but it could not solve the deep problems of poverty. There were too many people in need, and charity was uneven. Dickens believed that kindness and generosity were important, but he also wanted society to change. In A Christmas Carol, two gentlemen visit Scrooge to ask for money for the poor at Christmas.

[long-pause]

{img:32-debtors-prison-courtyard-families}

People who could not pay their debts could be sent to prison, sometimes with their whole families. In Victorian England, people who owed money and could not pay it back could be sent to a debtors' prison. Historians note that London had several, including the Marshalsea, the Fleet and the King's Bench. Prisoners stayed there until they could pay their debts, which could take years. Some never left. Their families often lived with them in the prison, because they had nowhere else to go. Even so, life in a debtors' prison was not like life in an ordinary prison. Prisoners could have visitors, buy food and sometimes work. Those with money could rent better rooms. The poor lived in crowded, dirty conditions. Historians note that Dickens's own father had been imprisoned in the Marshalsea. Dickens later wrote about debtors' prisons in several novels, including Little Dorrit, in which the heroine is born and grows up inside the Marshalsea. Imprisonment for debt was finally abolished in England in 1869.

[long-pause]

{img:33-costermonger-family-small-room-supper}

Even in the poorest homes, families tried to celebrate Christmas in some small way. For poor families in London, Christmas could be a difficult time. Historians note that many could not afford a special dinner, presents or decorations. Some worked through Christmas Day because they could not afford to lose a day's wages. Even so, most families tried to mark the day in some small way. They might buy a little meat, a few oranges or some sweets for the children. They might decorate their room with a sprig of holly or a bit of greenery. Neighbours sometimes shared what they had. Historians note that some poor families saved for months to have a proper Christmas dinner. Others received help from charities or employers. Dickens understood the importance of these small celebrations. In A Christmas Carol, the Cratchit family is poor, but they make their Christmas dinner a joyful feast. Their goose is small, and their pudding is modest, but they are happy, because they are together.

[long-pause]

{img:34-ragged-boy-girl-shadows-street-corner}

In 1843, many thousands of children in London lived on the streets, with no home at all. In 1843, thousands of children in London had no proper home. Historians note that some were orphans, while others had run away from cruel or poor families. They slept in doorways, under bridges, in markets and in cheap lodging houses. They survived by begging, running errands, selling small goods or stealing. Even so, some of these children found help. Ragged schools, missions and charities offered them food, shelter and education. Later in the century, the doctor Thomas Barnardo founded homes that cared for thousands of homeless children. Historians note that the plight of street children shocked many Victorians and inspired reforms. Dickens wrote about such children in Oliver Twist, describing a gang of young pickpockets. In A Christmas Carol, the children named Ignorance and Want are a warning. The ghost tells Scrooge to beware them both, but most of all the boy, Ignorance. Dickens believed that education was the key to a better future.

[long-pause]

{img:35-chestnut-seller-brazier-street-snow}

In winter, street sellers roasted chestnuts over glowing braziers on the street corners. The streets of Victorian London were full of people selling food, drink and goods. Historians note that these street sellers were a vital part of the city's life, especially for the poor, who bought most of their food from them. In winter, chestnut sellers set up small braziers, metal containers filled with burning charcoal, on street corners. They roasted chestnuts over the coals and sold them hot, in paper cones. Even so, it was a hard way to earn a living. The sellers stood outside for hours in the cold, wind and rain. They earned only a few shillings a week. Yet their braziers gave out a welcome warmth, and people gathered around them to warm their hands. The smell of roasting chestnuts filled the streets. Historians note that hot food was especially popular in winter. Sellers also offered baked potatoes, hot pies, pea soup, coffee and other warming foods.

[long-pause]

{img:36-baked-potato-man-can-oven-street}

Hot baked potatoes, sold from shiny metal ovens, warmed the hands and stomachs of working people. One of the most popular street foods in Victorian London was the baked potato. Historians note that potato sellers carried large metal cans, heated by a charcoal fire underneath, which kept the potatoes hot. The cans were often polished and decorated with brass, so that they shone in the gaslight. The sellers cried out to passers-by, offering potatoes with butter and salt for a halfpenny each. Even so, the potato sellers did more than sell food. On cold days, working people bought hot potatoes to warm their hands, holding them in their pockets before eating them. The journalist Henry Mayhew later estimated that there were about two hundred potato sellers in London in the 1850s. They sold their potatoes mainly from October to April. Historians note that potatoes were a cheap and filling food, important in the diet of the poor. A hot potato on a winter's evening was a small comfort in a hard life.

[long-pause]

{img:37-muffin-man-bell-tray-on-head-fog}

The muffin man walked the streets in the late afternoon, ringing his bell to announce fresh muffins for tea. In Victorian London, the muffin man was a familiar figure. Historians note that he walked the streets in the late afternoon, carrying a wooden tray of muffins on his head, covered with a green baize cloth to keep them warm. He rang a hand bell to announce his arrival, and people came out of their houses to buy muffins and crumpets for tea. Even so, the muffin man's trade was not always welcome. In 1839, Parliament passed a law banning the ringing of bells in the streets, because of the noise. But the muffin men continued, and the law was rarely enforced. Victorian muffins were soft, flat, round breads, toasted by the fire and eaten with butter. Historians note that they were a popular treat at teatime, especially in winter. The muffin man and his bell became a symbol of cosy Victorian afternoons, as families gathered around the fire, toasting muffins and drinking tea.

[long-pause]

{img:38-holly-ivy-seller-basket-market-stall}

Before Christmas, sellers brought holly, ivy and mistletoe from the countryside to sell in the streets. In the weeks before Christmas, the streets of London were filled with sellers of greenery. Historians note that they brought holly, ivy, mistletoe and laurel from the countryside around London and sold them from baskets, barrows and stalls. People bought them to decorate their homes, shops and churches. Holly, with its bright red berries and shiny green leaves, was especially popular. Even so, the custom of decorating with greenery was very old, older than Christianity. Ancient peoples had brought evergreen plants into their homes in winter as a sign of life and hope during the dark season. Christians later gave these plants new meanings. Holly came to represent the crown of thorns and the blood of Christ. Historians note that mistletoe had its own special custom. Couples who met beneath it were expected to kiss. In A Christmas Carol, the Ghost of Christmas Present sits on a throne made of holly, mistletoe and ivy.

[long-pause]

{img:39-carol-singers-waits-lantern-door-snow}

Groups of singers, called waits, went from house to house singing carols at Christmas. Singing carols at Christmas was an old tradition in England. Historians note that in the Middle Ages, carols were songs sung and danced in a circle, not only at Christmas but at other festivals too. Over time, they became connected with Christmas. Groups of singers, called waits, went from house to house in the weeks before Christmas, singing carols and playing instruments. In return, they hoped to receive money, food or drink. Even so, carol singing had declined in the early nineteenth century. Many old carols had been forgotten. In the 1820s and 1830s, collectors such as Davies Gilbert and William Sandys began to gather and publish old carols. Historians note that this helped revive the tradition. In A Christmas Carol, a boy stops at Scrooge's keyhole to sing a carol, but Scrooge seizes a ruler and frightens him away. Later in the century, many of the carols still sung today became popular.

[long-pause]

{img:40-ballad-seller-song-sheets-crowd}

Ballad sellers sang the latest songs in the street and sold printed copies for a penny. Among the street sellers of Victorian London were the ballad sellers. Historians note that they sang songs in the streets and sold printed copies, called broadsides, for a penny or less. The songs told stories of love, crime, disasters, battles and daily life. Some were old folk songs, while others were new, written about recent events. At Christmas, ballad sellers sold carols and songs about the festive season. Even so, their trade was changing. Cheap newspapers and magazines were becoming more common, offering news and entertainment to working people. Yet the ballad sellers remained popular, especially among the poor. Historians note that many people who could not read learned songs by listening to the ballad sellers. Some broadsides were decorated with simple woodcut pictures. Collections of these printed songs survive in libraries today, giving historians a glimpse of the songs and stories that ordinary Victorians enjoyed in the streets of London.

[long-pause]

{img:41-camden-town-terrace-houses-1843-winter}

Dickens imagined the poor clerk Bob Cratchit and his family living in Camden Town, where Dickens himself had lived as a boy. In A Christmas Carol, Bob Cratchit, Scrooge's poorly paid clerk, lives with his family in Camden Town, in north London. Historians note that Dickens knew Camden Town well. He had lived there as a boy, in 1822 and 1823, when his family first came to London. They lived in a small house in Bayham Street, a poor and shabby area at the time. Even so, Camden Town was not a slum. It was a district of small, cheap houses, home to clerks, craftsmen and labourers. In 1843, it was changing quickly. The railway from London to Birmingham had been built through it, and new houses were going up. Historians note that the Cratchits' home was probably based on Dickens's memories of Bayham Street. The family lives in a small house with four rooms. They are poor, but clean, loving and cheerful. Dickens wanted his readers to see the dignity of such families.

[long-pause]

{img:42-clerk-high-stool-ledger-cold-office}

Clerks worked long hours in cold offices, copying letters and keeping accounts by hand. In 1843, there were no typewriters or computers. Historians note that every letter, account and document had to be written by hand. This work was done by clerks, who sat at high desks in offices, writing with pens and ink. Clerks copied letters, kept accounts, recorded business transactions and filed papers. They worked long hours, often from early morning until evening, six days a week. Even so, the pay was often low, especially for junior clerks. In A Christmas Carol, Bob Cratchit earns fifteen shillings a week, which was not enough to support a large family comfortably. His office is cold, because Scrooge will not let him have more than a tiny fire. Historians note that many clerks in London lived on the edge of poverty. They had to dress respectably for work, which was expensive, yet their wages were barely enough. Dickens had worked as a clerk himself as a young man, in a lawyer's office.

[long-pause]

{img:43-mother-daughters-cooking-small-kitchen}

In a working family, the mother managed the home, the money and the meals with great skill. In a Victorian working family, the mother was usually responsible for running the home. Historians note that she managed the household money, shopped for food, cooked, cleaned, washed clothes and cared for the children. In poor families, this required great skill. She had to make a small income stretch to feed and clothe everyone. Older daughters helped with the cooking, cleaning and care of younger children. Even so, many mothers also earned money, by sewing, washing or taking in lodgers. Their work was hard and never ending. In A Christmas Carol, Mrs Cratchit is described as brave in a twice-turned gown, which means a dress that had been taken apart and resewn inside out to make it last longer. She prepares the Christmas dinner with care and pride. Historians note that Dickens showed great respect for the working mothers of London, who held their families together in difficult circumstances.

[long-pause]

{img:44-small-boy-crutch-family-hearth}

In Victorian London, many children suffered from illness and disability, and few could afford a doctor. In A Christmas Carol, the youngest Cratchit son, Tiny Tim, is small, weak and walks with a crutch. Historians note that his illness is not named in the book. Some doctors have suggested that he may have suffered from rickets, a disease caused by a lack of vitamin D, or from tuberculosis. Both were common among poor children in Victorian London. Rickets was caused partly by poor diet and lack of sunlight, in a city darkened by smoke and fog. Even so, many poor families could not afford a doctor or proper medicine. Children with illnesses or disabilities often suffered without help. Many died young. Historians note that Dickens wanted his readers to feel for children like Tiny Tim. In the book, the Ghost of Christmas Present warns Scrooge that Tim will die if nothing changes. At the end, Scrooge helps the family, and Tim lives. His words, God bless us, every one, became famous.

[long-pause]

{img:45-children-playing-street-hoops-camden}

Children in Camden Town played in the streets, but many also helped their families by working. Children in working families in Victorian London often had short childhoods. Historians note that many began working at an early age, running errands, selling goods in the streets, helping in shops or working in factories. Some went to school, but education was not yet compulsory. It would not become so until the 1870s and 1880s. Even so, children also found time to play. They played games in the streets, such as hopscotch, marbles, skipping and chasing. They rolled hoops with sticks and spun tops. Poorer children made their own toys from whatever they could find. Historians note that in A Christmas Carol, the Cratchit children are excited about Christmas. Martha, the eldest daughter, works as an apprentice to a milliner, a maker of hats. Peter, the eldest son, is about to start his first job. The younger children help their mother and dream of the Christmas goose. Their joy is simple and real.

[long-pause]

{img:46-cratchit-style-family-goose-table}

For a family like the Cratchits, a roast goose and a pudding were a great Christmas treat. In A Christmas Carol, the Cratchit family's Christmas dinner is one of the most famous scenes. Historians note that it shows what Christmas meant to a poor family in 1843. The family has a small goose, with apple sauce and mashed potatoes, followed by a Christmas pudding. The goose is not large, but the family praises it as if it were a feast. Everyone helps, and everyone is happy. Even so, the scene also shows their poverty. The goose has been cooked at the baker's shop, because the family has no oven big enough. The pudding is small for so large a family. Yet no one says so. Historians note that this was a realistic picture. Many poor families in London took their Christmas dinner to the local baker to be cooked. The bakers charged a small fee. At the end of the meal, Bob Cratchit raises a toast to Mr Scrooge, the founder of the feast, despite his meanness.

[long-pause]

{img:47-leadenhall-market-geese-turkeys-hanging}

Before Christmas, the poulterers' shops and markets were filled with geese and turkeys. In the days before Christmas, London's markets were busy with shoppers buying food for the holiday. Historians note that poulterers, sellers of poultry, hung geese, turkeys, chickens and ducks in rows outside their shops. Leadenhall Market, in the City of London, was especially famous for its poultry and game. Thousands of birds were brought to London from the countryside for Christmas. Even so, in 1843, the goose was more common than the turkey for Christmas dinner, especially among ordinary families. Turkeys were larger and more expensive. Historians note that geese and turkeys were often walked to London from farms in Norfolk and Suffolk, sometimes wearing little boots to protect their feet. The walk could take weeks. By the end of the nineteenth century, the turkey had become more popular. In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge sends a boy to buy the prize turkey from the poulterer's shop and has it delivered to the Cratchits.

[long-pause]

{img:48-covent-garden-market-fruit-vegetables-1843}

Covent Garden was the great fruit and vegetable market of London. Covent Garden, in the West End of London, was the main market for fruit, vegetables and flowers. Historians note that a market had been held there since the seventeenth century. In 1830, a fine new market building with colonnades was built. Every morning, long before dawn, carts arrived from the farms and market gardens around London, loaded with produce. Porters carried baskets on their heads, stacked impossibly high. Even so, the market was not only for the wealthy. Costermongers, street sellers of fruit and vegetables, bought their goods there early in the morning and sold them around the city during the day. At Christmas, the market was especially busy, with apples, pears, oranges, chestnuts, nuts and evergreen plants. Historians note that Covent Garden remained a market until 1974, when it moved to a new site. Today, the old market building is a popular place for shopping and entertainment, still full of life.

[long-pause]

{img:49-smithfield-meat-market-cattle-1843}

At Smithfield, cattle and sheep were driven through the streets to the great meat market. Smithfield, just outside the old City walls, was London's main market for live animals for centuries. Historians note that every week, thousands of cattle, sheep and pigs were driven through the streets of London to Smithfield, where they were sold to butchers. The market was crowded, noisy and dirty. The animals were kept in pens, and the ground was covered with mud and waste. Even so, Smithfield was essential to feeding the city. In the days before Christmas, it was especially busy, as butchers bought beef, mutton and pork for Christmas dinners. Historians note that many people complained about the market. The animals caused chaos in the streets, and the conditions were cruel and unhealthy. Dickens described the market in Oliver Twist. In 1855, the live animal market was moved to a new site in Islington. A covered meat market was later built at Smithfield, and it still operates today.

[long-pause]

{img:50-grocer-shop-window-raisins-oranges-figs}

Grocers' shops were piled high with raisins, figs, oranges and spices for Christmas puddings and pies. In the weeks before Christmas, grocers' shops in London were filled with special goods. Historians note that they sold raisins, currants, candied peel, figs, dates, almonds, oranges, lemons, sugar and spices such as cinnamon, nutmeg and cloves. These were needed for Christmas puddings, mince pies and cakes. Many of these goods came from distant lands, brought by ships from Spain, Turkey, the Mediterranean and the colonies. Even so, they were expensive, and poor families could afford only small amounts. Historians note that tea and coffee were also sold by grocers, and tea had become the most popular drink in Britain. In A Christmas Carol, Dickens described the grocers' shops in rich detail. He wrote of the raisins, the almonds, the cinnamon and the candied fruits, and the customers so eager that they tumbled up against each other at the door. The shops were a feast for the senses at Christmas.

[long-pause]

{img:51-shoppers-crowded-street-parcels-evening}

On the evenings before Christmas, the shopping streets were crowded with people buying food and presents. In the last days before Christmas, the shopping streets of London were crowded. Historians note that shops stayed open late, lit by gas lamps, and people of every class came out to buy food, presents and decorations. Oxford Street, Regent Street and the Strand were full of shoppers. Markets and street sellers did a busy trade. Even so, Christmas presents in 1843 were usually simple. Families exchanged small gifts, such as books, gloves, handkerchiefs, toys and sweets. Large and expensive presents were rare, except among the wealthy. Historians note that the custom of giving presents at Christmas was growing, but many people still gave presents on New Year's Day instead. The crowds of shoppers created a festive atmosphere. People called greetings to each other, and street sellers cried out their wares. Children ran ahead of their parents, excited by the lights and the shop windows. The city seemed to glow with warmth and anticipation.

[long-pause]

{img:52-baker-shop-christmas-pies-cakes}

Bakers sold mince pies and cakes, and on Christmas Day they roasted dinners for families without ovens. Bakers played a special role at Christmas in Victorian London. Historians note that in the days before Christmas, they sold mince pies, Christmas cakes, buns and bread. Mince pies were small pies filled with a mixture of dried fruit, spices, suet and sometimes meat. They had been eaten at Christmas for centuries. Even so, the bakers' most important service was on Christmas Day itself. Many poor families had no oven at home, only a small fire for cooking. So they took their Christmas goose or joint of meat to the local baker, who roasted it in his large oven for a small fee. Historians note that the law usually forbade bakers to bake on Sundays and holy days, but they were allowed to cook dinners for the poor. In A Christmas Carol, the Ghost of Christmas Present sprinkles incense from his torch on the dinners carried to the bakers' shops, making the people carrying them cheerful.

[long-pause]

{img:53-christmas-market-evening-lanterns-stalls}

On Saturday nights before Christmas, the street markets were lit by flaring lamps and filled with working families. In Victorian London, Saturday night was the main shopping time for working families. Historians note that many workers were paid on Saturday, and they went straight to the street markets to buy food for the week ahead. The markets were held in streets such as the New Cut in Lambeth and Whitecross Street. Stalls were lit by flaring lamps and candles, creating a lively and colourful scene. Even so, the markets were especially busy before Christmas. Families bought meat, vegetables, fruit, sweets and small presents for the holiday. Sellers shouted their prices, trying to attract customers. Musicians played, and children ran between the stalls. The journalist Henry Mayhew later described these markets vividly, with the lights, the noise and the crowds. Historians note that the markets were a vital part of working-class life. They offered cheap food and goods, and a chance to meet neighbours and friends. They were the heart of community life.

[long-pause]

{img:54-dickens-writing-late-night-candle-pages}

Dickens wrote A Christmas Carol in only six weeks, in October and November 1843. Charles Dickens began writing A Christmas Carol in October 1843, soon after his visit to Manchester. Historians note that he finished it by the end of November, in only about six weeks. He wrote it while also working on the monthly instalments of his novel Martin Chuzzlewit. Even so, he wrote with great excitement and emotion. He later said that he wept and laughed while writing it, and that he walked the streets of London at night thinking about it. The original manuscript survives and is kept in the Morgan Library in New York. It shows many corrections and changes, crossed-out words and additions written in the margins. Historians note that it reveals how carefully Dickens worked on every sentence. He chose words for their sound as well as their meaning, because he knew the story would be read aloud. The book was short, only about thirty thousand words, so that it could be read in a single evening.

[long-pause]

{img:55-publisher-office-printing-proofs-1843}

Dickens paid for the book's printing himself and insisted that it should be beautifully made. A Christmas Carol was published by Chapman and Hall, who had published many of Dickens's earlier books. Historians note that Dickens had quarrelled with them over money, so he made an unusual arrangement. He paid for the production of the book himself and received the profits, while the publishers received a commission. He insisted that the book should be beautiful, with a red cloth binding, gold lettering, gilt edges and coloured illustrations. Even so, this made it expensive to produce. He set the price at five shillings, which was affordable for middle-class readers but too much for the poor. The book sold very well, but because of the high production costs, Dickens's profits were disappointing. He had hoped to earn about one thousand pounds, but he received only about two hundred and thirty. Historians note that he was bitterly disappointed. Yet the book's success with readers was enormous, and it made his name even more famous.

[long-pause]

{img:56-john-leech-artist-etching-plate}

The artist John Leech made four coloured illustrations and four black and white drawings for the book. The illustrations for A Christmas Carol were made by John Leech, a popular artist who worked for the comic magazine Punch. Historians note that he made four full-page coloured etchings and four black and white wood engravings. The coloured pictures had to be painted by hand, copy by copy, which was slow and expensive. One of the most famous shows Mr Fezziwig's Christmas ball, with dancing couples and a fiddler. Another shows the Ghost of Christmas Present, a jolly giant in a green robe trimmed with fur, surrounded by food. Even so, Dickens was very particular about the illustrations. He checked each one carefully and asked for changes when he was not satisfied. Historians note that Leech's pictures helped shape how people imagined the characters, especially the Ghost of Christmas Present. His image of a large, bearded figure in a fur-trimmed robe later influenced the popular image of Father Christmas.

[long-pause]

{img:57-bookshop-window-red-books-december}

The first six thousand copies of A Christmas Carol sold out by Christmas Eve. A Christmas Carol went on sale on the nineteenth of December 1843. Historians note that it was an immediate success. The first printing of six thousand copies was sold out by Christmas Eve. More printings followed quickly, and by the end of 1844, it had been reprinted many times. Reviewers praised it warmly. The writer William Makepeace Thackeray called it a national benefit. Even so, the book's success also caused problems. Within weeks, a publisher named Parley's Illuminated Library produced a cheap, pirated version, changing some words to avoid copyright. Dickens sued and won, but the pirate publishers declared bankruptcy, and Dickens had to pay large legal costs. Historians note that he was angry and frustrated. Yet the book continued to sell. Stage versions also appeared quickly. By February 1844, at least eight different plays based on the story were being performed in London theatres. The story had captured the public's imagination.

[long-pause]

{img:58-scrooge-character-old-miser-counting-house}

The story of Ebenezer Scrooge showed readers that it was never too late to change. The hero of A Christmas Carol, Ebenezer Scrooge, is a cold, mean and lonely old businessman. Historians note that he hates Christmas and refuses to give money to the poor. He pays his clerk, Bob Cratchit, very little and keeps his office cold to save coal. On Christmas Eve, he is visited by the ghost of his dead business partner, Jacob Marley, who warns him to change his ways. Then three more spirits visit him: the Ghosts of Christmas Past, Christmas Present and Christmas Yet to Come. Even so, the story is not only a ghost story. It is a story about memory, regret and hope. The spirits show Scrooge his lonely childhood, the joy of others at Christmas and the sad future that awaits him if he does not change. Historians note that by the end, Scrooge has become generous and kind. The name Scrooge has since entered the English language, meaning a mean and miserly person.

[long-pause]

{img:59-fezziwig-ball-dancing-warehouse-fiddler}

One of the happiest scenes in the book is a Christmas dance held by a kind employer named Fezziwig. In A Christmas Carol, the Ghost of Christmas Past takes Scrooge back to his youth, when he was an apprentice to a kind old merchant named Mr Fezziwig. Historians note that on Christmas Eve, Fezziwig cleared his warehouse and held a party for his workers, family and neighbours. There was a fiddler, dancing, cake, roast meat and mince pies. Everyone was happy, and the party cost Fezziwig only a little money. Even so, Scrooge realises that his old master's kindness meant a great deal to his workers. The scene shows that a good employer could make his workers happy with simple kindness. Historians note that Dickens may have based Fezziwig's party on memories of happy celebrations from his own life. The scene was illustrated by John Leech, and it became one of the most popular parts of the book. It showed the joy that Christmas could bring when people shared their good fortune with others.

[long-pause]

{img:60-yule-log-fireplace-old-manor-hall}

Many Victorian Christmas customs were very old, going back to medieval and even ancient times. Christmas had been celebrated in England for more than a thousand years by 1843. Historians note that many customs were very old. In medieval times, Christmas was a twelve-day festival, from Christmas Day to Twelfth Night on the fifth of January. There were feasts, games, plays and music. Great houses burned a Yule log, a large log that was supposed to burn throughout the festival. Even so, Christmas celebrations had declined in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. In the 1640s and 1650s, the Puritans, who thought the festival was too wild and too Catholic, banned Christmas celebrations. After the restoration of the monarchy in 1660, Christmas returned, but in a quieter form. By the early nineteenth century, many old customs had faded. Historians note that the Victorians revived and reshaped Christmas. Writers such as Washington Irving, Walter Scott and Dickens helped create a new interest in old traditions, mixing them with new customs.

[long-pause]

{img:61-mistletoe-hanging-doorway-couple-parlour}

Kissing under the mistletoe was a popular Christmas custom, especially among young people. Mistletoe is a plant that grows on trees, with small white berries. Historians note that it was considered special by ancient peoples, including the Celtic druids, who believed it had magical powers. In England, it became connected with Christmas. A sprig of mistletoe, sometimes made into a kissing bough with greenery, ribbons and apples, was hung from the ceiling or in a doorway. Couples who met beneath it were expected to kiss. Even so, there were rules. In some households, a berry was picked from the mistletoe after each kiss, and when the berries were gone, the kissing had to stop. Historians note that the custom was popular among servants and young people, though some disapproved. In The Pickwick Papers, Dickens described a lively Christmas party at a country house, with kissing under the mistletoe. The custom remains popular today, and mistletoe is still sold at Christmas in markets across Britain.

[long-pause]

{img:62-christmas-pudding-boiling-copper-steam}

The Christmas pudding was boiled in a cloth for hours and served flaming with brandy. The Christmas pudding was the highlight of the Victorian Christmas dinner. Historians note that it was made with suet, flour, breadcrumbs, sugar, raisins, currants, candied peel, eggs and spices. The mixture was tied up in a cloth and boiled for many hours in a large pot or copper, a big metal container used for washing clothes. When it was ready, the pudding was turned out onto a plate, decorated with a sprig of holly, doused with brandy and set alight. Even so, puddings were often made weeks before Christmas. Families gathered on Stir-up Sunday, the last Sunday before Advent, to mix the pudding. Each member of the family stirred it and made a wish. Coins or small charms were sometimes hidden inside. Historians note that in A Christmas Carol, Mrs Cratchit's pudding is described as being like a speckled cannonball, hard and firm, blazing with brandy and with holly stuck into the top.

[long-pause]

{img:63-german-christmas-tree-candles-1840s}

The Christmas tree was a German custom that was only beginning to appear in England in the 1840s. The custom of decorating a tree at Christmas came from Germany. Historians note that it had been popular there since at least the sixteenth century. In England, Queen Charlotte, the German wife of King George the Third, had a decorated tree at court in 1800. Prince Albert, Queen Victoria's husband, also brought the custom with him from Germany. Even so, in 1843, Christmas trees were still rare in English homes. Most families decorated with holly, ivy and mistletoe instead. The trees became popular after 1848, when the Illustrated London News published a picture of the royal family around their tree. Families across the country began to copy them. Victorian trees were usually small, placed on a table and decorated with candles, sweets, fruit, paper ornaments and small presents. Historians note that the lit candles were beautiful but dangerous. Fires caused by Christmas trees were common. A bucket of water was often kept nearby.

[long-pause]

{img:64-twelfth-night-cake-party-crown}

In 1843, Twelfth Night, on the fifth of January, was still an important part of the Christmas season. In 1843, the Christmas season did not end on Christmas Day. Historians note that it continued until Twelfth Night, the evening of the fifth of January, before the feast of the Epiphany. Twelfth Night was a time of parties, games and fun. The centre of the celebration was the Twelfth Night cake, a large, rich fruit cake decorated with sugar figures and icing. A bean and a pea were hidden inside. Whoever found the bean became the king of the party, and whoever found the pea became the queen. Even so, Twelfth Night celebrations declined during the Victorian period. Queen Victoria is said to have disapproved of the rowdy parties. Many Twelfth Night customs, including the cake, were moved to Christmas. Historians note that the Twelfth Night cake became the Christmas cake, decorated with white icing and festive figures. Dickens himself loved Twelfth Night and held a party each year, also celebrating his son Charley's birthday.

[long-pause]

{img:65-parlour-games-blind-mans-buff-family}

Victorian families played games at Christmas, such as blind man's buff and snapdragon. Games were an important part of Victorian Christmas celebrations. Historians note that families and guests played games in the parlour after dinner. One of the most popular was blind man's buff, in which one player was blindfolded and tried to catch the others. Another was snapdragon, in which raisins were placed in a dish of brandy and set alight, and players tried to snatch them from the flames. Even so, some games were quieter. Word games, guessing games and charades, in which players acted out words or phrases, were popular. Historians note that in A Christmas Carol, Scrooge's nephew Fred holds a Christmas party where the guests play blind man's buff and a guessing game called Yes and No. Scrooge, invisible beside the Ghost of Christmas Present, becomes so excited that he joins in, though no one can hear him. Dickens himself loved games and played them enthusiastically with his family and friends.

[long-pause]

{img:66-carol-book-hymns-church-choir-1843}

Many of the Christmas carols sung today were written or became popular in the Victorian period. The Victorian period was a great age of Christmas carols. Historians note that many carols that are still sung today were written, translated or made popular during Victoria's reign. Some were old carols revived by collectors. Others were new. Hark! The Herald Angels Sing had been written by Charles Wesley in the eighteenth century and was set to music by Felix Mendelssohn in the 1850s. Good King Wenceslas was written by John Mason Neale in 1853. Silent Night, written in Austria in 1818, was translated into English in the 1850s and 1860s. Even so, in 1843, carol singing was still mainly a popular custom, sung in the streets and in homes, rather than in churches. Many churches preferred hymns and psalms. Historians note that by the end of the century, carols had become an important part of church services at Christmas. Collections of carols were published, and choirs sang them in churches across the country.

[long-pause]

{img:67-christmas-eve-shops-closing-snow-street}

On Christmas Eve, the shops closed late, and people hurried home through the cold streets. Christmas Eve in Victorian London was a busy and exciting day. Historians note that shops stayed open late, as people bought the last things they needed for Christmas. Markets were crowded, and street sellers did a brisk trade. Workers were paid their wages, and some received extra money as a Christmas bonus. Even so, not everyone had a day off. Many people worked until late on Christmas Eve, including shop assistants, clerks and servants. In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge keeps Bob Cratchit working until the office closes on Christmas Eve, and grudgingly gives him Christmas Day off. Bob then runs home, sliding on the ice in the street with a group of boys, in honour of its being Christmas Eve. Historians note that as evening fell, the shops closed their shutters, the streets grew quieter, and people hurried home to their families. The bells of the churches began to ring for evening services.

[long-pause]

{img:68-family-decorating-parlour-holly-ivy}

On Christmas Eve, families decorated their homes with holly, ivy and other evergreens. In Victorian homes, decorations were usually put up on Christmas Eve. Historians note that it was considered unlucky to bring evergreens into the house before then. Families decorated their rooms with holly, ivy, laurel, rosemary and mistletoe. They placed greenery over pictures, mirrors and doorways, and around the mantelpiece. Some made garlands and wreaths. Even so, decorations were simple compared with today. There were no electric lights or shop-bought ornaments for most families. Paper chains, ribbons and candles added colour and light. In wealthier homes, the decorations were more elaborate. Historians note that the decorations were usually kept up until Twelfth Night, when they were taken down and burned. It was considered unlucky to leave them up after that. The scent of evergreens, the glow of candles and the warmth of the fire created a special atmosphere. For children, the decorating was one of the most exciting moments of the season.

[long-pause]

{img:69-christmas-eve-ghost-story-fireside}

Telling ghost stories around the fire was an old Christmas Eve tradition. In Victorian England, Christmas Eve was a time for telling ghost stories. Historians note that this was an old tradition, going back centuries. In the long, dark nights of winter, families gathered around the fire and told tales of ghosts, spirits and strange happenings. The darkness outside and the warmth inside made the stories both frightening and comforting. Even so, ghost stories at Christmas were not meant to be truly terrifying. They were a kind of shared entertainment, enjoyed by young and old. Dickens loved the tradition. He described A Christmas Carol as a ghost story of Christmas, and he wrote it to be read aloud by the fire. Historians note that he wrote several other Christmas books and stories in the following years, many of them with ghosts. Other Victorian writers, such as Elizabeth Gaskell and later M. R. James, also wrote ghost stories for Christmas. The tradition continued throughout the century.

[long-pause]

{img:70-midnight-church-service-candles-snow}

Some people attended church services late on Christmas Eve, while bells rang across the city. On Christmas Eve, some churches in London held services in the evening. Historians note that the bells of many churches rang to mark the beginning of Christmas. In some places, bell ringers rang the bells at midnight to welcome Christmas Day. Even so, midnight services were not as common in 1843 as they are today. They were held mainly in Catholic churches and in a few Anglican churches. Most people went to church on Christmas morning instead. Historians note that the sound of bells ringing across the city on Christmas Eve was a special part of the festive season. People walking home through the dark, cold streets heard the bells from near and far, echoing over the rooftops. In A Christmas Carol, the bells play an important part. Scrooge hears a bell ring before Marley's ghost appears, and each spirit arrives as a church bell strikes the hour. The bells mark the passing of his long night.

[long-pause]

{img:71-children-hanging-stockings-fireplace}

In 1843, Father Christmas was a jolly figure of feasting, not yet the bringer of presents to children. In 1843, the figure of Father Christmas was well known in England, but he was different from the Father Christmas of today. Historians note that he was a jolly old man, dressed in a long green or brown robe trimmed with fur, who represented feasting, drinking and merriment. He did not bring presents to children. Even so, things were beginning to change. In America, the poem A Visit from Saint Nicholas, published in 1823, described Saint Nicholas arriving on Christmas Eve in a sleigh pulled by reindeer, bringing toys and filling stockings. This idea slowly spread to Britain. By the end of the nineteenth century, Father Christmas had become a bringer of presents, and children hung up stockings for him on Christmas Eve. Historians note that John Leech's picture of the Ghost of Christmas Present, in a green robe trimmed with fur, helped shape the image of Father Christmas in Britain.

[long-pause]

{img:72-quiet-snowy-street-night-christmas-eve}

By late on Christmas Eve, the streets were quiet, and the city waited for Christmas morning. Late on Christmas Eve, London grew quiet. Historians imagine the scene: the shops closed, the markets empty, the streets silent except for the footsteps of a few late walkers and the occasional cab. Snow might cover the roofs and pavements, though white Christmases were less common than people imagined. Gas lamps glowed softly along the streets. Even so, in many homes, people were still awake. Mothers finished preparing the Christmas pudding and stuffing the goose. Fathers wrapped small presents. Children lay in bed, too excited to sleep. Historians note that the image of a snowy Victorian Christmas owed much to Dickens. When he was a child, in the 1810s, England had several very cold and snowy winters. He remembered them all his life and filled his Christmas stories with snow and frost. In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge wakes on Christmas morning to find a clear, bright, cold day, with frost in the air and bells ringing.

[long-pause]

{img:73-christmas-morning-church-families-walking}

On Christmas morning, families dressed in their best clothes and went to church. On Christmas morning in 1843, many families in London went to church. Historians note that people dressed in their best clothes, and children were washed, combed and dressed carefully. The churches were decorated with holly and evergreens, and the services included special readings, prayers and hymns about the birth of Christ. Even so, not everyone went to church. Many working people were too tired or too poor, and some had lost touch with religion. Historians note that a religious census held in 1851 showed that less than half of the people in London attended church on a typical Sunday. Yet Christmas was a special day, and attendance was usually higher. After the service, people greeted their neighbours and friends, wishing each other a merry Christmas. In A Christmas Carol, the transformed Scrooge goes to church on Christmas morning and then walks through the streets, smiling at everyone and patting children on the head.

[long-pause]

{img:74-boy-carrying-goose-to-bakers-oven}

Families without ovens carried their geese and joints to the baker's shop to be roasted. On Christmas morning, the streets of poorer neighbourhoods in London were filled with people carrying dishes to the bakers' shops. Historians note that many families did not have ovens large enough to roast a goose or joint of meat. Instead, they took their dinner to the local baker, who cooked it in his large bread oven for a small fee. The dishes were usually placed on a bed of potatoes, which roasted in the fat. Even so, this custom required careful timing. The baker cooked many dinners at once and had to make sure that each family got the right dish. Families collected their dinners at about one o'clock and carried them home, steaming and smelling delicious. Historians note that Dickens described this custom in A Christmas Carol. The Ghost of Christmas Present takes Scrooge through the streets, where people carry their dinners to the bakers' shops. The ghost sprinkles water from his torch, and quarrels turn into laughter.

[long-pause]

{img:75-christmas-dinner-table-wealthy-family}

Wealthy families sat down to grand Christmas dinners with turkey, beef, pies and many other dishes. For wealthy families in Victorian London, Christmas dinner was a grand affair. Historians note that the table might include roast turkey or goose, roast beef, boiled ham, game pies, soups, vegetables, sauces, jellies, mince pies and the Christmas pudding. Wine, port and punch were served. Servants cooked and served the meal, which could last for hours. Even so, the servants themselves often had to work hard on Christmas Day. They prepared and served the family's dinner and cleaned up afterwards. In many households, they were given their own Christmas dinner, sometimes with gifts from their employers. Historians note that Christmas dinner became more elaborate during the Victorian period. Cookery books, such as Eliza Acton's Modern Cookery for Private Families, published in 1845, gave recipes for Christmas dishes. Later, Mrs Beeton's Book of Household Management, published in 1861, became the most famous Victorian cookery book, with many Christmas recipes.

[long-pause]

{img:76-servants-own-christmas-dinner-kitchen}

Servants worked hard on Christmas Day, but many were given their own dinner and small gifts. In 1843, a great many young women in London worked as domestic servants. Historians note that large households employed many servants, including cooks, maids, footmen, nursemaids and coachmen. Even modest middle-class households often had one or two servants. At Christmas, servants had extra work, preparing food, decorating the house and serving guests. Even so, many employers treated their servants kindly at Christmas. Servants were often given a special dinner, sometimes with a goose or a joint of meat, a pudding and beer or wine. They might also receive small presents, such as cloth for a new dress, gloves or money. Historians note that some households held a servants' ball or party during the Christmas season. Yet the servants' Christmas depended greatly on the kindness of their employers. Some were treated generously, while others were given little or nothing. Their day off usually came later, after the family's celebrations were over.

[long-pause]

{img:77-queen-victoria-windsor-christmas-1843}

Queen Victoria and Prince Albert spent Christmas 1843 at Windsor Castle with their young children. Queen Victoria and Prince Albert usually spent Christmas at Windsor Castle, west of London. Historians note that they celebrated in a mixture of English and German styles. In 1843, they had three young children: Victoria, Albert Edward and Alice. The royal family exchanged presents on Christmas Eve, following the German custom, with decorated trees on tables in the royal apartments. Even so, their Christmas also included English traditions, such as roast beef, plum pudding and a boar's head. Victoria wrote in her journal about the joy of seeing the children's delight. Historians note that the royal family's Christmas became a model for families across Britain. Newspapers and magazines described their celebrations, and people copied their customs. Victoria and Albert's example helped make Christmas a family festival, centred on children, home and gifts. Their love of the Christmas tree, in particular, made it popular throughout the country.

[long-pause]

{img:78-afternoon-family-games-fireside-christmas}

After dinner, families spent the afternoon and evening playing games, singing and telling stories. After Christmas dinner, Victorian families relaxed together. Historians note that they played games, sang songs, danced, told stories and ate nuts, oranges and sweets. In homes with a piano, someone might play while others sang. In poorer homes, a fiddle or a voice might be the only music. Even so, the afternoon was also a time for rest. After a large meal, some people dozed by the fire. Others took a walk in the fresh air. Visitors came to wish the family a merry Christmas. Historians note that in A Christmas Carol, the Cratchits spend the evening around the fire, roasting chestnuts and drinking a hot punch made from gin and lemons. Tiny Tim sings a song about a lost child travelling in the snow. The family is poor, but happy and grateful. Dickens wanted to show that the true spirit of Christmas lay in love, family and kindness, not in wealth.

[long-pause]

{img:79-scrooge-style-old-man-generous-gift}

In the story, Scrooge wakes on Christmas morning a changed man, and sends the Cratchits the prize turkey. At the end of A Christmas Carol, Scrooge wakes up on Christmas morning and discovers that the spirits have done their work in a single night. Historians note that he is overjoyed. He throws open his window and asks a boy in the street what day it is. When the boy says it is Christmas Day, Scrooge sends him to buy the prize turkey from the poulterer's shop and has it delivered to the Cratchits. Even so, this is only the beginning of his change. He gives a large gift of money to the gentlemen collecting for the poor. He goes to his nephew Fred's house for Christmas dinner and is welcomed warmly. The next day, he raises Bob Cratchit's salary and promises to help his family. Historians note that the ending made readers cry and laugh. It showed that anyone could change, and that kindness could make a real difference. Dickens wrote that Scrooge became as good a friend as the city knew.

[long-pause]

{img:80-boxing-day-tradesmen-christmas-box-door}

On the day after Christmas, called Boxing Day, tradesmen and servants received Christmas boxes. The twenty-sixth of December was known in Britain as Boxing Day. Historians note that the name came from the custom of giving Christmas boxes, small gifts of money or goods, to tradesmen, servants and others who had served people during the year. Postmen, milkmen, bakers' boys, lamplighters and other workers went from house to house collecting their Christmas boxes. Even so, the origin of the name is debated. Some historians believe it came from the boxes kept in churches to collect money for the poor, which were opened on the day after Christmas, Saint Stephen's Day. Others believe it came from the boxes given to servants. In many households, servants were given the day off on Boxing Day to visit their families, after working hard on Christmas Day. Historians note that Boxing Day became an official public holiday in 1871. It is still a holiday in Britain and many other countries today.

[long-pause]

{img:81-pantomime-theatre-stage-audience-1843}

On Boxing Day, the theatres opened their Christmas pantomimes, which were loved by families. Pantomime was one of the great entertainments of the Victorian Christmas season. Historians note that new pantomimes opened in London theatres on Boxing Day, and families flocked to see them. Victorian pantomimes were based on fairy tales and nursery rhymes, such as Cinderella, Jack and the Beanstalk and Puss in Boots. They included music, dancing, jokes, spectacular scenery and magical transformations. Even so, the most famous part of the early Victorian pantomime was the harlequinade, a comic chase involving the characters Harlequin, Columbine, Pantaloon and the Clown. The great clown Joseph Grimaldi had made the role famous in the early nineteenth century. Historians note that Dickens loved pantomime and edited Grimaldi's memoirs in 1838. Over time, the harlequinade became shorter, and the fairy tale became the main part of the show. Pantomimes remain a popular Christmas tradition in Britain today, with audiences joining in with songs and shouts.

[long-pause]

{img:82-carol-stage-play-1844-theatre}

Within weeks of its publication, A Christmas Carol was being performed on the stages of London. A Christmas Carol was so popular that theatres quickly turned it into plays. Historians note that by February 1844, at least eight different stage versions were being performed in London. In those days, there were few laws to protect authors, so theatres could adapt books without asking permission or paying the author. One of the most successful versions was produced at the Adelphi Theatre, with the approval of Dickens. Even so, Dickens was not always pleased with the adaptations. Some changed the story or added scenes. Yet he attended at least one and was moved by it. Historians note that the plays helped spread the story to people who could not afford to buy the book or could not read. Since then, A Christmas Carol has been adapted for the stage and the screen countless times. It is one of the most frequently adapted stories in the English language, performed every Christmas around the world.

[long-pause]

{img:83-dickens-public-reading-stage-lamp-1853}

In later years, Dickens performed public readings of A Christmas Carol to huge audiences. In December 1853, Charles Dickens gave his first public reading of A Christmas Carol at the Town Hall in Birmingham, to raise money for a new institute for working people. Historians note that it was a great success. About two thousand people came to listen. Dickens read for about three hours, using different voices for the characters and acting out the scenes. Even so, this was only the beginning. Over the next seventeen years, Dickens gave hundreds of public readings across Britain and America. A Christmas Carol was always one of his most popular pieces. He shortened it for performance and read it in a dramatic and moving style. Historians note that the readings were exhausting and may have damaged his health. He gave his last reading in March 1870, finishing with A Christmas Carol. He died on the ninth of June 1870, at the age of fifty-eight, and was buried in Westminster Abbey.

[long-pause]

{img:84-victorian-christmas-legacy-family-tree-1850s}

In the years after 1843, the Victorian Christmas grew into the festival that is known today. In the decades after 1843, Christmas in Britain changed greatly. Historians note that the Christmas tree became popular after 1848. Christmas cards became common in the 1870s and 1880s, as printing became cheaper and the postal service grew. Christmas crackers, invented by the London sweet maker Tom Smith around 1847, became a popular part of Christmas dinner. Turkey gradually replaced goose as the favourite Christmas meal. Even so, the heart of the Victorian Christmas remained the same: family, food, generosity and kindness. Historians note that Dickens's A Christmas Carol played an important part in shaping these values. It encouraged people to celebrate Christmas joyfully and to remember the poor. After reading it, some employers gave their workers extra money or a turkey at Christmas. Charities received more donations. The book helped make Christmas a time not only of celebration, but also of giving, which it remains for many people today.

[long-pause]

{img:85-dickens-museum-doughty-street-study}

Today, the house where Dickens once lived in Doughty Street is a museum about his life and work. Charles Dickens lived in many houses in London during his life. Historians note that the only one that survives is at 48 Doughty Street, in Bloomsbury, where he lived from 1837 to 1839. There he wrote Oliver Twist and Nicholas Nickleby. Today, the house is the Charles Dickens Museum. It is furnished as it might have been in his time, with his desk, chairs, books and personal objects. Even so, the house he lived in when he wrote A Christmas Carol, at Devonshire Terrace, no longer exists. Each December, the museum decorates its rooms for a Victorian Christmas and holds readings of A Christmas Carol. Historians note that Dickens's legacy is celebrated around the world. His books are read in many languages, and his characters are known to millions. His Christmas story, in particular, has never lost its popularity. Every December, families and theatres around the world tell it again.

[long-pause]

{img:86-victorian-street-christmas-night-snow-calm}

On Christmas night in 1843, the city slept, and the little red book lay waiting on many tables. On Christmas night in 1843, London was quiet at last. Historians imagine the scene: families gone to bed after a long and happy day, fires burning low in the grates, the streets empty and silent. In some homes, a small red book with gold edges lay on the table, its pages read aloud by the fire that evening. Even so, not every home was warm and full. In the slums and the workhouses, Christmas had brought little comfort. Dickens knew this, and he hoped that his book would help change it. Historians note that A Christmas Carol did not end poverty in London, but it touched the hearts of many readers. It reminded them that Christmas was a time to care for others, and that it was never too late to change. More than one hundred and eighty years later, its message is still heard every December, as the lights glow and the bells ring once again.
